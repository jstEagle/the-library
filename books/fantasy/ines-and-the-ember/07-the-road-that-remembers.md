## 7. The Road That Remembers

The road out of the city was called the Pilgrim's Way on Sefa's maps, and the Toll of Names by everyone who'd survived it, and "that road that eats you" by everyone who hadn't. Ines had heard none of these names before three days ago. Now she knew all of them, and she hated the last one best, because it was honest.

"You pay as you walk," Sefa explained, unrolling a scrap of hide that looked like a map of a snake having an argument with itself. The road on it kept changing shape even as she held it down with four smooth stones and her boot. "Every mile, it takes something. A memory. It doesn't ask which one. You don't get to choose."

"Then how do people keep anything?" Ines asked.

"They don't," said Old Brannic cheerfully. He was walking at the back with his pack over one shoulder like a man going to visit relatives he didn't like. "That's the trick of it. By the time you reach the far end, you can't remember why you were walking, only that you must have had a very good reason, because look — you're still walking."

Ines decided she did not like Brannic.

Ada stood at the head of the group with their hands in their coat pockets and their face doing that thing where it looked calm in a way that made everything around it worse. Like a person standing very still in a room where the floor is tilting. Ines had learned to read Ada's face the way you learn to read weather: the stillness meant the storm was coming later, and it would be bad.

"We could go around," Ada said. "It adds nine days."

"Nine days is nine days closer to the mountain," Brannic said. "And I've got maybe eleven in me, if we're being honest, which nobody ever is at the start of a journey."

Sefa tapped the map. "Around means through the Ash Market anyway, and the market takes memories too, except there you choose them, and choosing is worse." She said this like someone reporting that the soup was cold. Ines was starting to understand that Sefa treated catastrophe the way other people treated rain.

"So we take the road," Ada said.

"So we take the road," Sefa agreed.

Nobody asked Ines. She was eleven. She had an ember in a tin box and a brother's soul on deposit under a mountain and a dead mother's signature in a ledger, and apparently all of that added up to cargo, not crew. She'd stopped arguing about it somewhere outside the guild doors, mostly because arguing made Ada do the thing where they got quieter, and quiet Ada was worse than loud Ada, who didn't exist.

But she had a plan. She'd had it since the night the stars went out, when she'd sat up in the guild's guest cot and rehearsed it against the dark: *I will not forget Tam.*

Tam. Her brother. Sixteen years old, tall for his age, laughed like a door falling off its hinges, couldn't cook anything without burning it, once convinced her the neighbor's goat spoke fluent Low Courtish and made her apologize to it for a week. Sold. His soul sold, by their mother, to pay a debt Ines had never been allowed to see the paperwork for. All that was left of him fit in a tin box that burned without heat, and Ines carried it against her sternum under her coat, and when she pressed her hand flat over it she could almost pretend it was his heartbeat.

She was not going to forget him. Not on purpose, not by accident, not because some road had an appetite.

* * *

The first mile was easy. The second mile was easy. The third mile, Ines started counting things.

*Tam's laugh.* Door-hinges. She said it to herself like a spell. *Tam's laugh, door-hinges.*

"Talking to yourself?" Brannic asked, falling into step beside her. He walked like his knees were negotiating a treaty.

"Yes."

"Good habit. On this road especially." He didn't ask what she was saying. That was the thing about Brannic — he was nosy about everything except the things that mattered, which Ines had decided was either cowardice or kindness, and she hadn't picked yet.

"Mile four," Sefa called from behind, holding her map. The map had begun to sweat, which maps shouldn't do. "First losses usually land here. Small ones. The color of your front door. The name of a childhood pet. Nothing load-bearing."

*Load-bearing,* Ines thought. As if memories were beams in a house and the road was checking which ones it could pull without the whole thing collapsing. She hugged the thought of Tam tighter. *Tam. My brother. Sixteen. Burned the eggs every morning and ate them anyway and said charred food builds character.*

Something flickered at the edge of her mind — a smell, woodsmoke and something sweeter, a festival? — and then it was gone, and she felt the absence the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expected it. She grabbed for it. Gone. Whatever it had been, it had been hers, and now it wasn't, and no one had even apologized.

"It took something," she said out loud, because saying it made it real and real things could be fought.

"What?" Sefa asked.

"I don't know. That's the whole problem."

"That's always the whole problem," Brannic said. "With roads and with life."

Ada, ahead of them, said nothing. Ada was walking like the road owed *them* money, which, knowing Ada, it might.

* * *

By mile six, Ines had developed a system, and the system was repetition, and the repetition was exhausting, and she did it anyway. Every hundred steps: *Tam. Brother. Sixteen. Ember. Tin box. Under my coat. Mother signed. The court below bought him. We are going to get him back.* She said it in her head, then out loud when her head got slippery, and Brannic started calling it her litany, and she decided that was an acceptable word for it.

The trouble was the others weren't doing it.

Or — worse — they were, and it was working differently on them.

At mile seven, Ada stopped mid-stride, turned around, and looked at the road behind them with an expression Ines had never seen before. Not fear. Something more like a man checking his pockets after leaving a shop and finding them lighter than expected, and being unable to remember what he'd bought.

"I think it took something," Ada said.

"What?" said Sefa.

"I don't know." Ada's voice was perfectly level. That was the frightening part. "I counted what I know and something's missing from the count, but I can't tell what, because if I could tell what, it wouldn't be missing."

"That's normal," Sefa said. "That's how it works. You feel the hole, not the thing."

Ada nodded, once, and turned back around, and kept walking, and here was the thing that scared Ines, the thing she turned over that night at the fireless camp (no fires on the road; the road disliked light, or possibly just disliked hope): Ada hadn't seemed upset. Ada had seemed *bookkeeping*. Like a loss entered in a column, balanced against something else, filed away. Like losing a memory was a fee, and fees were fine, and fine was a category Ada lived in most of the time.

Ines lay with her back against her pack and the tin box under her hand and watched Ada sit apart, sharpening a knife that never seemed to get any sharper or any duller, and she thought: *what kind of person loses pieces of themselves and doesn't scream about it?*

Then she thought: *the kind of person who has lost so many pieces already that one more doesn't make a sound.*

That thought frightened her more than the road did.

* * *

Mile ten. The trees along the road had gone strange — too straight, too evenly spaced, like soldiers pretending to be a forest. Sefa said the road grew them itself, from the memories it ate, which Ines chose not to believe and also could not stop believing.

Her litany was wearing grooves in her skull. *Tam. Brother. Sixteen. Ember. Tin box.* But the road was clever. She began to notice that. It didn't take the center of a thing. It took the edges. It left her knowing Tam's laugh was door-hinges and stole the actual sound of it. It left her knowing he burned the eggs and stole the smell of breakfast, the specific gray morning light of their kitchen, the way his boots squeaked on the third stair. She still had Tam. She was losing Tam's world around him, the frame, and the picture was starting to float loose.

So she talked. Out loud, walking, to anyone who'd listen and Brannic, who'd listen to anything.

"He once convinced me our neighbor's goat was cursed," she told Sefa, "and I apologized to it for a week, and then the goat bit me, and Tam laughed so hard he fell in the trough."

"Was the goat cursed?" Sefa asked, with the seriousness of a cartographer confirming a landmark.

"No. Tam was a liar. A good one. He'd have liked the guild, probably. Is that true? Did I make that up?"

"You said it yesterday too," Sefa said. "Same words. So either it's true or you're consistent, and both are useful on this road."

At the next rest, Ines caught Ada watching her. Not the usual watching — Ada watched everything, catalogued everything, Ines was fairly sure she'd been entered into some internal ledger the moment she'd broken into the guild — but a different quality of attention. Softer. Sadder.

"You repeat things," Ada said.

"It works."

"It does." A pause. "I used to do that. When I was young. Before I learned that forgetting could be a mercy."

Ines stared at them. "A mercy?"

"Some things cost less to lose than to carry." Ada said it gently, like it was a kindness, and Ines felt something in her chest go hot and narrow, hotter than the ember ever got.

"You don't get to decide that," she said. "About me. About my brother. About—" She stopped, because she'd almost said *about yourself*, and she wasn't sure she had the right, and also she was absolutely sure she did.

Ada looked at her for a long moment. Then Ada said, "No. I don't," and went back to the knife, and Ines stood there feeling like she'd won an argument and lost something else, something she couldn't name, which on this road was becoming a familiar feeling and she hated it, hated it, hated it.

* * *

Mile thirteen was where the road got hungry.

They all felt it at once — a sort of leaning, like the ground tilted toward the center of the path. Sefa checked her map and made a noise like a kettle giving up. "Thirteen's a feeding mile. Big ones come loose here. Hold on to whatever matters."

"Hold on *how*?" Ines demanded. "You said we don't get to choose!"

"You don't choose what it takes," Sefa said. "You choose what you hold. There's a difference. Small, but it's the only difference there is."

Ines gripped the tin box through her coat and ran her litany faster. *Tam. Brother. Sixteen. Ember. Laughs like door-hinges. Burns the eggs. Goat. Trough. Third stair squeaks.* The third stair squeaks — that one was new to the litany, rescued two miles ago, and she repeated it like a prayer, *third stair squeaks, third stair squeaks,* until it wore smooth as a river stone.

Brannic sat down on a milestone and announced he was going to think about his wife.

"Your wife?" Ines said.

"Forty-one years. Dead nine. Best argument I ever lost." He closed his eyes. "Road can't have her. I'll fight it for her. I'm old, I'm stubborn, and I've nothing better to do than win."

He sat there for a quarter hour, eyes shut, fists clenched on his knees like a man arm-wrestling the invisible. When he opened his eyes again they were wet, and he said, "Still got her. Mostly. Lost her wedding ring's color. Gold, I think. Might've been the good gold or the everyday gold." He shrugged. "Worth it. She's worth more than a color."

Ines understood, suddenly, why Ada kept Brannic around, and it wasn't the debt he owed the guild. It was this. He knew exactly what things were worth, and he paid without flinching, and he grieved what he lost out loud, immediately, in front of everyone, because grief hidden was grief the road could pretend hadn't happened.

Which was the opposite of Ada. Which was the point.

Because at mile fourteen, Ada stopped again, and this time Ada smiled.

Not a big smile. One corner of the mouth. But a smile, unmistakable, standing on the road that eats people, after losing another piece of themselves, and Ines felt cold all down her spine.

"What," she said. "What are you—"

"I remembered something," Ada said. "Or rather — I remembered that I'd forgotten something, and then I realized I was glad."

"Glad?"

"There was a voice." Ada's eyes had gone somewhere else, somewhere behind the world. "Someone used to speak to me. I've been carrying the sound of it for years like a stone in my boot. And the road took the sound, and what's left is just the fact of it — that I was spoken to, once, by something that mattered — and the fact weighs less than the sound did." They flexed their empty hands. "I feel lighter."

Ines wanted to throw something at them. She had excellent aim; she'd spent a childhood throwing apples at Tam's head. But you didn't throw things at the person leading you under a mountain, so instead she said, in the flattest voice she owned:

"You're supposed to fight it."

"For what?" Ada said. "For the privilege of hurting?"

"Yes!" It came out louder than she meant. A bird went up off the road, if those were birds. "Yes! Because if you don't fight, then it's not losing, it's — it's *giving up*, and you're teaching the road it can just take, and—" She was shaking. She knew she was shaking. Eleven-year-olds aren't supposed to shake at adults, but nobody had told the road that either. "If forgetting is easy for you, then what happens when it wants something big? What happens when it gets to the mountain and wants to know why we came?"

Ada went very still.

"And what happens," Ines said, quieter now, because the loud part was spent, "when it comes for *me*? If you've practiced letting go, will you let go of me too? Will it be easy? Will you feel lighter?"

Silence. The straight trees leaned in. Somewhere behind them, Brannic pretended not to listen, badly.

Ada crouched down, slowly, until they were nearly her height, which they never did, and said: "No."

Just that. No speech, no ledger-talk, no calm. Just *no*, said like a hand catching a falling cup.

"But you'd want to," Ines said. "Part of you. The part that feels lighter."

"...Yes," Ada admitted. "Part of me would want to. That part has been practicing for a long time." They stood up. "So watch me, Ines. Watch me the whole way. If I start letting go of things easily — if I start feeling lighter — tell me. Loudly. You're clearly good at loudly."

Ines wiped her face with her sleeve, which was filthy, and agreed, and hated that she'd agreed, and was glad she'd agreed, all at once, which she was beginning to suspect was what family felt like.

* * *

They made camp at mile fifteen, at the edge of a bridge that appeared out of the fog like something surfacing to breathe. Stone, old, older than the wards, older probably than the guild. A toll-house squatted at its near end with one lit window, and inside the window sat a shape that might have been a person and might have been a desk that had learned to wear a cloak.

"The toll-keeper," Sefa said. "Takes names here. Not coin. You cross, you leave a name behind."

"Whose name?" Ines asked.

"Yours. Any of yours. Your given name, your mother's name, the name you were called as a child, the name you'll take someday. It keeps them. Nobody knows why. Nobody who's crossed has come back able to say."

Brannic looked at the bridge the way a man looks at an old enemy across a battlefield. "I've crossed this before," he said quietly. "Long ago. I don't remember crossing it. I remember the far side. Isn't that how it always goes."

And then Marrow arrived.

Ines had met Marrow exactly once, briefly, at the guild — a ghost shaped like a debt-collector, all ink-stained cuffs and patient menace, the kind of dead man who still kept office hours. He drifted up the road out of the fog like a rumor with a briefcase, and Ada greeted him with the wary nod of someone greeting a former colleague at a funeral.

"You followed us," Ada said.

"I was sent," Marrow said. "By the guild. By the part of the guild that isn't speaking to the part of the guild that refused to come." He looked at the bridge for a long time. Ines noticed his hands. Ghosts didn't need to fidget, and he was fidgeting. "I know this crossing. I know the keeper. I worked this road, once — before. For them. For the court below."

The fog seemed to lean closer.

"I collected names," Marrow said. "For years. I told myself it was clerical work. Paperwork. That I wasn't taking anything, only filing it." His voice did something complicated. "Then one night I filed a name and watched what the court did with it, and I walked off the job, and I have been a debtor of theirs ever since. They don't chase ghosts. They wait. Roads like this one are their fingers."

Ada said, carefully, "Why are you telling us this?"

"Because you're going to need a guide across, and the keeper knows me, and hates me, which is a form of respect." Marrow drifted a step nearer the bridge. "And because I want a price. Not payment. A price. When this ends — however it ends — I want my own name back. The one I surrendered when I took their employment. It'll be in the court's ledgers, filed under debts outstanding. Erase the debt, return the name, and I'll walk you across this bridge and every bridge between here and the mountain's door."

Ines looked at the ghost offering to sell his betrayal backward into redemption, and she thought about Tam, and about how everyone in this strange, ragged company was carrying a debt they were trying to walk off, and she thought: *this is what family is. People whose shame matches yours.*

Ada looked at Marrow for a long moment, and then did the thing Ines had seen them do only twice before: they took out the little black book they kept in their inner pocket, licked their thumb, and turned to a fresh page.

"One name," Ada said. "Returned intact. Witnessed and entered."

"Witnessed and entered," Marrow agreed, and put out a hand that cast no shadow in the toll-keeper's lamplight, and shook on it.

Beyond the bridge, the fog rolled and folded, and somewhere underneath it, faint as breath, Ines heard the road humming — a low, patient note, like something enormous waiting to be fed.

She pressed her hand to the tin box under her coat.

*Tam,* she told it silently. *Brother. Sixteen. Third stair squeaks. We're coming.*
