## 8. The Toll of Names

I have been dead for sixty-one years, and in all that time I have learned exactly one thing worth teaching: everyone is running from a debt they don't want to name.

The living are worse about it than the dead. The dead have had their accounts settled by force; there's a certain honesty in that. But the living will carry a ledger around inside their ribs their whole lives, growing heavier every year, and never once open it to look at the entries. They'll call it grief. They'll call it shame. They'll call it nothing at all, and drink, and walk faster at night.

I know because I did it. For two hundred years before I died, I carried other people's debts for a living, and I told myself that made me clean. It didn't. But we'll get to that.

Here's what happened at the bridge.

* * *

The fog came up to our knees like something patient, and out of it stood the toll-keeper, and I knew him before he raised his lamp.

That's not a figure of speech. I mean I knew him. Ossian Vael. Third-rank collector, Court Beneath, Department of Outstanding Obligations. He trained me. He taught me how to read a soul's weight by the way a person hesitates before saying their own name. He was old when I was new, and now he stood on a bridge in the middle of nowhere wearing a lamplighter's coat, and he pretended not to know me, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in a century.

"Passage is one name per traveler," he said. "Payable now. Non-refundable."

"A name," Ada repeated. They always were the careful one, our guildkeeper. "What does that mean, exactly? Because if you're thinking of taking mine, I'd like to see the paperwork first."

Ossian almost smiled. I saw it happen behind his eyes and get strangled before it reached his mouth. Good instincts, both of them.

"It means," he said, "that when you cross, I will hold your name. You'll keep walking, talking, eating, bleeding. But no one will be able to say what you're called. Not your friends. Not your enemies. Not yourself. You'll feel it like a word on the tip of the tongue that never arrives." He lifted the lamp. "Some travelers find it restful."

"And some find it permanent," Sefa said. She had her map-skin half unrolled already, though there was nothing on this bridge to chart but fog and us. "How long do you hold them?"

"Until paid back." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Or until someone pays for you."

Ines stepped forward. Eleven years old, furious, exhausted, carrying her brother's soul in a biscuit tin, and she looked that ancient creature dead in its hooded face and said, "You can't have Tam's name. Or mine. We've only got the one each."

And that, gentle reader — well, there's no reader, I'm a ghost talking to myself in the dark, but bear with me — that was the moment I understood I was going to do it. Because she said *we've only got the one each*, and she said it like a person defending the last coin in the house, and I remembered being new at the court, and being handed my first ledger, and being told: *names are just currency that talks.*

They're not. That's the lie the whole court runs on. A name is the handle by which love gets a grip on you. Take it away and people can still function, but nobody can reach them anymore. The court doesn't collect names because they're valuable. It collects them because it can't abide being loved by anything it owns.

"Everyone step back a pace," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the only kind of steady I've ever had.

Ada looked at me. Brannic looked at me. Even the ember in the girl's tin box seemed to lean, the way a dog leans when it hears a door.

"Marrow," Ada said carefully. "What are you doing?"

"Negotiating," I said. "It's what I'm for."

* * *

Here is what I have never told any of them, and what I told the bridge, because the bridge was owed the truth before it would let us pass, and because — if I'm honest, and I've decided to try being honest, it's terrible, I recommend it sparingly — because I was tired.

Two hundred years ago, give or take a decade I've sanded off the edges of, I was alive. Poor, hungry, talented at arithmetic, and unlucky enough to be noticed by a recruiter for the Court Beneath. They don't take corpses, whatever the stories say. They take the living. They offer you a salary. The currency is years — other people's — and the work is collections.

I told myself I was only doing the arithmetic. I didn't take souls, I said. I just kept the books. I just calculated what a man's remaining summers were worth against what he owed, and wrote the number down, and handed it to someone whose job it was to collect. The number was always fair. That was my religion: fairness. I could put a price on anything and sleep afterward, because the price was *correct*.

Then one winter a woman came in owing nine years, and I priced them, and she signed, and it wasn't until the collectors led her out that I saw she was pregnant. Nine years, starting immediately. Her child would be born in the court's keeping. I checked the contract. Perfectly legal. Perfectly fair. I had written it myself, in my own neat hand, and my hand had not shaken once.

I requested a transfer to the deep archives. Then I stopped requesting things. One night I walked out of the court with nothing but the ledger I'd been issued — the one with my own account in it, because the court keeps an account on every employee, oh yes, indenture dressed up as career — and I ran, and I died badly somewhere in the border marshes, and instead of dissolving like a good little ghost I stayed, because my debt hadn't been discharged and I refused to let them write *settled* next to my name when it hadn't been. Spite, ladies and gentlemen. It has kept me animate for sixty-one years. It is a terrible fuel source and it burns dirty, but it burns.

"So," I said to the bridge, to Ossian, to all of them, "you want a name. I have one. It's mortgaged to the court, technically, but possession is nine-tenths, and I am standing right here."

"You worked for them," Ada said. Not a question. Their voice did the thing it does when they're filing something away in a drawer they don't open in company.

"I did. Two hundred years. Collections. I was good at it." I let that sit. Shame is like medicine; swallow it whole or it tastes worse going down in pieces. "And I deserted. Which means somewhere under the mountain there is a page with my name on it, marked *outstanding*, and every year the interest compounds. You want to know why I haunt a guild of impossible things? Because impossible things are the only ones left that might cancel a debt like mine."

The fog moved. Ossian's lamp burned very still.

"What are you offering?" the toll-keeper asked, and his voice had changed. Not warmer. Nothing about the court gets warmer. More *attentive*, the way a scale becomes attentive when you set something on it.

"Passage for five," I said. "No names taken. And in exchange — when this is over, however it ends — you take me down with you. To the court. I'll walk in the front door. I'll settle my own account, on my terms, and I'll serve as guide on the way, because I know those halls better than anyone living, dead, or otherwise assembled here tonight."

"That's not erasure," Sefa said sharply. "That's surrender with extra steps."

"It's the difference between being collected," I said, "and arriving on purpose. Ask any debtor. It's the whole difference."

Nobody asked any debtor. Ines was looking at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors I no longer owned — the look of someone doing sums they don't want the answer to. Children are the best auditors in the world. They haven't learned to fudge.

"You knew the toll-keeper," she said. "Before he raised the lamp. You knew him."

"I did."

"He's one of *them*."

"He's the man who trained me. And he's pretending not to know me, which either means mercy or a trap, and I've survived sixty-one years by betting on mercy and keeping a knife loose in case it's a trap."

Brannic laughed. Old Brannic, who owes the guild his death and keeps postponing it like a dentist appointment, laughed long and hard and wheezing, and clapped me on a shoulder that his hand went straight through, and said, "Oh, I like him better now. Isn't it funny? The dishonest ones make the best company once they come clean. It's the pious ones you want to count your spoons around."

"Brannic," Ada said.

"I'm just saying—"

"*Brannic.*"

He subsided. Ada turned back to me, and I watched them weigh it — I actually watched the scales move behind their eyes, because Ada Blackwood does everything in ledger-format, even grief, even trust. And then they said the thing that undid me more thoroughly than dying ever managed:

"Witnessed and entered?"

Because they weren't asking whether I'd keep my word. They were asking whether I wanted it *written down*. Made official. Counted. Sixty-one years of being an unregistered haunting, a rounding error, a thing the world declined to record — and this stubborn, silent-god-bothered clerk offered me the one thing the court had convinced me I'd forfeited forever: an entry. A line in a book with my name on it, saying I had chosen.

"One name," Ada said. "Returned intact. Witnessed and entered."

"Witnessed and entered," I agreed, and put out a hand that cast no shadow in the toll-keeper's lamplight, and shook on it.

Ossian Vael lowered the lamp. For one moment his hood tilted, and beneath it, where his face used to be, I felt — not saw, felt, the way you feel weather — something that in a living man might have been called sorrow, and in a collector of the Court Beneath was probably just an audit finding itself inconvenient.

"You were the best of them, Marrow," he said quietly. "That was always the problem."

Then the fog opened, and the bridge was ours, and we crossed.

* * *

We made camp on the far side, in the lee of a fallen milestone that Sefa swore had once marked the distance to a city that no longer agreed to exist. Brannic built a fire with the competence of a man who has built ten thousand fires and intends to build several thousand more. Ines sat with the tin box in her lap and her knees drawn up, and did not speak, and did not need to; the ember's glow leaked through the seams of the box and lay along her chin like a second, kinder jawline.

Sefa sat down beside me. This required some negotiation, as I do not entirely occupy space, but she has gotten good at sitting near ghosts; she leaves a gap exactly my shape and calls it cartography.

"You lied," she said pleasantly.

"About which part?"

"The part where you said you know the halls better than anyone. You said it like a boast. It wasn't a boast. You said it like a man reading his own sentence." She sharpened a pencil against the milestone. She always sharpens pencils when she's about to say something true; I think it gives her hands somewhere lawful to be. "You're not coming back, are you. From under the mountain. Whatever you arranged with that bridge."

I considered lying to her. She'd have caught it, and worse, she'd have *entered* it somewhere, in that head of hers where she keeps maps of moving places, including, I suspect, the inside of people.

"No," I said. "Probably not. Debts like mine don't get erased, Sefa. They get transferred, or they get paid. I've spent sixty-one years making sure nobody else paid mine. That was the whole point of deserting — not freedom. Just refusing to let the bill land on anyone else."

"And now you're guiding us straight to the creditor."

"Now I'm guiding a child to her brother," I said, "and a god's clerk to their god, and if my account happens to come due in the lobby on the way in, then fine. Fairness, Sefa. It was my religion. I'm trying to die in accordance with it."

She was quiet a while. The pencil stopped.

"My maps used to be wrong," she said suddenly. "Did I ever tell you? Before the guild. Every map I drew, the places moved — roads wandered, coastlines crept — and everyone said I was a bad cartographer, and I believed them for eleven years. It wasn't me. It was the places. But I'd already swallowed the story that I was the failure, and a swallowed story stays down." She looked at me across the gap shaped like me. "So. For the record. Witnessed and entered: you were the best of them, and that was the problem, and it isn't anymore."

I am a ghost. I cannot cry. I want that noted in the minutes. What happened to my face was condensation. Fog. Very damp night.

Across the fire, Ines had unhooked the tin box's lid by a finger's width, and the ember breathed its heatless light upward onto her face, and she was whispering to it. I pretend I don't listen to the living. It's a professional courtesy between me and myself, and we both know it's a fiction.

"—and Marrow's coming too," she was whispering. "He was bad but he stopped. That counts. Ada says stopping counts double if it costs you." A pause. The ember flickered, attentive. "Yes. Like you. Obviously like you."

Brannic passed around a bottle of something that tasted like a barn fire's apology, and even Ada drank, one measured swallow, entered and witnessed. And I sat there, sixty-one years dead, two hundred years damned, and thought: *this is what the court never understood.* It files souls on shelves. It thinks a soul is a thing you can own because you can weigh it.

But a soul isn't the weight. A soul is the *owing*. Love is just debt freely renewed, daily, without interest, and the court has been trying to foreclose on the one asset in creation that appreciates every time you give it away.

Somewhere past midnight, Ines fell asleep against the tin box, and the ember dimmed politely, the way a candle dims when its reader closes the book.

And that was when Sefa sat bolt upright, map-skin snapping open across her knees, and said, in a voice with no wry in it at all:

"Marrow. My maps. The road ahead — it's *moving*. All of it. Everything between here and the mountain is drifting toward the Ash Market like iron filings toward a magnet." She looked up, and the firelight made her look young, which she was not. "Something down there is pulling. And I don't think it's waiting for us to arrive."

I looked east, where the fog was thinning, and far off — impossibly far, days off — there was a smudge of orange light on the horizon that might have been dawn, except that dawn does not flicker, and dawn does not pulse, slow and regular, like something breathing.

Or counting.

"Get some sleep," I told them, all of them, my improbable, indebted, impossible family. "Tomorrow we sell things we can't afford to lose."

The light on the horizon pulsed again. Once. Twice.

Like a ledger, opening.
