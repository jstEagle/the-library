## 9. Ash Market

I have mapped every kind of place there is to map. Cities that migrate like herons. Rivers that run uphill on Tuesdays. A monastery that exists only in the reflection of a lake that dried up two hundred years ago — I charted it anyway, because a place that insists on existing deserves the courtesy of being found.

But I had never mapped a market before, and I want it noted, for the record, that I did not want to start with this one.

The Ash Market sits in a valley that shouldn't hold it. That's not poetry; that's surveying. When we crested the ridge at dusk, Sefa's instruments — my instruments, I should say, since I am Sefa and this narration is unfortunately mine — told me the valley was forty paces deep. The market filled it to the brim and spilled over the edges like something poured too fast by someone who wasn't measuring.

"Everything here is sold too fast by someone who isn't measuring," Marrow said, drifting beside me. He doesn't walk so much as arrive repeatedly, one afterimage at a time. "That's the whole business model."

"What's the commodity?" Ines asked. She had the tin box clutched to her chest with both arms, the way children carry things they've been told are precious and have decided are sacred.

"Everything you can't hold," Brannic said. He was walking with his usual gait, which is to say the gait of a man who has been dying for eleven years and has developed opinions about pacing himself. "Memories. Years. Shadows, if yours is good quality. Names, if you're desperate. Regret sells poorly — everyone's already got some."

Ada said nothing. Ada had been saying very little for three days, which from Ada was normal, but lately their silences had developed a texture I didn't care for. They used to be the silence of a person conserving words like coins. Now it was the silence of a person standing in a room where a debt is about to come due.

We went down into the valley because there was no other way through. The road that remembers had delivered us here — it takes its toll in memory, and we'd all paid, some more gracefully than others — and the road ended at the market's edge as if it had been leading us somewhere all along, which, knowing roads, it probably had.

* * *

Let me describe the Ash Market properly, because if I'm going to be the cartographer of record for this disaster, I'm going to do it right.

It smelled of woodsmoke and old paper and, underneath both, something sweeter and worse: the smell of things burning down to what can't burn. The stalls were built from doors — just doors, standing upright, hinged to nothing, each one opening onto a stall that was bigger or smaller than the door suggested, depending on how much the merchant liked you. Lanterns hung from wires strung between them, and the light they gave was the color of remembered afternoons, which is a color I can't name but recognized instantly and mistrusted immediately.

The merchants were mostly human-shaped. Mostly.

There was a woman selling years out of glass jars — five-year jars, ten-year jars, one jar labeled simply *the summer you were nineteen*, which I looked at longer than I should have. There was a man whose shadow had been sold off his body years ago and who now rented other people's shadows by the hour, wearing them like ill-fitting coats. There was a stall that sold nothing visible at all; you stood in front of it and felt something leave you, and then the merchant handed you a receipt, and you never knew quite what you'd lost until you needed it.

And everywhere, threaded through the crowd like grey thread through grey cloth, were the quiet ones. Collectors. Not court agents yet — I'd know one now, after what happened, though I didn't then — but their lesser cousins, the ones who buy on spec. They moved through the market touching people lightly on the shoulder, and where they touched, people leaned toward them like plants toward a lamp, saying yes, yes, take it, whatever it costs, take it.

"That's the trick of a place like this," Marrow murmured. "Nobody comes here to sell. They come here to stop carrying."

Ines pressed closer to Ada. "Are we buying or selling?"

"Yes," said Ada.

* * *

Here is what I knew, walking into that valley, and here is what I failed to do with the knowledge:

I knew the road ahead cost more than we could pay in coin. The pass beyond the market — the one Brannic had been quietly studying maps of, the one he kept not-quite-asking questions about — required passage, and passage required either money we didn't have or trade goods of the sort this market dealt in. I knew Ada had been counting our resources the way Ada counts everything: precisely, silently, and with an expression suggesting the total keeps coming up short.

What I failed to do was watch Ada's hands.

I'm a cartographer. Reading terrain is my trade, and people are terrain — they have contours and fault lines and places where the ground gives way without warning. I should have seen it coming. Instead I was busy arguing with a merchant about whether his map of the pass was drawn to scale or drawn to persuade, and when I turned around, Ada was at a stall near the market's heart, and Ines was standing beside them looking up with an expression I will be honest about even though it shames me: she looked hopeful. She thought they were shopping.

The stall was small. One door, dark wood, no lantern. The merchant was old in a way that seemed less like age and more like erosion — as if he had been rained on for a very long time by something that wasn't water. His goods were laid out on a cloth that might once have been white: a row of small clay vessels, unglazed, sealed with wax, each one humming faintly at a different pitch.

Memories. Good ones. The kind that keep.

"We need passage through the pass," Ada said. Their voice was level. It was the guild voice, the ledger voice, the voice they used for transactions, and I remember thinking, distantly, professionally, *that voice is doing its job too well.*

"The pass is held," the merchant said. "The toll-keeper beyond takes payment in kind. Coin means nothing to him. But I can give you what opens the road." He touched one of the clay vessels. It hummed. "This one is dear."

"How much?"

He looked at Ada for a long moment. Merchants like him don't price goods; they price sellers. Whatever he saw in Ada made him smile, and I wished he hadn't.

"One memory," he said. "Of a voice. Any voice, so long as it mattered."

The market noise went on around us — haggling, laughter, the wet sound of someone weeping over a jar — but our little circle went quiet. Ines looked at Ada. Brannic looked at Ada. Marrow drifted a half-circle closer, which is how ghosts lean in.

"Don't," Ines said. Just that. One syllable with a whole argument inside it.

"It's fine," Ada said.

It was not fine. I want that on the record too. Everything about it was the opposite of fine, and the worst part — the part I still turn over at night like a stone with something underneath it — is that Ada made it look fine. Made it look easy. Made it look like inventory management, like moving one item from one column to another, and I understood later that this was deliberate, that Ada performed casualness the way a soldier performs calm, so that the rest of us wouldn't have to carry the weight of watching it hurt.

Ada reached past the merchant and chose a vessel themselves. Didn't haggle. Didn't ask which memories were on offer. Just picked one up, weighed it in their palm, and nodded.

"This one."

"You know what you're giving?" the merchant asked. There are rules in these places, even in the bad ones. Consent has to be informed. It's the only law the Ash Market obeys.

"A voice," Ada said. "One voice. The only one I ever prayed to."

* * *

I need to back up, briefly, because I didn't understand what that meant until later, and the shape of my ignorance matters.

I knew Ada was bound to Veth. Everyone in the guild knows the story, or the version of the story the guild tells: sworn young, bound by contract, keeper of the ledgers for a god of debts and mercy who stopped answering prayers some years ago. I knew Ada hadn't prayed since the silence began. I knew — because Brannic told me once, drunk, by a fire, in the confessional tone old men reserve for other people's secrets — that Ada had loved that god. Not worshipped. Loved. The way you love a teacher who was also, without ever admitting it, a parent.

What I didn't know was what Veth's voice sounded like. Nobody did, anymore. It was one of those things the guild carried collectively without anyone holding it — like the original charter, like the founder's face. And now I watched Ada hand it over, the last copy in the world, to buy a child safe passage through a mountain pass, and file the transaction under *necessary expenses.*

The merchant broke the wax seal. Ada spoke into the vessel — I didn't hear the words, or maybe there were no words, maybe it was just breath shaped like remembering — and then the merchant stoppered it again, and the hum of that little clay pot changed. Went deeper. Warmer. Like a coal settling.

"There," the merchant said. "Paid."

Ada set the pot down on the cloth with exaggerated care, the way you set down something breakable when your hands aren't steady, and turned away, and said, "Sefa. The map."

And I gave them the map, because I'm a professional, and my hands were steadier than theirs, and that's the whole tragedy of competence: it gets volunteered for exactly the moments it's least suited to.

* * *

We made camp that night at the mouth of the pass, under a shelf of rock that my instruments insisted was geologically impossible and geologically furious about it. Brannic cooked. Brannic always cooks; he says a man postponing his death should at least die well-fed eventually, and his stew is the single strongest argument I know for delaying the inevitable.

Ines sat close to the fire with the tin box in her lap. The ember inside it glowed through the seams, faint and gold, and I noticed — because noticing is my job, even when I wish it weren't — that she kept turning to look at Ada.

"Ask me anything," Ada said finally. They were sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening. "You're staring."

"I'm checking," Ines said.

"Checking what?"

"If you're still you." She said it the way children say devastating things: plainly, without cruelty, with terrible accuracy. "The man at the market took something out of you. I watched. You smiled at him. You don't smile."

"I smile."

"You perform smiling." She hugged the tin box. "My mother did that. Toward the end. Sold things off piece by piece and smiled while she signed, so we wouldn't cry, so we wouldn't make it harder, and it didn't help, it just meant I had to memorize her face twice — once for me and once for when she forgot herself." Her chin came up. "So. Are you still you?"

Ada put the knife down. I watched them try to answer, and I watched the answer not arrive, and I understood then what had actually been sold — not just a voice, but the sound of it, the memory of hearing it, the whole sensory archive of what Veth sounded like when it spoke to the person who loved it best. What remained was the fact of the love with the experience scooped out of it. A room with the furniture gone. You still know it was a room. You can still see the pale rectangles on the walls where things hung.

"I'm still me," Ada said at last. "Less, maybe. But me."

"Less isn't nothing," Ines said fiercely. "Less adds up. Everybody here is less. That's what this whole stupid country is made of — everybody's less." She pointed at Marrow, at Brannic, at me. "Him. Him. Me. We're all somebody's remainder." Then she pointed at Ada, and her finger shook, and her voice didn't. "But you don't get to pretend it didn't happen. You don't get to smile."

"No," Ada said quietly. "I don't suppose I do."

They didn't smile. They sat with it instead, by the fire, with the rest of us, four remainders and a girl with an ember, and it was the least comfortable and most honest hour of the trip, and I would not trade it, which is a strange thing to say about grief, but cartographers learn early that the truest maps are the ones that hurt to draw.

* * *

The agent came at dawn.

I saw him first, because seeing things first is my entire value to this expedition and I cling to it. He came up the pass road from the direction we were headed, which was wrong — everything about him was wrong the way a drawing is wrong, accurate lines assembled into an inaccurate man. Grey coat. Grey hat. A face like a form filled out correctly. He walked at a pace that suggested he had never once hurried and never once needed to.

Marrow stiffened — a ghost stiffening is a strange thing, his edges went sharp — and said, "Court."

We were on our feet. Ada stepped in front of Ines without appearing to move, the way Ada does things, and the agent stopped a polite distance away and inclined his head.

"Keeper of Ledgers," he said. "A pleasure. Your credentials travel ahead of you. Word moves quickly through the low places." His eyes — I couldn't tell you their color; I've tried since, and there's nothing there to retrieve — moved to Ines, to the tin box, and stayed there a moment too long. "And the asset travels with you. How convenient."

"She's not an asset," Ada said.

"Everything is an asset," the agent said, with the mildness of a man reading a weather report. "The question is only whose. At present, hers is contested. The court's claim is older than her grief and considerably better documented." He produced, from nowhere, a slip of paper, and touched it to Ada's shoulder, and the touch left a mark — I saw it happen, a grey smudge on the cloak, faint as ash, that did not brush off, did not fade, and did not want to.

"What is that?" Ines demanded.

"A receipt," said the agent. "For services pending." He tipped his hat to all of us individually, which somehow felt like being catalogued, and walked on past us up the pass road, unhurried, correct, gone.

We stood in the cold morning looking at the mark on Ada's shoulder. It didn't glow. It didn't pulse. It just *was*, the way a lien is, the way a claim is — invisible until it isn't, painless until it very much isn't.

"He marked us," Brannic said.

"He marked *me*," Ada said. They touched the smudge and their fingers came away clean, and something crossed their face that I had no map for — guilt, maybe, or recognition, the look of a bookkeeper finding their own handwriting in a stranger's ledger. "The rest of you, he only counted."

"Same thing, eventually," Marrow said. "Counting becomes claiming. I worked for them. I know the arithmetic."

Ada looked down the pass road, where the agent had gone, and then at Ines, and then — this is the part I keep returning to — at their own hands, turning them over slowly, as if checking for other marks, other receipts, other handwriting they might have missed.

"Then we'd better move faster than arithmetic," Ada said.

And we shouldered our packs and went up into the pass, five remainders and a mark, and behind us the Ash Market smoked in its too-small valley, and somewhere in a clay pot on a merchant's cloth, a god's voice hummed on without anyone left alive who remembered how it sounded — except, I think, the pot itself.

Which is a sentence I wrote in my journal that night, and beneath it, because I am who I am, I drew a small map of the valley with one X on it, at the stall with the dark door.

Cartographers mark things we mean to come back for.
