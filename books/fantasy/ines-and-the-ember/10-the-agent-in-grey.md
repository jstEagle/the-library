## 10. The Agent in Grey

The pass narrowed that evening the way roads do when they've decided to stop being polite about it. Stone rose on either side of us like the pages of a closed book, and the sky above shrank to a grey ribbon with no stars in it — there were no stars anywhere now, of course, but up here the absence felt less like a theft and more like a held breath.

We made camp in a hollow where Sefa said the rock curved "kindly," which is a thing only a cartographer would say about stone. Brannic got a fire going from wood he'd been carrying since the Ash Market, which he refused to explain, and Ines sat with her back against her pack, the tin box on her knees, watching me the way she'd been watching me all day.

She hadn't spoken since we left the market. Not because of the memory I sold — she didn't know about that one, not precisely, though she knew I'd paid for something at the stall with the dark door. She was quiet because quiet was how she counted. I recognized it. It's how I count too.

"Say it," I told her, when the fire had settled. "Whatever it is you're chewing on. Say it or swallow it, but pick one."

"You looked sad," she said. "At the market. When you came out of that tent-thing. You looked sad, and then you pretended you didn't."

"I'm a guildkeeper. Pretending is most of the job."

"That's what my mother used to say." She turned the tin box over in her hands. "Before she stopped pretending anything at all."

Brannic, wisely, found something urgent to do with his boots. Sefa studied her maps by firelight as though the firelight might reveal something new about them. And I sat with an eleven-year-old's grief across the flames from me and understood, with the particular clarity that comes only when you're tired and hungry and have sold something irreplaceable that afternoon, that I had no honest answer for her.

So I gave her the next best thing.

"Yes," I said. "I was sad. I paid for your passage out of the market with something I can't get back, and I'd do it again tomorrow, and I resent both of those facts equally. That's the whole truth. Is it enough?"

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate reviewing a claim.

"It's more than anyone else ever gave me," she said finally. "Most people just say they're fine."

"Most people are liars with better posture."

And she laughed — one short, surprised bark of a laugh, gone almost before it arrived, but real. Brannic grinned at his boots. Sefa didn't look up from her maps, but I saw her pen pause, and cartographers mark things they mean to come back for.

That was the last good hour we had before the agent in grey found us.

* * *

He did not arrive. That's the thing I keep returning to, the detail I've written three different ways in my journal trying to make it behave. He did not arrive, because arrival implies movement, a before and after, a moment when he wasn't there followed by a moment when he was. One instant our camp contained four people and a fire, and the next it contained five, and the fifth had always been sitting on the flat rock at the edge of the light, hands folded on his knee, as if he'd been waiting for us to notice him since before we set out.

He wore grey. Not the grey of ash or storm or old wool — the grey of a ledger page, of an entry waiting to be filled in. His face was pleasant and unremarkable in the way that makes you certain you'll never be able to describe it afterward. He had a satchel on his lap, buckled shut, and he sat with the patience of someone whose employer does not experience time the way we do.

Sefa saw him first. She went very still, the way she does when a place starts moving under her feet, and said, quietly, "Ada."

"I see him."

"Do you? Because I'm not sure seeing is—"

"He sees us," Brannic rumbled, hand drifting toward the axe he no longer had the heart to use. "That's the part worrying me."

Ines moved. That's what I remember most clearly, later: not the agent, not the voice, but Ines moving between me and the stranger with the tin box clutched to her chest like a shield, small and furious and eleven years old, ready to fight whatever the world sent with nothing but herself and her brother's soul.

"No one's taking anything tonight," she said.

The agent inclined his head to her with genuine courtesy. "No one is taking anything tonight," he agreed. "I am here to offer a trade. Trades are freely entered. That is rather the point of them." His eyes — I want to say they were grey too, but honestly I couldn't swear he had eyes — came to rest on me. "Keeper Blackwood. Or should I say, former keeper. The Guild of Impossible Things has such interesting records. We've been auditing them for some time."

"We're not for sale," I said.

"Everything is for sale. That is not cynicism; it is bookkeeping." He unbuckled the satchel — slowly, deliberately, letting us watch — and drew out a single sheet of paper, cream-colored, heavy, official in the way that documents are official when the institution behind them has never once lost an argument. He laid it on the rock beside him. "But let us not waste the night on philosophy. My principals have authorized a settlement. You will find its terms generous."

"I don't make deals with people who don't have faces I can remember."

"A common complaint. Usually from debtors." He folded his hands again. "The terms, Keeper. Listen to them, at least. Your guild taught you that much, I assume — hear the whole contract before you sign or refuse. The girl walks free. Unmolested, uncollected, unrecorded. Her brother's soul remains where it is, which changes nothing for her; the dead are dead regardless of filing. In exchange, you deliver the ember willingly. No tricks, no substitutions, no clever amendments. Willingly. Freely. With a clear signature."

"And if I refuse?"

He spread his hands, and the gesture was so perfectly reasonable that it frightened me more than any threat could have.

"Then we proceed to collection. All of it. The girl, the ember, the debts of everyone in this company — including yours, Keeper, and yours is older than you know. Refusal simply moves the transaction from the voluntary column to the involuntary one. The totals are identical. Only the experience differs." He paused, and added, with what I can only describe as professional regret: "My principals prefer the voluntary column. There is less paperwork."

Behind me, Brannic shifted. Sefa's pen had stopped moving entirely. And Ines — Ines was looking at me over her shoulder, and her eyes said what her pride wouldn't: *don't.*

Not *save me.* Don't. She'd watched adults trade her brother away already. She knew exactly what a person was worth on paper, and she was daring me, silently, to prove the arithmetic wrong.

I stood up. Slowly, because my knees had opinions, and walked to the edge of the firelight until I stood close enough to the agent to smell him — and he smelled of nothing at all, which is somehow worse than brimstone. Cold paper. An absence where a smell should be.

"Here's what I know about contracts," I told him. "I've kept ledgers for a god of debts for longer than you've been filed. And the first rule — the rule underneath every other rule — is that a contract is only as good as the party signing it can afford to honor it. So before I refuse, I want to audit *your* principal. Who holds my god's silence? Who benefits?"

Something happened to the agent's face then. I hesitate to call it an expression. It was more like weather moving behind glass.

"You ask questions above your clearance," he said.

"I ask questions above everyone's clearance. It's why I'm bad at my job and good at staying alive."

"The god is not silent, Keeper." And here his voice changed — dropped its pleasantness entirely, and became what it had probably been all along: the flat tone of a clerk reading from a record. "The god was *taken*. Nine years ago. Chained beneath the mountain, in the deep stacks, because it committed the one offense my principals cannot forgive." He leaned forward slightly. "It forgave debts. Freely. Without collection. Without documentation. It tore entries out of the great ledger and burned them, and called it mercy, and worse — it taught others to do the same. Your guild exists because of that heresy. Every impossible thing you've ever returned, every debt you've quietly marked *paid in full* when it wasn't — you were doing its work. Do you understand now why the wards fell? Why the stars went out? This is not repossession, Keeper. This is *auditing*. The court beneath the mountain is coming to collect everything your god ever forgave — every mercy, every erased line, every soul set free without payment. Including hers." A nod toward Ines. "Including, I suspect, your own."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, wind moved through the pass like a long exhale.

Nine years. Nine years of prayers returned unopened, of silence, of wondering what I'd done wrong, of carrying a god's absence like a stone in the chest — and the answer was not abandonment. The answer was chains. Veth had not gone quiet. Veth had been *silenced*, locked in the dark beneath the world for the crime of being kind, and I had spent nine years ashamed of a prisoner.

I felt something in me shift — some load-bearing wall of guilt giving way, and something else taking its place. Not peace. Nothing so gentle. Purpose, maybe. Or fury wearing purpose's coat.

"You're lying," Ines said suddenly. Her voice shook. "You have to be lying. Gods don't get chained."

"Gods get chained constantly," the agent said, not unkindly. "It is practically their occupation. Yours was simply better at mercy than most, and mercy is expensive. Someone must pay for it. Eventually. Always." He picked up the contract from the rock and held it out to me. Cream paper. Blank signature line. Waiting. "So. The offer stands, and now you understand its true shape. Deliver the ember, and the girl goes free — free of *all* of it, the old claims included. Refuse, and the audit proceeds against every account your god ever touched. Think carefully, Keeper. You are not choosing for yourself alone. You never were."

And that was the trap in it, laid bare. Not the ember. Not Ines. The offer was built so that refusing meant condemning everyone Veth had ever helped — every debtor, every deserter, every soul the guild had quietly freed. Refuse, and I signed their names to the court's collections. Accept, and I signed away one girl's brother and proved, once and forever, that mercy loses to arithmetic.

Unless.

Unless the third option. The one the agent's neat columns didn't have room for. The one that had been forming in the back of my mind since he said *chained*, since he said *deep stacks*, since I understood that the court beneath the mountain was not a fortress but a *filing system* — and filing systems can be amended.

I took the contract from his hands. He almost smiled.

Then I tore it in half, and quarters, and eighths, and let the pieces fall into the fire, where they burned green and smelled, briefly, of cold iron.

"My counteroffer," I said, "is that we're coming down anyway. All the way down. And we're not delivering anything. We're collecting. A god, a brother, and every entry your masters ever stole. Consider it a repossession notice. The Guild of Impossible Things is calling in its oldest debt."

For the first time, the agent moved as though time applied to him. He stood, brushed ash from his grey sleeve, and regarded me with something that in a man I would have called respect.

"So be it," he said. "Collection it is. I am required to inform you that the deep stacks do not grant audiences. They grant *outcomes*." He buckled his empty satchel. "And Keeper — one courtesy, since you've been courteous. The god you seek remembers you. Even now. Even chained. Especially chained. Prisoners have very little else to do." 

He stepped backward into the dark beyond the firelight and was not there anymore, and had perhaps never been, and the space where he'd sat looked like any other rock.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Brannic exhaled like a man setting down a weight.

"Well," he said. "That went well."

"We're going to rescue a god," Sefa said faintly. She'd begun drawing already, fast, her pen scratching — the mountain, the pass, the deep stacks she couldn't possibly map yet, mapping anyway, because that's what she did with fear. "We're four people and a child going to rescue a chained god from a bureaucracy of the dead."

"Five," Ines corrected.

Everyone looked at her.

She stood there with the tin box hugged to her chest, chin up, firelight in her eyes, and she was not asking permission and she was not offering herself as payment and she was not afraid — or rather, she was terrified, and had decided terror was simply another item to carry.

"Five," she said again. "My brother's in there too. And I'm the one holding the compass."

* * *

We broke camp within the hour. Nobody suggested sleeping; nobody could have. We walked up through the pass under a starless sky, five figures on a road that remembered us whether we wanted it to or not, and I brought up the rear, as I always do, counting heads.

Four remainders and a mark, I wrote in my journal, by the light of Brannic's stubborn fire-reflection on the snow. And beneath it:

*Veth did not go silent. Veth was taken. Nine years of unanswered prayers were not neglect — they were a kidnapping, and the kidnappers file paperwork.*

*I sold a memory of your voice to buy a child's passage, and I thought that was the worst bargain I'd ever make. I was wrong. The worst one is still ahead, and I intend to win it.*

*Hold on. Whoever's listening down there in the dark — hold on. We're coming, and we're bringing the whole guild's worth of trouble with us.*

Cartographers mark things we mean to come back for.

So do gods, I think. Even chained ones. Especially chained ones.

And somewhere beneath the mountain, in the deep stacks where souls hum on their shelves, I would have sworn — then and now — that something heard me. Something patient. Something that had been counting the days too.

The road bent upward. The mountain waited, patient as a creditor, and we walked into its shadow with our names still ours and our debts unpaid, five remainders and a mark, going to collect.
