## 11. Brannic's Last Good Night

I've been dying for sixty years, and I'll tell you the secret of it: it's mostly bookkeeping.

The Guild of Impossible Things holds my death in trust. Wrote it down the night I signed on, back when Ada was still learning which end of a ledger to open. *One death, Old Brannic, payable on demand.* And every year since, when the auditors came round, I'd look them in the eye and say, "Not yet. Market's bad. Ask again next spring." Sixty springs of asking. You'd think they'd have stopped by now. You'd think *I'd* have stopped.

But here's what nobody tells you about postponing a thing forever: eventually you get good at it. Better than good. I got so good at not dying that not-dying became its own kind of work, and like all work, it wore grooves into me. I woke up each morning and checked that I was still owed, the way a farmer checks his fences. Still there. Still mine. Still waiting.

Then Sefa spread her maps across the debtors' table and showed us the pass through the Grayteeth, and I felt something in my chest that I hadn't felt since my wife was alive: relief.

Because the pass is cursed. Everyone knows it's cursed. The curse takes one life from every company that crosses, and it doesn't take just anyone—it takes the one who's most ready. It sniffs you out like a hound smells fear, except what it smells is *willingness*. And I have been marinating in willingness for six decades.

"You're smiling," Ada said. They always notice. It's their curse as much as mine.

"I'm hungry," I said. "There's a difference."

"There isn't," Ines said, eleven years old and sharper than any blade we carried. She looked at me with those eyes that had already buried too much. "You're going to do something stupid."

"Child," I said, "everything I've done for sixty years has been stupid. This will be the first smart thing."

* * *

We made camp at the mouth of the pass that evening, because even a man planning to die wants a decent meal first. There's an art to a last supper, and I am nothing if not an artist of postponements finally coming due.

I'd been carrying provisions for this exact night for longer than any of them knew. Salted boar, aged three years in honey and juniper—the kind of meat you save for weddings or funerals, and I'd never been sure which mine would be. A bottle of plum brandy from a valley that doesn't exist anymore; Sefa mapped it once, then watched it move away without her. Flatbread I'd baked myself, because if you want a thing done with love you do it yourself, and if you want it done with *apology*, you do it yourself twice.

Ada watched me lay it all out and went very still, the way they do when they're reading a ledger only they can see.

"Brannic," they said carefully, "what is this?"

"Dinner," I said. "Sit down before it gets cold. That's not an impossible thing, but it's close, out here."

Ines sat first, because children are honest about food. Then Sefa, who never needs telling twice where a meal is concerned. Marrow drifted down from wherever ghosts go when they're pretending not to listen—he can't eat, but he likes to hover over the brandy and complain about how he can remember the taste and not the warmth. Ada sat last, and they sat like someone sitting at a bedside.

"Tell me what you're doing," Ada said.

"I'm cooking," I said. "You used to be better at letting people do things."

* * *

Here's what I know about Ada Blackwood that they'd deny under oath: they are the kindest person I have ever met, and they have built an entire life around making sure no one finds out. The guild's whole doctrine—stay invisible, stay unaccountable, stay safe—was never cowardice. It was Ada deciding that the world could only be helped by people who couldn't be hurt, and then discovering that the two are the same thing, and choosing help anyway, quietly, in the margins, where no one writes it down.

Except Veth wrote it down. Veth wrote everything down. That's the joke of it—a god of debts who forgave them all, keeping perfect books of mercies nobody ever repaid.

I signed my contract with the guild sixty years ago because I owed a debt I couldn't pay and didn't deserve to survive. Ada took me in anyway. Never asked why. Just opened the ledger, wrote my name, and said, "The guild deals in impossible things. Staying alive when you shouldn't be—that counts."

So tonight, over honeyed boar and brandy older than the girl across the fire, I told them the why. All of it. The village I failed to defend. The people who died because I took coin from both sides and thought I could serve neither well enough to matter. The name I stopped using. The reason "Old Brannic" is old—because the man who owned that name died in that village, and I've been wearing his ghost ever since.

Nobody interrupted. That's the guild way—we're collectors of things other people won't hold, and shame is heavy cargo.

When I finished, Ines reached across the fire and put her hand on my knee. Eleven years old. Hand like a sparrow's.

"That's not why you're doing it," she said. "Dying tomorrow. You're doing it because of us."

Smart girl. Terrifyingly smart girl.

"I'm doing it because I'm owed a death," I said, "and I'd rather spend it on something than have it spent on me. There's a difference between paying and being collected from. Your brother taught me that—" I caught myself. "The ember. When it flares near Ada. Things that were loved don't stop teaching, they just change classrooms."

Marrow laughed from his corner, a sound like wind through a keyhole. "He's rehearsed that speech. I've heard him practicing to the mules."

"I have not," I said.

"He has," Sefa confirmed. "Weeks. He does voices."

"I do *one* voice," I said, "and it's the voice of a man finishing his business, which is more than most of you can claim."

And then, because it was true and because the fire was warm and because a man should say true things on his last night, I said: "Sixty years I kept that death in my pocket like a stone, checking it was still there. Tonight I'm setting it down. Not because I'm brave. Because I finally found something worth the weight."

* * *

Ada walked with me after the others slept. We didn't go far—the pass mouth glowed faintly ahead, hungry patient—but far enough that the fire was an orange memory behind us.

"You could still refuse," Ada said. "We find another way. Sefa says there's a southern route, weeks out of our way, dangerous but survivable—"

"Sefa also says there's a sea that moves," I said. "She's usually right, but right isn't the same as timely."

"The guild owes you—"

"The guild owes me nothing." I turned and looked at them properly. Young face, old eyes. My favorite contradiction. "That's the whole point of you, Ada. You keep books of what's owed, and you've never once collected from me. Sixty years of grace. Do you know what I did with it? I hoarded it. Like a miser. Grace is meant to be *spent*."

They were quiet a long time. Stars—or the holes where stars used to be—wheeled overhead, dark and wrong.

"When I was young," Ada said finally, "I prayed to Veth to make me useful. I didn't ask for anything else. No happiness, no safety. Just useful." They laughed, short and bitter. "I think the god took me literally."

"It's a literal god," I said. "Best kind. Listen—I need you to do something for me, and it's harder than fighting."

"Name it."

"Don't turn this into a debt." I held up a hand before they could argue. "I know you. I know the ledger-brain. Tomorrow, when it happens, some part of you is going to start calculating what you owe me, how to repay a dead man, how to carry it. Don't. What I give tomorrow is a gift. Gifts aren't entries. They're just... given. Promise me you'll let it be that."

Ada's jaw worked. I watched them try to fit my words into their categories and fail, and it was the best thing I saw all night, watching a keeper of ledgers meet something that wouldn't file.

"I promise," they said at last, and their voice cracked on it, and I pretended not to notice because that's what family does.

* * *

Morning came grey and cold, the way mornings do when they know what's expected of them.

I ate a hard breakfast, checked my boots, and gave away my possessions like a man dealing cards. Knife to Ines—"It's older than your mother, probably, and meaner than you, which is saying something." Maps to Sefa. The rest of the brandy to Marrow, who couldn't drink it but could hold the bottle, and who cradled it like a relic. To Ada, nothing, because I'd already given them everything the night before, and because some gifts you deliver in person.

At the pass mouth, the curse was waiting. I could feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound—not pain exactly, but *attention*. It knew. Gods below, it knew, and it was glad.

"One condition," I said to the darkness between the peaks. "One life, freely offered, and everyone else walks through untouched. That's the toll. Take it or leave it."

The pass said nothing, but the air changed, the way a room changes when a contract is signed.

I turned to the others. Ines was crying, furious about it, wiping her face with her sleeve like the tears were misfiling themselves. Sefa had her maps clutched to her chest. Marrow stood closer to solid than I'd ever seen him. And Ada—

Ada looked at me the way you look at a door closing.

"Brannic," they started, "the ledger—"

"Forget the ledger." I grinned at them, and it felt good, grinning, like stretching muscles long unused. "For one morning, be a man with friends instead of a clerk with accounts. Can you do that? For me?"

A breath. Two.

"Yes," Ada said.

"Then watch me walk," I said, "and remember it badly, and tell it wrong at parties. Make me taller. Give me a better beard. That's all the immortality I ever wanted—to be a story improved in the retelling."

I stepped toward the pass, and the dark leaned in to meet me like an old dance partner, and I thought: *sixty years of springtimes, and every one of them was borrowed, and every borrower knows the day the loan comes due, and the trick—the only trick—is to leave something behind that keeps earning interest.*

Behind me, small and fierce, Ines shouted my name. Not "Old Brannic." My real one. The dead man's name, the village's name, the one I'd buried.

She'd gotten it out of Ada somehow. Clever girl. Terrible, wonderful girl.

I didn't turn around. If I turned around, I'd have to see their faces, and I'd just finished telling Ada that gifts aren't entries, and I meant to die owing nothing.

But I raised one hand, back to them, and waved.

The pass closed around me like a book.

And somewhere behind, faint but unmistakable, I heard the ember flare—warm, brief, bright—and I understood, walking into whatever comes next, that things which were loved don't go out.

They just change classrooms.
