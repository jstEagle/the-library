## 12. Under the Mountain's Skin

The mountain had a wound on its eastern side, Sefa said, and she said it the way you'd tell someone a bathhouse was closed for repairs. Casual. Practical. Like the fact that a mountain could be wounded at all was just another entry in her maps.

"It's not a wound," Brannic grumbled, adjusting his pack for the fourth time. "Mountains don't get wounds. Mountains get caves."

"Caves are how mountains *heal*," Sefa said. "This is different. Look."

I looked. I'd been looking at it since we crested the ridge an hour ago, and I still couldn't decide if I wanted to see it or not. The rock face split open in a long diagonal gash, maybe forty feet of it, edges smooth as cut meat. No rubble at its base. No scree. Just a dark seam in the world with warm air breathing out of it, slow and steady, like something enormous asleep.

"Does it breathe?" I asked.

"Everything breathes," Ada said. They were crouched at the edge of the gash, one hand flat against the stone, head tilted like they were listening to it. They did that a lot lately. Listened to things that weren't talking. "The question is what it's breathing out."

I knew the answer before anyone said it. The tin box in my satchel had gone warm against my hip about a mile back, and it hadn't cooled since. The ember knew where home was. It was pulling.

I put my hand over the satchel and felt it: a faint tug, patient and constant, like a fish on a line that never tired. Tammy. My brother. Or the piece of him that was left after Mother signed him away — and I have to be fair here, even now, even furious, because being unfair to the dead is a luxury I can't afford anymore. She didn't sign him away because she didn't love us. She signed him away because the court beneath the mountain sent a man in grey with papers that made everything sound reasonable, and when you're starving and your daughter is eleven and your son is coughing blood into a rag, reasonable is the most dangerous word there is.

*Collateral,* the papers had said. *A loan against future prosperity.* And then she died anyway, and the court kept the collateral, because courts always keep the collateral.

"Ines." Ada's voice, close. I hadn't heard them come over. They did that too — moved quiet as a debt collector. "Last chance to say no. Once we're inside, the road doesn't run backward."

"You said that about the memory road," I said. "And the bridge. And the ash market. You say it every time like you're giving me a choice, but we both know I'm going in."

Ada's mouth did the thing it does — not quite a smile, more like a door opening a crack in a house everyone thought was empty. "Yes. But I'll keep saying it. That's the arrangement."

"What arrangement?"

"The one where I treat you like a person instead of a parcel." They stood, brushed stone dust off their knees. "Brannic. Point."

Old Brannic unslung his axe and grinned his graveyard grin, the one with too many teeth in it for a man who owed the guild his own death and kept postponing the payment. "Point," he said happily, and walked into the mountain.

* * *

Inside, it was warm. Not summer-warm. Blood-warm. The air came at you thick and slow, and the walls of the tunnel were slick in places, and the light — well. There wasn't any, at first. Then there was, and I wished there hadn't been.

The souls were shelved.

That's the only way I can say it. Imagine the biggest pantry you've ever seen, and then imagine it goes on forever, and instead of jars there are people — or the shapes of people, folded small, glowing faintly, each one sitting in its own niche carved into the living rock. Rows and rows of them, up past where the light reached, down below where the floor should be. And they hummed. All of them. A low note each, slightly different, and all those notes together made a sound like a hive the size of a country.

Sefa stopped walking so fast she nearly went over. Brannic raised his axe at a shelf, which is the most Brannic thing I've ever seen, because what was he going to do? Chop a soul?

"Don't touch them," Ada said quietly. "Don't speak to them. Don't look for faces."

"Why not?" I whispered.

"Because some of them will look back. And once one of them knows you've seen them, you'll hear their note in your sleep until you come back for them. The court counts on that. It's how they get volunteers."

I looked anyway. Of course I looked. I'm eleven, not a saint.

Most of the shapes were just shapes — dim, curled, unfinished-looking, like bread dough someone had abandoned mid-rise. But one, three rows up, had a face. An old woman's face, eyes closed, mouth moving. Not humming. Counting. Her lips went *one, two, three, four* over and over, and I understood suddenly that she'd been counting since before my mother was born, and that somewhere above us the stars were still gone, and that this was why. This whole mountain was full of the light they'd taken.

My chest did something complicated. I made myself look away.

The ember pulled harder. Left at the next fork. The tin box was almost hot now, and when I pressed my palm against the satchel I could feel it *ticking*, quick little pulses, faster than a heart should go.

"Tammy's close," I said.

Ada glanced at me. "How close?"

"How should I know? Ask him." I pulled the box out before I could think better of it. Bad idea. Terrible idea. The moment the lid cracked, the humming changed.

It didn't stop. It *shifted*. All those thousands of notes bent toward us at once, the way grass bends under wind, and out of the mass of sound rose one note that was higher and clearer and shaking, and the ember flared in its box with a light like a struck match, and every hair on my arms stood up.

"He hears me," I said. My voice came out small. "He knows I'm here."

"Close it," Ada said, sharp, and I'd never heard them sharp at me before, and I flinched, and they saw me flinch, and something crossed their face like regret moving fast behind glass. "Ines. Close it gently. If the court hears that note rise, they'll know exactly where we are and exactly what we're carrying."

I closed the lid. Gently. The ember's glow bled through the seams anyway, a thin gold line, stubborn.

We walked. The shelves went on. Somewhere in the dark behind us, the old woman counted on.

* * *

We camped in a side pocket of the tunnel where the shelving stopped for a stretch — a bare place, cold relative to everything else, which is to say it felt like a cellar instead of a body. Sefa drew a map by touch, marking our route in her strange moving-ink way, muttering that the tunnels had shifted twice since her last survey and that the mountain was "digesting." Nobody asked her to explain that. Nobody wanted her to explain that.

Brannic took first watch, which meant he sat against the wall with his axe across his knees and told a story about a bear he'd once fought in a snowstorm, except by the end of it the bear was his business partner and the snowstorm was a wedding, and none of us had the heart to point out the story had gotten away from him.

I couldn't sleep. The humming came through the stone, muffled but everywhere, like sleeping inside a bell that someone was always about to strike.

So I got up, and I found Ada sitting apart, sharpening a knife they never seemed to use, and I sat down next to them without asking, because that's what family does, and I immediately ruined it by saying:

"You're going to trade me."

Ada's knife stopped.

"I've been thinking about it," I went on, because apparently I'm the kind of person who keeps talking when she should stop, "the whole time. Since the guild. Everyone keeps looking at me like I'm the payment. Marrow wouldn't come, fine, ghosts are ghosts. But Sefa looks at me and does sums. And Brannic looks at me like he's sorry already. And you—" My voice cracked, which I hated, so I pushed through it meaner. "You look at me like a ledger entry. *One soul, minor, pre-owned, damaged.* And I know how this ends. We get down there, and the court says somebody has to pay, and everybody looks at the cheapest thing in the room, and that's me. That's always been me. Mother proved it. So don't—"

"Don't what?" Ada said softly.

"Don't pretend you're different from her."

The silence after that was long enough to count things in. I counted the pulses of the ember through the tin. Forty-one. Forty-two. Ada set the knife down, careful, the way you set down something that could go off.

"I'm going to tell you something true," they said, "and you can believe it or not, and I'll survive either one, but I'd rather you believed it."

"Fine."

"I have spent my whole life making sure nobody could ever be traded away on my watch. Do you know why?" They turned and looked at me straight, and in the ember-light leaking from my satchel their face looked older than I ever let myself see. "Because someone once looked at *me* the way you just described. Like a solution to a problem. Cheap. Portable. Pre-owned." A breath. "I broke every rule I have to get you this far, Ines. Breaking rules is expensive. I intend to keep paying."

"That's what a person would say," I said, "right before they traded me."

"Yes," Ada agreed. "It is. And I can't prove otherwise with words. Words are the court's currency; I won't counterfeit them." They reached over, slowly, giving me time to pull away, and tapped one finger against the tin box. Through the metal, the ember pulsed — and pulsed *back*, quick and bright, like a knock answered. "But that in there is your brother, and your brother has been listening to every word we've said since we entered this mountain. So ask him. He knew Mother's handwriting. He'll know mine by morning. If I ever move to trade you, he'll flare before I finish the sentence, and you'll have warning no contract can forge."

I stared at the box. The gold seam glowed steady. Patient. *Him.*

"That's the dumbest safeguard I've ever heard," I said.

"Probably."

"It relies on you telling the truth in front of a witness who can't talk."

"Yes."

"And it means I have to trust a dead boy's opinion of your handwriting."

"Welcome to the Guild of Impossible Things," Ada said. "Our standards are exactly that high."

And I laughed. It came out wet and ugly and surprised, a laugh with a sob hiding inside it wearing a laugh's coat, but it was real. Ada watched me laugh with that door-crack expression, and for one whole moment — one — I believed them completely.

Then the humming changed again.

Not shifted this time. *Stopped.* All of it, all at once, ten thousand notes cut silent like a choir told to hush. The silence rushed down the tunnel toward us, and in it I could hear footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. Paper-dry.

Brannic was on his feet with his axe before I finished standing. Sefa blew out her map-light. Ada's hand found my shoulder and pressed, once: *behind me.*

Out of the black came a smell like ink and cold iron, and a voice polite as a filing cabinet:

"Visitors. How unexpected. The Auditor will see the child now."
