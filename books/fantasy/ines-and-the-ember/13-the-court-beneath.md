## 13. The Court Beneath

There is a particular smell to paperwork that has outlived its owners. I'd always assumed it was dust. Standing in the antechamber of the court beneath the mountain, holding a summons that had been waiting for me since before I was born, I learned it is something else entirely. It is the smell of patience.

The tunnels behind us had been lined with souls the way a merchant's cellar is lined with wine—shelved, labeled, humming faintly in keys that made my teeth ache. Ines had walked the whole way with her hand pressed flat against the tin box, as if she could feel her brother through the metal. Maybe she could. The ember pulled at us all by then, a warm thread through the cold, and we followed it down and down until the tunnel opened without warning into a hall so vast that Sefa's breath left her in a whistle.

"By every map I never drew," she murmured.

The court beneath the mountain was not what the stories promised. No throne of bone. No howling tyrant. Instead: desks. Thousands of them, arranged in terraces descending into a green-lit gloom, each one occupied by a clerk who did not look up when we entered. Quills scratched. Stamps thudded. Somewhere far below, a bell rang the hour, though no hour had passed down here since the stars went out. The light came from nowhere and everywhere, the color of pond ice, and it made everyone's face look like something already filed away.

"It's an office," Ines said, with the profound disgust only a child can muster for bureaucracy. "It's just an *office*."

"The worst kind," I said. "An efficient one."

Marrow drifted ahead of us, translucent and uneasy, which for Marrow was saying something. He had worked here once, before he deserted, before the guild gave him a second death to look forward to instead of a first. He kept his hands in his coat pockets like a man walking past the house of an old lover.

"They'll have a record of me," he said quietly. "Desertion. It's still open on their books."

"Then stay behind me," I said, "and don't sign anything."

"That's your whole plan, isn't it. Don't sign anything."

"It's served me well."

"You're literally the keeper of ledgers, Ada."

"A person can hold contradictions," I said, "if they carry them carefully."

* * *

We did not get ten steps across the marble before a bell rang—not the hour-bell but a smaller, sharper thing—and a clerk detached from the terraces like a leaf letting go of a branch. It was tall, grey-robed, and faceless in the way that fog is faceless: nothing missing, just nothing there. When it spoke, its voice was the sound of a stamp coming down on paper.

"Visitors. Unregistered." A pause, during which I felt myself being read. Not looked at—*read*, cover to spine. "State your business."

I had rehearsed this. I had rehearsed this on the road, in the ash market, in Brannic's memory while we burned his body according to his instructions, which had included jokes. But rehearsal is a coin that spends differently depending on whose hand holds it, and mine were sweating.

"We've come to file a claim," I said.

The clerk tilted its head. "A claim of what nature?"

"Inheritance," I said. "A soul held in trust against a debt improperly contracted. The debtor was a minor at the time of signature. The contract is voidable under the Second Principle of Consent." I recited it the way you recite a prayer you no longer believe but cannot forget. "We are representatives of the claimant."

The clerk produced, from somewhere in its sleeve, a ledger. Of course it did. Everything down here was a ledger; I felt almost at home, which frightened me more than any monster would have.

"Representatives require credentials," it said. "Certified standing. Seal of a recognized order."

And there it was. The wall between us and everything we wanted, built not of stone or warding but of paperwork.

"I see," I said pleasantly. "And where does one obtain certification?"

"From the Office of Recognized Orders," said the clerk. "Sub-basement nine. Take a number."

It handed me a number. It was 4,417. I looked back at my companions. Sefa was studying the terraces with a cartographer's hunger, memorizing exits. Marrow was trying very hard to be less visible than a ghost can manage, which is to say not very. Ines was glaring at the clerk with an expression I recognized from mirrors—the look of someone who has been told the world works a certain way and has decided, on insufficient evidence, to disagree.

"Thank you," I said, and turned us all around, and walked us back toward the shadows of the entrance tunnel, and waited until the clerk's attention had returned to its desk.

Then I took out my kit.

Every guild member carries one. Ours is small: needles, inks ground from things that shouldn't exist, a mirror that shows what a document *wants* to be rather than what it is, and a book of seals—impressions only, taken over decades from every institution foolish enough to let a guild auditor near their correspondence. The Guild of Impossible Things has spent two hundred years being invisible, and invisibility, it turns out, is mostly stationery.

"What are you doing?" Ines whispered.

"Forging our credentials," I said.

Her eyes went wide, then narrow. "You said the guild doesn't break rules."

"The guild doesn't break rules," I agreed, wetting a needle in ink the color of midnight. "But the guild also says a contract signed by a child is no contract at all, and the court down here disagrees, so we are going to have a disagreement about whose rules count. I'm choosing ours." I glanced at her. "You can object."

She thought about it with her whole face, the way she did everything. "No," she decided. "Forge them good."

"I always do."

* * *

Here is the thing they don't tell you about forgery, the thing that makes it a sin rather than a trick: it requires love. You must understand the document you're copying better than its makers did. You must know why the seal sits where it sits, why the ink is this shade and not that, why the phrasing bends toward mercy in clause four and away from it in clause seven. To forge a thing is to study it until you could have made it yourself—and then make it anyway, for purposes its makers would have hated.

I forged us into an order that has never existed. The Brotherhood of the Quiet Reckoning—a name I invented on the spot, drawing on the court's own aesthetic: quiet, grey, fond of reckoning. Their credentials declared us certified advocates, empowered to represent claimants in matters of soul-custody, bearing the seal of recognition of the Office of Recognized Orders itself. Forging a thing with its own seal is either the deepest insult or the highest flattery; I've never decided which.

Sefa watched me work with her arms crossed. "You're good at this," she said. There was something in her voice I didn't like. Not accusation. Something worse. Recognition.

"I am."

"How long?"

"Since before you joined." I blew the ink dry. "The guild didn't start as keepers of impossible things, Sefa. It started as thieves of them. We laundered our conscience later, and called the laundering a philosophy."

"And now?"

"Now I'm using the old skills for the new philosophy, and I can't tell you honestly whether that makes it better or just makes me a thief with better taste in causes." I held up the credentials. They gleamed. They were perfect. I hated them a little. "Ask me again after we get her brother back."

* * *

The clerk received our papers the way a priest receives an offering: without surprise, without warmth, with total attention. It read the credentials top to bottom. It turned them over. It held them near its blank face, and I stood there feeling the specific terror of the forger watching their work examined—which is the same terror as the liar watching someone pray, because both of you know exactly what the truth looks like and how far away you're standing from it.

"Advocates of the Quiet Reckoning," it said at last. "Your order is... recent."

"Founded in response to the current crisis," I said smoothly. "The collapse of the wards has generated considerable claims volume. We were chartered to assist."

"Chartered by whom?"

"The Office of Recognized Orders."

Something happened in the fog of its face. I want to believe it was suspicion. I choose to believe it was the bureaucratic equivalent of a headache—the pain of a system encountering its own recursion. An office certifying an order that claims certification by that office. Down here, where everything is filed, perhaps even paradox has a drawer.

"Take a seat," it said. "Your claim will be audited."

Audited. The word went through me like cold water through cloth. Because I knew what auditing meant down here. I had seen the shelves in the tunnels. Souls, filed. Audited. And forgotten—that was the third step, the one nobody mentioned, the one the hum in the walls was made of. Every soul on those shelves had once been somebody's brother. Somebody's Ines. And the court had processed them so thoroughly that even the grief had been archived.

We sat. We waited. Ines gripped the tin box. Marrow counted the clerks under his breath—an old collector's habit—and stopped counting somewhere past three hundred. Sefa drew maps on the back of her hand in ink that faded as she drew, mapping a room that didn't want to be mapped.

Hours passed, or what passes for hours when no hour has passed since the stars went out. And in that waiting, I had time to notice the thing I should have noticed immediately.

The clerks never stopped working. Not one of them. Not to eat, not to rest, not to look at us. And yet the shelves in the tunnels were full, and the terraces were full, and the whole vast machine of the court ran without pause—and I thought about Veth, silent these long years, and about what the agent in grey had told us on the road. Chained. Its voice stolen into the ledgers.

A god of debts and mercy, chained beneath a bureaucracy. And every prayer ever prayed to Veth, every plea for forgiveness, every whispered *please let me owe a little less*—where did those go, if the god couldn't answer? Into the ledgers. Filed. Audited. Forgotten. The silence we'd mourned wasn't absence. It was backlog.

"Ada." Ines tugged my sleeve. Her voice was very small. "The ember's doing something."

I looked. Through the tin, through her fingers, a glow pulsed—steady, rhythmic, like a heartbeat keeping time with something. And then I heard it, faint beneath the scratching of ten thousand quills: a sound like a voice trying to remember how to be a voice. Coming from everywhere. From the ledgers themselves.

The bell above the nearest terrace rang sharply. Every clerk in the hall turned its head toward us at once.

"The audit will see you now," said our clerk.

And from the depths of the court, from a door taller than any cathedral's, came a second sound—low, shackled, straining—and Ines gasped and clutched the box to her chest, because she knew it too.

It was a voice. It was *the* voice. And it was saying, in the tone of a god who has spent years composing a single sentence:

"*Bring me the ledger-keeper.*"

Every faceless head swiveled to me.

Well. I thought, standing, smoothing my forged robes. So much for infiltration.
