## 14. Audience with the Auditor

*— Ada*

The court beneath the mountain did not look like a place where souls were kept. It looked like a tax office designed by someone who hated light but loved paperwork.

Corridors of black stone, lit by lamps that burned without flame — I recognized that trick, and it made my stomach turn, because it was Veth's trick, Veth's old quiet fire, stolen and strung up like trophies. Clerks moved through the halls in grey robes, faceless not because they had no faces but because I could not, no matter how hard I looked, remember any detail of one. Their footsteps made no sound on the stone. The only noise in the entire court was the sound of pages turning, thousands of them, everywhere at once, like a forest of paper breathing.

Marrow walked ahead of us with his chin up and his hands visible, which was the posture of a man who had worked here once and knew exactly how the dogs were trained. "Don't touch anything," he murmured. "Don't sign anything. Don't say *thank you*. Gratitude is considered an acknowledgment of debt."

"What do we say?" Ines whispered.

"Nothing. We present our claim and we wait."

"I'm good at waiting," Brannic said. "I've been postponing my death for forty years. Waiting is practically my profession."

Sefa said nothing at all, which frightened me more than anything else, because Sefa always had a map for everything, and I could see her eyes moving over the corridors, cataloguing turns, measuring distances, building us an escape route she clearly did not believe we'd live to use.

They took us to a chamber called, according to the brass plate on the door, *Audience Hall Seven*, which told you everything about how many audience halls there were. Inside: a desk. A chair behind the desk. And behind the chair, a figure.

I want to describe the Auditor. I have tried, since, many times, and every attempt comes out wrong. Tall, I think. Or perhaps simply *present*, the way a wall is present. It wore grey the way the clerks wore grey, but better, the way a master's signature looks next to a copyist's. Its face —

Its face was polite. That is the only word I have ever found. Not blank, not hidden. *Polite.* The way a locked door is polite.

"Good evening," said the Auditor, though there was no evening down here; there was no anything down here. "You are here regarding claim number—" it consulted a page that had not existed a moment before, "—eleven thousand four hundred and six. The matter of one soul, male, aged fourteen at time of sale, sold by one Mirabel Voss, deceased, to the Court Beneath, payment pending. Accompanied by," its polite face turned toward Ines, "a residual fragment. How interesting. Fragments rarely walk in on their own feet."

"It's my brother," Ines said. Her voice didn't shake. I was proud of her and terrified for her in equal measure, which I was learning was simply what it felt like to love someone. "His name is Tomas. He's eleven now. He'd be eleven, if you'd let him be anywhere."

"Tomas Voss," the Auditor repeated, and somewhere in the walls, a page turned. "Yes. Here he is." It laid a ledger open on the desk — a real ledger, leather-bound, and I hated that even down here, even in this place, they used ledgers. It meant they could be beaten with their own tools. "Sold nine years ago. Collateral against a debt incurred by the mother. The mother is deceased. The debt has therefore passed to — " another page turned somewhere, " — the surviving sibling. Who is present. Who is eleven years old. Who is carrying the collateral in a tin box."

"The contract says," Ines said, stepping forward, and I saw Marrow's ghostly hand twitch toward her shoulder and stop, "that the soul returns when the debt is paid. My mother paid. She paid for nine years. Every coin she ever earned went into that debt until the day she died, and then I finished it. I have the receipts."

She set them on the desk. Nine years of receipts, folded and refolded until they were soft as cloth, tied with string, carried across a country where the stars had gone out. The Auditor looked at them for a long time.

"These are valid," it said at last.

The word landed in the room like a dropped coin. Brannic exhaled. Sefa's shoulders came down half an inch.

"The debt is discharged," the Auditor continued. "The claim is just. The Court acknowledges full payment received and hereby releases all claim upon the soul of Tomas Voss."

Ines made a sound — small, involuntary, the sound of a girl who had been holding her breath for three years.

"You may collect him," said the Auditor, "upon payment of the processing fee."

And there it was. There it always was.

* * *

*— Ines*

I had imagined this moment since I was eight years old. I had imagined it angry and I had imagined it tearful and once, memorably, I had imagined it with fireworks, because I was seven when I started imagining and seven-year-olds are allowed fireworks.

I had never imagined the fee.

"What fee," Ada said. Their voice was very flat, the way it got when they were most dangerous. I'd learned that on the road. Flat voice, still hands. The flatter the voice, the closer to the knife.

"A soul of equal weight," said the Auditor, pleasant as a shopkeeper discussing the weather. "Standard practice. Souls may not leave the Court unbalanced. One enters the ledger, one must exit it. Your brother's entry will be struck; a new entry must be made in its place. Equivalent mass. Equivalent — " the polite face tilted, considering, " — warmth."

"That's not in the contract," Ada said.

"The contract governs the debt. The fee governs the release. Two documents. I'm surprised a guildsman needs this explained."

I watched Ada's hands. They had gone white around the strap of their pack. Behind me, Marrow drifted forward, and I heard him say, low, "There's no arguing this part. I processed fees for sixty years. There's no arguing."

"There's always arguing," Ada said. "Arguing is the guild's whole religion."

"Not here."

"Then we'll found a branch office."

But even as they said it, I could see the arithmetic happening behind their eyes, the same arithmetic happening behind everyone's eyes, and nobody saying it out loud, because saying it out loud would make it true:

Someone had to stay.

A soul of equal weight. Tomas weighed — well, whatever an eleven-year-old boy's soul weighs, which is apparently quite a lot, because the Auditor had checked and pronounced the market rate. Someone in this room had to sit down in that chair and become a line in a ledger so that my brother could walk out into the starless night and breathe.

Ada wouldn't let it be me. I knew that already, the way you know weather coming. Ada had been building toward standing between me and things since the night they first opened their door and pretended to be annoyed about it. Sefa would offer, because Sefa was kind in that quiet cartographer's way, mapping exits for other people she never took herself. Brannic would offer, because Brannic had been trying to pay off his death for forty years and might see this as a convenient counter.

And none of it was fair. None of it was *right*. It was my brother. My mother's debt. My receipts, my string, my tin box, my ember burning warm against my ribs like a second heart.

So before anyone could arrange themselves into nobility — before Ada could take that breath they took, the one that meant *stand behind me* — I stepped forward, put both hands flat on the Auditor's desk, and said:

"I'll stay."

* * *

*— Sefa*

I have mapped places that move. I have charted a coastline that rearranged itself nightly out of spite, a city that migrated south every winter like a bird, a river that flowed uphill on Sundays. I thought I understood, professionally, that the world does not care what is fair. Maps taught me that early. Fairness is not a feature of terrain.

And still, when the child said it — when Ines Voss, eleven years old, ninety pounds soaking wet, laid her hands on that impossible desk and offered herself up like a coin — something in me broke along a line I hadn't known I was carrying.

"No," said Ada.

"Yes," said Ines.

"No," said Ada again, louder, and the clerks in the corridor outside paused at the sound, because I suspect no one had raised their voice in this hall in a century. "Absolutely not. Out of the question. Sit down."

"You can't stop me. It's my claim. Marrow said — Marrow, tell them, the claimant sets the terms of collection—"

"The claimant doesn't set the *fee*," Marrow said, wretched. He looked, for the first time since I'd known him, genuinely pained, which for a ghost is saying something. "Child, listen to me. I worked this desk. I stamped these releases. Do you know what happens to souls filed here?"

"They get forgotten," Ines said. Her chin was up. Her voice only shook a little. "I know. But people forget things all the time and keep going. I forgot what my mother's laugh sounded like and I'm still here. Tomas won't be forgotten. I'll come back for him too. That's how it works, isn't it? You pay, and then you fight your way back out. Everyone in this party has done something like that."

"She's learned negotiation from watching us," Brannic said softly, to no one. "Gods help us all. We've raised a monster."

"This isn't negotiation," Ada said. "This is a child volunteering for a filing cabinet."

"This is a child doing the math," Ines snapped, and her voice cracked clean down the middle, and I saw, finally, under the fury, the eight-year-old who had watched her mother die one receipt at a time. "One of me or one of him. That's the math. And I'm the one holding the box, and I'm the one who read the contract, and I'm the one who *walked here*, and none of you get to decide that my brother is worth less than any of you. He's worth more than all of you. He's worth more than the whole stupid guild."

Silence. Even the pages in the walls seemed to hold their breath.

And Ada — Ada Blackwood, keeper of ledgers, servant of a silent god, the flattest voice and the stillest hands in two kingdoms — looked at that furious, terrified child, and I watched them lose the argument without saying a single word more. Because there was nothing to say. Because the math was right. Because the math was monstrous, and being right and being monstrous had apparently decided to share a room tonight.

"We'll find another way," Ada said quietly. It wasn't a statement. It was a prayer with the serial numbers filed off.

"There is no other way," said the Auditor, polite as ever. "Though I will note, for the record, that the Court maintains a deep inventory. If the claimant prefers, an equivalent soul may be selected from existing stock — one of similar weight and warmth. Some find that arrangement more comfortable."

"And whose are those?" Ada asked. "The ones in stock. Where did they come from?"

"People sold them," said the Auditor. "Or lost them. Or never came to collect. The Court keeps what is owed to it. That is all the Court has ever done."

I looked at Ada then, and Ada looked at the Auditor, and something passed between them that I did not understand yet — some recognition, some old grief clicking into a new lock. Later I would learn what they'd realized in that moment. Later, much later, when we were counting ourselves back home and coming up one short in a way no ledger could explain.

But that came after. First there was this: Ines, opening the tin box. The ember rising into the air between her palms, small and stubborn, burning without heat, the last warm thing in the coldest room I have ever stood in.

"Tomas," she whispered to it. "It's me. I'm coming to get you. Wait for me. Don't you dare be brave about it — be annoying. Be exactly as annoying as you always were."

The ember pulsed. Once. Twice. Like something knocking on a door from the inside.

And then it turned — I saw it, we all saw it — like a compass needle finding north, and it began to drift, slowly, steadily, away from the desk, away from the fee, away from the Auditor's polite and patient face, deeper into the dark of the court, pulling toward something further down.

Toward whatever the Court was keeping below its ledgers.

The Auditor rose from its chair. For the first time, its politeness developed a hairline crack, and beneath the crack I glimpsed something that looked almost like alarm.

"That area," it said, "is not open to visitors."

"Well," said Ada Blackwood, already moving, already following the light, "then it's lucky we're not visitors. We're auditors."

And they walked straight past the desk, into the dark, with a god's stolen fire lighting their way.
