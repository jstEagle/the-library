## 16. Amendment to the Ledger

I have forged a great many documents in my time. Guild work demands it. We once produced a death certificate for a man who refused to die on schedule, and a marriage license for two lighthouse keepers who were, technically, the same person. I am very good at making paper say things it was never meant to say.

But standing in the Court Beneath the Mountain, with Veth's chains still ringing in my ears and Ines's hand gripping the back of my coat like an anchor line, I understood that what I was about to propose was not forgery.

Forgery is lying to power. This would be *correcting* it.

* * *

The Auditor's clerks had given us a waiting alcove—courtesy of the dead, apparently, extends even to the living who haven't yet been filed. It was a niche carved into black stone, furnished with a bench worn smooth by centuries of anxious sitting, and lit by a lamp that burned without flame, which in this place I had stopped finding remarkable about four hours ago.

Sefa sat with her maps spread across her knees, though she wasn't looking at them. She was looking at me the way you look at a bridge before you decide whether to trust it with your weight.

"You've got the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The one you get when you're about to do something the rest of us will have to live with." She folded her maps with unnecessary precision. "I've seen it twice. Once in Saltmere. Once the night you decided we'd take the road that eats memories instead of going around."

"And both times I was right."

"Both times you were *lucky*, which you then mistook for right." She glanced toward Ines, who had fallen asleep against Brannic's pack—or against where Brannic's pack used to be; we hadn't let anyone carry it since the pass—and lowered her voice. "Whatever it is, say it to all of us at once. No heroics by ambush. That's not how this family works."

"We're not a family," I said, out of habit.

"No," Sefa agreed mildly. "We're a guild. Which is worse."

* * *

Marrow came back from his reconnaissance as a cold spot in the air and then, reluctantly, as himself. He had been a debt-collector in life and remained one in death, which meant he could read the court's filing system the way I read ledgers: fluently, and with a low simmering contempt.

"It's possible," he said, without preamble. "What you're thinking. I can see it forming behind your eyes like weather."

"You don't know what I'm thinking."

"I knew you for eleven years, Blackwood. You alphabetize your guilt." He drifted closer, and the lamp guttered, offended. "You want to amend their books. Put the god's sentence onto another signer. Transfer of lien. It's been done exactly once, in the reign of the Auditor's predecessor, and the clerk who drafted the amendment spent the next nine hundred years being audited personally."

"What happened to the transfer?"

"That's the part they don't shelve." Marrow's voice went quieter, which for a ghost is saying something. "There's a clause. Every amendment requires consideration—a soul of equal weight, we know that already, the Auditor told you. But a *transfer* clause is different. It doesn't take a soul. It takes a name. Surrendered permanently. Not sold, not traded, not held in escrow. Given. The court keeps names the way a miser keeps coins: because giving them up is the only thing that costs them anything."

Ines stirred. I moved, without deciding to, so that my body stood between her and the conversation.

"How permanent?" I asked.

"Blackwood."

"How. Permanent."

Marrow looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face made him answer honestly, which I have never forgiven him for. "The person remains. They walk, they breathe, they eat terrible rations and complain about them. But no record anywhere will hold their name. Not the guild's books. Not their own memory, eventually. Not the mouths of the people who loved them. A surrendered name doesn't die—it's *unwritten*. And unwritten things can't be written back. There's no loophole. Believe me. Loopholes are my native country, and there are none there."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me when he said it, because honesty is the whole point of what came after, and I might as well start practicing.

There was fear. Of course there was fear. I am a coward in the way all careful people are cowards—I have simply trained my cowardice into something useful, like a dog that barks at the right moments.

But underneath the fear, shamefully, there was relief.

Veth chained beneath the mountain for forgiving debts too freely. Veth, whose silence I had carried like a stone in my chest for years, telling me in that voiceless way gods communicate through shackles: *I erased your debt long ago. You have been serving a god who asked nothing back.* All those years of ledger-keeping, of exactness, of paying down a balance I thought I owed—the balance was never there. I had been tithing to an empty column.

And here was a clause that required only a name. Mine was, if I am honest, the least-used thing I owned. I had spent a decade making myself invisible. The guild's whole doctrine was *be nothing, owe nothing, be owed nothing.* I had practiced for this erasure my entire adult life. It felt less like sacrifice than like a job I was uniquely qualified for.

That's the trap, if you're wondering. That's always the trap. The people most willing to give themselves up are the people who were taught early that they were worth little. Grace isn't supposed to feel like coming home. When it does, you should check the foundations.

* * *

I called the others together in the alcove, because Sefa was right—that much I owed her. No heroics by ambush.

Brannic was dead eleven days and still managed to attend, in the sense that we had brought his pipe and someone—me, if you must know—had taken to filling it and setting it on the bench beside Ines at meals. Grief does strange bookkeeping. You keep accounts open for people who can't collect.

"Here's the shape of it," I said. "The Auditor has ruled Ines's claim valid. Her brother's soul gets released. Payment required: a soul of equal weight. Ines volunteered—" Ines's head snapped up at that, furious, and I held up a hand. "Which is not happening, and we'll fight about it later. But there's an alternative instrument. A transfer clause. It moves Veth's imprisonment onto a willing signer. The court releases the god, the god unmakes their claims, every shelved soul in this mountain goes home—including Ines's brother, including, presumably, everyone the court has ever collected on. The cost is not a soul. The cost is a name. Surrendered forever. Unwritten."

Silence. The lamp burned its flameless burn.

"Whose name?" Sefa asked, in a voice that already knew.

"Mine," I said. "Obviously."

"Nothing about this is obvious," Sefa said, rising. "Nothing about this is *anything*. You are not signing yourself out of existence because you've decided your name is the cheapest thing on the table—"

"I've done the arithmetic, Sefa."

"I've seen your arithmetic! Your arithmetic once concluded that eating was optional!"

"Sefa." Marrow's voice cut cold and clean between us. "Let her finish. Let her say the whole stupid brave thing, and then we'll tear it apart properly."

So I said it. All of it. That Veth had freed me of a debt I'd spent years repaying, and that the only repayment left was the kind you can't schedule. That I was the guild's keeper of ledgers, and amendments were my craft, and if anyone could draft a clause the court couldn't wriggle out of, it was me. That Ines was eleven years old and had already lost a mother and nearly lost a brother and was not going to lose anything else today, not while I held the pen.

And then, because I am not entirely a coward, I said the true thing underneath:

"And because my name has been half-gone for years anyway. Because I built myself into a shadow on purpose, and this is just the paperwork catching up. Because some of us were always going to end up a footnote. Better a footnote in a liberation than a margin-note in a ledger nobody reads."

"Oh, that's *rot*," Ines said.

Everyone turned. She was standing, small and rigid, the tin box clutched to her chest, and her eyes were wet and blazing.

"That's rot and you know it. You think I can't tell? You talk about yourself like you're reading an inventory. 'Some of us.' 'A footnote.' You don't get to decide you're worthless and then call it math." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it the way she pushed through everything, headfirst, furious. "You're the person who checked under my bed for monsters and then *apologized to the bed*. You're the person who gave up her own memory so I could keep mine. You're the person Brannic died for, and he laughed doing it, because you made him believe the last night of his life was worth staying awake for. That's not a footnote. That's a whole *book*, and you don't get to close it yourself!"

"Ines—"

"You always do this!" she shouted, and now she was crying, which she would deny later with violence. "Everyone always does this. Mama signed the contract alone. Papa walked out alone. Everyone decides alone what they're worth and then they're *gone*, and I'm supposed to just—" She stopped. Breathed. Eleven years old, and she breathed the way soldiers breathe, deliberately, one at a time. "If you sign it, I'll find you. I don't care what the clause says. I don't care if your name is unwritten. I found a soul in a tin box across three kingdoms. I'll find *you*."

And there it was. The thing I had not accounted for. I had prepared arguments for Sefa's logic and Marrow's law and even, in a drawer of my mind, for Veth's silent assent or refusal. I had not prepared for a child promising to love me past the edge of erasure.

It nearly worked. I want that on the record. It nearly worked, and if it had, I would be someone else now, and this chapter would have a different ending.

But the ember chose that moment to flare.

It did that sometimes—when truth was spoken near it, when love got loud. The tin box glowed through the seams, gold as a struck match, and the whole alcove filled with a warmth that had no source and needed none, and I remembered what Veth had shown me in the deep dark: *forgiveness is not forgetting. Forgiveness is remembering, and letting it cost you anyway.*

Unwritten things can't be written back, Marrow said.

But maybe they could be *carried*. Maybe a girl with an ember and a stubborn streak a mile wide could hold a name the way you hold a coal through a long winter—not usefully, not safely, but alive.

Or maybe I was rationalizing. I've had a lot of practice at that too.

Either way, I picked up the pen.

* * *

The court provides signing rooms the way banks provide them: small, windowless, designed to make you feel that the transaction is larger than you. The desk was obsidian. The amendment form materialized when Marrow spoke the old collector's cant—three words in a language the dead use for foreclosures—and unrolled itself across the stone like something waking up hungry.

I drafted the clause myself. That was the one mercy of the process, that they let me write my own disappearance in my own hand, in my own careful script, with the crossbars and serifs I'd been perfecting since I was nine years old copying my mother's signatures.

*Amendment to the Record of the Court Beneath,* I wrote. *Whereas the entity known as Veth stands imprisoned under seal of prior judgment; and whereas the claimant Ines holds valid title to one (1) soul, currently warehoused; and whereas the undersigned offers full consideration—*

My pen hovered over the consideration clause.

*—to wit: the name of the undersigned, surrendered absolutely and in perpetuity, to be struck from all records, memories, registers, and recollections, present and future, mortal and divine.*

Below it, the standard rider, the one every impossible contract carries, the one I'd read aloud to clients a hundred times without ever expecting to sign it myself: *The undersigned affirms this surrender is freely given, fully informed, and irrevocable.*

"Read it back," Marrow said quietly. "All of it. Out loud. That's the law, even here. Especially here."

I read it back. My voice didn't shake until the last line, and then only a little, and I choose to believe that was the cold.

"Ada." Sefa stood in the doorway of the signing room, and behind her Ines, held back by nothing but her own exhausted obedience. Sefa's face was doing something complicated—anger and grief and, worst of all, understanding. "Last chance. Walk away. We go home, we raise the girl, we grieve the god. People do it every day. It's called being sensible, and you'd be *terrible* at it, but you could try."

"If we walk away," I said, "the court collects. Not just Ines's brother. Everyone. Every bargain the wards were holding shut comes due, and the world spends the next century being repossessed one soul at a time." I dipped the pen. "You taught me the maps, Sefa. You know better than anyone: sometimes the only good road is the one that costs the mapmaker."

"That's not wisdom, that's a *motto*."

"Mottos are wisdom with the boring parts removed."

She laughed—one broken, furious note—and then crossed the room and gripped my shoulders hard enough to bruise. "Then hear me. Whatever happens, whoever you become, some part of you stays in the people you signed for. That's not poetry. That's how debts actually work. You taught me that."

"Get it right, then," I said. "Remember me as punctual."

And I signed.

The ink took the name the way snow takes footprints—completely, warmly, without any intention of giving it back. I felt it go. Not pain, exactly. More like setting down a pack you've carried so long you'd forgotten its weight, and discovering that without it you don't float. You *tilt*. You lean into the empty space where the carrying used to be and almost fall.

The amendment flared white along its lines, sealed itself, and rolled shut with a sound like a door closing in a very large house.

Somewhere above us, impossibly far above, stone began to groan.

Chains, I thought. Somewhere in the dark of the mountain, chains the length of centuries were beginning to break—and the first thing my god's freedom cost the world was me.

Ines was screaming my name. I could hear her fighting through the doorway, through Sefa's arms, through the court's polite machinery, screaming it into the shaking dark as if a name could be thrown like a rope, as if love could catch what the ledger dropped.

Hold onto it, kid, I thought, as the edges of me went soft and bright. Hold onto it like a coal.

The last thing I remember clearly is the ember's light, gold and stubborn, refusing—in defiance of every clause, every rule, every law of the court beneath the mountain—to go out.

After that, the dark took the rest.

Or almost all of it.

Almost.
