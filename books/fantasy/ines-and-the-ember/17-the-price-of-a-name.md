## 17. The Price of a Name

I have watched a lot of terrible things happen in my eleven years. I watched my mother sign away my brother's soul with a smile on her face because the man from the court told her it was "only paperwork." I watched the stars go out one by one like candles being pinched by someone with very large fingers. I watched Old Brannic walk into a cursed pass whistling, which was somehow worse than all of it.

None of that prepared me for watching Ada Blackwood disappear.

It started small. It always starts small. That's the trick of it — nobody warns you because nobody notices until it's too late to do anything but scream.

* * *

The amendment was still wet on the page.

Ada had signed it — signed it with the guild's own pen, the one they kept wrapped in oilcloth because it wrote in things truer than ink — and for a moment nothing happened at all. The court beneath the mountain went quiet in the way a room goes quiet when everyone is doing arithmetic behind their eyes.

Then the Auditor said, in its polite, faceless voice: "The amendment is accepted. Surrender of name commences."

"Commences," Ada repeated. They were standing very straight, the way they did when they were pretending not to be afraid, which was most of the time, which I hadn't understood until that exact moment.

"You may wish to say your goodbyes quickly," the Auditor said. "The process is thorough. We pride ourselves on thoroughness."

"I'll bet you do," Ada said. And then they turned and looked at me, and their face did something complicated — the kind of thing Ada's face did instead of crying.

"Ines—"

"No." I said it fast, before they could get anything else out, because I knew if they got a whole sentence out I would fall apart, and falling apart was a luxury we could not afford. Not yet. Not while there was still something to be done about it. "No goodbyes. Goodbyes are for people who are actually leaving. You're just — you're being stupid in a bureaucratic way. There's a difference."

"There really isn't," Sefa said quietly. She was holding her maps against her chest like a shield, and her knuckles were white. Marrow stood beside her, translucent as ever, and even he looked stricken, which for a ghost is saying something.

"It's fine," Ada said. "It's a clean trade. Veth walks free, your brother's soul goes home, the court's books balance. That's the whole job. That's what the guild does — impossible things, done properly, with receipts."

"You're not a receipt," I said.

"No," Ada agreed. "I'm a ledger entry now. Which, frankly, is where I always felt most comfortable."

And that was when the fading started.

* * *

Here is how you erase a person, according to the court beneath the mountain:

You don't take them anywhere. You don't drag them off screaming. You simply begin removing them from the record, and the record is everything. The record is the world.

First the ink on the amendment began to move — Ada's signature lifting off the page like smoke rising off a doused candle, curling upward, dissolving into the dark above the ledgers. Then the ledgers themselves reacted. All around us, the great shelved volumes of the court — thousands of them, millions, souls filed and audited and forgotten — began to rustle, pages turning on their own, searching for references to a person who was being unmade.

And everywhere a page found Ada Blackwood, the writing faded.

I saw it happen. I saw a ledger near me flip open to an old entry — *Blackwood, A., sworn to Veth, debts assumed, mercy rendered* — and the letters went grey, then pale, then gone, leaving blank vellum where a life used to be written.

"Stop it," I said.

"The process cannot be stopped," said the Auditor. "Only completed. Please stand clear of the records."

"STOP IT."

Somewhere deep in the mountain, something groaned. Not the Auditor. Not the ledgers. Something older, down in the dark under the court, where the chains were beginning — I hoped, I prayed, I bargained — to come loose.

Veth was rising. That was the deal. That was the whole point. Ada's name was the price of the lock coming open, and the lock was opening, and the price was being paid, and I stood there in a bureaucracy of the dead watching my friend get deleted line by line.

Because that's what they were. My friend. I don't know when it happened. Somewhere between the night I broke into the guild with a tin box and a temper, and the ash market where Ada sold a memory of their god's voice so I could keep walking, and every cold campfire and every bad joke and every time they checked on me in the night pretending to be checking the perimeter — somewhere in all of that, Ada Blackwood stopped being the mercenary who almost turned me away and became the person I was not going to let the universe lose.

"Ines." Their voice was thinner already. I turned and — this is the part I will hate until I die — I couldn't remember, right then, in that moment, whether their eyes were brown or grey. I had looked at this person every day for weeks. Brown or grey. Gone.

"Say it again," I said. "Your name. Say it loud, so the books can hear it."

Ada smiled. It was a terrible smile, crooked and brave and furious all at once. "Ada Blackwood," they said, clear as a bell. "Keeper of ledgers, Guild of Impossible Things. Sworn of Veth. Debtor to no one."

The ledgers shivered. And then, softly, horribly, the words began to slide out of the air itself, like breath off glass.

* * *

You want to know what erasure sounds like? It doesn't sound like anything. That's the horror of it. It sounds like a room getting quieter. It sounds like a name being spoken into a space that has already forgotten how to hold it.

But I am Ines. I am eleven years old and furious, and I have been carrying my brother's soul in a tin box since I was ten, and if there is one thing I know how to do, it is refuse to accept the terms of a contract.

So I screamed their name.

"ADA BLACKWOOD!"

I screamed it at the ledgers. I screamed it at the Auditor. I screamed it at the dark ceiling of the court, at the shelves, at the blank pages, at whatever machinery ground beneath the mountain turning a person into nothing.

"ADA BLACKWOOD! ADA BLACKWOOD! Everybody hear me? EVERYBODY HEAR ME? Their name is ADA BLACKWOOD! They kept ledgers! They broke guild law for a girl they'd known one day! They sold their own memory of their god's voice without blinking! They're mean and stubborn and they pretend they don't care and they care more than anyone I have ever met in my entire life, and their name is ADA BLACKWOOD, and you don't get to have it!"

My voice cracked. I didn't stop.

Sefa joined in — she shouted it too, over and over, her cartographer's voice steady as a compass needle. Even Marrow, who barely had a voice left to shout with, mouthed the name like a prayer. The court filled with it, three voices hammering one name against the silence, and I swear on my brother's ember that some of the ledgers flinched.

And through it all, Ada stood there, fading, watching me, and their expression — the last expression I would ever see on that face — was not fear.

It was wonder. Like they couldn't quite believe anyone would fight this hard to keep them. Like no one ever had.

"Oh," they said. Just that. Just *oh*, soft, like a door opening somewhere inside them that had been shut for years. "Oh, kid."

"Don't you *kid* me," I sobbed. "Don't you dare. You come back. You hear me? Whatever hole they put your name in, you find it, and you climb out, because you owe me. You owe me a debt and I'm putting it in the ledger and the guild honors its contracts, YOU TAUGHT ME THAT—"

The mountain groaned again, louder, and this time the floor moved. Dust sifted down from the dark. Far below, something vast shifted its weight, and the sound it made was not a roar and not a word but something between — the sound of a throat that had not been used in a hundred years remembering how.

The chains, wherever they were, were breaking.

And Ada — Ada was nearly transparent now. I could see the shelf behind them. I could see the blank page where their life had been.

They raised one hand — a hand going grey at the edges, going thin — and pressed it briefly, gently, to the top of my head, the way you'd steady a lantern in wind.

"Hold onto it," they said. "However much you've got. Hold onto me however you can. Memory's just another kind of debt, Ines. And I know somebody up there—" their eyes flicked upward, toward the groaning dark, toward the rising god— "who never could resist forgiving one."

And then they smiled that crooked smile one last time, and came apart into the air like ash off a log, and the place where Ada Blackwood had been standing was empty, and the court was silent, and I was screaming a name that the dark was already trying to swallow.

* * *

Except the dark didn't swallow it.

That's the thing. That's the thing I need you to understand, whoever reads this, whoever remembers, whoever comes after: the dark tried, and it failed, because I did not let go.

I felt the name go — felt it slide out of my own head like water through fingers, felt myself reaching for *Ada* and finding a shape with no word attached, a face with no label, a hole in the middle of my heart wearing a person's outline. For one horrible second I stood in the court of the dead and could not remember who I was screaming about.

But my mouth remembered. My mouth kept saying it. *Ada Blackwood. Ada Blackwood.* And the ember in the tin box against my chest flared — hot, hotter than it had ever been, though it burns without heat, though heat was never its language — and I understood, in the way you understand things in the deep places of the world, that my brother was holding the other end. That somewhere in the ember was a fragment of warmth that refused to forget, and it was braided around the name, anchoring it, the way you anchor a tent in a storm.

Love against erasure. That was the whole battle. No swords, no spells. Just a girl refusing to put down a name.

And the mountain heard me.

* * *

It rose like a verdict.

I felt it before I saw it — a pressure through the floor, through my boots, through my teeth, the way you feel thunder in your chest before the sound arrives. The ledgers all around the court burst open at once, every volume, every page, a storm of paper like startled birds. The Auditor took one step back — the first time I ever saw it move like something with a body — and its polite faceless head tilted upward toward the ceiling, which was cracking.

Light came down through the crack. Not starlight — there were no stars; the court had taken those too. Older light. Warmer. The light of something that had spent a century in the dark and come up glowing anyway.

The floor split. The shelves bowed. And up through the wound in the world climbed Veth.

I don't know what I expected a god to look like. Bigger, maybe. Angrier, definitely. But Veth rose slow and quiet, and what I saw — what any of us saw — was something like a figure made of open pages, something like hands, something like the feeling you get when a debt you thought would crush you turns out to be forgiven. It was not loud. It did not need to be. When it moved, the whole court leaned toward it, the way plants lean at a window.

And the first thing the freed god of debts and mercy did — the very first thing, before unmaking a single claim, before freeing a single soul — was look down at me.

At the girl screaming a name into the dark.

It extended one hand, made of patient paper and old kindness, and touched the air where Ada had been. And the ember in my box burned so bright I had to squint, and somewhere in the shaking dark of the mountain, in some vault or drawer or locked page where surrendered names are kept, I felt something answer.

Not destroyed. Not gone. *Held.*

Veth could not speak — its voice was still scattered through the court's ledgers, stolen syllables filed under a thousand headings — but it didn't need to. It looked at me, and the look said everything a contract needed saying:

*Paid in full. Not yet returned.*

Then it turned to the shelves, and the shelves began to break, and the souls of the dead poured upward past us like birds released from a burning aviary, thousands upon thousands, streaming home through the cracking dark.

Sefa grabbed my arm. Marrow was shouting something. The Auditor was receding into the chaos, its tidy world collapsing around it.

I didn't move. I stood in the middle of the court beneath the mountain, in the rain of freed souls, clutching a tin box, and I said the name one more time — quietly this time, just for me, just to keep it warm:

"Ada Blackwood."

And far above, through the splitting ceiling of the world, I saw the first star come back on.

One. Then another. Then all of them, all at once, like the sky remembering itself.

Which meant the wards were returning. Which meant the court's power was breaking. Which meant somewhere in this collapsing mountain was a vault full of surrendered names, and one of them belonged to my friend, and I had a god standing right in front of me who owed the guild several centuries of back wages.

I wiped my face. I picked up the tin box. I turned to the god of mercy, and I held out the oldest ledger I'd snatched from a broken shelf, and I opened it to a blank page.

"Right," I said, in my best Ada voice, which wasn't very good yet, but I had years to practice. "Let's talk terms."
