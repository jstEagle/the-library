## 18. When the Stars Came Back

### I.

The mountain screamed first.

I want to be honest about that, because later everyone would talk about the singing and the light and the souls going home like birds, and all of it was true, but it started with a scream — the mountain itself, groaning through its roots like an old man standing up after too long in a bad chair. The floor of the court bucked under us. Shelves of souls rattled in their niches, thousands of little lights shivering in their jars and boxes and ledger-bound cages, and somewhere far above us, stone split along seams no one had ever been allowed to see.

I was on my knees where Ada had been standing. That's the part I keep coming back to. One moment they were there — hand still wet with ink from signing, face doing that thing it did when it was terrified and pretending to be bored — and then the amendment took hold, and the dark took them, and my knees hit the floor because there was nothing left to stand next to.

"Ines." Marrow's hand on my shoulder. He was flickering like a candle in wind, his ghost-shape barely holding together, and even half-dissolved he managed to sound like a man reading out a bill. "Look up."

I looked up.

Veth rose.

I don't have words for it, and I'm a girl who has learned to use words like weapons, so believe me when I say I tried. Veth had been a silence for as long as anyone could remember — a god you prayed to and got nothing back, a name in old contracts that never answered. But now the silence itself stood up out of its chains, and it turned out silence has a shape, and the shape was enormous and patient and *kind*, which is somehow worse than enormous and terrible. Kindness that big makes you feel every unkind thing you've ever done at once.

The chains didn't break. They *unmade* — unraveled into threads of light that drifted upward like dandelion seeds, and each thread, as it rose, passed through a shelf, and each shelf opened, and each soul came out.

That was the singing everyone talks about. Not music exactly. More like the sound a house makes when everyone who left it comes home at the same time. Thousands of small lights rising off their shelves, streaming up through the wound in the mountain's side, pouring into the dark sky like a river running the wrong direction.

And one light circled back down.

It came straight at me. Small, warm, stubborn — I knew it before I knew it, the way you know your own front door in the dark. It hovered in front of the tin box hanging at my throat, and the box opened on its own, and my brother's ember flared up bright enough to light the whole ruined court, and then —

"Tam," I said. His name. I'd carried it so long I'd almost worn it smooth.

The ember went out.

For one horrible second the world was just dark and dust and me screaming inside my own skull, and then warmth pressed against my chest — not fire, not light, just *warmth*, the specific warmth of a boy's head falling asleep against your shoulder on a long cart ride, and a voice, thin and crackly and annoyed in the way only brothers can manage:

"*Ines. You're squishing me.*"

I am not going to tell you what I did next. Some things belong to me.

* * *

### II.

Sefa here. Cartographer, debtor, survivor of things I will bill someone for eventually.

We were camped at the mountain's foot when it happened, because none of us had the legs to climb back down the pass and Brannic's ghost — yes, he got one, don't ask me how, ask him, he'll explain at length — insisted we wait for the others. So we waited, and I mapped, because mapping is what I do when I can't do anything else. I was charting the fallen wards, or what was left of them, tracing the old boundary lines across my paper like scars across a body.

Then the paper moved.

Not the lines. The *paper*. Every map I've ever drawn — and I've drawn maps of places that move, places that lie, places that eat surveyors — every one of them shifted at once, the way a dog shifts in its sleep, and then settled. And I felt it in my teeth before I understood it in my head:

The wards were coming back.

Not rebuilt. *Returned*. Like coats from a cloakroom. All those years of bargains the court beneath the mountain had been collecting on — the starless night, the repossession, the slow quiet theft of everything our borders were quietly holding in trust — all of it reversing, unwinding, being handed back with interest. I watched my map redraw itself, ward-lines blazing fresh across valleys and rivers, and I thought of every village that had gone quiet since the stars went out, every person who'd woken to find a year missing or a shadow sold or a name called in by some grey-faced collector.

They were getting their receipts back.

Old Brannic sat up so fast his ghost knocked over the teapot. "Stars," he said. "Boy, look at the stars."

I looked.

You have to understand what the starless night did to people. It wasn't just dark. It was *audited* dark — a sky with everything valuable removed, itemized, filed away. We'd gotten used to it the way you get used to a toothache, which is to say badly. And now, one by one, points of light kindled overhead like someone walking through a great dark house lighting lamps, and the whole sky remembered what it was for.

Brannic cried. A dead man, crying at stars. I drew a quick map of the moment instead, because somebody had to, and I'm glad now that I did, because it's the last clean thing I have from that night.

Because that was also the night we realized Ada wasn't coming down the mountain.

* * *

### III.

Marrow speaking. Formerly of the court below, currently of nowhere in particular, which is a promotion.

When Veth unmade the court's claims, it unmade mine too. I felt my debt — the one I'd carried since I deserted, the one that kept me tethered to this world like a fishhook through the cheek — simply *dissolve*. Gone. Forgiven. Not transferred, not paid, not rolled into some new arrangement. Forgiven, the way Veth used to forgive things, freely, before the court decided that free forgiveness was bad for business and stole a god's voice to stop it.

I should have felt light. Everyone says that — forgiven feels light. What I actually felt was unmoored, like a boat cutting loose from the only dock it's known. I had defined myself by that debt for longer than I care to admit. Who is a debt-collector with no debts? I stood in the ruins of the court I used to serve, watching souls stream home like birds at dusk, and I felt absolutely nothing holding me to the world.

Except one thing.

Ada Blackwood had signed themselves into the ledger of surrendered names to buy my freedom, and the girl's, and the boy's, and every soul on every shelf, and I knew — professionally, intimately, in the way only someone who has worked the court's books knows — what happened to names that get surrendered.

They aren't destroyed. The court doesn't destroy assets; destruction is just loss with extra steps. They're *held*. Filed. Kept in the deep archive where the court keeps everything it intends to use someday.

But the court was unmaking. And when a filing system unmakes, everything in it goes... somewhere.

I went looking through the wreckage while the others celebrated. Old habit. You can take the collector out of the court, etcetera. I found the great ledger hall half-collapsed, shelves of records crumbling into light, and I searched for the entry — Blackwood, Ada, surrendered name, one (1) — and here is what I found, and I report it plainly because someone has to:

The entry was gone.

Not crossed out. Not amended. *Gone*, as if it had never been written, as if no one of that description had ever signed anything anywhere. I checked the guild's copy in my memory — collectors carry copies, it's how we ruin people — and it was blank there too. I checked the master index. Blank. I checked the archive of held names, the deep vault, the place where surrendered names go to wait.

Empty shelf. Dust. Nothing.

Everywhere in creation that Ada Blackwood had ever been written down, the writing was gone. The guild's ledgers. The court's rolls. The toll-keeper's bridge book, if he kept one. Even, I suspect — though I couldn't verify this, and it made me cold in a way ghosts shouldn't get cold — even in the memories of the people who loved them.

A name surrendered forever means forever, apparently. It means everywhere. It means even the people who knew you wake up knowing they've lost something without knowing what.

I stood in that empty vault a long time. Then I went and told the girl, because she deserved the truth, and because — old habits again — I have never once in my existence delivered good news without delivering the invoice attached to it.

* * *

### IV.

Ines again.

We walked home slow, because Tam couldn't walk fast yet — a soul poured back into a body that's been empty for three years comes back hungry and wobbly and complaining, and Tam complained beautifully, and I let him, because every complaint was proof. The wards hummed overhead as we passed under them, warm as a hand on your head. People came out of their houses to watch the stars come back. An old woman in the border villages grabbed my sleeve and said, "It's over, then?" and I said, "Yes," and she said, "Who do I thank?" and I opened my mouth to say *Ada Blackwood* and

nothing came out.

There was a shape where the name should be. A hole shaped exactly like a person. I knew — the way you know a stair isn't there in the dark — that someone had stood beside me the whole way down the mountain, someone who taught me to read contracts and argue with gods and sell a memory without selling yourself, someone whose face I could see perfectly in my mind and whose name I could not say, could not remember, could not even feel the absence of properly, because the absence had been sanded smooth.

I screamed their name into the dark on the mountain. I remember screaming. I don't remember what I screamed.

Marrow told me the rest, gently, for a ghost. Gone from every ledger. Including ours. Including the ones at the guild, the ones Ada kept in that careful slanted hand, the ones that were basically Ada's diary if diaries were passive-aggressive about outstanding balances.

So here is what I did when we got home.

I sat down at the Debtors' Table, in front of the oldest ledger — the one older than the guild, the one nobody claims — and I read it. Cover to cover. Twice. Looking for loopholes, because if anyone could survive being erased from every book in creation, it would be the person who spent their whole life finding the loophole in every book in creation.

And on the last page, in handwriting I knew — slanted, careful, faintly smug, a hand I cannot attach a name to but would know blindfolded in a hurricane — there was a new entry.

It said:

*One debt outstanding. Owed to a girl with an ember. Payable in person.*

* * *

### V.

Sefa, closing the books, so to speak.

The guild reopened in spring. New rules, painted on the door in letters big enough to read from the road: NOTHING IMPOSSIBLE GETS BOUGHT. ONLY RETURNED. Business is slower now. Word gets around.

We set a place at the Debtors' Table anyway. Nobody discussed it; we just did. Brannic's ghost sits in his old chair when he visits, complaining about the tea. Marrow stays — says he's retraining as a debt-*forgiver*, which he insists is a real profession, and honestly, with Veth answering prayers again, it might be. Tam goes to school and steals bread and calls me "map lady," which I permit because he's earned it.

And the girl reads the ledgers. Every night. She's found entries none of us ever saw, in a hand none of us can name, and she reads them aloud at the table like scripture, like evidence, like a summons.

Last week she showed me the newest one. Fresh ink. Same slanted, careful, faintly smug hand.

It said: *Coming soon. Keep the tea warm.*

I put the kettle on.
