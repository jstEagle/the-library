## 20. An Entry in Someone Else's Hand

The ledgers smell like dust and old candle smoke and something else I can't name—like the inside of a box that once held something alive. I've been sitting in the archive for six days now. Seven, if you count the night I fell asleep on the floor and Sefa found me at dawn with a page stuck to my cheek and ink on my chin like a bad beard.

She keeps telling me to go home. She keeps telling me there's nothing left to find. She keeps telling me grief does this to people, makes them read the same book over and over looking for a sentence that was never written.

I keep telling her she's wrong.

Not because I have proof. Because Ada taught me better than to accept *nothing* as an answer. Ada—who read contracts the way other people read love letters, who could find the loophole in a curse, who once told me, in that flat voice they used when they were being serious and didn't want anyone to notice they were being serious: *"Every erasure is an entry somewhere else. Nothing gets removed from the world for free. The court doesn't destroy things. It files them."*

So I've been reading the filing system.

All of it. Every ledger in the guild's archive, from the newest—the one with Brannic's death written in it, the one that still smells faintly of the honey wine we drank at his last dinner—to the oldest, which is less a book than a brick with a spine, bound in leather gone black as tar, its pages soft as cloth from centuries of hands.

I'm on the oldest one now. Third pass. The first two times I read it like a person reads: front to back, word by word. This time I'm reading it the way Ada taught me to read anything that matters—which is to say, suspiciously. Looking not at what's written but at what's *off*. A margin too wide. An entry out of order. Ink that doesn't match the ink around it.

Because here's the thing nobody says out loud about the dead: you don't stop talking to them. You just stop expecting answers. And I have been talking to Ada for six days, out loud, in this cold room, telling them about my day, about the soup Marrow leaves for me even though he can't eat, about how the guild voted on the new rules and Sefa cried and pretended she didn't. And every time I finish talking, I look up at the empty chair across the table, and I wait, one second, two seconds, the way you do when you've asked a question into silence.

Ada never answered.

Until last night. Last night, something answered.

* * *

It happened the way it always happens in stories, which is to say badly and at the worst possible hour. Past midnight. Everyone asleep except me and the lamps and the moths throwing themselves against the glass like tiny idiots who haven't learned what light costs.

I had worked my way to the middle of the old ledger, the section where the handwriting changes every few pages because the guild's first scribes kept dying and being replaced, generation after generation, all of them writing entries about debts so old the names attached to them had worn smooth as river stones. Debts owed to people no one remembered. Debts forgiven by gods who no longer spoke. Page after page of things that had been impossible once and then, through sheer bureaucratic stubbornness, became merely difficult.

And then, between an entry from before the border wards were ever raised and an entry that should have come forty years later, there was a gap.

A gap of exactly one page.

I'd noticed it on my first pass and told myself it was damage. Water, maybe, or fire, or just time eating the paper the way time eats everything. On my second pass I held the page up to the lamp and looked for scorch marks, water stains, the brown bloom of rot. Nothing. The page wasn't damaged. It was *blank*. Clean. As blank as a page gets when it has been waiting a very long time for someone to write on it.

Last night, someone finally did.

I want to be honest about this part, because Ada would want me to be honest, and honesty is the whole point of the new guild rules anyway: I didn't see the ink appear. I looked down at the next entry—a debt of three shadows, sold in some year I can't be bothered to remember—and when I looked back up at the blank page, it wasn't blank anymore.

There was writing on it.

My first thought was that I'd finally cracked. Six days alone with dead people's accounts will do that to a person; Sefa warned me; Marrow warned me; even Old Brannic, who is dead and therefore qualified to speak on such matters, had probably warned me, in whatever place dead people do their warning from. My second thought was to check the ink against the rest of the book, because if I was hallucinating, at least I could hallucinate rigorously.

The ink was fresh. Still wet at the tail of the last letter. It hadn't been there an hour ago—I knew, because I'd checked this page eleven times today, which is a normal number of times to check a page if you are me and your entire family has been filed away by a bureaucracy of the dead.

And the handwriting—

Look. I know what you're going to say. Grief makes you see faces in clouds and voices in wind and handwriting in random scratches of ink. I know. I said it to myself too, standing there with the lamp shaking in my hand, reading the entry once, twice, five times, my heart doing something in my chest that felt less like beating and more like arguing.

But I spent four months on a road with Ada Blackwood. I watched them write in ledgers every single night, hunched over by firelight, complaining about their hand cramp, licking the nib of their pen like a weirdo. I know their handwriting the way I know my own face. The way the letters lean forward, like they're in a hurry. The way they cross their t's with one sharp stroke, impatient. The little hook they put on the ends of words, like the pen couldn't quite bear to let go.

The entry on the blank page was written in that hand.

It said:

*Entry 1,001. Debt incurred: one (1) name, surrendered under Amendment, Court Beneath the Mountain, in exchange for the release of the god Veth and the restoration of one soul belonging to Tomas of the Ash Road. Creditor: a girl with an ember. Terms: payable in person.*

That was all. No date. No signature. Just those lines, in Ada's handwriting, in a book that hadn't taken a new entry in forty years.

*A girl with an ember.*

Me. It meant me. Someone had written me into the oldest ledger in the world as a *creditor*, and the debt was Ada's name, and the terms were *payable in person*, and I stood in that archive at one in the morning holding a lamp and shaking and laughing and crying, all three at once, so thoroughly that a moth flew into my open mouth, which is the kind of detail Ada would have loved and I am including it purely to honor them.

Then the room went dark.

* * *

Not lamp-dark. Not blown-out-candle dark. Dark like the space between stars, which I know about personally, having spent a season under a sky where the stars had been repossessed. The lamps didn't gutter. They simply stopped, all of them, at once, politely, the way a courtroom stops when the judge enters.

And in the dark, something arrived.

I didn't see it at first. I felt it—in the pressure of the air, in the way the dust motes hung motionless, in the sudden awareness that the archive was much, much older than its walls suggested, that I was standing inside something vast and patient that had been listening to me talk to an empty chair for six days.

"You check that page eleven times a day," said a voice.

It wasn't a voice, exactly. It was more like remembering a voice—like the sensation of a word arriving already understood, the way the ember used to warm without heat. But if I had to give it a sound, I'd say it sounded quiet. Quiet the way mountains are quiet. Quiet the way a god who spent years chained beneath a mountain, forbidden to speak, would be quiet even after the chains came off.

Veth.

The god of debts and mercy. The god Ada served. The god who went silent, who got taken, who got freed because Ada signed their name away. I had heard about Veth the way you hear about weather in another country—all secondhand, all story. Marrow talked about it sometimes, in his foggy ghost way. Sefa had drawn its symbol once on a napkin and refused to explain why. And Ada—Ada had prayed to it exactly once in my hearing, early on, before everything, and gotten silence, and I had yelled at them for it, because I was eleven and furious and everyone I loved had abandoned me and here was one more abandonment to add to the pile.

"I do," I said, out loud, to the dark. "Eleven times. Twelve on bad days."

"Good," said Veth. "Persistence is a form of prayer. You've been praying for six days. Most people give up after six minutes."

I fumbled for the lamp. Struck a match. The flame caught, and the light came back slow, like blood returning to a numb limb, and when I could see again, the dark had organized itself into a shape standing across the table from me, in the space where the empty chair was.

I don't know what I expected a god to look like. Something big, probably. Wings, maybe. Fire. The ember in my tin box had been small and fierce and orange, and I think I assumed its original owner would be made of the same stuff.

Veth was none of that. Veth was a figure like a hole in the shape of a person—there and not-there at once, edges that my eyes kept sliding off, a presence I could only describe by what it wasn't. It wasn't loud. It wasn't large. It wasn't cruel. It stood very still, and it looked at me—not down at me, though it was tall, but *at* me, level, the way you look at someone whose opinion matters.

"You wrote the entry," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is a trick I learned from Ada, who used it constantly, usually right before doing something terrifying.

"The entry wrote itself," Veth said. "I only held the pen. Debts are strange things, little creditor. When a name is surrendered properly—freely, knowingly, for the sake of another—it does not vanish. It cannot vanish. Erasure is not destruction. Erasure is *transfer*. The court took Ada Blackwood's name out of every book in the world. But a name has to go somewhere. Names are like souls that way. Like embers. Nothing that burns goes out just because someone puts it in a box."

"And where did it go?"

"Here," Veth said. "Into the oldest ledger. Into the one book the court never thought to audit, because it predates the court, because it belongs to me, and because—" the god's not-face tilted, and something that was almost humor moved through the dark like a fish under ice, "—the court has always been terrible at reading other people's books. They're auditors. They only know how to read their own."

I sat down. My legs had decided the matter without consulting me. I pulled the old ledger toward me across the table and put my hand flat on the new entry, on Ada's handwriting, on the words *payable in person*, and I asked the question I had been carrying since the mountain, since the amendment, since the moment Ada's fingers started going transparent around the pen and Ines screamed their name into the dark until her voice broke:

"Is Ada alive?"

The god was quiet for a long moment. Long enough for me to count my own heartbeats. Long enough for the moth to recover and throw itself at the lamp glass twice more.

"That," Veth said, "is the wrong question. You're asking it like names are candles—lit or unlit, alive or dead. But you of all people should know better. You carried your brother's soul in a tin box for a year. Was he alive in there? He was *held*. Waiting. Undiminished. A name that is surrendered isn't killed. It's deposited. Filed. Kept—intact, complete, every memory and habit and sharp-edged kindness preserved—by whoever holds the ledger it lands in." The pause again, that mountain-quiet. "And I hold this ledger."

"So Ada is—"

"Held," Veth said. "In the oldest account I keep. The one for names given freely. It is a small account, little creditor. In four hundred years, it holds fewer entries than the court files in a day. Because almost no one gives a name freely. Almost everyone either clings to theirs until death takes it, or sells it cheaply to someone who'll waste it." Another tilt of the not-face. "Yours is the third entry. You're in good company. The second is a cartographer who once mapped a road that didn't exist yet, so that it would exist. The first is mine."

I didn't ask what that meant. Some questions you save. Some questions you hold the way Ines holds embers, in a closed box, close to the chest, until the moment is right to open it.

What I asked instead was the practical question, the contract question, the question Ada trained me to ask:

"What do I owe, and what do I get?"

Veth's answer came fast, and it came in the language of ledgers, because of course it did. This was a god of debts. It had been silent for years, but it had clearly been keeping books the whole time.

"You are listed as creditor," it said. "Which means the debt is yours to collect or forgive. If you forgive it, the name returns to the general dark, dispersed, and Ada Blackwood becomes a story people half-remember. If you collect it—" the god spread its not-hands, "—then the terms apply as written. Payable in person. Which means you must go and receive payment yourself. Which means descending again, past the wound in the mountain's side, past the shelved souls, down to the place where surrendered names are kept. The court is broken. Its claims are unmade. But its vaults are still sealed, and sealed things require a key, and the only key the vaults recognize is the person the name was paid *to*."

"Me," I said.

"A girl with an ember," Veth agreed. "Though I notice the ember is gone now. Spent. Given back to your brother, where it belonged." The god's attention moved, briefly, to my coat pocket, where the empty tin box still lived, because I am not sentimental, I just don't throw things away. "You'll need proof of what you are. Proof that love redeems. Fortunately—" and here the almost-humor surfaced fully, briefly, like a whale breaching, "—you have a ledger entry written in a dead woman's handwriting, in a book four hundred years old, attesting to exactly that. The court's own clerks, may they rest in unemployment, certified the transaction themselves. Bureaucracy cuts both ways, little creditor. That is the first thing Ada Blackwood ever understood about it, and the last thing they taught you."

"It's not a dead woman's handwriting," I said. "That's the whole point."

"No," Veth said softly. "It isn't. Is it."

I closed the ledger. I picked it up—gods, it weighed as much as Brannic did, and Brannic weighed as much as a pony—and I held it against my chest the way I used to hold the tin box, and I looked at the hole in the shape of a person across the table, and I said the thing I had walked down a remembering road and through an ash market and under a mountain's skin to earn the right to say:

"Then I'm going to collect."

Something in the dark moved. Not Veth—something behind Veth, or through Veth, some vast slow agreement, the sense of a door unlocking in a house with ten thousand doors. The lamps brightened. The dust resumed falling. The moth gave up on the lamp and landed on the ledger instead, resting on the cover like a small grey seal of approval.

"One condition," Veth said.

"Of course there's a condition. There's always a condition. Ada said conditions are just love wearing a suit."

The god was quiet again, and then it laughed—or did the thing that stands in for laughter when the laugher spent years with its voice locked in a ledger. It sounded like wind finding a crack in a wall.

"Take someone with you," Veth said. "You will want to argue that you work better alone. You will be wrong. Ada Blackwood also believed they worked better alone, and it took a girl breaking into their guild with a tin box to prove otherwise. Don't make them wait longer than necessary. Held things grow restless. Held things dream. And a name that dreams—" the god's not-eyes fixed on me, gentle and terrible, "—dreams of the person who comes to collect it."

I blew out the lamp. I didn't need it anymore. Somewhere in the walls of the guild, in the oldest book in the world, in handwriting I'd know anywhere, Ada Blackwood was waiting to be paid.

I went to find Sefa. We were going to need a map of a place that officially doesn't exist.

Again.
