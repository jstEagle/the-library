## 21. Ines and the Ember

Sefa's map of the place where surrendered names go was, technically, not a map. It was a napkin.

"It's all I could get," she said, smoothing it flat on the guild's scarred oak table with both hands, like she was apologizing to it. "The place doesn't exist, Ines. That's not poetry. That's a cartographic fact. You can't map something that has been removed from the record of the world. The best I can do is map everything around it and leave a hole."

The hole was in the middle of the napkin, drawn in charcoal: a ragged circle about the size of my fist, sitting underneath the mountain like a stone at the bottom of a well.

"That's it?" I said. "A hole?"

"Every map is mostly holes," Sefa said. "This one is just honest about it." She tapped the circle once, gently, the way you'd tap an egg you weren't sure was good anymore. "Here's what I know. The way down is through the wound in the mountain's side — the same one we came in by, but deeper. Past the court. Past the shelving halls. There's a stair that only goes down, and it doesn't count steps, because counting would be a kind of remembering, and nothing down there is allowed to remember anything. Including you."

I looked at the hole in the napkin. I was eleven years old and I had already buried a mother, sold a brother, walked into a mountain full of dead people's paperwork, and screamed a name into the dark until my throat bled. I did not feel brave. I felt like a girl holding a tin box, which is what I was.

"Ada went down there," I said. "And Ada knew everything."

"Ada also came back without knowing anything," Sefa said quietly. "That's the price. You understand that, don't you? Even if you win. Even if you drag their name out of that hole by its ears. They won't remember you. They won't remember any of it. You'll be standing in front of someone who looks at you like a stranger and asks why you're crying on their doorstep."

"I know," I said.

"Do you?" She looked at me for a long time. Sefa had eyes like ink spilled on purpose — they went exactly where she wanted them to. "Because I've watched you practice your argument for three days. You're very good. You sound just like them. But you're arguing like someone who wants to be right." Her voice went soft. "You need to argue like someone who wants them *back*."

* * *

Marlowe — my brother, Marlowe, alive, warm, real, currently eating the guild out of house and home as payment for two years of missed dinners — tried to stop me at the door.

"You're eleven," he said.

"You were twelve when you signed yourself away so I'd have bread," I said. "Don't you dare use age at me like it's a rule. Rules are negotiable. That's the first thing this house taught me."

He crouched down so we were eye to eye. He had Ada's habit of doing that — no. Not Ada's. Ada had learned it from somewhere too, probably. People keep passing things down without knowing it, like debts.

"Ines," he said. "Come home. Please. I just got you back. I spent two years in a box on a shelf learning exactly how much a person can miss being a person. Don't make me learn what it's like from the other side."

I put my forehead against his. His warmth came through, and under the warmth, faint as a coal under ash, the ember answered — the little piece of him that had stayed behind in the tin box even after the rest of him came home. Proof. That was the whole point of proof.

"If you can be patient," I said, "so can I. And I'm better at contracts than you ever were."

He laughed, wet-eyed, and let me go, which is the bravest thing anyone in that guild did, including me.

* * *

The wound in the mountain's side had half-healed while we were gone, the rock knitting itself shut like skin over a bad cut, as if the mountain were embarrassed about what it kept inside. Sefa's map got me to the lip of it and then the map ended, because maps are honest, and past that point there was nothing honest left to stand on.

I went down alone. That was the deal I'd made with myself on the walk up, and deals matter, especially the ones nobody witnesses. Veth was the god of debts and mercy, Marrow had told me once, and mercy is just a debt you forgive on purpose. I intended to collect.

The stair didn't count steps. I know because I counted them myself, out loud, and around four hundred my voice started coming back to me wrong — flattened, like a coin rubbed smooth. By six hundred I couldn't have told you my mother's face if you'd asked. I stopped counting then. Some payments you refuse to make.

Instead I talked to the ember.

"Keep me honest," I told it. "You're the only thing down here that remembers being loved. When I start forgetting, burn."

It glowed through the tin like a heartbeat. My brother's warmth, the fragment that hadn't fit back into him when Veth poured him home — the part that had been love itself, maybe, the part too big to file. The court beneath the mountain had audited everything else about Marlowe and found it acceptable collateral. They had never managed to audit this. Love doesn't itemize.

The dark got thicker the lower I went. Not empty-dark. *Full* dark. Dark like a ledger with every line filled in. And near the bottom, where Sefa's hole in the napkin should have been, the dark spoke.

Not in words. Words are for things that want to be understood. It spoke the way a locked door speaks, the way a stamped form speaks: *no*. A vast, patient, administrative *no*, older than the mountain, older than the stars going out, the accumulated weight of every name ever surrendered, filed forever, forgotten on purpose.

I stood at the edge of it with my tin box and did what Ada Blackwood taught me.

I opened a negotiation.

* * *

"I'm here about an entry," I said, and my voice only shook a little, and I decided the shaking could stay because courage isn't not-shaking, courage is shaking in the right direction. "In the oldest ledger in the world. A debt owed to a girl with an ember, payable in person. I'm the girl. This is the ember. I'm paying in person. So pay up."

The dark pressed against me like deep water. Like a hand made of filing cabinets. Somewhere in it, faintly, I heard writing — the sound of a pen moving across a page, endless, unhurried, recording my arrival in a book I could not see.

"You took a name," I said. "Ada Blackwood. Mercenary. Keeper of ledgers for the Guild of Impossible Things. Bound to Veth, god of debts and mercy. You have it down there, filed under surrendered assets. And I've come to contest the entry."

The pen-sound paused. If darkness can be interested, it was interested.

"I know what you're going to say," I went on, walking forward into it, one step, another, the ember burning brighter with each step like it was arguing for me. "You're going to say the surrender was voluntary. Signed, witnessed, binding. I've read the amendment. Ada wrote it themselves — clause, signature, everything clean. You love clean, don't you? Everything down here is clean. Filed, audited, forgotten. Well, I've got news for you."

I held up the tin box and opened it.

The ember flared.

Not heat — it never gave heat, that was the whole impossible thing about it — but light, warm gold light, the color of my brother laughing, the color of soup on a cold night, the color of Ada's lamp in the guild window the night I broke in with a contract and a grudge. The dark recoiled from it the way paper recoils from flame. Not burned. *Reminded.*

"This," I said, "is a fragment of a soul your own court sold, traded, shelved, and lost track of. Your books say it was paid in full. Your books are wrong. Because when my brother was restored — when your claims were unmade and every soul poured home like birds — this piece stayed behind. It didn't come back with him. Do you know why?"

The silence had a texture now. Listening.

"Because it was never his," I said. "It was mine. It's what loving him turned into when there wasn't enough of him left to hold it. And your auditors weighed it, and measured it, and filed it, and they never once figured out what it was. You can't. It doesn't reduce. It doesn't amortize. It isn't collateral for anything, because it was given freely and freely-given things can't be repossessed. Your whole court runs on taking. This is the one thing in existence you can't take, because it only exists as long as it's being given."

I took another step. The dark was thinner here, or I was bigger; I honestly couldn't tell which.

"And here's my claim," I said. "Item one: a name, Ada Blackwood, surrendered under an amendment proposed to save a child — me — from volunteering. Volunteering for a debt that was never mine, incurred before I could walk, signed by a dead woman who was tricked, in a court that has since been dissolved by its own prisoner. Item two: the god Veth, freed, whose entire nature is forgiving debts, whose forgiveness your ledgers recorded as a crime. Item three—" my voice cracked and I let it, because Ada taught me that honesty is leverage, "—item three: a girl who walked down a stair that doesn't count steps, alone, carrying the only thing in your archives you never managed to own. I am owed a name. I am here in person. Payable means payable."

The pen-sound started again. Faster now. Agitated. Somewhere far above, I swear I felt the mountain shift its weight, like a man in a chair who has just realized the person across the desk has read his own contract more carefully than he has.

"You want to know what love can redeem?" I said to the dark. "Look at the exhibit. Look at it. Two years in a box, and my brother came home still able to laugh. A god chained for centuries for forgiving too much, and the first thing it did free was unmake every cruel claim you ever filed. A mercenary who spent a lifetime staying invisible, and the last thing they did was sign their name away so a kid wouldn't have to lose herself. Every single thing you people called an asset fought you by being loved anyway. That's the flaw in your ledger. That's the error in your books. You can file a name. You cannot file what a name *means* to somebody."

The dark gathered itself. I felt it deciding — the enormous, grinding slowness of a thing that has never once been contradicted trying to work out whether contradiction is possible.

And then, in the deepest part of it, something moved that was not the dark. Something quiet. Something that felt, impossibly, like a hand resting briefly on top of my head, the way Ada used to when I got an answer right and refused to be smug about it.

Veth. Down here even now, in the place where names are held, keeping watch over the surrendered ones. Keeping Ada's name safe the way you keep a coat for someone who's stepped outside and will be cold when they come back.

The dark yielded. Not defeated — *outmaneuvered*, which is different, and better, and permanent. I felt the terms settle over the whole buried place like snowfall: a debt acknowledged, a creditor present, payment due.

One page, somewhere in the oldest ledger in the world, turned itself over.

And a voice — small, human, familiar, coming from everywhere and nowhere, sounding astonished and annoyed and awake all at once — said:

"...Ines? Why does my mouth taste like fountain pen?"

* * *

I ran toward the voice. Everyone always says you shouldn't run toward strange voices in the dark, and everyone is wrong, because some voices you would cross a stair that doesn't count steps to reach, and some names you scream so hard into the black that the black finally, *finally*, gives them back.

The ember blazed in my hands like a sunrise with a grudge.

Somewhere ahead, in the hole in the middle of Sefa's map, a light was coming on — warm, gold, stubborn, the exact color of a lamp in a guild window — and a shape was standing up in it, brushing off centuries of dark, wearing a face I had fought the whole world not to forget.

"Ada," I said. "You're late. We need to talk about your handwriting."

They squinted at me, this stranger-girl with the tin box, and frowned, and said the words I had walked into hell prepared for:

"Who are you supposed to be?"

I smiled. I had practiced this part too.

"I'm your creditor," I said. "And business," I held up the oldest ledger in the world, which had followed me down, because some books know whose side they're on, "is about to pick up."
