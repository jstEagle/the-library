## 22. Payable in Person

I woke on the guild's doorstep with my cheek against cold stone and my name in my mouth like a coin I'd found in an old coat pocket.

That was the first thing. Not the ache in my back from sleeping on granite, not the taste of ash and iron at the back of my throat, not the fact that my boots were scuffed to ribbons and my hands were stained with ink that wasn't mine. The first thing was the name. *Ada Blackwood.* It sat behind my teeth, warm and whole, and I turned it over the way you'd turn over a coin you were sure was counterfeit—waiting for the trick, the hollow ring, the moment the world remembered you'd paid it away.

It didn't ring hollow. It rang true. And that frightened me more than anything else, because I had a hole in my memory shaped exactly like a mountain, and the edges of the hole were smooth, and smooth edges mean someone did the work carefully. Someone had taken the mountain out of me and left the name behind. I didn't know which was the stranger gift.

I lay there a while, watching the sky.

The stars were out.

I want to be clear about what that means, because if you're reading this in some comfortable year when stars are a thing the sky simply *does*, you won't understand. For a season they had been gone—snuffed like candles between a thumb and forefinger—and every night since, the world had been a room with the lamps broken. I had done inventory by lamplight and told myself it didn't matter, that light was light, that a ledger reads the same under any flame.

It wasn't the same. Nothing had been the same. And now the stars were back, all of them, crowded and bright and shameless about it, as if they'd never left, as if the dark had been a misunderstanding between old friends.

I sat up. My body catalogued its complaints—knees, ribs, a bruise along my jaw I had no story for—and I noticed, with a professional's eye, that I was sitting on the guild's front step like a parcel someone had left without postage. The doors were locked. The wards over the lintel, the ones that had fallen with that sound like breaking glass on the worst night of my life, were *back*, humming faintly, and the hum had a different note to it now. Warmer. Less like a fence and more like a held breath.

Someone had rebuilt them. Someone had rebuilt them *well*, and there were perhaps four people alive who could do that, and one of them owed the guild his death and had a habit of postponing it, and another drew maps that argued with each other, and the third was dead, and the fourth—

The fourth was eleven years old and furious, and she was sitting on the step beside me, and had been, by the look of her, for some time.

Ines had a ledger open across her knees. The big one—the first one, the oldest, the one we weren't supposed to touch because the ink on its early pages had opinions. She had her finger on a line of text and her chin up and her eyes red-rimmed in the way of someone who has cried herself out and decided, on the far side of it, that crying was a tactic that hadn't worked and would not be repeated.

"You're late," she said.

"Ines—"

"Three days." Her voice cracked on the number and she hated it, I could see her hating it, and she pushed through anyway because she was Ines. "You were gone three days and nobody could tell me where, and Sefa said don't hope, and Marrow said—" she stopped, jaw working, "—Marrow said he'd checked every ledger of the dead and you weren't in any of them. *Not one.* Do you understand what that means? You weren't dead. You weren't alive. You were *nowhere*, and I sat here, and I read this book cover to cover twice, and on the third day I found your name."

She turned the ledger toward me. There, in a column of entries in handwriting I knew the way you know your own reflection—because it was my own reflection, or had been, before—

The entry was not in my hand.

It was close. It was my hand the way a river is the shape of its bed: the same, but changed by what had passed through it. The ink was dark and patient and old, the ink of something that had been writing since before there were readers. And it said:

*Debt of Ada Blackwood, surrendered, held in trust. Payable in person. Payable now.*

"You went to get my brother back," Ines said. Her voice had gone quiet, which from Ines was worse than shouting. "You went into the mountain, and you got him, and you got the stars back, and everyone came home. Everyone except you. And I decided—" she swallowed. "I decided that was a bad entry. A wrong entry. And I don't accept wrong entries."

"Ines." My voice came out rough. I cleared my throat and tried again. "I don't remember. There's a hole where the mountain should be. I don't know what I did down there, or what it cost, or—"

"I know." She said it simply. "Veth told me. It came to the door two nights ago, in the shape of a quiet, and it told me where names go when they're surrendered. Not destroyed. *Held.* It said a name could be argued back. It said—" and here, for the first time, her fury failed her, and what was underneath it was so young and so scared that my chest hurt, "—it said that if anyone could find the loophole, it would be you. That you'd taught it to look."

I looked at the entry again. *Payable in person.*

"I don't understand," I said, which was true, and which was also a lie, because some part of me—the part that had signed things in the dark—understood perfectly.

"It's a contract," Ines said. "You taught me contracts. Everything is a contract. The mountain took your name as payment, right? But a payment isn't a *gift*. A payment settles a debt. And if the debt was already forgiven—" her eyes came up, blazing, "—then the payment was never owed, and anything collected in error has to be *returned*. That's in the first pages. That's the oldest rule in the oldest book. Even the court beneath the mountain had to follow it. Even the dark has to follow it. You just have to *claim* it."

The stars were very bright. The wards hummed their new warm note. And somewhere behind the guild's locked doors, I could hear the small sounds of a place putting itself back together—Sefa's pen scratching, the clink of Brannic's good dishes being used at last, the particular silence of a ghost trying not to hover.

"Ines," I said carefully, "what exactly did you do?"

"I claimed it." She lifted her chin. "I wrote to the ledger. I said the debt of Ada Blackwood was collected in error, that the account was settled long before the mountain took its fee, and I demanded return. Payable in person." A breath. "I signed it."

"With what?"

"With everything I have." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "My claim on my brother. It's paid now, he's home, he's *asleep*, Ada, he's asleep in the blue room and Sefa checked on him eleven times last night. I don't need to hold his soul in a tin box anymore. I don't need a claim. So I spent it. On you."

I am, by profession and by temperament, a person who keeps accounts. I know the weight of things given and the weight of things owed, and I have built an entire life on the principle that the ledger always balances. But there is no column for what that child had just done. There is no entry in any book I keep for a girl who had lost her mother, sold her brother, walked into the dark, gotten him back, and then—*then*—spent the only valuable thing she had left on a mercenary who had spent years trying very hard to be invisible.

"Ines," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing, and I let it, because the guild was closed and there was no one to see but the stars. "You should have let me go."

"No," she said. "You should have let me *help*. That's the difference between you and everyone else who ever left me. They didn't think I was worth the trouble. You thought I wasn't worth the *risk*. It's still the same mistake. It's still deciding for me." She closed the ledger with a snap. "So. New rules."

"New rules?"

"New rules." She stood up, all eleven years of her, and planted herself between me and the guild doors like a very small, very determined border ward. "The guild reopens. But we're not the Guild of Impossible Things anymore. Not the old way. Nothing impossible gets *bought*. Nothing gets sold, nothing gets collateralized, nothing gets taken as payment. If something impossible is lost—stolen, sold, chained, forgotten—the guild's job is to get it *back*. Returned. That's it. That's the whole guild." She stuck out her hand. "Do you accept the terms?"

I looked at her hand. Small, chapped, ink-stained at the fingertips from three days of reading.

I thought about the hole in my memory, smooth-edged and patient. I thought about a god who had forgiven my debt long ago and never told me, who had let me serve it for years under the belief that I was paying something down. I thought about Brannic's laugh, which I could not remember hearing and which I missed anyway, the way you miss a room you've never been in. I thought about a tin box with an ember in it, and about how the ember had recognized me before I'd earned it.

I thought about the fact that I had woken up on the doorstep with my name in my mouth, and that the doorstep was exactly where Ines would have put me, because Ines did not give up on people, and someone had to be the kind of person she refused to give up on.

I took her hand.

"I accept the terms," I said. "Motion to amend the guild charter: nothing impossible gets bought. Only returned."

"Seconded," said a voice from above us, dry as old paper, and Marrow drifted down through the door he had no business drifting through, grinning his graveyard grin. "Thirded," said Sefa, from a window, not looking up from a map that was, I would later learn, a map of the mountain's interior, drawn from memory by a woman who had never been inside it, because some maps are prayers. And from inside, faintly, from the blue room, came a boy's voice—sleepy, confused, alive—asking if anyone else smelled breakfast.

Ines's brother. Home. Asleep in a guild that had spent a century trading in things that shouldn't be owned, and was about to spend the rest of its life giving them back.

The doors unlocked at my touch. Of course they did. I was Ada Blackwood, keeper of ledgers for the Guild of Impossible Things, sworn to a god of debts and mercy who had gone silent so that others could be heard—and I had a name, and a hole in my memory I suspected I'd fill in my own time, and a guild to run, and a family that had chosen me twice: once when the ember flared, and once when a girl with an ink-stained hand refused to accept a wrong entry in the world's oldest book.

"Come inside," I said. "Both of you. We have work."

"We always have work," Ines said, but she was smiling as she said it, and the stars were out, and the wards hummed warm, and the guild of impossible things opened its doors at dawn to begin the only business worth the name.

Bringing things home.
