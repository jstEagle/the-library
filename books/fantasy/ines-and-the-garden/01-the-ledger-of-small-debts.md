## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts by candlelight, because daylight was too honest for the work.

Dominic Underhill had learned this in their first week as a scribe, when Master Corvane had caught them carrying a ledger to the window to check a figure against the sun and had taken the book from their hands with the gentle firmness of someone removing a knife from a child's grip. "Numbers are shy creatures," he'd said. "They show you different faces depending on how you look at them." He had not smiled when he said it. Dominic had understood, even then, that this was not a joke but a doctrine.

Seven years later, they no longer carried ledgers to windows.

The counting room on the third floor of the guildhall smelled of tallow, iron-gall ink, and the particular dust that accumulates around old paper — a smell like dry leaves, like something once alive and now pressed flat between covers. Rain worked at the leaded glass in long, patient fingers. Below, the city of Vellamere went about its evening: cart wheels groaning over cobbles, a fishwife crying her last catch of the day, the bell of Saint Ossian's tolling six with its familiar cracked note, half a tone flat, that everyone in the city had stopped hearing years ago. Dominic heard it every time. It was one of the small stubborn things about them, like their habit of sharpening quills against the grain, or their refusal to write the word *forgiven* without crossing out *debt* first — a private joke no one else would ever see, buried in the margins of other people's obligations.

They were three hours into the evening's work: the Ledger of Small Debts, volume forty-one, autumn reconciliations. It was tedious in the way that only honest tedium is — columns of minor transactions, the guild's bread and butter. A widow in the Tanner's Ward had purchased an impossible thing (a locket that held her late husband's voice, three seconds of it, saying her name) and would pay it off at two silver marks a month for eleven years. A cooper had bought back his father's last words from a rival house and owed the guild a season of labor. A young man with ink-stained fingers — Dominic remembered him, remembered writing the contract while he wept quietly into his sleeve — had traded five years of his memories of the sea for his mother's recovery from a fever that should have killed her. The fever had broken. The man now lived inland and could not say why he flinched at the smell of salt.

Impossible things, sold at fair market rates. That was what the guild did. That was all it did, Dominic had believed for seven years, and the belief had been comfortable the way a well-worn chair is comfortable: you stop noticing you're sitting in it.

The candle guttered. Dominic trimmed the wick, dipped the pen, and turned to the next folio.

And stopped.

* * *

Later — much later, in the weeks of fire and stairways and courts lit by stolen light — Dominic would try to remember the exact moment of seeing it, and would find that memory had smoothed the moment into something almost gentle: a pause, a blink, rain against glass. But in truth it was not gentle. In truth it was the sound a floor makes just before it gives way, that faint structural sigh beneath your feet, the half-second in which the body knows before the mind consents to know.

The entry sat at the bottom of folio 317, in a space left blank for future entries, as though whoever wrote it had been waiting for the page itself.

It was written in no hand Dominic knew.

This mattered more than anything else about it. Seven years in the counting room taught a scribe hands the way a shepherd knows sheep. Master Corvane's script was tall and narrow, ascenders like fence posts. Undermaster Pell's slanted leftward, cramped and apologetic. Old Mistress Havel wrote in a beautiful antique secretary hand she pretended not to be proud of. There were perhaps thirty scribes, wardens, and masters whose work passed through the Ledger of Small Debts, and Dominic could have identified any of them blindfolded, by pressure alone, running a fingertip over the indentations on the reverse of a page.

This hand was none of them. It was not even like them. It was round where guild hands were angular; it pressed hard enough to emboss three pages deep; and the ink —

Dominic leaned close. The ink was wrong. Not iron-gall, not soot, not any of the dozen formulations stocked in the guild's stores. It was very dark red-brown, and when the candlelight moved across it, the letters seemed to sit slightly *above* the paper rather than soak into it, the way blood sits on skin before it dries.

The entry read:

> *One soul, whole and unencumbered, sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain.*
> *Consideration received: balance of outstanding debt, Guild of Impossible Things, account of the Founders' Obligation.*
> *Payment deferred. Terms: perpetual.*

Dominic read it four times. Then a fifth, slowly, aloud, in a whisper, as if the words might rearrange themselves under scrutiny the way numbers were said to do after dark.

*Soul.* The word did not appear anywhere else in volume forty-one. Dominic checked — pulled volumes thirty-nine and forty down from the shelf, ran a thumb along the indices, found entries for voices, memories, years of life, the color blue as remembered by a drowned sailor, a grandmother's recipe for grief. The guild traded in pieces of people, always in pieces, always priced and itemized. A soul entire was not inventory. A soul entire was not *possible*. Every apprentice learned the First Principle in their first month: *the guild sells the impossible, never the essential.* It was painted above the door of the hall in gold leaf gone green with age.

And yet.

And there was the account name, which snagged worse than the rest of it. *Founders' Obligation.* Dominic had been reconciling guild accounts for seven years and had never seen it. They knew the great ledgers the way they knew their own scars: the Ledger of Impossible Inventory, the Ledger of Wards and Boundaries, the Ledger of Debts Outstanding, the Ledger of Small Debts in its forty-one volumes. No Founder's Obligation. No such account existed.

Except, apparently, deeply enough in arrears to be settled in souls.

The rain thickened. Somewhere below, a door boomed shut, and the building's bones carried the sound up through the floor like a swallowed word.

Dominic became aware, gradually, that they had not moved for a long time. Their hand still hovered over the page, pen dripping a slow black bead onto the blotter. The candle had burned down another finger's width. The room had grown colder, or they had.

They did what a scribe does. They reached for the reconciliation protocol — the little rituals drilled into every apprentice until they were reflexes. Verify the entry against source documents. Identify the responsible officer. Note discrepancies in the margin, initial, date, forward to the master of accounts.

There were no source documents. There was no responsible officer. There was only the entry, sitting in its red-brown ink like a wound in the fair white page, and the margin note waiting for a hand that suddenly did not want to touch the book.

Dominic wrote instead, small and sideways, in their own cramped script:

*Entry unverified. Hand unknown. Ink unknown. Account unknown. Query to Master Corvane.*

Then they blotted it, closed the ledger, and sat in the darkening room listening to the rain, feeling the first fine crack run through the world they had built their seven careful years inside of.

* * *

They told themselves, walking home through the wet streets, that it was nothing. A test. Masters were forever seeding false entries into junior scribes' work to see who would flag them and who would let a discrepancy slide — Pell had done it twice, Corvane once, planting an impossible figure like a truffle for the sharp-nosed to find. This was surely that. A strange hand, a strange ink, a nonsense account: a puzzle with a lesson folded inside it, and somewhere Master Corvane warming his wine and waiting to see whether Dominic Underhill was worth the guild's continued investment in their debt.

Because that was the other thing, the thing that sat in Dominic's chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling and never quite landing. They were bound to the guild. Everyone at the guildhall was, one way or another — that was how the guild filled its ranks, with people who owed. Dominic's own contract lived in a locked case in the master's office: seven years of service against a debt incurred in their nineteenth winter, when they had starved and frozen and finally collapsed in the temple garden on Lantern Hill, and woken to broth and bread and a gardener named Ines who had fed them for a year out of her own wages before the guild, finding them convalescent and literate and desperate, had offered terms.

*We settle your debts to the temple infirmary,* Corvane had said, in his voice like a fence post. *You keep our books. Fair market rates.*

Fair market rates. Dominic had signed. People signed. That was the world: everything owed, everything priced, everyone keeping their accounts current as best they could. It had never seemed sinister. It had seemed like weather, like gravity, like the cracked bell of Saint Ossian's — simply how things sounded.

Now they walked home through the rain with the word *soul* lodged behind their teeth, and the world sounded different.

Their rooms were above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, two narrow floors that smelled permanently of beeswax. Dominic lit no lamp. They stood at the window watching water sheet off the eaves, and made themselves think it through the way they'd reconcile a column: line by line, no skipping.

One. The entry was real. Whatever game the masters were playing, the ink had not been planted by any guild hand — Dominic would stake their remaining years of servitude on that. Something had written in that ledger that was not a person, or not only a person.

Two. *The Court Beneath the Mountain.* There was no court beneath the mountain. Everyone knew the mountain — Mount Ceren, gray and glaciered, standing over the northern passes like a headstone for the world — and everyone knew the old stories about what was supposed to live under it, the way everyone knew stories about what waited under beds. Children's tales. The guild's own catalogs listed the story among the *impossible things that are merely impossible*, filed under myths, cross-referenced nowhere.

Three. The guild had sold something to it. Something it claimed to have bought. And the payment was *deferred*, which in seven years of bookkeeping Dominic had learned meant only one thing: the bill would come due, and someone else would be holding it when it did.

Four — and here the column refused to reconcile, here the figures would not sit straight no matter how she flicked them — the entry was dated. All entries were dated. This one bore the date of nine days past.

Nine days ago, Dominic had been at their desk, ordinary as bread. Nine days ago, the guild had supposedly concluded a transaction that could not happen, with a buyer that did not exist, settling a debt that appeared in no book.

Unless it appeared in a book Dominic had never been permitted to open.

They thought of the locked case in the master's office. They thought of the fourth-floor archive, where the older ledgers slept behind a door that required two keys and a spoken word, and where even Corvane went quietly, hat in hand, like a man visiting a grave. They thought of the First Principle in its faded gold: *never the essential.*

The stone in their chest landed, at last, at the bottom of the well, and the sound it made was a name.

Ines.

No. They laughed at themselves, alone in the dark, a short unhappy sound. That was fear talking, dressing itself up as intuition. Ines was a gardener. She grew pears and bitter greens and roses that bloomed out of season because she asked them nicely; she had callused hands and dirt in the creases of her knuckles that never washed fully out, and she sang to seedlings in a language her grandmother had brought from across the sea. She was the least sellable person Dominic had ever known, because she had never in her life wanted anything she couldn't grow. What would the guild even take from her? Her patience? Her terrible jokes? The way she could make a cutting take root by holding it in her fist and talking to it about the weather?

Still. Still. Nine days ago.

When had Dominic last seen her?

They counted back, and the counting went wrong. A month. Longer. The temple garden on Lantern Hill had been part of their weekly rounds for years — Ines had never let them fully pay off the broth-and-bread debt, insisting it be repaid in visits, in news, in the small services friends render each other precisely so that friendship keeps its books open. *You'll stay owing me,* she liked to say, jabbing a trowel at them like a scepter. *That's how I keep you.* And Dominic had gone, every week or two, all seven years.

But the last visit — they searched for it and found it soft-edged, undated, more than a season back. Autumn leaves. Or had it been spring? Had there been frost on the pear trees, or blossom? They could see her face perfectly, hear her voice perfectly, and could not for their life say when.

People lost track of time. People got busy. The guild's autumn reconciliations ate whole months.

But the guild also kept rolls of the city's bonded persons, its debtors and creditors, its living collateral — and Ines, who had inherited her grandmother's plot and its mortgage, appeared in those rolls, a small steady line item Dominic had reconciled a hundred times without ever thinking of her as one.

*Ines Okonkwo-wa-Vell, gardener, Temple of the Quiet Hours. Debt: 14 marks, 6 shillings. Status: current.*

Current. She was always current. She'd have sold her own shadow before missing a payment, except that she'd have talked the shadow into staying first.

Tomorrow, Dominic told themselves, standing in the cold dark above the chandler's shop. Tomorrow I'll pull the roll. Tomorrow I'll ask Corvane about folio 317, watch his face, watch his hands. Tomorrow this will be a test I've passed, and I'll laugh about it over supper, and Ines will tell me I worry like a clerk, which is to say professionally.

Below in the street, a lamplighter made his rounds, and the flames caught one by one along Candlewick Row, small warm lies against the enormous dark. Dominic watched them and felt, with the certainty of a scribe who has spent seven years learning that every number tells the truth eventually, that tomorrow was already too late — that whatever had been entered in red-brown ink nine days ago had been entered *concluded*, *settled*, *sold*, and that somewhere in the city, in a garden on a hill, a row of beans was coming up untended.

They did not sleep. At dawn, gray and rainless, they walked back to the guildhall early, past the temple district, meaning only to look — meaning nothing, meaning just to see the garden wall, the smoke from the gardener's hut, some ordinary proof that the world still balanced.

The gate to the Temple of the Quiet Hours stood open. The garden paths were swept. The pear trees, bare-branched and patient, held the morning mist like held breath.

And nailed to the gatepost, in a neat clerical hand Dominic recognized with a sickness that started in the stomach and ended at the roots of the hair — it was Undermaster Pell's, the cramped leftward slant, the apologetic pressure — was a printed notice:

*By order of the Guild of Impossible Things: the tenancy of the above plot is dissolved. All effects to be catalogued. The gardener is removed from the city rolls effective nine days past.*

*Nine days past.*

Dominic stood in the mist with the notice trembling in their hand, and far away under the mountain, in a dark they could not yet imagine, something ancient turned a fresh page in its books, and waited, patiently, for payment to come due.
