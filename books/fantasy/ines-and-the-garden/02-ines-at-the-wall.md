## 2. Ines at the Wall

The city rolls were kept in a room that smelled of tallow and old rain, three floors below the guildhall's grand archive, where the clerks who could not be trusted with impossible things were trusted instead with ordinary ones. Dominic had spent four years among those ordinary ledgers — births, deaths, trades, taxes, the slow sediment of a city that believed itself unremarkable. It was good work for hiding in. Nobody looked twice at a scribe who knew where everything was written.

Dominic went down on the morning after finding the entry, carrying a candle stub and a lie prepared for the under-clerk: an audit of residency records, routine, nothing to see. The lie was unnecessary. The under-clerk was asleep over his own ledger, ink drying on his cheek like a small black scar, and Dominic stepped past him into the stacks without disturbing so much as the dust.

The rolls were organized by parish, then by street, then by the alphabet of ordinary lives. Temple Ward came near the end, its pages soft from handling, because Temple Ward was poor and poor parishes moved often — people arriving, people leaving, people being carried out feet first when the fever took them. Dominic knew these pages the way some people know psalms. They had read them before, idly, in the early months of their employment, looking for their own name out of a superstition they never admitted to. *If I am written somewhere, then I am somewhere.*

They found the Ines entries quickly. There were only four.

*Ines Vael, gardener, admitted to Temple Ward, spring census.* That one was eleven years old, the ink gone brown as dried blood. Then two more censuses, each a single line, each noting her trade in the same clerkish hand: *gardener.* And then nothing. The most recent roll — the one compiled this past autumn — simply did not have her. Dominic ran a finger down the column of names twice, three times, the way a person touches a tooth that has begun to ache, hoping to find the hurt is imagined.

She was not there. There was no death record. No departure record. No transfer to another parish. In a city that wrote down everything — that was the whole point of the Guild of Impossible Things, that nothing should happen which had not first been recorded — Ines Vael had simply stopped existing on paper, the way a candle stops existing when you pinch it.

Dominic stood in the cold of the records room with the candle guttering and felt something shift in their chest, some load-bearing grief giving way at last, and behind it — behind the grief — a colder thing beginning to move.

Because there was no such thing, in this city, as an unwritten absence. Someone had removed her. And Dominic knew exactly who kept the pens sharp enough to erase a life cleanly.

---

The first time Dominic met Ines, they had been stealing.

Not well. Not even competently. They had been nineteen and starving in the way that makes thought narrow to a corridor with one door at the end, and they had climbed the temple garden wall on the south side of Temple Ward because the wall was low there and the garden beyond it grew things: apples, late plums, beans climbing strings, greens in beds dark as velvet. Dominic had not eaten properly in six days. They remembered — would always remember — the exact quality of the hunger, how it had stopped feeling like want and started feeling like weather, something happening *to* them rather than *in* them.

They got one hand on the wall's coping stone and a voice said, from directly below, "You'll want the ladder. The wall crumbles on that side and I'm the one who has to mend it."

Ines stood among the bean rows with dirt to her elbows and a knife in her belt for cutting stems, and she did not look alarmed or amused or anything in between. She looked at Dominic the way she might have looked at a weed whose root system she was already assessing.

"I wasn't—" Dominic began.

"You were," she said. "It's all right. Hunger's honest. It's everything else people lie about." She tilted her head. "Can you work?"

"I — what?"

"Work. Hands. Can you use them for something besides climbing walls?" She flexed her own fingers, earth cracking in the creases. "I've more beds than back. You help me with the harvest, you eat. You eat well. You come back tomorrow, same terms. That's the whole contract. No paper."

Later — much later, in the warm middle years of knowing her — Dominic would learn that Ines distrusted paper with a ferocity she applied to almost nothing else. *Paper is where promises go to be forgotten,* she said once, thinning carrots with quick precise pulls. *A promise should live in a body. Bodies remember. Paper lets everyone off.* At the time, nineteen and chewing the first honest apple in a week, juice running down their wrist, Dominic had only nodded and meant it as agreement rather than philosophy.

But the body remembered. That was the thing about Ines. Everything with her lived in the body.

She taught them to prune by putting their hands on the branch and hers over theirs, so they learned the cut through their palms before their heads understood it. She taught them the smell of soil that was healthy versus soil that was merely wet — one alive, faintly sweet, like bread beginning; the other dead and mineral, a grave pretending to be a bed. She taught them that you talk to plants not because they hear but because it keeps *you* honest about what you're doing to them. She worked from before dawn until the light failed and sang under her breath while she worked, tuneless, half-swallowed songs that Dominic later realized were prayers in a form worn smooth enough to pass for humming.

And every evening of that year, she fed them. Beans and barley. Apples baked with honey when the tree gave generously. Once, extravagantly, a fish she'd traded cuttings for, cooked over the garden's little fire with the smoke going up through the pear branches into a sky that still, in those days, had all its stars.

"Why do you do this?" Dominic asked her once, in the hungriest month, when they had begun to be afraid the arrangement would end — afraid the way you fear waking. "You don't know me. I stole from you."

Ines was tying up tomato vines, and she didn't stop working to answer, which was her way; important things she said sideways, to her hands.

"You didn't steal from me," she said. "You stole from a wall. The garden feeds whoever's hungry. That's what a garden *is*. Everything else — the temple claiming it, the priests blessing it, the tithe baskets — that's all just people standing between the hungry and the food, taking their cut." She snapped a sucker off the vine and flicked it onto the compost heap. "I'm not doing charity. I'm doing maintenance. A garden that doesn't feed people is just decoration, and I don't keep decorations."

Then she'd looked at them — really looked, the assessing look, root-system look — and added, quieter: "Besides. You've got hands that want work and eyes that count things. You'll be dangerous someday. Better fed than desperate."

She laughed when she said it, so it sounded like a joke. It was not a joke. Nothing Ines ever said was entirely a joke, and Dominic understood this too late, the way one understands everything about gardens: that the planting happened seasons before the harvest, that the deepest roots are invisible, that what looks like idleness underground is the whole work.

---

"Dominic."

The under-clerk's voice, close, made them flinch hard enough to rattle the shelf. He was awake, rubbing the ink scar off his cheek, squinting at them through the gloom with the mild suspicion of a man who had slept through worse.

"Audit," Dominic said. "Residency rolls. Master's orders."

"Mm." He peered at the open book, at the parish name, at the gap in the column where a line should have been. For one terrible moment Dominic was certain he saw it too — the absence, the pinched-out candle. But he only yawned. "Temple Ward. Lots of churn there since the wards started flickering. People leave. Can't blame them."

"People leave *records*," Dominic said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "There's no departure entry. No death. That's irregular."

He shrugged, the shrug of a man paid to copy, not to care. "Talk to the ward registrar, then. If you can find him. Half the ward's registrar offices closed last month." He wandered back to his desk, and Dominic stood alone in the stacks with the candle burning low and the truth assembling itself piece by piece in the dark, patient as mold.

Here was what they knew, laid out now in the clerk's order their mind insisted on: Ines had been absent from the autumn census. There was no record of leaving. The border wards — the great wards, the ones the guild maintained, the ones the whole city's safety was mortgaged against — had been flickering for weeks, and everyone spoke of it in the lowered voice reserved for weather and plague. And last night, in the master ledger, in a hand no scribe of the guild would own to, Dominic had found a single line: *one soul, sold, payment deferred.*

At the time, reading it, they had thought: *whose? Some stranger. Some debtor. Some name I don't know.* That had been the horror of it — abstract, architectural, a crack in a wall they'd assumed was solid everywhere.

Now the abstraction had a face. Dirt to the elbows. Tuneless singing. An apple pressed into a starving hand with the words *eat, we'll talk after.*

Dominic closed the roll with exaggerated gentleness, the gentleness of fury, and carried the candle back up through the guildhall's sleeping corridors, past the archive doors, past the sealed room where the masters kept the inventory of impossible things, and stopped at the window at the end of the hall where the city lay spread out below.

Temple Ward was visible from here — or its roofline was, the temple's old dome and, beside it, the low green smudge of the garden wall. Even at this distance, even at night, Dominic's eye went to it the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth. They had not visited in months. Work, they'd told themselves. Debt, they'd told themselves — the debt, the binding debt, the one the guild held on them like a leash, the reason they were a scribe at all and not whatever else they might have become. *When the debt clears,* they had promised themselves, and then, *when this quarter ends,* and then, *soon.*

The garden wall stood green-black under the sickly flicker of the wards' light, and it looked, from three floors up, exactly as it had looked on the worst night of Dominic's life, when a voice from among the bean rows said *you'll want the ladder* and meant *you'll want to live.*

They thought about the entry again. *One soul, sold, payment deferred.* Deferred — that word had snagged in them even last night, clerk-brain catching on an irregularity. Debts were paid or defaulted; *deferred* meant someone was waiting. Meant the transaction wasn't finished. Meant—

Meant that if the guild had sold Ines, they had sold her recently, and recently was recoverable. Recently could be followed.

Dominic pressed their forehead briefly against the cold glass, and made the kind of promise that lives in a body, because bodies remember, because a woman who distrusted paper had taught them where promises belong.

Below, far off toward the mountain's shoulder, the wards flickered — once, twice — and for a heartbeat the whole sky stuttered like a lamp about to fail.

Somewhere beneath the guildhall, in a locked case, the master ledger waited with its page of no-hand writing. And somewhere in Temple Ward, a garden stood untended, its beds going to seed, its gate unlatched, as if it were waiting too.

Dominic turned from the window and went to find out which of the masters had signed the autumn census — and who had ordered the line struck.
