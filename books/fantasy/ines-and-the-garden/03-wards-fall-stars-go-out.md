## 3. Wards Fall, Stars Go Out

The night the stars went out, Dominic was balancing a column of candle-wax expenditures, and the last thing they felt before the world broke was irritation.

That was the thing about the Guild of Impossible Things: it made tedium out of miracle. Dominic had spent six years learning that. The guildhall's eastern wing, where the junior scribes worked, smelled of tallow and old glue and the particular sourness of ink left too long in a cold room. Outside the tall windows, the city of Vell lay spread in its winter dark, and above the city the border wards burned in their slow, patient way — a ring of pale gold light that had hung over the mountain passes since before anyone living had been born. Scribes were taught, in their first week, to stop seeing it. *The wards are like the sky,* Master Corvane liked to say. *You do not thank the sky for being overhead.*

Dominic had stopped seeing it years ago. They were seeing it now only because the column of figures would not balance — off by three coppers, again, the third time this month — and they had looked up in pure frustration to curse the window, the light, the night.

And the ring of gold was flickering.

Not the way candlelight flickers. Not the way anything flickers. It was more like breathing — a long, slow contraction, as if the whole luminous circle had inhaled and was holding, holding, holding —

The column of figures slid out of Dominic's mind entirely.

They stood. Their chair went over backward and the crack of it against the floorboards was very loud in a room where forty other scribes were still bent over their desks, still scratching, still asleep at their work without knowing they were asleep.

"Corvane," Dominic said. Their voice came out strange, flattened. "Master Corvane. The wards."

The old man did not look up. That was the first wrongness, though Dominic would only understand it later: that the masters had not been surprised. Corvane's pen moved on, steady as a clock, across some document Dominic could not see the face of.

The wards exhaled.

There was no sound, exactly. Or rather, there was a sound, but it did not arrive through the ears. It arrived through the teeth, through the fluid of the eyes, through the wet architecture of the inner body — a *pop*, vast and intimate, like the world's own ear clearing after a long descent. The ring of gold did not shatter so much as *come apart*, unbraiding itself into a thousand thousand threads of light that hung in the air over the mountain for one impossible, beautiful moment, like a loom with its warp cut, like hair unbinding, like —

Then every star in the sky went out.

Not dimmed. Not veiled by cloud. *Out*, all of them at once, the whole burning census of heaven cancelled in a single stroke, and the dark that came in behind them was not the dark of night. Night has texture. Night has the grey suggestion of horizon, the amber stain of the city at its edges, the honest small lights of lamps and windows. This was a darkness with weight and temperature, a darkness like water closing over the head, and Dominic heard — distantly, from the direction of the docks, from the direction of the temple district, from a thousand throats at once — the city begin to scream.

* * *

The guildhall came apart in an orderly fashion, which was somehow worse than if it had come apart in panic.

Dominic stood at the window watching the dark eat the sky, and around them the scriptorium emptied in silence. Forty scribes rose, gathered their coats, and filed toward the doors in the trained, shuffling quiet of people who have been taught their whole lives that questions are a form of debt. No one screamed. No one asked. They simply left, and the great room's lamps guttered in the draft, and Dominic was alone with the wax-ledger and its three unaccounted coppers and the absolute blackness where the stars had been.

Then the masters began to burn things.

It started in the west wing — a smell first, sharp and resinous, and then a light that was wrong in a way Dominic's body understood before their mind did, because it came from *below*, thrown upward against the corridor walls, and fire in the guildhall moved downward, always downward, toward the stone hearths, never up. Dominic went toward it. Later they would not be able to say why — cowardice and courage wear the same shoes at the beginning.

The archive room was already thick with smoke. Master Corvane stood at the great iron brazier with his sleeves pinned back, feeding documents into the flame with the same unhurried precision he brought to everything, and behind him, in ranks, the other masters were pulling ledgers from the shelves — the *real* ledgers, the ones kept behind the grate, the ones junior scribes were told were merely duplicates — and opening them to the fire one by one.

"You should be at your desk, Underhill," Corvane said, without turning.

"The stars are out, Master."

"Yes." He fed another page to the flames. Dominic caught a glimpse of it as it curled — columns of figures, a date, and a word written in a hand they did not know, a word that looked like *deferred* — and then it was ash. "You'll want to note the discrepancy in the morning. Three coppers, was it? It's always three coppers. The ledger rounds what the heart can't."

"Master Corvane." Dominic's voice did something they had never heard it do: it cracked, and kept going anyway. "What did we *do*?"

For the first time the old man turned. Firelight climbed his face, and Dominic had looked at that face for six years — dry, exact, faintly disappointed, a face like a closed ledger — and it was the same face, that was the horror of it, nothing in it had changed, nothing in it was ever going to change.

"We kept the accounts," he said. "That is all the guild has ever done. We keep the accounts, Underhill. Someone must."

"The *wards* —"

"The wards were an expenditure." He said it the way he said *candle-wax*, the way he said *binding-thread*, the way he had said a hundred small words over a hundred small columns while Dominic sat three desks away learning arithmetic. "Everything is an expenditure. The wards were simply the largest line item, and tonight the account has come due, and we are — " he glanced, almost tenderly, at the burning shelves — "tidying."

Behind him, two masters wrestled a chest toward the back of the room, toward the door that should not have been there.

Dominic had worked in this wing for six years. They knew its walls the way they knew the backs of their own hands, the way a scribe knows anything — by the marks of it, the seams, the water stains, the places where the plaster had been patched. There was no door in the back wall of the archive. There was a map of the northern aquifers in a frame, and below it a shelf of obsolete tax schedules, and that was all there had ever been.

But there it was now: a door of black wood without hinges or handle, standing slightly ajar, and from the gap came a draft that did not smell of smoke or stone or anything that belonged to the surface of the world. It smelled of deep water and old metal and, faintly, unbearably, of *garden* — of loam and green and growing things — which made no sense at all, and which made Dominic's eyes fill with tears they could not explain.

"Where does that go?" they whispered.

Corvane looked at the door the way a man looks at a debt collector.

"Down," he said.

* * *

They opened it all the way, and things came up.

Dominic did not stay to see them clearly. They remembered afterward only fragments, the way one remembers a fever: a procession of masters carrying the chest through the black door; a cold that rolled out ahead of it like water from a breached hull; the sound the door made closing, which was not the sound of wood but of a mouth finishing a word. And in the corridor, as they fled, they passed the guildmaster herself — old Mother Ashe, who had signed Dominic's indenture with a hand so light it barely dented the paper — standing in the great hall beneath the guild's motto, the one carved in stone over every door: *NOTHING IS IMPOSSIBLE; SOME THINGS ARE MERELY EXPENSIVE.*

She was watching the sky through the clerestory windows, where there was nothing now to watch. Her hands were folded. Her face was calm. And she was speaking — softly, rhythmically, in the cadence of recitation — and as Dominic passed close enough to hear, the words resolved, and they were the words of an audit.

*"...received: one ward, greater, sixty years' term, paid in full. Received: one light, celestial, quantity forty-one, condition good. Received: one name, worn, resale value negligible. Received: one soul, gardener's trade, payment deferred —"*

Dominic stopped walking.

The hall tilted. Not really — the stones did not move — but something in Dominic tilted, some inner floor whose level they had trusted all their life, and for a moment they were falling in place, in the middle of the burning guildhall, in the middle of the starless dark.

*A soul, gardener's trade.*

Ines had hands that smelled of thyme even in winter. Ines had fed them bread and stewed nettles through the hungriest year of their life and had never once asked when they would pay it back, because Ines did not believe in debt, Ines had said so, laughing, dirt to the elbows — *the garden doesn't keep books, Dom, it just grows* — and Ines had been missing from the city rolls for nine days, and the guild's ledger, read aloud in the voice of the guildmaster, said *received: one soul, gardener's trade, payment deferred.*

The guild had not lost the stars.

The guild had *spent* them.

* * *

Dominic did not remember crossing the hall. They remembered the cold of the flagstones through their shoes, the motto over the door, the way the smoke moved in the clerestory light like something swimming. They remembered that their body had made the decision before they had, the way a body flinches from a blade, and that when they came back to themselves they were standing at the archive door with the grate-key in their hand — the junior scribes' key, the one that was only supposed to open the duplicate ledgers, the one Corvane had never bothered to change because who would dare.

Behind them, in the west wing, the fires were spreading now, genuinely spreading, past the braziers and into the shelves, and the masters had gone through the black door and the door was gone and the wall was only a wall again with a map of the northern aquifers hanging on it, and the guildhall was empty of everyone but the dead ledgers and the living fire and Dominic Underhill, debt-bound, indentured, three coppers short and now some vast and uncountable amount more.

The grate was locked. The key turned. The great ledgers sat in their ranks, and Dominic pulled the master ledger down — it weighed like a sin — and carried it to the window, because the light was better there, or because some part of them wanted to be able to throw it into the street and run, and opened it with hands that would not stop shaking.

The entries were in no hand they knew. That was the thing. Six years in the scriptorium and Dominic knew every clerk's fist in the guild, could identify Corvane's cramped precision or Mother Ashe's floating hand from a single word, and these pages were written in a script that was not any of theirs — too old, too even, letters like rows of planted seeds — and every entry was the same shape:

*Received: [one thing impossible]. Paid: [one thing that should not be for sale].*

*Received: one bridge that stands in two rivers at once. Paid: the memory of the mason's daughter, entire.*

*Received: one winter that did not come. Paid: forty years from the life of the lamplighter's guild, apportioned.*

*Received: one ward, greater. Paid: —*

And there, on the newest page, the ink still smelling of the brazier's heat, the entry Dominic had heard read aloud in the great hall, now seen whole, with its full terms, its date, its seal — a seal like a root seen from below, like something grown rather than stamped:

*Received: one soul, gardener's trade, sound, willing. Paid: the debt of D. Underhill, scribe, second year, principal and interest, in full.*

Dominic read it four times.

The words did not change. That was the cruelty of words; Dominic of all people knew it, had built their whole life on it — words stay where you put them, they hold still while the world burns, and this one held still, this one sat in its row of planted letters and refused to mean anything else:

*the debt of D. Underhill.*

Ines had not been taken. Ines had been *spent*. Six years ago, when Dominic had lain fevered in the temple almshouse with the guild's physicians shrugging and the guild's fees climbing, when the indenture had doubled and redoubled and every scribe in the eastern wing had said *no one survives that bill* — someone had paid it. Someone had walked into the guildhall with dirt under her nails and no belief in debt, and had paid it, and the guild had taken the only thing she had, and filed it, and deferred it, and waited nine days, and sold it *down*.

The stars had not gone out for the city's sins.

They had gone out for Dominic's.

Somewhere below the floor, faint as a heartbeat, something knocked — once, twice — as if the mountain itself had turned over in its sleep. The fire was at the archive door now. Dominic could feel it on their neck, hear the old tax schedules popping like green wood, and they had perhaps a minute, perhaps less, before the master ledger and everything it proved became ash and the only witness left in the world was a scribe with a bad memory for faces they'd sold.

They did not think. Thinking was for people whose worlds were still standing.

They gripped the page — Ines's page, *the debt of D. Underhill, in full* — and tore.

The ledger resisted. Old paper, guild paper, paper that had been paid for, and it fought the way everything the guild owned fought, with a low grinding reluctance, and then it came free with a sound like a small bone breaking, and Dominic had it, one page, one proof of claim, one soul's receipt — and the fire reached the shelves, and the smoke leaned down over them like a hand, and through the black window the starless dark pressed against the glass from the outside, patient as a creditor.

Dominic ran.

Behind them the archive burned. Ahead of them the city screamed. And in their fist, crumpled and warm as a living thing, the torn page said what it had always said, in its patient planted letters, waiting to be read aloud somewhere far below:

*Paid: the debt of D. Underhill, in full.*
