## 4. What the Guild Trades

The guildhall did not sleep so much as it held its breath.

Dominic had learned this in their first year, when they were still young enough to believe the building was merely old. The Guild of Impossible Things kept hours like a monastery and secrets like a bank, and between the last bell of evening and the first grey suggestion of dawn, the corridors settled into a silence that was not empty but *attentive*. The stones listened. The portraits of dead masters — painted, every one, with their eyes slightly averted, as if they could not quite bear to look at what they'd built — listened. Even the lamps seemed to lean in, their flames burning low and blue, the color of a flame that has been told to be quiet.

Dominic moved through that listening silence with a scribe's practiced softness, ink-stained fingers trailing along the wainscoting, and told themselves a story: *I am only going to the archive for the quarterly abstracts. I am only doing my job. Scribes are permitted in the archive at any hour; it says so in the bylaws I copied out myself, twice, in fair hand.*

The lie helped. Lies usually did. That was rather the point of them.

They had not slept. After finding the entry — *one soul, sold, payment deferred* — written in no hand they knew, in ink that seemed to drink the lamplight rather than reflect it, Dominic had sat at their desk until the candle drowned in its own wax, turning the page over and over as if the back of it might confess something the front would not. It did not. Ledgers never confessed. That was their whole nature: they recorded, and recording was a kind of silence.

But Ines was missing from the city rolls. Ines, who had fed them through the hungriest year of their life, who had pressed warm bread into their hands behind the temple wall and said, *eat first, ask questions after,* who had taught them the names of herbs by making them smell each one blindfolded until rosemary became a person and thyme became a season. Ines, whose name had simply — *stopped* — appearing anywhere, as though the city itself had agreed to forget her on schedule.

And now this entry. One soul. Sold.

Scribes were permitted in the archive at any hour.

* * *

The archive occupied the guildhall's oldest wing, three floors of iron shelving sunk half-below ground, where the air tasted of cold stone and older paper. Dominic descended the spiral stair with a hand-lamp and let the dark close behind them like water. Down here the guild's whole history stood shelved in calfskin and vellum: contracts for weather, deeds to dreams, the patent on a door that opened onto yesterday. Impossible things, catalogued and cross-referenced, each one authenticated by a master's seal and a scribe's fair copy.

Dominic had always found the archive comforting. Tonight it felt like standing inside a mouth.

The master ledger lived in the inner vault, behind a door that required no key — only a question. This was the sort of thing the guild found charming. Dominic set their palm against the iron and spoke the words carved above the lintel: *"What is owed?"*

The door considered. Then it swung inward, because the truest answer, the one the door had been waiting all these years to hear, was apparently *everything.*

Inside, the master ledger lay open on its lectern beneath a glass case that was, tonight, already raised. As if someone had expected a visitor. As if the book itself had been left out like bait, or like an apology, or like a dare.

Dominic lifted the lamp.

The ledger was enormous — a foot thick, bound in leather gone black with handling, its pages soft as cloth at the edges. And it was not one ledger at all, Dominic realized as they turned the first leaf. It was many, sewn together across decades, a palimpsest of the guild's real business conducted in the margins of its imaginary one. Here were the impossible things, yes, entered in the masters' hands with their flourished seals: *one bottle of last winter's thunder, sold to Lady Vess of the Amber Court; one door, key included, opening onto a garden that no longer exists.* And beside each entry, in a smaller, plainer script — the same script, over and over, decade after decade, the handwriting of some long-dead under-scribe who had been made to keep the second set of books — ran the column headed *consideration received.*

Thunder, bottled: paid for in six months of a cooper's memory of his daughter's laugh.

A door to yesterday: paid for in a widow's remaining years, seventeen of them, transferred to the account of a buyer in the capital.

A dream, warranted true: paid for in a child's name, surrendered entire, so that the child grew up answering to whatever people called her and never knowing what she'd lost.

And down the decades, entry after entry, the same plain script, the same quiet arithmetic, until Dominic's lamp-hand began to shake and the shadows swung across the shelves like something drowning.

*One soul, sold, payment deferred.*

There it was again. Third page from the current quire. But now Dominic could read it properly, could see how the entry connected backward through the columns like a root system, and the root went deep. Souls were not the guild's occasional scandal. They were its *currency of last resort* — the payment tendered whenever an impossible thing exceeded what memory or years or names could cover. And the guild's inventory had grown extravagant of late. Bottled stars alone — Dominic counted — two dozen sold in the past year, each one priced against somebody's everything.

Two dozen stars. Dominic thought of the night sky over the city, thinner than it had been in their childhood, the constellations going ragged at the edges like old embroidery. Everyone said the stars were dying naturally. Everyone said it was the age of the world turning.

Everyone had been lied to by people with very good penmanship.

Dominic turned pages faster now, hunting, and found what they were looking for on a leaf dated four years past — the year of the fever, the year Dominic had lain in the temple infirmary with their lungs full of water and their debt to the guild compounding hourly, because the guild did not heal people for free and Dominic had signed for the cure the way one signs for anything when the alternative is dying.

Beside their own name, in the plain script: *debt assumed by third party. Consideration rendered in full. Account closed.*

Third party. Dominic read the line ten times. There was no name attached. There never was, in these books; that was the elegance of the design, the anonymity that let everyone sleep. But the date was the week Ines had stopped coming to the wall with her bread. The date was the week she vanished from the rolls.

Ines had paid their debt.

Not with money. Not with labor. With the only coin the guild accepted for sums that large — and she had done it *willingly*, the entry noted, in a marginal hand almost gentle: *voluntary conveyance, no coercion alleged, witness: the undersigned.*

Witness: the undersigned. Some master had watched a gardener sign away her soul to settle a scribe's fever-bill, and had witnessed it, and had gone home to his supper.

Dominic set the lamp down very carefully, because their hands wanted to throw it.

The betrayal did not arrive all at once. It came in layers, the way cold comes through a coat. First: *they took her.* Then: *she gave herself, and they let her, and they profited.* Then, coldest of all: *they have been letting people do this for a hundred years, and writing it down beautifully, and calling it balance.* Every impossible wonder on the guild's shelves — every marvel Dominic had copied and catalogued and secretly loved, because even debt-bound, even resentful, they had loved this work, had loved being near the world's strangeness — every marvel stood on a foundation of the harvested. The guild did not make impossible things. It *traded* for them, and the trade goods were people, and the ledgers were beautiful, and beauty, it turned out, was just theft with good binding.

Dominic stood in the vault a long time, breathing the cold paper air, and felt something in themselves finish setting, like plaster, like bone.

Grief curdles if you leave it. What it becomes depends on the vessel. In Dominic it became a terrible clarity, calm as a ruled margin: *I know where the proof is. I am the only living person who can read these columns fluently. And I am still, tonight, inside the vault.*

They drew their scribe's knife — the little bone-handled blade used for scraping errors from vellum, for cutting quires, for the small surgeries of the page — and reached for the leaf bearing Ines's contract.

* * *

Three floors above, in a study where the fire burned without wood — one of the guild's own products, purchased at a price the Guildmaster had never asked to know precisely, because a man in his position learned which questions ruined the appetite — Aldous Vane, Guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things, sat reviewing the night's correspondence and feeling, for the first time in twenty years, the faint chill of a man whose books might not balance.

He was not a cruel man. He wished that understood. Cruelty was wasteful, and waste offended him more deeply than sin did. Everything he had built rested on a simple observation he'd made as a young factor in the southern trade: that value flows toward those who can bear to look at its source. Others looked away; he looked; therefore others paid and he collected. The souls were regrettable. He had never once enjoyed the entries. But the alternative to the trade was not virtue — the alternative was the guild's collapse, the wards failing, the impossible inventory reverting, the court beneath the mountain coming up the stairs hungry. He had seen what lay below, once, early, in the way one sees a thing that thereafter organizes every decision. Balance was not greed. Balance was the dam holding.

And the girl — the gardener — had been *voluntary*. He had checked. He always checked. She had come to the counter herself, laid her signature down like a woman paying a bill, and asked only that the account she settled be closed clean. Vane had found that almost admirable. People imagined he wanted weeping and begging. He did not. He wanted exactly this: transactions conducted with dignity, entries made in fair hand, everyone walking away owing precisely what they owed.

Which was why the irregularity troubled him.

An under-scribe — Underhill, the debt-bound one, competent, punctual, unremarkable — had requested the quarterly abstracts at an hour when no one requests abstracts. The night clerk had logged it and thought nothing of it. Vane thought of little else. He had built his life on noticing which numbers moved against expectation, and a scribe with a dead friend in the soul-columns was a number moving against expectation.

He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the starless border of the sky where the wards hummed their thin gold hum. Two dozen stars sold this year. The court below grew bolder in its pricing; the deep thing beneath the court grew hungrier in its demands; and the margins, which had been comfortable for a century, were narrowing like a corridor. Soon there would be a reckoning of some kind. There was always a reckoning. His entire art was ensuring it arrived on terms he had drafted.

He rang the small silver bell that summoned Warden Corvane, and when the warden appeared — big, quiet, smelling of lamp oil and iron — Vane spoke without turning from the window.

"The Underhill scribe is in the archive."

"You're certain, sir?"

"I am always certain about the books." He watched the wards flicker. "Go down. Bring me whatever they're holding, and bring the scribe to my study. Unharmed." A pause, and then, because precision mattered: "Unharmed *if possible.*"

Corvane went. Vane remained at the window, watching the thin gold light of the wards pulse like a heartbeat, and permitted himself the smallest sigh. He had liked the Underhill scribe's copies. Fair hand, clean margins, no flourishes. It was a shame, the way the best work always ended up on the wrong side of an entry.

Below, in the cold of the vault, Dominic's knife whispered through old vellum — a sound very like a breath drawn before speaking — and the page came free.

Somewhere overhead, a ward flickered. Somewhere below, something ancient turned over in its sleep, having heard, at last, the tearing of its paperwork.

Dominic folded Ines's contract into their shirt, over their heart, where the fever had once nearly stopped it, and ran.
