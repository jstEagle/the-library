## 5. A Page Torn Out

The master ledger weighed more than a sleeping child. It was bound in boards of black oak faced with leather gone the color of dried blood, its spine stitched with wire because thread would not hold it, and it lived on a lectern in the Guildmaster's private counting room behind a door that required two keys and a spoken word. Dominic had carried ledgers all their working life — the day-books, the tallies, the great annual volumes with their brass corners — but they had never carried *this* one. It had the heft of something that did not want to be moved.

They had waited three nights for the counting room to empty. On the first night the Guildmaster worked until dawn, quill scratching like a beetle in a wall. On the second, wardens came and went at odd hours, their boots loud on the marble, and Dominic had flattened themselves against the corridor's cold stone with a stack of innocuous receipts clutched to their chest like a shield. But on the third night the Guildmaster left at the eleventh bell, and the wardens were called away to the eastern gate, where the wards had begun to sing — a thin, high keening that no one wanted to name aloud, as if naming it might make it true.

Dominic set down the receipts. They took out the key they should not have had — copied from wax impressions taken over months, pressed into stolen blanks by a locksmith who owed them a favor and could not be traced — and they said the word they had heard through the door crack a hundred times without ever meaning to learn it.

*Verity.*

The lock turned like an animal deciding, at last, to trust.

Inside, the counting room smelled of tallow and old iron and, beneath it, something sweeter and wronger — the smell of the ledger itself, which was not the smell of paper. Paper smells of rags and size and time. This smelled faintly of cut flowers and copper, the way a room smells after blood has been spilled near roses.

The lectern stood in the center beneath a hooded lamp. The ledger lay open — the Guildmaster never closed it; closing it, Dominic understood now, would have been an admission that what it contained could be shut away — and there, on the page before them, in a hand written in ink so fresh it still shone, was Ines.

**Ines Okonjo-Wren, gardener, temple of the Verdant Path. One soul, whole and willing. Sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain. Payment received: remission of one debt, being the debt of D. Underhill, scribe, incurred year of the Grey Frost. Consideration deferred.**

There it was. Not hidden. Not even disguised. Filed between a shipment of bottled thunder for a client in the Salt Reaches and the purchase of a bridge that folded itself into a pocket, as though a woman's soul were a line item like any other, as though she were inventory.

Dominic read the entry four times. Their hands shook so badly on the fourth reading that they had to press them flat against the lectern to stop the words from swimming. *Payment received: remission of one debt.* Their debt. Their life, bought back from the fever year, from the winter the cough nearly took them — bought not by the guild's charity, as they had always been told, not by the Guildmaster's famous mercy, but by Ines. By Ines walking into the guildhall and offering herself up like a tithe, like a harvest, because someone had told her that a soul was the going rate for a scribe's continued breathing.

And the consideration — her payment, whatever the court had promised her in return — was *deferred*. Deferred. As if she were a bill that could wait. As if she had not already waited two years in the dark under the mountain while the guild's clerks wrote *pending* beside her name in their tidy hands.

Dominic had come here intending to copy the page. That had been the plan: copy it, hide the copy, return the ledger, keep their rebellion small enough to survive. Scribes survived by making copies. It was the first thing you learned — never alter the original, never take the original, the original belongs to whoever holds power, and your work is to serve the record, not to *be* the record.

But standing over the ledger now, with the lamp guttering and the wards singing their terrible song somewhere above, Dominic understood that a copy would save nothing. A copy proved nothing. The court beneath the mountain did not deal in copies; it dealt in originals, in the thing itself, in the true and singular object. If Ines's contract was ever to be contested — if anyone, anywhere, was ever to stand before that court and say *this sale was made in bad faith, this soul was taken under false pretenses, this entry is a crime wearing a clerk's neat handwriting* — then someone would have to hold the original page. Hold it, or lose everything trying.

The knife was already in their hand. They had brought it the way they brought a pen: because the work required it. It was a binder's knife, a slim blade of good steel with a bone handle worn smooth by other hands, meant for lifting the glued spines of old books without tearing them. It had never been meant for this.

Dominic set the edge of the blade at the top of the page, where the parchment met the binding cord, and paused.

What they felt was not fear. Fear had been with them since the night they found the entry, a low constant hum like the wards' singing, and they had learned to work around it the way one works around a toothache. What they felt now was stranger: a kind of vertigo of consequence. Every choice they had made since childhood had been made inside the ledger's logic — debts recorded, payments scheduled, penalties accruing in fair and lawful order. Even their grief had been filed properly. Even their suspicion of the guild they had kept, these last weeks, in the careful format of a memorandum, dated and signed and addressed to superiors who would never read it.

Cutting the page ended all that. Cutting the page was not a memorandum. It was not a petition. It was a hand reaching into the machine and taking out the one gear that mattered, and there was no procedure for it, no form, no fee schedule, no appeal. For the first time in their adult life, Dominic Underhill was about to do something that existed outside the books entirely.

They thought of Ines's hands. Everyone who had known her remembered her hands — broad-palmed, dirt-dark in the creases no washing ever reached, quick and certain among the bean rows and the rose stocks. She had fed them during the hungry year, when their apprentice stipend failed and the guild let them run tabs they could never clear. She had never once asked what they could pay. She had said, once, kneeling in the temple garden with her fingers full of seedling and loam: *A debt isn't a chain, Domi. It's just a row you haven't finished planting.* She had believed that. She had believed the world could be tended like a garden, that anything choked could be cleared, anything fallen could be lifted back onto its trellis.

She had sold herself to a court that ate souls, to pay for the life of a scribe she'd fed soup to, and the guild had written *consideration deferred* beside her name.

Dominic drew the blade down.

The parchment parted with a sound like a held breath released — a soft, fibrous whisper, then a small dry snap as the cord gave. The page came free whole, cleaner than it had any right to, as if the ledger had only ever been holding it loosely, as if some part of the book itself had been waiting for someone brave or foolish enough to ask. The stub where the page had been showed pale and raw against the black binding, and the entries before and after — the bottled thunder, the folding bridge — leaned inward slightly, as though huddling against the gap.

Dominic folded the page twice along its old creases and slid it inside their shirt, against the skin over their heart, where the warmth of their body could keep the ink from cracking. It sat there like a second heartbeat.

Then the lamp went out.

Not guttered. Not blown. *Went out* — snuffed between one breath and the next, the way a candle goes out when a door opens somewhere far away and the whole house inhales. And in the sudden dark, Dominic heard, distantly, through floors of stone and years of habit, the sound of the guildhall changing.

* * *

They came up out of the counting room into a corridor filling with smoke.

It was not yet fire — it was the breath *before* fire, the acrid sweet reek of burning vellum, of glue, of the ox-tallow that bound the guild's oldest records. Somewhere below, in the deep archive stacks where the impossible things were documented, someone had put a torch to the shelves. Dominic knew the smell intimately; they had spent half their life among those documents, and now the documents were becoming weather.

They understood at once what was happening, the way a scribe understands — by pattern, by precedent. The masters were burning the evidence. The wards were failing overhead; the stars were going out one by one beyond the high windows; the court beneath the mountain was stirring; and the Guildmaster's answer to all of it was flame. If the trades could not be defended, they would be erased. No record, no crime. No ledger, no debt. And Ines — Ines most of all, whose page was the loosest thread in the whole unraveling garment — would simply cease to have ever been sold. She would be a missing woman, a sad story, a line in the city rolls marked *whereabouts unknown*, and the guild would go on trading souls with the ash still warm on its hands.

Unless the page was already gone. Unless someone had already taken it.

Dominic ran.

The corridors of the guildhall had been built to impress clients and disorient petitioners — long axes of pale marble, branching staircases, doors that looked like windows and windows that looked like doors. Dominic had spent eleven years learning its lies, mapping every service passage and dead stair, every route the architects had drawn and then pretended to forget. Now the knowledge poured out of them like water finding a channel. They went down the servants' stair behind the scriptorium, along the passage that ran behind the ovens, past the door of the room where new apprentices were taught to rule lines straight — and everywhere, the smoke thickened, and everywhere, boots.

The wardens came in pairs, lanterns swinging, and they were not shouting *fire* the way people shout fire, with panic in it. They were calling names. *Underhill! Underhill, report to the Master of Records!* Calm voices, official voices, the voices of men doing a task — and Dominic, crouched in the dark of a linen closet with their heart slamming against the folded page, understood that the task was them. The Guildmaster had done the arithmetic already. One scribe, known to be discontented, known to have access, unaccounted for on the night the archives burned. The conclusion wrote itself in the Guildmaster's own tidy hand.

They waited in the closet among the altar linens until the boots passed, breathing through their mouth, tasting smoke and starch. Then they moved again.

The archive stairs were already lost. Dominic saw them from the landing — a throat of orange light vomiting heat, the great shelving collapsing inward like a lung, and warden after warden feeding the flames with armloads of day-books, throwing the guild's own memory into its own mouth. A young archivist stood at the bottom of the steps, weeping, holding a single folio against her chest and refusing to burn it, and a warden was arguing with her in a voice gone hoarse with authority, and neither of them looked up. Dominic turned away. There was nothing they could carry out of that room except what they already carried, and what they carried was worth more than everything burning.

They crossed the scriptorium. Their own desk stood there in the lamplight of the emergency flares the wardens had lit, and on it lay the memorandum they had drafted weeks ago and never sent — *On Irregularities in the Annual Accounts, Being a Preliminary Inquiry* — forty pages of careful, lawful, useless prose. Dominic picked it up, and for one mad moment considered leaving it open on the desk like a confession, a message to whoever came after. Then they dropped it into the brazier by the door and watched their lawful self curl and blacken and go up. If they were going to be a criminal, they would not be a half-hearted one. The page against their chest was worth more than every memorandum they would ever write.

The last corridor to the postern stair was the worst. Smoke lay in it from ceiling to knee, a grey flood with orange light moving underneath, and halfway along it a warden stepped out of a side door directly into Dominic's path.

He was older, heavyset, his face reddened by the heat, and Dominic knew him — Warden Coss, twenty years in the guild's service, a man who had once taught them how to hold a quill so the ink wouldn't spider. He raised his lantern and his voice together. *"Stop. You're wanted for questioning. The Masters will hear—"*

And Dominic, for the first time in their life, lied to a superior's face with their whole body. They pointed wildly back down the corridor — *"He went the other way — the Underhill boy — through the scriptorium — I saw him!"* — and Coss, whose eyes were streaming with smoke, whose world had narrowed to duty and fire, hesitated for exactly the length of one swallowed breath. It was enough. Dominic shoved past him — shoulder to chest, an impact that sent the older man staggering into the wall — and ran, and did not look back, and heard behind them the warden's oath dissolve into coughing, and did not care, and hated themselves for not caring, and kept running anyway.

The postern stair let out into the yard behind the stables, where the snow lay dirty and trampled and the sky was wrong.

Wrong the way a face is wrong when the expression doesn't match the wound. Overhead, where the stars had been — where stars had been every night of Dominic's life, the fixed patient furniture of heaven — there was nothing. Not cloud. Not haze. An absence with edges, a blackness so complete it looked solid, and along the horizon to the east the border wards were visible even from here: a shimmering curtain of light that had guarded the city since before the guild had a name, now flickering like a guttering lamp, now failing, now flaring in desperate pulses, singing that thin high note Dominic had heard three nights running and would hear, they suspected, in their sleep for the rest of their life.

Other figures were moving in the yard — servants fleeing with bundles, a master in nightclothes shouting orders no one obeyed, horses screaming in the burning stable's heat. Nobody looked at a scribe slipping along the wall with their collar up and their arms wrapped around their own ribs. In a world coming apart, a guilty conscience looks like everyone else.

Dominic went over the wall by the drain-spout, the way apprentices go over walls, the way they had gone over this very wall a dozen times in their youth to drink cheap wine and watch the river. The drop was shorter than memory. The snow broke their fall and soaked them to the waist, and they lay in it for a moment, gasping, feeling the folded page rise and fall against their heart with each breath — *still there, still there, still there* — and then they got up and ran.

They ran through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and fear, past shuttered houses behind which lamps were lighting at absurd hours, past a temple bell tolling alarm in the wrong rhythm, past a knot of neighbors staring upward at the starless sky and crossing themselves with the wrong hand, the old way, the way people do when they reach for the oldest protections first. And all the while the page burned against Dominic's chest — not with fire, but with purpose, with the specific gravity of a thing that must not be lost — and all the while their mind, the scribe's mind, the mind trained to weigh and record and doubt, kept turning over one sentence from the ledger like a stone in a stream:

*Consideration deferred.*

Deferred by whom? Deferred to when? What had the court promised Ines for her soul, and why had the guild never paid it? What else was written on the pages before hers and after hers, in that book that smelled of roses and copper? Who else was walking the city tonight, alive and unaware, with their name in a black ledger under the word *sold*?

The old temple garden lay south, downhill, where the city thinned into orchard rows and the ground began its long descent toward the roots of the mountain. It was the only place Dominic could think of that was theirs — theirs and Ines's, the place where she had fed them, where the beans climbed their trellises in summer, where the soil had been turned by human hands for six hundred years without pause. If the fallen wards had torn the world anywhere, if the road down had opened anywhere, it would be there. Gardens remember. Gardens keep faith. And somewhere beneath the frost-blackened beds, beneath the roots that Ines had tended and sung to, there was a stair.

Dominic ran toward it through the falling snow, under the emptied sky, with a stolen page against their heart and the guild's wardens fanning out behind them like a net, and the last light of the failing wards caught their shadow and stretched it long ahead of them down the hill —

— a shadow that fell, unmistakably, in the shape of someone walking down a staircase into the earth.
