## 6. The Road Beneath

The shadow knew before Dominic did.

They stopped at the edge of the temple garden with their breath tearing out of them in white rags, and they looked down at the shape their own body cast across the snow — a long, descending figure, stair-stepped, going down into ground that was flat as a laid table. The wards had not merely fallen, then. They had *opened*. All the years of the guild's careful sealing had been a lid, and the lid was off, and beneath the old temple garden there was a throat.

Dominic's legs were shaking. Not from the run — or not only from the run. From the understanding that the shadow had been right all along, that some part of them had already known where this road went, that the body is honest even when the mind is still negotiating. They pressed a hand to their chest, to the folded page hidden inside their coat, and felt it there like a second heartbeat: *one soul, sold, payment deferred.* Ines's name in no hand Dominic knew. Ines's name in the guild's own ink.

Behind them, up the hill, the guildhall burned orange against the starless dark. Shouting carried thin and strange through the snowfall, the way sound does when the sky has been taken away — as if the world had gone deaf on one side and everything now happened at a distance, behind glass. The wardens would find the archive. The wardens would find the gap on the shelf where the master ledger should have been. It would take them time to work out what was missing, because no one suspects the scribe; the scribe is furniture, the scribe is the pen, the scribe is the hand that moves and is never read. That was the one mercy of being nobody. But it would not take them forever.

*Go down,* said the shadow.

"I don't want to," Dominic said aloud, to no one, to the snow, to the garden. Their voice came out small and cracked and human, and it was somehow worse for being true.

Because here was the thing they could not stop knowing: they were afraid of stairs. Not of falling down them — of what waited at the bottom of any descent. Every debt has a bottom, and Dominic had spent eleven years in the guild's ledgers learning exactly how deep a person can be owed before there is nothing left of them but the owing. Ines had pulled them out of that once. Ines had stood in the temple garden with dirt to her elbows and bread in her hands and said, *Sit down before you fall down, scribe, you look like a comma someone forgot to finish,* and had fed them through the hungriest year of their life without ever once asking what the hunger cost or who would be paid. And the guild had answered that kindness by writing her into a column.

So yes. Dominic was afraid. They were afraid the way you are afraid of a door you have to walk through, which is the worst kind of afraid, because it doesn't stop your feet.

They stepped over the low wall into the garden.

* * *

The snow lay thick over the beds, unbroken, and yet the garden did not feel asleep. It felt — Dominic had no better word for it, and they were a professional with words — *attentive.* The bare apple trees stood in their rows like people listening. The rosemary hedge, black-green under the white, gave off its scent sharp enough to sting, though rosemary sleeps all winter and should have smelled of nothing but cold. The well rope was drawn up taut, as if something below had taken hold of the other end.

And the stair was there, exactly where the shadow had shown it: between the second and third apple tree, where Ines used to kneel to graft the spring cuttings, a seam had opened in the earth like a wound deciding to be a doorway. Stone steps went down into a darkness that was not empty. It moved. It breathed a slow warm air upward that smelled of loam and rain and something older underneath, something mineral and deep, like the inside of a mountain thinking.

Roots had come through the walls of the stairwell.

That was the detail that undid Dominic's last pretense of calm. Roots do not grow downward into sealed stone in a single night. But there they were — pale, tender, unmistakably *new* growth threading down along the steps like fingers laced together, like hands making a rail for a traveler. And Dominic knew roots. They had spent that hungry year learning the garden alongside Ines because there was nowhere else to be, and Ines had taught them that roots are the memory of a plant, that a tree remembers every year of its tending in the grain of itself, that if you prune with anger the wood grows crooked. These roots grew straight. These roots grew *toward* the stair, deliberately, the way a dog turns toward a familiar footstep.

"Who tended you?" Dominic whispered, and immediately hated themselves for it, because the answer was obvious and saying it aloud made it grief instead of wonder. *She did. She knelt here every spring. Of course the garden knows her. Of course it opened.*

The garden wanted her back. The thought arrived whole, the way true things do, and Dominic found they could not argue with it. Whatever the court beneath the mountain was, whatever economy of souls it ran, Ines had passed through this soil on her way down — or her name had, or the part of her that gardens are made of — and the roots had reached after her the way roots reach after water. The stair was not a wound. The stair was a *hand*, held open.

It did not make the fear smaller. It made the fear beside-the-point, which is sometimes the only mercy courage gets.

Dominic took out the torn ledger page and looked at it one more time by the light of the burning guildhall. The paper was heavy, ragged-edged where they'd cut it free with the bone knife from their own desk — the knife they used for trimming quills, the only blade a scribe owns. The entry sat in its column, neat as a grave-marker:

*Ines Vael, gardener. One soul, sold to the Undercourt. Payment received: remission of outstanding debt, account of D. Underhill.*

There it was. There was the thing they had been carrying since the night they found it, the thing that had turned their stomach to stone: the payment line. Not gold. Not favor. *Remission of outstanding debt, account of D. Underhill.* Her soul had been the coin that bought Dominic's continued existence. Eleven years ago, when Dominic's debt had grown too large for even the Guild of Impossible Things to pretend it was collectible, someone had paid it. Dominic had assumed — had *chosen* to assume — a benefactor, an act of clerical charity, a rounding error in their favor. They had built a life on top of that assumption the way you build a house on a filled-in well.

Ines had known. Ines had signed herself away to keep a starving scribe alive, and she had never said, and she had kept feeding them bread anyway, and she had let them believe they were slowly working their way free.

Dominic folded the page along its old crease and put it back against their heart.

"You're not a payment," they said to the dark, to the stair, to her. "You're not a line item. I'm coming to correct the record."

The roots stirred. Dominic chose not to think about that.

They began to descend.

* * *

The stair was older than the temple above it. That became clear within the first hundred steps. The temple garden was two centuries old at most; the stones underfoot were worn concave by feet that had walked them when the city was a camp, when the river ran in a different bed, when the stars — *the stars,* Dominic thought, and made themselves look back up the stairwell at the small square of starless sky, the hole where heaven used to be, and felt the vertigo open under them like a second stair going down. The guild had done that. The guild had sold the light out of the sky to balance its books, and every person in the city had looked up last night at a ceiling of black and told themselves it was cloud.

The air warmed as they went. Snow-smell gave way to loam, loam to stone, stone to that deep mineral breath. The roots thickened on the walls until they were less like intrusions than like veins, less like veins than like *writing* — and Dominic, who had spent their life reading marks that other people made, slowed, and looked, and understood that they were reading.

Not words. The roots didn't carry words. They carried something older than words: *tending.* Years of it, layered like rings in wood. A season of watering in drought. A winter of wrapping the fig against frost. Hands pulling weeds with patience rather than violence. Pruning cuts made clean, at the node, so the tree could heal. Decades of small generosities pressed into living wood, and all of it flowing downward, following her, the way water follows the shape of the land.

"She fed everyone," Dominic said to the dark. Their voice bounced strange off the stone, came back sounding like someone else's voice, someone braver. "Of course everything down here remembers her."

Their candle — they had thought to bring a candle, one small competence in a night of none — guttered in the updraft and steadied. The flame leaned downward, toward the deep, as if pulled.

Somewhere below, faint as a pulse, came the sound of water. And under the water, so faint Dominic might have invented it, something else: a rhythm. Regular. Patient. The sound of a hoe striking earth, or a spade, or — no. Not quite. The sound of *work,* unhurried, endless, the sound Ines's tools had made in the garden above on every morning Dominic had ever crept in hungry, that soft metronomic industry that meant *someone is keeping the world going, you may rest.*

Dominic stood on the stair in the dark with their heart slamming and their candle leaning and listened to the sound of her working, far below, alive, and felt something in their chest crack open that was not despair for once.

Then the candle went out.

Not guttered. *Extinguished* — pinched, precisely, as if by wet fingers. The dark closed over them complete and total, the first true dark of their life, darker than any cellar or closed book, because every other dark had a sky somewhere above it and this one did not. Dominic's breath went high and fast. Their hand found the wall, found the roots, found them *warm.*

"It's all right," they whispered, and did not know whether they were telling the truth to themselves or to the garden or to her. "I'm coming. I'm slow, but I'm coming. You always said I was."

*You look like a comma someone forgot to finish,* the memory said, in her voice, with her laugh under it. *Come on, then. Finish.*

Dominic laughed — a wet, broken, astonished sound — and wiped their face with the back of their hand, and kept walking. Down past the roots that remembered being tended. Down past the sound of water. Down into the warm breathing dark, one hand on the living wall, the stolen page over their heart, the wardens' torchlight a dying orange smudge far above and behind.

And at the bottom of the stair, where the steps leveled out into a floor and the floor opened into a vastness that breathed, a light was waiting — green-gold, patient, the exact color of new leaves — and beside it, seated on a root that had grown itself into a bench, a figure rose and turned toward the sound of Dominic's footsteps.

A gatekeeper. Old as the stair. With eyes like seedlings just breaking soil.

It spoke, and its voice was the voice of the whole garden, above and below, every root and ring of it:

"A toll, traveler. A true name, to pass."
