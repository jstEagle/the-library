## 7. Toll of Names

The stair did not end so much as arrive.

Dominic had counted the steps at first — a scribe's habit, counting what could be counted when everything else was uncountable — but somewhere past the four hundredth step the numbers had slipped through their fingers like water, and after that they simply walked. The walls of the stair were not stone. They were root. Enormous roots, pale as bone where Dominic's stolen lantern touched them, and threaded through them ran veins of something that glowed faintly gold, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart. The air smelled of turned earth and rain that had fallen years ago and never dried.

And the roots remembered.

That was the worst of it — worse than the dark pressing behind them, worse than the sound of the wardens' boots still echoing distantly above, muffled now by leagues of earth. The roots remembered being tended. As Dominic passed, they felt it: a warmth like recognition, like hands in soil, like someone kneeling in the dark with a trowel and singing under her breath because she thought no one was listening.

Ines had sung while she worked. Dominic had never told her they could hear her from the scriptorium window, three streets away, on nights when the wind came off the temple garden carrying everything.

*She's down there,* Dominic told themselves, gripping the torn ledger page inside their coat, pressed against their ribs like a second heartbeat. *She's down there, and I'm going to get her back.*

The page was the only proof of claim in all the world. Ines Okonkwo — no relation, though the shared name had always felt like an omen, or a joke the world played — one soul, sold, payment received. Signed in the Guildmaster's own hand with ink that still smelled faintly of iron. Dominic had cut it out with a letter knife while the archives burned above, and the cut edge of the page still wept a fine black dust, as if the ledger bled from the wound.

*One soul, sold.* And below it, in smaller script, the line Dominic had read forty times and still did not understand: *Debt assumed by seller. Consideration: one life, previously rendered.*

Previously rendered. Whose life? Rendered how? To whom?

The questions turned over and over like stones in a mill. Dominic climbed down through them.

* * *

The stair ended at a door.

It was not a grand door. That was the first surprise. After the vastness of the descent — after roots thick as cathedral pillars and darkness that swallowed the lantern's light whole — the door was small, low, ordinary. Old oak, iron-banded, its wood gone silver-grey with age. It stood alone in a hollow where the roots drew back on either side, leaving a clearing perhaps ten paces wide, floored in soft black loam. Moss grew on the doorframe. Someone had hung a wind chime there once; only the hook remained.

Dominic stood before it for a long moment, breathing.

Then the door opened — not swung open, but opened the way an eye opens, the wood seeming to thin until there was a figure standing in the gap, and the figure had been standing there all along, waiting, patient as furniture.

It was tall and narrow and dressed in grey robes that might have been bark or might have been cloth. Its face was smooth as river stone, featureless except for two shallow depressions where eyes should be, and within those depressions there was a light — dim, warm, the color of candlelight seen through closed eyelids. When it spoke, its voice was the voice of dry leaves settling.

"A traveler," it said. "From above."

"I'm looking for someone," Dominic said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "A woman. A gardener. She was taken — sold —"

"Everything taken comes here eventually." The gatekeeper tilted its head, a movement like a branch bending. "The road beneath receives all roads. But nothing passes this door without paying toll."

"Toll." Dominic's hand went instinctively to their coat, to the page. "I have money. Guild scrip —"

The gatekeeper made a sound that might have been laughter if laughter were made of rustling leaves. "Scrip. Promises written by men who do not keep them. No, little scribe. This far down, we deal in true things only." It raised one long finger, tipped with something like lichen. "Your toll is your name. Your true name. The one you were called before you learned to call yourself anything else. Give it freely, and pass."

Something cold moved through Dominic's chest.

They knew about names. Every scribe did. The Guild taught that names were currency in the old economies, before coin, before contracts — that a true name given was a true self given, that the courts below could hold a name the way a fist holds a moth. The Guild's first lesson to every apprentice, delivered with a smile: *Never give anyone your whole truth in writing.*

But that was exactly why the lesson existed, wasn't it? Because the Guild knew what names bought. Because the Guild had been selling.

"No," Dominic said.

The light in the gatekeeper's eyes flickered — not anger, Dominic thought. Interest. "No?"

"I won't give you my name." They lifted their chin. "But I'll pay. I'll pay something else. Something true."

For a long moment the gatekeeper said nothing, and the silence of the hollow deepened around them both, and Dominic became aware that the roots lining the clearing had gone very still — stiller than roots should be, stiller than things that merely grew. Listening.

"Interesting," the gatekeeper said at last. "Most give the name. Most are eager to. A name feels like such a small thing, doesn't it? A word. A breath. They don't understand until afterward that the word was holding up the whole house." It stepped aside, and the doorway behind it was simply dark — not empty dark, but full dark, dark with weight and attention. "Very well. If you will not pay in name, you may pay in memory. One memory, freely surrendered, of my choosing. That is the rate. Name or memory. There is no third price, and there is no passing without payment."

"One memory," Dominic repeated. "Of your choosing."

"Yes."

"And I won't know which one until it's gone."

"That is generally how surrender works." The gatekeeper's stone face gave nothing back, but the candlelight in its eyes burned a shade warmer. "I will tell you this, scribe, because you surprised me, and surprises are rare down here: the memory will be one you can live without. I am old, and I have watched many travelers pay, and I have never yet taken a memory that killed its giver. What remains is always enough. Whether it is enough *for you* — that is a different question, and not mine to answer."

Dominic's heart was hammering. They thought of turning back — of course they thought of it. The stair went up. The wardens were up there, and the burning archives, and the Guildmaster with his iron-scented ink, and a city whose stars had gone out. Going back was death too, just a slower kind, the kind that came with paperwork.

And Ines was down here. Ines, who had fed them during the hungry year — bread crusts saved from the temple kitchen, wrapped in cloth and left on the scriptorium windowsill every evening without a note, without ever once letting on she knew how close Dominic had come to starving. Ines, who had pulled them out of the canal when they fell through the ice, laughing at them even while she rubbed feeling back into their hands. Ines, whose name was written in a ledger in the Guildmaster's hand as *payment received*.

*You don't get to hesitate,* Dominic told themselves. *Not for her.*

"All right," they said. "One memory. Freely given. Take it."

The gatekeeper extended its long hand, palm up. "Kneel," it said, "and breathe out slowly. Do not hold the memory. Holding makes it tear. Let it go the way sleep lets go of a dream — gently, or not at all."

Dominic knelt in the black loam. The earth was cold through their trousers. They bowed their head over the gatekeeper's palm, and for one absurd moment they wanted to pray, and realized they didn't know any prayers that weren't also contracts, and laughed — a small, cracked sound in the hollow.

"What's funny?" the gatekeeper asked.

"Nothing," Dominic said. "Everything. Go on."

The gatekeeper's fingers settled on the crown of Dominic's head, light as falling leaves.

And the memory began to leave.

It did not hurt. That was the horror of it — it did not hurt at all. It left the way warmth leaves a room when a door opens in winter: gradually, then suddenly, and you only notice when the cold arrives. Dominic felt something loosen behind their eyes, felt a door open somewhere in themselves, and images rose and drifted past like leaves on dark water —

— a kitchen, small and steam-filled, copper pots on the wall —

— a voice humming, off-key, some hymn half-remembered —

— flour on dark brown hands, kneading, always kneading —

— a woman turning from the stove, saying something, mouth opening, saying —

Gone.

The gatekeeper withdrew its hand. Dominic knelt in the loam, blinking, and reached for their mother and found — a shape. A warmth. A kitchen with copper pots and a humming voice and flour-dusted hands, and where the face should have been there was a smooth blankness, like the gatekeeper's own stone countenance, like a portrait left out in the rain.

"Mama," Dominic said aloud, testing the word. It still worked. The word still worked. But the face behind it was gone — the exact angle of her jaw, the scar above her eyebrow from the kettle, the way her eyes crinkled when she said their childhood name, the name itself, the true name, the one the gatekeeper would have taken instead — all of it smoothed away, filled in with warm grey fog, like frost on glass wiped clean by a sleeve.

"Oh," Dominic said. Very quietly. "Oh, you took her face."

"The memory of her face," the gatekeeper corrected, and its voice was almost gentle. "She herself remains. You remember that she loved you. You remember the kitchen. You remember the bread. Only the seeing is gone." It paused. "I told you I take what can be lived without. I do not promise it can be lived with easily."

"Why that one?" Dominic's voice shook. They got to their feet, knees stiff, hands fisted at their sides. "Of everything — why her face?"

The gatekeeper regarded them for a long moment. The candlelight in its eyes had dimmed, banked like a fire left to burn through the night.

"Because you offered freely," it said, "and free offerings must cost, or they teach the wrong lesson. And because—" it hesitated, and the hesitation was somehow more human than anything else about it, "—because faces are how we know that love was real. Take a face, and the love becomes a story. Stories can be doubted. I needed to know, little scribe, whether you would still walk through this door for a woman you are not related to, for a debt you cannot prove, carrying nothing but paper — even after the world took something from you that you can never buy back." The light flared slightly. "Now I know. Go in. And when you bargain with what waits below — and you will bargain, everyone bargains — remember what you just paid. Remember that you know the price of things. Most who come down here have forgotten."

Dominic stood very still. The grief was strange — grieving a thing they couldn't picture, mourning a face that was already becoming a fact rather than a memory: *my mother had a face; I no longer know what it looked like.* Someday, they understood, even the fact would soften. Someday they would remember only that they had once known.

"You're cruel," they said.

"I'm a door," the gatekeeper answered. "Doors are honest. It's the rooms beyond them you want to worry about." It stepped fully aside, and the dark doorway stood open, and from somewhere far within came a smell that stopped Dominic's breath:

Loam and green growing things. Water moving over stone. And underneath it, faint as a thread of smoke — the ghost of a scent Dominic would have known anywhere, in any world, in any dark.

Bread. Someone down there was baking bread.

* * *

Dominic paused at the threshold, one hand on the silvered oak.

Behind them, the stair climbed away into darkness, toward the burnt archive, the broken wards, the starless sky. Ahead, the dark breathed out warmth and growing things and the smell of home. The torn page pressed against their ribs. *One soul, sold. Debt assumed by seller. Consideration: one life, previously rendered.*

Whose life? The question surfaced again, sharper now, and for the first time Dominic let themselves look at it straight on instead of turning away.

*Consideration: one life, previously rendered.* Someone had paid for something with a life before Ines was ever entered in the book. And Ines had *assumed the debt*. Willingly, the phrasing implied. Voluntarily. Which meant—

Which meant Ines hadn't been taken. Ines had *traded herself*. For whom? For what?

The answer arrived like ice water, and Dominic refused it, refused it, refused it — because the only person Ines loved recklessly enough to sell herself for, the only person whose life could possibly have been *previously rendered*, the only debt Ines had ever carried that weighed more than her own freedom—

"No," Dominic whispered into the dark.

The gatekeeper said nothing. Doors are honest. It let them stand there and break a little, quietly, at the threshold.

Then Dominic breathed out — slowly, the way one releases a dream — and stepped through the door, down into the court beneath the mountain, following the smell of bread into the dark.
