## 8. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The stair ended where no stair should end.

One moment Dominic's boots were on stone worn smooth by roots and rain, the next by something else entirely — a polish that came not from water but from centuries of feet that had never once been in a hurry. The last step let down into a hall so vast that Dominic's first instinct was to look up for a ceiling, and their second was to wish they hadn't.

There was no ceiling. There was sky.

Or what passed for it here: a darkness arched overhead like the inside of a closed hand, and scattered through that darkness, stars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They hung at every depth, some near enough to show their seams — glass, faintly, the curve of blown bottles — and some so far above that they might have been real. Each one burned with a small, contained ferocity, the way a coal burns when you cup your hands around it. And between them moved shapes: slow, deliberate figures walking on nothing, or on air made solid by old agreement, carrying the lights from place to place on long silver poles, rearranging heaven as a servant rearranges candles before a feast.

Dominic stood very still.

They knew these stars. Not these particular ones — but this kind. Every scribe of the Guild of Impossible Things learned the inventory by heart eventually, even the entries marked *provenance unclear*. A star in a bottle was an impossible thing. It had been catalogued. It had a shelf.

*We didn't lose the stars,* Dominic thought, and the thought arrived whole and terrible, like a stone dropped down a well whose bottom they only now heard strike. *We sold them.*

The guild's ledgers had a section no apprentice was permitted to read, filed under acquisitions with dates going back further than any master would admit. Dominic had always assumed the gaps were clerical. Now, watching a figure below them tilt a bottled star on its pole and pour its light into a fountain — an actual fountain of light, pooling and spilling across marble — they understood that the night sky over the city had not gone out. It had been *harvested*, slowly, across decades, one sale at a time, each transaction balanced so neatly that no one above ever noticed the dark arriving.

Except the wards had fallen all at once. Which meant someone had called in everything at once.

"New arrival."

The voice came from beside them, though nothing had approached. Dominic turned and found a person — or the shape of one — standing just within the edge of their shadow. It wore court clothes of a fashion two centuries dead, cut beautifully, and its face was pleasant the way a locked door is pleasant: symmetrical, closed, offering nothing.

"You'll want to learn the courtesies quickly," it said. "Here, courtesy is currency. Rude guests are simply... spent."

"I don't have anything to spend," Dominic said, and immediately heard the mistake.

The shape smiled. "You said that rudely. That will cost you." It extended one white-gloved hand, palm up, patient as a collection plate. "A memory, I think. Something small. The color of a room you loved. The taste of bread from a particular morning. We're not monsters, whatever you've heard. We take only what you can spare."

Every instinct Dominic had honed in ten years of guild bookkeeping screamed the same lesson: *nothing here is free, and the price is never posted until after you've paid.* But there was also the other lesson, the older one, the one Ines had taught without ever meaning to: *when you're hungry, take the food offered, and find out later what it cost.*

"I'm looking for someone," Dominic said carefully. "A gardener. Ines."

"Names are tolls here, not introductions." The shape's smile widened by a degree. "But see how you're learning? You gave me a name for free. That was generous of you. Generosity is noted." It drew a small black book from its coat — and Dominic's stomach dropped, because it was a ledger, of course it was a ledger, the whole world beneath the mountain ran on ledgers — and wrote something in it with a pen that left no visible ink. "Ines. Gardener. Sought by a debtor. There. Now everyone who matters knows, and knowing is a debt too."

"I can't pay memories," Dominic said. "I need mine."

"Everyone says that." The shape tucked the book away. "And yet everyone pays. Come. You'll be presented. It's customary. Refusing custom is the rudest thing a guest can do, and we've already established how costly rudeness is for you."

It turned and walked, and Dominic followed, because the alternative was to stand alone in a stolen sky with no map and fewer allies than they'd started with — which was, they were beginning to understand, exactly what the court counted on.

* * *

The hall opened onto galleries, and the galleries onto gardens, and the gardens were the worst of it, because they were beautiful.

Roses bloomed in beds of black gravel, each flower lit from within like a lamp. Vines heavy with fruit climbed trellises of pale bone-white wood, and the fruit glowed too, softly, the way the stars overhead glowed. Fountains ran with light instead of water. Everything grew. Everything thrived. And none of it — Dominic realized as they walked the gravel path behind their guide — none of it cast a shadow.

That was the tell. Real light makes shadows; it's the first honest thing light does. This light fell on everything equally, lovingly, and left nothing darker anywhere, which meant it wasn't light at all but *price* — the glow of things paid for, burning off the value stored inside them. The roses were beautiful because somewhere, someone had forgotten a summer. The fruit shone because a child somewhere above no longer remembered being born. The court had taken everything the guild sold it — souls, years, names, and yes, stars — and planted it all, and this was the harvest.

"It's lovely, isn't it," said a voice from a bench.

An old woman sat among the roses, hands folded, dressed in mourning grays gone soft with age. Her eyes were clear in a way that nothing else here was. "Don't answer," she continued, before Dominic could speak. "Answering is agreeing, and agreeing is a contract. Just listen. You have a scribe's face. I was married to a scribe, once, before he was bought." She looked up at the false sky. "I come here to watch the stars. I know eleven of them personally. That one—" she lifted a finger toward a blue-white burn near the arch of the dark, "—is called the Ferryman's Lamp. A fisherman traded it for his daughter's fever, back when trades were still kind ones. He visits it sometimes. Or he did. I haven't seen him in forty years."

"Are you a prisoner?" Dominic asked.

"What a rude question." But she said it gently, almost fondly. "No. I'm a widow. My husband's debts died with him, and the court honors widows — it costs them nothing and looks gracious. I'm the only free person here, which is to say the only one with nothing left worth taking." She studied Dominic's face with uncomfortable precision. "You're hunting someone. Don't confirm it — confirming is a contract. But whoever it is, find them before the Presentation. After the Presentation, everything anyone seeks becomes property in dispute, and disputed property goes to auction."

"The Presentation?"

"The court presents its new guests to the King. It's an honor." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Everything here is an honor. That's how you know it's a trap."

Somewhere ahead, a bell rang — a deep, sweet bell, and every star in the vaulted dark brightened in answer, and Dominic felt the pull of it in their chest like a hand closing around something soft.

"That's the summons," the widow said. "Go on. Be presented. Bow correctly. Speak only when spoken to, and then say less than you were asked. And whatever the King offers you—" she paused, and for a moment her clear eyes looked suddenly, terribly tired, "—remember that the most dangerous word in this entire court is *yes*."

Then she folded her hands again and became, in the space of a breath, merely an old woman among roses, and the guide in dead fashions was at Dominic's elbow once more.

"This way," it said. "You're expected. How flattering for you."

* * *

They walked through corridors where the walls were hung with portraits that watched, politely, and past doors that breathed — actually breathed, slow swell and fall, as if the mountain itself slept behind them — and at every threshold a servant materialized to offer some small courtesy: a chair, a drink, a compliment. Dominic refused each one. By the fourth refusal, they understood the game completely: every acceptance created a debt, and every refusal created a *grudge*, and grudges here went into books too. There was no move that didn't cost. The etiquette wasn't decoration. It was the architecture of the trap itself, load-bearing politeness, and the whole glittering court rested on it the way the false sky rested on nothing at all.

The throne room, when they reached it, was smaller than Dominic expected, and colder.

No stars here. The walls were plain black stone, and at the far end, on a dais of three steps, sat the King of the Undercourt on a chair that was only a chair — deliberately, pointedly only a chair, as if ostentation itself had been taxed out of existence. He was not tall. He was not crowned. He looked, absurdly, like a tired clerk at the end of a long quarter, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had read too many numbers for too many years.

And yet the room bent toward him. The shadows — the few the court permitted — leaned his way like plants toward sun.

"Kneel," murmured the guide.

Dominic knelt, and kept their eyes down, and counted heartbeats while silence stretched. Ten. Twenty. Thirty. The silence was a test too; everything was a test; the whole court was one continuous examination with fees attached to every answer.

"Look up," said the King at last.

Dominic looked up.

Up close, the clerk's face resolved into something older and stranger: the eyes were ink-stained too, as if he had cried ledgers, and when he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that Dominic had to lean forward to catch it — leaning forward, Dominic realized a heartbeat too late, was also a courtesy, also a small bow, also a debt.

"A scribe," the King said. "From the Guild of Impossible Things. We do so much business with the Guild. Their paper is always... immaculate." He turned one stained finger, and a page appeared in the air before him, glowing faintly — Dominic's own guild credentials, the ones sewn into their coat lining, copied without their knowledge, presented without their permission. Another theft dressed as ceremony. "You've come about an entry. They always come about an entry."

"I've come about a person," Dominic said.

"Mm." The King regarded them with something that might, in a kinder face, have been pity. "The Guild sells us persons. You may address me about *entries*. That is the courtesy of my court: we call things what the books call them. It saves everyone the embarrassment of grief."

Something in Dominic's chest went hard and hot. "Then let me address the entry. Page four hundred and twelve, master ledger, Guild of Impossible Things. One soul, sold, payment deferred. Name of record: Ines."

For the first time, the King blinked.

And around the throne room, in the corners where the shadows leaned, Dominic saw movement — figures detaching from the dark, servants of some lower order, turning their attention toward the small kneeling scribe with the audacity to quote numbers at a king. The temperature of the room changed. Somewhere, a bottled star dimmed, as if flinching.

"You tore that page out," the King said softly.

"I borrowed it."

"Borrowed." He tasted the word. "Do you know what happens to people who borrow from ledgers in my court, scribe? They become *line items*. I have a shelf of them." He rose — and rising, he stopped looking like a clerk at all; the room tilted subtly toward him, the way water tilts toward a drain. "But you interest me. The Guild sends buyers, wardens, apologists. It has never once sent someone who quoted the page number." He descended one step. Two. "So here is my courtesy, freely given, which means it is the most expensive thing you will receive tonight: you may petition for your entry. Formally. Before the full court, at the Reading of Accounts. Bring your proof. State your claim. And understand—" he stopped, close enough now that Dominic could smell old ink and cold stone, "—that every petitioner stands before the court *naked*. Your debts will be read aloud. All of them. Including the ones you've hidden from yourself."

He straightened. Behind him, the empty chair seemed somehow still occupied.

"The Presentation is concluded," he said, to the room, to the shadows, to the listening portraits. "The scribe is received. Let them be lodged in the guest wing — the *unpaid* wing — until the Reading."

The shadows moved to escort Dominic out, and as they rose to their feet, the King added, almost as an afterthought, in the voice of a man noting a footnote:

"And scribe. The gardener you seek — she is in the orchards. Ask anyone. Everyone knows the orchards." A pause, thin as a knife. "Everyone knows her, too. Or knew her. The orchards keep what's left."

* * *

The guest wing was a corridor of doors, and each door had a nameplate, and each nameplate was blank.

"They erase themselves," said the widow, who appeared at Dominic's elbow as if she'd been waiting — perhaps she had; perhaps in a court of debts, kindness was the only thing with no market price, and so it wandered freely. "Guests who stay too long forget why they came. The blank plates are the ones who've been here longest. Some of them knock on their own doors at night, trying to get in." She pressed something into Dominic's hand: a stub of candle, ordinary tallow, smelling of home. "For the dark hours. The court's light tells you what everything's worth. Tallow just tells you what's near you. Sometimes that's the more valuable information."

She was gone before Dominic could thank her — thanking, presumably, being another contract.

Alone in a cell with a blank nameplate outside it, Dominic set the candle stub on the floor, took the torn ledger page from inside their coat, and read it again by tallow-light. *One soul, sold, payment deferred.* Ines's name in a stranger's hand. And beneath it, in the margin, in handwriting Dominic now recognized with a lurch as their own — their own hand, from years ago, from a document they had no memory of writing — a single line:

*Debt assumed by D. Underhill.*

Dominic stared at it until the candle guttered.

They had signed something. Years ago. In the hungry year, in the fog of fever and gratitude, someone had put a pen in their hand — and the guild had collected on that signature the only way it ever truly collected: not in coin, but in the coin that mattered. Ines hadn't been seized. She'd been *traded*. Traded against a debt that was, line by line, Dominic's own.

The candle went out. In the dark, the blank nameplate on the door seemed to wait.

Tomorrow, the orchards. Tomorrow, whatever was left of her.

Tonight, Dominic sat in the stolen dark beneath a stolen sky, holding a page that proved the worst thing they had ever suspected — that the debt binding them had never been theirs alone to pay, because someone else had already paid it, quietly, with herself — and understood, with the clarity of the fully ruined, that they had not come here to bargain.

They had come to balance the books.
