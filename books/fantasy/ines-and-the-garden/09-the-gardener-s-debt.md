## 9. The Gardener's Debt

The orchard had no seasons.

Ines had learned this the way she learned everything down here: slowly, by touch, by the stubborn habit of paying attention that had kept her alive in every year of her life, including the ones she could no longer name. The trees above — the ones she had tended at the temple, back when back was a place and not a word — had known winter as a rest, a held breath, a time when even the busiest gardener could sit with cold hands around a warm cup and let the world do its own work.

These trees never rested. They stood in rows beneath a ceiling of stone veined with pale light, and they fruited on no schedule she could find, and their fruit was wrong in ways she had stopped trying to describe to herself. Apples the color of candlelight through skin. Pears that hummed faintly when ripe, like struck glass. One tree near the far wall that bore small dark plums which whispered if you held them too long — not words, exactly, but the shape of words, the feeling of someone about to say your name.

She did not hold those anymore.

*You are Ines,* she told herself each morning, kneeling in the black soil that was warm as bread fresh from an oven. *Your name is Ines.* And each morning the telling cost a little more effort, like lifting a bucket from a well that deepened daily. She had begun writing it on things. On the handle of her spade, scratched small: INES. On the inside of her wrist, where the skin was soft and the letters stayed clean: I-N-E-S. Once, on the trunk of the humming pear tree, until the bark had grown over it overnight, smooth and unmarked, swallowing the name the way still water swallows a stone.

The court did not take names all at once. That was the cruelty of it, though cruelty implied intent, and she suspected the court was only doing what courts do — collecting what was owed, without malice, without mercy, without ever once looking up from its books. Her name wore thin the way a coin wears thin, passed hand to hand for centuries until the face stamped on it goes smooth. Some mornings she woke and the name was there, whole. Other mornings it came to her in pieces: *I-nes*. Or just *nes*, a sound like a sigh. Or nothing at all, and she would kneel in the warm dark soil and feel along the inside of her wrist with her thumb, reading the scratches there like a blind woman reading a page, and remember.

She remembered other things better. That was the strange economy of this place — it took the self first and left the skills, the way you might sell a house but keep, somehow, the habit of climbing stairs. Her hands knew grafting. Her hands knew when soil wanted turning and when it wanted leaving alone, knew the difference between a branch that needed pruning and one that needed patience. Her hands were still entirely hers, and they worked the underground orchard row by row, hour after hour, because the court required it and because — this was the part she did not say aloud, even to herself, especially to herself — because the work was the last thing that felt like hers.

Above, somewhere impossibly far above, there had been a garden with real sun in it. She was almost sure. There had been a wall she leaned against at midday, warm stone against her shoulder blades, and rosemary under her fingers, and someone —

Someone.

There was always a someone at the edge of that memory, a figure standing just past the rosemary beds, blurred the way things are blurred when you look at them through water. She had tried for a long time to bring the figure into focus. She had stopped trying. Focusing on it hurt, the way pressing on a bruise hurts, and the court taught you quickly — or her body learned it without being taught — that pain here was not a warning. It was a price. Everything was a price. You paid attention and lost something; you paid nothing and lost everything, gradually, politely, in installments.

So she gardened. She let the name wear thin. She ate the candlelight apples when she was hungry, and they tasted like the memory of sweetness rather than sweetness itself, which was fitting, and filling enough.

It was during the pruning of the whisper-plums that she heard footsteps that did not belong.

* * *

Dominic had been walking for what felt like days and might have been hours; time below the mountain moved like syrup off a spoon, and the bottled stars overhead gave no day or night, only a dim gold constancy that made his eyes ache for a horizon. He had given up his mother's face at the gate and since then had caught himself, twice, reaching for a memory that ended in fog where a woman should be. He carried the grief of that loss like a stone in his chest, and he carried the torn ledger page folded against his heart like a second heartbeat, and he followed the sound of water and the smell — the impossible, unbearable smell — of green things growing.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing that had stopped him in the third corridor of the King's palace, that had made a chamberlain with eyes like polished coins frown at him and ask if he was quite well. Green. Growing things. He had followed the scent down a servants' stair, past doors of black wood carved with ledgers in relief — every surface in this court was covered in accounts, columns of figures flowing like ivy over architraves — and through a gate of living root into air that was warm and wet and smelled of turned earth.

An orchard. Under the mountain. Rows of trees bearing fruit that glowed faintly, tended by figures who moved slowly, who knelt in the soil with the particular posture of people who have forgotten why they are kneeling but remember how.

He scanned them the way he had once scanned debtor rolls, quick and systematic, name by absent name. A broad man hauling water. A thin woman deadheading flowers that burned instead of bloomed. An old man asleep against a root, smiling.

And then, third row from the wall, a figure on her knees with a pruning knife in her hand, working a dark tree with a competence so familiar it hit him like a physical blow.

Ines.

Not as he had last seen her — she had been thinner then, in the hungry year, all elbows and laughter, feeding him stew she could not afford from a bowl she claimed wasn't her last. Not as he had imagined her a hundred times since, in the guildhall at night, guilt sharpening his imagination into cruelty. She was older. Her hair was bound back with a strip of cloth. There was dirt to the elbow and a serenity on her face that frightened him more than grief would have, because it was the serenity of someone who has set down a burden so heavy they've forgotten they were carrying it.

"Ines," he said.

His voice cracked in the middle of her name, and the word came out half-swallowed, and she did not turn.

"Ines." Louder. Steadier. He walked toward her between the rows, and the whisper-plums murmured as he passed, and he did not care, he would pay whatever they whispered later. "Ines Okonkwo. Temple gardener. You fed me when I had nothing. You told me debt was just weather — it passes. You said—" His throat closed. "You said nobody starves in a garden, if the gardener has anything to say about it."

She turned then. Slowly, the knife still in her hand, and looked at him with eyes that were kind and empty as two cups turned upside down.

"You're new," she said. "They always send the new ones to me first. It's kinder, somehow, when it's someone whose face I don't know yet." She smiled, and the smile was hers, exactly hers, and it broke him open. "Don't grieve, whoever you are. It wastes the attention, and attention is the only coin worth keeping down here."

"I'm not new." He knelt in the soil across from her, close enough to touch, not touching. "Look at my hands. Scribe's calluses, right side, ink stains that won't come out. Look at my boots — temple-issue, third repair, you stitched the left sole yourself and mocked my walking the entire time."

She looked at his hands. Something moved behind her eyes, distant, like a fish turning under ice.

"Everyone here has a story," she said gently. "The court collects them. It keeps them in jars, I think. Or maybe the stories are the jars." She returned to her pruning, and the knife whispered through a branch, and the cut end bled sap that glittered like ground glass. "You should go before the steward counts you among the workers. They don't ask permission. They just add you to the column."

"Ines." He said it again, and again, and began saying other things, low and steady, the way you talk to someone trapped under rubble: the temple wall in summer, the smell of the rosemary, the winter she'd traded her own coat to a pawnbroker and lied about losing it, the way she whistled off-key when she thought no one listened, the way she said *enough* when she served food — *enough, enough* — pressing the bowl back into your hands because she always gave more than she had.

And piece by piece, like frost melting off a window, recognition came back into her face — not the whole of her, not yet, but enough. Enough that her hands stopped moving. Enough that the knife trembled. Enough that she reached out and took his wrist, hard, hard enough to bruise, and stared at the ink stains on his fingers as though reading them.

"Dominic," she said.

The name came out rusty, dragged up from somewhere deep, and the moment she spoke it the air in the orchard changed — every tree shivered at once, every glowing fruit brightened by a fraction, and somewhere far off in the dark between the rows something made a note in a book. He felt it happen. He felt his own name grow slightly heavier in his mouth, the way coins weigh more when they're being counted.

"Don't," she breathed. "Don't say mine. Not yet. It's worn so thin, Dom, it's worn nearly through, and every time someone speaks it, it costs—" She pressed his hand against the inside of her own wrist, against the small scratched letters. "See? I keep it written. I keep forgetting I've written it, and then I find it again, and for a moment I'm so happy, and then I can't remember why."

He was crying. He discovered it without any drama, the way you discover rain has started — simply wet, simply happening. "I came to get you out."

"Out." She tested the word like a fruit she didn't recognize. "There isn't out. There's up, and up is where the sold things come from, and nothing sold comes back. I know that much. I remember that much." Her grip tightened. "Do you know what I remember? I remember signing. I remember the guildhall, the good paper, the pen they gave me with the silver nib, and I remember thinking: this is fair. This is fair, because he'll live, and he doesn't even know, and he must never know—"

"What?" The word fell out of him like a dropped tool.

But her face had gone smooth again, the fish gone back under the ice, and she was looking past his shoulder at something in the dark of the orchard, and her expression was no longer confusion. It was fear — sharp, immediate, fully hers.

"They're coming," she whispered. "When a name gets spoken down here — really spoken, meant — the books notice. Steward's coming, or worse. Listen—"

He listened. Beneath the murmur of the whisper-plums, beneath the drip of water somewhere in the dark, there was a new sound: footsteps, unhurried, precise, accompanied by the soft dry rustle of pages turning.

Ines pulled him down behind the plum tree's trunk, her hands suddenly sure, suddenly the hands of the woman who had once hauled him bodily out of a frozen canal and sworn him to silence with a look. "Whatever happens," she murmured, "don't tell them why you came. Don't show them anything you love. Down here, showing is selling."

The footsteps drew closer. Between the rows, a light approached — not torchlight, but the glow of a single page, luminous, held by a tall figure whose face was a blank where a face should be, and whose voice, when it came, was the voice of a ledger read aloud:

"One soul, unaccounted. Present yourself for entry."

Beside him in the dark, Ines's hand found his and gripped it, and her name — *her* name, worn thin as it was — rose in his throat like a shout he knew would cost them both everything.

The blank-faced figure stopped at the head of their row, and turned.
