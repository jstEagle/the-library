## 10. Terms of Trade

The petition was a formality, and the court knew it, and I knew it, and the knowing sat between us like a knife laid on the table between two people who both intend to use it.

I had spent the hours since my arrival learning the shape of the Undercourt's courtesy. It was not like the guild's courtesy, which was a wall built of procedure — you knocked, you waited, you were admitted or not. The court's courtesy was water. It flowed around you, found every seam in your clothing, pooled in your boots, and if you stood still too long it began to seep up through your skin. Every bow had a meaning. Every title had a second title underneath it, and a third under that. When the chamberlain — a tall person whose face seemed assembled fresh each time I looked at it, out of features borrowed from everyone I had ever failed — offered me wine, I understood it was not hospitality but an audit. The vintage was pressed from something. Everything here was pressed from something.

I declined the wine. The chamberlain's borrowed face arranged itself into what might have been approval, or might have been the memory of approval worn by someone else long ago.

"State your petition," said the King.

We were in the Hall of Accounts, which was not a hall so much as a throat — a vast descending space where the walls were shelved floor to impossible ceiling with ledgers, each one bound in leather gone soft with handling, each one breathing. I am not being poetic. They breathed. I had watched one inhale for the better part of an hour while I waited, its spine swelling and settling, swelling and settling, like a sleeping animal dreaming of sums. The light came from bottles set into niches between the shelves — small green bottles, each holding a star the size of a fingernail, each star burning with the particular stubbornness of something that does not know it has been stolen.

The King sat beneath the largest of them. He was not what I expected, though I could not have said what I did expect. He looked tired. That was the horror of him, more than the crown or the dark silk or the way his shadow fell the wrong direction — toward the light instead of away from it. He looked like a man who had been awake for three hundred years doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic never came out even.

He looked, in fact, like the Guildmaster's older brother.

"The page," he said. "You may present the page."

I took it from the oilcloth inside my coat. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I had expected them to shake, and their steadiness felt like a stranger's hands resting on mine, guiding them. The torn ledger page lay flat on the table of black stone between us. Ines's name at the top in the clerk's hand I knew too well — the same hand that had written my own debt, seven years ago, in ink mixed with something that made the letters sink slightly into the paper, as if the words were trying to hide.

*One soul, sold. Payment deferred. Term: until recalled.*

"I petition for her release," I said. "The contract names the Guild of Impossible Things as seller. The Guild's authority to sell is void — I can demonstrate this from their own ledgers, which I have read entire, and which record sales made against collateral the Guild did not own. But I do not come to argue voidness. I come to buy her back."

"With what?" the King asked. Not cruelly. With the flat patience of a man who had heard ten thousand petitions and been moved by none of them, because being moved cost money.

"I have nothing," I said. "That is the truth of me. I have no coin, no land, no name worth the saying. What I have is knowledge. I have read your books' counterpart above — the Guild's master ledger, decades of entries. I know what they sold you, and when, and how they priced grief by the pound." I kept my voice level. Somewhere in the shelves behind me, a ledger exhaled. "And I know how to keep accounts. Give me work. Give me a hundred years of it. Let me pay her term down line by line, the way any debtor pays."

The King regarded me for a long moment. A bottle-star flickered overhead, and his shadow swung lazily across the black stone like a slow fish turning in dark water.

"You offer labor," he said. "Time. Years of a scribe's life, entered against a soul's account."

"Yes."

"That is not how souls are priced here." He turned his hand over, palm up, and a small object appeared in it — I did not see him take it from anywhere; it simply arrived, the way things arrived in this place, already there before you noticed their absence. It was a seed. Or it looked like a seed. "A soul is not a sum," he said. "It is a *balance*. You cannot pay a balance with effort. Effort is only motion. A balance requires weight." His fingers closed around the seed. "Someone must stand in her place. That is all a soul-sale ever is. One weight removed from one side of the scale, another weight added to the other. The scale does not care whose weight it is. The scale has never cared."

The words went through me slowly, the way cold water goes through cloth.

"No," I said.

"You have not heard the terms."

"I don't need to hear them. I heard enough."

"Hear them anyway." And there — for the first time — something moved behind the King's tired eyes. Not kindness. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has watched many people arrive at this exact moment, standing exactly where I stood, wearing exactly my expression, and who has learned to be curious about which way each one will break. "Ines of the temple garden walks free. Her account is closed, her name restored to her, her memories returned whole — including the ones she surrendered willingly, which are the most expensive kind. She walks up your stair and into your ruined city and plants whatever she likes until she dies of old age in the sun." He paused. "And another soul enters the court in her place. Voluntarily given. Freely named. Weighed, measured, and entered into these books in her stead."

The Hall of Accounts was very quiet. The ledgers breathed around us, hundreds of spines rising and falling, and I thought: *every one of these is a person.* I had known it abstractly since the night I found the entry in no hand I knew. But abstraction is a luxury of the unopened book. Standing in the throat of the thing, hearing it breathe, I understood that every volume on those shelves had once walked around in the sun complaining about the weather.

"And if I refuse?" I asked.

"Then she stays." The King said it without relish, which somehow made it worse. Cruelty would have been easier to hate. This was just bookkeeping. "You should understand, scribe, that your refusal costs you nothing. That is the trap of righteousness — it is free. She stays. You go home. You live a long life haunted by a clean conscience, because you will tell yourself, correctly, that you never agreed to trade a soul for a soul. The scale stays balanced. The books stay shut. Nothing changes except that a gardener remains underground forever, tending trees that fruit with bottled light, forgetting a little more each year, until one day she cannot remember why the name *Dominic* makes her hands ache."

"Do not," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and somewhere in the shelves a ledger rustled as if pleased.

"It is a fair offer," the King continued, relentless as interest accruing. "Fairer than most. Most petitioners learn of this clause and begin bargaining immediately — *not me, but someone else; not a stranger, but someone wicked; not the innocent, but the guilty.* They arrive wanting justice and leave having purchased a substitute. I have watched saints do it. I watched a bishop trade a beggar's child for his own mother, and weep while he signed, and believe himself merciful." He leaned forward, and his wrong-falling shadow leaned with him. "So I ask you plainly, scribe, because plainly is the only currency I respect: when you say *no*, whose *no* is it? Yours? Or hers?"

I opened my mouth to answer and found nothing there.

Because that was the wound of it, wasn't it. The question under the question. Ines had fed me during my hungriest year — soup made from garden scraps, bread with the heel saved for me without comment, the quiet dignity of a woman who gave food the way other people breathed, without making it a transaction. And I had come down a broken stair past roots that remembered her hands, carrying a torn page like a sword, certain of my own righteousness. Certain I was the rescuer.

But the King's question turned the page over and showed me its other side.

If I refused the trade, I chose for her. I chose her captivity so that my hands could stay clean. If I accepted, I chose for someone else — some other soul, some other gardener, some other hungry scribe not yet met — and dressed the choice up as rescue. Either way, someone paid. Either way, I was the Guild. The Guild of Impossible Things, which had taught me that everything survives because something else is spent, which had balanced its beautiful impossible ledgers for a century on the quiet arithmetic of other people's lives.

I had stolen a page and called it rebellion. It was just moving the debt.

"I need time," I said.

"Time is the one thing we sell," said the King. "But very well. Courtesy of the court: one day. You may walk the orchards. You may sit with her, if she consents to sit with you — consent is also a currency here, and rarer than most." He rose, and the whole hall seemed to rise with him, shelf after shelf of breathing books tilting back like a tide going out. "I will tell you something else, scribe, gratis, because you amused me and amusement is scarce below the mountain. You are not the only petitioner today."

The chamberlain materialized at my elbow, borrowed face smooth as an egg.

"There is a warden of your Guild in the antechamber," the King said, settling back onto his throne of black stone. "Come down the stair behind you, following your scent through the wards like a hound. He carries a knife that cuts pages out of existence rather than out of books. He has come to erase your evidence and, I suspect, your testimony along with it." The tired eyes glinted. "And behind him — later tonight, by our reckoning, which is not your reckoning — comes a nobleman of the upper city, old Vesseryn, come to buy back the son he sold us nineteen years ago to cover his gambling debts. He brings gold. Gold is charming. We let him think it works."

Two doors at the far end of the hall stood open now, and through one I could smell cold iron and lamp-oil — the warden, waiting. Through the other came the faint, absurd perfume of the upper world: horse, rain, money.

"So," the King said, folding his hands. "One gardener's freedom. Three petitioners. One seat vacant on the scale." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing I had seen since the stars went out. "Choose quickly, scribe. The others are choosing too — and in this court, unlike yours, all bids are final."

* * *

They gave me a cell that was meant to feel like a kindness: a room walled in living root, warm, dim, smelling of loam and rain that had fallen centuries ago. A bed of moss. A candle that burned without shrinking. Even the courtesy of my imprisonment was a trap — comfort now, so that the surface world would feel harsher when I climbed back to it alone, so that I would remember this softness and wonder why I had ever refused such reasonable terms.

I sat on the moss bed with the torn page in my lap and read Ines's contract again, though I knew it by heart, though the reading changed nothing, though I had begun to suspect that reading anything down here changed only the reader.

*One soul, sold. Payment deferred.*

Deferred. The word snagged me, as it had snagged me the first night in the guildhall, and I turned it over the way I used to turn coins over looking for the shave-mark that proved them false. Deferred by whom? Contracts deferred payment to a *term*. This one deferred to a *recall* — *until recalled* — which meant the Guild had kept the option open. Which meant the Guild had always intended to recall it. Which meant—

Which meant Ines was not cargo. She was collateral, held in reserve, a debt the Guild could call due whenever its own accounts ran thin. And the Guild's accounts, I knew better than anyone alive, ran thin constantly. Impossible things are expensive. The inventory rots, the wonders break, the miracles need feeding.

She had been a line of credit. For seven years, the Guild had survived its lean seasons on the fact of her, held underground, growing their orchard, forgetting her name one syllable at a time.

I put the page down and pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes and let myself, for exactly sixty heartbeats, despair. I counted the beats. Scribes count; it is what we are for.

At fifty-eight, a sound reached me through the root-walls. Faint. Rhythmic. Metal on stone — but gentle, patient, unhurried. Someone, somewhere in the orchards beyond my cell, was digging.

I knew that rhythm. I had heard it through the temple garden wall through a whole hungry year: two short strokes, a pause, a long scrape. The sound of a woman who talked to the earth while she worked, and left space in the rhythm for the earth to answer.

My cell had no door that I had seen. It didn't matter. The roots had carried me a message, or Ines had sent it without knowing, or the garden itself — hers before it was theirs, tended before it was harvested — was done waiting for the courts and their scales.

I got up, put the torn page inside my coat against my heart, and followed the sound of the digging.
