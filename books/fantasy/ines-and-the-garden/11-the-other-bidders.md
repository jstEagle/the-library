## 11. The Other Bidders

The court did not sleep, but it pretended to, the way a cat pretends not to watch you. Dominic learned this in the hours after their petition was lodged — hours spent in a guest alcove hung with curtains of woven dark, listening to the Undercourt breathe. Somewhere below, water moved through channels cut before the mountain had a name. Somewhere above, courtiers drifted past on errands that made no sound, their footsteps swallowed by floors that drank noise the way the sky above now drank light.

Dominic could not sleep either. The torn ledger page lay against their chest, sewn into the lining of their coat where they'd stitched it with thread pulled from their own cuff. Every time they closed their eyes they saw the archives burning, saw Warden Corvane's face turning toward them across the smoke like a compass needle finding north.

So they were awake when the herald came.

It was not a person. It was a shape of silvered shadow that stopped at the mouth of the alcove and spoke with a voice like a coin dropped down a well.

*Petitioner. You are summoned to the Hall of Accounts. Your claim will be heard tonight.*

"Tonight?" Dominic sat up. "I filed at dusk. Petitions take—"

*The Court is efficient,* said the shape, and there was something in its tone that might have been amusement, or hunger. *Efficiency is one of our currencies. Come.*

They walked. Of course they walked; everything in the Undercourt was walking, processions within processions, courtesies nested inside courtesies like the rings of a tree. Dominic counted doorways to keep their nerve steady — forty-one arches of black stone veined with something that glimmered faintly, and it took them eleven arches to understand what the glimmer was.

Stars. Bottled stars, set into the stone like jewels in a crown, each one humming a note too low for ears and too deep for comfort. Each one, Dominic thought, a theft. Each one a piece of the sky that had gone dark the night the wards fell, and here it was, winking politely at petitioners as if it had always lived underground.

The Hall of Accounts opened before them without doors.

It was vast in the way a throat is vast. Tiered galleries rose on every side, filled with figures whose faces Dominic's eyes refused to hold — not hidden, exactly, but *deferred*, as though seeing them clearly would constitute a debt the viewer couldn't pay. At the center stood a dais of green glass, and behind it, on a throne grown rather than built from pale root-wood, sat the King of the Undercourt.

He was smaller than Dominic expected. That was somehow worse.

And between Dominic and the dais, already waiting, stood two other figures.

One wore grey. Guild grey, travel-stained, with the high collar and iron clasp of the wardens' office, and even before the figure turned, Dominic knew the set of those shoulders. They had watched them across twenty years of guildhall corridors, always just ahead or just behind, always carrying something heavy.

Warden Corvane turned, and smiled the way a knife smiles.

"Scribe," she said. "You've cost me a very long walk."

* * *

The third petitioner stood apart from both of them, near the edge of the hall where the green glass floor gave way to living rock. He was old in the way that money makes people old — soft-handed, hollow-cheeked, dressed in clothes worth more than the street he'd been born on. He held his hat against his chest like a man in a temple, and his eyes were red-rimmed and ferociously awake.

He looked at Dominic and saw nothing. He looked at Corvane and saw nothing. He looked only at the throne, and his lips moved silently, over and over, shaping a word. A name. Dominic read it off his mouth: *Tobin. Tobin. Tobin.*

"Tobin," the King said, and his voice filled the hall the way cold fills a cellar. "You have petitioned for the return of a soul sold under contract. State your standing."

"I am Aldous Vane-Merrick." The noble's voice cracked on his own name, as if he'd forgotten how it fit. "Merchant-lord of the Salt Quarter. And the soul is my son's."

"The contract was signed willingly."

"He was *sixteen*." The word tore out of him. "He signed because I taught him that debts are sacred. Because I raised him to believe that anything can be bought, and then the guild came to him with a price and he — he was sixteen, and I had taught him the arithmetic better than I'd taught him anything else." His hands shook around the hat. "I have spent nine years buying back everything else he ever sold. His letters. His childhood dog. A lock of hair he gave a girl in the springtime. I bought all of it back, at ruinous rates, so that when I bought *him*, there would be nothing of him left scattered. I am ready to pay whatever remains."

A murmur moved through the galleries like wind through wheat. Dominic felt it in their teeth.

The King inclined his head a fraction. "Your claim is heard. Petitioner Corvane. State your standing."

Corvane stepped forward, and her boots rang on the glass with a sound that was almost military, almost liturgical. "I stand for the Guild of Impossible Things," she said. "I make no petition. I make a demand. This scribe—" she did not point, but the whole hall's attention swung to Dominic anyway, a physical weight, "—has stolen guild property. A page of the master ledger. I am authorized to recover it by any means, and to erase the claim it represents. The soul named Ines Okonkwo-Vale is guild collateral. Sold lawfully. Recorded properly. The guild does not release collateral because a runaway clerk has feelings about it."

"Lawfully," Dominic said, and their own voice surprised them — steady, clear, too loud in that swallowing hall. "Is that the word? Let me read you the entry, Warden. *One soul, sold, payment deferred.* No name of the seller. No witness. No consent clause. I've copied ten thousand contracts. I know what lawful looks like. That page isn't lawful. It's *tidy*. There's a difference, and the difference is a woman who fed me when I was starving."

"Sentiment," Corvane said, "is not currency here."

"No," said the King, softly, and every gallery leaned in. "But it is *interest*. And interest compounds." He rose from the root-wood throne, and up close he was not small at all; he was simply folded, the way a blade is folded, and unfolding he seemed to fill the hall the way night fills a valley. "Three claims stand before me. A father who would buy back his son. A guild that would erase its evidence. And a scribe who would buy back a gardener with a torn page and no money at all." His eyes found Dominic's, and they were the color of the space between stars. "How delightful. How very *efficient*. We shall hear them together."

* * *

Together meant cruelty, Dominic discovered. Together meant watching Vane-Merrick's offer — deeds to salt warehouses, a generation of harvest-tithes, a family name surrendered outright — weighed on scales that appeared out of the floor like surfacing fish. Together meant hearing Corvane argue jurisdiction with the fluency of someone who had sold her conscience in installments years ago and never missed the payments.

But mostly it meant understanding.

Understanding settled over Dominic slowly, the way cold water settles over a swimmer who has gone too far from shore. They had come down the stair believing themselves an exception — one scribe, one stolen page, one rescue. A story with two people in it.

But the hall was full. The galleries were full. And as the King called recess between claims — recess, in a court that didn't sleep, meaning only that everyone stopped pretending otherwise — Dominic looked out at the deferred faces in the tiers and saw them plainly for the first time. Not courtiers. *Petitioners.* Hundreds of them. Thousands. People who had lost someone to a contract, or lost themselves, waiting decades for a hearing, paying courtesy-fees in memories and minutes just to keep their place in line.

An economy. That was the truth of it, arriving all at once, terrible in its simplicity. The guild above was not the disease. The guild was a *branch office*. Souls flowed downhill like water finding the sea, and the sea was here, and the sea had rules, and every rule was designed so that no one ever finished paying.

Vane-Merrick stood alone by the edge of the hall, hat crushed in his hands. Dominic crossed to him — three steps, five, the courtesy-distance the court demanded, and they hated that their feet obeyed it automatically now.

"You bought back his letters," Dominic said. "All of them?"

"Every scrap. Nine years." The old man laughed, and it was a horrible sound, dry as ledger-paper. "Do you know what the brokers charge for a memory of a smell? My son's room smelled of cedar and ink. I paid four hundred crowns and a summer of my own childhood to get that smell back, and it sits in a jar in my house, and I open it every morning, and it isn't him. None of it is him. It's just... proof he existed." He looked up, and his eyes were the most human thing in the entire underground. "Why are you here, scribe? What did they take from you?"

"Nothing." Dominic's hand went to their chest, to the hidden page. "She wasn't taken from me. She was taken *for* me. That's worse."

Before Vane-Merrick could answer, a bell tolled somewhere in the roots of the hall — a sound with no source, a sound that came up through the floor, through the rock, through the soles of Dominic's boots and into the marrow of them. Every bottled star in the walls flickered. Every deferred face in the galleries turned at once toward the throne.

The King had gone still in a way that furniture goes still.

"That," he said quietly, "is not scheduled."

Corvane's hand had moved to her collar. To the iron clasp. And Dominic saw — with the awful clarity of a scribe who has spent a life reading fine print — that the clasp was not a clasp at all. It was a seal. A guild seal, pressed into iron, and it was glowing.

"What did they give you," Dominic breathed, "to carry that down here?"

Corvane looked at them, and for one unguarded instant the knife-smile fell away, and underneath it was something tired and old and frightened.

"A pension," she said. "And a promise that when the erasure is done, my own entry gets removed from the book." Her thumb moved over the glowing seal. "Everyone's in the book, scribe. Even me. *Especially* me. Why do you think I volunteered?"

The bell tolled again, deeper, and this time the green glass floor cracked beneath the throne like ice beneath a skater — a single line, running from the dais down into the dark, down past the orchards, past the courts, down toward something older than the King and hungrier than the guild, something that had begun, at last, to call in its accounts.

The King looked down at the crack, and Dominic watched — unmistakably, undeniably — a king flinch.

"Petitioners," the Undercourt King said, and his voice had lost its cellar-cold and found something faster, thinner, almost human. "The Court will reconvene. Immediately. All claims will be heard *tonight*."

And in the sudden scramble of courtiers, in the flare of the guild seal, in the widening crack that breathed cold air smelling of deep earth and older bargains, Dominic's hand found the page against their chest — and understood that the game had just changed for everyone in the hall.

Because something below was calling in debts.

Even the King's.
