## 12. Ines Remembers

The orchard did not sleep.

Ines had learned that in her first weeks below — or what she thought were weeks; time down here was a slow syrup poured over everything, and she had stopped trusting her own counting long ago. The trees stood in rows that curved when no one watched them, and their fruit glowed faintly, each apple and pear and plum lit from within like a lantern with a soul inside it, which was, she suspected, exactly what they were. The court's gardeners — there were others, hollow-eyed men and women who moved through the rows without speaking to one another — called them *the paid*. The fruit was payment. The trees grew from debts.

She had stopped asking questions. That was the first thing the mountain took: not memory all at once, but the appetite for it. Questions required a self to ask them, and hers had been wearing thin at the edges for so long that she sometimes caught herself standing in the middle of a row with dirt on her hands and no idea how long she'd been there, or why the soil felt familiar in a way that ached.

Tonight — if it was night; the ceiling of the cavern was dark stone, but somewhere above it the world turned whether the stars existed or not — tonight something had changed.

It began with her hands.

She was pruning a pear tree whose branches had grown into shapes that hurt to look at, all elbows and argument, and her shears — she'd asked for shears on her second day, and the steward had given them with a smile that meant *this will cost you later* — her shears paused mid-cut because her hands remembered something her mind did not.

A wall. Sun-warmed brick. The particular resistance of rose stems when you cut them back hard in autumn, the way the thorns catch your sleeve as if the plant is objecting to being tidied. And a voice behind her, young and sharp-tongued and starving, saying: *You're doing it wrong. You're supposed to talk to them.*

Ines stood very still among the glowing trees.

The memory came back the way water comes back to a dried riverbed — not all at once, but in a seep, a darkening of the dust, then a trickle, then too much at once.

*You're supposed to talk to them.* Dominic. It was Dominic's voice. She knew it before she knew anything else about it, the way you know a footstep on your own stair. Thin, defensive, hungry in a way that had nothing to do with bread — though it had that too. She had found them half-collapsed against the temple garden wall in the worst winter of the bad years, a scribe's grey robe gone greyer with street-dust, fingers too stiff to hold a pen, eyes with the flat shine of someone who has decided not to hope anymore because hoping costs more than they could pay.

She had fed them. That part surfaced easily, warm as a coal held in both palms: soup made from the temple's winter stores, bread she'd traded three hours of weeding for, the look on Dominic's face when they realized the food wasn't a trick. People didn't give things in that city. Everyone knew that. Everyone except Ines, who had been raised by a grandmother who said *a garden is proof that giving isn't losing*, and who had believed her, and who had built her whole soft stubborn life on that belief.

Then the riverbed filled faster, and the water went cold.

The guild. The Guild of Impossible Things, whose tower she could see from the temple wall, whose debt-collectors wore dove-grey and never raised their voices. They had come for Dominic in the spring — no, late winter, there had still been ice in the fountain basins — with a document covered in seals, and they had read aloud a number so large it stopped meaning anything, and they had said the word *default*, and they had said what happened to defaulters, and Dominic had gone the color of old candle wax.

And Ines had—

She set down the shears. Her hands were shaking, and the shaking was itself a memory, her body remembering what her mind had been made to forget.

She had gone to the guildhall herself. She remembered the walk now, every cobblestone of it, the way you remember the road to your own execution. She had stood in a marble room where the light came down green through impossible glass, and she had spoken to a man whose name she couldn't recall — his face was still blurred, some ward still holding there — and she had said, in a voice that only shook a little:

*I'll take the debt.*

Because that was how it worked, the clerk had explained, patient as a man explaining arithmetic to a child. Debts could be transferred. Souls could be collateral. A life saved created an obligation, and obligations wanted settling, and the guild had been keeping Dominic alive on its books for years the way a farmer keeps a sick animal alive — not out of kindness, but because a dead asset earns nothing.

*You would be selling yourself,* the man had said. Not unkindly. That was the horror of it, Ines thought now, gripping the rough bark of the pear tree — he hadn't been cruel. Cruelty would have been easier to hate. He had been gentle and reasonable and utterly without mercy, the way a ledger is without mercy, and he had laid out the terms on the table between them like a meal.

Full settlement of Dominic Underhill's account. All arrears forgiven. Their freedom papers issued, their name struck from the debtor rolls. In exchange: one soul, entered into service beneath the mountain, term indefinite, memory of the transaction retained by the seller until arrival, thereafter subject to standard attenuation.

*Standard attenuation.* She had asked what that meant. He had told her, in the same mild voice. She would remember why she came. She would forget that she chose it. So that she would not grieve, he said. So that she would serve contentedly. It was, he assured her, a kindness written into the contract.

*A kindness,* Ines whispered to the orchard, and the nearest tree shivered though there was no wind.

She had signed. She remembered the pen scratching, the sound enormous in the quiet room. She remembered thinking, with a clarity she had never felt before or since: *This is the truest thing I have ever done.* Not the vows she'd taken at the temple. Not any promise she'd made to any living person. This — trading everything she was for the life of a half-starved scribe who talked to plants and flinched at kindness — this was the realest coin she had ever spent.

And then she had walked home through the last of the winter ice, and packed her grandmother's trowel, because she was not going anywhere without it, and she had left a note on Dominic's pallet that said only: *Gone ahead. Tend the roses. — I.*

Had Dominic ever found the note? Had they understood it? She tried to reach for what came after and found fog — the attenuation working even now, even here, smoothing away the edges of her own departure. But the choice itself, the signing, the *truest thing* — that had come back whole, and it burned in her chest like a swallowed coal.

They had told her she sold herself to pay a stranger's debt.

That was the story the court told, the story the ledgers told, the story she had half-believed in her thinning hours: that she was collateral, purchased, a line item. Even the word they used for her down here — *asset* — had been designed to make her small.

But she hadn't been taken. She had *gone*. She had walked into that marble room with her chin up and her grandmother's blessing in her pocket, and she had bought a life with her own, and no one — not the guild, not the court, not the attenuating dark — could take that from her, because it was the one thing they hadn't thought to erase. They erased the choosing. They kept the debt. Fools. The choosing was the treasure.

Ines laughed. It came out cracked and wet and startled a roosting bird from the pear tree — a bird down here, in the starless dark, white as bone, blinking at her with offended dignity before it flapped off between the rows.

Because there was more coming. The riverbed was flooding now, and she braced her forearm against the trunk and let it.

Dominic had come *down*. She had seen them — yesterday? an hour ago? time was syrup — she had seen a thin figure in a warden's stolen coat standing at the orchard gate with a torn page in their fist, and her heart had known them before her head did, the way soil knows rain. They had looked at her with such terrible careful hope, saying her name like a question, like a prayer they were afraid to finish. *Ines. Ines, do you know me?*

And she had — she did — but slowly, the recognition arriving through fog, and she had hated the fog, and now she understood the fog, and understanding it made her furious in a way that felt wonderfully, dangerously alive.

They had come for her. Of course they had come for her. Stubborn, impossible, ledger-brained Dominic, who owed everyone and trusted no one, who had once eaten her soup and then insisted on washing the bowl three times because they couldn't bear the weight of the favor — they had cut a page out of a master ledger and walked down a stair that shouldn't exist, into a court that ate names, to drag her home.

Except.

Except that it wasn't a rescue. Not really. Not the way they thought.

The realization arrived gently, almost shyly, and Ines pressed her hand flat against the pear tree's bark and felt the tree's slow pulse under her palm, the debt humming in the wood like a struck bell that never quite stops ringing.

It wasn't a rescue because there had never been a debt running in only one direction. Dominic thought they owed her — for the soup, for the winter, for the life she'd bought them. And she had let them think it, in the years before, because watching someone try to repay a kindness is one of the quiet pleasures of loving them. But the truth was older and simpler than any ledger: she had fed Dominic because feeding people was what she was *for*. And Dominic — she searched the fog and found it — Dominic had given her things too, things they'd never counted. The mornings they'd shown up at the garden wall with some absurd scavenged treasure: a book of botanical engravings missing forty pages, a jar of honey "liberated" from a noble's pantry, their own company, hour after hour, talking to her roses in that dry sarcastic voice while their hands learned the work. They had stayed. Hungry people don't stay. Frightened people don't stay. Dominic had stayed through a whole turning of the seasons, and had made her laugh until her ribs hurt on days when the grief for her grandmother sat on her chest like a stone, and had never once, not once, made her feel that her softness was foolishness.

*You're doing it wrong,* they'd said, that first day, about the talking-to-plants. And then they'd done it anyway. Every day. Because it mattered to her.

So no. Whatever the guild's books said, whatever the court's books said, whatever *she* had signed in that green-lit room — the accounts between her and Dominic had been balanced for years. Balanced and closed and sealed with something no ledger had a column for. She had sold her soul to save them, yes. And they had come down into the dark to save her, yes. But underneath both transactions ran the same current, and neither of them had ever been buying anything. They had been *returning* something. Passing the same coal back and forth across a wall, hand to hand, winter after winter, each certain the other was warmer for holding it.

The rescue was mutual. It had always been mutual. It had started years ago against a sun-warmed brick wall, and it had never stopped.

Ines wiped her face with the heel of her hand and found she was smiling, which surprised her, given that her face was soaked. Somewhere in the rows behind her, the other gardeners moved in their silence, tending the paid, and she thought: *I remember. I'm the only one down here who remembers why I came, and I remember it on purpose, and that makes me dangerous.*

Her grandmother's voice, from so far away it might have been another life — and perhaps it was: *A garden keeps its own books, little vine. And its books don't lie.*

The trowel. Her hands went to her apron pocket and found it there, exactly where it had always been, the wooden handle worn smooth and dark by two generations of her family's grip. The guild had let her keep it. The court had let her keep it. They had taken her memories and her name-thinness and her years, and they had never thought to take a gardener's trowel, because to them it was a tool, worthless, a stick with a blade.

It was not a tool. It was a promise with an edge.

Ines straightened, and the orchard seemed to lean toward her, row upon row of glowing fruit, all those trapped payments, all those souls hanging heavy on the bough. She could feel them now — really feel them, the way she'd always been able to feel a thirsty plant across a courtyard. Grief in the plums. Longing in the apples. One tree near the far wall that hummed with something bright and defiant, and she found herself walking toward it before she decided to, drawn the way water is drawn downhill.

The tree at the far wall bore pears like her grandmother's, and when she laid her palm against its trunk, it pulsed against her hand like a heartbeat answering a knock.

*Hello,* she said to it, softly, in the old way. *I see you. You're not a payment. You're a person. Whoever you were, whoever you are — I see you.*

The tree shivered. A single blossom opened out of season, out of turn, white as the bone-bird, and Ines felt something shift in the deep soil of the cavern, some vast attention turning toward her the way a sleeper turns toward a lamp.

She should have been afraid. She registered the absence of fear with distant interest, the way you notice weather through a window.

Because here was the thing the attenuation had missed, the flaw in their kind accounting: they had erased her *choice*, but they had left her *reasons*. And reasons grow back. Reasons are seeds. You can burn a garden to the roots and the seeds wait in the ash, patient as scripture, and all they need is one honest watering.

Somewhere beyond the orchard, beyond the bottled stars and the courteous traps, Dominic was negotiating with kings for her life. Ines smiled again, smaller now, steadier, and tightened her grip on the trowel.

*No,* she said to the dark, to the court, to the whole rotten economy of souls. *You have it backwards. Nobody buys anybody. Not her. Not ever again.*

Far off, through the stone, she heard — or imagined she heard, but her hands had never lied to her yet — the sound of a great bell beginning to toll, deep beneath even this deep place, as if something very old had just opened its eyes and found its own books being read.

The trees leaned closer. Ines rolled up her sleeves.

There was work to do, and for the first time in longer than she could count — which was, she thought grimly, not long at all — she knew exactly what it was.
