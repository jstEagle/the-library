## 13. Midnight Audit

The court did not sleep. That was the first thing Dominic had to unlearn about it.

In the guildhall above, night meant lamplight and quiet, the scratch of nibs on paper, the small honest sounds of work done alone. Here, night was a performance. The court dimmed its bottled stars on a schedule no one explained, and the nobles drifted to their alcoves and their amusements, but nothing stopped. Somewhere a stringed instrument played in a key that made Dominic's teeth ache. Somewhere two ladies argued over the provenance of a memory the way merchants above might argue over silk. The whole mountain hummed like a hive that had forgotten how to rest.

Dominic sat in the guest alcove they had been granted — *granted*, the word itself a hook — with Ines's torn contract page pressed flat beneath a slate, and tried not to think about what the court's courtesy would cost them later.

*You are owed hospitality,* the chamberlain had said, bowing at exactly the correct depth, which is to say the depth that made refusal impossible. *The court extends its protection while your petition is heard.*

Protection. Like a fence protects a sheep.

They had been three days in the Undercourt. Three days of watching every word land like a stone in still water, watching ripples spread toward faces that never gave anything back. Three days of sleeping in a bed too soft, eating food too perfect, tasting in everything the faint metallic note of something paid for by someone who hadn't agreed to pay.

And through all three days, one thought had been turning over in Dominic's mind like a coin between fingers:

*Every court keeps books.*

It was the scribe's instinct, older than their debt, older than their fear. You could blindfold Dominic and put them in any hall in the world — a temple, a countinghouse, a warlord's tent — and within an hour they would find the records. Because power, whatever else it was, whatever masks it wore, was always arithmetic. Power was knowing who owed whom. And whoever kept those accounts believed themselves safe from audit.

That belief was the flaw. It was always the flaw.

* * *

The third night, Dominic stopped pretending to sleep.

They dressed in the grey clothes the court had provided — clothes that fit too well, another small alarm bell they'd learned to stop hearing — and slipped the contract page inside their shirt, against the skin, where it lay warm as a second heartbeat. Then they stood in the doorway of their alcove and listened.

The corridor beyond was lit by a single bottled star set in an iron sconce, and up close Dominic could see what distance had hidden: the star was *tired*. Its light came in slow pulses, like breath, like something alive and exhausted. Faint scratches marked the glass from the inside, as if the light were trying to get out.

*Don't think about that now.* There would be time for horror later. There had to be, because right now there was only work.

The court's geography obeyed etiquette rather than architecture. Halls went where protocol said they should go. But every etiquette had a servant class, and servants moved along practical lines — the short ways, the service stairs, the routes that existed because someone had to carry coal and linens and the small brutal necessities of comfort. Dominic had spent three days mapping them with their eyes while pretending to admire tapestries.

So they took the servants' stair behind the kitchens, down past the wine vaults where bottles glowed faintly with stored summers, past a laundry where sheets steamed in water that smelled of rain that had fallen nowhere, until the music faded overhead and the air grew cold and mineral, and the walls stopped being carved and started simply being *stone* — old stone, the mountain's own bones, which even the court did not bother to dress up.

At the bottom of the stair was a door.

Not a grand door. A plain door of black wood, iron-banded, with a lock that was old before the court was old. And beside the door, cut into the rock in letters worn nearly smooth, a single word in a script Dominic knew only from the guild's oldest reference volumes:

**RECORDS.**

Dominic's heart was going hard against their ribs. They pressed their palm flat against the door and felt, very faintly, a vibration — the deep, patient hum of the mountain itself, or of something in the mountain.

*Even kings keep books,* they told themselves. *And even kings believe no one will ever read them.*

The lock was a puzzle-lock, the kind the Guild of Impossible Things had invented and sold to a hundred courts, because the guild had always understood that locks were just debts with teeth. Dominic had picked their first one at fourteen. This one fought them for what felt like an hour and was probably four minutes, and then surrendered with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a bone setting.

Inside, the dark smelled of dust and cold iron and, beneath it, something sweeter — the smell of very old paper, vellum cured in honey, the smell of records that mattered.

Dominic struck no light. They didn't need to. The shelves gave off a glow of their own, a faint phosphorescence rising from the spines, because these books were not merely written in. They were *fed*. Every spine pulsed gently, and when Dominic drew close, the nearest volume turned its glow toward them like a face.

"Right," Dominic whispered, to the books, to themself. "Let's see what you know."

* * *

The court's library of accounts was organized by debt, not by date, and once Dominic understood that, the logic unfolded like a flower.

Shelves near the door held recent obligations — small things, mostly. A baroness who had purchased her third husband's affection and now paid interest in years of her own life, deducted monthly; Dominic could see the deductions, neat little marks marching down the margin like footprints. A musician who had bought one perfect song and was paying for it in the memories of his childhood, already down to a single summer he could no longer name. Page after page after page of it, and every page bore the same lesson: *nothing here is free, and most people here have forgotten what they paid.*

Deeper in, the debts grew older and larger. Whole lineages mortgaged. Wars purchased outright, their casualties listed under *costs incurred*. A famine, Dominic saw with a lurch, that had been *arranged* — scheduled like a delivery — to soften a kingdom for annexation. The handwriting on that entry was beautiful. That was somehow the worst part. Someone had taken pride in it.

Dominic worked fast, copying nothing — taking nothing, touching nothing more than necessary, because they had learned in the burning archives above that stolen pages could be hunted, but knowledge carried in the skull could not. They read the way they'd been trained to read: not word by word but pattern by pattern, letting the ledger's structure tell them where its heart was.

And the structure said the heart was deeper. Every debt in this room, without exception, traced upward — or rather, downward — through a chain of countersignatures to a single authority. The King's seal appeared everywhere, yes, but always as guarantor, never as origin. Beneath the King's mark, on every oldest page, sat a second signature: a spiral, drawn in a single unbroken line, tightening inward toward a center that seemed, when Dominic looked too long, to go on looking back.

*Something below the court,* Dominic thought. *Something the court owes.*

They found the crown's own account an hour before dawn-that-wasn't-dawn, in a case apart from the others, bound in white leather gone ivory with age, chained shut with a chain whose links were each stamped with the spiral sigil.

The account of the Undercourt King himself.

Breaking the chain was out of the question. But the case had been built by courtiers, and courtiers loved display, and display required glass. One pane had been replaced sometime in the last century and seated imperfectly in its lead. Dominic worked it loose with a scribe's knife — the same knife that had cut Ines's page from the master ledger, and their hands shook now as they had not shaken then, because this was a larger theft. This was theft from the thief of thieves.

The book fell open, as old well-read books do, to the page most often consulted.

And there it was.

The entry was centuries old and had been renewed, renewed, renewed — a debt rolled forward so many times that the original principal had become almost quaint, almost pathetic in its smallness. A loan. A *loan*, taken at the founding of the court, from the thing below — the thing whose signature was a spiral — to purchase the first bottled star. To buy light. The King of the Undercourt, in his beginnings, had not conquered his kingdom. He had *borrowed* it, one star at a time, and every star in every sconce above Dominic's head was collateral.

The interest had compounded for six hundred years.

Dominic read the renewal clauses twice, three times, lips moving though they made no sound. The terms were elegant and monstrous, as all the best contracts are. The debt could be called at any time. The debtor could offer payment in kind. And if the debtor defaulted —

If the debtor defaulted, the creditor would take the crown.

Not the circlet of iron and starlight the King wore to ceremonies. The *crown* — the true one, the one Dominic had glimpsed only in fragments across three days of watching: the web of obligation that held the court together, the invisible architecture of every debt in this room, with the King at its center like a spider who had forgotten, long ago, that the web was not his.

The King was in default. Had been in default, by the ledger's own cold arithmetic, for eleven years. Eleven years of renewals bought with delays and distractions and, Dominic now understood with a clarity that felt like falling, with *new souls* — new principal injected into the account to disguise the shortfall. The guild above had not been selling souls into a thriving market.

The guild had been making payments. On the King's behalf. Into an account the King could no longer cover.

Ines had not been sold to the court.

Ines had been sold *for* the court. Her soul was a payment installment, a bag of grain thrown into a granary that was already empty, and somewhere below — below the court, below the records room, below anything with a name — the true creditor was waiting, patient as stone, for the moment it could reach up and take what it had owned all along.

Dominic closed the book with hands that would not quite steady.

*Even kings owe,* they thought, and the thought rang in them like a bell struck in an empty hall. *Even kings owe, and no one has ever dared to say so aloud.*

Hope arrived strangely — not as warmth but as arithmetic. Because Dominic was a scribe, and scribes knew the one law stronger than any king: *the books do not lie, and the books bind everyone who appears in them.* The King had hidden behind his ledgers for six hundred years, safe in the certainty that no one would ever audit the auditor.

But Dominic had found the error. And an error, once found, could be *entered*.

* * *

They were rebalancing the loose pane in its lead when they heard footsteps in the corridor outside — unhurried, formal, the tread of someone wearing court shoes on stone and not caring who heard.

Dominic killed their breathing, slid the white book back into its case, and pressed into the shadow between two shelves as the black door opened.

The Guildmaster stood in the doorway.

He had come down the mountain. Of course he had — the wards were fallen, the road was open, and the guild's masters had been burning papers for days. He wore travelling grey instead of his ceremonial robes, and he looked older than Dominic had ever seen him look, older than the man who had signed their indenture with a kind smile and a fair-seeming rate. He carried no lamp. He did not need one; the books lit him from both sides, and his shadow lay long across the floor between the shelves.

He walked to the white-leather case and stood before it for a long moment, reading the glass, reading perhaps the faint disturbance of dust where a pane had been loosened and reseated by hands less practiced than a master's.

"You've seen it," he said quietly, to the dark, to Dominic, to the books. Not a question.

Dominic stepped out of the shadow. There was no point in anything else. "Eleven years," they said. Their voice sounded strange to them — steady, when everything in them was shaking. "He's been in default for eleven years."

"Yes." The Guildmaster did not turn. "I calculated it myself, the year you were indentured. Do you know why I took you on, Dominic? Your figures were good, but they weren't the best that year. I took you because you asked, in your interview, why the guild's interest rates never changed. Nobody asks that. Curiosity is expensive. I've been paying yours ever since."

"The guild has been paying *his* debts. With people. With Ines—"

"With whoever the arithmetic required." Now he turned, and his face in the booklight was tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "You think I don't know what it costs? I have signed every entry for thirty years. I know the weight of every name. And I will tell you what I have learned, since you seem determined to learn it the hard way: the alternative to the system is not justice. It is the spiral. It is the thing below coming up to collect, and it does not distinguish between the guilty and the innocent. It takes the crown, and the crown is *everything* — the wards, the borders, the sky's arrangement, the whole fragile machinery that keeps the dark orderly. Break the King's credit and you break the world's bookkeeping, and boy, girl, scribe, whatever you are — *no one survives an unaudited dark.*"

"So we keep feeding it," Dominic said. "Forever. One Ines at a time."

"We keep the lights on."

"And who audits *him*?" Dominic pointed at the white book, and their voice cracked open at last, all the grief and fury of weeks pouring through the crack. "Who audits the King? Who audits *you*? Someone has to read the accounts aloud. Someone has to say the numbers where everyone can hear them. That's all an audit is — the truth, spoken plainly, in front of witnesses. And if the truth unmakes the court, then the court deserved—"

The Guildmaster raised one hand, and Dominic stopped, because the gesture was pure habit, pure authority, the motion that had ended a thousand arguments in the guildhall above.

But they were not in the guildhall.

"I could erase you," the Guildmaster said softly. "One entry. Even here, even now, I retain that much. Think carefully about what your justice purchases."

"I have," Dominic said. "It purchases the truth. Spoken plainly. Before witnesses." They pulled the contract page from against their chest, and held it up between them — Ines's name, Ines's soul, the guild's own signature confessing everything. "And I have witnesses."

For the first time, the Guildmaster looked afraid.

Behind him, far down the corridor, a bell began to ring — the court's summons, the sound Dominic had been told meant *petitioners assemble*. And beneath the bell, so deep it was felt rather than heard, the mountain shivered, and every book on every shelf pulsed once, all together, like a single vast heartbeat answering something stirring far below.

The spiral signature, on the oldest page of the oldest book, seemed to tighten.

Dawn-that-wasn't-dawn was coming. And Dominic intended to be standing in the court when it arrived, with the King's own default in their hands, ready to read the accounts aloud.
