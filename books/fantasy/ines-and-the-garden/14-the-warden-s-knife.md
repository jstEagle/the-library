## 14. The Warden's Knife

The bell was still ringing when the corridor behind them went quiet.

Dominic had learned, in nine years of keeping the Guild of Impossible Things' books, that silence in a place built of noise was never empty. Silence was a held breath. Silence was a door closing somewhere you could not see. They stopped mid-stride on the court's long gallery — a walkway of black stone arched over nothing at all, lit by bottled stars strung like lanterns along its rail — and turned slowly, the way one turns toward a sound that has not yet happened.

Warden Corvane stood at the far end of the gallery.

He had come up through the petitioners' stair without ringing a single step, which should have been impossible; the stair announced every footfall in this place, Dominic had heard it announce their own arrival like a town crier. But Corvane moved the way guild wardens were trained to move, the way debt moved: silently, inevitably, already inside the walls before anyone thought to look for him.

He was not wearing his mask. That was the worst part. Dominic had seen Corvane perhaps two hundred times across the years, always masked, always gray-robed, a shape rather than a man. Now his face was bare and ordinary — broad, weathered, the face of a farmer or a ferryman, someone who might once have been kind. His eyes were pale as skimmed milk.

"Scribe," he said. His voice was gentle. That was worse than the knife.

"Corvane." Dominic's own voice came out thin. The ledger page — Ines's page, torn from the master book, folded now against their chest where it rose and fell with each breath like a second heart — seemed to burn through the cloth. "You came down yourself. The Guildmaster must be frightened indeed."

"The Guildmaster is frightened." Corvane began to walk. Not quickly. There was no hurry in him at all; the gallery was narrow, the rail low, and there was nowhere to go but past him or over the edge into the dark beneath the arches, which was not dark the way night was dark but dark the way the inside of the mountain was dark — patient, total, old. "I am not. I have done this work thirty years, scribe. Do you know what I have learned in thirty years?"

"That people beg."

"That everyone bargains." He smiled, and it transformed his ordinary face into something almost warm, and that warmth was the most frightening thing Dominic had ever seen. "The court below. The masters above. The King himself, if the rumors are true. Everyone believes they can trade their way out. You are standing in the one market in all the worlds where that belief is *inventory*, scribe. And you have brought something to sell."

"I'm not selling anything."

"No." Corvane's hand drifted to his belt, to the long gray sheath there, and the gentleness did not leave his voice. "That's what makes this difficult. Everything else in this place can be bought. You can only be *ended*. Give me the page, Dominic Underhill. Walk back up the stair. Forget her name — we will help you forget it, that service is free, it has always been free — and live out your span in the sunlit world with your debts forgiven and your sleep untroubled. Every one of us would take that bargain. I took it, thirty years ago. That is how I became what I am."

The bottled stars hummed overhead in their glass. Somewhere far off, the bell still tolled, though slower now, as if the mountain itself were counting.

And beneath everything — beneath the stone, beneath the gallery, beneath the humming stars — Dominic felt again that vast pulse from the oldest shelf, the heartbeat answering something stirring far below. Closer now. Or maybe just more awake.

"You said," Dominic managed, "that everyone bargains."

"Yes."

"Then here is mine." They drew the page from their coat and held it up, and even folded, even creased, it caught the starlight and threw it back wrong, the ink gleaming like a vein of ore. "You want this page. I want Ines Okonkwo walking free beside me up that stair. One page for one soul. That is a trade the court itself would honor. Make it, and I will burn my copy of the King's default on the way out, and the Guildmaster sleeps another century."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment — Dominic believed it might work. Corvane's milk-pale eyes fixed on the page, and something moved behind them, some old hunger or old grief.

Then the warden sighed, and it was the sigh of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he had forgotten it was heavy.

"There is no version," he said, "in which the Guild lets either of you leave. You know that. I know that. We are both guild-trained, scribe; we both keep honest books. So let us not insult each other with arithmetic."

The knife came out of the sheath without a whisper.

It was not a large blade. It was a working tool, the way a scribe's pen was a working tool — plain, gray, unadorned, made for one purpose and worn smooth by decades of that purpose. Dominic had seen its like only once before, in an inventory entry they had never dared to ask about: *one knife, for the correction of entries.* Correction. As if a life were a line item with a smudged figure.

Dominic ran.

It was not brave and it was not clever and it was the only thing left. The gallery was forty paces long and Corvane covered ground like poured water, unhurried, certain, and Dominic got perhaps ten paces before the warden's hand closed on their collar and spun them around, and the knife drew back—

—and stopped.

Not because Corvane relented. Because a pair of pruning shears had closed around his wrist.

* * *

Ines had followed the sound of the bell.

She had been in the orchard, as she was always in the orchard, her hands moving among the roots of trees that grew downward instead of up, fruit hanging pale and heavy in the starless air. She had not known why the bell mattered. She had not known much of anything for a long time — her name worn thin as an old coin, her memories sold off in installments until what remained of her was mostly hands and habit and a stubborn green tenderness toward growing things. But the bell had rung, and her hands had stopped, and something in her that predated memory had said: *someone you planted is about to be harvested.*

So she had come, carrying her tools, because a gardener carries her tools the way a soldier carries weapons — without thinking, without choosing, the way the body remembers what the mind has lost.

Now she stood between Dominic and the warden, and the pruning shears were locked around Corvane's wrist, and her grip did not tremble at all.

"Let go of them," she said.

Her voice was rough with disuse. Her hair had gone half to gray in the lightless air. She wore the court's gray livery, but under it, at the collar, a thread of green showed — a scrap of the temple garden's old cloth that she had kept, or that had kept her. She looked at Corvane the way she looked at blight: not with hatred, but with the flat professional assessment of something that would have to be cut out before it spread.

"Ines." Dominic's voice broke on the name. Ten paces away, close enough to touch, and unable to touch her, pinned in a killer's grip. "Ines, run—"

"She doesn't run," Corvane said quietly. He was looking at the shears on his wrist with something like respect. "Gardeners don't run. That's the trouble with gardeners. I've ended three of you, over the years. You're always the ones who fight."

"You didn't end any of them." Ines's grip tightened; the steel of the shears creaked against bone. "You *filed* them. There's a difference, and the difference is that they were people, and you wrote them down as lines." She turned her head, just slightly, and her eyes found Dominic's, and for one instant — one flickering, unbearable instant — recognition crossed her face like sun through cloud. "*Dom.*"

She remembered. Not everything. Maybe not even very much. But she remembered *them*.

Something in Dominic's chest tore open, and everything they had been holding together — the fear, the guilt, the cold arithmetic of survival — came apart in that single syllable.

Corvane felt the change too. Whatever he had expected from the scribe — begging, bargaining, the usual collapse — it was not this: Dominic going still, going calm, the way a ledger goes calm, every figure suddenly in its place. He shifted his weight to break her grip and drive the knife home.

Ines twisted the shears.

There was a crack like green wood snapping, and Corvane made a sound that no warden should ever make, and the knife rang off the black stone. Then it was chaos — fast, graceless, real. Ines drove her shoulder into him. Dominic grabbed for the fallen blade and missed and grabbed again. Corvane, one-handed, bleeding, was still stronger than both of them together, and he got his good hand around Ines's throat and lifted her, and her feet left the ground, and Dominic did the only thing left in the world to do.

They swung the ledger page — the original, the proof, the only claim Ines had — straight at his face.

It should have done nothing. It was paper. But paper written in the court's ink, paper that *was* a soul's contract, was not merely paper here, and where it struck Corvane's cheek it burned like a brand, and he roared and flinched and his heel came down on the gallery's low rail—

—and the rail, which had held for centuries, which had held petitioners and kings and things older than kings, chose that moment — or was chosen; Dominic would wonder forever whether the mountain itself reached up and plucked him — to give way.

Corvane went over the edge.

* * *

He did not scream on the way down. That was the detail that would stay with Dominic longest, in all the nights that followed. He fell the way he had walked: silently, inevitably, a debt coming due. The dark beneath the arches received him without a ripple, without a sound, the way deep water receives a stone. One moment his ordinary, kind, terrible face was turning up toward them, milk-pale eyes wide — and then the dark closed, and he was gone, and the gallery was silent except for the humming of the bottled stars.

Ines crumpled to her knees, coughing, hands pressed to her bruised throat. Dominic knelt beside her, and this time there was nothing between them, and they held each other — clumsily, desperately, two people who had forgotten how bodies worked — while the bell tolled its slow count somewhere above.

"I remember you," Ines whispered into their shoulder. Her voice was wrecked and wondering. "I don't — there's so much gone, Dom, there's holes in me like moth-eaten cloth, I can't find the edges of things — but I remember the winter. The wall. You were starving and you wouldn't take charity, you were too proud, so I paid you to pull weeds. A copper a day for weeds that weren't weeds, that I'd planted myself the week before." A broken laugh. "You knew. You knew they weren't weeds. You pulled them anyway, because you needed the copper and I needed you to eat, and neither of us ever said."

Dominic could not speak. The memory was there, whole and warm and terrible — the frozen garden, the false weeds, the copper coins pressed into their palm like small warm hearts — and they understood, in a single annihilating flash, what chapter twelve of their own story had been all along: *she had sold herself to pay the guild's debt for their life.* Every coin. Every weed. All of it leading here, to a dark orchard under a dead sky, her name worn thin to keep theirs intact.

"I'm sorry," they said, because it was inadequate and true. "Ines. I'm sorry. I'm getting you out."

"You already did." She pulled back and looked at them, and her eyes were clear in a way they had not been since the stair. "Whatever happens next — you already did."

Then her gaze slid past Dominic's shoulder, to the edge of the broken rail, and went wide.

"Dom," she said. "What's down there?"

Dominic turned.

The dark beneath the gallery was no longer dark. Far, far below — deeper than the court, deeper than the King's throne, deeper than anything the ledgers had ever named — something was rising. It did not rise quickly. It did not need to. It came up through the black like a season comes through soil: gradually, totally, changing everything it touched simply by arriving. And as it rose, the bottled stars along the gallery rail began, one by one, to flicker — then to strain against their glass, pressing at the confines like moths, like birds, like souls.

And from the depths below, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that Dominic, keeper of the guild's books, recognized in their bones:

The sound of a page being turned.
