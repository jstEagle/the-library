## 15. Bargaining with the Deep

The dark below the court was not empty.

I had believed, in the way a scribe believes in margins — that what lies outside the written line is blankness, nothing, a place where no entry can be made. I had spent my life trusting margins. But as the court above us went silent — every bottled star dimming at once, every courtier frozen mid-courtesy like insects in amber — I understood that the dark beneath the Undercourt was not blank. It was *waiting*. It had been waiting longer than the court had existed. Perhaps it had been waiting for someone foolish enough to open its books.

The floor of the great hall split along a seam no eye had drawn.

Not an earthquake. Earthquakes are clumsy things, all noise and rubble. This was precise, deliberate, the way Ines cut a graft: one clean stroke, exactly where the living wood would part most willingly. The flagstones separated like pages being turned by a patient reader, and out of the opening came a smell I had no name for — old rain on stone that had never seen sky, the scent of roots before there were gardens to root in, something mineral and vast and *aware*.

Ines's hand found mine. Her grip was earth and iron.

"Don't look away," she said quietly. "It hates that."

"How do you know?"

"Because the orchard knows." She nodded toward the black trees she had tended these months, their fruit like held breath. "Everything down here grows toward something. Even it."

And then it came up.

I will try to describe it honestly, though description is my trade and this thing resisted trade of every kind. It did not have a body so much as a *presence* — a rising column of darkness denser than the dark around it, shot through with veins of slow grey light, like moonlight trying to remember how to be water. Where it passed, the bottled stars in their niches guttered and leaned toward it, straining against their glass like moths toward a window they'd forgotten the meaning of. It did not speak. It did not need to. When it settled at the edge of the dais — and it was taller than the dais, taller than the pillars, tall as a decision you cannot take back — every soul in the hall felt the same sentence pressed into them, wordless but unmistakable:

*You owe.*

The King rose from his throne.

I had seen him before only at a distance, through the flattering gloom of the court, and he had seemed then like any monarch: imposing, ornate, slightly ridiculous in the way all power is when you look too long. Now, with the deep thing beside him, he looked small. His crown — which I had noticed in the audit, because I notice everything now, because noticing is the only weapon I have ever owned — his crown sat wrong on his head. Too heavy. Tilted, as if it were slowly pulling him down into the stone.

He knelt.

The King of the Undercourt knelt to the thing from below, and the sound the court made was not a gasp. It was silence with teeth.

* * *

Let me tell you what I found in the audit, because everything that happened next grew from those numbers like a plant from seed.

Three nights ago — or what passes for night in a place lit by stolen stars — I had asked the court's archivist for access to the royal accounts. I had expected refusal. Instead the archivist, a gaunt creature whose fingers were stained with ink older than cities, had looked at me for a long moment and said, "No one has ever asked. Not once in nine hundred years. Do you know why?"

"Because it's forbidden."

"Because everyone here already owes too much to dare look at anyone else's books." He had led me down to a vault where the King's own ledger lay chained to a lectern of black iron, and he had watched me read it with the expression of a man watching someone drink from a well he'd always suspected was poisoned.

The entries were beautiful. That was the horror of them. Whoever kept the King's books wrote in a hand so fine it seemed to apologize for its contents, and the contents were these:

*Crown, one. Held in trust. Interest accruing.*

*Interest payable in: years. Memories. Names. Stars.*

*Default date: [the ink here shimmered, refusing to hold still] — soon.*

The crown was mortgaged. The throne itself was collateral. The King who had built his glittering court on the souls sold upward from the world above — Ines among them, Ines *first* among them — was himself a debtor, and the creditor was the thing now standing in his hall, patient as winter, collecting at last.

Every system of debt, I thought, looking up at that towering presence, has a bottom. And the bottom always comes due.

* * *

The warden's body was still somewhere below us. I tried not to think about it. I failed, the way I always fail at not thinking about things.

He had come at me with a knife meant for the contract page in my coat — Ines's page, the proof of her claim, the single torn rectangle of paper for which men had burned archives and hunted me across two worlds. Ines had met him with a pruning hook and a spade, tools made for cutting dead wood and turning soil, and she had used them the way she used everything: precisely, without cruelty, without hesitation. He had gone over the orchard terrace's edge into the deep dark, and the dark had taken him the way soil takes a seed, and I had stood there afterward shaking, holding a page worth more than my life, understanding that I had crossed a line from which bookkeeping could never retrieve me.

"He came to erase you," Ines had said, cleaning her tools with terrible calm. "People who come to erase other people shouldn't be surprised by the dirt."

Now, as the deep thing's attention swept the hall like a lighthouse beam, I saw the Guildmaster's warden's absence noted in the faces of the courtiers — a gap where a petition should have been. The noble with the sold son clutched his papers to his chest. The archivist had retreated to a doorway, arms crossed, wearing the expression of someone who has waited nine hundred years for an appointment to arrive.

And the King, still kneeling, still crowned with another's collateral, turned his head just enough to look at me.

"You," he said. His voice was beautiful and ruined, like a bell cracked from ringing too long. "Scribe. Auditor. *Thief.* You read my books."

"I read everyone's books," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "It's what I'm for."

"Then you know what comes now." He gestured at the presence, and even that gesture cost him; I could see the crown's weight in the tremor of his arm. "It does not negotiate. It collects. That is all it has ever done. That is all anything down here has ever done — we collect from above, and it collects from us, and the chain goes down past knowing. There is no bottom to bargain with. There is only the next link."

"Every chain has a first link," I said. "I've been reading your interest schedules. Someone set the terms. Someone *wrote the first entry.*"

The deep thing moved.

I don't mean it walked or flowed. I mean that the concept of it shifted, the way a thought shifts when you finally understand it, and suddenly its attention was entirely on me — all of it, the whole crushing weight of a regard older than the mountain, focused on one debt-bound scribe with ink-stained fingers and a torn page in their coat.

And into my mind, not as words but as arithmetic, it poured the sum of what I owed.

I had known, abstractly, that my debt to the Guild of Impossible Things was large. They had bought my life during the fever year — paid for physicians, medicines, the impossible intervention that pulled me back from the edge of dying — and I had signed myself into servitude to repay it, and the interest had compounded the way interest does, silently, monstrously, until twenty years of labor had barely touched the principal. I had carried that number like a stone in my chest for two decades.

But the number the deep thing showed me was different. It was not my debt to the guild. It was the *guild's* debt — the whole of it, every soul sold, every star bottled, every year and memory extracted to balance ledgers that should never have balanced — presented to me as a single figure, itemized, audited, *correct*.

It was showing me the books. All of them. Top to bottom.

And at the very bottom of the very last page, in a hand finer than apology, was a line I will carry until I die:

*Total outstanding: everything taken, plus everything promised, plus everything feared. Payable by: whoever holds the ledger.*

* * *

"Ines," I whispered. My mouth had gone dry as old vellum. "It's showing me the accounts."

"I can feel it." Her hand tightened on mine. "Everyone can. What is it saying?"

"That the whole system is one debt. Not thousands of little ones. One. And someone has to hold it." I swallowed. "And it's asking who."

The King laughed — a short, awful sound, quickly strangled. "Don't. Whatever you're considering, scribe, don't. I knelt before it for three centuries rather than answer that question. Do you know what happens to whoever holds the whole ledger? They become the bottom of the chain. They become what everything else owes. It will eat you down to your name and use your bones for a bookmark."

"It collected from you," I said. "For three centuries. And what did it get? A court full of frightened creatures trading each other's mothers for candlelight. You didn't pay the debt, Your Majesty. You *laundered* it. Pushed it upward, outward, onto gardeners and children and anyone who couldn't fight the terms." I stepped forward, and the courtiers flinched back from me as if I'd drawn a blade. Maybe I had. "That's not payment. That's fraud. And I am — God help me, after everything — I am still a scribe of the Guild of Impossible Things, and there is one thing a scribe cannot abide."

"Order?" the King sneered.

"*Fraud in the accounts.*"

I reached into my coat and drew out the two things I carried: Ines's contract page, soft at the edges from my handling, and behind it — wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than paper has any right to be — the master ledger of the Guild of Impossible Things. Stolen from the burning archive. Every entry. Twenty decades of souls sold to keep an inventory of miracles balanced. Every name. Every price. Every deferred payment and quiet crime, recorded in the neat hands of clerks who told themselves, as I had told myself, that keeping accurate records of a monstrous thing was different from committing it.

I held the master ledger up to the grey-veined dark.

"You want what you're owed," I said. "Fine. Here is what you're owed — all of it, in one volume, itemized and true. The guild's entire book of debts. Every soul they sold, every year they stole, every star they bottled and shipped down this mountain. Take it *whole*. Not the court's laundered version. Not the chain of frightened middlemen passing the bill downward. The original creditors and the original crimes, surrendered complete."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against my eardrums like deep water.

"The guild will be ruined," Ines said softly. Beside me, not restraining me. Never restraining me — that was the miracle of her, that she stood beside every reckless thing I did without ever once making it hers. "The masters will wake tomorrow and find their trades voided, their inventory worthless, their power gone."

"Yes."

"The city above depends on them. The wards, the granaries, the—"

"The wards fell anyway, Ines. The stars went out anyway. The guild was already spending the city to save itself — we just weren't allowed to see the entries." I turned to face her fully. "Someone has to hold this debt. That's what it's telling me. Someone always has to hold it. The only choice is whether it's held honestly — one debtor, one accounting, one end — or dishonestly forever, passed hand to hand like a hot coal until the world burns."

"And if you offer it," she said, "and it takes the ledger — what happens to the holder?"

"I don't know."

"Dominic."

"I don't know," I said again, and heard my own voice crack on the honesty of it. "But I know what happens if nobody offers. It collects from the King, the King's court collapses, the collapse tears upward, and everything above and below goes into default at once. I've read enough ledgers to know how a cascade failure reads. It reads like the last page."

The deep thing had not moved throughout. But the grey light in its veins had quickened, brightening like sap rising, and I understood — with the strange clarity that comes only at the exact center of a gamble, when the dice are in the air and no outcome can be reached in time to matter — that it was considering.

It had been collecting for eons. Piece by piece. Soul by soul. Year by stolen year. And piece-by-piece collection is slow work, and slow work breeds resentment, and resentment breeds fraud, and fraud breeds the endless laundering I had traced through three sets of books. What I was offering was not a payment.

It was a *closing of accounts*. One transaction. Total. Final. The kind of entry a bookkeeper dreams of and never dares write: *account settled in full; book closed; no further liability either direction.*

End the system, or die in it. Those were the terms. They had always been the terms. I had simply spent twenty years pretending the second option wasn't also the first.

"Take it," I said to the dark. "The whole ledger. The guild's debts, surrendered entire — and I'll enter myself as guarantor. Whatever remains unpaid after the book closes, I owe. Me. Personally. Freely entered, freely signed." I set the master ledger on the flagstones between us, and the sound of it landing rang through the hall like a struck bell. "But I want terms. Written terms. Ines walks free — her contract voided, her name restored, her years returned or accounted for. And the bottled stars go home. Every one. Back to the sky they were taken from."

The King surged up off his knees. "You *dare* — you stand in my court and dictate—"

"Your court is collateral, Majesty," I said, and did not look at him, because looking at him would have been mercy and I had none left. "You knelt to it before I ever opened a book. I'm just the auditor. I'm telling you what the audit found."

The deep thing bent.

Slowly, like a mountain deciding to bow, it inclined its vast attention toward the ledger on the floor. The grey light in its veins gathered, pooled, focused — and I felt it reading. Actually reading. Turning the guild's twenty decades of crimes page by page, and around me the court reacted to what it read: courtiers weeping as memories long sold surfaced in them like drowned things; the noble with the sold son pressing his papers to his mouth; the archivist in the doorway mouthing numbers, always numbers, bless him forever.

Then the presence straightened, and into every mind in the hall it pressed a single wordless sentence, and this time I heard it as words, because it wanted me to hear it as words:

*A guarantor must sign in blood and name.*

"Name," I said. "Gladly. Blood—" I glanced at Ines.

She was already unpinning the thorn-spine from her sleeve, the gardener's tool she'd used on the warden, and her eyes when they met mine were wet and fierce and absolutely certain. "Prick the thumb," she said. "Left hand. Scribes sign with the right; let the left do the honest work for once."

I opened my hand. She pricked it, quick and clean as planting a seed, and one bright drop welled up, red as anything that ever grew.

And before I could press it to the page — before the oldest debt in the world could be closed by the smallest signature ever written — the King spoke.

"Wait."

His voice had changed. The ruin in it was still there, but underneath the ruin was something new, something I recognized from a thousand negotiations in the guildhall above: the sound of a man who has just realized the game has moved to a table where he still holds cards.

"Scribe," said the King of the Undercourt, descending from his throne with his mortgaged crown tilting on his brow and desperation dressed as generosity in every line of him, "before you bind yourself to that — come. Sit. Let us talk terms, you and I. For I can offer you things that thing cannot: wealth beyond the guild's dreaming, your debt dissolved, your freedom written in law — and *her*, scribe. Her restored to you whole, every year returned, every memory intact, and no blood, no signature, no price at all. Only withdraw the audit. Close your books, and walk out of my mountain rich and free and owed nothing by anyone."

He smiled, and it was almost kind.

"Every debt can be forgiven, scribe. Even yours. Especially yours. All you have to do is stop counting."

The drop of blood trembled at the edge of my thumb.

Behind me, Ines said nothing at all — and her silence was the loudest voice in the hall, because it trusted me to answer, and trust, I was beginning to understand, was the one currency down here that had never once been debased.
