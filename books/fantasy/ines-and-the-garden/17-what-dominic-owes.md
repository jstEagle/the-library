## 17. What Dominic Owes

The silence that followed my words was not empty. It had weight, texture, a grain like old paper — the silence of a court that had heard ten thousand bargains and knew precisely how many of them were lies.

The King did not answer at once. He sat upon his throne of black root-wood, and the crown upon his brow — mortgaged, defaulted, quietly rotting from within like a beautiful fruit with a worm at its heart — caught the light of the bottled stars and threw it back wrong, in colors that hurt to name.

"You would refuse," he said at last, and his voice was silk drawn over broken glass, "wealth."

"I would refuse *your* wealth," I said. "There is a difference."

"There is not." He rose, and the court bowed with him like grass under wind, all those elegant bodies folding in unison, and I understood suddenly how much of his power was simply this: everyone agreeing, always, because disagreement had been priced out of their reach generations ago. "Coin is coin. Freedom is freedom. A soul restored is a soul restored. You are a scribe, Underhill. You of all people should know that an entry is an entry, whatever ink writes it."

"And you," I said, "of all kings should know that a debt forgiven by the debtor's own hand is not the same as one bought off by a third party." I held up the torn page. Ines's contract, cut from the guild's master ledger, its edges still ragged where my knife had gone through. "This page says her life was sold to pay for mine. Not metaphorically. Not in the way courts love to say things. Literally. The guild owed the Undercourt a debt for my survival — the healing after the archive fire, the years of bread I ate at their table, the very pen I write with. And Ines paid it. She walked down here and gave herself so that I could keep walking up there."

Ines stood beside me in the orchard row, her hands still earth-dark from the trees she tended, trees that grew bottled light instead of fruit. She said nothing. She had said nothing since the warden fell, since the deep thing surfaced, since the King began offering me the world. But her shoulder touched mine, and that was its own kind of sentence.

"So forgive it," the King said, spreading his hands, and oh, he made it look easy, he made mercy look like a door already open. "I forgive the guild's debt. I restore her name — whole, unworn, every memory returned to her like rain to a dry field. I strike your obligations from every book in my keeping. You walk out of here owing nothing to anyone, either of you, forever. Say yes, scribe. It costs you nothing but your pride."

And there it was. The trap, laid so gently I almost wept at its craftsmanship.

* * *

Let me tell you what I understood in that moment, standing in a court lit by stolen stars, because it took me twenty-nine years and one descent beneath the world to understand it.

Every kindness I had ever received had been entered somewhere.

When I was nine and fevered, the temple physician bled me back to health, and the bill went to my mother, and my mother paid it by selling her loom, and the loss of the loom shortened her life by winters I cannot count. When I was nineteen and starving in my first year at the guildhall, Ines fed me — figs from the temple garden, bread she claimed was surplus, though temples do not have surplus — and I thought then that some things were given freely. I built my whole self on that belief. That somewhere, outside the ledgers, generosity existed.

She had been paying for me the entire time. Every fig was a coin set aside against a debt I never saw recorded, because the recording happened below, in books I was never meant to read, in a currency I was never meant to know existed.

And now the King offered to erase it all. To wipe the slate with a gesture. To make me clean.

But a slate wiped by another's hand is still a slate. The columns remain. The system remains — the great quiet machinery that turns one person's suffering into another person's comfort, that lets masters burn papers while gardeners burn their names. If I accepted his forgiveness, I would walk out of here free, and Ines would walk beside me whole, and everything would be exactly as it was before, except that I would owe the King. A debt of gratitude is still a debt. Mercy given by a king is still a transaction; it merely hides its price in a currency called loyalty.

And someday — next year, next decade, when the guild rebuilt its ledgers, when the court found new souls to buy — someone would come to collect. They always came to collect. The only question was who paid.

"No," I said.

The word fell into the court like a stone into a well, and the well was deeper than anyone had guessed, because we heard it fall for a long time.

* * *

The King's face did not change, but the light did. The bottled stars overhead dimmed by a fraction — a court holding its breath.

"No?" he said softly.

"No." My voice shook. I let it shake. I was done pretending that steadiness was the same as strength. "You are offering to restart the cycle with better terms. That is all any of this has ever been — the guild sells souls to balance its books, you buy them to light your court, and the deep below waits to collect on *you*, and when it collects, you will need more souls, and the guild will find them, and the wheel turns. If I take your mercy, the wheel turns once more with me greased into its rim. I won't do it."

"Then what will you do?" And here, for the first time, something moved behind the King's beauty — not fear exactly, but its cousin, the recognition of a shape he had not planned for. "You cannot end debt, scribe. Debt is older than I am. Debt is how the world remembers itself."

"Perhaps," I said. "But I can choose what I owe, and to whom, and on what terms. That is the one freedom no ledger has ever managed to price." I turned to the chamber beyond the throne, where the court's own books waited on their lecterns of bone-pale wood — the true records, the ones even the guild's masters had only ever seen copies of. "I want to enter myself into your books. Alongside her. As myself. Not as collateral. Not as payment deferred. As a debtor who chooses her debts."

A murmur ran through the assembled court — that rustling, silken sound, like moths against glass. Somewhere among them, I felt rather than saw the noble who had come to buy back his sold son turn toward me, and I felt the warden's absence like a cold draft, and I felt, far below us all, the deep thing turning over in its long sleep, listening.

"That is not how it works," the King said.

"It is exactly how it works," I said. "I have read your books, Your Majesty. All night, while you slept and schemed and sent wardens to kill me. I audited them. Do you know what I found? Every soul in this court was entered *by someone else*. Sold by a guild, gambled by a father, traded by a temple. Not one of them chose it. Your entire economy runs on consent that was never asked for." I stepped forward, and the court drew back from me as if I carried plague, or fire, or truth. "So I am asking. For the first time in the history of this place, someone is *asking*."

* * *

They brought the book.

I had expected ceremony — a procession, a reading, the full theater of a court that mistook ritual for power. Instead a small clerk came forward, a gray person of indeterminate age whose fingers were stained the same ink-stain as mine, and they carried a ledger no larger than a prayer book, and they opened it on a stand of woven roots, and that was all. The simplicity of it frightened me more than any throne.

"The form," the clerk said, and their voice was dry as August. "State your name."

"Ines Okonkwo—" I began, and stopped, because the clerk was looking past me.

At Ines.

"She enters first," the clerk said. "Her account is open. Yours does not exist yet. Debts attach to debts, scribe. That is the oldest rule, older than the King, older than the mountain. You cannot enter alone. You may only enter *with*."

I looked at Ines. She looked back at me, and in her eyes was the orchard, and the wall where she'd taught me which figs were ripe, and the long hungry year, and the fire, and the stair, and the warden falling into the dark — all of it, compressed into a single steady gaze.

"You don't have to," I whispered. "I'm the one who—"

"Hush." She smiled, and it was the smile from the garden wall, crooked and warm and entirely unsold. "You hushed me once, when I tried to tell you. Down in the orchard, when the memories started coming back. You said *not now, later, when we're safe.*" Her hand found mine, earth-warm. "We're not safe, Dominic. We may never be safe again. So I'm telling you now." She turned to the clerk. "My name is Ines Vey. Gardener. I sold myself to the Guild of Impossible Things to pay the debt of Dominic Underhill's life, and I did it freely, and I would do it again, and I am done being a line in other people's arithmetic. Enter me as I am."

The clerk wrote. The scratch of the pen was the loudest sound in the court.

"Name entered," the clerk said. "Debt acknowledged: one life, given for one life. Balance outstanding: none." They looked up, and their gray eyes held something that might, in a less ruined place, have been wonder. "Your account is clear, gardener. You may leave."

"I'm not leaving," Ines said.

"Then state your purpose."

"I stay as witness. And as co-signatory." She squeezed my hand. "Whatever he owes, I hold half."

* * *

"Now you," the clerk said to me.

I stood before the little book, and I will be honest — my hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the strangeness of it, the vertigo of doing a thing that had never been done, of writing myself into a record instead of writing others. All my life I had been the pen. Now I was the page.

"State your name."

"Dominic Underhill. Scribe, third rank, Guild of Impossible Things." My voice steadied as it went, finding its footing the way a foot finds a stone in a dark river. "I enter freely. No one sold me. No one traded me. I was not coerced, though gods know attempts were made — a warden with a knife, a king with honeyed words, a guild with thirty years of quiet theft. I come of my own will, and I name my own debts."

The clerk's pen hovered. "The form requires debts stated. Speak them."

And here is the thing I had not known until that moment, standing in the light of stolen stars: I had spent my whole life believing I owed nothing. That was the guild's great gift to me, the poison hidden in the bread — the belief that my survival was costless, that the world had simply been kind. Standing before the book, I finally read my own account, the one written in lives instead of ink.

"My mother's loom," I said. "Sold to pay for my fever-cure when I was nine. I owe her a lifetime of weaving she never got to do."

The pen scratched.

"Ines Vey's freedom. Thirty years of it, spent buying mine without my knowledge or consent. I owe her every day of it back, and interest besides, and the interest is that she never again gets to sacrifice for me in secret."

Scratch.

"The city above, whose stars the guild sold to light this court. I helped keep those ledgers. My neat little entries, my tidy columns — every one of them was a star going out over someone's roof. I owe the sky."

Scratch. Scratch. The court had gone utterly silent. Even the deep thing below seemed to lean closer.

"And last," I said, "the debt I cannot number and will not try to: the debt of having believed I was innocent. Of thinking that because I did not hold the knife, the cutting was not mine. I owe the understanding that comfort purchased with another's blood is not innocence. It is complicity, deferred. And deferral, as every scribe knows, accrues."

The clerk finished writing and looked up. "Account opened. Debts declared: four. Terms?"

"Terms," I said, "set by me. First: no transfer. My debts bind me and my co-signatory alone. They cannot be sold, assigned, or inherited — not by the guild, not by this court, not by anything older than this court sleeping underneath it. Second: repayment in labor, not in flesh. I will work. I will grow things, mend things, teach things, carry things. What I have, I give. What I am, I spend. But no one takes my body, my memories, or my name as payment, and neither does anyone take hers." I lifted the torn page. "Third: this contract — Ines's sale — is void. Not bought back. Void. It was invalid from the moment it was written, because she was never told what she was signing away, and a signature given blind binds no one. I invoke the oldest law in your books, the one from before the mountain had a King: *no debt stands that was never understood.*"

I tore the page in half.

The sound it made was small. The consequence was not. Every bottled star in the vault overhead flared at once — not flickering now but blazing, straining against their glass like birds against a window — and far below, in the deep dark where the older thing waited, something exhaled. The whole mountain breathed with it. Dust sifted from the ceiling of the world.

The King was on his feet. "You dare cite the Root Law in my court—"

"Your Majesty," the clerk said mildly, without looking up from the book, "the Root Law predates the court. The court exists inside it, not the other way around. The declaration is valid." They closed the ledger with a soft, final sound, like a door shutting gently on a long illness. "Accounts are joined. Debts are declared. The Reading will proceed."

"What Reading?" the King demanded.

The clerk finally raised their head, and looked at him, and looked at his crown, and looked — I swear it — through both, down into the dark where his mortgage lay.

"All accounts must be read aloud before the deep," the clerk said. "Yours included. It has been waiting three hundred years for its audit, Majesty. The scribe has just scheduled it."

Overhead, the stars burned against their bottles like a sky remembering it was owed.

And the deep began, slowly, patiently, joyfully, to rise.
