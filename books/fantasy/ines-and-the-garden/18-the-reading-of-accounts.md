## 18. The Reading of Accounts

The hall where the court gathered to hear petitions was not built for what was about to happen in it.

Dominic understood this the moment they were led in. It was a long vaulted chamber hewn from the mountain's living stone, its walls ribbed like the inside of a shell, and every surface glittered with the bottled stars — thousands of them, hung on silver wires from ceiling to floor, each one a stolen light humming faintly in its prison of glass. The light they cast was beautiful and wrong: it fell without shadow, pooled without warmth, made every face in the chamber look like the face of someone recently drowned. The court sat in tiers along both walls, the great families in their moth-wing cloaks, their faces powdered pale, their eyes dark as wells. At the far end, on a throne of black root-wood that had grown rather than been carved, sat the King beneath his mortgaged crown, and beside him — this was new, this had never happened before, the ushers whispered it among themselves like a rumor of plague — stood the deep thing's emissary, a shape too tall for the room, folded over and over like a letter that refuses to be read, wearing darkness the way other creatures wore fur.

And in the center of the floor, alone, a single lectern of pale wood. On it, a ledger. The guild's ledger, surrendered whole, bound in grey leather gone soft with decades of handling. Beside it, the court's own books, carried up from the under-vaults by clerks whose hands shook so badly they had needed help setting them down.

Dominic stood at the lectern because no one else would.

They had argued for this — had demanded it, in the end, with the particular stubbornness of a person who has already lost everything they were afraid to lose. Not a private settlement. Not a quiet trade conducted in some antechamber between the King's stewards and the deep thing's silence. A public reckoning. Every debt read aloud. Every name spoken into the air where the whole court could hear it and be unable, afterward, to claim they had not known.

The King had agreed because he had no choice left. That was the thing about being cornered by your own arithmetic: eventually the only door out is the one marked *truth*, and you walk through it knowing it leads somewhere you cannot buy your way back from.

"Petitioner," said the Herald of Accounts — an old woman with a voice like dry paper, appointed by neither crown nor court but by the deep thing itself, which apparently kept traditions older than either — "the floor is yours. Read."

Dominic opened the ledger.

Their hands were steady. They noticed this with a kind of distant surprise, the way you notice the weather has changed. Three days ago — or three years; time below the mountain moved like water finding its level — their hands had shaken so badly over these same pages that they had torn Ines's contract crookedly, ragged-edged, proof of a crime committed in haste. Now the shaking was gone. Not because they were brave. Because there was nothing left in them to spend on fear. They had paid the toll at the gate. They had lost their mother's face. They had watched a warden fall screaming into the dark with their own guilt wrapped around him like a shroud. Whatever was left of Dominic Underhill after all that was very small and very hard, like a seed, and seeds do not tremble.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," Dominic read, "kept two sets of books."

The words went out into the starless light and did not echo. The chamber drank sound the way it drank everything else.

"The first set recorded wonders. Mirrors that remembered. Doors that opened onto elsewhere. Cages for storms. These were real. I copied their descriptions myself, entry after entry, year after year, and I believed in them the way a clerk believes in anything: thoroughly, and without looking underneath."

A rustle moved through the tiers. Someone laughed — one short, sharp note — and was silenced by the people around them, though whether out of respect or fear Dominic could not tell.

"The second set recorded costs. And the costs were souls."

Dominic turned the page. The vellum was thin as skin. The entries marched down it in the neat hand of scribes who had come before — scribes like Dominic, debt-bound, hungry, grateful for the work, copying numbers without once asking what the numbers were *of*.

"I will read them," Dominic said. "Not all of them. There are four hundred and eleven. We would be here until the mountain itself is spent. But I will read enough that no one in this room can say they did not know."

* * *

They read.

They read the name of a lamplighter sold in the Year of the Long Frost, payment deferred against a mirror that showed tomorrow's weather. They read the name of a ferryman's daughter, eleven years old, traded for a door that opened onto a summer that had ended everywhere else. They read the name of a midwife, a soldier, a baker, three brothers sold together as a lot, discounted — the word *discounted* was written right there in the margin, in a master's hand, with a little flourish, as if souls came in bundles like cloth. They read the name of a woman entered only as *the singer, voice retained, remainder sold*, and Dominic's voice cracked on that one, cracked and then steadied, because steadying was the whole point now, steadying was the work.

Ines stood at the edge of the tiers, where the ushers had tried to seat her and she had refused to sit. She wore the orchard's green still, leaves in her hair, her name worn thin as an old coin but brightening hour by hour since she had begun to remember. When Dominic's voice cracked, she did not call out. She put her hand flat against the wall of the chamber — against the living stone, the mountain's own body — and held it there, the way she used to press her palm to frozen soil in early spring, testing for the life underneath. As if to say: *I am here. Keep reading.*

So Dominic kept reading.

They read the guild's crimes in order of magnitude, because that was how an audit worked and Dominic had been an auditor's apprentice once, back when they thought arithmetic was innocent. They read the falsified valuations — souls entered as *assets, miscellaneous*. They read the forged consents — page after page where the signature of a dead man authorized the sale of a living woman. They read the entry from nine years ago, the one that mattered most, the one Dominic had known was coming since the orchard, since Ines had looked at them across the bean-rows and said *you never asked why I was here*:

*"One soul, freely given, in settlement of a life-debt incurred on behalf of D. Underhill, scribe. Payment received in full."*

Dominic stopped. The chamber waited. The deep thing's emissary unfolded one fold, the way a listener leans in.

"That is my name," Dominic said. "In that entry. That is me. Everything I am — every year I lived past the winter I should have died in — was purchased with her. I did not know. I want that entered into the record: I did not know. But ignorance is not innocence, and I will not hide behind it now. She bought my life without telling me, and the guild let her, because the guild prefers its debts unpaid and its creditors forgotten. I have owed her since before I knew her name meant anything. I owe her now. Enter it."

The Herald of Accounts wrote it down. Her pen scratched like something burrowing.

* * *

Then came the harder part, and everyone in the chamber knew it was coming, and the tiers went very still, and the bottled stars hummed a half-tone higher as if they too were bracing.

"The court's own accounts," Dominic said, and lifted the second book, the under-vault book, the one the trembling clerks had carried up. "I audited them. It took two nights. I am a slow reader and a thorough one; those are the only virtues my profession ever gave me, and tonight they are enough."

They read the court's debts. The favors owed to the things in the roots. The promises made in the founding years, when the first King had bargained with the dark to keep his people fed through a famine, and the interest had compounded ever since, quietly, generation upon generation, each monarch inheriting the obligation and deferring it and telling themselves the same lie the guild told: *later. Later we will settle. Later there will be more. Later the bill will go to someone else.*

"And then," Dominic said, "there is the crown."

They turned to the final page. It was marked with a black ribbon, placed there by the under-vault clerks themselves, who had found it and gone pale and said nothing, because some truths are too heavy for servants to carry upstairs unasked.

"The crown of the Undercourt is mortgaged," Dominic read. "It has been mortgaged for sixty-one years. Its holder — the present King — has missed four consecutive payments. The collateral is not the crown. The collateral is the court. Every soul in this chamber. Every soul in the orchards, the warrens, the nurseries. You are all of you listed in this book as security against a debt your King took on before most of you were born, and he has not told you, and he has let the interest grow, and now the debt is called due, and the thing below has come up to collect."

Silence. Total, physical silence, the kind that has weight. Then, from the tiers, a sound Dominic had not expected: not outrage, not denial. Grief. A woman in a moth-wing cloak began to weep, quietly, the way people weep at funerals for things they loved and are only now admitting they loved. Others followed. The great families of the Undercourt, who had spent centuries perfecting the art of the courteous trap, discovering all at once that they had been inside a trap the whole time — that their elegance, their etiquette, their bottled stars, were the furnishings of a debtor's cell.

The King did not move. He sat on his throne of grown wood with his mortgaged crown catching the cold light, and his face did the thing faces do when a man finally stops performing: it emptied. Dominic watched it empty and felt no triumph. Only the strange, level pity of an auditor who has found the error at last and knows the error was always going to be found, and knows also that finding it helps no one who is already gone.

"You may speak," Dominic said to him. It was not theirs to grant, technically. But the Herald inclined her head, and the deep thing's emissary folded itself stiller, and so it became permitted, and permission is a kind of law even below the mountain.

The King stood. His voice, when it came, was not the voice he used for bargains. It was smaller than that. Older.

"I was young," he said. "The famine was real. The offer was real. I told myself what every debtor tells himself." He looked at the tiers, at his weeping court. "I thought I would fix it before anyone had to know. I thought there would be time. There is never time. Time is the first thing they take."

He sat back down. He did not ask for mercy. Dominic noted that, and entered it silently into the record they were keeping behind their eyes: *he did not ask for mercy.* Whatever else the King was, he was, in this one moment, honest, and honesty below the mountain was so rare it glowed.

* * *

Then the Herald of Accounts rose, and the room changed again, because the Herald did not speak for the court or the King or the guild. She spoke for the book.

"All debts having been read," she said, in her paper-dry voice that somehow reached every corner of the vaulted dark, "the reckoning stands open. Let any party show cause why the accounts should not be settled in full. Let any party speak now, or be silent forever under the terms of the oldest law, which is not written anywhere, because it was never necessary to write it down."

And one by one, they spoke.

A steward of the moth-wing families stood and confessed, in a voice like breaking glass, to forty years of signing transfers. A nursery-keeper stood and named the children she had failed to protect. An old lord stood and offered his own soul against the total, and the Herald wrote it down without comment, because the record does not judge; the record only remembers. Even the warden's surviving partner, the one who had come below to erase evidence and had instead watched his fellow fall into the deep, stood with his wrists bound and said, plainly, hoarsely: "We knew. Those of us in the archive wing knew. We told ourselves it was accounting. May whatever hears this hear it."

Dominic stood through all of it at the lectern, and their voice did not waver anymore. Something had happened to it during the reading — during the hours of names, the lamplighter, the ferryman's daughter, the singer, Ines. The voice had started as a scribe's voice, careful, trained, slightly apologetic, the voice of someone reading aloud in a room where they might be corrected. Somewhere around the hundredth name it had become something else. Not louder. Plainer. A voice with no ornament left on it, the way a bone is plain. Justice outside any law, spoken in the only language law understands: the truth, in order, without mercy and without cruelty, at full volume, until it is done.

Ines watched from the wall, her hand still pressed to the living stone, and Dominic felt her there the way you feel a fire in the next room — not seeing it, but warm toward it, oriented by it.

Last of all, the deep thing's emissary unfolded — fold after fold after fold, taller and taller until its hooded darkness brushed the ceiling of stars — and it did not speak in words. It spoke the way the mountain speaks, a pressure, a settling, a vast attention turning its face toward the ledger on the pale lectern. And the Herald translated, because translation was her office:

"It accepts the reading. It requires settlement. It names its price."

Every creature in the chamber leaned away from that sentence at once, the way grass leans from wind.

"The guild's ledger entire," the Herald said. "All four hundred and eleven names, and the debts attached, and the power that was purchased with them — surrendered. Not destroyed. Surrendered. Returned to the dark from which all of it was borrowed." She paused, and her dry voice did something almost gentle. "And the crown's default, called due. And the court's charter, voided. And the stars—"

She stopped. Looked up. All around the chamber, on their silver wires, the bottled stars had begun — faintly, terribly, hopefully — to shine brighter, straining against their glass like birds hearing a window open somewhere far above.

"—the stars," the Herald finished, "go home."

The King closed his eyes. The weeping woman in the moth-wing cloak reached out and took the hand of the stranger beside her, and the stranger let her. Ines pushed off from the wall and crossed the floor to stand beside Dominic at the lectern, leaves in her hair, her thin-bright name almost whole again, and she did not touch the ledger, and she did not need to. She simply stood there, a gardener in a court of debtors, the one free thing in the room.

Dominic looked down at the ledger — at the grey leather, the soft corners, the four hundred and eleven names they had carried down a stair cut through remembering roots, the page with Ines's contract hanging loose where they had torn it, the whole terrible weight of it — and understood that the next moment belonged to them. No one else could lift it. No one else would.

They set both hands on the cover.

"Settled," Dominic said. Their voice was steady. It had never been steadier, and it never would be again, and that was fine; a voice only needs to be steady once, at the right moment, the way a blade only needs to be true for the length of one stroke. "In full. Before this court, before the record, before whatever hears. The Guild of Impossible Things surrenders its accounts. Let the reading be witnessed."

The Herald wrote the final line. The emissary drew its folds together like a fist closing. And high overhead, on their silver wires, the first bottled star cracked — a hairline of white light splitting the glass — and the whole chamber held its breath, because everything, everything, depended on what happened next.
