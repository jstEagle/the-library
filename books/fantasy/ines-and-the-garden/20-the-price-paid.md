## 20. The Price Paid

The silence after the reading of accounts was not silence at all.

It was the sound of a great weight being set down — the groan of stone easing after centuries of bearing what it was never meant to hold. Dominic stood in the court beneath the mountain with the ledger's ashes still warm in their satchel and watched the Undercourt King come apart like a knot untied.

He did not scream. That was the terrible part, and the merciful part, both at once. He simply grew *less*. The crown went first — it had been mortgaged so long ago that when the deep thing called in its claim, the metal seemed almost relieved to go, flowing off his brow like water finding its level, running down the dais steps in bright rivulets that sank into the flagstones and left no stain. Then the centuries peeled away from him, decade by decade, each one a thin translucent sheet lifting from his skin like bark from a birch. Dominic saw him young once — a boy in a shepherd's coat, standing in a green field under stars that had not yet been bottled — and then the field went too, and the boy, and there was nothing on the dais but a drift of pale dust that the last breath of the court's dying air stirred into a slow spiral and let fall.

Around the hall, the court was unmaking itself with the same strange gentleness. The courtiers — those who had been courtiers for three hundred years, those who had traded their faces for favor and their names for rank — were folding inward like letters returned to sender. One of them, a tall figure who had spent the entire audit watching Dominic with eyes like coins at the bottom of a well, paused beside them on her way to whatever came next.

"You read well," she said. Her voice was already half-gone, fraying at the edges like cloth worn through. "In four hundred years, no one has read aloud in this hall. They only whispered. It changes things, being said plainly."

"I'm sorry," Dominic said, because they could not think of anything else to say to someone dissolving.

"Don't be." She smiled, and for one moment the smile was a living woman's smile, startled and real. "We forgot we could leave. You reminded us." And she unfolded into light, or dust, or memory, and was gone.

* * *

Above them — far above, past the stair, past the garden, past the whole wounded city — the guildhall was waking to its own emptiness.

Master Corvane woke first, as he woke every morning, before the bell, reaching by habit for the master ledger that had sat at his bedside for thirty years. His hand closed on nothing. For a moment he thought himself robbed, and felt the old cold anger rise — and then he remembered, all at once and completely, everything: the deferred payments, the deferred souls, the neat columns in his own hand where human lives had been entered as *assets received* and *consideration pending*. He remembered them not as entries now but as people. A cooper's daughter. A ferryman. A gardener named Ines whose bread he had eaten at a midsummer feast without knowing whose debt had bought the wheat.

He sat up in the dark and found his hands would not stop shaking, and understood that this, too, was payment — the only kind left to him. Every trade voided. Every gain unwritten. The guild's impossible inventory — the lamp that burned backward, the door that opened onto yesterday, the coat with nine pockets full of weather — sat in its vaults below, going ordinary. By noon the masters would find the wards' stones gray and dumb as river pebbles. By evening the creditors they had never known they had would arrive with polite smiles and empty hands, asking after debts the guild no longer had the means to deny.

Corvane dressed slowly, in the dark, and did not light a lamp. There was a kind of honesty in sitting in the dark, he thought. He had sold other people's light for forty years. He would sit in what remained of his own.

* * *

Below, the deep thing finished its collection.

Dominic never saw it clearly — afterward they could not have described it, and suspected that anyone who claimed to have seen it clearly was lying or mad. It rose through the floor of the court like a thought rising through sleep, vast and patient and older than the mountain, older perhaps than the idea of mountains. It gathered the surrendered ledger — the whole of it, decades of soul-sales, the guild's crimes and the King's default and every bargain struck in whisper and bad faith — and it took the pages into itself the way soil takes rain.

Then it turned its attention to Dominic, and Ines, and the warden's empty armor still lying where it had fallen, and the two surviving petitioners huddled by the wall — the noble, Aldric, with his son's contract clutched to his chest like a shield.

*It is done,* the deep thing said, though it made no sound. The words arrived the way warmth arrives, or the sense of being watched. *The accounts are settled.*

"Not all of them," Dominic said. Their voice cracked on the words; they had been speaking for hours, reading debts aloud until their throat was raw, and now the adrenaline was draining out of them and leaving only exhaustion and a strange floating grief. "There are two names still in your books. Mine. And hers." They reached for Ines's hand. Her fingers were cold and earthy and gloriously, unmistakably present. "We entered ourselves freely. That was the price of justice — I understand that. So take us, if taking is what comes next."

The attention of the deep thing settled over them like snowfall.

*You misunderstand what you purchased,* it said. *When you gave your debt to me — the debt that bound you to the guild, the debt that bound her to the court — you did not sell yourselves. You paid what you owed. What remains after a debt is paid belongs to no book.* A pause, long as a season. *You are the first free persons this court has held in six hundred years. I suggest you leave before someone finds a way to charge you rent.*

And then — Dominic would swear to this later, though no one ever believed them — the deep thing laughed. Not cruelly. The way the ground laughs after a long drought, when the first rain hits it: a deep, shuddering relief that shakes loose small stones.

It sank. The court began, gently, to close.

* * *

They climbed.

The stair under the old temple garden had been a wound when Dominic descended it — a ragged tear through root and stone where the fallen wards had ripped the world open. Now it was healing as they climbed, the walls knitting smooth behind them, the roots drawing back into the earth with something that felt, absurdly, like satisfaction. Dominic's legs shook. Their hands were cut from the warden's knife, from the torn ledger page, from gripping Ines too hard during the moments when the deep thing's presence had pressed down on the hall like the bottom of the sea. Every muscle they owned had been spent. They climbed anyway, one step and then another, because Ines was climbing ahead of them and because the air above smelled — faintly, impossibly — of rain.

"Tell me something true," Ines said. She did not look back. Her voice was rough from disuse; she had been half-forgotten for months, her name worn thin as an old coin, and every word cost her something. But she kept talking, the way she used to talk over the seed trays in the temple garden, filling silence the way she filled everything — with growing things. "Anything. I've spent so long in a place where nothing was true. Tell me the sky is still up there. Tell me the basil died while I was gone and you let it."

"The basil died," Dominic said, and heard their own voice break into something between laughing and crying. "I watered it. I swear I watered it. It died anyway, out of spite."

"That's my basil." Still she did not look back, but Dominic saw her shoulders shake. "That's exactly my basil."

They climbed. Somewhere below them the last of the bottled stars was finding its way home. They could see the light of it through gaps in the stair-wall — brief gold flares passing upward like fish surfacing, each one trailing a thread of brightness that hung in the dark a moment and then snapped free. When a star passed close, the whole stair lit up warm as a kitchen, and Ines stopped once and put her palm against the stone as one went by, and said, softly, "Go on, then. Go home." As if it were a bird she'd raised.

Near the top, the roots closed overhead and the stair became a tunnel of living wood, and then there was grass under Dominic's hands, and night air, and —

Stars.

Not all of them. Not yet. The sky above the city was mostly black, the way it had been since the wards fell, but scattered across it — low in the east, high in the south, one stubborn bright point almost directly overhead — were returning stars, streaking home in slow golden arcs, and where each one landed the darkness healed around it like skin closing over a wound.

Ines sat down hard in the ruined garden among the broken planter-stones and the weeds that had taken the temple grounds in her absence, and looked up, and said nothing for a long time.

Dominic sat beside her. Empty-handed. The stolen ledger page was ash; the satchel was ash; the pen they had carried for eleven years, the good pen with the cracked nib that had written every entry of their indenture, had gone into the deep thing's books along with everything else. They had nothing. They had never had less.

They had never felt richer.

"Ines," they said.

"I know." She leaned against them, her head finding the hollow of their shoulder as if no time had passed at all, as if she had not been sold and half-forgotten and defended with a pruning hook. "I know. You don't have to say it."

"I sold myself into the books to get you out. I need you to know I'd do it again."

"And I need *you* to know," she said, and now she did turn, and her eyes in the starlight were wet and furious and alive, "that I sold myself first. To pay for your life. Years ago. Before you ever knew there was a ledger." Her hand found theirs in the dark and gripped it, gardener-strong, dirt still in the creases of her knuckles. "So whatever we owe each other, we're even. We've been even since before either of us knew how to count. Do you understand? Even. Free. Nothing owed."

Dominic thought about arguing. About pointing out that love was a debt that never cleared, that gratitude compounded daily, that — but no. That was ledger-thinking. That was the guild's arithmetic, the court's arithmetic, the whole rotten economy of survival-at-someone-else's-expense that they had just burned down from underneath. Ines was offering them something else, something that had no columns and no interest and no terms.

"All right," they said. "Even. Free. Nothing owed."

"Good." She lay back in the weeds, pulling them down with her, and they lay side by side in the ruined temple garden under the healing sky, too exhausted to move, too awake to sleep, watching the stars come home one by one. "Tomorrow," she murmured, her voice already sliding toward unconsciousness, "we start planting. The beds are wrecked. The well's probably fouled. We'll need to beg seed stock off half the city, and nobody will trust us, and the temple council will want explanations we can't give."

"Mm."

"And you'll have to learn actual gardening instead of just carrying things and looking apologetic."

"Mm."

"But tomorrow." Her breathing slowed. "Tonight we lie here and watch the stars come back, and we don't owe anyone a single thing, and that —" a yawn broke the sentence, and she finished it half-asleep, smiling — "that is the whole job, Dominic. That's the whole job."

The last thing Dominic saw before exhaustion took them too was a star settling into place directly above the temple — the same stubborn bright point that had hung there through the worst of the dark — and the darkness closing smoothly, seamlessly, completely around it, as if the sky had never been broken at all.

As if it had only been waiting for someone to come home.

* * *

They woke to birdsong and frost.

The frost was wrong for the season, and the birdsong was right, and the combination meant the world had tilted somewhere in the night and was finding its new balance. Dominic sat up stiff and cold in the weeds, Ines already gone from their side, and found her twenty feet away standing at the edge of the collapsed stairwell, looking down into the dark.

"It sealed," she said, without turning. "Overnight. Stone grown clean over it, like a scab. You'd never know there was a road down there at all."

"Is that good?"

"I think," Ines said slowly, "it means the road isn't needed anymore. Roads stay open while there's traffic." She crouched and pressed her hand flat to the new stone, and Dominic saw her lips move — not a spell, not a prayer, just a gardener saying something quiet to ground she intended to work. "It means we're not going back. Either direction."

She stood, brushed off her knees, and surveyed the ruined garden with the expression Dominic had seen her wear over blighted beds and drowned seedlings: not despair, but inventory. What lived. What didn't. What could be saved.

"Well," she said. "First order of business."

She pulled the pruning hook from her belt — the same tool she had raised against the warden, notched now along one edge where it had bitten something harder than wood — and drove it into the soil of the nearest bed, and levered up the first clod of the morning.

Somewhere across the city, bells began to ring — not the guild's measured counting-bells, but the old alarm pattern, then breaking off, then starting again in a new rhythm none of the towers had used in living memory. People would be in the streets. People would be asking questions. The guild's masters would be waking to vaults full of ordinary objects and ledgers full of blank pages, and some of them would be relieved, and some would be ruined, and Master Corvane, Dominic thought, was probably sitting in the dark in his fine clothes, learning at last how to be poor.

None of it could be helped from a garden bed. All of it, maybe, could be helped *from* a garden bed, eventually, if enough people planted enough things and kept honest enough books about it.

"Dominic," Ines said, wrestling the clod apart, revealing a fat pale worm and, beneath it, dark soil that smelled of everything the world was supposed to smell of. "You're a scribe. I'm going to need records. Seed counts, planting dates, who gave what, who owes —"

"Nothing," Dominic said. "Nobody owes anybody. New rule."

Ines grinned — the first true grin Dominic had seen on her face since the orchard, wide and crooked and entirely unguarded. "Fine. Then you'll need a different word for the book. Because we're keeping one. Everyone who comes here gets written down — name, what they brought, what they're good at. Not debts." She worked the soil as she talked, quick and sure, her hands remembering faster than her mind had. "*Accounts.* Of a different kind."

Dominic looked at her — at the notched tool, the ruined beds, the returning stars fading one by one into the paling east — and felt in their chest the specific ache that precedes beginning.

"What kind of accounts?" they asked.

Ines looked up, and behind her the sun cleared the temple wall and struck the fresh-turned earth, and it steamed faintly, like something breathing.

"The kind that grow," she said.
