## 21. Planting Season

The first thing I learned about the world after the stars came back was that it smelled different.

I had not expected that. I had braced myself for the strangeness of light — for the shock of open sky after years beneath the mountain, where the only stars were bottled and the only seasons were the King's moods. I had prepared myself for grief, too, because grief travels with you like a stone in your shoe. But no one had told me that a city could have a smell, and that its smell would change.

Before, Vellharrow had smelled of coal smoke and tallow and the sour breath of crowded streets — a city holding its breath. Now, walking up from the temple stair with Dominic's hand in mine, I caught green things. Wet earth. Someone's bread baking three streets over. The river, unbothered by any of it, moving the way rivers do.

"Smell that?" I said.

Dominic breathed in like a man testing wine. "Bread."

"The whole city smells like something growing over something dead." I said it without meaning to be profound. It was simply true, and truth comes out of me sideways sometimes, the way weeds come through flagstones.

The temple garden had gone to ruin in our absence — of course it had; gardens are promises that require someone present to keep them — but ruin is only another word for raw material. The wall still stood. The well still held water. And underneath the bindweed and the self-sown nettles, under the fallen branches of the plum tree that had been old when I was an apprentice, the soil remembered me.

I knelt down in the mess of it and pushed my fingers into the earth, and the earth said *oh, there you are* in the wordless way soil has, and I sat down right there among the nettles and cried like a child.

Dominic crouched beside me. They didn't touch me. They never did, in those first weeks — they'd hover near, hands half-raised, as if they were afraid I might be a ledger entry that would vanish if handled wrong. "Ines?"

"I'm all right." I wiped my face with the back of my wrist, leaving a stripe of good black dirt across it. "It's just — I spent four years being forgotten on purpose. Being remembered by dirt is —" My voice cracked. I laughed at myself, which helped. "Give me a minute."

They gave me a minute. Then they gave me their coat to kneel on, which was very like them: practical tenderness, wrapped around something enormous they couldn't say aloud.

We started clearing the next morning.

* * *

You cannot rebuild a garden alone, and we did not try.

Word moves strangely in a city whose stars went out and came back. People had seen the sky empty and refill. People had felt the wards fall — some of them had felt, though few could name it, the moment when every debt in Vellharrow quietly stopped existing, when the guild's great ledgers burned themselves blank in the night and the masters woke to find their trades voided and their vaults full of nothing but paper ash. There were riots at first. Then there was silence, the stunned kind. Then people began, cautiously, to look at one another.

Into that looking, a garden is a good thing to look at.

The first to come was a boy named Tam, maybe twelve, who had been bound to a chandler until his contract dissolved and his master turned him out with nothing. He stood at the gate with his fists in his pockets and asked if we needed labor, and I looked at his hollow cheeks and said we needed a gardener's apprentice, which was a different question entirely, and watched him not understand for a moment and then understand completely.

Then came Marla, who had been a ward-keeper on the border before the wards fell — she arrived with her hands still scarred from the backlash and asked, in a voice like gravel, whether we had any use for someone who knew how to hold a boundary. I gave her the north wall and told her walls could grow things now instead of keeping things out.

Then others. A widow who knew herbs. Two sisters who had been sold, briefly, to the court beneath the mountain, and returned when the trades voided — they flinched from enclosed spaces and worked best under open sky, so we gave them the orchard rows. An old man who had been the guild's gardener once, decades ago, before he understood what his masters were balancing their books with; he came to the gate carrying a bundle of tools wrapped in oilcloth and could not speak at all, just held the bundle out, and I took it, and he stayed.

By midsummer we were twenty. By autumn, thirty. We never posted a notice. Word simply moved, the way water finds low ground: everyone discarded by the old systems, everyone whose worth had been written down somewhere in someone else's book, eventually heard that there was a place on the temple hill where the books were different.

Because they were different. That was the whole point.

* * *

Here is what I know about ledgers, having been one.

When the deep thing below the mountain accepted the guild's accounts — when Dominic stood in that impossible court and offered up the entire weight of recorded debt, every soul-price and deferred payment and interest accrued on human lives — it did not simply erase them. Erasure would have been mercy of a kind, but the deep thing does not deal in mercy. It deals in balance. And so the debts were *returned*: every stolen year given back to the one who lost it, every sold soul released to itself, every coin extracted unjustly flowing backward through the city like a tide going out.

And what remained, when the tide went out, was us. Bare-handed. Free.

Free is a strange country. I had been property so long that freedom felt like a fever at first — too much room, too many choices, no one to tell me what I was worth. For the first month I woke before dawn out of habit, waiting for a bell that no longer rang, waiting to be counted toward someone else's balance.

So we made new bells. New counts.

The garden keeps its own books now. Not of debts — Ines, I told Dominic the first week, if you ever write *owed* in that book I will bury you in the compost — but of seeds saved, of names, of favors given freely and marked down only so they can be remembered, never repaid. It sits on a shelf in the tool shed, a plain bound volume that Dominic made with their own hands, scribe-trained fingers stitching the spine. On the first page, in their careful hand:

*What was given:*
*A coat, to Marla, in the cold of the first week.*
*Bread, to Tam, daily, no questions.*
*A name restored: Odile Okonkwo's mother, spoken aloud at the gate, so that someone besides her son remembers her face.*

That last one undid me when I read it. Dominic had paid a memory at the toll-gate beneath the mountain — their mother's face, gone forever, traded for passage. But names can be rebuilt the way gardens can, from cuttings, from other people's remembering. So we asked everyone who had ever known her. The baker's daughter, grown old herself. A retired priest. Piece by piece, we grew the face back: her laugh first, then her hands, then, one evening in October, her whole self, standing in Dominic's eyes as they described her to me over supper.

"She liked the rain," Dominic said, and smiled, and cried a little, and kept eating. "She liked the rain and she thought I was funny. Both of those things are true even if I'm the only evidence left."

*Given freely,* I wrote in the book that night. *A face, restored. No repayment required or possible.*

* * *

There are hard days. I want to be honest about that, because the old systems survived on dishonesty — on the lie that suffering was orderly, that ledgers were neutral, that everything balanced in the end if you just kept the arithmetic clean.

Some nights Tam wakes screaming. He was sold once, briefly, before the voiding — a week in the court's orchard, a week of being half-forgotten — and he carries it in his sleep. I go to him and sit by his cot the way someone once sat by mine, in the years I don't fully remember, and I don't ask him to talk. I tell him about seeds instead. About how a seed is a thing that agrees to be buried and trusts the dark to turn it into something else. He usually falls asleep before I finish. That's fine. The story isn't really for him anyway. It's for me.

Some days Marla stands at the north wall staring at the horizon where the wards used to shimmer, and her scarred hands shake, and she asks me — every time, the same question — "Was it my fault? Could I have held it?"

"No," I tell her, every time. "The wards fell because the guild starved them. You can't hold a wall someone else is selling the stones out of."

And some days Dominic goes quiet in the way they do, the old guilt rising like groundwater, and I find them sitting under the plum tree doing sums on scrap paper — auditing, always auditing, the habit of a lifetime — counting up the people the guild destroyed and finding the number unbearable.

"You saved one," I tell them, kneeling beside them, taking the paper away. "You always say it like it's small. One. Me. As if I'm arithmetic."

"You're not small," they say. "The saving was. It was one page, torn out of one book. It changed nothing about the system."

"It changed everything," I say, "because systems are made of pages. Tear enough of them and the binding fails." I put my hand flat on the earth between us. "Look around you, Dominic. Thirty people who were entries. Look at what grew out of one torn page."

They look. That's the thing about Dominic — they can be argued with, which is more than I could ever say for the guildmasters. They look, and the guilt recedes a little, the way fog recedes when the sun insists.

* * *

Winter came, the first honest winter in years — real snow, real cold, the sky wheeling overhead with all its stars accounted for. Astronomers in the capital published frantic pamphlets proving the constellations had returned *changed*, a few stars missing forever, a few new ones burning where none had been. The ones freed from the bottles below, people said. The court's hoard, scattered back across heaven.

On the longest night we planted garlic.

It's tradition, or became one that night, which is the same thing if you keep doing it. Everyone in the garden came out with lanterns and cold-reddened hands, and we pressed cloves into the frozen ground in rows, each clove a small act of faith that spring would bother arriving. Tam planted his crooked. Marla planted hers in a perfect line, because she was Marla. The old man who never spoke planted his with a ceremony so precise it must have been older than any of us, and when he finished he touched two fingers to the soil and then to his forehead, and I copied him, and it felt right.

Dominic stood at the edge of the lamplight, watching. They had a scrap of paper in their hand — I saw it, the white rectangle against the dark — and I knew without asking what it was. Another page. Another audit. They carried the habit like a scar.

"What is it?" I asked, coming to stand with them.

"A list," they said. "Of everyone who came through the gate this year. Names, and what they brought, and what they need." They paused. "It's not a ledger. I've checked. There's no column for what anyone owes."

"I know," I said. "I've read it. You wrote my name in it the day we opened the gate, and under 'brought' you wrote *plum tree, established*, and under 'needs' you wrote *nothing*."

"That last part was aspirational."

"It was accurate." I bumped my shoulder against theirs. Cold air, warm shoulder, lantern-light on snow. Somewhere above us the new stars burned, unhurried, unowned. "Nothing owed. Say it until it stops feeling like a trick."

"*Nothing owed,*" they repeated, obedient and skeptical, and I laughed, and the sound of it went up into the winter sky where nobody could bottle it.

* * *

Spring, when it came, found the garlic up in straight green spears, and the plum tree blooming so heavily it looked embarrassed, and the gate of the temple garden standing open from dawn to dark, as it always did, as it always would.

I was turning compost — good work, honest smell, worms doing the accounting — when I noticed the stranger at the gate.

Not one of ours. Not one of the usual frightened, hungry, discarded ones, either. This was a woman in traveling clothes gone gray with road dust, middle-aged, upright, with the particular stillness of someone who has made a long journey on purpose. She stood at the gate a while without entering, reading the sign we'd nailed there — *All who enter may leave freely. Nothing here is owed.* — and I watched her mouth move through the words twice, as if translating, or disbelieving.

Then she stepped inside, and walked past the beds and the bee-hives and the children turning soil, straight to where I stood with my fork in the compost heap, and stopped, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't parse — hope and dread braided together, tight as rope.

"Ines," she said. My name in her mouth sounded strange and formal, like a word from an old song. "My name is Odile Okonkwo. I'm looking for my brother. Dominic Underhill. They passed through here — I've followed them through three cities and every rumor says —"

The fork slipped from my hand.

Three cities. Road dust. A face I had never seen but had helped rebuild, piece by piece, from other people's memories — the laugh, the hands, the love of rain — and here it was, standing in front of me, wearing dust and hope, *real*.

"They're in the shed," I managed. "Writing. They're always —" My voice failed. I grabbed her sleeve instead, this stranger, this miracle, and pulled her across the garden toward the tool shed, past the seed racks, past Tam gawking with dirt on his nose, toward the door behind which Dominic sat hunched over the good book with a pen in their hand.

From inside, faintly, I could hear the scratch of the nib — the sound of a life being written down honestly, at last.

I raised my hand to knock.
