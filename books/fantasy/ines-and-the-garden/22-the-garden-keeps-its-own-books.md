## 22. The Garden Keeps Its Own Books

*Ines's voice*

The first book we kept was a seed book.

Not a ledger — I want to be clear about that, because the difference matters more than anything else I have learned in two lifetimes, the one above and the one below. A ledger is a list of what you owe. A seed book is a list of what you have. Dominic understood this before I did. They had spent their whole life inside other people's arithmetic — columns of debt, entries in no hand they knew, souls sold to keep impossible things possible — and when they finally held a blank book in their hands, on the first warm morning after the stars came home, they did not know how to write in it.

I showed them.

You start with what you saved. That is all. You start with the bean seeds from the court's orchard, the ones that came up through my fingers like small dark moons when the bottled light burst free and everything down there that had been hoarded began, at last, to give itself away. You start with the pear cutting I smuggled up the stair in the lining of my coat, wrapped in damp moss, its roots already reaching for something even in the dark. You start with the marigold seeds the temple children brought us in paper twists, and the fennel that volunteered itself in the cracked corner of the old wall, and the single clove of garlic someone left on our doorstep with no note at all.

We wrote them all down. Not as debts. Not as assets. Just as *here*. Here is a thing that exists now because someone chose to keep it alive.

The garden had been dead for three years by then — dead since the wards fell, since the guild burned its papers, since Ines Okonkwo vanished from the city rolls and her beds went to weed and her glasshouse panes were carried off one by one until only the skeleton remained, iron ribs against a starless sky. But dead is a word for things that cannot come back, and gardens are never that. Gardens are only ever sleeping, or waiting, or being patient in a way that would embarrass us if we paid attention.

The soil remembered me. I know how that sounds. I know exactly how that sounds, and I say it anyway, because it is true. When I knelt in the old bed where I had grown basil for the temple kitchen, the earth turned over under my hands soft as bread dough, black and breathing, full of worms that had survived on nothing but rain and stubbornness. The roots I had left behind — comfrey, deep-rooted, unkillable — had been holding the hillside together all this time, doing the work I could not do, keeping the garden's bones from washing away into the stair that led down to the court beneath the mountain.

Roots remember being tended. I had written that once, in a letter to Dominic, back when letters between us were the only currency either of us trusted. I had forgotten writing it. They had kept it. When I came up out of the ground with my name worn thin as an old coin, it was Dominic who read my own words back to me until I could hear them in my own voice again.

That is also a kind of bookkeeping. Remember that too.

* * *

*Dominic's voice*

I want to tell you about the morning the Guild of Impossible Things stopped being impossible.

It happened quietly, which surprised everyone, including me. We had all braced for fire — for masters vanishing in the night, for wardens burning whatever evidence the voided trades had left behind, for some last desperate act of ledger-balancing that would take the rest of us down with it. Instead, on the third morning after the stars returned, the guildhall doors simply stood open. All of them. The doors that should stay shut, standing open in daylight, and the masters sitting in the great hall with their ledgers stacked in front of them like men waiting for a verdict.

In a way, they were. The Reading of Accounts had been spoken aloud below the mountain, every crime entered into a record older than the guild, older than the mountain, and there is no appeal from a record like that. The trades were voided. The souls sold — Ines, the nobleman's son, the forty-one others whose names I read aloud while my voice shook and then steadied — were returned or released or, in the cases too late to mend, mourned openly, publicly, with their names said in full for the first time in decades.

And the guild's inventory — the bottled storms, the doors to nowhere, the clocks that ran on borrowed hours — all of it went inert. Impossible things, it turns out, are only impossible when you pay for them with other people. Paid for honestly, they become merely difficult, and difficult things can be done in the open, slowly, by many hands.

The masters dissolved the guild themselves. I watched them do it. Master Corvane — who had signed Ines's contract with a hand so steady it made me sick for years without knowing why — set his seal into the brazier himself and let it melt. He looked smaller afterward. Not penitent, exactly. I am not sure he ever learned penitence. But smaller, the way a man looks when the architecture of his certainty has been removed and he must stand on the ground like everyone else.

He came to me before he left the city. He stood in the doorway of the scriptorium where I still worked, finishing the last honest documents of my indenture — because yes, I finished it, all of it, every page, because a debt honestly incurred and honestly served is not the same thing as a soul sold, and I needed to know the difference in my own hands — and he said, "Underhill. What will you do now?"

"Grow things," I said.

He stared at me. "You were the best scribe the guild ever produced."

"I know," I said. "That's why I know what books are for."

I don't think he understood. I have made peace with that. Some lessons only take root in soil that has been turned.

* * *

*Ines's voice*

The people came slowly at first, and then all at once.

They came the way people come to a lit window on a cold night — one at a time, drawn by something they couldn't name, standing at the gate a long while before knocking. The woman whose husband had been sold to the court eleven years ago, who had been told he died of fever, who walked through our rows of beans with her hand over her mouth and then sat down in the dirt and stayed until dark. The boy who had worked the guild's drying rooms, whose lungs were full of paper dust, who asked if he could learn to grow something instead. The old temple bell-ringer, half-blind now, who could still tell a healthy seedling by smell and taught us all to do the same.

We took them in. That was the whole rule, and it wasn't even a rule — it was just what the garden was *for*, now. The old systems discarded people the way a scribe discards a spoiled page: cleanly, without ceremony, the error struck through and the account rebalanced elsewhere. We had both been struck-through pages. We knew what it was to be the error in someone else's arithmetic. So the garden became the place where the struck-through went, and it grew fat and generous on them, because gardens always do. Discarded people make the best gardeners. We have something to prove to no one but the soil.

Dominic built the benches. I mean this literally — they measured, planed, joined, sweated over every joint, refusing help out of some scribe's pride in doing a thing completely — and then they sat on the finished bench, looked at their blistered hands, and laughed until they cried. "I spent ten years making entries," they told me, "and I have never once made a *thing*." Now they make things constantly. Cold frames. A water clock, honest, powered by nothing but gravity and rain. A little shed with a green door where the seed book lives, chained to nothing, locked to nothing, open to anyone.

Because here is the part I want carved somewhere permanent, in stone if I can manage it, in the memory of everyone who reads this if I cannot:

**The seed book has no debts in it.**

It has seeds saved: 214 varieties as of last frost, each one dated, each one named for the person who brought it. It has names remembered: the forty-one, and the ones before the forty-one, going back through the guild's whole rotten history, recovered from fragments of ledger, from court testimony, from the memories of the living — every soul the old system erased, written now in a good round hand on good rag paper, read aloud every year on the night the stars went out, so that the darkest day of the world became the day of remembering instead. It has favors given freely: who lent whom the cold frame, who taught whom grafting, who sat with the widow Amara through her worst winter, who carried water for whose bed during the drought month. None of it owed. All of it given. And here is the miracle, the one I still cannot stop marveling at, though I lived through worse miracles below the mountain:

Nobody keeps score anyway.

The favors circulate. They come back around changed, transformed, unrecognizable — a favor returns as a different favor, larger or smaller, later or sooner, and it does not matter, because no one is counting. The book records them the way a garden records rain: gratefully, without expectation, as weather.

* * *

*Dominic's voice*

There is one entry in the seed book that is not a seed, a name, or a favor. It sits alone on its own page, and we have agreed it stays there, and we have agreed never to explain it to visitors unless they ask, and most of them ask.

It says: *One memory, surrendered freely at the toll of names. A mother's face. If anyone finds it, growing anywhere, it belongs to whoever recognizes it.*

I gave up my mother's face at the gate beneath the temple garden, and I have never gotten it back, and I have stopped looking for it, mostly. But sometimes — this is the strange grace of it — sometimes I see her anyway. In the set of a stranger's jaw at market. In the way the widow Amara hums while she works, a tune I do not know and somehow know completely. In Ines's laugh, once, at dusk, when the light came through the glasshouse at a particular angle and she turned her head and for half a heartbeat I was five years old and utterly safe.

I wrote the entry because a lost thing should have an address. Even if no one ever comes for it. Even if it never comes home. An address says: *this mattered. This was someone's.* The guild's ledgers never said that about anything. They said what things cost. Our book says what things meant.

Ines thinks the memory is in the garden somewhere. She may be right. She believes things like that — that nothing truly given is ever destroyed, that it goes into the ground and comes up green in some form we don't recognize. I used to think that was sentiment. Then I watched a court built on hoarded light dissolve into ordinary generosity, and I watched forty-one erased names come back to the mouths of the living, and I decided she might be right about everything.

* * *

*Ines's voice*

Last night we planted the pear tree.

The cutting from the court's orchard — the one I carried up the stair against my heart, wrapped in moss, through the dark, past the gate where Dominic paid in a mother's face, up into a world with no stars — that cutting spent two years in a pot, sulking, refusing to grow more than a hand's height. It knew where it had come from. Trees hold grudges; any orchardist will tell you. It had been taken from a place where it was fed on bottled light and tended by the half-forgotten, and it did not trust the open sky, even after the sky filled back up with stars, even after the first real rain.

So we waited. We repotted it twice. Dominic talked to it — actually talked, low and reasonable, the way they negotiated with the Undercourt King, and I swear the tree listened the way the King never did. And last night at dusk, when it had finally put out a foot of new wood, hard and honest and smelling of pears, we carried it to the center of the garden and planted it where the old temple's heart-stone used to sit, in the exact spot where the stair to the court had opened and then closed forever, sealed under six feet of good soil and a young tree's roots.

Dominic dug. I held the tree upright. The neighbors formed a ring around us without being asked — Amara, the boy from the drying rooms, the bell-ringer leaning on his daughter's arm, a dozen others whose names are all in the book — and when the roots went into the earth, everyone reached down at once and pressed the soil firm with their hands, all those hands, layered over ours, tamping the tree into the world.

No one spoke for a moment. Then the bell-ringer said, "What will it grow?" and Dominic said, "Pears," and everyone laughed, and then Amara said, quieter, "No. What will it *grow*?" and nobody laughed, because she meant the other thing, the true thing, and everyone heard it.

A tree planted over a sealed door to a court of traded souls. A tree fed by the memory of everything that happened down there. What will come off its branches, in twenty years, in fifty? Sweetness, we hope. Sweetness grown out of grief, which is the only sweetness that lasts. Fruit enough for everyone who comes to the gate, which is the only economy worth practicing. Shade for whoever needs to sit and not explain themselves, which is the only justice most of us ever get.

And underneath it all, holding it all, drinking from it all: roots that remember being tended, growing down through the dark toward the place where the court used to be, finding nothing there now but empty stone and clean groundwater, and turning, as roots do, toward the nearest nourishment — which is us, which is this, which is the garden above and everyone in it.

* * *

*Dominic's voice*

This morning I opened the seed book to a fresh page and found that someone had already written in it.

Not a debt. Not a theft — the book sleeps in the unlocked shed, and anyone could, and no one ever has. An entry, in a careful round hand I didn't recognize, dated yesterday:

*One pear tree, planted by many hands. Location: the old heart-stone. Expected yield: unknown. Given to: whoever comes.*

Below it, a second line, in a different hand, younger, less certain:

*One question, offered freely: when the fruit comes, who will we be?*

I stood in the doorway of the green-door shed for a long time, reading those two lines, while the morning came up gold through the glasshouse panes and Ines called from the bean rows that the fennel had self-seeded again, shamelessly, everywhere, the way generosity does.

Who will we be? I don't know. That is the honest entry. We were a scribe and a gardener, a debtor and a debt, a rescuer and a rescue, and none of those words fit anymore, and the new ones haven't grown in yet. We are building belonging instead of being granted it, and building is slow, and the plans change daily, and the book fills up with seeds and names and favors that no one counts, and the tree puts down roots through a sealed door into the dark, and the dark, as far as we can tell, lets it.

But I know this much. I know it the way I know the weight of a ledger, the way I know the sound of a contract being torn, the way I know Ines's footsteps on the garden path before I see her:

Whatever we are becoming, we are becoming it together, and no one is selling it, and no one is buying it, and the book it's written in belongs to all of us, and the first word in it was never *owe*.

It was *here*.

Here is a thing that exists now because someone chose to keep it alive.

Here is the garden. It keeps its own books. And this morning, for the first time, it is asking us a question — and we get to answer it with our hands.
