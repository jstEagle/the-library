## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There are three ways to die in the palace of Vell Marrow, and I have survived all of them so many times they've stopped being interesting.

The first way is the obvious one: poison. That's my trade, technically. Royal Food-Taster to His Radiant Majesty Aurel the Third, who is not radiant and was never the Third by any honest counting, but titles are like shoes in this city—nobody asks where they came from as long as they fit. Every morning I stand in a marble antechamber off the royal kitchens while a nervous boy named Pell brings me a covered tray, and I eat a spoonful of everything His Radiance will eat that day, and then we all wait to see if I fall over.

I have never fallen over. Fifteen years. Not once. Not a stomach cramp, not a nosebleed, not so much as a bad taste in my mouth that wasn't already there before breakfast.

The second way to die in Vell Marrow is ambition, which kills faster than arsenic and is harder to detect. The third way is being noticed. I have made a religion of avoiding both.

My name is August Villalobos. If you asked anyone in the palace—the chambermaids, the guards, the Chancellor himself—they would take a moment to place me, and then their faces would do that soft, apologetic thing faces do when they're reaching for a name they've heard a hundred times and never once thought about. *The taster. Yes. Small fellow. Quiet.* That is the whole of my reputation, and I have polished it for fifteen years the way other men polish swords.

This morning, the tray had eggs poached in wine, a heel of white bread with butter and honey, smoked river trout, stewed quince, and a small cup of chocolate, because His Radiance has developed a taste for the southern bean and the treasury has developed a taste for indulging him.

I started with the eggs, as always. Wine-poached eggs are a coward's dish—too much acid, too much sweetness, anything nasty hiding in them gets masked twice over—but cowards' dishes are exactly where assassins like to work, and I am paid, modestly, to think like an assassin who has given up.

Eggs: clean. Bread: clean, though the honey was new, from a different apiary than last week, and I noted it. New suppliers get noted. Trout: clean. Quince: clean, cloying, forgettable.

Then the chocolate.

Now, I want to be clear about what happened next, because for years afterward—in the weeks when everything I believed about myself came apart like wet paper—I returned to this moment again and again, trying to decide how long I had actually known. The honest answer is: immediately. The comfortable answer, the one I told myself while I stood there holding the little porcelain cup with steam curling up past my face, is that it took me a moment.

It did not take me a moment.

Nightshade. I knew it the way you know your own name. Bitter at the back edge, sweet underneath, a green darkness folded into the chocolate the way a liar folds truth into a compliment. Belladonna, prepared properly—which is to say, prepared by someone who knew what they were doing, because badly prepared nightshade tastes like a hedge and this tasted like nothing at all until you went looking. A dose sized for a man of His Radiance's considerable appetite. Enough to stop his heart between the second sip and the third.

And beneath it, threaded through it like a hair through dough, something else.

I have tasted every poison this empire's alchemists can brew and most of the ones its enemies can smuggle. I know what hemlock does, what aconite does, what the gray toadstools from the northern fens do. This was none of them. It was older. That sounds like nonsense—I know it sounds like nonsense—but taste is a language, and this was a word in a language I didn't speak, and every instinct I owned, instincts I hadn't even known I possessed, stood up at attention all at once. It tasted the way a locked door smells. It tasted like a cellar. It tasted, God help me, like something waiting.

I put the cup down.

"Problem, Master Villalobos?" Pell asked. He's nineteen, freckled, terrified of me for reasons I've never understood, since the scariest thing about me is my ledger handwriting.

"The chocolate's bitter," I said. "His Radiance complained about the batch on Tuesday. This is the same batch."

Was it? Almost certainly not. Did His Radiance complain about Tuesday's batch? He complains about everything; the odds were excellent. Pell squinted at the tray, sighed the sigh of a kitchen boy whose morning has just gotten longer, and carried the cup away to be remade.

Which left the rest of the chocolate, the poisoned remainder, sitting in the warming pot on the taster's sideboard, mine to deal with.

Here is what you must understand about my job, the part they don't put in the hiring notice: a food-taster who finds poison has two duties. The first is to report it. The second—and this is the one that keeps men like me employed—is to make sure the finding never becomes an incident. An incident means investigations. Investigations mean witch-finders walking the kitchens with their brass instruments and their lists of questions, and witch-finders ask questions like "Where were you on the night of—" and "Who are your associates—" and "What do you know about—" and every one of those questions is a trapdoor over the same pit, because a man who sells charms out of a stall in the night market cannot survive scrutiny. A man who sells charms should not exist at all.

So I reported nothing, and I disposed of the chocolate, and I did it the way I've done it eleven times before in fifteen years—eleven times, yes, I count them, each one a small stone I carry—by pouring it into the clay pot where the kitchen dumps its ash, stirring it under, and covering it with yesterday's cold cinders. Nightshade breaks down in ash lye fast enough that no dog will die and no diviner will find it. Then I finished the safe portion of His Radiance's breakfast, signed the ledger—*all dishes tasted, no adverse effects*—and walked out into the corridor wearing the face of a man thinking about absolutely nothing, which is the face I wear best.

But my hands weren't steady. And that was new.

Fifteen years, and the poisons had never once bothered me. I'd stopped wondering why around year three. Some men don't bruise. Some men can't carry a tune. I couldn't be poisoned. It was simply the shape of me, like being left-handed, like having crooked little fingers. The guild physician tested me when I was hired—drip of thallium in wine, watched me for an hour, shrugged, wrote *inherited tolerance* in his book, and moved on to more interesting patients. Inherited from whom? Nobody asked. Nobody ever asks the taster about his family. The taster is furniture that eats.

I told myself the shaking was the near-thing itself. Any man's hands shake after swallowing nightshade, even if the nightshade slides off him like rain off wax. I told myself that all the way down the servants' stair, all the way across the inner court, all the way to my rooms above the old granary where the palace houses its less memorable staff.

I did not believe a word of it. That chocolate had been aimed. Not at His Radiance—any of a dozen dishes could have carried death to him, and the assassin had left those alone. Aimed at the cup I would drink from, the one dish where the poisoner could be certain the taster would take a full swallow rather than a courtesy sip. Someone in those kitchens knew exactly how I worked. Someone had built a dose for *me*, and laced it with whatever that older thing was, and the message underneath the murder was plain as a footprint in flour:

*We know what you are.*

The trouble was, I didn't.

* * *

By nightfall I had done what I always do with unbearable thoughts, which is bury them under commerce.

Twice a week, when the moon is dark or nearly so, I go down to the Tallowmarket—the night market that sprawls through the tannery district like spilled soup—and I sell charms. Real ones. That's the joke nobody in the palace would find funny: the empire forbids charm-work on pain of the witch-finder's iron, and its own food-taster is the best charm-seller in six districts. My immunity apparently extends only to poison. Poverty finds me perfectly vulnerable.

My stock is small and honest, which is to say, mostly useless and priced accordingly. Knots of red thread against cramps. Dried rue in a sachet against nightmares. Copper coins hammered thin and stamped with a spiral that I tell buyers is for luck in court cases, because everyone in Vell Marrow has a court case and luck is what they can afford. I keep a few serious pieces under the counter for serious customers—a fever-break that works maybe one time in five, a salve for burns that works better than it should—and I charge accordingly and never explain. Explanation invites questions. Questions invite the iron.

My rule—one rule, fifteen years, the closest thing I have to a creed—is: *be invisible.* Invisible in the palace, invisible in the market, invisible in the ledgers of the world. A man who is invisible cannot be interrogated, conscripted, blamed, or missed. I chose it young and I have kept it like a vow, and if you'd asked me last week what the rule had cost me, I'd have said nothing, because I'd have been lying, and lying smoothly is also part of the craft.

That night the market was thick with fog off the tannery ponds, the good kind of fog, the kind that blurs faces and makes the lantern light go soft and dishonest. I had my stall open between a woman selling eels and a man selling, allegedly, relics of Saint Ferren's foot, and business was its usual trickle. A dockhand bought a knot for his mother's cough. Two students bought luck spirals and giggled, because students think danger is a flavor.

Then the washerwoman came.

Her name was Maren—everyone in the Tallowmarket knows Maren, she washes for half the merchants on Tanner's Row and has arms like hawser rope and a voice that carries over fog. She'd bought a fertility charm from me in the spring. I remembered it because she'd haggled, which almost nobody does over fertility charms; the desperate pay asking price and thank you for it. She'd haggled anyway, won, pressed her coin into my palm like it was a secret, and said, "Third year trying. If this doesn't take, I'm done hoping," and I'd given her the standard line—*it helps what wants to happen along*—and hated myself mildly, the way I always do, because the standard line is true and insufficient and I have never found a better one.

She arrived at my stall that night carrying a bundle of blankets, and she unwrapped it right there between the eels and the false saint's foot, and inside the blankets was a baby. Red-faced, furious, alive, perhaps three weeks old.

"You said it helps what wants to happen along," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she laughed at herself for cracking, and held the bundle up like evidence at a trial. "Well. It wanted to happen along *fast*, Master Villalobos. Conceived within the month. Born early and strong as an ox. The midwife swears she's never seen a child take to the breast like this one. There's something in her. Something *keen*."

"That's wonderful," I said, and meant it, and felt the floor of the world tilt one degree.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I have turned over ten thousand times since. My charms are thread and dried herbs and hammered copper. They are theater with a margin. I sell hope at cost plus labor, and the labor is the honest part. In fifteen years, I have never once sold a charm that worked. I'd have known. You know. The fever-breaks fail four times in five and I refund the fifth grudgingly. Nothing I sell does anything, and everyone sensible knows it, and they buy anyway, because hope at cost plus labor is still a bargain in this city.

Maren's baby was not hope. Maren's baby was a fact, red and yelling, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of milk and woodsmoke.

"Must have been a good month for it," I said, which was the smoothest lie available, and Maren laughed and paid me double for a blessing-charm for the child, and I took the coin because refusing would have required explaining, and gave her the spiral-stamped copper and watched her walk off into the fog like a woman walking on air.

I closed the stall early.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the canal, because the long way is emptier and I needed empty. The fog had followed me up from the Tallowmarket, thinning as it climbed, and the gas lamps along the canal burned halos into it, and the water below was black and slow and smelled of the river's endless patience.

Let me describe my life as I understood it that night, because it was the last night I understood it.

I wake before the bells. I taste the tyrant's breakfast. I sign the ledger. I buy bread from the baker on the corner who calls me "master" without meaning anything by it. Twice a week I sell harmless magic to people who need to believe in something, and I am careful, and I am quiet, and I am alone—not lonely, I'd have told you, *alone*, there's a difference, isn't there?—and at the end of each year I have a little more money hidden in the wall behind the stove, and the usurper lives, and the empire grinds on, and nobody, nobody in the whole vast machine of Vell Marrow, looks twice at August Villalobos.

Eleven poisons in fifteen years, and my body shrugging them off like a duck sheds canal water. A chocolate cup aimed at me personally, laced with something that tasted like a locked door. A washerwoman's impossible baby, born keen, born *something*, three streets from my stall.

Three facts. On their own, each one survivable. A man can live his whole life alongside three unconnected oddities, the way you can live beside a crack in the ceiling.

But standing at the canal rail with the fog beading on my collar, I made the mistake of connecting them. I'm a taster; connection is my entire profession. One ingredient out of place tells you what's cooking. And these three ingredients, set side by side in the pan of my skull, began to smell like a dish I had no recipe for—something old, something patient, something that had been simmering under this city for longer than the usurper's stolen throne, longer than the Godfall they teach children about in school, longer, maybe, than the stones the palace stands on.

Something that knew my name.

A barge went by below me, lanterns doubled in the black water, a bargeman singing something tuneless and content. Ordinary. Everything ordinary. The lamps, the fog, the far-off bells of the midnight watch. The most dangerous city in the world, going about its evening.

I went home. I checked the coins behind the stove—still there; paranoia is a habit, not a symptom. I banked the fire. I lay down in my narrow bed above the granary and stared at the ceiling beam and told myself, in the firm voice I use on frightened customers, that tomorrow I would taste the breakfast, sign the ledger, and begin, quietly, carefully, invisibly, finding out who in those kitchens wanted me dead—or wanted me something worse than dead.

Sleep took its time. When it finally came, it came strange and warm, like sinking into a bath someone else had drawn, and at the very bottom of it, just before the dark closed over, I heard—not heard, *felt*, the way you feel a hearth from two rooms away—a pulse under the floor of the world. Slow. Deep. Patient as the river.

Like something enormous, turning over in its sleep beneath the city.

Getting ready to wake.
