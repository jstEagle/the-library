## 2. Charms for the Desperate

There is a particular hour in the night market of Vell's Crossing when the lamps have burned down to their dregs and the honest merchants have gone home, and what remains is the other kind. That was my hour. Fifteen years of tasting poison for a man I'd cheerfully see dead had taught me to move through crowds like smoke through a keyhole, and the night market after midnight was where smoke went to do its business.

I want to be clear about something before we go further: I am not a fraud. A fraud sells you nothing and calls it magic. I sold you almost nothing and called it magic, which is an entirely different profession, and one with better margins.

The charms were real — or real enough. A knot of red thread tied nine times while thinking of someone's name will keep that name warm in your chest for a season. A pinch of grave-dust from a person who died content, sewn into a hem, makes sleep come easier. None of it would survive a witch-finder's inspection, but then neither would most of my customers, and that was rather the point. The usurper's gods were official gods, licensed gods, gods you paid tithes to and received nothing back from but sermons. The old charms were small, illegal, and honest about being small. People liked that. People trust a thing more when it admits its limits.

My rule — the rule that had kept me alive past thirty, which in my line of work is roughly the life expectancy of a mayfly with a gambling problem — was simple: be invisible. Not clever-invisible, not disguise-and-false-moustache invisible, but forgettable. The kind of face a witness describes as "a man, I suppose, medium." I had cultivated it like a gardener cultivates moss: patiently, in the dark, with no expectation of praise.

So there I was at my corner behind the tannery stalls, hood up, hands cupped around a stub of candle, selling immortality by the thimbleful to people who couldn't afford bread.

* * *

Marta Oves came to me just past the second bell.

I knew her the way I knew half my customers — by her troubles rather than her face. She washed linens for three of the noble houses up the hill, which meant her knuckles were cracked white and her forearms were strong as dock rope, and she had been married eleven years to a cooper named Tam who was kind and sober and built like a wine barrel himself, and they had no children. Eleven years. In Vell's Crossing that made you a subject of charity and suspicion in equal measure, and Marta had run out of patience for both.

"You're the charm-man," she said. Not a question. She looked around once, quick, the way everyone did, though the alley held nothing but a cat with opinions and a pile of tannery refuse that smelled like sin concentrated.

"I'm a man selling thread," I said. "Whatever you imagine it does is between you and your imagination."

"Save it," she said. "The washerwomen talk. You sold Bess Corrin a knot for her husband's cough and he stopped coughing."

Bess's husband had stopped coughing because he'd also stopped drinking river water out of the same jug as his consumptive brother, but I saw no profit in mentioning this. "Thread and knots," I said. "Coin and silence."

She put a cloth bundle on the barrel between us and unwrapped it with the care of a woman handling eggs. Inside: coins, mostly coppers, a few silvers worn thin, and one gold piece that made my stomach clench, because a washerwoman carrying a gold piece at midnight in the night market is a woman who has sold something she'll miss.

"That's too much," I said, and I want it noted for posterity that I said it.

"It's what it's worth to me." Her chin came up. The lamplight caught the lines around her eyes, eleven years of steam and scrubbing and wanting. "A child charm. The real kind. Not a knot to think pretty thoughts over."

Here is the trouble with my trade: the fertility charm was the one thing I sold that I could not honestly vouch for. Coughs ease. Sleep comes. Grief dulls on its own schedule, and a charm gives grief a schedule to blame. But children either come or they don't, and every wise-woman and hedge-priest and charm-man in the city had been taking coin for that particular hope since before the Godfall, and the ratio of babies to coins sold had never once favored the customer.

"Marta," I said, and lowered my voice, which was stupid, because the alley was empty except for the cat, who had heard worse. "I can sell you a charm. Red thread, nine knots, your name and Tam's, laid under the bed-frame. And I'll tell you truthfully what I tell no one else: it works sometimes. Maybe one time in ten. Maybe less. Anyone who promises you more is robbing you."

"And the other nine times?"

"The other nine times you've bought thread and a bad night's sleep wondering if you wasted your gold."

Something moved across her face — not anger. Something wearier than anger. "You're the only one in this whole stinking market who told me the odds," she said. "Do you know how strange that is? Do you know what the shrine-men charge? What the new temple charges, for their licensed prayers, their official intercessions?" She laughed, short and bitter. "Forty silver for a prayer to a god who hasn't answered anyone in two hundred years. At least you're cheap and honest about being useless."

"Useless at a discount," I agreed. "It's my niche."

She pushed the gold piece toward me anyway. I took the silver and left the gold, and she didn't argue, which told me the gold mattered to her more than pride, and I felt the familiar sour taste — not poison, conscience; different flavor entirely — that always came with this part of the trade.

I tied the thread. Nine knots, her name and Tam's, the little murmured words my old master had taught me, words he'd claimed came from before the Godfall, though for all I knew he'd invented them over breakfast. It was theater. It was comfort. It was a woman's hope given a shape she could hold at three in the morning when the wanting got loud.

"Lay it under the bed-frame," I said. "North side. And Marta — whatever happens or doesn't happen, that's not failure. That's just the world."

She took the charm like it was a live coal. "Thank you," she said, fierce, and pressed the gold piece into my hand anyway before I could stop her, and walked off into the dark with her shoulders square, and I stood there feeling like both the best and worst man in Vell's Crossing, which was, on balance, a typical Tuesday.

* * *

Three nights later, she found me again.

I want to describe precisely how wrong that was, because at the time I didn't yet understand it myself. Customers did not come back after three nights. Customers came back after a month, sheepish, or after a year, glowing, or never, having decided the thread was nonsense and the silver a lesson. Three nights was nothing. Three nights was not even enough time for hope to properly take root.

But there she was, at my corner, and she wasn't walking — she was floating, the way people float when some news inside them is too large for a body to carry normally.

"You're well," I said carefully. "That's good. If the charm's done nothing, remember what I—"

"I'm with child."

The cat, my only witness, yawned.

"That's—" I said. "That's wonderful. Congratulations. Though I'd gently note that three nights is rather fast even for optimistic accounting, and the charm likely had no—"

"August." She had never used my name. I had never given it to her. The sound of it went through me like a fishhook. "I'm with child, and I'm going to tell you something, and you're going to listen, because I've had three days to turn it over and I still don't believe it myself." She gripped the edge of the barrel. Her knuckles, those cracked white knuckles, were trembling. "The night I laid the thread under the bed — that very night — I prayed. Not to the licensed gods. To the old one. The Hollow god, the one my gran used to whisper about, the one under the city. I haven't thought about her in twenty years, but I was lying there with your silly thread in my hand and I just — I asked. Out loud. Like she was listening."

The market noise seemed to recede. Or perhaps my hearing did. I remember noticing, distantly, that my hands had gone cold.

"And I felt it," Marta said. "Warm. Like a hand on my belly. Like someone putting a blanket over a sleeping child. And now I'm with child, and the midwife says I'm six weeks along, which means—" her voice cracked, and steadied, washerwoman-strong, "which means it started before I ever bought your thread. Before I ever met you. The prayer came first, August. Your charm was decoration."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Somewhere in my skull a very careful, very tidy clerk who had kept my worldview in order for fifteen years dropped his ledger and stared at the wall.

"That's not possible," I said.

"No," she agreed, smiling through tears. "It isn't. And yet here I am." She pressed something into my hand — a small loaf, still warm, wrapped in cloth. "For the charm-man. Even useless charms deserve bread." And she floated away into the dark, one hand resting on her belly like it was already holding someone.

I stood in the alley for a long time. The cat left. The lamps burned lower. Eventually I became aware that I was holding the bread against my chest like a talisman, which was ironic, and that my heart was doing something irregular, which was concerning, and that somewhere beneath the cobbles of Vell's Crossing, under two hundred years of silence, something had heard a washerwoman ask for help —

— and answered.

* * *

I walked home the long way, which was foolish, because the long way passed the Hollow district, and sensible people avoided the Hollow district the way they avoided open graves. But my feet were making decisions without consulting me, and I had learned long ago that when your body knows something your mind doesn't, it's best to follow and find out why later.

Let me explain what the Hollow is, briefly, because visitors always ask and locals always lie. When the Godfall came — two centuries back, when the old gods went silent and the usurper's line rose on the strength of their absence — the sunken quarter at the city's heart simply stopped being maintained. Streets collapsed. Wells soured. The crown declared it condemned, walled the low entrances, and let the city's memory do the rest of the burying. Now it's a dent in the map, a place where fog collects even on dry nights, where the wealthy build their garden walls high so they don't have to look down into it. Children dare each other to touch the old shrine-stones. Nothing lives down there, everyone agrees. Everyone agrees very quickly and very loudly, the way people agree about things they'd rather not examine.

I stood at the rim, on the walkway above Tanner's Stair, and looked down into the dark.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. It was a hole full of ruined houses and two hundred years of the city's swept-out shame.

Except.

There was moss on the stones below. Green moss, living moss, thick as velvet, in a district where nothing had grown since my grandfather's grandfather. And the air coming up out of the Hollow was warm. Not sewer-warm, not rot-warm. Hearth-warm. The warmth of a room where someone has just gotten up from a chair and the chair is still holding the shape of them.

And underneath everything — under the wind, under the distant clatter of the night market packing up, under my own traitor heartbeat — I felt it. Faint as a pulse taken through a winter coat. Slow. Enormous. Patient in the way that only very old things are patient.

A heartbeat under the city.

Now, I should tell you what a rational man would have done. A rational man would have gone home, locked his door, drunk himself sensibly unconscious, and woken to conclude he'd eaten bad cheese. A rational man would certainly not have whispered, into the warm dark rising from the ruins, the way you might test whether a sleeping dog is truly asleep:

"...Hello?"

The heartbeat did not change. The warmth did not stir. The moss stayed moss.

But every hair on my arms stood up, and deep in my chest — in the exact center, in a place I had never once thought about in thirty-one years of owning a chest — something answered. Not words. Not movement. Just a turning-toward, the way a plant turns toward light, involuntary and ancient and utterly outside my permission.

I took my hand off the railing very slowly, the way you do when you realize you've been leaning over a well.

* * *

My rooms were above a chandler's shop on Candle Lane, chosen for the irony and kept for the rent. I climbed the stairs, bolted the door, sat down at my table, and did what any professional does when his certainties collapse: inventory.

Item: a washerwoman's prayer, answered instantly, impossibly, by a god officially dead for two centuries.

Item: the answer preceded my charm, meaning my charm was irrelevant, meaning my entire trade — the small honest comforts, the thread and knots and grave-dust — had just been revealed as decorations on a machine that apparently worked fine without me.

Item: the Hollow was warm, and green, and beating.

Item: something in me had turned toward it like a compass needle, and I had not asked it to.

I sat with that last item for a long while. Because here is the thing I hadn't said to Marta, the thing I barely said to myself: the warmth she described, the hand on her belly, the blanket over the sleeping child — I knew that warmth. I had known it all my life, in the one place I'd never thought to look. Every time I'd swallowed poison and felt it simply... set down its weapons. Every time the nightshade should have stopped my heart and instead felt, in my memory of those moments, oddly like being tucked in. I had called it immunity. I had called it a gift, a quirk, a trick of blood. I had built fifteen quiet years on top of it like a house on a foundation I never inspected.

And tonight, sitting alone with a loaf of bread and a dead god's pulse in my ears, I finally asked the question I should have asked at eight years old, when a farm boy who couldn't be poisoned was bought by a palace and taught to be invisible:

What exactly was I immune *for*?

Outside, far below, the city slept. Under the city, something else did not. And in the middle of the map of my safe, small, carefully invisible life, a crack had opened — thin as a hairline in porcelain, quiet as a knot of red thread — and through it, warm as a hearth, patient as moss,

something was looking back.
