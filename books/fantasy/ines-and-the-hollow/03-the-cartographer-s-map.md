## 3. The Cartographer's Map

There are three kinds of lies in the palace of Vael Amaranth, and I have drawn all of them.

The first kind is the small lie: the surveyor who rounds a riverbend because his boots were wet and he wanted his supper. The second is the convenient lie: the boundary that bends to favor whichever duke paid for the survey. The third is the official lie, and those are my favorites, because they are the only ones commissioned in advance. A mountain that does not exist on the Crown's maps has never been climbed by anyone the Crown needs to pretend not to see. A road erased from the archive was never marched down by soldiers who were never sent.

I am Ines Corvane, Second Cartographer of the Royal Survey, which means I am the person who decides which lies get inked and which get filed. It is delicate work. It requires a steady hand, an excellent memory, and the moral flexibility of a well-made compass needle—which is to say, none at all, but a great deal of precision about which way one points.

On the morning this story properly begins, I was correcting a lie about a bridge.

* * *

The Archive of Works sits in the east wing of the palace, below the waterline of the old cisterns, which is why every map drawer smells faintly of pond and why the lamps burn with a greenish patience. It is cold enough in winter that the ink slows, and in summer warm enough that the vellum sighs. I have spent eleven years there, and I can navigate it blind, which I once did for three days during a lamp-oil shortage and found the experience restful.

The bridge in question spanned the Sere at a village called Tallow's Rest, and according to the master survey of forty years past, it had collapsed. According to the tithe rolls, the village had paid bridge-tolls every year since. Someone was lying, and since the tolls flowed into a cousin of the Treasurer, the lie was going to stay on the map. My job was to make the collapse look plausible—add a flood line, note the pilings as rotted, date the ruin generously. I am very good at making ruins look older than they are. It is, if you think about it, the same skill as making new things look like nothing at all.

I had just finished shading the flood line when Tam brought me the season's field surveys.

Tam is my junior, a nervous young man with ink-stained cuffs and the conviction that everything interesting is also dangerous, which in our line of work is less paranoia than professional training. He set the folios on my table the way one sets down a sleeping animal.

"Field returns from the southern circuit," he said. "And the Hollow district resurvey."

"The Hollow." I looked up. "Who ordered a resurvey of the Hollow?"

"No one ordered it. The surveyor did it on his own initiative. There's a note." Tam produced the note as though it might bite him. "He says the ground has changed."

The Hollow district is the sunken quarter beneath the eastern terraces of the capital—the part of the city the Godfall broke and the Crown buried rather than rebuilt. Every child knows the story: when the old gods fell—or fled, or died; the histories are admirably vague on which—their temples cracked, their streets sank, and the wise king of that age (the usurper's great-grandsire, though we do not call him that) ordered the whole quarter filled and sealed. Now it is cellar country, ash-country, a district of tanners and rag-pickers living on top of a graveyard nobody prays over. On the master map it is marked with the surveyor's most dismissive symbol: a hatch of diagonal lines meaning *built-over ruin, no value.*

Surveyors do not resurvey things with no value. Surveyors resurvey things that pay them or frighten them.

"Read me the note," I said.

Tam read: *"Ground subsiding further along the old processional way. Fresh paths worn through fill where none marked before. Recommend full re-survey at Crown expense."* He lowered the paper. "Crown expense. He wants money."

"He wants to be right about something," I said, and held out my hand for the folio.

Here is what I love about field surveys, and I recognize that loving them marks me as a particular sort of person: they cannot help telling the truth even when they lie. A surveyor can fudge a boundary, but he cannot fudge a footpath without leaving evidence—a dotted line where the dots are too regular, a width measurement copied from a neighboring path. Feet are honest. Feet go where feet go, and they wear the record of it into the ground, and a good cartographer reads ground the way priests supposedly read souls.

I unrolled the Hollow resurvey across my table and weighted its corners with the brass horses I inherited from my predecessor, who inherited them from someone who probably stole them from a dead man, because that is how all the best instruments arrive.

At first glance it was a competent, dull piece of work. The sunken streets rendered in the standard ruin-hatch. The fill zones shaded. The tanneries noted with their stink-radius carefully omitted, because noting a stink-radius is how a surveyor gets thrown down a tanner's well.

Then I looked at the footpaths.

They were drawn as dashed lines, the convention for informal ways—desire paths, we call them, the tracks people make by wanting to go somewhere often enough. And they were everywhere. Not the usual scatter of shortcuts between tannery gates and water pumps. These converged. A dozen of them, maybe more, coming in from the north terraces, from the rag-picker warrens, from the river stairs—all bending, all curving, all funneling down toward the center of the buried quarter like threads being pulled through the eye of a needle.

And at the eye of the needle, the surveyor had drawn a small circle and labeled it, in his tidy hand: *old shrine, ruined.*

I sat back. I did the thing I always do when a map surprises me, which is to stop trusting my eyes and start trusting my memory. I reached for the master survey of the capital, the big one, the one commissioned after the Godfall and corrected so many times it had the textual integrity of a much-mended coat.

Every shrine in the old city was marked on it. Of course it was—the post-Godfall surveyors were thorough men working under orders to find every last place the old gods had been worshipped, so the Crown could decide what to do about each one. Most were demolished. Some were converted. And some—seventeen, if memory served, and memory usually does—were simply marked *ruined* and left under the fill, too broken to matter.

Seventeen shrines, scattered across the sunken districts and the far parishes. Seventeen little circles with the word *ruined* beside them, filed and forgotten for sixty years.

I pulled the master survey. I pulled the parish surveys. I pulled, because by then my heart had started doing something interesting against my ribs, the regional surveys for the territories outside the walls—the mill towns, the ferry villages, the places where the old religion had never quite been burned out of the kitchens.

And I laid them side by side, and I looked at the footpaths.

Around every single shrine marked *ruined*, the recent surveys showed fresh paths. Worn earth. Desire lines, converging. At the shrine under the mill town of Harrow Ford: four paths, all newer than the surveyor's own base lines—he'd dated his own traverse stations, bless his careful soul, and the paths cut across them. At the ferry crossing at Lowmarsh: paths coming up from the water, as if people were arriving by boat at night to visit a ruin. At the two shrines inside the capital itself besides the Hollow one: paths, paths, paths, all of them bending inward, all of them converging, sixty years of official silence wearing itself into the dirt.

Someone was visiting the ruins. Many someones. Regularly. Quietly enough that no surveyor had thought to ask why—but not quietly enough to fool the ground.

People do not walk at night, in the rain, by boat, to look at rubble. People walk to pray. And people only pray where they believe something is listening.

* * *

Now, I want to be clear about what I did next, because I am told by certain parties that my character is defined by my choices, and this was a choice.

I could have filed the observation. That was the safe thing, the professional thing. Note the anomaly, route it upward, let the Archivist-Major decide whether it warranted the attention of anyone above the waterline. If it turned out to be smugglers, fine. If it turned out to be heretics—if it turned out to be *worship*—then the witch-finders would come into the archive with their requisition chits and their gloves, and they would take the maps, and they would take the surveyors, and they would take whoever had noticed the pattern and ask him, at length and with instruments, what else he had noticed.

I have seen what the witch-finders do with a pattern. They do not see converging footpaths. They see a web, and then they need a spider, and then they need flies, and the arithmetic of it always ends the same way: with a list of names, and fire doing the sums.

So yes. The safe thing was to file it.

Instead, I locked the archive door, which is permitted to Second Cartographers during the dinner hour, and I made copies.

Not facsimiles—I want to be precise about this, because the distinction may matter later, to someone, in a room with worse lighting. A facsimile is evidence. What I made was *work*. I took a fresh sheet of good linen-backed paper and began, in my own hand, with my own conventions, to draw a new map: a map of the seventeen ruined shrines, positioned not by parish or by territory but relative to one another, the way a astronomer plots stars rather than a clerk plots taxes. Around each shrine I plotted its footpaths, scaled and dated from the surveys. Where the surveys disagreed, I noted the disagreement. Where the paths were undated, I estimated from wear-width, using the tables I worked out myself years ago and have never shown anyone, because a woman who can date a footprint from a map is a woman who should keep certain talents in a drawer.

It took me until well past the dinner bell. My lamp drank oil. Somewhere above me the palace went through its evening noises—boots, bells, the distant complaint of a man who is always complaining about something—and I sat below the waterline drawing a picture of a secret, and felt the particular joy that I suspect forgers feel, which is the joy of making something true out of other people's carelessness.

When I finished, I did not step back and admire it. I stepped back and *looked at it*, which is different, and it was looking at it that stopped my breath.

Because the paths did not just converge on the shrines.

Between the shrines—shrine to shrine, ruin to ruin, across parishes, across the river, from the mill towns toward the capital—there were fainter lines still. Old drovers' tracks, mostly, roads that predated the Godfall and had been officially rerouted decades ago. Roads that no one used anymore, according to every toll record in the archive.

But they connected the shrines. All of them. In a pattern.

I am not a mystical woman. I want that on the record too. I believe in distances, bearings, and the honest treachery of projection. But I stood in the cold green lamplight of the Archive of Works and looked at a shape emerging from sixty years of deliberate forgetting, and the shape was not accidental. Nobody's feet make a figure like that by wanting supper. That was design. Old design, worn deep, walked by generations of people who knew exactly where they were going and why—and then the Godfall came, and the Crown filled in the hollows, and the walking went underground, literally, and kept on.

The old religion hadn't died. It had been *mapped over*. And I had just unmapped it.

* * *

I did three things before the night watch made its round.

First, I put the original surveys back exactly where they lived, aligned to the finger-smudge marks on the drawer runners that tell me Tam has been reading things he shouldn't, which is another entry on the long ledger of things I will deal with later.

Second, I folded my copy small and sewed it into the spine of my personal day-book, between the pages where I keep my tide tables and my grandmother's recipe for cough syrup, because no witch-finder on earth will search a cough syrup recipe. I have hidden things in that book for eleven years. It is the truest map I own, and it looks like a bore.

Third, I sat down at my table, pulled a blank sheet toward me, and began drafting the report I would file—the official one, the lie of the third kind, commissioned in advance by myself.

*Field surveys reviewed. Footpath anomalies attributable to seasonal drainage patterns and livestock movement. No pattern of significance observed. Recommend no action.*

I wrote it in my best clerical hand, the flat one, the one with no personality in it at all. I dated it correctly. I signed it. And as I sanded it, I caught myself thinking—not for the first time, but for the first time with this particular flavor—*who taught me to do this?*

Because here is the thing I have never said aloud, not to Tam, not to the Archivist-Major, not to the dark: I was not trained to make these maps. I was trained to make these *decisions*. When I entered the Survey at nineteen, a frightened provincial girl with a gift for angles, someone spent eleven years teaching me which truths to surface and which to sink. Master Ondrel, my predecessor, called it "cartographic judgment." He drilled it into me with the patience of a man planting an orchard he would never sit under. When the requisitions came from the witch-finders' office—and they came, twice a year, every year, hunting shrine networks, hunting congregation routes, hunting exactly the pattern I now had sewn into my day-book—it was Ondrel who showed me how to prepare the fakes. How to age paper. How to draw a false footpath with convincing irregularity. How to lie with such precision that a lie becomes indistinguishable from diligence.

I always assumed it was caution. Bureaucratic scar tissue; a man who had survived the purges and learned to keep the archive alive by feeding it safe answers.

But tonight, sitting alone below the waterline with a secret the size of a god in my bag, I found myself wondering whether Ondrel had been cautious at all. Whether he had known. Whether the old man who taught me to protect the maps had been protecting something in them—someone, perhaps—some network of walkers on old roads, praying in ruins, kept alive for sixty years by one careful liar in a basement handing the witch-finders beautiful, meticulous, worthless paper.

Ondrel died four years ago. Fell down the cistern stairs, they said. He knew those stairs better than his own hands; he had walked them ten thousand times in the dark.

I sand my report. I file it. I climb the stairs slowly, counting them, the way he taught me, and at the top I pause and look back down into the green dark where all the true maps sleep in their drawers, patient as seeds.

Somewhere under this city, feet are finding their way to the old shrines along roads that officially do not exist. Something buried is being visited. And I have just become the only person in the palace of Vael Amaranth who holds the whole pattern in one pair of hands.

I should be afraid. I am, a little. But underneath the fear, embarrassingly, irrepressibly, is the feeling I have chased my whole life across every blank space on every chart:

*I wonder where those paths actually lead.*

Tomorrow, I decided, I would find out.

I just didn't yet know that I wouldn't be the only one asking—that somewhere upstairs, a food-taster with forgettable eyes was already carrying the answer around in his mouth.
