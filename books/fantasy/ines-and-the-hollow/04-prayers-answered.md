## 4. Prayers Answered

There is a particular silence that follows a miracle, and I had never heard it before that Tuesday.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I've ever kept. Miracles were not part of my business model. Charms are a different trade entirely — a charm is a small brass lie you solder shut and sell for three coppers, and the customer's own hope does the rest of the work. Hope is tireless. Hope will work for free until it dies of old age. All I ever did was give hope something to hold onto while it labored, the way you give a mule a nosebag so it doesn't notice how far it's walking.

Fifteen years of that. Fertility charms, fever charms, a modest line in protection-from-the-neighbor's-dog. Never anything grand enough to attract attention, never anything that worked well enough to invite scrutiny. A charm that works too well gets you asked *how*. And how, in my experience, is the first question on every road that ends at a pyre.

So when Marta the washerwoman came back to the night market with her baby on her hip — the baby she'd bought the charm for eleven days earlier, the baby that should not have existed for another year at the very earliest — I did what any sensible man does when his worldview cracks down the middle.

I checked the stitching.

* * *

"Say that again," I said, "slowly."

Marta beamed at me with the radiant, slightly unhinged joy of a woman who has been answered by the universe and wants everyone else to feel included. She was a big woman, Marta, forearms like dock rope, and she held the infant out toward me as though presenting evidence at trial. Which, in a sense, she was.

"I prayed," she said. "Like you told me. Every night, over the charm, like you said. And on the ninth night the fever came on me — you know, the woman's fever, the one that took my sister — and I thought, well, there's an end to it. And then." She paused for effect. Marta understood effect. "Then the room went warm. Not fire-warm. Hearth-warm. Like someone had been sitting by my bed all night keeping it, and I woke up and the fever was gone and—" she bounced the baby, who gurgled with the smugness unique to infants who have upended their mother's anatomy ahead of schedule, "—and here he is. Early. Whole. Loud."

"That's not possible," I said.

"You sold me the charm!"

"The charm is brass and river clay and a prayer I wrote myself, and I am not ordained by anything except the tax office." I turned the little medallion over in my fingers. It looked exactly as it had when I'd made it: cheap, honest in its dishonesty, the sigil stamped shallow because deep stamps cost more tools than I owned. Except. The sigil was no longer shallow. The lines had darkened, sunk into the metal like old scars, and when I tilted it toward the lantern light they seemed to move — not move, exactly, but to have the quality of having just finished moving, the way water looks after a fish passes under it.

I dropped it into her palm fast, the way you drop something that's bitten you.

"Well?" Marta said. "Aren't you going to say something? Everyone says the charm-seller of Tanner's Row must be blessed himself, to make them work like this."

Everyone. My stomach performed a slow, professional roll. *Everyone* was talking. *Everyone* was a word that got men like me visited by men like Serel Doss, whose name I knew the way you know the name of a disease — distantly, formally, praying you'd never meet it in person.

"What everyone says," I said, "is that Marta works hard and God — whichever one is taking calls these days — noticed. Take your boy home. Don't tell anyone where you bought the charm. Tell them you found it in the river."

"In the river?"

"Rivers are romantic. Nobody interrogates a river."

She laughed, kissed the baby's head, and went off into the market crowd trailing warmth behind her like a lit wick. I stood there among my trays of trinkets feeling the way a man feels when the floor of his house turns out to have been a lid all along.

Because here is the thing about the fever Marta described. The woman's fever, the hearth-warmth, the *someone sitting by the bed keeping it* — I knew those details. Not from her. From the old stories, the ones the Godfall burned out of the official record two hundred years ago and left smoldering in the places records don't reach: nursemaids' rhymes, kitchen superstitions, the mutterings of very old women in very small villages. The Hollow god had been a hearth-keeper before it was anything else. Before the usurper's line threw it down and buried its district under rubble and called the rubble progress, it had been the god of banked fires and safe births and bread rising overnight.

And its prayers, according to everything I'd ever read in the tasters' guild's forbidden back shelves, had a signature. Warmth without flame. Answered too completely, too fast, too *kindly*.

Eleven days. Marta's prayer had taken eleven days. In the old stories, the Hollow god's answer time was measured in the same breath as the asking.

The gods were dead. Everyone knew the gods were dead. That was the whole architecture of the modern world: the usurper's dynasty sat on the throne because the gods had failed, and the witch-finders swept the ashes to make sure nothing was sprouting, and sensible people like me sold brass lies to hopeful people and called it commerce.

Except somebody, apparently, was answering.

* * *

By Thursday I had stopped sleeping, which was inconvenient, because Thursday was when the others came.

They arrived separately, which was worse. Clients who arrive together compare notes; clients who arrive separately have each experienced their miracle alone, in the privacy of their own lives, and have come to me the way witnesses come to a magistrate — individually certain, collectively damning.

First was Oskar, the lamplighter with the burned hands, whom I'd sold a pain-charm to in autumn. He laid his hands flat on my counter and showed me the skin: smooth. Not scarred-smooth. Smooth the way new things are smooth, unlined, pink as a child's palm. He'd carried those burns since he was nine years old. He wept a little, and thanked me, and I gave him his money back because taking coin for this suddenly felt like picking pockets at a funeral, and he refused it, and we had an awkward standoff that ended with me shoving four coppers into his coat pocket and telling him to buy his wife something nice.

Second was a girl I didn't recognize, which frightened me more than the other two combined, because I keep meticulous mental records of my clientele and strangers do not appear in them. She was maybe sixteen, dressed in good wool, and she set a small cloth bundle on my counter and unwrapped it with the reverence of an acolyte.

"My grandmother's," she said. "Her memory went. Eight years of not knowing my name. Last week she woke up and asked for tea and told me the story of how she met my grandfather, word for word, the way she used to. She said she'd dreamed of a warm door." The girl looked at me with terrible, grateful eyes. "She still has your charm on her cord. I wanted you to know it worked. I wanted you to know someone knows."

*I wanted you to know someone knows.* There was a sentence to lose sleep over. I thanked her, smiled the smile I keep for occasions exactly like this — warm, brief, forgettable — and waited until she'd gone before I put my head down on the counter between two trays of luck-knots and breathed through the panic the way the guild had taught me. Four counts in. Seven counts out. Panic is a luxury; panic takes up space that vigilance needs.

Third came the offering.

He was an old man, bent like a question mark, and he didn't want anything. That was the strangest part. He shuffled up to my stall near midnight, when the market was thinning and the lanterns were burning low, and he set a bundle on the counter — a burnt offering, actual ash and char wrapped in oilcloth, the kind of thing that hadn't been legal to carry in the capital since the Godfall edicts — and he said, in a voice like dry leaves:

"She said to bring it to the man with the hollow hands."

"I don't have hollow hands."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

And then he walked away before I could ask a single one of the approximately forty questions climbing up my throat, and I was left standing in the gutter with a bundle of illegal ash and the distinct sensation of being a chess piece that had just realized the board was bigger than sixty-four squares.

I should have thrown it in the canal. Let me say that plainly, for the record I am not keeping: the correct move, the survival move, the move fifteen years of careful invisibility demanded, was to drop the thing in the black water and go home and drink until the shaking stopped.

I unwrapped it instead. Curiosity is the flaw they never bred out of me, whoever *they* were, whatever breeding amounted to.

It was a cinder — a knot of charred wood the size of a fist, burned down past recognition, and it should have been cold. Ashes from any ordinary fire are dead things; heat leaves them and doesn't come back. This one hummed. Not sound, exactly. Vibration. A low, patient tremor against my palms, like a cat's purr felt through a wall, like a hearth that has gone down to coals but hasn't gone out.

And my skin drank it.

I don't know how else to describe what happened next, and believe me, I've spent the intervening nights trying. The warmth moved *into* me. Through the calluses, through the skin, into whatever is underneath skin that skin doesn't get a vote about. It didn't burn. It *filled* — the way a dry house fills with the smell of bread, everywhere at once, no path you can trace. For one long moment I stood in the night market holding a dead god's ember against my chest, and every ache I carried — the knee that ached in rain, the shoulder the guild training had ruined, the older, nameless ache I'd assumed was just the price of being alive — all of it went quiet. Warm. Kept.

Like someone had been sitting by my bed all night, keeping the fire.

I dropped the cinder. It landed in the gutter and lay there glowing faintly, patiently, the way coals glow when they're waiting for morning. Around me the market went on — fishwives arguing, a fiddler tuning, nobody noticing that the air above my stall had gone soft and golden and smelled, impossibly, of woodsmoke and fresh bread in a city where ovens were taxed.

And something in me answered back.

That's the part I keep circling, the part I can't file anywhere. Because it wasn't a voice. It wasn't the god speaking — I'd know, I think, if something that large addressed me directly; I suspect it would be less like hearing and more like being read. It was smaller than that. It was *mine*. Some door in me that I had never known was a door swung open onto a warm room, and from inside the room, something stirred — not waking, not yet, but turning over in its sleep the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar footstep on the stair.

Recognition. That's the word I finally found, around dawn, staring at my ceiling. Whatever lived in that warm room recognized the cinder. Or the cinder recognized it. Fifteen years I'd lived in this body, and apparently I'd had a lodger the whole time, and the lodger had family.

* * *

I did what I always do with unbearable information: I organized it.

Back at my rooms above the chandler's shop, I sat at my table with a candle and a sheet of paper — not writing anything down, never writing anything down, but arranging facts in the air the way a card sharp arranges a deck — and I dealt them out.

Fact one: three miracles in nine days, all traced to charms I made, none requiring charms I made. The charms were luggage. Something else was doing the traveling.

Fact two: the signature matched the Hollow god. Hearth-warmth, complete answers, kindness with weight behind it. The buried god. The defeated god. The god whose corpse the capital was built on, in the most literal sense — every foundation stone south of the river quarried from the sunken district they'd entombed after the Godfall.

Fact three: the old man. *She said to bring it to the man with the hollow hands.* Someone knew what I was before I knew. Someone was sending me mail through the wreckage of a dead religion, and the address was accurate.

Fact four — and this was the one I kept turning face-down and turning back up again, the card that wouldn't stay in the discard pile — fact four was the answering. The warm room. The lodger. Because the stories, the forbidden ones, the ones Master Corvane had let me read at fourteen with the shutters closed and his hand heavy on my shoulder — the stories said the old gods hadn't simply died at the Godfall. They'd been *poured somewhere*. Contained. And containment requires vessels, and vessels require bloodlines, and bloodlines —

No. That road led to a place I wasn't ready to go, a place shaped suspiciously like my own reflection. I was a food-taster with a side business in brass. I was nobody's bloodline. I had built a life out of being forgettable, and it had worked, it had *worked*, for fifteen years, and the entire strategy now lay in ruins because some washerwoman couldn't have the decency to stay barren.

I pinched out the candle and sat in the dark and listened to the chandler's stock cooling downstairs, tick and settle, tick and settle, the building breathing the way buildings do.

Underneath it — faint, patient, unmistakable once you'd heard it — something hummed.

Not the cinder. I'd left the cinder in the gutter, or told myself I had. This hum was in the floor. In the stone. In the bones of the city itself, down where the buried district slept under two hundred years of pavement and denial. A heartbeat with a hearth's patience. *Banked,* it said, in the way that heat says things. *Not out.*

I pressed my palm flat against the floorboards, and the warmth rose to meet it like a dog that knows its name.

"Oh, no," I said aloud, to the floor, to the god, to whatever clerk of the universe kept files on fools. "No. Find someone else. I'm immune to poison and allergic to destiny, and I am *very* difficult to kill, which means I plan to live a long, dull, entirely mortal life, and you are not in it."

The warmth pulsed once — amused, I could swear it was amused — and settled again beneath the city, patient as coals.

I sat there until dawn with my hand on the floor, hating myself for not moving it.

And that was how, three streets away and one week early, Ines Corvane came to spread her survey maps across a table in the palace archive and find the same heartbeat I'd found — converging lines, fresh footpaths, every ruined shrine in the capital quietly, secretly, *warmly* alive — while I sat above the chandler's shop learning the first lesson of the new age:

The gods were done being dead.

And worse — far worse, the thought that finally drove me from my chair to pace the dark rooms like a man whose shadow has started walking ahead of him — worse was the realization that the old man's words kept circling back to, the words I could not stop hearing in his leaf-dry voice:

*She said to bring it to the man with the hollow hands.*

Someone had sent him. Someone knew my name before the miracles did. And in this city, the only people who went looking for men like me wore the grey coats of the witch-finders — or served something older, and hungrier, and considerably less kind.
