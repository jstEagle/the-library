## 5. The Witch-Finder's Visit

The summons came the way summons always came to the Office of Surveys: sideways, through a clerk, on paper that smelled faintly of tallow and fear.

*By order of the Commission for Spiritual Integrity, all cartographic materials pertaining to religious structures, active or ruined, within the capital and its environs are to be made available for inspection.*

I read it twice, standing in the map room with my sleeves chalked to the elbow from an afternoon of tracing parish boundaries nobody had asked about in forty years. The second reading was slower than the first, because the second time I was not reading it as a cartographer. I was reading it as a woman doing arithmetic.

Religious structures. Active or ruined. Within the capital *and its environs*.

The environs were where I lived.

"Well," I said aloud, to no one, because the map room at that hour contained only me and the ghost of a surveyor named Pell who had died before I was born and whose ink still smelled of cloves if you warmed it with your breath. "Well," I said again, because repetition is free and panic is expensive.

The thing about being a palace cartographer is that everyone assumes your work is decorative. Maps are what you hang behind thrones. Maps are what you print on the backs of almanacs so farmers know when to plant turnips. Nobody looks at a map and thinks *dangerous*. A map is a list of places wearing a pretty dress.

But a map is also a confession. Every line I draw is a decision about what matters. Every symbol is a judgment call. And for eleven years I had been making judgments — small ones, private ones, the kind that never make it into the legend — about which ruins were really ruins, and which footpaths were really footpaths, and which shrines marked with a tidy little cross-and-bar notation were actually still receiving visitors.

I had told myself it was professional thoroughness. Cartographers are supposed to notice things; noticing things is the entire job. If a road shows wear, you record the wear. That's not sedition, that's accuracy.

Except that lately the wear had started forming patterns, and patterns have opinions.

* * *

Serel Doss arrived at mid-morning on a day when the light through the map-room windows was thin and gray, the kind of light that makes vellum look like skin and everything else look like evidence.

He did not look the way witch-finders are drawn in the pamphlets. There was no black hat, no iron collar, no smiling cruelty. He looked like a tax assessor: middle-aged, neat, a man whose boots were polished but whose coat was cut for someone with more money than he had ever been permitted to keep. His eyes were the problem. They were pale — a gray so light it read almost as white in certain lights — and they did not blink so much as they *retracted*, the way a cat's pupils do, gathering information instead of releasing it.

"Cartographer Corvane." He said my name correctly, which unsettled me more than getting it wrong would have. People who get your name right have looked you up. "You'll forgive the informality of the visit. The Commission prefers unannounced."

"The Commission prefers a great many things, I imagine." I gestured him toward the long table where the working copies lived. "Tea?"

"No tea." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way one declines a gift one suspects of being poisoned. Which, given my profession's proximity to the kitchens, was either paranoia or flattery. "We'll begin with the index, if you please. All holdings relating to ecclesiastical sites."

"All of them."

"All of them."

There are two hundred and six entries in that index. I know because I wrote the index myself, three years ago, during a slow winter, and I organized it the way I organize everything: by district, then by date of last survey, then by a third category that appears in the ledger as *structural condition* but which I privately think of as *how dead is it, really*.

Structural condition has five grades. Collapsed. Subsiding. Standing but disused. Maintained. And a fifth grade I invented because the four official ones kept failing me: *anomalous*. Anomalous meant the stones said one thing and the ground said another. Anomalous meant moss where moss shouldn't grow, footpaths where footpaths had no reason to be, warmth in cellars that hadn't held a fire since the Godfall.

Eleven entries carried that fifth grade. Eleven. I remember the number the way you remember the number of stairs in your childhood home — not memorized, just *known*, worn into me by repetition.

Doss asked to see the index. Then he asked to see the originals. Then he asked, in a voice that stayed perfectly level while doing something unpleasant to the air around it, why several of the anomalous entries showed surveys dated within the last two years.

"Because I survey," I said. "It's the job."

"You survey *ruins*."

"I survey whatever the ground tells me is worth surveying. Ruins shift. Ruins settle. Ruins occasionally stand back up, though that's rare and usually involves fraud on the part of whoever's rebuilding them." I pulled the relevant folder and laid it flat between us, squaring its edges against the table with the unconscious precision of someone who has spent her life making corners meet. "Here. The Hollow district, eastern quarter. Last surveyed this past spring."

"And what did you find?"

"Moss."

He waited. Doss waits beautifully; it may be his single greatest talent. He waits the way a fisherman waits, with the line already cast, and he lets you feel the pull of the hook without ever seeming to touch it.

"Living moss," I said finally, "on stone that the records say has been dead for two hundred years. Growing in a pattern. Not spreading — *arranged*. Moss doesn't arrange itself. Moss is a democracy of spores. This was a monarchy."

"Curious." He wrote nothing down. I watched his hands, which stayed folded in his lap the entire time we spoke, and I understood that whatever memory he used in place of notes, it was better than notes. Notes can be stolen. Notes can be forged. Notes can be burned. A man who keeps everything behind his eyes cannot be raided.

Unless his eyes can be read. But that was a problem for braver women than me.

* * *

Here is what I had prepared for, and here is what I had not.

I had prepared for requisition. Years ago — and I will come back to the question of *years ago*, because that question is currently eating me alive — I had begun keeping two versions of certain documents. The working set, honest, detailed, annotated in my own cramped hand with observations that would have gotten me hauled before the Commission if anyone read them closely. And the archive set, clean, official, bland as boiled mutton. The archive set said *ruined* where the working set said *anomalous*. It said *no access recorded* where the working set said *footpath widening annually, traffic consistent with pilgrimage, footwear varied, including children's sizes*.

Children's sizes. I want that sentence to sit there for a moment, because it is the sentence that changed my life, and it did so quietly, the way most important sentences do. I had been mapping footpaths into the Hollow for years before I understood what the footpaths meant. And one spring afternoon, measuring tread-widths out of pure methodological stubbornness — you record what wears a path, even if all you can record is the width of their shoes — I found that the paths were being walked by families. Whole families. Regularly. To shrines that the crown had declared empty since before anyone's grandmother was born.

I filed nothing. I copied everything. That was the rule, though I couldn't have told you then who had given it to me or when.

So when Doss asked for the originals, I gave him the originals. The real ones. The boring ones. The ones that said *ruined* in confident red ink and included charming little marginal sketches of collapsed archways rendered with genuine artistic feeling, because I am, among other things, a decent draftsman, and a good forgery should be beautiful. Beauty is credibility. Nobody audits something lovely.

He took them. He thanked me. He complimented the marginal sketches, specifically the archway in the western Hollow, and said it was a fine piece of work, and I felt the compliment land somewhere between my shoulder blades like a hand.

Then he paused, at the door, with the folders under his arm, and turned back.

"One more matter, Cartographer. Purely administrative." His voice did the gentle thing again. "Your predecessor. Pell."

"Pell died thirty years before I was born."

"Yes. I'm aware." He smiled — a small, correct smile, deployed like a signature on a document. "I'm speaking of your *immediate* predecessor. Master Aldery. You served under him for four years, I believe."

Master Aldery. Four years of my apprenticeship, spent learning lettering conventions and how to hold a ruling pen so the ink doesn't blob, and how to check a measurement twice because once is hope and twice is knowledge. A dry old man with ink-stained cuffs and a cough he claimed was dust and everyone knew was worse. He taught me projection methods. He taught me how to render terrain so a soldier could read slope at a glance. He taught me —

He taught me, I realized, standing very still in my own map room, a great many things that had nothing to do with cartography.

He taught me to always keep a duplicate set. He taught me that the archive is the first place searched and therefore must never contain anything true. He taught me that compliments from officials are invoices. He taught me — I am nearly certain he taught me this, though he never once said it aloud — that when you find something on a map that frightens you, you copy it, you hide it, and you say nothing, until you understand why finding it frightened you.

"Master Aldery retired to his sister's farm in Vellmark," I said. "I corresponded with him briefly. Letters stopped some years ago."

"They stopped," Doss agreed, "some years ago." He let that hang. He was letting it hang deliberately; I could hear the shape of the silence, the way you can hear the shape of a keyhole by what fits near it. "His retirement was, shall we say, lightly documented. As was his service. As was—" and here he tilted his head a degree, a degree being enough, "—his *training* of you."

"My training consisted of pens and projections."

"Of course it did." He shifted the folders under his arm. "Thank you for your cooperation, Cartographer Corvane. The Commission will be in touch regarding follow-up inspections. Routine. Nothing for a professional to concern herself over."

Nothing for a professional to concern herself over. I have heard that phrase before, in other mouths, in other decades, in the histories I read as a girl about the purges after the Godfall, when the Commission's ancestor-body burned its way through the shrine districts and called the ash *cleanup*. Nothing to concern yourself over is what they say when the concerning has already been scheduled.

He left. The door closed with the particular softness of a door closed by someone who intends to come back through it.

* * *

I stood in the map room for a long time afterward, doing what I do best, which is looking at surfaces and reasoning downward.

Reasoning one: Doss asked for ecclesiastical holdings. Not military, not civil, not hydrographic. Someone above him has decided that shrines matter now. Shrines have not mattered to this government in two hundred years. Therefore something has happened — recently, or recently *noticed* — that makes old gods administratively relevant.

Reasoning two: he asked about Aldery. Witch-finders do not ask idle questions; every question is a probe with a purpose, and the purpose of asking an apprentice about her master is to establish whether the master trained the apprentice in anything *besides* the nominal trade. Which means either Aldery is already on a list somewhere, or Aldery's students are. Or both.

Reasoning three, and this is the one I kept circling like a bird over water: he complimented the forgery.

No. Wait. Let me be precise, because precision is the only weapon I own. He complimented the archway sketch. And the archway sketch exists only in the archive set. Only in the version I built to be handed over. Which means one of two things. Either Serel Doss genuinely admired a drawing of a ruin, which is possible — witch-finders are people, people like pretty things — or Serel Doss knows exactly what the archive set is, and was telling me, in the only language such men use, that he saw the seams.

That he knows the maps I gave him are the polite ones. That he is letting me keep my secrets for now, the way a hunter lets a wounded animal keep its den, because dens have back doors and back doors lead to other animals.

Or — and this possibility arrived later, sitting alone over cold dinner, and it arrived with the weight of something that had been waiting a long time to arrive — or he was telling me that he *approved*. That the fakes were adequate. That whoever set the standard I'd built them to had set it well.

And that was the thought that would not leave.

Who taught me?

I have been turning this over for hours now, and I want to describe the experience honestly, because honesty is the one luxury left in this building. It is like reaching into your own pocket and finding a key you don't remember cutting, for a door you don't remember having, in a house you thought you knew every room of.

Aldery. It must have been Aldery. Four years, side by side at the same table, his cough and my ink, his corrections in the margins of my practice sheets — *again, Corvane, the coastline lies to everyone, your job is to lie less* — and threaded invisibly through all of it, lessons I absorbed without labeling. Keep duplicates. Never file truth. Compliments are invoices. Copy first, speak never.

But where did *he* learn it? A master cartographer does not spontaneously develop counter-surveillance instincts. Those are taught, or bought with blood. Aldery's letters stopped some years ago — Doss knew that; Doss knew it the way you know a debt is outstanding. Retired to a sister's farm. Did Aldery have a sister? I wrote the condolence inquiry myself, years back, when the letters stopped, and got back a note in a hand I didn't recognize saying the family thanked me for my regard. I filed it under correspondence and thought no more about it, because thinking more about it was not yet a habit I had.

I am having the habit now.

I went down to the deep stacks tonight, after hours, with a lamp and my keys and the specific courage of a woman who has decided that fear is a material to be surveyed like any other. I pulled Aldery's personnel file. Thin. Thinner than mine, and I've done nothing. Dates of service, a commendation for the coastal survey of '61, a note about the cough. No family listed. No sister. No farm in Vellmark.

So either the file was cleaned, or the sister was fiction, or both, and both is what my money says. Both is what my whole training says, the training I didn't know I was receiving, the curriculum written by someone whose name I never learned, delivered by a dry old man with ink-stained cuffs who lied to me for four years in the kindest possible way — by teaching me to lie too, well enough that today a witch-finder walked out of my map room holding two hundred pages of beautiful, confident, deliberate falsehood, and thanked me for them.

I sat down at my table and I did the thing I do. I drew.

Not a map. A diagram. Of what I know, arranged spatially, because I think in space and always have. Center: the Hollow, footpaths converging, children's shoe-prints, living moss on dead stone, a heartbeat I heard last week through fifty feet of limestone and told absolutely no one about. Ring one: Doss, requisitions, questions about Aldery, pale retracted eyes, a compliment shaped like a warning. Ring two: the Commission, awake after two centuries, hunting shrine networks, which means hunting pilgrims, which means hunting families, which means — I drew this connection with a line that shook slightly, and I am not a woman whose lines shake — which means the people walking those paths are already condemned in some office I will never see, and the only question is whether my maps help find them.

Ring three: me. Duplicate sets. Hidden truths. A dead teacher with a fictional sister. A key in my pocket I don't remember cutting.

I looked at the diagram for a long time. Then I did what any competent cartographer does with a document she cannot afford to be found holding.

I burned it. Carefully, in the lamp, over a basin, watching the ink curl and go orange and then black, and I confess — because we are being honest tonight, in this room, with ourselves — I confess that burning it felt less like destroying evidence and more like closing a window before a storm. The knowledge stayed. Knowledge stays. That is the whole horror of my profession: you can burn the map, but the territory is already in you.

Before bed, I did one more thing. I took the true working set — the anomalous entries, the footpath measurements, the eleven shrines that are not dead, the tread-widths, the dates, the pattern I have been assembling in secret like a woman knitting a shroud — and I moved it.

Not hid. Moved. Hiding is for people who expect to be searched. Moving is for people who expect to be *raided*, and there is a difference, and the difference is that hiding assumes you'll be there to defend it.

I won't be. When they come back — and Doss said routine follow-up inspections with the smoothness of a man reading a menu he wrote himself — I intend to be elsewhere. Possibly legitimately. Possibly not.

And underneath the fear, which is real, and the defiance, which is growing, there is a third thing now, and I hardly know what to call it. Recognition, maybe. The sense that I have been prepared for something. That the caution in my hands was installed, deliberately, by someone who foresaw this exact season — the Commission waking, the shrines mattering, the witch-finders at the table — and armed me for it the only way you can arm a cartographer:

With the habit of lying beautifully, and the instinct to survive long enough to finish the map.

Tomorrow I request travel authorization for a field survey. Eastern districts. Ground-truthing, I'll call it in the paperwork. Verification of the archive set, I'll tell Doss's clerks, if they ask, and I'll say it with a straight face because I have an excellent face for it.

But I know where I'm actually going. I'm going to walk the footpaths myself, all the way down into the Hollow, past the last street the city admits to, down to where the moss grows in patterns and the stone is warm and something beneath the capital is keeping time.

Someone taught me to be careful. Fine. Careful got me this far.

Now I want to know what the caution was *for*.

And there is exactly one way to survey a secret, and it is the oldest method in my trade, older than any instrument in this room:

You go there yourself, and you measure what's true.
