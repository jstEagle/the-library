## 6. What the Nightshade Wanted

There are two ways to investigate a poison. The first is the way the court physicians do it: you write a report, you blame a servant, and the servant's family lights candles for a week before anyone remembers they existed.

The second way is mine. You go back to the plate.

I kept the nightshade. That will sound mad to anyone who has never tasted for a king, but a taster who destroys evidence is a taster with nothing but his word, and my word is worth exactly as much as my invisibility. So when I spat that breakfast into the silk and pronounced the eggs dead, I did not throw the rest to the fire like an honest man. I folded a portion into my sleeve, the way you'd pocket a coin someone dropped in a crowd, and I carried it home in my mouth like a secret.

My rooms above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Row are small enough that a stranger would call them poor and generous enough that I call them safe. Three walls of books I've written under other men's names—charm manuals, mostly, the kind of thing that gets a man hanged if he's caught writing it and rich if he isn't. A bed. A kettle. A locked box under the floorboard where I keep the things that matter: eleven years of ledgers, a lock of hair I won't explain to anyone, and now, a twist of cloth holding the most interesting poison I've ever failed to die of.

I set it on the table and lit two lamps, because some work you do by one light and some work you do by all the light you can beg, borrow, or buy.

Nightshade is honest work, as poisons go. It has a signature. It tastes the way a struck bell sounds—one clean note of bitterness, then numbness spreading down the throat like frost on a windowpane. I have swallowed it four times in my career, on purpose, to calibrate myself against it, the way a musician tunes to a fork. I know nightshade the way a shepherd knows his dog.

This was not nightshade.

Oh, it wore nightshade the way a spy wears a livery coat—the cut was right, the color was right, you'd swear your oath on it from across a room. But underneath the bell-note there was that second taste, the one that had made me spit twice instead of once. I'd called it *old* in the moment, which is what my tongue says when it doesn't have a better word. Old things taste old because they were made slowly, by people who had time, by methods nobody uses anymore because the fast way works well enough for killing.

So I did what any sensible charlatan does when confronted with something genuinely outside his expertise.

I got out my books.

* * *

Here is what fifteen years of selling charms teaches you that no guild ever will: everything old in this city is still *here*. The usurper burned the temples, yes, and melted the bells, and put the priests' heads on spikes along the Processional Road where the crows could be patriotic. But you cannot burn a recipe out of the world. Somewhere in some cellar, some grandmother is still measuring out grandmother-things, and the knowledge passes hand to hand like water finding cracks in stone.

Which means somewhere in this city is a book—or a woman, women are usually better at keeping this kind of thing—that knows what tastes like nightshade but isn't.

I spent three nights looking. I want to be clear about how much I didn't sleep, because later, when people ask me why I did what I did, I want it on record that exhaustion was a contributing factor.

The answer came from the least likely shelf: a book I'd bought years ago for its binding, a fat dull treatise on kitchen herb-lore written by some palace cook three reigns ago, back when cooks could still admit to knowing things. Between a recipe for eel jelly and instructions for candying angelica, there is a page the author clearly hoped no one would read. It describes a preparation the old kitchens used "in the god-days," for flavoring festival bread. Belladonna base, cut with something he calls *hollow-moss ash* and something else he calls, in a handwriting that suddenly goes careful and small, *the sleeper's salt.*

He gives no quantities. He gives no source. But at the bottom of the page, in the same careful small hand, there is one line more:

*Not food. Not death. A door, if the eater is made right.*

*A door.*

I sat back and looked at my hands for a long time.

Because here is the thing about being poisoned. You assume the poisoner wanted you dead. It's the natural assumption. Poison is expensive, risky, treasonous—it must be aimed at a life. Every manual, every magistrate, every grieving widow operates on this principle: poison equals murder.

But I wasn't dead. And the usurper wasn't dead—I'd tasted for him that morning and cleared every dish but the one meant for me, and he had eaten his breakfast with the hearty appetite of a man with no enemies, which is either true innocence or the best acting I've seen since I started this trade.

The eggs had been set at *my* place. My place at the tasting table is nothing—a stool at the end, below the salt, where the furniture goes to be ignored. Nobody poisons the stool at the end unless the stool at the end is the target.

And the poison wasn't a knife. It was a key.

"A door, if the eater is made right."

I am made right. I have been made right for so long I forgot I was made at all. Immune since birth, the guild ledger says—inherited, whatever that means—and immune men don't just happen in a world where immunity is the whole of your employment. Somebody bred me for this table the way you breed a dog for scenting, and somebody trained me, and somebody taught me to be invisible, and I have spent fifteen years being grateful for all of it without once asking what the gratitude was buying them.

Now someone had seasoned my breakfast with a door, and I was starting to wonder what was on the other side, and whether I'd already put my shoulder against it in my sleep.

* * *

Finding the cook took two days, and I'm almost ashamed of how easy it was, because it shouldn't have been. That's the part that frightens me now, looking back. It should have been hard. A conspiracy that seasons a royal breakfast with temple-poison ought to bury itself deep, ought to leave no thread loose anywhere. Instead I pulled one thread and the whole garment came apart like wet paper.

Undermaster Grell. Third kitchen, the pastry station, twenty-two years of service, a round soft man with flour in his eyebrows and a reputation for never having an opinion. I know him the way you know everyone in a palace—you learn their children's names because their children sell things in the outer court, and you learn their vices because vices are currency. Grell's vice was dice. Grell owed money to the wrong kind of lender. And three months ago, Grell's debts had been paid off, all at once, by a cousin in the country who everyone agreed was remarkably generous.

Cousins in the country are not remarkably generous. Cousins in the country want something.

I watched him for two days. This is the other thing charm-selling teaches you: watching. You watch a customer's hands when you name a price; you watch their eyes when you promise results. Grell's hands told me everything. They went to his apron pocket eleven times in one morning—eleven times, always the same pocket, always the same little touching motion, like a man checking a relic or a talisman. On the second day, during the bread shift, he took it out.

A small tin. The kind apothecaries use for salves. He held it a moment too long, and he looked at it the way you look at a thing you're afraid of, and then he put it away and went back to his almond paste, and I understood that I was not looking at a murderer.

Murderers look at their instruments with purpose. Grell looked at his tin the way a man looks at a debt collector's letter—he was frightened of it, he resented it, and he did not feel he had any choice about opening it.

That's when I stopped thinking of this as an assassination and started thinking of it as a delivery.

* * *

I followed him home that night. This is easier than it sounds; palace servants walk home like sheep, in flocks, along the same streets, complaining about the same things. Grell peeled off alone at Tanner's Gate, which was my first surprise—undermasters with paid-off debts live in decent rooms near the palace, not down among the tanneries. He went into a courtyard I knew, because I'd sold a love-charm to a girl there once, and through a window with a candle behind it I watched him meet someone.

The someone was a woman in grey. Ordinary as bread. She took the tin from him, weighed it in her hand, and gave him something small and flat in exchange—which I would have bet my ledgers was not money. Then she said something to him, and Grell put his hand over his face, both hands, like a man receiving bad news or a blessing, and the woman in grey patted his arm and left.

I gave them a hundred count and went in through the window she hadn't.

Grell was sitting at his table with the little flat thing in his palm, turning it over. When he saw me he made a sound like a stepped-on cat and came up out of his chair, and I'll grant him this: for a soft man, he moved fast toward the poker by the hearth.

"Sit down, Undermaster," I said. "You don't want to do that. I taste for the king. Do you understand what that means? It means I know what fear smells like coming off a person, and yours is the worst I've had in a year. Sit down and we'll talk about your cousin."

He sat. He held the little flat thing against his chest like a child with a medal, and I leaned over and looked at it, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards into the cellar of the world.

It was a token. Stamped lead, the size of a coin, and on it was pressed a mark I knew—not from books, not from the burned temples, but from my own palm, where I'd had it since birth and always assumed it was a birthmark shaped like nothing in particular. A spiral, closed on itself, with a line drawn through it like a needle through a heart.

The sigil of the Hollow.

"Where did you get this," I said, and my voice came out wrong, quieter than I meant it, and Grell heard the wrongness and started to cry.

"I don't know what it is," he said. "I swear it on my mother. They told me it was a blessing. They said the eggs needed seasoning, just the one plate, just the one morning, and the cousin would forgive the rest of the debt, and I thought—" He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, leaving a white streak of dried flour across his cheekbone, absurd, heartbreaking. "I thought it was politics. You know? Great lords poisoning each other, that's nothing to do with the likes of me. I didn't know it was *that*." He pointed at the token, or maybe past it, at me. "What is it? What did I season?"

"You seasoned a door," I said, before I could stop myself, and then wished I hadn't, because Grell looked at me with the particular horror of a simple man who has just realized the conversation has gone somewhere over his head and under the ground.

"They said—" He swallowed. "They said whoever ate it wouldn't die. That was the condition. That was the *rule*, they were very firm, they made me swear an oath on it. Whoever ate it would not die. They only wanted... they said they wanted to see if it would open."

"Open," I repeated.

"In a person." His eyes came up to mine, and there was something worse than fear in them now. There was recognition, the dumb animal kind, the kind that doesn't know what it knows. "They asked me questions about you, after. Which stool you sat at. Whether you ever seemed ill. Whether you dreamed."

The lamps guttered. Both of them, together, though the windows were shut. In the silence after, I could hear my own heartbeat, and beneath it—faint, patient, unmistakable, the way you can hear the sea in a shell if the shell is the right shape—I could hear something else keeping time.

"They asked whether you dreamed," Grell whispered. "And I told them yes. Everyone dreams. Why does it matter if you dream?"

I looked down at my own right palm, at the birthmark I had carried for thirty-one years, at the closed spiral with the line through its heart, and I turned my hand over quickly, as if hiding it from him, as if hiding it from the room.

"It doesn't matter," I lied, with all the smoothness of fifteen years' practice, and even I didn't believe it. "Go to bed, Undermaster. Forget my face. If anyone asks, you seasoned eggs and nothing happened, and I'll make sure nothing happens to you."

He believed me, because people believe what lets them sleep. I let myself out through the window and stood a long time in the courtyard, breathing air that smelled of tallow and tanneries, feeling the token's absence in my pocket like a coal.

Because here is what I hadn't said, what I couldn't say, what I am only now writing down in the privacy of my own skull where no witch-finder can requisition it:

They hadn't asked Grell whether *the eater* dreamed.

They'd asked whether *I* dreamed. They had known, before the eggs ever reached the table, exactly which stool I sit on. The poison had never been aimed at the king, or at me-as-target, or at anything so simple as a life. It had been aimed at me-as-door, measured and fitted like a key cut for one specific lock, and the questions afterward weren't curiosity.

They were quality control.

Somewhere in this city there is a cell of people who know what I am—who have known it longer, probably, than I have—and they are checking their work. And tonight, standing in a courtyard with flour-dust still hanging in the air of a frightened man's kitchen, I understood with total clarity that the safest life I have ever built, the invisible life, the perfectly forgettable life, ended this morning at breakfast.

You cannot be invisible to people who are looking specifically for you.

I walked home the long way, through streets where the lamps burned steady and ordinary, and I did not go past the Hollow district, because I was afraid that if I got close enough, the thing beneath the stones would hear my footsteps and know them.

It already knew my name. I was sure of it the way you're sure of weather coming.

Tomorrow I would burn the books I couldn't defend and move the ledgers. Tomorrow I would find out who the woman in grey was and which of my clients had sold my habits to her. Tomorrow I would start being someone new, someone unfindable, someone with no stool at any table.

But tonight, walking home under a sky with no mercy in it, I put my hand in my pocket and found, without remembering putting it there, a pinch of grey ash that had not been there before—hollow-moss ash, fine as flour, smelling faintly of rain on warm stone.

Someone had seasoned *me*, too. And whatever door they'd opened, I could feel it standing open behind my ribs, and something on the other side was waiting, politely, the way family waits, for me to come home.
