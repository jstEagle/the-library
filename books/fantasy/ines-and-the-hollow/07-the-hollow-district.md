## 7. The Hollow District

There are three kinds of lies in cartography, and I have told all of them professionally.

The first is the lie of omission: the road that exists but goes unmarked because a duke paid for its absence. The second is the lie of exaggeration: the marsh drawn wider than it is, to discourage tax collectors with soft boots. The third is the lie of scale — the small truth stretched until it becomes a large falsehood, which is how empires are made.

What I was about to do was none of these. What I was about to do was worse. I was going to walk into my own map.

I told myself it was survey work. I wore my field coat, the one with fourteen pockets, and carried my instruments — chain, compass, plumb line, the folding rule with the cracked brass hinge I've been meaning to replace for six years. A cartographer in the field is invisible in a way no other palace servant can manage. People assume you belong wherever you're standing, measuring. Measuring is the most official-looking form of loitering ever invented.

The footpaths had been nagging me for three weeks.

They started as smudges. On the old shrine surveys — the ones marked *ruined* in a clerk's hand so confident it bordered on religious — there were wear-marks on the approaches. Not roads. Roads are declared, graded, maintained; roads have opinions. These were paths, the kind feet make when enough of them agree on a destination without anyone asking why. And they converged. Every ruined shrine in the eastern quarter had one, and every one of them bent, eventually, toward the Hollow.

The Hollow is not on any current map. That's not a boast; it's an administrative fact. After the Godfall — the night the sky over the capital burned green and a quarter of the city sank forty feet into its own foundations — the Crown did what Crowns do with embarrassments. It buried them. Officially, the Hollow district was "condemned due to subsidence" and "absorbed into the Palace Works reserve." Unofficially, it was filled with rubble, salted with a curse or two for the superstitious, and struck from every map within a decade. My predecessor's predecessor drew the erasure order himself. There's a note in his hand in the margin of the last honest plan: *Let no one look for what we have agreed is not there.*

Which is, of course, the fourth kind of lie, and the only one that requires everyone's cooperation.

So naturally, people were walking there. At night. In numbers sufficient to wear paths through packed clay and old cobbles. And nobody had noticed, because nobody looks at the ground anymore. We all look at borders.

* * *

I went at dusk, which was either very brave or very stupid, and I have decided to call it methodological.

The approach was through the tannery lanes, where the smell does the work of a wall. The paths braided together near a collapsed aqueduct arch — I know it was collapsed because I've drawn it collapsed four times, from four different surveys, across sixty years of other people's hands. The arch still stood, actually. Another entry for the ledger of things my maps say that aren't true.

Beyond it, the ground dropped away.

Not dramatically. The Hollow doesn't announce itself as a pit or a crater. It descends the way a sentence trails off — a gentle grade, then a steeper one, then you realize the rooftops around you are below your knees and the sky has become a bowl above your head. Rubble had been pushed into terraces by whoever did the burying, deliberate as masonry in places, casual as grief in others. Between the terraces grew things.

That was the first wrongness, and I want to be precise about it, because precision is the only prayer I know.

Nothing grows in the Hollow. That's doctrine. The Godfall salted the earth; the surveys say so; the surveys say *sterile, barren, accursed* in that clerk's confident hand. But the terraces were furred with moss — not the grey crusty lichen that ekes out a living on tombstones everywhere, but moss the green of new copper, thick as velvet, climbing the dead stone in patterns. Patterns. I knelt and put my fingers on it before my caution could file an objection, and it was warm.

Stone in a sunken quarter, in shadow, in autumn, warm as a sleeping animal.

I took a sample. I'm not proud of it — tearing a piece of living green off something that might have been alive in a way I couldn't name — but I took it, wrapped it in oilcloth, and put it in pocket number nine. A cartographer's instinct: if you cannot explain a thing, at least own a piece of it.

Then I heard the singing.

It was far off, down in the bowl, thin as thread. Not words — or words worn down past recognition, syllables rubbed smooth like river stones. A dozen voices, maybe fewer, rising and falling in a rhythm that wasn't quite a song and wasn't quite breathing. I stood very still among the terraces with my heart going like a apprentice's hammer and thought, with the clarity that arrives precisely when it's least useful: *this is how surveyors die. Not in avalanches. In places their maps say don't exist.*

I went down anyway. I'd like to tell you it was courage. It was curiosity wearing courage's coat, which is the same thing I said about the archive, and about Doss's requisition, and about every bad decision I've made since I learned to read contour lines.

* * *

The paths led me along the lip of a sunken street.

You could still read the district under the rubble, the way you can read a letter someone tried to burn. A fountain basin, dry, its nymph missing her head. A stairway going up to a doorway that now opened onto solid earth. A shrine — I knew it was a shrine because of the shape, the little apse, the niche where a god's image would have stood — and the niche was empty, but the stone around it was worn smooth and dark by touching. Recent touching. Oil had been burned there; the soot was fresh.

And the moss grew thickest here. It climbed the apse in whorls, spiraling inward toward the empty niche like water finding a drain.

I stood in that dead street under a bruised evening sky, in a place erased by royal decree two hundred years ago, and I understood that I was standing in a church. Not a ruin. A church. Ruins don't get swept. Someone had swept the flagstones — I could see the broom-marks in the dust between the moss patches, neat parallel strokes, done recently, done *regularly*.

My hands were shaking. I noted this too, the way I note everything, because noting is how I keep the world at arm's length. Hands shaking. Pulse elevated. Mouth dry. Subject: fear. Cause: unknown. Recommendation: further study, ideally from a great distance.

Instead I followed the singing.

* * *

They were in the old market square, thirty feet below the level of the world, gathered around a fountain that should have been rubble and wasn't.

The fountain ran. Water moved in its basin, clear and cold-smelling, though every hydrological survey for two centuries marked the Hollow's water table as poisoned beyond recovery. Candles burned in niches around the square — real candles, beeswax by the smell, expensive — and the people around them were not cultists in the way the pamphlets draw them. No masks. No knives. They were a laundress I recognized from the palace wash-house, a lamplighter, two dockhands, an old man in a clerk's robes, a girl of maybe twelve holding a bundle of dried herbs like an offering or a grocery. Ordinary people. Congregation-shaped people.

They were singing to the empty niche above the fountain, and the fountain was running, and the moss glowed.

I want to be careful with that word, because I've spent my career being precise and this is the moment precision nearly failed me. The moss did not glow the way fire glows or phosphor glows. It brightened with the singing. Each rise of those worn-smooth voices, and the green deepened, warmed, the way coals answer a bellows. When the song dipped, the light dimmed. It was — I wrote this in my field book that night, my handwriting going ragged — *it was listening.*

A cartographer's whole trade is the belief that the world holds still while you measure it. That the land is patient, passive, a corpse of rock waiting to be named. Standing in that square, I felt the belief go out of me like heat out of an open door. The land was not holding still. The land was *waiting*, the way a sleeper waits, and everything green in it was the slow breath of something enormous turning over beneath the rubble.

Under my boots — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable once I let myself hear it — the city had a heartbeat.

I didn't run. I want that recorded. My legs wanted to; my whole animal inheritance voted unanimously for flight. But I am Ines Corvane, and I have spent fifteen years learning that the difference between a coward and a professional is whether you finish the measurement. So I stood in the shadow of a terrace with my pulse hammering in my ears, competing with the pulse in the stones, and I mapped it. By candle-glimpse and memory, because I dared not strike a light: the square, the fountain, the niches, the sight-lines, the two ways in and one way out that wasn't a way out at all. I fixed the location against the stars visible in the bowl of sky. I counted the congregation — nineteen, plus the child. I noted the hour, the moon, the temperature of the moss.

And then the singing stopped, all at once, mid-verse, the way birdsong stops when a hawk crosses the sun.

Nineteen heads turned. Not toward each other. Toward me.

I had chosen my shadow well; I truly believe I was invisible, that in any ordinary gathering I'd have gone unremarked. But the girl with the herbs looked directly at where I stood — not searching, *looking*, the way you look at a sound — and the laundress from the wash-house closed her eyes and said, clearly, in the silence:

"She's come to take its picture."

Not its picture. Its *measure*. But the word went through me like a splinter, because it meant they knew what I was. Or knew what I would be. Or — and this thought arrived cold and stayed — because *it* knew, whatever slept under the flagstones, and had mentioned me to them the way you mention weather.

The old clerk smiled at me across the square. He didn't beckon. He just smiled, kindly, the way you smile at someone arriving late to a service that has already begun, and he said: "There's room. There's always room. It remembers everyone who ever walked above."

I left. I climbed the terraces with my instruments clinking softly in their pockets, and no one followed, and the singing resumed behind me before I reached the lip of the bowl — the same verse, picked up exactly where it had broken off, as if my presence had been a rest in the music, a planned pause, a notation.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat at my desk with the lamp turned low and did what I always do with a thing too large to feel: I drew it.

The Hollow, from memory, at 1:2000. The square, the fountain, the apse-shrine, the swept stones. Then I overlaid it — tracing paper, my oldest habit — on the shrine network I'd copied weeks ago, the converging footpaths, the wear-marks on the approaches. And the lines came together the way lines do when they've been waiting to, all of them bending down and inward, every path in the eastern quarter flowing toward that sunken square like veins toward a heart.

Because that's what it was. I could pretend otherwise until morning, but the drawing wouldn't let me. Nineteen worshippers and a running fountain and warm moss and a heartbeat under the flagstones, and every footpath in the district arranged around it like arteries. The Hollow wasn't buried. The Hollow was *bedded down*. Two centuries of rubble and decree and erasure, and underneath it all, something had been lying there in the dark, healing, or growing, or simply waiting for the city above to forget it thoroughly enough to need remembering.

I looked at my own hand on the desk — ink-stained, steady again now that there was work in it — and I thought about the banquet, months ago, the accident with the brazier, the moment a young taster named August Villalobos caught a falling candelabrum barehanded and laughed it off, and the mark it left on his palm. A mark I'd seen for half a second and filed away as a scar, because filing things away is what I do instead of thinking.

It had been a spiral. Whorled inward, like water finding a drain. Like moss climbing toward an empty niche.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened — really listened, the way you listen for a clock after someone mentions it — and beneath the sounds of the palace settling, beneath the wind off the roofs, so faint I could have invented it, so faint I will spend the rest of this story deciding whether I did:

the floor was beating. Once. Twice. Patient as tide.

In the morning, I would have to decide what to file, what to copy, and what to burn. Tonight I only knew one thing with a surveyor's certainty: the maps were wrong. All of them. Mine included.

And somewhere above the wash-house cisterns, a food-taster who was supposed to be forgettable was walking around with the Hollow's signature written on his skin.
