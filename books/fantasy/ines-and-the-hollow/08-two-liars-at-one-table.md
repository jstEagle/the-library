## 8. Two Liars at One Table

**August**

There are three kinds of danger at a palace banquet, and I have survived all of them for fifteen years: the wine, the politics, and the seating chart.

The first two I can manage. The wine I taste before anyone drinks; the politics I survive by being so thoroughly unremarkable that ministers forget I'm holding a fork. But the seating chart is set by clerks in the Office of Precedence, and no charm I sell in the night market can protect a man from a clerk with a grudge and a list.

So when I found my name card placed directly across from *Ines Corvane, Royal Cartographer*, I understood that either the gods had a sense of humor—which, given recent evidence, they did—or someone in the Office of Precedence wanted me dead in a subtler way than nightshade.

She was already seated when I arrived, which meant she'd watched me cross half the hall toward her. I'd felt it the way you feel weather changing. She wore court grey with a cartographer's precision—every pin placed like a border drawn after a war—and she did not look away when I sat down, which most people do, because looking at a food-taster too long makes them think about poison, and thinking about poison ruins the pheasant.

"Ines Corvane," she said, offering her hand as if we hadn't been introduced twice before, years ago, in a corridor that smelled of ink and lamp oil.

"August Villalobos." Her hand was cool and dry, an ink-stain on the second knoble of her forefinger. "You've been promoted since last we spoke."

"I've been re-shelved. There's a difference." She withdrew her hand and unfolded her napkin with the crisp economy of someone who resented cloth. "They've moved me from the survey office to the archive. Apparently old maps need guarding."

"And do they?"

"From what?" She smiled. It was a good smile—practiced, warm at the edges, hollow in the middle. I recognized the craftsmanship. It was mine. "Moths, mostly. Ambition, occasionally."

A footman poured wine. I touched the rim of my glass to my lower lip—the smallest possible tasting, enough for the tongue, invisible to the table—and gave the footman my nod. The vintage was clean. Pity. A little belladonna would have given us both something honest to talk about.

"You look well," she said.

"I look employed. It's often mistaken for well-being." I cut into the pheasant. Under the honey glaze, the meat was exactly what it claimed to be, which these days struck me as almost touching. "And you? The archive agrees with you?"

"The archive asks nothing of me. That's its chief virtue." She ate three bites, precisely, then set her knife down at an angle that said *I am finished performing appetite*. "Though it does get one thinking. About how much paper a kingdom produces. How much of it contradicts itself."

"Paper always lies," I said. "That's why we invented maps."

"That's why we invented *better* maps." Her eyes came up and held mine, brown and level and patient as a surveyor's transit. "The old ones are full of errors. Shrines marked ruined that aren't. Districts marked empty that aren't. You'd be amazed what a careful person can find if she overlays forty years of surveys and looks at where the mistakes repeat."

I took a slow sip of wine I didn't want. Somewhere beneath the floorboards, beneath the foundations, beneath whatever the city stood on instead of bedrock, something I refused to name shifted its weight, the way a sleeper turns.

"Mistakes repeat," I agreed, "when the same hand makes them. Or the same fear."

"Is that what you think it is? Fear?"

"I think most things in this palace are fear wearing embroidery." I gestured at the hall with my fork—the banners, the gilded nonsense, the usurper's portrait watching us eat with painted eyes. "Including the pheasant."

She laughed. It was a real laugh, which was a tactical error on her part, and mine, because I felt it land somewhere under my ribs that I keep locked for professional reasons.

*Be invisible,* I reminded myself. *Be furniture.* Fifteen years of rule, and one cartographer with a laugh was dismantling it course by course.

* * *

**Ines**

He lied beautifully. That was the first thing I confirmed, and confirmation is a kind of pleasure, even when it confirms the worst.

August Villalobos had been a footnote in my working life for six years—a name on taster rosters, a face in corridors, a man whose comings and goings I had once, idly, professionally, begun to mark on the margins of district maps because his pattern bothered me the way a misprinted coastline bothers me. He vanished on Tuesdays. He returned by ways that didn't exist on any official plan. And he did it all with the studied blandness of a man who has practiced being overlooked until it became a discipline, like calligraphy or murder.

Now he sat across from me making jokes about pheasant, and every joke was a door closing politely in my face.

"Fear wearing embroidery," he'd said. Which was true, and also a deflection, because he'd understood immediately what I was asking about—the repeated errors, the shrines—and had answered without answering, the way a river goes around a stone.

I let him have the stone. For now.

"The taster's guild must be proud of you," I said, selecting the tone of idle small talk. "Fifteen years in the post. They say no taster has lasted so long since the Godfall."

"They say a lot of things since the Godfall. Most of it's wrong." He chewed, swallowed, and I watched his throat work with the detached attention I give to anything that might be relevant later. "Longevity in my profession isn't skill. It's dullness. Poisons come for interesting people."

"And you're not interesting?"

"I'm the least interesting man in this room. Possibly in this palace. It's my single greatest accomplishment." He raised his glass slightly, a toast to his own mediocrity, and drank. And there—there it was again, the thing I'd been circling for weeks in my notes: he tasted before he drank. Not the ceremonial sip the guild requires, performed for the master of ceremonies. Something smaller. A touch of the rim to the lip, half a heartbeat, done while reaching for his napkin. I'd seen it twice tonight already. A man checking his food the way other men check the exits.

"You must have a remarkable constitution," I said. "Or remarkable luck."

"Both. Luck is just constitution that hasn't been tested yet." His eyes flicked up, quick and dark. "Why do you ask, Mistress Corvane? Planning a dinner party?"

"Always planning. Mapping, really. It's the same instinct." I turned my wineglass by its stem. "Tell me—do you know the Hollow district? The old sunken quarter?"

Something happened behind his face. It was very small. If I hadn't spent six years studying that face in absentia, I'd have missed it entirely: a stillness, brief as a blink held too long, like a clock pausing between ticks.

"Everyone knows the Hollow," he said. "It's where the city keeps its regrets."

"It's where the city keeps its *foundations*. Regrets are just what we named them afterward." I smiled and reached for the salt—I didn't need it; the soup was fine—but the gesture bought me a breath. "I've been cataloguing the pre-Godfall surveys. Fascinating material. Did you know there were eleven shrines in the Hollow alone? All marked destroyed in the official record. But the foot traffic data—the old toll ledgers, the watch reports—show paths converging there for decades after. People kept walking to ruins. For years. Very devoted, walking to nothing."

"Perhaps they liked the exercise."

"Perhaps." I let the word sit between us like a coin on a table. "Or perhaps the ruins weren't ruins."

He looked at me then—not the banquet look, not the furniture look, but something older and more tired, and for one unguarded moment I thought: *he's going to tell me.* Whatever he carried, whatever made a food-taster walk nonexistent streets on Tuesdays, it was heavy, and heaviness wants setting down, and I had offered, in my oblique cartographer's way, to hold one end.

Then the moment closed, the way water closes over a dropped stone, and he was bland again, and I could have wept from frustration.

"Careful," he said softly. "In this palace, noticing things is how people disappear. I'd hate to lose the only person at this table who doesn't bore me."

* * *

**August**

She knew. Not everything—thank every indifferent god, not everything—but she knew the shape of the hole where the truth should be, and she was mapping it with the patience of her profession.

I ran through my options between the fish course and the meat. Option one: deflect harder. Risky; she'd already mapped my deflections. Option two: confess everything over the quail. Catastrophic; the hall held two hundred ears and at least four belonged to Serel Doss's people, who circulated at these functions like grease through silk. Option three—

Option three was the one I hated, because option three was honesty, in carefully measured doses, like medicine.

"The Hollow," I said, when the servants cleared our plates and the noise of the hall dipped into that lull before toasts. "You asked. So I'll tell you what I know, which is almost nothing, and you can decide whether it's worth your trouble."

Her whole body went very still, the way a deer goes still, or a hawk.

"I grew up on a farm near the eastern marches," I said, keeping my voice at the register of a man discussing drainage. "Old country. Before the Godfall, my grandmother's people kept shrines out there too. Little ones. Hearth-stones, mostly. When I came to the capital I walked through the Hollow once, out of—homesickness, I suppose. And the stones there weren't cold." I turned my knife on the linen. "That's all. Stones that shouldn't have been warm were warm. I told myself it was geothermal. I've been telling myself that for fifteen years."

It was more truth than I'd spoken aloud in a decade, and it cost me visibly, and I watched her understand that it cost me, and I watched her decide—visibly, deliberately—not to press.

"Warm stones," she repeated.

"Warm stones."

"My grandmother had a saying about that." She looked past me at the candles. "*What the city buries, it does not kill. It only makes it patient.*" Her eyes came back to mine. "Farmers' saying too?"

"Universal, probably." My mouth had gone dry. I drank wine I'd already tested and barely tasted it. "Ines—"

"AUGUST VILLALOBOS."

The herald's voice cracked across the hall like a whip, and every head turned, and fifteen years of invisibility ended in a single sentence.

* * *

**Ines**

Later I would understand that the toast was a trap, though at the time it looked like honor.

The master of ceremonies rose, golden chain glittering, and announced that the throne wished to honor "the faithful service of August Villalobos, Royal Food-Taster, fifteen years unsullied"—and the hall applauded, and August went white in a way that had nothing to do with modesty.

Because I was watching him closely—because I had been watching him all night, the way I watch coastlines for the small wrongness that precedes shipwreck—I saw what the applause hid.

He was afraid. Not of the honor. Of the *attention*. This man who had engineered his entire existence into a shadow was being dragged into the candlelight, and every instinct in him was screaming, and he was smiling through it, and the smile was the worst lie he'd told all evening because it wasn't for me. It was for the room.

The cup came out on a velvet cushion. Wine, dark as a closed door. The master of ceremonies invited the taster to drink to his own health—an old joke, an old ritual, the hall laughing—and August lifted the cup, and I saw his lips part for the testing touch, the little rim-taste I'd catalogued twice tonight.

And then I saw him stop.

Saw him decide.

He drank the whole thing. Deeply. Deliberately. Like a man swallowing a verdict.

The hall cheered. The usurper's portrait looked on with its painted patience. August set down the empty cup, and his hand was steady, and his face was pleasant, and only I—only I, at one table's distance, with six years of his patterns folded into my memory—saw the exact moment the color left his knuckles.

He excused himself within the quarter hour. Of course he did; a taster has duties. But as he passed behind my chair, he paused, and said, low, pitched for me alone:

"The archive. Thursday. The third drawer of the eastern case sticks—you'll want to lift, not pull."

Then he was gone, into the crowd, and I sat with my heart behaving disgracefully, and dissected the sentence like a specimen. It was an invitation. It was a warning. It was possibly a trap laid by a man who suspected I was hunting him and wanted to control the ground.

But underneath all of it, unmistakable, was the thing I had actually heard:

*I know you'll come. I know what you are. Lift, don't pull.*

Across the hall, near the dais, a lean man in witch-finder's grey was watching the doors August had gone through. Then, slowly, deliberately, he turned and watched me.

I raised my glass to Serel Doss, and smiled my best mapmaker's smile, and began composing the lie I would need for Thursday.
