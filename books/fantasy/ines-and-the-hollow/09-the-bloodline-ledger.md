## 9. The Bloodline Ledger

There are three ways into the Tasters' Guildhall after dark, and I have used all of them at least once, though never for anything worth hanging for until tonight.

The front door is a joke—two guards who play dice and lose at it, and a lock older than the usurper's grandmother that opens to a whisper and a shoulder. The cellar hatch is better: it lets you out behind the coal stores where nobody with clean clothes has business going. But the best way is the one nobody guards because nobody remembers it exists—the old sample chute, built back when guildmasters tested deliveries in the yard and sent the leavings down to the kitchens without troubling the stairs. It's a stone throat barely wide enough for a man who has spent fifteen years staying thin on principle, and it drops you into the records room itself, which tells you something about how much the guild ever valued its own paperwork.

I went down the chute at half past two in the morning, wearing dark wool and carrying a shuttered lantern, a pry-bar, and the particular nausea of a man about to read his own file.

Let me explain why a food-taster breaks into his own guild's records, since it will sound mad even to me now, writing it here where no one will ever read it.

For fifteen years my life has run on rails I laid myself. Taste everything twice. Spit what you can spit. Swallow what you must, and let the poison find nothing in you to hold onto. Be punctual, be dull, be forgettable. Sell your little charms to desperate people in the night market, charge fair prices, remember every name, and never once let anyone see you as anything more than what you appear: August Villalobos, taster third class, a man whose face slides off memory like rain off waxed canvas. I had rules the way other men have religion, and the rules had kept me alive when cleverer men died of a bad oyster or a jealous cook.

Then came the nightshade at breakfast—not just nightshade, but nightshade steeped in something older, something that had *looked* for me inside the poison like a hand searching a drawer. Then the washerwoman's prayer answered before she'd finished paying me. Then three clients in one week with miracles under their tongues, and the burnt offering that hummed against my palm, and my skin drinking the heat of it like a dry field drinks rain. And then, last night, the dream I won't write down yet because writing it might make it more true than it already is.

Something was waking up. Something knew my name. And I had begun to suspect, in the cold quiet way suspicion arrives when it has been waiting a long time for you to notice it, that the waking thing and I had met before—that we had been introduced, formally, by someone, some years ago, while I slept.

Because here is the thing I could not stop turning over: my immunity was never natural. I'd told myself it was—a farm boy's luck, a gut of iron, whatever comforting lie fits—but luck doesn't taste like anything. What happened in my mouth when poison touched it wasn't absence. It was *recognition*. The toxins would arrive and something in me would rise to meet them, courteous as a host greeting guests, and turn them away. Fifteen years I'd called that survival. Lying in the dark these past nights, I'd started calling it training.

And if I had been trained—if someone had bred or bought or bound me into this—then there would be paper. There is always paper. Bureaucracy is the one god that never sleeps and never forgives, and the Tasters' Guild keeps ledgers the way misers keep teeth.

So: the chute, the drop, the dust.

* * *

The records room smelled the way all old rooms smell when they belong to institutions: tallow, mouse, and self-importance. Rows of cedar cabinets, each drawer labeled by year in the fussy copperplate of clerks who believed their ink would outlive empires—which, given the state of the empire, it probably would.

Fifteen years ago I had been brought to this city in a carter's wagon with a bill of sale folded in the driver's pocket and a name I hadn't earned yet. The guild bought me the way they bought everything: cheaply, and with an eye to resale value. I remembered the intake day in fragments—the weighing, the tasting trials, a sour old man with ink-stained fingers watching me not die with an expression I had spent years deciding was professional satisfaction.

Master Corvane. That was his name. Old Corvane, dead now nine years, who taught me the rules one by one and never once raised his voice, and whom I had mourned, quietly and genuinely, because he was the closest thing to a father the trade ever gave me.

His handwriting was on the intake ledger. Of course it was. He signed everything; he was that kind of man.

What stopped me was not his signature. It was mine beside it.

August Villalobos, written in my own hand—or a hand so like mine that my eye refused the difference—and beneath it, in the same script, the entry I had come looking for and dreaded finding:

*Subject A.V., age 14. Immunity confirmed across eleven trials (belladonna, aconite, arsenic, compounds). Immunity type: inherited. Lineage: see sealed folio. Recommend retention.*

Inherited. In my handwriting. From a day when I was fourteen years old and had never held a guild pen in my life.

I stood there a long time with the lantern turned low, listening to my own breathing do something unprofessional.

People talk about betrayal as if it were a knife. It isn't. It's a slow cold water rising around your ankles while the room stays exactly as warm and familiar as it was a moment ago. Every entry in that ledger was in my hand. Not just the intake—the annual confirmations, the trial results, the little marginal notes in a style I recognized the way you recognize your own gait from behind. *Subject cooperative. Subject incurious. Subject shows no interest in lineage inquiry.* No interest. As if I had ever been asked. As if the question hadn't simply never been permitted to occur to me, the way certain doors in a house go unopened not because they're locked but because something in you walks past them every day without seeing them.

Someone had been writing me. Fifteen years of entries, forged forward through time in advance or copied backward from somewhere else, keeping me filed like a jar of preserves: *inherited, incurious, retained.* And the forger knew my hand well enough to fool the only expert on it—me, mostly, except for the small screaming part of me that kept saying *no, look closer, this is wrong, this has always been wrong.*

Look closer, then. I set the lantern on the desk and pulled the intake ledger fully into the light, and made myself read like a forger reads.

The deception was very good. That was the first insult. Whoever wrote those entries had studied my letterforms—the way my capital V leans left, the way I cross my t's low when I'm tired, the little hook I put on final s's that no teacher ever managed to beat out of me. They had studied me the way I studied poisons: patiently, affectionately, over years.

But affection leaves traces. On the twelfth annual confirmation, in the word *retained*, the forger's r had come out rounded instead of hooked—Corvane's r, the old man's habit bleeding through the disguise. And on the intake page, pressed faintly into the paper from the sheet above, there was a ghost of an earlier draft, blotted away but legible if you tilted the page against the light the way I tilt wine glasses, professionally, out of habit:

*Immunity type: ~~cultivated~~ inherited.*

Cultivated. Crossed out. Changed to inherited—for whose benefit? Mine? I had never seen this page until tonight. For the witch-finders' auditors, then, who came through every few years sniffing for bloodline irregularities. Someone had wanted the record to say *born this way*, because *made this way* would have drawn questions, and questions were dangerous—to whom?

To me, obviously. But also, I suspected, to whoever had done the making.

The sealed folio. *Lineage: see sealed folio.* Sealed folios live in the master's cabinet, iron-banded, three locks, and Master Corvane had died owning all three keys, which should have meant the cabinet was drilled open years ago. It hadn't been. I checked the dust on the hasps—undisturbed, a soft grey felt, the kind of dust that takes a decade. Nobody had opened that cabinet since the old man's hands had stopped being able to open it.

Which meant the keys had gone wherever dead guildmasters' keys go, which meant either the folio was unreachable, or—

Or the old man had left them somewhere for me. Because he had known, hadn't he. Sitting across from me at a hundred lessons, watching me not die, teaching me to be invisible—he had known what I was and what was coming, and he had spent his whole retirement building the walls of my ignorance brick by careful brick, and maybe, at the end, he had repented of one wall.

I didn't find any keys that night. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the one luxury this chapter affords me. I searched the cabinet seams, the false bottom I found behind the trial registers, the hollow under the floor slate where guildmasters traditionally hide their sins. Nothing. Whatever Corvane had left behind, he had hidden it better than he hid his handwriting, and I came away with dust in my cuffs and a ghost-word in my head: *cultivated.*

Not born. Made. Farmed, even—I kept thinking of the word *farmed*, and hating it, and knowing it was right. My family had been orchard folk before the tax collectors took the trees. I knew what cultivation looked like. Pruning. Grafting. Waiting seasons for fruit you'd decided on long before the tree did.

Somebody had decided on me before I was born. Somebody had tended me through fourteen years of childhood the way you tend a rootstock—kept alive, kept ignorant, kept *incurious*—and then transplanted me into the palace to ripen in the dark.

And the worst part, the part I keep circling back to like a tongue to a broken tooth: it had worked. I had loved him. I had sat at old Corvane's table and drunk his bad wine and thought, *here at last is a man who wants nothing from me.* He wanted everything from me. He wanted my whole life, and he got it, and he forged my gratitude into the ledger in my own hand so the record would show a willing subject, and I sat there in the dust reading my own forged contentment and understood that I had never once in fifteen years been a person in that building. I had been a vintage.

* * *

I climbed back up the chute with the dawn still an hour off and walked home the long way, through streets where the lamps were dying and the bakers were waking, and I did my best thinking, which is to say I did my worst thinking, thoroughly.

Here is what I know about being managed, now that I know I've been managed: it rearranges your entire past. Every kindness acquires a second floor. The master who fed me when I arrived half-starved—was that mercy, or feeding the stock? The rule he gave me, *be invisible*, which I adopted as wisdom and wore as armor—was it ever mine? Or was it the husbandman's instruction: grow low, grow quiet, don't draw the eye of anyone who might wonder what you're growing into? Even my loneliness, which I had always taken as the price of my caution, might have been a feature. A man with no one is a man no one tells things to, and a man no one tells things to never learns what he is.

I had built a life on the assumption that I was free, and freedom, it turns out, was the longest-running charm anyone ever sold me. At least my night-market customers got what they paid for. I got fifteen years of carefully curated incuriosity and a pension plan consisting of *retained*.

And yet—and here is where the wry part of me, the part that survives everything, lit its little lamp and spoke up—yet whoever cultivated me had also, in a sense, saved me. Fourteen years old, sold, immune, valuable: boys like that get dissected or weaponized or married off to somebody's ambitions. Instead I got a quiet trade, a room of my own, a market stall on Thursdays, and rules that kept me breathing. You can hate the gardener and still admit the greenhouse kept the frost off. Both things are true. That's the obscenity of it. Betrayal by your own past doesn't come with a villain you can point at; it comes with arithmetic, and the arithmetic keeps coming out ambiguous, and ambiguity is the one poison I have no immunity to.

But the ledger said *see sealed folio*, and the folio was sealed, and sealed things in my experience are invitations with the postage unpaid. Someone, sometime, had expected me to come looking. The forged entries weren't just a cage—they were a trail. *Inherited. See sealed folio. Retained.* Written in my hand so that when I finally read them, I would know the writer knew me. Knew me intimately. Loved me, even, in the terrible way gardeners love.

Old Corvane had been a hidden priest. I understand that now the way you understand a joke an hour late—suddenly, completely, with a groan. All those little rituals he kept, the salt at the threshold, the way he touched the doorframe twice leaving any room. I'd taken them for an old man's superstitions. They were observances. He was a priest of something, and I was his relic, his reliquary, his little box of holy bones that walked and tasted soup and never asked why it was carried.

And the thing in the nightshade, the thing that had reached for me through the poison like a hand through a keyhole—it hadn't been hunting a stranger. It had been calling on an old acquaintance. Checking whether the vessel was still *retained*. Still sealed. Still incurious.

Well. The entries were current through last spring. Whoever held the pen had stopped writing, or gone elsewhere to write, or—

Or was waiting for me to catch up.

I got home as the sky went the grey of dishwater, barred my door, and sat at my table with my little hoard of charms laid out in front of me—the fertility knots, the fever-spoons, the luck-braids, all of them dead weight now, plain thread and pewter, because their power had never been in the making. It had been in the answering. Something had been answering through me for years, patient as interest accruing, and the ledger proved the account was opened before I could walk.

One item remained in my inventory that wasn't mine: the burnt offering from the woman with the humming miracle, the shard of votive bronze that drank heat from my skin. I set it on the table between us, the way you sit across from a creditor.

It was warm. It had been cold in my pocket all the way home.

"You knew," I told it. "All of you knew. Him, the guild, whatever's under the city. Everyone read the file except the subject."

The bronze said nothing, being bronze, but the warmth pulsed once against the tabletop, deliberate as a knock.

And that was when I heard, from the street below my window, unhurried footsteps stopping at my door—and a voice I did not know saying, politely, to someone I couldn't see: "Yes, this is the one. The ledger lists him as retained."

* * *

I blew out my lamp, because fifteen years of rules don't die in a night, and stood very still in my own kitchen, listening to a stranger outside quote my file like scripture.

Retained. Somebody had come to collect.
