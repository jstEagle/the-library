## 10. Serel's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, which I have always considered an insult. Mondays are for executions and other administrative unpleasantness; Tuesdays are supposed to be for correcting survey errors and drinking bad tea in peace. Instead, a boy in witch-finder grey stood at my office door holding a folded card as though it might bite him, and perhaps it would have, if Serel Doss had written anything on it besides my name and the word *now*.

I finished the contour line I was drawing first. Not out of courage — out of the small, spiteful dignity available to people whose entire profession is making tyrants' property legible. If I was going to be interrogated or recruited or eaten, I was going to arrive with clean hands and ink under my nails like a professional.

"Cartographer Corvane," Doss said when they showed me into his office, "you smell of juniper ink."

"Everyone smells of juniper ink. It's the only ink that survives humidity. Would you prefer I smell of fear? I'm told it's very fashionable this season among your visitors."

He did not laugh. Serel Doss never laughed; I suspected he'd had the capacity surgically removed along with whatever else the inquisition takes from its officers. He was a long, dry man, the color of old rope, with eyes the pale grey of a map where nothing has been surveyed yet. He sat behind a desk so bare it looked newly widowed, and he gestured me into the chair across from it with two fingers.

"You filed nothing," he said.

"I file everything. It's my job."

"About the footpaths." He let that sit between us like a coin on a table. "Converging on the Hollow district. From shrines marked ruined in the Godfall surveys. You found the pattern three weeks ago. You filed nothing about it."

My heart performed a small, disciplined maneuver that I felt in my throat. Three weeks ago I had sat at my desk at midnight copying those paths onto private paper, burning the candle down to a stub, telling myself that curiosity was not treason, that noticing was not a crime. Apparently noticing *was* a crime, and I had been observed doing it.

"Ruined shrines attract squatters," I said. "Squatters make paths. Paths converge on sheltered ground. The Hollow is sheltered because half of it is underground. This isn't cartography, Witch-Finder, it's drainage."

"Mm." He opened a drawer. I want to record, for honesty's sake, that my palms were sweating and I was already composing the letter Ines Corvane's mother would receive explaining her daughter's regrettable accident on the palace stairs. But my voice held, because it always holds. Fifteen years of being the cleverest person in rooms full of men who thought maps were pictures had taught me that much.

He laid a sheet on the desk. My own sheet. A copy of my survey overlay — the one I kept in a locked drawer, under false labels, in a cipher of my own devising based on obsolete river names.

Someone had broken my cipher. Someone had copied my work. And someone had drawn, in red, a circle around the Hollow with a notation in a hand I didn't recognize: *confirmed.*

"Your fakes are good," Doss said. "The ones you gave my requisition officer last week. Good enough that I nearly believed them. The shrine positions were plausible, the decay patterns were consistent, you even got the ink age right — you aged the ink, which shows either great cunning or great practice." He folded his hands. "But you made one mistake, Cartographer. You made them too good. Real archives are messy. Real archives have water damage and clerical errors and a junior scribe who spelled 'Kettering' wrong in 1809. Your fakes were perfect. Perfection is a signature."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a confession or a lie, and I had a strong preference for lies but needed a moment to select a good one.

"Who taught you?" he asked suddenly, leaning forward. "The caution. The layered records. The instinct to prepare false documents years before anyone asks for them. That's not cartographer training. That's *someone else's* training. Who?"

And here is the thing I have never told anyone: I didn't know. There was a hole in my memory shaped exactly like that question. I could remember my mother teaching me stars, my father teaching me patience, my masters teaching me projection and scale. But the habit of duplicating everything, of keeping the true copy hidden inside the false one like a seed inside fruit — that habit had simply *always been there*, the way some people are born knowing how to whistle. When I tried to trace it back to its source, my memory went smooth and blank as unsurveyed ocean.

"No one," I said. "I'm naturally suspicious. It's a character flaw. My mother despaired of me."

Doss studied me for a long moment, and I had the distinct sensation of being measured for something. A coffin, possibly. Or a uniform.

* * *

"Here is what happens now," he said, rising to stand at the window behind his desk. Through the glass, the capital spread out in its smug morning haze, all spires and smoke, the Hollow district a sunken bruise at the city's edge that official maps rendered as a park. "You have committed, technically, obstruction of an inquisition inquiry. That is a crime with a cell attached to it. A damp cell. I've seen it."

"How generous of you to offer a tour."

"But," he continued, in the tone of a man laying down trump cards one at a time purely to watch his opponent's face, "you are also the best archivist in this palace, you have demonstrated independent initiative in exactly the area my inquiry concerns, and you lied to my officer with a skill I find professionally admirable." He turned around. "So instead of the cell, I am offering you a position."

"A position."

"In my inquisition. Cartographic division. Which currently consists of you, if you accept, and a man named Pell who cannot read elevation lines." He produced a second document from the drawer — the drawer seemed bottomless, like a magician's hat, like sin — and slid it toward me. "Terms are inside. Salary is double. Access to the full archive, including the sealed levels. And immunity for your earlier... discretion."

I looked at the document without touching it. "And in exchange?"

"In exchange, you find things for me. Shrines. Networks. People who pray to the wrong gods in the right places." His pale eyes settled on mine with the weight of a plumb line. "The old religion is waking up, Cartographer. Prayers are being answered in this city. Do you understand how obscene that is? For two hundred years the covenant of the Godfall has kept the divine quiet, buried, *manageable*. And now washerwomen are conceiving children they prayed for, and fever victims are waking whole, and somewhere under our feet something is listening again. The usurper does not intend to permit it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I arrest you for the obstruction, seize your personal papers — which I will find, because I found your cipher once and ciphers are habits, and habits repeat — and I conduct this inquiry without the benefit of the finest mapmaker in the capital." He smiled. It was the smile of a door closing. "I would genuinely rather have you, Corvane. But I can do without you. Can you do without your freedom?"

I should hate him for the elegance of it. Part of me did. A larger part of me was doing arithmetic.

Because here is what Serel Doss did not know, could not know: that my locked drawer contained not one secret but several, and the largest of them had a face. Grey eyes, forgettable features, a way of standing in banquet halls like furniture that had learned manners. August Villalobos, royal food-taster, the invisible man, the man whose disappearances I had been mapping for two years on a private chart labeled *river gauging stations* because even my own paranoia deserved a cover story.

His name was going to be on a list. I knew it the way you know weather coming — in the teeth, in the old scar on my wrist. A man who vanishes on a schedule aligned with shrine activity, who sells charms in night markets (yes, I knew that too; I had followed him twice, hating myself both times, unable to stop), who tasted poison at the usurper's table three weeks ago and disposed of it without reporting it — such a man was exactly the kind of name that ended up in witch-finder ledgers, written in red, circled, *confirmed*.

If I refused Doss, someone else would build the lists. Someone less careful, less corruptible, less — I flatter myself — *human*. And August Villalobos, whoever and whatever he was, would end up in a damp cell with a tour guide.

If I accepted, I would be the wolf's own cartographer. But wolves need maps, and maps can be drawn wrong. I had faked an archive once already. I could fake a hundred.

It is remarkable, the speed at which self-deception moves when it's running downhill.

* * *

"I'll need conditions," I said.

Doss raised an eyebrow a fraction of a millimeter, which for him was open astonishment.

"One: I report to you alone. No requisition officers, no Pells. Two: my archive stays under my key. You may consult it; you may not remove originals. Three:" — and here I made myself say it lightly, carelessly, the way you step onto thin ice by whistling — "when you compile your lists of persons of interest, I see them. All of them. If I'm mapping your networks, I need to know who's in them, or my geography will be fiction."

A pause. Long enough that I heard the clock on his mantel, which ticked slightly off-beat, like a limping man.

"Agreed," Doss said, and I understood that he thought he had just caught me — that my third condition was a tell, evidence of some connection I was trying to monitor, and that he was filing it away in whatever drawer served him for a heart. Let him. A spy who believes she's been caught is a spy with room to move.

He brought out the list then. The current one. He laid it flat and turned it toward me with two fingers, watching my face the whole time, and I gave him nothing, because my face has been surveyed and mapped as carefully as any coastline and I know every inlet of it.

Fourteen names. Merchants, a midwife, a retired priest of no particular god, a chandler. And there, seventh from the top, in Doss's narrow red hand:

**Villalobos, August. Royal taster. Immunity anomalous. Under observation.**

Under observation. Not arrested. Not confirmed. A thread, not a noose — yet. Something in my chest unclenched and immediately re-clenched around a different shape, because *under observation* meant watchers, meant reports, meant that every night market visit, every charm sold, every unexplained absence was being written down by someone paid to notice.

I had noticed him first, though. Some petty, shameful corner of me took comfort in that.

"Interesting list," I said. "The chandler's shop backs onto a shrine site. That'll save you a week."

Doss almost smiled. "You see why I wanted you."

I signed the terms. My signature came out steady, which surprised me; I had expected it to shake. Perhaps steadiness is just another kind of numbness wearing a good coat. He shook my hand — dry rope, cold grip — and told me to report Monday, and I walked out of the witch-finders' offices into the ordinary daylight feeling like a woman who had sold her house to buy back a single room.

* * *

That evening I did three things.

First, I burned my true copy of the shrine overlay — all of it, the footpaths, the dates, the beautiful terrible pattern — in my hearth, feeding the pages in one at a time so the flames stayed small. It hurt worse than I expected. Two years of work, of midnight candle-stubs, of the pure animal joy of *finding something*, gone up a chimney. But Doss had my cipher, and anything I kept, he could eventually take. The pattern lived in my head now. Heads are harder to raid than drawers, though I no longer assume impossible.

Second, I began a new map. Not on paper — on the inside covers of three different atlases, in three different hands, none of them mine. On it I marked what I actually knew: the Hollow's sunken streets, the shrines that might still breathe, and one small square near the tasters' quarters marked with a symbol only I would read as a person. A compass rose, drawn slightly wrong, pointing not north but inward. *Here stands a man*, the wrongness said. *Protect the error.*

Third, I wrote a letter I did not send. To whom, I couldn't say — to August, though we had exchanged perhaps forty words in two years, most of them about the placement of a dessert table at the Midwinter banquet. To the woman I might have been if I'd never learned to duplicate documents. To whoever had taught me caution and then walked out of my memory, leaving the door swinging.

*They asked me who taught me,* I wrote. *I said no one. I think I lied. I think there is someone, and I think they're the reason I knew to hide things before I knew there was anything worth hiding. If you exist: what did you know, and when did you make me into this?*

I burned that letter too. Unsent letters burn cleaner than sent ones; there's no one waiting on the other end to be disappointed.

* * *

Monday morning, I reported to the inquisition. Doss gave me a desk in a windowless room that smelled of ash and lamp oil, a stack of requisitioned parish surveys, and a list of questions. Where do people pray when their churches are closed. Which ruins have warm floors. Which wells taste of iron.

I answered with maps. Good ones — that was the horror of it, I was *good* at this, my pen moved through the witch-finders' service as smoothly as it had ever moved through the palace surveys, and each shrine I located, each network I traced, was a family I was painting a target on. I told myself the lie I had prepared: that every target I drew accurately was a target I could later draw inaccurately, that I was building trust in order to spend it, that the false archives I would feed Doss down the line would undo the true ones I fed him now.

It is a good lie. I intend to keep telling it until it suffocates or comes true.

On Thursday, Doss stopped by my desk and set down a single page. An observation report. Routine, he said. Just so I understood the quality of information flowing through his office.

I read it with the blank efficiency of a professional, and my blood turned to well-water, cold and still.

*A subject matching Villalobos was observed in the Lower Night Market, Tuesday last, transacting with a female launderer of the Soapmakers' Row. Subject departed. Launderer subsequently reported to neighbors a conception following seven barren years. Neighbors characterize event as miraculous. Recommend escalation to active surveillance.*

Tuesday last. The night I had followed him — no. The night before that. The washerwoman. The charm. The prayer answered *instantly*, impossibly, while August stood right there selling the instrument of it, looking, if my memory served, like a man watching his own house catch fire.

They had seen it too. They had written it down. And somewhere in a drawer in Doss's office, the entry beside August Villalobos's name was acquiring a second line.

"Curious," I said, handing the page back. "Soapmakers' Row. That's adjacent to the Kettering drainage channel — the one your fakes—" I caught myself. "The one the old surveys mislabel. I'll pull the geography tonight. Miracles love bad drainage."

Doss looked at me a moment too long. Then he nodded and moved on down the row of desks, a dry rope of a man trailing the smell of lamp oil, and I bent over my parish surveys and drew a shrine network with a steady hand while inside me two loyalties met for the first time, formally, like duelists, and bowed.

One wore my crown. The other wore grey eyes and a forgettable face and had never once noticed me noticing him, and I hated them both, and I knew — with the certainty of a woman who has spent her life reading terrain — which one I would follow when the ground finally gave way.

The question, as I sharpened my pen and reached for the next survey, was whether I would manage to be standing on solid earth when it happened, or whether I would take the whole map down with me.

Outside the windowless room, somewhere under the city, something warm was listening to prayers. And somewhere above it, a food-taster was about to discover that the inquisition had learned his name.

I intended to be the one who told him.
