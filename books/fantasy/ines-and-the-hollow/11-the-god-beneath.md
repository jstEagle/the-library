## 11. The God Beneath

I have always been a light sleeper. Fifteen years of tasting other people's breakfasts will do that to a man. You learn to wake at the creak of a floorboard three rooms away, at the particular silence of a kitchen that has gone too quiet, at the smell of almonds where no almonds should be. Sleep, for me, was never rest. It was a sentry post I happened to be lying down in.

So when I woke that night standing in the middle of my own room with my boots on and my coat buttoned wrong, I did what any sensible man would do.

I panicked quietly.

There is an art to quiet panic. I have refined it the way other men refine wine. My heart was going like a drum corps, but my breathing stayed level, my hands stayed steady, and my mind—my treacherous, well-trained mind—began taking inventory. Boots: on. Door: unlocked, which I never leave. Candle: burned down two fingers' worth, so I'd been up for some time. Memory of getting up: none whatsoever.

The last thing I remembered was dreaming. Which was itself unusual, because I don't dream. Or rather, I had trained myself not to, the way you train yourself not to flinch when the usurper's chamberlain watches you swallow. Dreams are doors, my old master used to say, and a taster keeps his doors shut. I'd taken it as a figure of speech. Master Corvin took everything as a figure of speech until it turned out to be a warning.

In the dream—and I could only assume it had been a dream, because the alternative was worse—there had been a warmth. Not fire. Fire is greedy; it takes the air and gives you nothing back but noise. This was older than fire. This was the warmth of bread left on a stone by the oven's mouth, the warmth of a hearth in a house where someone is waiting up for you. It had pulsed. Slowly. Patiently. The way a heart pulses when it has all the time in the world and knows it.

And it had said my name.

Not *August*. That was the part I kept circling back to, standing there in the dark with my coat buttoned wrong like a drunkard or a bridegroom. It hadn't said August. It had said something underneath August, the way bedrock sits underneath topsoil, and the sound of it had gone through me like a bell struck inside my ribs. I couldn't reproduce it now if I tried. I could only feel the shape of where it had been, the way your tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth.

My feet knew where they wanted to go. That was the truly alarming part. They were pointed at the door, and past the door, down through four floors of tenement and six streets of night market and one very steep set of drowned stairs, toward the Hollow.

*No*, I told my feet.

They waited, politely, the way a dog waits when it knows you'll give in.

Let me be clear about who I am, since apparently the universe had forgotten. I am nobody. That is not self-pity; it is professional discipline. I am the man whose name the guards misremember, whose face the portraitists skip over, whose presence at state functions registers as furniture. For fifteen years I have cultivated invisibility the way farmers cultivate orchards—pruning, grafting, patient seasons of work. Nobody looks at me twice. Nobody remembers me at all. It is the single greatest achievement of my life, and I would thank you not to laugh at it.

Men like me do not sleepwalk toward sunken districts where dead gods are buried. Men like me go back to bed.

I went back to bed.

* * *

The second night, I woke with my boots on again, and this time I was holding the latch.

I sat down on the floor—I find sitting discourages walking—and had a long conversation with myself. The gist of it was: *August, you are thirty-four years old, you have survived three dynastic purges, two poisonings intended for others and one intended for you, and the entire reign of a man who executes people for wearing the wrong shade of red. You did not survive all that by following warm feelings downstairs.*

The counterargument, from some part of me that did not sound entirely like me, was: *and yet.*

And yet the charm had worked without me. And yet the burnt offering had hummed against my skin like a struck tuning fork, and my skin had *drunk* it. And yet the nightshade in the usurper's breakfast had carried something old in its sap, something that had looked at me through the poison the way you look through a keyhole. And yet, and yet, and yet—the world had developed a persistent knocking, and I had spent a month pretending I wasn't home.

I locked the latch, wedged a chair under the handle, put my boots outside the door where I could not possibly sleepwalk into them, and slept like a stone.

I woke on the roof.

This requires some explanation, because my building does not have roof access. There is a hatch, but it is painted shut, and I have lived beneath it for nine years without once opening it. Nevertheless, there I was at dawn, sitting on the ridge tiles with my legs dangling over a four-story drop, watching the light come up over the capital, my coat buttoned correctly this time—which somehow made it worse, as though whatever moved me was learning, improving, taking notes.

From up there I could see the Hollow district. Everyone can see the Hollow; that's the joke of it. It sits in the middle of the city like a missing tooth, a sunken quarter ringed by newer streets that curve away from it as if embarrassed. After the Godfall—two hundred years ago, give or take a decade depending on which chronicler you bribe—they had pulled down the temples, broken the shrines, and buried the whole quarter under rubble and ordinance, and then built the city right up to its edges and nowhere into it. No roads cross it. No lamps light it. Maps mark it in gray with a little notation: *unstable ground.* Children dare each other to the rim and come back pale and lie about how far they went.

I had tasted for the usurper's court for fifteen years and had never once thought about the Hollow. That, I realized now, sitting on my roof in the cold morning air, was not an accident either. It was like realizing you've spent your whole life never looking at your own left hand. Something had arranged my attention the way a gardener arranges shade.

"Right," I said aloud, to the city, to the god, to whoever was keeping the ledger. "You have my attention."

Somewhere below the streets, faint as a pulse felt through a wrist, something warmed.

* * *

I held out four more nights. I want that on the record. Four more nights of chairs under latches and boots in the hallway and one memorable evening of tying my own ankle to the bedframe with boat rope purchased from a fishmonger who asked no questions, because in this city asking questions is a hobby with a short life expectancy.

On the fifth night I stopped fighting, partly because I was exhausted, partly because I had done the arithmetic. Whatever this was, it had walked me past sleeping guards, up a ladder, and across loose tiles without waking me, without scratching me, without so much as bruising my dignity beyond repair. If it had meant me harm, I was already dead several times over. And if it didn't mean me harm—

Well. That was the terrifying option, wasn't it? Things that don't mean you harm usually want something else. Things that want something else call it by gentler names.

I left the boots by the door. I even banked the fire, because some habits are load-bearing. Then I lay down, closed my eyes, and said, into the dark of my own skull: "Fine. Show me. But I'm walking awake, do you understand? Awake, on my own feet, or not at all."

The warmth pulsed once—amused, I could have sworn it was amused—and the sleep came down over me like a curtain drawn by a considerate hand.

* * *

I woke at the rim of the Hollow, and this time I was fully myself, and fully terrified, and walking anyway.

Dawn had not come yet. The sky was that color between colors, and the gaslamps along the rim guttered in a wind I couldn't feel. Behind me, the city slept its crooked sleep. Ahead, the ground simply gave up—a shelf of broken masonry descending into darkness, stairs that had been grand once, wide enough for processions, now cracked and heaved like a frozen river. Moss grew on the steps. That was the first thing that stopped my breath. Moss, green and living and damp, in a district that had been dead stone for two centuries. It climbed the walls in patterns too regular to be accidental—not covering the carvings but tracing them, filling in lines, completing them. As I descended, I understood that the moss was drawing. The dead quarter had grown itself a new iconography out of living green, and every line of it pointed down.

The air changed as I went. Warmer. Not the wet warmth of cellars but a dry, bread-smelling warmth, hearth-warmth, and with it came a sound so low I felt it in my teeth before I heard it in my ears. A pulse. Slow. Enormous. Once every few heartbeats, and each time it passed through me, the fear in me quieted a little, the way a fretful child quiets against a warm side.

*I know that trick,* I thought sourly. *Don't think I don't.*

The stairs ended at what had been a temple square, though "square" gives the wrong impression of order. Statues lay toppled in attitudes of casual violence, faces chiseled smooth—no, not chiseled. *Rubbed* smooth, worn away, as if two hundred years of hands had touched them in the dark. At the square's center, where the altar must have stood, the rubble had fallen inward, collapsed into a shaft, and from the shaft came the light.

I say light. It was the color of coals seen through a banked grate, orange and deep and moving slowly, and it rose out of the shaft in a column that did not illuminate so much as *remember* illumination. My shadow fell behind me, long and wavering, and I noticed distantly that my shadow had too many joints, or perhaps the light simply couldn't decide about me, and then I stopped noticing anything except that the warmth had gathered itself, the way a speaker gathers breath.

*Vessel.*

It did not come through my ears. It came up through the soles of my boots, through the bones of my legs, and bloomed somewhere behind my sternum, and it was a voice the way a cathedral is a room—technically accurate, wildly insufficient. It was a hearth-fire voice. It was the voice of the specific warmth of being seven years old and falling asleep to the sound of your parents talking low in the next room, safe, accounted for, loved in the unremarkable way that children never notice until they're grown and freezing.

"No," I said. My voice cracked in the middle, which was undignified, and I pressed on regardless, because dignity was already a casualty and retreat was not on offer. "No, I don't know what you think I am, but I'm a food taster. I taste soup. I have opinions about gravy. Whatever vessel you're looking for, you've got the wrong—"

*August-of-the-ash-and-the-hearth. Last of the carrying line. Little door, little lock, kept so long you forgot you were a door at all.*

Every word landed in me like a hand laid flat on a wound. I sat down. There was a fallen statue nearby, faceless and shoulder-high, and I sat on it like a man resting on a wall, because my knees had resigned without notice and it seemed better to choose the moment.

"I'm not anyone's last anything," I said. "I'm not a line. I'm a man who sells charms to washerwomen and tastes for a tyrant and keeps his head down. Whoever built me—" the word *built* came out before I could stop it, and the truth of it hit me sideways, "—whoever *trained* me, they made a taster. That's all. That's the whole job description."

*Taster,* the warmth said, and there was something in it that a human voice would have had to strain to make gentle, and did not need to strain at all. *What is a taster, little door? One who takes into his body what was meant for another, and is not harmed. What do you imagine you have been practicing, all these years?*

The shaft breathed. The coals brightened. And I understood—I understood the way you understand things at three in the morning, totally and uselessly—that fifteen years of swallowing death for a tyrant had not been a career. It had been a regimen. An exercise. A muscle kept in condition. Every dose I'd taken and neutralized had been a rehearsal, and I had never once been told what I was rehearsing for.

"You could have just told me," I whispered. "Whoever kept me. Corvin. Someone. Fifteen years. A note. A single note."

*You were safer not knowing. Knowing has a weight. We hoped the world would stay quiet enough that you would never need to carry it.*

"And now?"

*Now the world is loud.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin, the laugh of a man discovering the floor is further away than advertised. "Loud. Right. Prayers getting answered all over the city, shrines growing moss, witch-finders sniffing around the archives—yes, I'd say loud. And what happens now? What is it you actually *want* from me?" I leaned forward on my cold stone seat, and here is the thing I am not proud of: even terrified, even betrayed, some traitorous part of me had gone quiet and attentive, the way you go quiet when you finally, after years, hear your own name called from a crowd. "Because everyone wants something. The usurper wants obedience. The cult wants a weapon. The free cities, if the rumors hold, want a miracle. So let's have it plainly. What do you want?"

The warmth drew itself together above the shaft. For a long moment there was no voice at all, only the pulse, only the patience, and I had time to notice that I was crying, quietly, without drama, the way you cry when you're too tired to do it properly.

Then:

*Will you carry me?*

That was all. No thunder, no commandment, no list of demands. The oldest question in the world, asked the way you'd ask it across a kitchen table. Will you carry me. As if the god of the Hollow, the buried one, the one they broke a quarter of a city to kill—as if it were the one asking permission.

"That's it?" I managed. "No armies? No thrones? No smiting?"

*A door is not asked to march,* the warmth said. *A door is asked to open. Or not. It is yours to choose, August-of-the-hearth. It was always meant to be yours to choose. That is what the covenant promised, before men made it into chains. I will not pour myself through a locked door. I have been poured through enough of those.*

I sat on my dead statue at the bottom of a murdered district, in the orange dark, with a god's patience wrapped around me like a blanket, and I thought about the washerwoman with her answered prayer, and the humming offering, and the nightshade that had looked at me through the poison, and fifteen years of small kindnesses sold cheap in a night market by a man who thought he was hiding.

And I thought about the fact that I had walked down here—had been walked down here—and had not, at any point, actually tried very hard to stop.

"What happens if I say yes?" I asked.

*Everything,* said the god, with terrible gentleness.

"And if I say no?"

The pause stretched. The coals dimmed, courteous, giving me room.

*Then I will wait,* it said at last. *I have waited two hundred years in the dark. I can wait longer. But little door—the world will not wait with me. Others are already reaching for what sleeps in you. Some of them are not polite.*

Above me, far above, at the rim of the Hollow, a lantern swung. Small, human, ordinary—and moving down the stairway I had just descended, picking its way over the heaved stones with the careful step of someone who maps things for a living.

I knew that walk. I had watched it across banquet halls for years, telling myself I wasn't watching.

The god's warmth curled around me, amused and rueful at once, and said, in the tone of a host noting a second guest at the door:

*Ah. The mapmaker. She has been listening too.*
