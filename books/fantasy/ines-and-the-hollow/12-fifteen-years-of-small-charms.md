## 12. Fifteen Years of Small Charms

I have never been a man who dreams in order. My sleeping mind is a market stall after a riot—everything overturned, nothing priced. But that night, after the burnt offering drank the heat of my hand and hummed like a struck bell, the dreams came in order, one after another, like a ledger being read aloud to me. As if something under the city had opened a book and said: *let us review the account.*

So let me review it. Let me tell you how a farm boy from the Vetchlands became a royal food-taster, because I have spent fifteen years telling it wrong, even to myself, and it seems I am done lying in my sleep.

* * *

I was nine years old the day the taster-master came to Hollowmere Farm, and I remember it the way you remember a tooth being pulled: everything before is warm and everything after is cold.

My father kept pigs and my mother kept bees, and I kept out of the way, which was my chief talent. There were five of us children. I was the middle one, which in a farm family is a kind of vanishing act—you are never the eldest, never the baby, never the one who can plough straightest or milk fastest. I was the boy who stood at the fence and watched the road.

The road brought a carriage. That alone was an event. The road mostly brought mud.

The man who stepped down was old in the way of furniture—smooth, brown, built to last, impossible to imagine young. Grey coat, grey eyes, a grey ring of hair like a halo that had given up. He introduced himself as Master Corben Hale, Taster-Prime to the Crown, and he asked my father for a cup of water because the road was dusty, and my father, being a man who could refuse a tax collector but not a guest, gave him one.

Then Master Hale asked for a second cup, and asked if I might fetch it, because he liked to see how children carried things.

I carried it carefully. I was a careful child; it was all I had. And while I stood there holding the cup like it was a sleeping bird, he dropped something into it. I watched him do it. A little grey pinch from a little grey vial, quick as a conjurer, and I thought: *this is a test of whether I tattle,* and I drank the water anyway, because I was nine and thirsty and had already decided that disappointing a lord's servant was more frightening than any pinch of grey.

He watched me drink. I watched him watch. Nothing happened, because nothing ever did.

"Interesting," he said, in the tone of a man who has found a coin in a fish. He asked my father if the boy had ever been sick. My father laughed and said August had once eaten a bowl of spoiled cream rather than admit he'd found it, and suffered nothing but shame. Hale asked about my grandmother. My mother said her mother had been a hedgewife from up-country, one of the old families, the ones that knew words for things. Hale wrote something in a small book. I remember thinking he wrote slowly, like a man copying out scripture.

Then he offered my father money. Not a little. A number with a shape to it, a number that made my father sit down on his own fence rail.

"What work?" my father asked.

"Tasting," said Hale. "For the Crown. It is an honourable post. The boy will be fed, clothed, schooled. He will want for nothing."

My father looked at me the way you look at a pig you have decided to sell—apologetically, and already counting. My mother looked at me the way you look at a bee you must smoke out of a wall—sorry, and determined, and certain it is for the best.

I looked at the road, and I thought: *the road goes somewhere.* I was nine. I would have gone with a rag-and-bone man if he'd offered a number like that.

I have thought about that day for fifteen years, and only now, in the dark, with a god humming under my skin, do I ask the question I should have asked on the fence rail: what kind of man travels to a pig farm in the Vetchlands on the rumor of a boy who doesn't sicken? What kind of man carries grey powder and a small book, and writes in it like scripture?

The answer, I now understand, is: a priest. A priest of a god with no temples left, doing what priests do when the churches burn. Looking for the faithful in the unlikeliest of flocks.

He was not buying a taster. He was collecting a relic.

* * *

The palace, when I came to it, was everything the Vetchlands was not: loud, gold, and full of people who looked through you. Master Hale installed me in the tasters' wing—a narrow house against the kitchen court where six of us slept in three beds and learned the trade.

You think food-tasting is eating. It is not. It is reading.

Hale taught us to read a dish the way scholars read a page. The sheen on a soup that says oil of bitter-almond. The sweetness in wine that is too sweet, sweetness laid over bitterness like paint over rot. The way a cook's hands move when he is afraid, and the way they move when he is *paid to be* afraid. We learned the poisons by name and symptom—nightshade that dilates the eyes, hemlock that climbs the legs first, arsenic that tastes of nothing at all, which is the rudest thing about it. We learned antidotes, emetics, the politics of the buttery. We learned which lords wanted which rivals dead and which cooks could be bought for less than a season's wages.

And we learned the rule, which Hale repeated every morning like a prayer, and which I have repeated to myself every day since, because it kept me alive:

*Be invisible. A taster who is noticed is a taster who is used. Be the spoon, not the meal.*

The other boys chafed at it. They wanted to be seen—wanted the courtiers to know their names, wanted the danger to be a story they could tell in taverns. Two of them got their wish. One was poisoned in his third year by a rival faction that wanted the king to distrust his own table. The other was hanged for selling information, which he had certainly done, though I always suspected the information was planted on him. After that I understood the rule was not advice. It was a wall, and outside the wall were wolves, and the wall was made of being dull.

I became very dull. I became a master of dullness. I learned to stand at the usurper's—then the old king's, but the habit outlived the king—left hand, one pace back, face arranged into the expression of a man thinking about porridge. I learned to taste and swallow and give the small nod, and to make even the nod boring. Courtiers forgot me between courses. Servants forgot me between days. I once stood in a corridor for an hour while two ministers plotted a coup three feet away, because I was furniture, and furniture does not count witnesses.

I was good at it. God help me, I was *good* at it, and being good at invisibility is its own particular loneliness. You can be surrounded by a thousand people and be no one, and after enough years of being no one, you start to wonder if there is anyone left underneath to be.

* * *

Here is what I did not know, in those first years: that the training had a second curriculum.

Hale taught me poisons by day. By night—though I did not know it was teaching—he taught me other things. He had a way of assigning chores that were lessons in disguise. He sent me to the shrine district to buy candles, and made me memorize which shrines were still warm and which were cold, and I thought it was an errand. He had me copy out inventories in his cramped hand, and the inventories were lists of herbs and salts and old words, and I thought it was bookkeeping. He taught me the taster's discipline of the body—how to breathe through a symptom, how to slow the heart, how to hold a mouthful without swallowing and let the tongue read it—and I thought it was craft.

It was liturgy. Every piece of it. The breath-work was the old breathing, the one the hedgewives knew. The lists were a priest's inventory of a faith in hiding. The errands were a survey of what survived the Godfall, taken by a man too old and too grey to be suspected, using a boy too dull to be noticed.

And the small book—the one he'd written in at my father's fence—he kept in a locked drawer, and once a month he opened the drawer and wrote in it, and once a year he read a page aloud to me as if it were a rule of the guild, and I memorized it without understanding:

*"The vessel is not the property of the Crown. The vessel is not the property of the guild. The vessel is a door, and doors are kept, not owned."*

I thought it was guild cant. Doors, vessels—tasters' slang for the body, for the mouth, for the office. I wrote it out in my copybook between the antidote tables. I can still write it now, in his cramped grey hand, without thinking. Fifteen years of small charms, and that was the largest one, and I carried it in my own handwriting like a splinter I mistook for a bone.

* * *

The old king died the way old kings die, badly, and the usurper came up from the provinces with an army and a smile, and the tasters' wing changed hands the way everything did—quietly, in the night, with some of us not surviving the inventory.

Hale did not run. I want that recorded somewhere, even if it's only in my own head: the old man did not run. He sat at his desk with his small book and waited for the witch-finders, and the night before they came, he called me to his room and gave me two things.

The first was a purse of coin. "For charms," he said. "You'll sell them. Little ones. Fertility, fever, safe roads. Nothing that works, nothing that draws the eye. Sell them to the desperate and the foolish, and let them think it's trickery, and let the trickery make you small. A man with a side trade is a man with a weakness, and men with weaknesses are beneath suspicion. Be corrupt in the smallest possible way, August. It is the best camouflage there is."

He had thought this out. He had thought out *everything*, years in advance, the way you'd plan a garden you'd never see bloom. I understand now that the charm-selling was not corruption at all. It was a congregation. He was sending his last priest out into the night market to gather the desperate and the foolish, because a god with no temples keeps its faithful where the desperation is, and the desperate come to charm-sellers the way the sick once came to shrines.

The second thing was the small book. He put it in my hands and closed my fingers over it, and his hands were cold, and he said:

"You were never bought, boy. I know what your father took, and I know what it looked like. But I chose you the way you choose a seed—not for what it is, but for what it's from. Your grandmother's line goes back to the old families. Your blood has been keeping a secret since before this city had walls, and the secret is you. One day something under this city will wake, and when it does, you will feel it in your mouth before you taste anything again. When that day comes, remember the rule. Not the first rule. The other one."

"What other rule?" I asked.

He smiled, and it was the first time I'd seen him do it, and it made him look young, which frightened me more than the witch-finders would have.

"Doors are kept," he said, "not owned. Not by crowns. Not by guilds. Not by gods. When they come to own you—*when*, not if—you remember who keeps the door."

Then he told me to go, and I went, because I was nineteen and a coward and the witch-finders' torches were already in the lower court. I went over the kitchen wall with a purse of coin and a book I couldn't read, and I did not look back, and I have been not looking back ever since, which is a skill, like the tasting, like the dullness, like the small nod.

They burned him in the shrine square. I know because I made myself read the notice, and I made myself stand at the edge of the square a week later, invisible, one pace back, face arranged into porridge, while the ash was still in the gutters. I told myself I was there to confirm he was dead. I was there because he was the only person in the capital who had ever known what I was, and I had not thought to ask him a single true question while he lived.

* * *

That is the account. That is the ledger. Fifteen years of small charms, and every one of them his design: the immunity logged in the guild rolls as *inherited, benign, of no interest*—in his handwriting, though I never saw him write it, though it is my handwriting, because he taught me my handwriting, because he was building a paper trail for a boy who would one day need to look like he had always been exactly what he seemed. The charm stall in the night market. The rule of invisibility. The dullness, the small nod, the porridge face. All of it a priest's work. All of it a cradle.

I have been angry since I woke from the dreaming, and I have been grieving, and I have been trying to work out which one is bigger, and I think the grief is bigger, because the anger assumes there was a life I was cheated of, and I no longer believe there was. You cannot be cheated of a choice you were never given. A nine-year-old on a fence rail does not choose. He is carried, the way a cup of water is carried, carefully, to someone who intends to drink.

And still. And *still*. There is a part of me—the part that stood at the fence and watched the road—that keeps insisting: but the pigs, and the bees, and the mud, and the five of us at the table, and the road going somewhere, and somewhere might have been anywhere, and anywhere might have been mine.

That boy was real. That life was possible. Hale took it with a grey pinch and a small book, and he did it gently, and he did it for a god, and he did it, I think, believing with his whole grey heart that a door kept is better than a door broken—and he never once asked the door.

I sat up in the dark with the burnt offering's hum still in my palms, and I did what he taught me. I breathed the old breathing. I slowed the heart. I held the grief in my mouth without swallowing and let the tongue read it.

It tasted of nightshade. It tasted of something older than poison.

And beneath the floor, beneath the cellar, beneath the foundations of the palace where a farm boy's purchase had been recorded in his own handwriting fifteen years ago—something turned over in its sleep, and the taste in my mouth said, as plainly as a voice: *soon.*
