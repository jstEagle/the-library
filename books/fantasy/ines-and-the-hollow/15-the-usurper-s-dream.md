## 15. The Usurper's Dream

There is a particular way a man's shoulders sit when he has decided to kill someone at dinner. I have spent fifteen years learning to read shoulders the way other men read weather. The usurper's shoulders, on the night of the Feast of the Concord, were set like a man settling in for a long and pleasant evening—which worried me more than hunched ones would have.

The wine was the problem. Not because it was poisoned. Because it was *interested* in me.

I stood behind his chair with the tasting cup, as I had stood behind that chair through three coronation anniversaries, two purges, and one memorable assassination attempt involving candied figs. My routine never varied: pour, taste, wait ten heartbeats, nod. Fifteen years of nods. I had nodded so often at this table that I sometimes suspected my neck had developed the motion independently of my brain, like a bird's.

I poured. I tasted.

And the god under the city leaned up through the soles of my feet like a dog hearing its name.

* * *

Let me explain what tasting wine is like when you are me—or what it used to be like, before the night market, before the humming offering, before the dreams of hearth fire and hollow ground. It used to be a professional exercise. Bitterness here, suggesting arsenic. Almond, suggesting cyanide. A sweetness too far forward, suggesting something exotic and expensive, which in the capital's kitchens usually meant expensive poison. I catalogued and I swallowed and I lived, and that was the whole of the art.

This wine tasted of black cherries and iron and, beneath both, of something I can only describe as *attention*. The way a room feels when you realize someone has been watching you for longer than you've been aware of them.

I swallowed anyway. That's the job. And the swallow went down wrong—not painfully, not dangerously, but the way a key goes into a lock it wasn't cut for, grinding, almost fitting.

The hall tilted.

Not the hall. Me. The hall stayed exactly where chandeliers and marble put it. But something in me turned over, and suddenly I was seeing the banquet from underneath—from below the floor, from below the foundations, from that warm dark place where moss grows on dead stone and a heartbeat keeps time slower than any clock made by hands.

And threaded through that dark, bright as a wire pulled taut, ran the usurper.

That is the only way I can say it. Every person in a room makes some small mark on the world—a warmth, a weight, a smudge. Most people are candle smoke. Ines, when she'd brushed past me at the door that evening, had been ink and paper and rain. But the man whose wine I held in my mouth was a *wire*, humming with purpose, running from his chair down through the palace stone and out into the city, and where the wire touched the god's sleeping warmth, the warmth flinched.

He knows. That was my first coherent thought, arriving with the clarity of cold water. He knows something is down there, and he has been *measuring* it.

"Villalobos."

I surfaced. The hall reassembled itself around me—candles, courtiers, the smell of roast quail and beeswax. The usurper had turned in his chair and was looking up at me, and his eyes were pale gray, the color of a sky deciding whether to snow.

"You looked elsewhere," he said. "For a moment. You tasted my wine and your eyes went somewhere else entirely."

Fifteen years of practice came to my rescue wearing its usual plain clothes. "A flaw in the vintage, Your Radiance. A note of iron. Some cellars keep their barrels near the forge." I bowed the exact degree I always bowed. "The wine is clean. Excellent, even. The iron is honest."

"Honest iron," he repeated, and smiled, and the smile did not go anywhere near his eyes. "You have a poet's tongue for a man who swallows for a living."

"I have a survivor's tongue, Your Radiance. Poetry is what happens to it after enough near-misses."

Someone laughed—the court always laughs when I'm permitted wit; it's part of my function, the tame taster, the human sieve—and the moment passed, and the usurper turned back to his plate, and I stepped back into shadow with my heart going like a drummer boy's.

But he had seen. For half a second, he had seen me look at him from underground, and some animal part of him had felt the touch of it, the way you feel a draft from a door you thought was shut.

* * *

I did not sleep that night, which was perhaps the point of whatever had happened at the table, or perhaps simply the price. I sat in my narrow room above the charm shop—I still kept the shop, though lately I sold less and listened more—and I let the god's attention settle over me like a blanket that weighed four hundred years.

It wanted to show me something. I understood that much without words. Since the night the washerwoman's prayer had answered itself, since the burnt offering had drunk heat through my skin, the thing beneath the Hollow had been teaching me its vocabulary one syllable at a time. Warmth meant yes. Cold meant no. The heartbeat under the city sped up when I walked certain streets, and once—it had frightened me badly—it had kept pace with mine until I couldn't tell which pulse was whose.

So I lay down, and I stopped resisting, and I fell the way you fall in dreams: all at once and gently, through floor and foundation and grave-dirt, into the warm dark.

The god showed me the usurper's dream.

I don't know how it knew. Perhaps the wire runs both ways; perhaps a man who measures a god gets measured back. Perhaps attention, once given, cannot be fully taken home again. All I know is that I found myself standing in a dream that was not mine, in a body that was not mine, looking out through pale gray eyes at an empire.

It was built of corpses.

Not the metaphorical kind. In the dream, the land itself was paved with them—vast, serene, mummified shapes lying in rows across the whole horizon, each one the size of a hill, each one unmistakably a god. I recognized none of them by name and all of them by feel: that drowsy vastness, that sense of something enormous folded up small to fit inside the world. They lay on their sides with their knees drawn up like sleeping children, and roads ran along their spines, and cities glowed in the hollows of their ribs, and fields ripened in the shadows of their hands.

The usurper's dream-self walked among them the way a landlord walks his property. And the dream knew things the waking man might not have said aloud even to himself. I felt them arrive in his mind with the comfortable weight of long-held convictions:

*They are not dead. Dead things do not need guarding.*

*They are sleeping, and sleep can be harvested.*

*A dynasty fed on a god does not hunger for a thousand years.*

And beneath those, older, worn smooth as a stair-stone from repetition:

*I saw one wake when I was a boy. No one believed me. I will never be disbelieved again.*

I woke with tears on my face and no memory of weeping, which is a disagreeable way to start a morning. Dawn was graying the window. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past the shop, and a woman was singing off-key about lost love, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary with tremendous discipline, as if it knew what was coming and was determined to enjoy breakfast first.

Here is what I had learned, laid out neat as a taster's ledger:

One. The usurper knows the old gods exist, has known since childhood, and has built his entire reign on the assumption that they stay asleep.

Two. He does not want them dead. This was the part that turned my stomach over. Death he could arrange—he had anti-divine weapons rusting in vaults from the Godfall, everyone whispered about them and no one confirmed anything. What he wanted was worse. He wanted them *sleeping and available*. An empire built on the corpses of sleeping gods, the dream said, but corpses imply an ending, and endings are wasteful. He wanted milk, not meat. A dynasty fed forever.

Three. Something was waking, and he intended to find out what, and bottle it.

And four—the item I wrote last, in the smallest hand, because writing it large would have made it real:

Four. He had watched me look at him from underground, and he had not ordered me arrested, because a food-taster who dreams god-dreams is not a threat. He is an *instrument*. Someone had tried to wake me with poisoned breakfast once already; I had traced it to the kitchen cult and burned them out quietly, or thought I had. Now I wondered if the cult had been the usurper's all along, and the nightshade a test, and my survival of it a data point in someone's ledger.

I had spent fifteen years perfecting invisibility. It had never once occurred to me that invisible things get *collected*.

* * *

Midmorning brought a summons.

Not for me—for the wine. A page arrived at the shop with a sealed chit requesting the royal taster attend the private cellar audit, as His Radiance wished to discuss "the matter of the iron note." The page was maybe twelve, bored, chewing a honey stick. I tipped him a copper and read the chit three times.

Cellar audits were real things. I had attended a dozen. You walk the racks, you sniff corks, a steward argues with a vintner, everyone performs diligence. It was also, unmistakably, a summons dressed as errandry, which meant the usurper preferred his hooks baited with mundane things. So did I. Respect between professionals.

The private cellars sit below the west wing, deeper than the kitchens, carved into the same bedrock that somewhere far east of there becomes the Hollow. I noticed that for the first time that day—that the palace and the Hollow share a skeleton, that the wine racks and the sleeping god are neighbors separated by forty feet of stone and five hundred years of pretending otherwise. I wondered if the usurper had chosen the west wing deliberately. I suspected everything now. Suspicion was becoming a full-time occupation, and I was already employed.

He was there alone. No guards in the cellar itself—I heard them at the stairhead—and he stood with a bottle in one hand, reading its label with the concentration of a scholar. He did not look up when my boots found the bottom step.

"Do you know what this vintage is?" he asked.

"No, Your Radiance."

"It is pressed from vines grown on the shoulder of the Sleeper of Vell." He said it the way another man might say *the north slope*, flat, proprietary. "The old name for the hill. Before we renamed it after a battle nobody won. The roots go down sixty feet. The wine has always had that note of iron. The vintners think it's the soil." He finally looked at me, and his pale eyes were pleasant and bottomless. "It isn't the soil."

My mouth was dry in a way no wine could fix. "Your Radiance is fond of history."

"I am fond of *assets*." He set the bottle down with a click. "Tell me, Villalobos. Fifteen years at my table. You have tasted every poison in the civilized world and several uncivilized ones. You have never once been ill. Do you know how remarkable that is? Statistically, you should be dead eleven times over. I have had the numbers done."

"I've been lucky," I said, and both of us heard the lie land between us like a dropped glass.

"Lucky." He smiled his nowhere smile. "When you tasted my wine last night, your eyes rolled back and your pupils went wide as coins, and for one moment I would swear on my crown that you were listening to something *below* us." He tapped the flagstone with one shoe. Not idly. Precisely. "So here is my question, taster, and consider it carefully, because your answer determines whether you continue to be paid or begin to be studied: what did you hear?"

Every instinct I owned screamed at me to lie beautifully. I am very good at lying beautifully; it is the second skill they teach a taster, right after swallowing. But lying to this man in this cellar, standing on stone above a god he intended to farm—I felt the wire of him humming through the floor, felt the god beneath flinch away from his attention like a child from a raised hand—and I understood that he had asked the question not because he didn't know the answer, but because he wanted to see what I would do with it. My answer was a test of a different kind. He was auditing *me*.

"The iron note," I said slowly, "isn't in the wine."

"No?"

"It's in the drinker. Some wines carry things up from deep ground. Old things. Most people taste nothing. I taste—" I spread my hands, the gesture of a craftsman describing grain. "Echoes. Resonance. Call it a taster's curse. It's why I'm good at my work. Poisons shout. Everything else whispers, and I hear the whispering."

Silence. The cellar breathed its cool cellar breath around us, and far below—so far below that no instrument of the usurper's could ever measure it, but not nearly far enough for my comfort—a heartbeat slowed, listening.

"Echoes," the usurper repeated. He picked the bottle back up and poured two glasses, which he had never done in fifteen years, and handed me one, which I drank, because refusing a king's wine is its own confession. It tasted of cherries and iron and, faintly, of hillsides where enormous things slept with their knees drawn up.

"There are stories," he said conversationally, "of a quarter of this city that sank. Of shrines that aren't as ruined as my surveyors report. Of prayers getting answered lately by parties unknown." He sipped. "Stories interest me the way the iron note interests you—as echoes. And I have recently commissioned a thorough review of the cartographic archives. Maps tell truths that people forget to lie about." He watched me over the rim of his glass, and I kept my face doing nothing at all, and thanked every indifferent power that I did not yet know whether Ines Corvane's beautiful dangerous originals were still sitting in her archive drawers, waiting for witch-finder hands.

"So if a man wished to understand the echo under his city," the usurper went on, "he would want, say, a taster with unusual hearing. A man who notices things. Who moves unnoticed. Who has, these past weeks, been selling charms in the night market that—" he paused delicately— "*work.*"

The floor did not move, but I moved slightly relative to it.

"You keep excellent records of your little commerce, Villalobos. Or rather, someone keeps them for you. Did you imagine a man who tastes for his supper wouldn't taste his own city? Every prayer answered in the slums traces back to a charm with your maker's mark. Every miracle in the Warrens has your fingerprints, if one knows how to look." He finished his wine and set the glass down. "I am not angry. Anger is for surprises, and you are not a surprise. You are a *symptom*. And symptoms, properly read, indicate the disease."

"And what disease does Your Radiance diagnose?"

His smile widened by a degree, and for just a moment the pleasantness dropped away and I saw the boy who had once seen a god wake and been disbelieved, grown into a man who would never be disbelieved again because disbelief, in his empire, had become fatal.

"Recovery," he said. "The patient is waking up. And I intend to be holding the leash when it opens its eyes."

He dismissed me with a flick of two fingers, back to his labels, and I climbed the stairs on legs that belonged to someone calmer, past the guards who did not look at me, out into the noon light where the city roared and hawked and baked its bread, all of it unaware, all of it singing off-key about lost love.

I walked home the long way, through streets I knew, and beneath my boots—faint, patient, unmistakable—the heartbeat quickened to match my own.

It had heard him too. And it was not afraid.

That frightened me more than anything the usurper had said. Because I had felt the god's fear before, flinching from the wire of him. But walking home, listening to that steady deepening pulse, I realized the truth the dream had been trying to show me all along:

The god wasn't afraid of the leash.

It was waiting to see who would pick up the other end.

And somewhere in the archive above the map drawers, a woman with ink-stained fingers was copying everything.
