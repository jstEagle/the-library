## 16. A Covenant of Two

**August**

There is a particular kind of silence that lives underground, and I had spent thirty years learning to ignore it—the silence of cellars, of crypts, of the tasters' guild vault where they kept the reference poisons in stoppered jars like a library of small murders. I thought I knew every flavor of quiet the world could serve.

I was wrong. The Hollow's silence had a pulse.

"You're doing it again," Ines said.

"Doing what?"

"Sitting very still and pretending you're not listening to something." She didn't look up from her drafting board—a folding table she'd carried down three collapsed stairways on her back, because apparently cartographers are a breed of mule with better handwriting. "Your eyes go somewhere else. Left and slightly down. You've done it four times since we lit the lamps."

"I'm listening to you complain about my posture."

"I don't complain about your posture. I complain about your evasions. Your posture is actually quite good for a man who spent fifteen years hunched over other people's breakfasts."

We had been down here nine days. Nine days of her mapping and me listening, sleeping in shifts in the dry corner of what had once been a shrine to—nobody was sure anymore; the god's name had been scrubbed off the lintel so thoroughly that even the stone seemed embarrassed about it. Nine days of an arrangement neither of us had ever put into words, because words have a way of making things real, and real things can be taken away by witch-finders.

The arrangement was this: she drew, I listened, and at some point each night one of us said something true, and the other pretended it hadn't happened.

"The footpath network is older than the Godfall," Ines said now, tapping her stylus against the board. "That's what I keep coming back to. The paths weren't cut by pilgrims after the shrines were buried—they were cut *before*, as service roads. The city didn't build over the Hollow. It built *around* it, and then lied about why."

"And the heartbeat?"

She set the stylus down. "Say that again."

"The heartbeat. Under the floor. You said you heard it when you first came down here, chapter seven—" I caught myself. "When you first came down here, before you found me. You described it as a drum under the stone."

"I described it as *acoustics*."

"You described it as acoustics because you're a woman who files things she isn't ready to feel. I've read your marginalia."

"My marginalia are *technical*."

"They're love letters to your own skepticism." I got up and crossed to where she sat, because standing still had become difficult lately—the god under the floor pulled at me like a tide pulls at driftwood, gently, without any interest in my opinion about it. "Put your hand on the ground."

"August."

"Ines. Put your hand on the ground or I'll tell you what your pulse does when I stand this close, and then one of us will be embarrassed, and I'd rather it were the floor."

She gave me a look that would have curdled milk at forty paces, and then—because she was Ines, and curiosity always beat dignity in her internal elections—she knelt and pressed her palm flat to the mossy flagstone.

I watched her face do the thing faces do when the impossible files itself neatly under *confirmed*. Her lips parted. Her brow furrowed, then smoothed, then furrowed again, as if two clerks inside her head were fighting over the same ledger.

"That's not acoustics," she said quietly.

"No."

"That's a *rhythm*. That's—" She looked up at me, kneeling there in the lamplight with her hand on the floor of a murdered god's house, and for once she didn't reach for a technical term. "August. What did they do to you? What did they do that makes you able to hear this and me only able to feel it?"

And there it was: the true thing for tonight, arriving early, before the lamp oil was even low.

I crouched beside her. "My family bred for it," I said. "Not trained. Bred. Like dogs, or horses, or—like something you want to be able to carry a load without complaining. There's a word for what I am. The old taster-master used it once, when he was drunk, and then never again. *Vessel.*"

"Vessel," she repeated. Not a question. A measurement. She was weighing the word against everything she'd mapped, all those converging footpaths, all those 'ruined' shrines that weren't ruined, and I could see the sum forming behind her eyes.

"It means I was made to hold something," I said. "And the something is underneath us, and it's waking up, and it knows my name."

* * *

**Ines**

Here is what nobody tells you about conspiracy: it is mostly waiting. Waiting for lamps to burn down. Waiting for footsteps overhead that might be Doss's men. Waiting for the person beside you to decide whether today is a day they tell the truth.

I had kept secrets my whole life—fake maps in the archive, real maps under my floorboards, a whole second geography of caution taught to me by someone whose name I still couldn't remember, which was its own kind of haunting. But keeping secrets alone is just loneliness with better lighting. Keeping them with August was—

Well. It was the first time in my adult life I'd spoken aloud, daily, to another human being, and not felt like I was performing.

He listened. That was the thing. He listened the way he tasted, I think—with his whole attention, cataloguing, but also, somehow, *accepting*. When I told him about the fake maps I'd fed Serel Doss years ago, maps I'd aged with tea and cracked with a heated blade, he didn't ask why I'd prepared fakes for a witch-finder who hadn't yet come asking. He just nodded and said, "Someone taught you to survive before you knew what you'd need to survive. Same as me." And then he'd gone back to listening to the floor, as if I hadn't just handed him the strangest piece of myself I owned.

Tonight, after the vessel confession, we climbed out through the smuggler's gap to breathe air that hadn't been underground since the Godfall. We sat on the lip of the Hollow district, legs dangling over the sunken streets, watching the capital's lamps above and the dark below.

"Ask me," he said.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not-asking for nine days. You have a question. It's been sitting behind your eyes since I said the word *vessel*, and you keep reorganizing it into smaller questions, which is what you do instead of feeling things."

"I do not—"

"You alphabetized my charm inventory yesterday. My *inventory*, Ines. Of illegal charms."

"It was disordered," I said, with dignity. Then, because he was right and we both knew it: "Do you want it? The thing underneath. Do you want to be what it wants you to be?"

He was quiet long enough that I counted his breaths. Four. Five. Six.

"When I was a boy," he said, "on the farm, I ate a green plum off a tree everyone knew was poisonous. I was six. I wanted to see if the story was true. My mother found me sitting under the tree, fine, and she didn't scream. She went very still, the way you go still, and then she took me inside and burned the tree and never spoke of it. I think—I think she knew what I was. I think she'd known since I was born, and she spent my whole childhood hoping nobody else would find out." He turned a pebble over in his fingers. "So no. I didn't want it. I wanted plums that stayed dangerous and a mother who stayed ordinary. But wanting doesn't come into it. The covenant wasn't offered to me. It was installed."

"*Installed.*"

"Like a pipe. Like a door hinge." He smiled, and it was the tired smile, the one that made my chest do inconvenient things. "You asked. Now you know. Does the map change?"

I looked down at the Hollow, at the faint warmth rising from the streets where moss grew on dead stone, and I thought about the sigil overlaid across my shrine maps—the same mark scarred into his palm—and I thought about how, ten days ago, my greatest ambition had been a complete survey of the northern aqueducts.

"The map changes," I said. "The map has been changing since the night market. I just haven't redrawn the legend yet."

"What's the new legend say?"

I should have said something wry. Something deflecting. Instead I heard myself say, "It says: here be the man I—" and stopped, appalled, and August had the grace to look at the horizon and let me not finish the sentence, which was, I was beginning to understand, its own form of tenderness.

* * *

**August**

On the twelfth day, she kissed me, and it was entirely unsentimental, which is to say it was the most romantic thing that had ever happened to me.

We were arguing. We argued often and well—it was how we flirted, though neither of us would sign a document to that effect. The subject was whether the shrine beneath the old granary was active or dormant. She maintained dormant, based on footpath erosion patterns. I maintained active, based on the fact that when I stood on it, the god hummed against my ribs like a struck bell.

"Erosion patterns," I said, "can be wrong."

"So can your ribs."

"My ribs are primary evidence. You're working from secondary sources."

"I am a *cartographer*, August. Everything is secondary sources. That's the entire discipline. You walk around claiming direct revelation like some—some village ecstatic, and meanwhile I'm the one establishing survey control points while you make noises about bells."

"Make noises about bells," I repeated. "You mean like this?" And I took her hand and pressed it flat against my sternum, over the sigil-scar, where the god's attention gathered like heat in a hearthstone.

She froze. Not from fear—from recognition. Because through my chest, faint but unmistakable, she felt it too: the rhythm, the patient enormous patience of the thing below, beating in time with something in her as well. Fainter in her. A distant cousin of the rhythm. But there.

"You feel it," I said. "You've always felt it. That's why you mapped the footpaths instead of filing them. That's why you copied everything. You couldn't help it. It calls to mapmakers, maybe. People who can't rest until the shape of a thing is written down."

"That is not—" she started, and then her argument ran out of road, and she looked at my hand over hers, and then at me, and then she kissed me with the brisk efficiency of a woman crossing an item off a list she'd been keeping far longer than either of us admitted.

Afterward she said, against my collarbone, "This compromises our operational security."

"Tremendously."

"Doss would use it against us both."

"He'd have to catch us first."

"He will, eventually. He's methodical." She pulled back and looked at me with that clear gray gaze that had unnerved half the palace and undone me entirely. "August. If it comes to it—if he offers you a trade, my safety for your surrender—"

"Don't."

"—you take it," she finished. "You take it, and you don't look back, and you remember that I can lie to witch-finders in my sleep. I've had practice."

"And if he offers me *your* surrender for mine?"

"Then we're both idiots," she said, "and we should establish now which of us is worse at losing."

"We could settle it empirically," I suggested. "Arm wrestle."

"I grew up hauling survey equipment across three mountain ranges."

"I grew up drinking poison for a living. My arm has opinions."

She laughed—actually laughed, head tipped back, in the mouth of a buried god's shrine, and the sound went down through the mossy stone like a coin dropped into a well, and I swear the rhythm beneath us stuttered, just slightly, the way a sleeper stirs at a pleasant sound.

Later, much later, lying in the dry corner with the lamp burned to a blue bead, I listened to the god the way I'd learned to listen—not with dread now, or not only. It didn't speak in words. It spoke in warmth, in pressure, in the sense of something vast turning over in its sleep and finding, for the first time in centuries, that the dream was not entirely lonely.

*Carry me home,* it said, the way it always said, the way a hearth says *come sit down.*

"Not yet," I murmured. "But soon. And on my terms."

The warmth pulsed once. Agreement, or patience, or both.

Above us, faint through the smuggler's gap, came the sound I'd been dreading for days without letting myself name it: hoofbeats. Many. Moving not past the Hollow district but *toward* it, unhurried, organized, the cadence of men who already knew where they were going.

Ines was awake instantly. Our eyes met in the dark.

"That's not a patrol," she whispered. "Patrols don't ride in formation at midnight."

I reached for my boots. Somewhere above, the first torchlight bloomed against the underside of the clouds, orange and hungry, and the god beneath us woke a little more—and this time, it was afraid.
