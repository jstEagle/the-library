## 17. The First Shrine Falls

The thing about being a traitor, I have learned, is that it is mostly paperwork.

I had imagined torchlight and whispered passwords, daggers in the dark, the dramatic business of betrayal that fills ballads. What I had instead was three weeks of walking past Serel Doss's desk each morning with my heart performing a small controlled detonation behind my ribs, waiting for the day he laid my forged maps beside the real ones and said, *Corvane, we should talk.* Every morning he didn't. He nodded at me. He asked about ink supplies. Once he complimented my lettering on a survey of the eastern aqueducts, which was the single most frightening compliment of my life.

That is the arithmetic of treachery: you do not pay all at once. You pay in installments of dread.

And then came the morning he said, "Bring your field kit, Corvane. We're going out."

"Out" meant outside the palace walls, which witch-finders did not do for cartographic reasons. Witch-finders went out the way surgeons went in: with purpose and fire.

* * *

We rode east-southeast through the morning fog, past the tanneries, past the last honest farms, into the country where the capital's refuse went to be forgotten. Doss rode at the front and did not talk. His men — six of them, in plain grey riding leathers, no insignia, because the usurper liked his atrocities deniable — talked among themselves in the low bored voices of men who had done this before and found it adequate.

I catalogued them the way I catalogue everything, because cataloguing is how I keep from screaming. The tall one with the burn-scarred jaw: left-handed, favoring his right stirrup, an old hip injury. The young one who laughed too easily: new, still excited, the dangerous kind. The one they called Marn: quiet, competent, and the only one who looked at the passing villages with anything like apology in his face.

"You'll want to mark this on your map," Doss said to me, without turning around. "For the archive."

"Mark what, exactly?"

"Whatever's left."

I did not ask what would be left of what. Asking questions is a skill, and the first rule of the skill is knowing when not to use it.

We stopped at noon in a fold of hills where a shepherd's track wandered off the road toward a stand of black poplars. There was nothing marked there on any map I had ever drawn or inherited. Which was, I understood with a cold settling in my stomach, precisely the point. Unmarked things were my department. Doss had requisitioned my shrine maps weeks ago — the fakes, though he didn't know it — and if he was standing in front of an unmarked track looking like a man consulting an invisible document, then somewhere in his possession was information that had never passed through my hands.

Someone else was feeding him. Someone else knew where the shrines were.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited and stayed like a guest with muddy boots — someone else had known all along, and my careful forgeries had been irrelevant from the start. You can forge a map. You cannot forge a man who already knows the way.

"Corvane." Doss swung down from his horse. "You'll stay with the horses until I call for you. Then you'll come and draw what you see. Accurately." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes that I could not name and have spent since trying to. "It matters that it's accurate. Records are how we remember. And how we're remembered."

Then he walked up the shepherd's track with five men and a lantern-oil cart, and I stood among the horses and began, quietly, to understand what kind of day this was going to be.

* * *

The shrine sat in a hollow between the poplars, and it was not ruined.

That was my first thought, absurdly professional: *this is not on my maps because it is not ruinous, it is living, and I did not know living shrines existed anymore.* It was a low stone building older than the Godfall, older than the usurper's grandfather's grandfather, its walls furred with moss that was green — green, in the smoke-country, in the ash-season, green as a lie — and around its door someone had planted rosemary, and the rosemary was in flower.

And there were families inside it. I could hear them. A child laughing. A woman singing something tuneless and domestic, the kind of song you sing while kneading bread. The murmur of perhaps twenty people who had come to a hidden place in the hills because prayers were being answered again, because the old god under the city had begun to wake, because hope is a thing that makes people walk toward danger with flowers in their hands.

Doss's men spread out around the hollow with the unhurried efficiency of professionals. They barred the door. They stacked brush against the walls. The tall one with the burned jaw poured lamp oil along the foundation stones, whistling.

I stood by the horses, and I did the math.

It is important that you understand I did the math. That I stood there, twenty paces from a barred door, and calculated. Six men, armed. One of me, armed with a surveyor's knife and a strong right arm and absolutely no plan beyond a plan-shaped hole where a plan should be. If I shouted, they would kill me, and then they would burn the shrine anyway, and the only difference in the world would be one more body and one less witness. If I ran for the capital, I would arrive too late to matter and would spend whatever remained of my usefulness running. If I fought — I am a cartographer. I fight with a pen. My pen was in my saddlebag, and it weighed nothing at all.

There is a version of me, I think, in some other story, who charged down that hill and died beautifully and changed nothing. I have thought about her often. She is very brave and very dead and the shrine burns either way, and afterward nobody keeps the names.

I kept the names. That was the only weapon within reach. So I picked it up.

"Corvane!" Doss called, without looking back at me. "Come and see. You'll want the interior measurements."

He wanted a witness. That was the part I understood later, turning it over like a stone with something underneath it: he had brought me specifically, the archivist, the map-keeper, because he wanted what happened written down. Not to boast. To *record*. Serel Doss believed — I believe he genuinely believed — that what he did was necessary, and necessary things deserved accurate documentation, and accurate documentation required someone whose whole soul was accuracy.

He had chosen me as his conscience's clerk. I have hated many people in my life, but I have never hated anyone with the particular precision I reserved for that realization.

I walked down into the hollow, because refusing would have been louder than any scream, and loudness gets people killed, and I told myself — I told myself, and I want you to know how it tasted, like swallowing a nail — I told myself that a witness inside the circle might find some crack, some moment, some chance.

The door was barred from outside. Through the gaps in the old shutters I could see light moving, shadows of people. The woman had stopped singing. The child had stopped laughing. They knew. Of course they knew; you hear boots and oil-carts and you know. There was no pounding on the door, no screaming. Just a low sound, many voices together, that took me a moment to identify because I had never heard it except in the oldest texts I'd ever copied.

They were praying. Twenty-odd people, barred in their green-walled shrine, praying to a god asleep under a city miles away, in the last minutes they had.

And the terrible thing — the thing I will carry until they carry me — is that I felt the prayer land. Felt it the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm. Something vast and warm and drowsy turned over beneath the world, beneath *me*, and for one heartbeat every hair on my arms stood up and the moss on the shrine walls glowed faintly, gently, like embers remembering fire, and I thought: *it hears. It's waking. It's coming.*

It was not fast enough. Nothing was going to be fast enough.

"Light it," Doss said.

Marn, the quiet one, hesitated with the taper. Just a moment. Just long enough for me to memorize his face, because I am what I am, and faces are a kind of map.

Then he lit it, and the oil caught, and the green moss burned blue-green and sweet, like rosemary, like the garden at my mother's house, and the low praying inside became something else, and I stood at my appointed distance with my field kit in my hands and I did not look away. That much I decided early and kept: I would not look away. Someone owed these people a witness who watched, and I had volunteered by surviving, so I watched.

I listened, too. Names. People call out names when they die — their children's, their gods', their own. The wind was wrong for hearing most of it, but the wind gave me pieces, and pieces are where a map begins. A woman shouting *Toma, Toma, hold my hand.* An old man saying *Marisol* over and over like a rosary. A child's voice, high and furious rather than frightened, screaming a word I couldn't catch and have spent nights reconstructing from phonetics and context, because if I write down even one true name of one true dead person then the record has a pulse in it, and records with pulses are harder to burn than shrines.

The roof went. The walls held longer than they should have — old stone, god-touched stone, and I swear to you the moss fought, I swear the green held back the flame for full minutes that no ordinary ivy could have — and through it all Doss stood with his hat in his hands, bareheaded, like a man at a funeral, and watched, and his face was grief-stricken and resolute and utterly, immovably certain, and that combination frightened me more than cruelty would have. Cruelty can be killed. Certainty has to be outlived.

"How long," I heard myself say. My voice came out level. Cartographer's voice. Measuring voice. "How long have you known where the living shrines are? Because it wasn't my maps. My maps show ruins."

Doss looked at me across the heat, and for a moment I thought he would answer, and I braced for everything — arrest, revelation, the end of Ines Corvane in a poplar hollow.

"There are more," was all he said. "You'll draw this one accurately. We'll visit the others in order."

*In order.* He had a list. Somewhere in that grey man's grey coat was a list of living shrines, and mine — my forged ones, my careful coward's forgeries — had never been the source. Which meant the source was someone who knew the truth, and the truth had been sold, or leaked, or divined, and every hour I spent protecting August Villalobos by lying small, someone else was lying large, and people were burning for the difference.

* * *

We rode back in the late afternoon with the smell of smoke in our clothes, and the men were subdued in the way men are after this particular work — not guilty, exactly, or not yet, guilt being a crop with a long growing season — and Doss did not speak until the capital's walls rose out of the dusk.

"You kept count," he said suddenly. I must have looked blank. "In the hollow. Your lips were moving. You keep count of everything, Corvane. It's why you're good at your work." He reined in slightly, so that we rode abreast, and looked at me with those patient, terrible eyes. "Twenty-three. That's my count. Tell me yours matches."

"Twenty-three," I said, because it did, because of course it did, because I had counted them the way I count contour lines, and I hated him, and I hated myself, and I hated the strange awful respect that had grown between us like the moss on those walls, green in the ash-season, alive where nothing should live.

"Good," said Serel Doss. "Accurate records matter. When this is over — when the god is put down and the city is safe — someone will need to explain to the survivors why it was necessary. I intend that the explanation be exact." He nudged his horse forward. "You may not believe me now. Write it all down anyway. History will want a precise hand."

And I rode through the gates of the usurper's capital with smoke in my hair and twenty-three names half-caught in my memory, and I made a decision with the calm finality of a woman drawing a border she can never redraw.

I went home. I lit one lamp. I took out the good paper, the archival vellum, the sheet I had been saving for the master map of my life's work, and I wrote at the top, in my finest lettering, the heading I had once imagined would read *Consolidated Survey of the Hollow Districts.*

Instead I wrote: *The Record of the Burned.*

And beneath it, in a column, I entered the names I had caught — Toma, Marisol, the furious child whose name I would reconstruct syllable by syllable if it took me a year — and left ruled blank lines below them, dozens of them, a hundred, because I had seen Doss's face when he said *in order*, and I knew with cartographer's certainty that the list would grow.

Let the witch-finders keep their lists of shrines to destroy. I would keep the other ledger. Let history have both, and let it judge which of us was keeping accounts.

I worked until the candle drowned, and when I finally slept I dreamed of green moss glowing in the dark like a heartbeat, and woke with my hand pressed flat against the floor as if I could feel, far below, through stone and root and the bones of the buried quarter, something vast turning over in its sleep — grieving, maybe, or counting.

Counting, I decided. Like me.

In the morning there was a note slipped under my door in handwriting I almost recognized — cramped, hurried, smelling faintly of the night market — and one line only:

*They burned the poplar shrine. I felt it from across the city. We need to talk before Doss's list reaches you. — A.V.*

I sat down on the floor with the note in my hands and laughed until I cried, because of course he had felt it. Of course the poison-proof little charm-seller had felt twenty-three people die from across the city, because he was a vessel, because he was a door, because everyone I loved or needed was a locked room and I was collecting keys whether I wanted them or not.

I got up. I washed my face. I put the note in the ledger, between the names and the blank lines, where evidence belongs.

Then I went to find August Villalobos, and tell him exactly how many names I was carrying, and exactly what I intended to do about it.
