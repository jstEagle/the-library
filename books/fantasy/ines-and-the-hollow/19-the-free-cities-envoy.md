## 19. The Free Cities' Envoy

The envoy arrived the way all dangerous things arrive in this city: quietly, with paperwork.

I should have known from the paperwork. A requisition slip crossed my desk on a Tuesday — I remember because Tuesdays are when the palace surveyors bring me their errors to correct, and the slip was wedged between two maps of the eastern aqueducts that had been drawn by a man who clearly believed rivers were more suggestion than fact. The slip requested access to "historical hydrological surveys of the Hollow district, priority: crown business."

Crown business. In fifteen years of service I had learned that "crown business" meant one of three things: the usurper's paranoia, Serel Doss's paranoia, or money changing hands in ways no one wanted written down. This slip bore a seal I didn't recognize — not the usurper's sunburst, not Doss's iron eye, but a device of seven interlocking rings. Seven cities. The free cities hadn't sent so much as an insult across the border in a decade.

I did what I always do with things I don't understand. I copied them, filed the original where it belonged, and started asking questions in the tone of voice that makes people think they're telling *me* something.

* * *

Her name was Maren Vael, and she arrived at my office three days later under the cover story of a grain inspector, which fooled absolutely no one, least of all her, since she wore the disguise of a grain inspector the way August wears his invisibility — competently, and with visible contempt for anyone who might believe it.

She was small, gray-eyed, and had the particular stillness of someone who has spent years being underestimated and has decided to weaponize it. She looked at my maps the way I look at other people's handwriting: reading the person through the work.

"Cartographer Corvane," she said. "Your reputation precedes you."

"My reputation is that I make accurate maps," I said. "It's a low bar. People keep tripping over it."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Approval, maybe. Or the recognition of a fellow professional. "I need to see your shrine surveys. The old ones. Pre-Godfall."

"Those are sealed archive materials."

"I know." She set a purse on my desk. It made no sound at all, which told me it was heavy enough that coins would have clinked if there'd been room for them to move. "And I know you've already shown them to someone else recently. The witch-finder's requisitions are a matter of record even in my city. You gave him fakes."

My blood went very quiet, the way it does before a fall. "I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." She opened the purse, took out a single coin, and laid it flat. On it was stamped the seven-ringed seal. "I know what you mean, Corvane. I've read your fakes. They're very good. The error pattern is consistent — you age everything by the same twelve percent, you shift every shrine forty paces east of where the footpaths actually converge, and you always, *always* leave out the well at the center of the Hollow." She looked up. "You left it out of the real ones too. But you drew it somewhere else, didn't you? Somewhere only you could find."

I have been afraid exactly four times in my adult life, and I count carefully. This was the fourth. Not because she threatened me — she hadn't, yet — but because she had read my maps the way I'd hoped no one ever would: as a confession.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"The same thing everyone wants," said Maren Vael. "The vessel."

* * *

Let me be precise about what happened in that conversation, because I have replayed it enough times to wear grooves in it.

She told me about the prophecies. Not the ones printed in the capital's sanctioned hymnals — those are the usurper's property, scrubbed and gelded — but the older ones, carried out of the Godfall by refugees and kept alive in the free cities like seeds in a pocket during a long winter. *When the god wakes beneath the stone, a door shall walk among men, marked on hand and heart.* She recited it without piety, the way a soldier recites drill instructions.

"The free cities have spent ten generations preparing for the war we'll have to fight when the usurper's line comes for us," she said. "We built walls. We built alliances. We built nothing that can stop what he's building now — he's been buying anti-divine weapons off the black market for six years, Corvane. We've seen the manifests. If the god under this city wakes and someone carries it, the balance changes. The prophecy says a door will walk among men. We intend to find that door before Doss does."

"And when you find it?"

She looked at me with those gray eyes and answered honestly, which I almost admired. "Then we use it."

"Use it how?"

"That depends on the door," she said. "On whether it opens willingly."

I thought of August then — which is to say, I thought of him the way I had been thinking of him constantly for weeks now, an inconvenient warmth behind the sternum that I had decided not to examine too closely, the way one doesn't probe a healing wound. I thought of his hands, careful and quick. His terrible, perfect forgettability. The sigil burned into his palm that I had traced onto paper at three in the morning and then burned the tracing, and then hated myself for burning, and then drawn again anyway because some knowledge refuses to stay destroyed.

"The vessel isn't yours to use," I said.

"No one's is," Vael agreed. "That's rather the problem. Everyone wants him. The witch-finder wants him dead or caged. The usurper wants him — well, I'm still gathering intelligence on what the usurper wants, but I promise you it isn't benevolent. The cults want him. My cities want him. And you—" she tilted her head, "—you want him to remain a person. Which is the most expensive wish on that list, and the only one nobody's funding."

"How do you—"

"Corvane. I've been in this city nine days. Your name appears in three separate intelligence files, cross-referenced with his. You requisitioned pre-Godfall surveys the same month the taster stopped being invisible. You lied to Serel Doss with cartographic precision, which means you knew what to protect and chose to protect it." She stood, straightening her grain-inspector's coat. "I'm not your enemy. I'm simply the enemy who's honest. Think about which is worse."

She left the coin on my desk. Seven interlocking rings. I stared at it for a long time after the door closed.

Then I put it in the drawer with everything else I couldn't afford to think about yet, and went to find August.

* * *

He wasn't hard to find, which frightened me more than anything Vael had said. Fifteen years of being unfindable, and lately he'd developed a habit of being exactly where I could reach him — the night market, the archive steps, the little tea house near the map rooms where the owner watered the wine and pretended not to notice that August never ate.

He was sitting in the corner with a cup he wasn't drinking, and when I sat down across from him he didn't perform surprise. That's the thing about us now. We've stopped pretending to be strangers, though we haven't quite figured out what to pretend to be instead.

"You have a face," he said.

"I always have a face."

"A specific face. The one you get when someone's read your maps wrong." He turned the cup a quarter-turn. "Or right."

I glanced around. The tea house was nearly empty; the owner was arguing with a delivery boy about the philosophical nature of turnips. Still, I lowered my voice. "There's an envoy from the free cities in the capital. Her name is Maren Vael. She came for you."

August absorbed this the way he absorbs everything — completely, and without any visible change except that his hands went still. "For me specifically."

"For the vessel. You happen to be wearing it." I hesitated. "August. She talked about you the way Doss talks about shrines. As a target with a use value. She wants to carry you east and point you at the usurper's armies."

"And what do you want?"

The question landed harder than it should have. I looked at my own hands on the table — ink-stained, precise, hands that had spent a lifetime drawing lines between things and calling it knowledge. "I want you to stay a person," I said, and heard Vael's words come out of my mouth and realized she'd been right about the expense, because saying it felt like spending something I couldn't replace.

August was quiet for a moment. Then, wryly, gently: "That makes one of us. Well. Two." He counted on his fingers. "You, me. Possibly the tea house owner, who mostly wants me to finish my cup."

"It's not funny."

"It's a little funny. An envoy, a witch-finder, a usurper, a cult, and a god walk into a capital—" He stopped himself, and the humor drained out of his face, leaving something tired and clear underneath. "Ines. There's a part of me that understands all of them. That's the worst part. When Vael says 'use,' some corner of me thinks: yes, finally, a purpose, a shape, someone else deciding. Being a weapon sounds restful compared to being a man who has to choose."

"August—"

"But I don't get to be restful." He pushed the untouched cup aside. "So. What do we do about your envoy?"

"We," I repeated.

His mouth twitched. "You've been saying 'we' in your head for weeks. I've watched you do it. You have a very loud face."

* * *

What we did about Maren Vael was, in retrospect, both clever and doomed, which describes most of our plans.

I agreed to meet her again — alone, ostensibly, in the archive after hours, where she promised to show me the free cities' intelligence on the usurper's weapons purchases. I brought August hidden among the map drawers, because the archive at night is a maze I have known for twenty years and she had known for nine days, and because I wanted her to see him. Not the vessel. Him. I wanted her to look at the way he flinched when the lamp guttered, the way he apologized to furniture, the small human inventory of him, and see if it changed anything in her gray eyes.

It didn't. That's what I remember most clearly. She looked at August Villalobos — nervous, wry, mortal in every way that matters — and I watched her catalog him like terrain. Elevation. Access. Strategic value.

"So you're the door," she said.

"I'm a food taster," August said. "Whoever told you otherwise has been reading forged documents."

"The sigil on your palm is documented in three independent sources. Your immunity is logged in your guild's records as inherited, which is a lie someone wrote to manage you. And last month you walked into the Hollow in your sleep and came back smelling of moss and woodsmoke." She said it without triumph, which somehow made it worse. "I don't need you to confess. I need you to understand your options. Come east with us. Cooperate, and the free cities will protect you — from Doss, from the usurper, from whatever's waking under your feet. Refuse, and we'll take our chances extracting you anyway, because the alternative is letting that god fall into the hands of a man who wants to live forever on its back."

"You're offering protection," August said slowly, "in exchange for becoming a siege engine."

"I'm offering the only version of survival that includes the free cities' army between you and everyone else who wants you." She spread her hands. "Yes. You'd be aimed. At the usurper, who deserves it. At a war that ends either his dynasty or ours. Every tool gets aimed, taster. The question is whether you're aimed by people who'll mourn you or people who'll spend you."

"And which are you?"

She paused. Honest to the bone, that one. "People who'll spend you," she said. "But efficiently. With respect."

The lamps guttered. I felt August go still beside me, felt the air change pressure the way it does when the thing beneath the city turns over in its sleep, and I saw Vael feel it too — saw her eyes widen with something that was, for one unguarded second, awe instead of assessment.

"There," she whispered. "You feel that? That's why. That's worth any price."

"That," August said quietly, "is a person's dream. And you're standing in it."

* * *

We got her out of the archive without incident. She left the way she'd come, through the surveyors' entrance, with copies of nothing, because I had given her nothing, because the folder I'd prepared for her contained the same careful fakes I'd fed Doss, aged by twelve percent, shifted forty paces east. She probably knew. She'd let herself be fooled, or she hadn't noticed, and either way I'd bought time, which is the only currency cartographers deal in.

After she left, August and I stood among the map drawers in the lamplight, and he laughed — a short, exhausted sound with no humor in it.

"'With respect,'" he quoted. "She said it like that was the concession. Like being spent respectfully is a kindness."

"It's the kindness people offer," I said. "Doss wouldn't bother with respect. The usurper wouldn't either. The god—" I stopped.

"The god asks," August finished softly. "Did you know that? Underneath everything, in the Hollow, it asked me. *Will you carry me.* Nobody else in this entire mess has bothered to ask." He rubbed the sigil on his palm absently, a habit now. "An envoy who spends me respectfully. A witch-finder who'd burn me. A tyrant who'd wear me. A brother-cult that bred another one somewhere. And one god, asking permission." He looked up at me. "Do you know what the truly ridiculous thing is, Ines? The god is the only party in these negotiations I trust."

I didn't say anything. I reached out and took his hand — the marked one — and held it, and he let me, and we stood in the archive like two conspirators in a country that hadn't declared itself yet.

"Vael will try extraction whether you cooperate or not," I said finally. "She told me as much. 'We'll take our chances.' She has people here. Probably more than nine days' worth — she was scouting before she ever walked into my office."

"I know." He sighed. "Everyone wants a piece of me. Doss wants the piece that burns. The usurper wants the piece that rules. The free cities want the piece that wins wars. The cult wants—" he frowned. "Actually, I don't know what the cult wants. Which bothers me more than it should."

"The cult wants what it's always wanted," I said. "The god awake and obedient. And August — if Vael has people in this city, and the cult has cells in the palace kitchens, and Doss is closing the net from above—" I laid it out the only way I know how, in lines converging. "Every path leads to the Hollow. Yours, theirs, everyone's. Sooner or later we're all going to end up underground."

August was quiet a long moment. Then he said, in the lightest voice he owned, the one I'd learned meant the opposite:

"Well. At least we know the way down better than they do."

Outside the archive windows, far below the streets, faint as a memory of heat, something warm turned over in its sleep — and this time, I would have sworn on every map I'd ever drawn, it sounded less like a heartbeat than like a knock.
