## 20. Poison at the Peace Table

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a banquet hall when a man drinks something he shouldn't. I had heard it before—once, years ago, when a foreign envoy's cup was swapped as a joke between drunk cousins—but that silence had been embarrassed. This one was different. This one was hungry.

But let me start earlier, because the evening deserved its full accounting.

The peace table had been set in the Amber Hall, which is what the usurper called the long gallery where he received delegations too important to kill and too dangerous to ignore. The occasion was the armistice talks with the river provinces—the ones that had been "nearly concluded" for three years now—and the usurper had decided to mark the progress with a feast. Progress, in his court, meant that nobody had been executed that week and the treasury could still afford swans.

I stood at my station behind his right shoulder, as I had for fifteen years, tasting each course before it touched his plate. The routine was muscle memory by now: the silver spoon, the small pause, the careful roll of the substance across my tongue like a jeweler examining a stone. Wine from the southern vineyards—clean. The quail in juniper glaze—clean, though over-salted; someone in the kitchens was grieving, or careless, or both. The honeyed pears—clean.

And then the seventh course arrived on a platter of blackened silver, carried not by the usual servers but by a man I did not know, wearing livery I half-recognized: the palace colors, but cut wrong at the collar. New livery. New hands.

I tasted the sorrel soup.

For a moment there was nothing. Sorrel, cream, a whisper of white pepper. And then, underneath, arriving late like a guest who knows he isn't welcome: nightshade.

Now, nightshade and I are old acquaintances. It has been slipped to me perhaps forty times across fifteen years, usually by amateurs, occasionally by professionals, and it has never once done anything to me except taste like a mistake. My tongue catalogued it automatically—*atropa, third pressing, masked with fennel oil*—and I began composing the quiet logistics of disposal. A sleeve. A napkin. A stumble near the brazier.

Except.

Underneath the nightshade, folded into it so carefully that only someone who had spent a decade and a half learning poison's grammar would notice, was something else. Something that didn't taste like a plant at all. It tasted like cold iron and candle smoke and, absurdly, like the inside of a church. Like the memory of incense. It sat on my tongue and *waited*, the way the burnt offering in my workshop had hummed and waited, the way the god beneath the Hollow waited.

This wasn't a poison. Or rather, it wasn't *only* a poison. It was bait dressed as a knife. Someone wanted me to detect the nightshade, dispose of the soup quietly, and go home feeling competent. And whatever the second thing was, it wanted me to carry it out of the hall in my body, unswallowed, unexamined, a seed smuggled in a taster's mouth.

I know this because I almost did exactly that. Fifteen years of habit nearly made me the perfect courier for my own undoing.

I turned the spoon over in my hand and considered my options, all of them bad. Spit it into the napkin, and the watcher—whoever they were, and they were watching, I could feel it the way you feel a draft—would know their first layer had failed and try again tomorrow, less subtly. Pour it out, same problem. Refuse to taste? Tasters don't refuse. A taster who refuses is a taster who has already confessed to something.

Or I could swallow it.

Here is what I knew about the second thing: I didn't know it. Here is what I knew about myself: since the night in the Hollow, when a voice like a hearth fire had asked if I would carry it, my body had stopped being entirely mine. The god's senses leaked through mine at odd hours. I could feel the soup's second passenger the way you can feel a stranger standing too close in a crowd—not touching, but *present*, patient, interested in me specifically.

Swallowing unknown divine-adjacent substances seemed, on balance, unwise.

So I did the third thing, the thing no careful taster should ever do. I hesitated.

It was only a heartbeat—a spoon held a half-second too long above the bowl—but in the Amber Hall, a heartbeat is an event. The usurper noticed. Of course he did. He notices everything; it's the one competence I've never begrudged him. His pale eyes slid sideways to me, and I watched him read my face the way Ines reads maps, and I understood, with the particular horror of a man whose invisibility has just failed him publicly, that I had made a mistake.

"August," he said pleasantly, to the whole room. "You look troubled."

"Only deciding whether the sorrel is worthy of Your Majesty," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me.

"And?"

And here is where I should tell you about the second spoonful, because there was one. Protocol says one taste. But the usurper, who enjoyed theater more than protocol, gestured with two fingers: *more*. He wanted the full performance. He wanted his taster to drink deep and pronounce the dish safe while the court watched, because the court had been restless lately—prayers being answered in the streets, witch-finders burning shrines, rumors moving through the servants' corridors like rats—and a king needs his rituals to work in public.

I took the second spoonful. Then, because some part of me had apparently decided that if I was going to be ruined, I'd be ruined thoroughly, I drank the rest of the bowl.

The nightshade did nothing. It never does. It slid off me like rain off oiled leather.

The other thing did not slide off.

It went down like a coin dropped into a well, and I felt it fall—felt it pass my sternum, my ribs, settling somewhere below my heart where it began, very softly, to *warm*. Not painfully. That was the worst of it. It warmed the way a hearth warms a room you thought was empty, and somewhere far beneath the floor of the world, something stirred and said, without words, in the voice I had heard once in the Hollow:

*Ah. There you are.*

I kept my face still. Fifteen years of practice. I set down the bowl, bowed, and said, "The sorrel is excellent, Your Majesty."

The court relaxed. The music resumed. The usurper ate his soup.

And I stood behind him for four more courses with a lit coal in my chest, smiling the smile of a man who has just realized that everyone at the table might be holding a knife, and that for the first time in his life, he cannot tell which direction the knives are pointing.

* * *

The attempt, when it came, was almost elegant in its stupidity.

Dessert was a plum torte, and I tasted it, and it was clean, and the usurper ate it, and nothing happened, and the talks adjourned for the night with mutual expressions of satisfaction that fooled no one. The court rose in its glittering waves. And then a woman stepped out of the procession of departing guests—a minor countess, I'd seen her at a dozen functions, forgettable in the way I prided myself on being forgettable—and she stumbled against the usurper's wine decanter as she passed.

A stumble. An apology. A laugh. The decanter rocked; a servant caught it; a few drops of dark red wine spilled across the cloth like a wound.

And every instinct I have, every alarm bell installed in me by a dead priest who bought a farm boy and taught him to be furniture, went off at once.

Because the countess hadn't looked at the decanter. She'd looked at *me*.

Wine doesn't get poisoned after it's poured, not in front of forty witnesses. But the drops on the cloth were steaming. Very slightly. Very faintly. The same steam I'd once seen rise off a burnt offering in my own workshop, the offering that had hummed under my fingers while my skin drank its heat.

They weren't trying to kill the king. They were trying to make *me* drink.

"Your Majesty," I heard myself say, "allow me." And I picked up the decanter, and I poured the remaining wine into the king's cup, and before anyone—before the guards, before the witch-finders stationed at the doors, before Serel Doss himself, whom I saw across the hall going very still—could stop me, I lifted the cup and drank it down.

All of it.

The hall went silent in that hungry way again.

What was I doing? I'll tell you what I was doing. I was doing arithmetic. If the wine was poison, I was immune, and the countess's faction had wasted their shot and revealed themselves. If the wine was the other thing—the waiting thing, the bait thing—then it was meant for me anyway, and better I take it on my terms, in public, where swallowing it meant nothing, than let them feed it to me piecemeal in private where each dose could be calibrated. And if it was both—

It was both.

The nightshade hit first, a full decanter's worth, enough to stop the heart of an ox. I felt it arrive like a wave breaking over a seawall, and I felt the wall hold, the way it always holds, the way it has held since I was a boy in a farm kitchen drinking from a well someone had poisoned to test a rumor. My blood shrugged it off. Old magic. Borrowed magic. *Inherited*, said the ledger I'd found in the guild records, in handwriting that was mine and wasn't.

Then the second thing bloomed.

It was stronger this time. Much stronger. Whatever was in the soup had been a seed; this was a root. It spread through my chest like dawn coming up over water, and I felt the god beneath the city turn toward me the way a sleeper turns toward a window when light enters the room. The warmth climbed my throat. I could taste it on my breath—candle smoke, cold iron, old churches—and I knew, with absolute certainty, that if I opened my mouth right now, something would come out of it that wasn't words.

So I closed my eyes, and I did the only prayer I know how to do anymore, which is not a prayer at all but a negotiation: *Not here. Not now. Not through me. Lie down.*

And the warmth—listened. It banked itself, coals raked over, a fire told to wait. The pressure receded. I opened my eyes.

Forty people were staring at me.

The countess was gone. Slipped out during the silence, or maybe during the wine—I couldn't say, and neither, apparently, could the guards, which told me her exit had been arranged with the same care as her entrance. Doss was crossing the hall toward me with the unhurried gait of a man who has just watched a mouse solve a puzzle box, and his face was doing something complicated: suspicion, satisfaction, and under it all, faintly, unwillingly, awe.

The usurper was looking at me too.

He had risen from his chair. He was holding his untouched dessert fork like a scepter, and his pale eyes had gone wide in a way I had never seen in fifteen years—not anger, not fear. *Wonder.* The wonder of a man who has kept a locked box in his cellar for years and has just heard something knock from inside.

"August," he said, and my name in his mouth sounded different than it ever had. It sounded like an inventory entry. "Drink it again."

"Your Majesty—"

"*Again.*"

There was no refusing. There had never been any refusing; I had simply never before been asked for something visible. I tipped the decanter and drank the dregs, wine-stained and bitter, and the court watched my throat move, and when nothing happened—when I stood there flushed and breathing and *alive*—a sound went through the Amber Hall that I will hear until I die. Not gasps. Whispers. Hundreds of whispers starting at once, like wind through wheat, and every single one of them contained my name.

Fifteen years I had spent teaching this court that I did not exist. One decanter of wine, and I was the most memorable man in the capital.

* * *

They gave me new quarters that night. I want to be precise about that verb: *gave*. Nobody asked. Two guards appeared at my door in the tasters' wing—"for your protection," they said, with the flat courtesy of men who would also be preventing my departure—and escorted me up three floors to a room with a window that faced inward, onto a courtyard, onto the witch-finders' barracks.

On the desk, someone had left a tray. Fruit, bread, a small carafe of water. All of it, I noted professionally, untasted. Unpoisoned. They were done trying to kill me. That stage of the relationship was over.

I sat on the edge of the bed—it was a good bed, better than mine, that was somehow the most insulting part—and I pressed my palm flat against my sternum, where the warmth still lived, banked but present, a tenant who had signed no lease.

"You could have warned me," I said aloud, to no one, to the air, to whatever listened.

The warmth pulsed once. Not speech. Not quite. More like a shrug. The god beneath the Hollow had never claimed to be kind, only to be *old*, and old things watch young ones walk into traps with a patience that looks a great deal like indifference.

Someone knocked. For one absurd moment I hoped—hoped the way you hope against hope, knowing the shape of the disappointment in advance—that it would be Ines. She'd been at the feast; I'd seen her at the cartographers' table near the back, mapping the room the way she mapped everything, and our eyes had met exactly once, over the wine, at the exact moment the whispers started. What passed between us in that glance I am still unpacking. Alarm, certainly. Question, certainly. But also something steadier, something that said: *I know what you are, and I am still here.*

It was not Ines. It was a page with a note, unsigned, in a hand I recognized from fifteen years of requisition forms.

*The king requests your presence at morning audience. You will sit at the table. —by command*

At the table. Not behind it. Not beside it, as furniture. At it.

I sat in the borrowed room with the inward-facing window and understood, finally and completely, what had happened tonight. For fifteen years, my value had been my absence—the taster no one saw, the man whose name servants forgot by the next course. Tonight I had drunk poison before the whole court and lived, and there is no charm, no habit, no art of forgetting that can make a court unsee a miracle performed at dinner. The usurper had spent years wondering why his food-taster never aged, never sickened, never died, and now he had his answer served in wine, and answers, in that palace, are the beginning of use.

Somewhere below, in the Hollow, the god slept warm and waited. Somewhere across the city, Ines was presumably awake, doing what she always did with unbearable information: copying it, filing it, deciding what to do with it. Somewhere in the dark, the countess and whoever owned her were learning that their bait had been swallowed whole, in public, and recalculating.

And somewhere in the guild records, in handwriting that was mine and wasn't, a ledger had logged this night fifteen years ago. I was almost sure of it now. *Subject exposed. Vessel confirmed. Proceed to next phase.* Someone had written this evening before I lived it.

I lay back on the too-good bed and stared at the ceiling, and the coal in my chest breathed with me, slow and patient, and I thought: *I was invisible for fifteen years. I would give a great deal to be invisible again.*

Outside the inward-facing window, the witch-finders' barracks lamps burned late.

One of them, I noticed, was directly beneath my sill—as if someone had chosen this room so that I would always know exactly how close the fire was kept.
