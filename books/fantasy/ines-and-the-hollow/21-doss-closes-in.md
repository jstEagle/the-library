## 21. Doss Closes In

They came for me on a Tuesday, which I have always considered a small mercy. Mondays are too early in the week to be ruined properly; by Wednesday one has already committed to the ruin of the week and might as well see it through. Tuesday leaves you just enough time to be surprised.

The knock came at my door in the Cartographers' Wing before dawn — three knocks, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who has never once had to hurry. I knew it was Serel Doss before I opened it. Witch-finders do not send clerks to do their waking.

"Ines Corvane," he said, when I let him in. He did not wait to be asked to sit; he sat, in my one good chair, the one I kept for visiting engineers who might someday approve funding. "You look well."

"You look like a man about to ruin someone's Tuesday."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. Doss was not handsome, but he was *arranged* — grey hair combed with geometric precision, coat brushed, boots polished to a shine that suggested he polished them himself, nightly, as a form of prayer. His eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who has learned that everyone talks eventually and that hurrying them only spoils the flavor.

"I've been reviewing your archive," he said.

"Everyone reviews the archive. It's an archive."

"Not everyone finds what I found." He produced a folder from inside his coat — leather, unmarked, the kind of folder that has no business existing outside an inquisitor's office. "Your survey maps of the Hollow district. Filed over the past six years. Very thorough work, Mistress Corvane. Almost too thorough."

I poured myself tea. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I had expected them to shake, and their steadiness felt like meeting a stranger in my own skin. "Thoroughness is the job."

"Mm." He opened the folder and turned it toward me. Laid out across it were copies — good copies, wax-pressed, faithful — of four of my maps. The shrine surveys. The ones marked *ruined, access denied, structural collapse.*

"Do you know what I do all day, Mistress Corvane?" he asked.

"Find witches."

"I compare things." He tapped the maps. "I compare testimony against testimony. Ledger against ledger. Map against map. Most people lie badly — they lie in *words*, which shift under pressure like sand. But a map is a promise made in ink. A map says: *I stood here. I measured this.* And when a map lies—" he looked up at me, mild as milk, "—it lies in a language only another careful person can read."

I drank my tea. Outside, the palace was waking; somewhere below us, August Villalobos was presumably tasting some minister's breakfast and pretending not to exist, which was his particular genius. I envied him suddenly, fiercely. Invisibility is a skill. I had spent years building mine, and here was a man dismantling it with a folder.

"These are accurate," I said.

"The shrines are where you say they are. That's the remarkable thing." He drew out a second set of papers — field reports, inquisition surveys, done by Doss's own people within the last month. "My surveyors visited eleven of your 'ruined' sites. Nine were exactly as you described. Collapsed roofs. Rubble. No access." He paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses before naming the organ. "Two were not."

"Surveyors make errors."

"One of them was standing inside it when he wrote his report, Mistress Corvane. A living shrine. Fresh offerings. Footpaths worn into the stone — footpaths your map shows as solid rockfall." He folded his hands. "You mapped that site yourself. Fourteen months ago. You walked those paths — your own margin notes describe the gradient. And then you drew rubble over them."

The tea had gone cold in my cup, or perhaps I had simply stopped tasting it. I thought, absurdly, of my old instructor at the Survey Academy, Master Ellery, who used to say that every lie on a map is a debt, and debts compound. He'd meant cartographic simplification. Rivers straightened. Coastlines smoothed. He had not, I think, imagined this exact morning.

But he had taught me something else, too, without meaning to: *when caught, never retreat from precision. Retreat into it.* A liar caught in vagueness is finished. A liar caught in detail is merely challenged.

"You're right that there's an error," I said. "It's mine, and I'll correct it formally if you like."

Doss's eyebrows rose a fraction. I had surprised him. Good. Surprise is currency; spend it early.

"Go on," he said.

"Those two sites were re-surveyed after my original mapping. By the Office of Works — drainage remediation after the spring floods, three years back. They cleared the rockfall to assess foundation damage, found the structures unsound, and *re-sealed* them. But they filed their remediation under engineering works, not survey amendments, so the archive copy was never updated." I set down my cup. "I discovered the discrepancy last autumn. I should have flagged it. I didn't, because flagging it would have required explaining why I was cross-referencing engineering filings against six-year-old shrine surveys, and the honest answer is tidiness, and nobody believes tidiness."

It was, I want to be clear, almost entirely true. The Office of Works remediation was real — I had used its paperwork myself, once, to explain away a path I didn't want explained. The rest was architecture built on that one true beam. This is the secret of lying well: build on something load-bearing.

Doss studied me for a long moment. Then he turned to the second disputed map. "And the shrine of Saint Vessia?"

"Same remediation program. Different contractor." I reached across and took the map from him — taking it was important, handling my own work with the confidence of ownership — and pointed to the eastern margin. "You'll note my contour lines here. If I were falsifying access, would I draw contours consistent with a collapsed approach? Forgers simplify. Contours are the hardest thing in the world to fake because they have to agree with everything around them. Water flow, slope stability, the lot. Whoever drew these contours did the full survey." I met his eyes. "I did the full survey. What I failed to do was chase down a filing clerk's error afterward. That's negligence, Witch-Finder. Charge me with negligence."

Silence. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked — the palace's ancient heating, complaining.

"You're very good at this," Doss said at last, and there was no admiration in it at all.

"At maps? Yes."

"At making truth and lies wear the same coat."

"They're cut from the same cloth, Witch-Finder. Ask any witness in any court."

He closed the folder slowly. I understood then that I had not won. I had, at best, moved the game to a slower board. Doss was not a man who needed to win today; he was a man who collected Tuesdays, filed them, and waited for the pattern to emerge. I had seen his kind before, in the Academy — the examiners who never accused, only noted, until the notes became a net.

"Here is what happens now," he said, rising. "By order of the Inquisition, your archive access is suspended pending review. All keys. All drawer permissions. Your current commissions will be reassigned; you'll be given new ones — supervised."

"That cripples the survey office."

"The survey office will survive. Empires are very good at surviving cartographers." At the door he stopped, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "One more thing, Mistress Corvane. Six years ago you requested the pre-Godfall plats of the Hollow district. Restricted collection. Your request was denied twice, and then approved — on the third attempt — by an endorsement signature we have been unable to trace."

The floor seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way a ship tilts when it has struck something far beneath the waterline.

"I don't recall the endorsement," I said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I imagine someone recalled it *for* you." He stepped into the corridor, then paused again. "You know what interests me most, Corvane? Not the falsified sites. Anyone might protect a smuggler's route or a lover's tryst. What interests me is the *pattern*. Eleven shrines. Every path converging inward, toward the Hollow's center, like veins toward a heart. That's not negligence. That's not even sentiment." Now he did turn, and his pale eyes were almost kind, which was infinitely worse. "That's devotion. Someone taught you to guard something. I'd very much like to know who."

And he was gone, leaving my tea cold and my Tuesday thoroughly ruined.

* * *

They took the keys at noon. A junior inquisitor with ink-stained fingers and an apologetic cough, carrying a receipt book. I signed over eleven keys and watched him seal the archive door behind him, and then I stood in the corridor of the Cartographers' Wing feeling like a woman whose house has burned down while she was standing in the garden.

What frightened me was not the loss of access. I had copied everything that mattered years ago — the real maps lived in a oilcloth packet beneath the floor of my lodgings, and the sigil overlay, the one that had taken shape under my lamp three weeks ago, lived folded against my ribs, next to my heart, where paper warms to body temperature and starts to feel like part of you. No. What frightened me was the endorsement.

Because Doss was right, and I had been avoiding the arithmetic for years. My third request for the restricted plats had been denied. I remembered writing it, remember the sting of refusal, remember drafting the appeal mostly out of stubbornness. And then, six weeks later, a clerk had appeared at my door with the tube under his arm and a chit bearing a signature I had never questioned. *Approved per standing directive. Initials illegible.* I had been so pleased with myself — persistence rewarded! — that I had never asked the obvious question: *who in this palace wanted me to see those maps?*

Someone had opened doors for me. Someone had watched me find the converging paths and had let me keep finding them. Either I had been groomed as an asset, or I had been running on rails laid down before I ever entered the Survey Academy, and both possibilities ended with the same cold conclusion: my caution had never entirely been my own. Doss had asked *who taught you to guard something*, and the terrible answer was that I did not know, and that whoever it was had known me better than I knew myself.

There was one person who might understand that particular vertigo. One person in this palace whose whole life was a set of permissions granted by an invisible hand.

I found August Villalobos that evening, or rather, I arranged to be found. The tasters take their supper in a narrow room off the kitchens, a long table, bad light, the camaraderie of men who share a profession no one envies. I carried in a delivery of updated district plans for the steward's office — a pretext so thin it was practically gauze — and set them down beside him.

"Master Villalobos. The kitchen corridors. Steward wants them re-measured before the winter stores come in."

He looked up. August has a face built for being forgotten — pleasant, unremarkable, the sort of face your eye slides off like rain off slate. But his eyes themselves, when they actually meet yours, are older than the face. Fifteen years of tasting death in small doses will do that.

"Corvane," he said. "Cartography trouble?"

"Archive trouble." I kept my voice low, pitched under the clatter of the kitchen beyond. "The Inquisition suspended my access today. Doss himself."

Something moved behind his forgettable face — quick, controlled, gone. "Doss collects people the way other men collect coins. Don't take it personally."

"He showed me my own maps and told me they lied."

"And did they?"

"What a question to ask a stranger."

"We're hardly strangers." He squared the plans without looking at them, a man buying time with his hands. "We've sat at the same banquets for years. You always sit where you can see the doors. I've always noticed."

My heart performed a small, unauthorized maneuver. "And you always leave before the toasts. I've always noticed *that*."

For a moment we regarded each other across the bad light and the long table, two professionals recognizing the tools of the trade in each other's hands. There was a strange comfort in it, and a strange grief. It is lonely, being a liar among liars who cannot acknowledge the craft.

"Whatever Doss thinks he's found," August said quietly, "be careful. He doesn't need proof. He needs *pattern*. And patterns are easy to finish once someone else has started them."

"Is that professional advice or personal experience?"

His mouth curved — not quite a smile. "Taster's privilege. I don't discuss the menu." He gathered the plans. "Re-measure the kitchen corridors yourself, Corvane. Before the Inquisition does. Some rooms are better left unmapped."

He walked out through the servants' passage, and I stood there amid the steam and shouting of the kitchens, holding the shape of his warning like a hot coal passed hand to hand.

*Some rooms are better left unmapped.* From any other man, superstition. From the royal food-taster, it sounded like a man describing his own chest.

* * *

That night I did what I should have done years ago: I took out the oilcloth packet, spread the true maps across my floor by candlelight, and began, methodically, to plan their destruction.

Not burning — burning is panic, and panic leaves ash that reads like confession. I am a cartographer. I know how documents die properly: soaked, pulped, scattered, forgotten. I rehearsed it in my head, step by step, the way I'd rehearse a difficult survey. And then I sat back on my heels and admitted the truth I'd been circling for hours.

I couldn't do it.

Not because of the danger — the danger argued loudly for it, and Doss's pale eyes argued louder still. But those maps were the only honest record in the empire of what was happening beneath the city. The footpaths. The converging veins. The heartbeat I had heard with my own palm flat against dead stone in the Hollow, three nights ago, faint and vast and *patient*. If I destroyed them, and something happened to me, the knowledge died with me — and whatever was waking beneath the capital would wake unwitnessed, unmapped, at the mercy of whichever faction reached it first. The usurper's witch-finders. Or worse.

So instead of destroying them, I did the harder thing. I copied the critical sheets in miniature — reduced scale, coded margins, disguised as routine drainage surveys of the river ward — and hid the copies in three separate places, and told myself that a map divided is a map preserved.

Then I blew out the candle and sat in the dark and listened to the city above me creak and settle, and beneath it — so faint I could have imagined it, except that I had felt it through my boot soles and knew I hadn't — that slow, deep pulse, like a heart turning over in its sleep.

Doss had my archive now. He had my patterns, my methods, my handwriting. He would feed it all into his patient machinery of comparison, and the machine would grind, and sooner or later it would arrive at the name at the center of every path I had ever drawn — not the god's name, which no one knew, but the name of the man who walked between the shrines like a key walking between locks.

August Villalobos. The food-taster. The invisible man.

Doss didn't have him yet. But Doss had *me*, and I was the map, and every map, eventually, leads somewhere.

Three days later, a note appeared under my door in a hand I didn't recognize, unsigned, smelling faintly of smoke:

*They burned the shrine on Tanner's Row tonight. There were families inside. You will want to write down the names. — A friend of the archive.*

I read it twice. Then I got out a fresh sheet of paper, and a pen, and I began — with a cartographer's precision, every name spelled correctly, every date exact — to keep a different kind of record entirely.
