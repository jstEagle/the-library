## 22. The Hollow Opens

I have spent fifteen years learning the exact weight of a lie. A taster's life is a curriculum in deception: how to swallow a falsehood and keep your face smooth as standing water; how to nod at a courtier's flattery as though you hadn't already catalogued three ways to kill him with the wine decanter. I thought myself fluent.

Then the god started using my mouth to pray, and I discovered I'd only ever been conversational.

It began, as most catastrophes do, on an ordinary Tuesday. I was in my rooms above the chandler's shop, mending a sock with the particular concentration of a man who has nothing else to fear, when my jaw opened without my permission and said, in a voice like embers settling:

*Come home.*

"Absolutely not," I told it, around a mouthful of wool thread.

The god did not answer. It never argues. That's the worst part. Arguing implies the possibility of losing.

* * *

By Wednesday, the streets had opinions.

I noticed it first on my walk to the palace—the same walk I'd made four thousand times, so unremarkable that even the guards had stopped seeing me, which is precisely how I like it. But that morning people were stopping in the middle of Corvane Street, tilting their heads like dogs hearing a whistle no one else can hear. An old man knelt in the gutter outside the pie shop, hands pressed flat to the cobbles, weeping. Not grieving-weeping. Grateful-weeping. The kind of crying you do when something you begged for actually arrives.

"What happened?" I asked him, because I am occasionally an idiot.

"She answered," he said. "My daughter. Fevered nine days. This morning she sat up and asked for bread." He looked up at me with wet, shining eyes, and I felt the heat of his joy pass through me like sunlight through a window—except windows don't *drink*, do they, and I felt it settle somewhere behind my sternum, warm and heavy, like a coin dropped into a well.

"That's wonderful," I said, and walked very quickly away.

Because here is what I knew, and what he didn't: prayers weren't supposed to be answered. Not like that. Not instantly, not freely, not by anything with the power to cure a fever across half a city before breakfast. The whole architecture of my quiet life rested on the understanding that gods were dead, or sleeping, or at minimum extremely slow. Charms worked because they were small—little nudges of luck, little greases for the wheels of chance. I sold hope in affordable portions. Nobody expected miracles from a fertility knot tied in red thread.

But the thing under the Hollow was awake now, and it was answering everything. Every prayer. All of them. At once.

And every answer passed through me on its way out.

* * *

You want to know what it feels like, being a door? Let me tell you about doors. A door doesn't decide who comes through. A door doesn't get to say *not tonight, I'm tired, come back Thursday.* A door stands there and does its one job while the world walks through it, and if enough world walks through it often enough, the hinges start to sing.

That was the sound I couldn't stop hearing. A low hum in my teeth, in the bones of my feet, in the fillings of my back molars. The sound of hinges bearing weight.

Thursday, a baker's prayer for rain got answered with a cloudburst that flooded his cellar and ruined two hundred loaves. Friday, a widow prayed for her son to come home from the war, and he came home—deserting, which meant the usurper's provosts came too, and took her with him. Saturday, a child prayed for her sick dog, and the dog lived, and the child's father—a dockhand with more faith than sense—told everyone within shouting distance, and by Sunday there were four hundred people kneeling in the Hollow district with their palms against the stones, and the stones were *warm*, and I could feel every single palm like fingers pressed against the inside of my skin.

I sat in my room with my head between my knees and breathed the way the old taster-master taught me, the breathing for after you've swallowed something bad and are waiting to find out if you'll die.

"You're hurting them," I said to the god. "You understand that? You're answering prayers like a man throwing coins off a bridge. Some of those coins land in fountains. Some land on skulls."

The warmth behind my sternum pulsed—not unkindly. That was the horror of it. It wasn't cruel. It was *eager*. Fifteen hundred years asleep under the ruins of its own temples, and now it could hear again, thousands of small voices a day saying *please, please, please,* and it had forgotten—or never learned—that *please* is not the same as *should.*

*I give what is asked,* it said, in the hearth-fire voice that lived somewhere below language, below my ribs.

"People ask for terrible things."

*Then they will learn what they carry.*

"They're not carrying anything! *I'm* carrying it. You made sure of that." I heard my own voice crack and hated it. "What happens when you get impatient enough to stop asking?"

The silence that followed was worse than any answer. Because we both knew the answer. I'd felt it for days now, a pressure building under the city like water behind a dam, like breath held too long. The god had asked me, once, politely, in the dark of the Hollow: *will you carry me?*

I had said yes, because I am a coward and because belonging, even terrible belonging, is sweet to a lonely man.

But lately the question had stopped being a question. Lately it was less *will you carry me* and more *you are carrying me, thank you, hold still.*

* * *

Monday morning, the street cracked.

Not metaphorically. Corvane Street, forty paces from my door, split open with a sound like the world clearing its throat, a seam running thirty feet from the chandler's step to the fountain, and out of the seam rose steam that smelled—this is the detail I cannot make my mind put down—smelled like bread baking. Like a hearth. Like home, if home were a geological event.

People gathered. Of course they gathered. People are moths and cracks in the earth are candles. And I stood among them, hood up, heart hammering, listening to them argue about whether it was a blessing or a gas leak, and I wanted to scream *it's neither, go home, stop feeding it your attention, every prayer you say near that crack goes straight through me and I can feel you all.*

Instead I bought a meat pie and ate it standing up, because a man has to maintain appearances, and because chewing gives you something to do with a mouth that keeps trying to open on its own.

The pie tasted of ash. Everything tasted of ash now, faintly, the way everything tastes of copper when you've bitten your tongue. My body was busy. My body was doing work I hadn't agreed to, metabolizing divinity like a furnace metabolizes coal, and the waste product was coming out of me as dread.

Ines found me at the fountain.

She didn't approach like Ines. Ines approaches like weather—you notice the change in pressure before you see her. But this time she simply appeared beside me, close enough to speak without being overheard, eyes fixed on the crack in the street like a good citizen.

"It widened two inches since dawn," she said. "I measured."

"I'm eating a pie."

"I can see that. Very convincing. The trembling sells it." Her voice was flat and careful, the cartographer's voice, the one she used for terrain that might shift underfoot. "August. Last night I mapped the footpaths again. There are eleven new shrines. Not restored—*new.* People dragging furniture into cellars and calling it temples. And every one of them sits on a line that runs—" She stopped herself. Looked at me sideways. "Through you. Doesn't it. You can feel them."

I set down the pie. My hands were shaking, and lying to her had become a labor I no longer had strength for, which frightened me more than the crack in the street.

"Four hundred people knelt in the Hollow yesterday," I said quietly. "I counted their heartbeats. Do you understand that sentence? I *counted their heartbeats.* Through stone. Through a quarter mile of buried city. I lay in my bed and felt every palm on every paving stone like someone pressing fingers into my back, and then the god pushed, just a little, just to stretch—and the fever-cures and the rains and the fires all went out at once like sparks off a grindstone, and I felt where each one landed." I swallowed. "One of them landed wrong, Ines. A prayer for vengeance. A man whose brother died in the levy. He prayed for the magistrate who signed the order to burn. And the god—"

The hearth-fire voice stirred behind my ribs, warm, patient, *right.*

"The magistrate's house caught fire," I finished. "Everyone got out. This time."

"*This time,*" Ines repeated.

"This time," I said. "It's learning. Or I'm loosening. One of the two, and I genuinely don't know which, and that's the part that keeps me awake. Because a door that opens itself isn't a door anymore, Ines. It's a hole."

She was quiet for a long moment. Around us the crowd murmured at the crack, and beneath us—I pressed my boot to the stone, casually, testing—the heartbeat rolled on, vast and slow and content, a thing the size of a district dreaming pleasant dreams about answering mail.

"When I was a girl," Ines said at last, "my grandmother told me the Godfall wasn't a war. Everyone says it was a war, gods against men. But she said the gods lost because they loved us wrong. Too much. They gave us everything we asked for until we drowned in it, and then we killed them for the drowning." She turned to face me fully, and her eyes were doing the thing they did over maps—measuring, deciding, committing lines to memory. "So. What do we do? Because I've started drawing contingency routes out of this city, August, and I would very much like to know if I'm drawing them for two."

The warmth behind my sternum flared, and my mouth opened, and the ember-voice used my tongue:

*SHE MAY COME DOWN. SHE MAY SEE.*

I clamped my jaw shut hard enough to ache. Ines didn't step back. That's the thing about Ines—she catalogues miracles instead of fleeing them.

"It just spoke?" she asked. "Using your—"

"Yes. Without asking. Which is new. Which is *worse.*" I rubbed my face with both hands. "Two weeks ago it requested. Politely. Now it makes announcements. Doors don't get consulted, apparently. Doors get *used.*"

"And if it stops announcing and starts acting?"

I looked down at the crack. Steam curled up, smelling of bread, of hearth-smoke, of every safe fire that ever kept the dark off a family. Underneath the comfort of it, deep down, the heartbeat quickened—one beat, two—like something turning over in sleep, dreaming bigger.

"Then the city finds out what's been keeping it warm," I said. "And I don't think it will survive the gratitude."

* * *

That night I didn't sleep. I sat upright in bed with a candle burning and my palms flat on my knees, and I did the only thing left to a door that's learned to rattle in its frame: I listened.

The god was moving. Not rising—not yet—but gathering, the way a wave gathers a half-mile offshore, invisible from the beach but enormous, inevitable, already committed. I could feel it pulling at the roots of the city, at the old shrine-lines Ines had mapped, drinking the prayers of ten thousand sleepers, growing fat and warm and *impatient.* Every answered prayer fed it. Every wonder made it stronger. The people were building their god out of their own longing, brick by brick, and none of them knew they were laying the bricks inside my chest.

And beneath the patience, beneath the hearth-warmth, I felt the other thing. The thing it had shown me once in the dark of the Hollow, the reason it had slept so long and woken so hungry: the memory of what it was *for.* Vessels bred to carry it. Armies waiting to be lit. A war, patient as sediment, waiting for its door.

"No," I said aloud, to the empty room, to the thing wearing my ribs like a coat. "Whatever you're planning, whatever the priests built me for—we're not doing it. I didn't agree to be a weapon. I agreed to be a *taster.* Small sins. Affordable ones."

The warmth pulsed. Almost amused.

*YOU AGREED TO CARRY ME,* it said. *YOU DID NOT ASK WHERE.*

The candle guttered. Down in the street, the crack sang—one clear note, like a wineglass touched at the rim—and somewhere across the city, a church bell cracked in its tower, and a woman woke screaming from a dream of fire that healed instead of burned, and I sat in the dark shaking, counting heartbeats that weren't mine, feeling the tide gather a half-mile offshore.

At dawn, Ines knocked on my door with her satchel full of maps and her face pale as fresh paper.

"August," she said. "The Hollow district sank four inches overnight. And there are people standing at the edge of the crack, praying into it." She swallowed. "It's answering them *out loud.*"
