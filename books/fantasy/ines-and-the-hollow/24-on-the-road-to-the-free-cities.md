## 24. On the Road to the Free Cities

*August*

The trouble with fleeing a capital that's begun to answer prayers is that the roads out of it are full of people who think you're the answer.

We'd been walking three days when we came to Millbrook, and I knew something was wrong before we reached the well. In my fifteen years as a food-taster I developed a nose for trouble—almond in the wrong dish, a kitchen gone too quiet—and what I smelled on the road into Millbrook was candle smoke. Miles from anywhere. Candle smoke and burnt grain and something underneath it, sweetish, that made the back of my teeth ache the way the nightshade had.

Ines felt me stiffen and put her hand on my arm. She'd learned to read me faster than I'd have liked. "Village," she said.

"I can see it's a village."

"A village that smells like a funeral."

The well at the crossroads was draped in ribbons. Someone had tied hundreds of them—strips of shirt, strips of curtain, strips of what I chose not to look at too closely—and every ribbon had words inked or stitched or scratched into it. *Heal my son. Bring back the rain. Let Marta forgive me.* And beneath each petition, in fresher ink: *answered.*

"They're not all answered," Ines said quietly. She has a cartographer's eye; she reads things whole. "Look. Some are crossed through."

"Crossed through how?"

"With ash." She touched one ribbon where a black thumbprint smeared the words. "Someone's keeping records."

I should have turned us around then. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this—the taster who survived fifteen years of poison by knowing when to spit knew when to turn around, and didn't, because a girl was hungry and an inn had light in its windows. Love makes you stupid in very ordinary ways. That's the part no ballad gets right.

* * *

*Ines*

The inn at Millbrook was called the Answered Kettle, which should have been warning enough for anyone.

The common room was full, but nobody was drinking much. They were talking—or rather, one man was talking, standing on a bench with his sleeves rolled up like a man about to do honest work, except the work was prophecy. He had a burn scar down one side of his face, old, and he wore it like a medal.

"—and I said to her, I said, Mother, your boy's lungs were rotten from birth, the physicians said so themselves, and she prayed anyway, didn't she, knelt right down on this floor—" he stamped the floorboards, and the crowd murmured like wind in wheat "—and come morning, that boy drew breath like a bellows. Like a *bellows*. Because the god under the city heard her. The god hears *all* of us now."

I found a table in the corner and did what I do best, which is look boring while memorizing everything. August went for bread and ale, moving with that deliberate unremarkableness he wears like a second coat. Fifteen years of being furniture taught him things I'm only beginning to learn.

What I noticed, being a mapmaker, was the wall behind the bar. It was covered in a map—not a good one, drawn in charcoal on sacking, but recognizable. It was the road we'd just traveled, and radiating out from the Hollow district were lines. Footpaths. Shrine paths. The same convergence pattern I'd found in the archives a lifetime ago, except someone had extended them past the city walls, out along every road, and marked villages with little flames.

Every village we'd passed through. Every village ahead of us.

"Here you are." August set down the ale. He followed my eyes to the wall. His jaw did the small tight thing it does instead of going pale. "That's new," he said.

"That's organized."

"Since when do peasants organize?"

"Since their prayers started getting answered," I said. "Faith was always there, August. It just needed proof."

The prophet on the bench had moved on to a story about a blind woman and a field of wheat, and the crowd was weeping now, and I understood with a cold clarity that none of these people were lying. That was the horror of it. The god was answering. Small prayers, desperate prayers, the kind that had gone unanswered since the Godfall—and it was answering them *badly*, or at least strangely, because faith without doctrine curdles fast. I'd seen it in the archive margins, in the witch-finders' manuals Doss used to quote at me: *unstructured devotion breeds structure of its own, and the structure it breeds is fire.*

A woman sat down across from us without asking. Sixty-odd, washerwoman's hands, and around her neck a charm on a cord—a fertility charm, cheap tin, the kind sold in night markets by men trying to be invisible.

"You're him," she said.

August didn't blink. It's his finest skill. "I'm nobody, mother. Just travelers."

"You're the taster." She leaned in. Her breath smelled of candle smoke and fever. "My cousin works in the palace laundry. She says there's a man there who can't be poisoned. She says the god put him there to wait." Her eyes were wet and shining and absolutely certain. "Are you waiting, love? Are you waiting for the call?"

Somewhere behind her, the prophet shouted *the god hears ALL of us*, and the room roared, and in that roar the woman gripped August's wrist and pressed it against the tin charm, and I saw his face go slack with something between pain and recognition, because the charm was warm. Warm as a living thing. Warm as his palm.

"We're waiting for supper," I said, loud enough to crack through, and stood, and pulled coins from my purse, and smiled the smile I'd learned from watching courtiers lie beautifully. "Thank you for the welcome. We'll pray for you."

She let us go. But at the door I looked back, and she was still watching us, and so were four other people, and none of them were smiling.

* * *

*August*

We walked another two miles in the dark before Ines would let us stop, and then only because there was a hay barn and she was limping.

Her boots were wrong for the road. Everything about Ines is wrong for the road—she walks like the ground owes her a survey—but she hadn't complained once in three days, which worried me more than complaining would have. Complaints mean trust. Silence means a person is saving their strength for something.

I built a small fire in the barn's corner, screened with hay, and she sat down next to me closer than she needed to, which I noted, and treasured, and refused to examine.

"The charm was warm," she said.

"Yes."

"How warm?"

"Ines."

"I'm mapping, August. It's what I do. How warm?"

"Warm as my hand." I held it out, palm up, the sigil scar catching the firelight. She took my wrist and turned it over, studying the mark the way she studies coastlines, and her fingers were cold and careful and I thought, not for the first time, that if the world ever gave us a month of peace I might simply die of gratitude.

"It's spreading," she said. "Faint lines off the main scar. Like tributaries."

"Like roots," I said, because I'd felt them growing, and saying *tributaries* would have been a kindness to us both that I apparently wasn't in the mood for. "The god isn't just sitting in me anymore. It's... settling. Making itself comfortable."

"And the charms work now without you. The woman's prayer got answered the night she bought it—you told me. Before you ever touched the thing."

"Before I ever touched it." I watched the fire. "Which means either the god doesn't need vessels anymore, or—" I stopped.

"Or it needs you specifically," Ines finished. "And everything else is just... overflow."

Overflow. That was the word for Millbrook, wasn't it. A hundred ribbons on a well, half of them crossed through in ash. A prophet on a bench. A woman with a warm tin charm looking at me like I was a door she'd been promised. The god waking under the capital and leaking out along every road, answering what it could reach, and what it couldn't reach—

"What happens to the crossed-through ones?" I asked. "The unanswered petitions. Who crosses them out?"

Ines was quiet a moment. "Someone keeping score," she said. "Or someone deciding which prayers deserve answers." She looked at me. "August. If the god is choosing—"

"Then somewhere out here is a village full of people whose prayers were weighed and found wanting." I poked the fire harder than it deserved. "And they'll have heard about the neighbors."

* * *

*Ines*

We found that village the next afternoon, and I will carry it longer than any other place I've burned or saved or loved.

It was called Sarrow's End, and it had been prosperous once—you could tell by the stone lintels, the wide lanes, the orchard rows. Now the orchard was cut down, every tree felled and stacked into a great pyre in the square, unlit. Around the pyre, the villagers had built barricades. Not against soldiers. Against *prayers*. Charms hung from the barricade posts on long cords, kept at a distance, like lepers' bells. Signs painted in careful letters: NO MIRACLES HERE. WE PRAY TO NOTHING. WE OWE NOTHING.

An old man with a crossbow challenged us from behind the barricade until August showed his empty hands and said, "We're not praying for anything," and the man's face did something terrible and relieved.

"You'd be the first," he said, and let us in.

Their story came out over barley tea in a house with every hearth cold. Two months ago, Sarrow's End had prayed. Their wells were failing, the blight was in the wheat, and when word spread that prayers were being answered, they'd prayed harder than anyone. Whole nights on their knees. Children sent out with ribbons.

"And it answered," the old man said. His name was Coram. "Oh, it answered. The wells filled. The blight lifted. And then Tam's daughter, who'd been lame since birth—she woke up walking, and everyone wept, and that was the last good day." He stared into his cold hearth. "Because the god doesn't give. Have you noticed? It *trades*. The wells filled and the miller's heart stopped in his sleep. The blight lifted and the Hendersons' twins were born silent. My wife prayed for rain on our fields and it rained on our fields and drowned them, and she—" his voice went flat "—she went out in the flood to thank it. They found her downstream."

I am a cartographer. I deal in distances and elevations, in things that stay where you measure them. But I sat in that cold house and understood that I was looking at the truest map of the Hollow yet: a god with no malice and no mercy, granting wishes the way a landslide grants a valley's wish to be shorter. Power without judgment. Answered prayer as a species of weather.

"Why the pyre?" August asked softly. "Why not just stop praying?"

Coram looked at him a long time. "Because stopping isn't enough," he said. "Because it hears wanting. You don't have to kneel. You just have to need something, and it comes, and it takes its price. So we burned the orchard—we cut down the trees ourselves, before it could promise us fruit. We keep the fires ready. If it starts listening to Sarrow's End again, we'll show it what we think of its gifts." He turned his cup in his ruined hands. "You two are going east, aren't you. To the free cities."

"We haven't said so," I said.

"Everyone's going east. Everyone's heard there's a vessel coming, and the free cities will crown him, and the war will start, and all this—" he gestured at the barricades, the dead orchard, the cold hearths "—will get sorted one way or the other." His eyes settled on August with a weight I recognized, the same weight the washerwoman's had, the same weight I was learning to hate in every stranger's face. "You've got the look of a man carrying something heavy, friend. I won't ask. But I'll tell you what my wife would have told you, if she'd lived: whatever it is they want you to carry—set it down. There's no throne worth what it costs to sit on one. We prayed for rescue and we learned the lesson of our lives." He almost smiled. "Nobody's coming to save you. Save yourself, and save whoever's beside you, and count that a miracle."

When we left at dusk, the villagers stood at their barricades and watched us go, and no one blessed us, and it was the kindest send-off we'd received in a hundred miles.

* * *

*August*

That night, in a shepherd's hut with the door barred and the fire low, Ines fell asleep against my shoulder mid-sentence, which she'd deny under torture, and I sat very still and let the world shrink to the size of her breathing.

I want to write down what I actually felt, because everything else about those weeks has gone strange in my memory, lit from below like the Hollow itself. What I felt was this: that Coram was right, and that saving myself had never once occurred to me in fifteen years of survival. Survival isn't saving. Survival is just refusing to finish dying. But sitting in the dark with Ines Corvane asleep on my shoulder, her ink-stained fingers curled near the sigil on my palm, I caught myself planning a future—small, specific, absurd. A shop. A kitchen. Her arguing with me about the placement of a window. I caught myself wanting to survive *for*, and it terrified me more than Ash or Doss or the god ever had, because everything else wanted to use me, and this wanted me to live.

The god stirred, deep in the roots it was growing through me. Not speaking. Just turning over, the way something turns over in sleep when the bed shifts. And in its turning I felt the edge of its attention slide toward the east, toward the free cities, toward the armies mustering there, and I felt something I had never felt from it before.

Hunger. Not for worship. For *arrival*.

"Ines," I whispered, before I could decide not to.

She woke the way she does everything—completely, instantly, no fog. "What is it?"

"It's excited," I said. "The god. I've felt it curious, I've felt it patient, I've felt it like a banked fire. Tonight it's excited." I looked at her in the ember-light. "Something's happening east. Something it's been waiting for."

She was quiet. Then she reached over and took my hand, sigil and all, and held it the way you hold a compass needle steady while you take a bearing.

"Then we walk faster," she said.

We broke camp before dawn. And an hour into the gray morning, on the ridge above the river road, we saw why the god was hungry: far to the east, where the free cities lay, the sky was glowing. Not sunrise. Sunrise comes up soft. This was a pillar of light and smoke, distant and steady, like a shrine burning on a scale the world hadn't seen since the Godfall.

Between us and it, on the road below, a column of pilgrims stretched to the horizon, thousands strong, walking east, singing.

Singing a name.

Even at that distance, even on the wind, I heard it, and my blood heard it, and the sigil on my palm burned like a brand:

They were singing *vessel*.
