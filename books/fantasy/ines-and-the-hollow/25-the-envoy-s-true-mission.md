## 25. The Envoy's True Mission

I have been lied to by professionals. Witch-finders, ministers, a usurper's entire court built on the principle that truth is a leak to be patched. I thought I knew every shape a lie could take.

Then the free cities' envoy smiled at me over breakfast and told me August was going to save the world, and I learned that the most dangerous lies are the ones the liar believes herself.

* * *

We had made it as far as Marrowford, which is not so much a town as an argument between a river and a road that the road is losing. Three days of smugglers' tracks behind us, August sleeping badly against my shoulder in the cart, waking sometimes with his hands glowing faintly under the blanket like coals someone had forgotten to bank. The god in him ran hot these days. He joked about it — "at least one of us will be warm this winter" — but I had started counting the hours between his fevers the way I once counted contour lines: looking for the pattern underneath.

The envoy found us at the Drowned Lamb, the only inn in Marrowford with both a roof and opinions about its wine.

She did not arrive. She *condensed*. One moment the common room held eleven people; the next it held twelve, and the twelfth was a tall woman in travel-grey with a courier's satchel across her chest and the particular stillness of someone who has crossed three borders without once being asked for papers. She chose our table without asking that either.

"Ines Corvane," she said, sitting. "Cartographer. And August Villalobos, formerly of His Majesty's kitchens." She pronounced *formerly* the way a magistrate pronounces *guilty*. "My name is Veyra Solenne. I represent the Concord of Free Cities. You've been very difficult to find."

"We weren't hiding," I said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "That's what made you difficult."

August, beside me, went very carefully still. I have watched him do this at the usurper's table a hundred times — the taster's trick of becoming furniture while everything dangerous happens around him. But Veyra Solenne was not looking at him. She was looking at me, and her smile had the patient geometry of a trap already sprung.

"You're the woman who burned her maps," she said.

The room tilted slightly. I kept my face where it was. "You've been misinformed."

"I've been *informed*, Mistress Corvane. There is a difference, though I understand why you'd confuse them, given your employer." She unfolded her hands on the table. "Doss sent a false trail north. A good one — I'll grant it craft. River fords, ferry manifests, a lovely forged receipt from a carter who does not exist. We read it, admired it, and ignored it, because we were never following Doss. We were following the god."

At the word *god*, two things happened. The lamp between us flared an inch taller. And August's hand found mine under the table, gripping hard enough to hurt.

"You knew," he said quietly. "Before we ever left the capital. You knew where we'd go."

"Mistress Corvane has spent months drawing shrines," Veyra said. "People who spend months drawing shrines eventually want to see one. It's not prophecy. It's cartography." Her eyes came to me, and there was something almost warm in them, which frightened me more than the coldness would have. "You did excellent work, by the way. The sigil overlay. We've had copies since midwinter."

I thought of my hidden drawer, my careful copies, the map I'd burned rather than surrender. Somewhere in the free cities, a stranger had unrolled my life's work and read it like instructions.

"What do you want?" August asked.

Veyra turned to him then, and her whole manner changed — softened, brightened, became something reverent. I had seen that exact transformation before, on the faces of the faithful in the Hollow district, and later on the faces of fanatics in villages where answered prayers had curdled into hunger. It is a look I have come to dread more than any weapon.

"I want to bring you home," she said.

* * *

They gave us a private room, because Veyra understood theater, and in the private room she gave us the truth, because Veyra understood that the best cages are built from honest timber.

She spread documents on the table. Real ones — I checked the seals, the watermarks, the hand. I have spent my career detecting forgery; these were genuine, which somehow made them worse.

"The Concord has been preparing for the god's return for sixty years," she began. "Since the Godfall archives were first opened to our scholars. We know what vessels are. We know what they were bred for. And we know—" here she paused, choosing her words with a mason's care, "—what the usurper has been building beneath his citadel."

"Anti-divine weapons," August said. "From the Godfall era. We know."

"You know they exist. Do you know how many?" She let the silence answer. "Neither do we. Estimates range from dozens to hundreds. What we do know is that when even one of them fires, it doesn't kill a god. It kills everything the god touches. Everything connected to it. Every shrine, every vessel, every answered prayer within—"

"How far?" I asked.

Her silence answered that too.

"So you understand the problem," she went on, gently, as if explaining weather to a child. "If the usurper fires those weapons at an army marching under divine protection, he doesn't just break the army. He breaks the free cities' faith forever. He breaks the god. He breaks—" she looked at August, and her voice did something soft and terrible, "—he breaks *you*."

"And your alternative?" August asked. His voice was level. Fifteen years of tasting other people's poison had given him that voice, and I hated how well I knew it.

Veyra drew out the last document. A single page. A diagram.

I am a cartographer. I have drawn siege plans, aqueduct routes, flood projections. I know a payload diagram when I see one, even dressed in scholarly notation. This one showed the Hollow district. It showed the network of shrines — my shrines, my lines, my sigil — arranged in a ring. And at the center, marked with a symbol I recognized from no tradition older than about sixty years, it showed a point of concentrated divine force, positioned above the usurper's muster grounds, with release vectors radiating outward.

They had drawn August as an artillery piece.

"The covenant made him a carrier," Veyra said. "The old priests designed vessels to transport divine power across distance and deliver it where it was needed. That design is still in him. All we propose is to update the delivery coordinates." She tapped the center of the ring. "He carries the god to the capital — which he can do safely, because the weapons can't fire on their own soil without destroying the citadel too, and the usurper knows it. He stands above the army. And he releases. Not a bomb, Master Villalobos. A *dawn*. Divine fire, precisely aimed, falling only on the men who built their empire on sleeping gods. The war ends in an afternoon. Tens of thousands of free cities' soldiers go home alive. And you—"

"And me," August said.

"—become the man who ended the tyranny. A saint. Literally. We'll build you cathedrals."

I watched my love listen to his own funeral described as a coronation, and I watched him do the arithmetic behind his eyes, because of course he did the arithmetic. That was the cruelty of it. The plan was *good*. That was what made it monstrous. Every number came out right except the one where August walked away afterward, and Veyra Solenne had clearly done her own sums on that column and found them acceptable.

"There's a flaw," I said.

Veyra looked at me with polite interest.

"Your diagram assumes the god is cargo," I said. "Something he carries and sets down. Has anyone asked the god?"

For the first time, the envoy's composure flickered — not much, a hairline crack, but I have spent years reading hairline cracks in things people insist are solid.

"The god is power," she said. "Power doesn't have preferences."

"Everything alive has preferences," August said quietly. "Believe me. I've been listening to one for weeks."

* * *

After she left — she took the room upstairs, courteous as a knife left handle-out — August sat by the window for a long time, watching the river argue with the road.

"They'll take us whether we agree or not," he said finally. "Won't they. That's why she found us instead of waiting for us. An escort is already on the road."

"Probably several days out."

"And if we run?"

"Then we run from everyone. The witch-finders, the usurper, the Concord, the cult, Ash." I said the last name and felt him flinch. The other vessel had been a week behind us and gaining; refugees on the road spoke of a quiet man in ash-grey whose shadow sometimes moved wrong. "There's nowhere left that isn't someone's plan for you."

He laughed — a small, real, awful laugh. "You know what the worst part is? Part of me wants to say yes. I stood over that diagram and some ancient thing in my blood sat up like a dog hearing its name. They didn't build that into the plan, Ines. They built it into *me*." He turned. The lamplight caught the faded burn-scar on his palm, the sigil I had once traced from memory onto paper and thereby, unknowingly, signed away half my life. "How do you fight a cage when part of you is the bars?"

I crossed the room and took his scarred hand in both of mine.

"Ask the god," I said. "Not the Concord. Not the prophecy. Not the part of you they bred. Ask what's actually in there. Because I've seen its work, August. The moss on dead stone. The heartbeat under the city. The way it answers prayers *gently* when you teach people to ask gently. Whatever that thing wants, I don't believe it wants to be a dawn falling on an army." I swallowed. "And I don't believe it wants to be carried anywhere by a man who dies delivering it."

"You don't know that I die."

"I've read their diagrams. I know exactly what 'release vectors' means." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. Fifteen years in that court taught me to control my face; the road had taught me I no longer wanted to. "I burned my maps for you. I lied to a witch-finder with perfect coordinates. I am not going to stand in a cathedral square and watch them aim you at anything."

He pressed his forehead to mine. Under my hands, his pulse beat hot and double — his own heart, and something older keeping time a half-step behind, like a second drummer learning the song.

"Then we need a third option," he murmured.

"We always do."

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat up with my field kit and did what I do, because the world goes mad and the pen stays sane. I redrew Veyra's diagram from memory — I have a surveyor's memory, it is the one gift my profession gave me — and then I did to her plan what I had done to Doss's archive maps. I looked for what she'd left out.

It took until the grey hour before dawn, and then I saw it, and my hands went cold around the stylus.

Every vector in her diagram radiated outward from August. Delivery, release, dispersal. But a vessel, according to the old covenant texts August had translated for me in the cart-cart, whispering like a boy with a stolen book, was never meant to *emit*. A vessel was a road, not a cannon. Power flowed *in*, was *carried*, and was *laid down* — deposited, rooted, given to the ground itself. The old shrines weren't antennas. They were graves. Beautiful ones. Places where gods had been set down like tired children and covered over with prayer.

Veyra's engineers had taken a burial rite and inverted it into a weapon. Or — worse thought, arriving like ice water — they hadn't inverted anything. They had simply never known. Sixty years of scholarship, and the free cities had studied the vessel the way the usurper studied the anti-divine weapons: as hardware. Nobody had asked the software.

Nobody had asked the god.

Except August had. Weeks ago, alone, in the dark, he had asked the question I'd told him to ask, and I remembered — I suddenly, horribly remembered — the night he'd come back from that conversation silent and shaken, and said only: *"It doesn't want worship. It wants to go home."*

Home. The Hollow. Beneath the capital. Directly beneath the usurper's citadel and its arsenal of Godfall weapons, like a spring sealed under a powder magazine.

Oh, I thought. Oh, that's why the Concord needs him delivered. Not aimed at the army.

Aimed at *that*.

I woke him with the lamp in my hand and my heart going like a drum.

"August. Wake up. Their plan isn't a bomb." I laid the redrawn diagram on the blanket between us, my lines over her lines, the truth showing through like bones under skin. "It's a key. They don't want you to destroy the arsenal. They want you to open the Hollow *while the usurper's weapons are still buried inside it* — and let the god rise straight up through them. Let the god's own rising detonate everything the tyrant ever stockpiled. One act. No soldier spends a drop of free cities' blood."

He stared at the map. The lamp flame leaned toward him, the way flames had begun to do.

"That would work," he said at last, hollow. "God help me, Ines, that would actually work. And it would take the whole city with it. Half the capital sits on the Hollow's roof."

"Yes."

"And they know that."

"I think," I said slowly, "the Concord decided a long time ago that some losses are arithmetic. The same way they decided you are." I met his eyes. "They're not your allies, August. They're just a different cage with better curtains."

Downstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, I heard the inn door open and boots on the boards — many boots, moving in the disciplined rhythm of soldiers who have traveled all night to arrive exactly on schedule. Veyra's escort, early. Or perhaps never late at all, and simply letting us finish our breakfast before the cage door closed.

August looked at the window, at the road, at the east where a second army marched behind a man named Ash, and then at me.

"Well," he said, with the weary ghost of the charm-seller's grin I'd fallen in love with, "it seems we're leaving through the kitchen."

And below us — below the inn, below the road, below all the roads in the world — something vast and warm and patient shifted in its sleep, and every lamp in the Drowned Lamb guttered once, like a held breath.
