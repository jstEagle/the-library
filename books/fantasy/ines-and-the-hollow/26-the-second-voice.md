## 26. The Second Voice

I had always assumed that if the god inside me ever spoke to someone else, it would be to complain about me.

It's the sort of assumption a man makes when he's spent fifteen years being the only one of his kind. You get proprietary about your own strangeness. You tell yourself: *I am a unique catastrophe. There is exactly one of me, and the world has learned to survive it.* Comforting, in its way. Like being the only person in a room who knows the floor is rotten — at least you know where to step.

I was wrong about the floor. I was wrong about the room. And I was very wrong about being the only one.

* * *

It happened on a night when I had gone looking for quiet, which in my experience is the universe's cue to send company.

The Hollow district after dark was the only place in the capital where nobody looked at me twice. The witch-finders had sealed three of its gates with iron chain and official seals, which — as any smuggler, lover, or priest will tell you — is just the city's way of writing *enter here* in a formal hand. I slipped through the fourth gate, the one behind the collapsed dye-works, and walked down into streets that the Godfall had buried and the god's waking had half-dug up again.

The moss was out. That was the thing nobody who hadn't been down there could understand. Dead stone, stone that had been dead for a hundred years, wearing a skin of green like a man waking with stubble. It glowed faintly where my feet fell, which should have been terrifying and was, instead, the closest thing to a homecoming I had ever felt. The god beneath the city knew me the way a hearth knows the hand that's fed it.

I'd taken to coming down to listen. Not to the god itself — that was still mostly a warmth behind my ribs, a pressure, a sense of something enormous turning over in its sleep and murmuring — but to the city above it. Prayers drifted down through the stone like grease through a cook's apron. *Let my son's fever break. Let the rent wait. Let her look at me the way she used to.* The god answered what it could, which was more than it should have, and the fires in the poor quarters burned a little brighter every week for it.

I was crouched at the edge of the old shrine square, listening to a prayer from somewhere above — *let me find him before he finds me*, which struck me as a prayer I could have written myself — when the second voice arrived.

It didn't come from the god. That's what I noticed first, before I even noticed the words. The god's presence was a warmth, a bass note, a thing you felt in your back teeth. This voice came from *beside* me, human-throated, and it said:

"You hear them too."

I did not startle. Fifteen years as a food taster teaches you that startling is a luxury for people whose bodies aren't regularly trying to kill them. But every muscle I own went to its stations, and my hand found the little knife in my sleeve, and I turned.

He was standing at the far edge of the square where the moss thinned, and I want to be fair to him: he looked like nobody. That's a professional skill, being nobody, and I recognized the craftsmanship the way a locksmith recognizes a good lock. Grey cloak, unremarkable boots, a face you'd forget between one blink and the next. Medium height, medium build, medium everything. I had spent a decade and a half perfecting that exact costume, and here was someone wearing it better than me.

"You've been following me," I said.

"Three weeks." He didn't apologize. I respected that. "You're careful. The woman who maps you is more careful. But you come down here every fifth night, and you stand in the same place, and you listen."

"The woman who maps me," I said, "is none of your business."

"No," he agreed. "But she's the reason I found you. You leave traces in her maps, did you know? Little absences. Rooms you weren't in. Streets that bend around you." He tilted his head. "She draws you by drawing where you aren't. It's rather beautiful."

Every instinct I had — and I had a good many, most of them sharpened on the whetstone of paranoia — told me to run. But there was a second instinct, newer, deeper, the one that had been growing since the night the washerwoman's charm worked without me, and it said: *stay. You know what he is.*

"You're a vessel," I said.

The moon was behind cloud, but the moss gave enough light to see his face do something complicated. Not surprise. Relief, maybe. The relief of a man who has practiced a speech for weeks and is told he can skip the first page.

"Ash," he said. "That's the name they gave me. It isn't the one I was born with, but I was born in a cellar, so the name barely counts."

"August."

"I know." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at one corner, and it frightened me more than anything the witch-finders had ever done, because I knew that smile. I wore it. It was the smile of a man being pleasant while he counted the exits. "August Villalobos. Taster to the usurper. Fifteen years of swallowing poison and smiling about it. Fifteen years of selling charms to the desperate and telling yourself they were placebos." He shook his head slowly. "They weren't placebos, were they? They were you. Leaking."

The moss under my feet brightened, and I realized with a lurch that the god was — not angry, nothing so simple — *attentive*. It had noticed him the way a dog notices another dog at the end of the street. Two doors. One house.

"Who sent you?" I asked.

"The same people who sent the nightshade."

I had traced that, in the weeks after. A cell in the palace kitchens, four cooks and a steward, all of them wearing little hammered-tin symbols under their shirts — the closed eye, the buried seed. The cult of the Hollow, or what was left of it. The poison hadn't been meant to kill. It had been meant to *wake*, and it had, and I had spent a long ugly night afterward understanding that my whole careful life had been someone else's incubation schedule.

"They wanted to see if you'd bloom," Ash said, reading my silence with uncomfortable ease. "The nightshade was a key cut to your particular lock. Old recipe. My keepers had it from their grandmothers, who had it from the last of the taster-priests." He paused. "You knew about the taster-priests, I assume. The man who bought you off your father's farm. Old Corvane's predecessor."

"You know a great deal about me."

"I should. I was raised on you." He said it without drama, which made it worse. "Every vessel line has its keepers. Yours got the palace. Mine got the cellars under the Weepers' Quarter. They prayed over me before I could walk. Fed me ash and honey. Taught me the god's hundred names and then taught me to be quiet so I could hear it better." He looked down at his own hands, turned them over, as if checking they were still his. "They were very thorough. I was a very good door."

"And now?"

"And now the door is tired of being a door." The warmth in his voice guttered, just for a moment, and I saw under it: something hollowed and hungry and very young. "You know what it's like. Don't you. You know what it's like to feel something enormous leaning on the inside of your ribs, asking, asking, asking."

I did know. I knew it so well that my throat closed around the answer, and I gave him silence instead, and he took it as the yes it was.

* * *

We walked. I don't know why. Caution said go home, bar the door, tell Ines, and I would have — except that telling Ines meant watching her face do the arithmetic of *two of them*, and I wasn't ready. So instead I walked the buried streets with the other half of my catastrophe, and he talked, and I listened, and the god beneath us listened too, and the moss glowed brighter wherever we walked together than it ever had for me alone.

"The free cities are coming," Ash said. "You've heard. Everyone's heard. An army mustering around a rumor. Do you know what the rumor is?"

"A vessel," I said. "A weapon that walks."

"A door," he corrected gently. "That's all any of us are. But doors matter, August. A door decides what comes through." He stopped at the lip of the old shrine square, where the god's heartbeat was loudest — I could feel it in my molars, that slow warm *tum, tum* that had been under the city since before the Godfall, since before the usurper's grandfather's grandfather. "They want to carry the god to the capital and open us over the usurper's army. My keepers want the same thing from the other direction. The usurper wants to capture the god and bottle it. Everyone wants the same door, opened their way."

"And you?"

He was quiet for a while. The prayer-drift from above had thinned; the city was sleeping, and even its prayers slept.

"I want it to stop *asking*," he said finally. "You don't know what it's like from my side. Fifteen years — how long for you? — of being a cup that something keeps pouring into. It doesn't mean to hurt. That's the worst part. It's a hearth fire, it's warm, it *loves*, in the way a river loves the sea, without ever once considering whether the sea wanted it. And every prayer it answers through me burns a little more of me away. I used to have a favorite color. I don't anymore. I used to be afraid of the dark. I'm not afraid of anything now, and I would give a great deal to be afraid of something again."

I thought of the burnt offering that had hummed under my hand, of my skin drinking the heat. I thought of the ledger in the guild records, fifteen years of entries in my own handwriting that I had never written. *Inherited. Stable. Contained.* Someone had been tending me like a lamp. Someone had been tending him like a lamp too, and lamps exist to be burned.

"What are you offering?" I asked. Because he was offering something; he'd walked three weeks of careful footsteps to offer it, and a man that patient doesn't waste the approach.

"Brotherhood," he said simply. "Two doors, one god. We open together or not at all. That's the only way it ends well — did your keepers teach you that? Mine did. The covenant was always meant for two. One vessel alone is a weapon that anyone can aim. Two vessels together—" he spread his hands, "—two doors, locked to each other. Nobody aims us. We choose."

"Choose what?"

"War, if it must be war." He said it the way other men say *rain on the harvest*. "The usurper's line was built on buried gods. Every throne in this city is mortared with them. Someone has to dig them up, August. Someone has to open the doors and let the old world back in, and it will be terrible, and then it will be over, and then—" he smiled again, that warm corner smile, and this time I saw the boy in the cellar underneath it, the one who'd never had a favorite color that survived, "—then it will be quiet. Don't you want it to be quiet?"

God help me, I did. That's the honest entry for the ledger. I stood in the buried square with the heartbeat of a god under my feet and the lean of something enormous against the inside of my ribs, and I wanted — more than safety, more than Ines, more than the quiet little shop I'd once imagined for myself — I wanted the asking to stop.

And that was exactly what frightened me. Because I had heard this speech before. Not from him. From every priest, every envoy, every witch-finder, every keeper: *you are the key, you are the door, you are the chosen hinge of history, open.* The words changed. The wanting-to-be-needed never did. Ash had been raised on it from the cradle, and I had been raised on it from the age of ten, and we were both standing in the dark agreeing that the cage was a nest.

"You said war *if it must be*," I said. "Who decides the must?"

He looked at me for a long moment. The moss-light moved on his face.

"The god decides," he said. "Through us. That's what vessels are *for*."

And there it was. The whole architecture of it, laid out as gently as he could lay it: two doors, one god, and neither of us holding the handle. He thought brotherhood would save us. He thought two cups poured into each other would somehow stay cups. He had been emptied so long and so thoroughly that he'd mistaken the pouring for a partnership.

"No," I said.

He didn't move. The moss didn't dim. Somewhere above us, a city slept, and beneath us something warm turned over and listened with both its ears.

"August—"

"I've been a door my whole life," I told him. "I know what walks through doors that other people open. I'm not doing it. Not for the free cities, not for your keepers, not for the god, and not for you. Whatever this is — brotherhood, covenant, war — it doesn't get to be decided *through* me. If the god wants a door, it can ask me like a person asks. And if it can't ask like a person—" I shrugged, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, "—then it can stay buried."

Something crossed Ash's face that I will be a long time forgetting. Not anger. Grief. The specific, terrible grief of a man watching the one thing he'd hoped for walk away — because I understood, suddenly, that I was the first person in his entire life who had ever said *no* to him. Not his keepers, not the god, not the prophecy. Everyone else had always said *yes, come in, pour through, we are your door.* And here was the other door, the twin, the brother, standing in the moss-light with his hand on the frame, refusing.

"You'll change your mind," he said softly. "When you see what they do to cities that have a god and no door. When you see what my half does when I stop holding it back. I don't want that, August. But it doesn't need my *wanting* to happen. It only needs my tiredness." He stepped back, and the shadows took the craftsmanship of his nobody-costume, and he became the kind of man you forget between blinks. "I'll find you again. When the army marches. We'll talk about quiet."

Then he was gone, down a street I would have sworn was a dead end, and I stood alone in the shrine square with the god's heartbeat under my feet, and I noticed — this is the part I haven't told Ines, the part I will have to tell her, because she reads my absences like handwriting — I noticed that the warmth behind my ribs had *shifted*. Leaned, very slightly, toward the direction he'd gone. The way a hearth leans toward a window left open.

Two doors. One god. And I had just learned that the god, patient as stone, warm as embers, was perfectly willing to try the other one.

I climbed out through the dye-works gate into the cold ordinary dark, and went to find Ines, and tell her the war had found its second soldier — and that he wore my face the way a key wears the shape of its lock.
