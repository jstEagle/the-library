## 27. Brother Ash

He came back the next night, which I suppose was considerate of him. Most men who want to recruit you for a holy war at least have the decency to ambush you.

I'd told Ines everything, or nearly everything — the dye-works, the voice like a hearth, the second door walking around in a borrowed body with my face's poor cousin on it. She had listened the way she listens to everything, with her whole spine, and then she'd said, "Don't meet him alone," and I had said, "Of course not," and we had both known I was lying, because that is what we do instead of holding hands.

So when the knock came at my rooms above the charm shop — three soft raps, patient as a debt collector — I opened the door alone, and there he stood in the lamplight with his hood down and rain in his hair, looking exactly like what he was: me, if I had been raised by people who wanted something from me and never once pretended otherwise.

"August Villalobos," he said. "Or should I say brother?"

"You should say Ash," I told him, "because that's your name, and flattery this early in the evening is going to make me suspicious."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if it had ever been furnished. He looked past my shoulder at the shop below, the little jars of charms I could no longer charge anyone for, since half of them were working without me now.

"I brought bread," he said, holding up a cloth bundle. "The kind with the hard crust. I remembered — no. That's wrong." He corrected himself with terrible gentleness. "I *learned* it. You like the hard crust. The god remembers liking it through you, and I get the echo."

"That's the least romantic thing anyone has ever said to me."

"It wasn't meant to be romantic." He stepped inside when I moved aside, because of course he did, because of course I let him. "It was meant to be true. I've found that truth is the only currency you can't counterfeit. Everything else — faith, loyalty, love — someone taught us those. Truth just *is*."

I took the bread anyway. It seemed rude not to, and besides, I was curious whether he'd poisoned it. He hadn't. I could tell by smell, which used to be a professional skill and is now just a habit, like flinching at bells.

We sat across from each other at my own table, two men with one face between them, and outside the rain kept up its small honest noise against the shutters.

"Tell me about the cellar," I said.

Ash set down his cup. For a long moment he didn't answer, and I watched him decide — really decide — to be honest, the way you watch a man decide to put his hand into a fire because the alternative is colder.

"There were nine of us," he said. "Born in the same cellar under a shrine in the Marrow Hills, though 'born' is generous. We were *decanted*. The cult keeps old knowledge — older than the Godfall, older than the covenant. They knew how vessels are made. Not born, made. A bloodline prayed over for six generations until the children come out thin as paper, so something big can be written on them."

"Nine," I repeated.

"Nine." He turned his cup slowly on the table, once, twice. "They prayed over us every day. That was the training, if you want to call it that. Kneeling in the dark while priests poured words into us like water into jars, seeing which of us would hold and which would crack. I cracked early. I used to hear the god in my sleep before I ever heard it awake — warm, patient, saying *soon, soon*. I thought it was a lullaby." His mouth did the almost-smile again. "It was a countdown."

"And the others?"

"Two held better than me. They went first, years ago, when the cult tried an early waking. What came out of them wasn't the god. It was the god's *hunger*, wearing their voices, asking for things." He stopped turning the cup. "The priests burned what remained and apologized to it. That's the part I've never managed to stop thinking about. They apologized. To it. As if it were the injured party."

The lamp guttered. Neither of us moved to fix it. Down in the street a cart rattled past, some carter with a load of cabbages and no idea that the fate of the empire was sitting over hard crust bread in a charm shop, deciding whether to be a weapon or a man.

"They emptied you," I said. "That's what you said last night. Emptied."

"To make room." Ash spread his hands, palms up, and I saw the mark on his palm — the sigil, the same one burned into mine during that banquet accident Ines witnessed, the same one her maps made when she laid them all together. Except his was older. Deeper. Scarred over so many times it looked less like a brand than a plowed field. "You got fifteen years of being left alone, August. Someone loved you enough — that old taster-master, whoever he was — to keep you *full*. Full of small life. Charms and hard crusts and being forgettable. Every year they didn't use you was a year they fed you instead. My people didn't believe in feeding. They believed in fasting. So when the god finally speaks through me, there's nothing in the way. No August-shaped man to muffle it."

"And that's what you want."

"That's what I *am*." He said it without pride, which was somehow worse. "You keep talking about wanting as if it's a thing I still have a supply of. I had wants once. I wanted to see the sky more than once a week. I wanted a name that wasn't a number. I wanted—" He paused, and for a heartbeat the hearth-warmth behind his eyes flickered and I saw underneath it a boy in a cellar counting cracks in the ceiling. "I wanted to matter. And then I mattered so much that nothing else of me survived. Be careful what you pray for. Someone might answer."

I got up and relit the lamp, mostly to have something to do with my hands. When I sat back down, I asked him the question I'd been carrying since the dye-works.

"Why brotherhood? Why come to me at all? You've got an army coming. You've got the free cities' envoy eating out of your hand, priests fighting over who gets to aim you. You don't need me."

"Don't I?" He leaned forward, and the lamplight made our shadows twin each other on the wall behind him, and I hated that. "One vessel carries the god like a man carries water in his hands — spilling, losing most of it on the road. Two vessels carry it like a channel. The covenant was always meant for two; ask the god yourself, it will tell you. Together we could wake it *whole*. Not the hunger that burned out of my brothers. The whole of it — the warmth, the answering, the thing that makes prayers work. Ruled through us. Guided through us. No more usurper burning shrines. No more witch-finders. No more cities where the desperate pray into silence." His voice had gone low and certain, the certainty of a man reciting something learned young. "The age of kings who starve gods ends, and the age of gods who feed people begins. Through us, August. Through *us*. All you have to do is open."

"*All*," I said.

"You make it sound like a price."

"Because it is one. I've seen what comes out of a vessel when the door opens too far. I've felt it trying to open *me* in my sleep, patient as floodwater. You're not offering brotherhood, Ash. You're offering to hold me under until I stop struggling."

He didn't flinch. That was the worst of it. A lesser zealot would have flinched, would have gotten angry, and anger I know how to fight — I've spent fifteen years surviving angry kitchens and angrier courts. But Ash just looked at me with a grief so settled it had become furniture, and said:

"I know. I've done the arithmetic too. I'm not asking you because it's painless. I'm asking because the alternative is worse, and you haven't done that arithmetic yet, because nobody ever let you near the numbers." He counted them off on scarred fingers, quiet as a ledger. "The usurper has weapons from the Godfall in his cellars. I've seen the manifests. Things built to unmake divinity, and he'll use them on the city itself rather than let the god rise. My army is forty thousand faithful who think I'm a prophet and will burn everything between here and the capital if I die unanswered. The free cities want to pour you onto a battlefield like oil. And underneath all of it, the god is waking *anyway*, August, with or without our consent, and every prayer in this city is getting answered right now by something that doesn't understand mercy yet because nobody carried it gently enough to teach it." He folded his hands. "Those are the numbers. Brotherhood isn't the good option. It's the one where fewer children burn."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I grieve," he said simply, "and march, and try to carry it alone, and probably fail, and a great many people will die of my failure. And I will still not hate you. That's the difference between us, maybe. I was never taught hatred. There wasn't room for it. There was barely room for me."

I looked at him for a long time — at my face gone strange with otherness, at the plowed-field palm, at the boy in the cellar still visible underneath the doctrine like a coin at the bottom of a well. And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I am telling you now because you have followed me this far and deserve the ugly parts: I understood him. Completely. Fifteen years of being a vessel in storage, a weapon kept oiled and ignorant, a farm boy bought and bound and called a career — I had been lied to with kindness and he had been emptied with prayer, and both of us ended up at the same table with the same face and the same mark, being asked to be doors.

The only difference between us was that someone, once, had slipped me a hard crust of bread and told me to stay invisible, and that single act of smuggled tenderness had been enough to build a man in me instead of a hinge.

"Ash," I said. "Do you remember your number?"

His head came up sharply. "What?"

"The name they gave you instead of a name. Your number. Do you remember it?"

For a long moment he said nothing, and the lamp hissed, and somewhere below us the city went on breathing. Then, very quietly, in a voice that had no doctrine in it at all:

"Seven."

"Seven," I repeated. "Not Ash. Seven. They burned eight children into tools and gave you a number, and now you want to hand them the world to rule through, because the men who emptied you told you it was holy." I leaned forward. "I'm not refusing the god, brother. I'm refusing *them*. Every priest who knelt over a cradle in that cellar. Every master who wrote 'inherited' in my file. Every king and envoy and prophet who looks at either of us and sees a key. The god asked me one question, Ash. Not *will you fight for me*. Not *will you open*. Just — *will you carry me?* Carrying means choosing the road. Doors don't choose. I choose."

He stared at me, and I watched something happen in him that frightened me more than any threat could have: hope. Small, starving, cellar-bred hope, the kind that has learned not to show itself and shows itself anyway.

"And if you carry it," he said hoarsely, "where? Where do you carry a god, August, when everyone alive wants it pointed at someone else?"

"I don't know yet," I admitted. "But I know it isn't here, and it isn't an army, and it isn't through you."

He rose. He stood there a moment with the lamplight on his ruined palm, and he looked — there's no better word for it — *bereaved*. Not defeated. Bereaved, like a man watching a ship leave that he'd convinced himself was a mirage.

"I believed you'd say yes," he said. "I need you to know that. I didn't come here to threaten you. I came because I genuinely believed that of the two of us, you were the one who'd been kept human enough to say yes." He pulled up his hood, and just like that he was a stranger again, a shape in the doorway with rain beyond. "Seven days. That's how long it takes my army to reach the walls. If you change your mind — if you ever change your mind — pray. Loudly. I'll hear it. The god makes sure of that much."

"Ash—"

"Brother," he said, from inside the hood, and it sounded like a farewell and a wound at once, and then he was gone down the stairs and into the dark, and the rain closed over him like water over a stone.

I sat at my table for a long time after, with the untouched bread between my hands, doing the arithmetic he'd left me. Forty thousand faithful. Seven days. A usurper with Godfall weapons in his cellars. A god rising whether we consented or not. And one path — mine, unwalked, unwritten — that involved carrying something enormous and warm and patient to a home I hadn't found yet, through a country full of people who all wanted it aimed.

I went downstairs, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and began to write to Ines. Halfway through the second line, boots hammered up the street outside — palace boots, four pairs, moving fast — and stopped beneath my window, and a voice I knew too well called up through the rain:

"August Villalobos! Open in the name of the Crown!"

And behind the officer, at the edge of the lamplight, stood a woman with rain streaming off her hood and her hands already bound — and even at that distance, even soaked and surrounded, Ines Corvane looked straight up at my window with an expression I had learned to read across a banquet table, across map drawers, across every lie we'd ever told each other.

It said: *don't come out. It's a trap. Let them take me.*

And it also said — because she was Ines, and honesty always leaked out of her eventually, like water finding cracks —

*I'm sorry.*
