## 29. Interrogation by Candlelight

They moved me on the third day.

I know this because I had begun counting the drips of water in the corner of my cell the way a cartographer counts contour lines—methodically, obsessively, because if you stop measuring you start screaming. Forty-one drips to the hour, give or take the ones I lost when I dozed sitting up against the wall with my wrists still cuffed above me. The usurper's dungeons did not believe in comfort. They believed in posture.

The new cell was better and worse. Better: a cot, a bucket that wasn't decorative, a door with an actual bar of light beneath it. Worse: chains anchored to the floor, which meant someone expected me to be here long enough for my legs to forget other arrangements. And there was a table. Two chairs. A candle already lit, its flame standing very straight, as though it too had been instructed to behave.

Interrogation furniture. Someone had read about me. Someone had prepared.

I sat on the cot and waited, because waiting was the one weapon they'd left me, and I intended to use it until it broke.

* * *

Serel Doss came at what I judged—by the candle's burn, by the ache in my knees—to be evening. He came alone, without his grey-cloaked witch-finders, without the little brass instruments he liked to lay out on tables like a surgeon laying out knives: the pins, the lenses, the bowl of water for scrying lies. Just Doss, a ledger under one arm, and a second candle he set beside the first so that the light doubled and my shadow fell twice against the wall.

"Cartographer Corvane." He said my name the way he'd said it in the archive, months ago, when he requisitioned my maps and I handed him fakes I'd spent years preparing. "You look terrible."

"You always did have a gift for observation."

He almost smiled. Almost. It was strange, seeing him without the cloak and the retinue—he looked smaller, and older, and tired in a way that didn't come from lack of sleep but from something heavier, something carried. His eyes went to my chained wrists, then away, as if the sight cost him something he'd rather not spend.

"I'm going to ask you questions," he said. "And you're going to answer them. And then one of two things happens."

"Yes?"

"One: you tell me where Villalobos is, what he carries, who else knows. You cooperate fully. In return, your sentence is commuted from treason to misprision. A fine. Perhaps a posting somewhere cold and far from maps."

"And two?"

"Two," Doss said, and sat down across from me, folding his hands on the table like a man settling in for a long sermon, "we continue these conversations nightly until you break, and then we learn everything anyway, and you get nothing. The crown is patient. I am less so, lately, but the crown makes up the difference."

"That's not much of a choice."

"It's the only kind the palace sells." He opened the ledger. "Let's begin with the Hollow district. Your survey of the sunken quarter, dated four years past. You marked eleven shrines as ruined. My field teams found nine of them intact. Explain."

"They were intact when I surveyed them," I said. "Ruins decay unevenly. Stone falls, stone stands. Any mason could tell you that."

"Any mason," Doss repeated. "But you're not a mason. You're the best mapmaker this court has produced in thirty years, and the best mapmaker this court has produced does not make errors of that magnitude. Nine shrines. All mislabeled. All along footpaths that converge on the Hollow's center. That's not decay, Corvane. That's design."

I said nothing. Silence, in interrogation, is a currency; spend it too fast and you're bankrupt by midnight.

Doss waited me out. He was good at it. He'd been good at it in the archive too, standing over my shoulder while I unrolled maps for him, watching my hands, watching my eyes, missing nothing except—thank every dead god—the drawer where I kept the truth.

"The shrine at Cinders Row," he said. "Your original survey notes describe a collapsed roof and standing water. My team found a living congregation. Fresh rushes on the floor. A fire pit that had been used within the week. Families, Corvane. Children." His voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Doss; it never rose. "You knew."

"I knew there were squatters in old buildings. Squatters are common in the lower quarters. The crown's rents being what they are."

"Families praying to the god beneath the city."

"If they were, they were breaking the law, and the law has remedies, and I'm sure your teams applied them." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the voice I used for describing coastlines. "I map terrain, Serel. What people do in the terrain is not my department."

"No," he said softly. "Your department is knowing exactly where everyone will be before they know it themselves."

* * *

He worked through the ledger for two hours. Question after question, each one precise, each one a small scalpel: the date I first surveyed the Hollow, the date I copied the shrine network into my private folio, the names of anyone I'd shown it to, whether I had ever met August Villalobos outside official functions, whether I had ever met him inside them, what we discussed at the autumn banquet, why my records showed me requesting taster's-guild staffing rosters—a request I had, in fact, made, and had made stupidly, and had spent three sleepless nights regretting.

I lied throughout. I lied with geometry, with dates, with the dry music of coordinates. I told him I'd requested the rosters to cross-reference kitchen deliveries against my supply-route maps of the palace district—which was even true, in the sense that it was the excuse I'd written down at the time, and the best lies are true things wearing stolen coats. I told him Villalobos was a dull man I'd spoken to perhaps six times, that he talked about spices, that I'd found him forgettable.

That last one hurt to say. Not because it was dangerous—it was safe, it was the safest thing I said all night—but because saying it felt like a small betrayal, and I was beginning to keep a running tally of those, and the tally was getting long.

Doss closed the ledger near midnight. Or what I judged to be midnight. The candles had burned down to stubs, and my shadow had stopped moving on the wall, and my wrists ached in a way that had become conversational background noise.

"Well?" I said.

"Well," he said, and didn't move. He sat looking at the candle flame, and for a long moment the witch-finder of the capital—the man who burned shrines and broke networks and whose name mothers used to frighten children into orthodoxy—looked, frankly, exhausted.

"You're wondering why I came alone," he said.

"The thought crossed my mind."

"I've done this work nineteen years." He turned the ledger over in his hands, not reading it, just holding it. "Nineteen years hunting the old faiths, the hidden priests, the vessels, the charms. And in nineteen years I have held to a simple principle: the gods are gone or sleeping, and everything that claims otherwise is either fraud or ambition, and both deserve the pyre. Fraud deceives people. Ambition kills them. Either way, the lie spreads faster than the truth can stamp it out."

"And now?"

"Now I stood in a shrine on Cinders Row," Doss said, "and watched a woman pray over her fevered daughter, and the fever broke while I watched. Not medicine. Not trickery—I checked for trickery, Corvane, I checked for twenty minutes, I have checked every night since. The fever broke because she asked it to. And the child woke up and asked for bread." He set the ledger down. "I have burned forty-one shrines in my career. I would like to know how many of them were warm."

The cell was quiet. Somewhere above us, the palace went on with its insomnia—boots in corridors, a far-off door. Underneath it all, faint as a pulse through stone, I thought I felt it again: that slow deep rhythm I'd heard once in the Hollow, the heartbeat under the city. Or I imagined it. By then I couldn't always tell.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because tomorrow the questioning changes hands." He said it evenly, but I saw his jaw tighten. "His Majesty has taken a personal interest in your case. Not in you—in what you know. There are men coming who do not use candles and ledgers. They use other things. I have seen their results." A pause. "I signed the order transferring you to them. My signature. My seal."

"Then why come here tonight?"

"Because I wanted to look at you first." He stood, finally, and pushed the chair back with his boot. "Nineteen years, Corvane. Do you know what I learned in nineteen years? That the crown doesn't fear the old gods. The crown fears anything it cannot own. And I have just discovered that the thing waking under this city cannot be owned—and that His Majesty intends to try anyway. Chains. Engines from the Godfall. Whatever else the vaults hold." He shook his head slowly. "You cannot chain a wellspring. You can only poison it. And poisoned wells don't distinguish between kings and everyone else who drinks."

My heart was doing something undignified. I kept my face still, because faces were the last equipment I had left.

"You sound like a convert," I said.

"I sound like a man who reads his own reports." He gathered the ledger, tucked it under his arm, and walked toward the door. Then he stopped. He stood with his back to me for a moment, and when he spoke again his voice was lower, stripped of the interrogator's polish, just a tired man in a stone room.

"There's a loose bar in the window grate above the water channel. Third from the left. It's been loose for some time—old iron, poor maintenance. The night guard rotates at the fourth bell, and the eastern corridor is unwatched for roughly the length of a candle. The channel runs down to the river gate, and the river gate grate was removed last month for repairs that have not yet occurred." He recited it all flatly, like reading coordinates off a chart. Then: "I know you're a cartographer. I assume you can find your way along a watercourse."

"Why?" The word came out of me raw. "Why would you—after Cinders Row, after everything, why give me this?"

He didn't turn around. "Because I put families into a burning shrine," he said, "and told myself it was necessary, and the telling has stopped working. Because whatever Villalobos is carrying, he hasn't aimed it at anyone yet, and everyone who wants to aim it—including the man whose seal I wear—should be slowed down. And because—" here he paused, and I heard something in the pause that I hadn't expected to hear from Serel Doss in a hundred years of pausing, "—because you lied to me beautifully, Corvane, and I have spent nineteen years wishing more people would. The ones who lie to me are usually the ones trying to save something."

Then he opened the door, and the corridor light spilled in, and he was gone, and I sat alone with two guttering candles and a set of escape instructions I had no right to trust.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I tested the chains instead—out of habit, out of spite—and found them exactly as loose as a man's conscience might leave them. The anchor bolt turned a half-circle in its socket. The cuff's rivet had been filed, subtly, expertly, by someone who understood iron the way I understood ink. Enough to work free in an hour of quiet effort. Not enough to be obvious to any casual inspection. Grace, when it comes from unlikely hands, arrives disguised as negligence.

I sat back against the wall and thought about Doss. About the shrine on Cinders Row—I hadn't known he'd been there, hadn't known he'd watched a fever break, and the knowledge rearranged him in my mind the way new survey data rearranges a coastline. For fifteen years I had filed Serel Doss under threat, under hunter, under the reason I kept false maps in my public drawers. He was still all of those things. But he was also, apparently, a man who had built his life on a certainty and then watched the certainty perform a miracle in front of him, and who had responded not with fanaticism but with doubt—and doubt, in a witch-finder, is the most dangerous substance in the palace. More dangerous than nightshade. More dangerous than gods.

He could be lying, of course. This could be the longest game ever played: free the prisoner, follow the prisoner, let her lead them to Villalobos, to the vessel, to the thing the crown wanted to own. Every piece of his confession could be bait. Nineteen years of craft says he's capable of it.

But he'd signed the transfer order. If he wanted to play me, he had easier boards.

And there was the other thing, the thing I kept turning over like a coin worn smooth: *the ones who lie to me are usually the ones trying to save something.* He'd known. Maybe not everything—but he'd known I was lying, all those months ago in the archive, handing him my beautiful fakes, and he'd taken them anyway, and filed them, and said nothing. How long had he been choosing not to see? How many of my forged shrine reports had passed across his desk and been accepted with a nod? I had thought I was deceiving him. Perhaps we had been collaborating, in the way of two people on opposite sides of a wall who both pretend not to hear the other digging.

The fourth bell came. I heard the guard rotation—bootsteps changing pitch, a door, a yawn. The eastern corridor went quiet.

I worked the rivet. Iron is honest material; it resists, then gives, then resists again, and if you listen to it, it tells you the truth about itself. The cuff came free with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a gunshot. I froze. Counted forty drips. Nothing came.

Then I stood—for the first time in days, upright, my knees reporting their complaints—and I looked at the open cell door, and the dark corridor beyond it, and the route Doss had drawn for me in words: window, channel, river gate, night.

August was out there. Somewhere between the free cities and this prison, carrying half a god and a war he never asked for, probably convinced by now that I was dead or worse. And Ash was marching. And the usurper was sharpening engines from the Godfall. And every faction in the world wanted the same man aimed at every other faction.

I had one candle's length of darkness, a water channel, and a debt to a hunter who had broken his own covenant to pay it.

I blew out the candles, and stepped through the door, and began to map my way out of the dark.
