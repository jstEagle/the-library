## 30. The Gathering Storm

The free cities smelled like every city that has ever called itself free: salt, smoke, money, and the particular incense of people who have recently discovered a religious opinion.

I came through the Salt Gate at dusk, in the company of a wool merchant who had charged me passage and then spent six days telling me about his gout. He was the last person alive who didn't know what I was. I found myself reluctant to leave his cart. Behind us, the road we'd traveled was full of fires — villages where prayers had been answered too enthusiastically, where the god's impatience had leaked up through me like water through a cracked jar. Ahead of us, the walls of Vellharbor rose black against a sky bruised orange by a hundred signal pyres.

"Gods," said the merchant, squinting at the pyres. "War's come early this year."

"Not war," I said. "Auditions."

He laughed. I hadn't meant it as a joke.

* * *

You should understand something about the free cities, because I only half understood it myself, and understanding arrived the way most of my education did: painfully, and in public.

The five cities — Vellharbor, Ostmere, Cael Row, the Twin Sarns, and little bitter Kessick up the coast — had spent three hundred years being very proud of having no king. What they had instead was councils, guilds, banks, and a standing argument. They had also, once upon a time, been the places the old gods kept as favorites, before the Godfall broke the shrines and the usurper's line taught everyone that divinity was best handled the way you handle a snake: from a distance, with a stick, and preferably dead.

So when the rumor crossed the mountains — *a vessel walks, a man who cannot be poisoned, the god rides him like a rider rides a horse* — the free cities did not fall to their knees.

They fell to their ledgers.

By the time I reached Vellharbor, there were four armies mustering on the plain outside the walls, and every one of them was there to protect me. That is not sarcasm. That is the exact word each general used. *Protect.* The Ostmere column flew banners of the Hearthfather, the hearth-god whose voice I'd heard beneath the Hollow, warm as coals. The Vellharbor militia flew no god at all, just the city's chained-anchor sigil, because Vellharbor's council had decided the vessel belonged to whichever polity could guarantee his safety, and they intended to guarantee it harder than anyone else. The third army was a confederation of temple militias from Cael Row, who had gotten wind of me through channels I still don't fully understand and had marched two hundred miles singing. And the fourth —

The fourth was smaller. Quieter. Camped apart, under grey banners with nothing sewn on them at all. Nobody would tell me who they were. Everybody watched them the way you watch a dog that isn't barking.

I stood on the Salt Gate's walkway looking down at all of it, and the thought that arrived was not awe. It was the thought I used to have standing in the palace kitchens, watching a tray go out to the usurper's table: *somewhere in all this machinery, I am an ingredient.*

* * *

They found me within the hour, which should tell you everything. Fifteen years of practice at being invisible, undone by a rumor traveling faster than I could walk.

A delegation met me at the gatehouse — I say *delegation* because it was eleven people arguing in three languages before they'd finished introducing themselves. There was a priest of the Hearthfather named Brother Anselm, round and sweating and sincere, who took my hands in both of his and said, "Vessel," with such naked reverence that my skin crawled. There was a woman in Vellharbor council colors named Magistrate Ferren who did not take my hands but did look at them the way an appraiser looks at silver. There were two men from the silent grey-banner camp who said nothing at all and made the other nine unconsciously angle their bodies away.

And there was a boy, maybe sixteen, holding a lantern, who looked at me and burst into tears.

"He answered my sister," the boy said, when he could speak. "The fever prayer. In Marrowfield. She was dying and we prayed at the old stone and she sat up in the night." He was crying and grinning at once, and the whole delegation went quiet around him, and I felt the weight of every eye turn back to me like a hand pressing down on the crown of my head.

"I didn't do that," I said.

"You carry Him," said Brother Anselm gently. "It is the same."

"It is not the same," I said, and heard how it sounded, and shut my mouth.

Magistrate Ferren smiled her appraiser's smile. "Come inside, sir," she said. "You've had a long road. Let the cities argue over your comfort while you sleep."

It was beautifully put. It was also, translated out of diplomatic, exactly what it was: *let the cities argue over ownership while you're locked somewhere convenient.*

I went. Of course I went. A man who has spent fifteen years tasting other people's food knows when refusing a meal will cost more than eating it.

* * *

They gave me a house. Not a room — a house, on Lanternmere Square, with a cook, a guard detail of twelve, and windows that had been measured for shutters while I was still crossing the plain. The shutters went up the second morning. For my protection, they said. Against assassins, they said. The shutters were on the *outside.*

On the first evening, Brother Anselm brought supper personally and watched me eat it. I want to be fair to Anselm; I think he genuinely loved his god and genuinely believed he loved me, and couldn't feel the difference between those two things, which is the whole tragedy of faith in one sentence. He watched me eat and his eyes shone.

"You don't need to test it," he said. "We know."

"That's rather the point of testing," I said. "The knowing."

He laughed like I'd made a charming joke. Then, leaning in, dropping his voice: "The Hearthfather stirs in you. I can see it. When you slept on the road — our scouts said the lamps near your camp burned blue. That's His color, vessel. That's His sign." He gripped my wrist, warm and damp. "Ostmere has waited three hundred years to give Him back His cities. Say the word and the Hearthfather's host marches tomorrow. Say the word."

"Say the word to whom?" I asked. "Him? Or me?"

Anselm blinked. For one moment something moved behind his face — doubt, maybe, or its cousin, fear — and then the shine came back and he patted my wrist like I was a good horse and left.

I sat in my shuttered house and listened to the god under my ribs, that hearth-warm presence that had been growing louder since the Hollow, since Ash, since the road. It wasn't speaking. It never spoke in words unless I asked directly. But it was *leaning*, the way a dog leans against a door it wants opened. Toward the armies. Toward the noise. Toward the ten thousand throats outside the walls singing hymns with the wrong words in them.

*Don't,* I told it silently. *Whatever you're thinking. Don't.*

The warmth pulsed once, patient, unreadable, and settled again like embers banking for the night.

That frightened me more than shouting would have.

* * *

The second day brought the priests of Cael Row, and the third day brought the lawyers, and I honestly couldn't tell you which was worse.

The Cael Row delegation was led by a high cantor named Seraphine Ott, a narrow woman with a voice like a blade being drawn slowly, and she did not want my blessing. She wanted my *provenance*. They had records, she informed me, going back past the Godfall. Records of vessels. Records of the breeding programs the old priesthoods had run in the last centuries before the Fall, when the gods grew desperate and desperate gods make poor shepherds. She laid a vellum genealogy on my table, older than the usurper's dynasty, older than the tasters' guild, and pointed to a name written in faded iron-gall ink.

*Villalobos.*

My family name. On a list of vessels. Dated a hundred and forty years before my grandfather was born.

"We don't dispute Ostmere's claim of affinity," Cantor Ott said, in the tone of a lawyer conceding a technicality. "The Hearthfather may indeed favor you. But custody is another matter. The vessel is a sacred instrument, and instruments belong to the tradition that made them. Cael Row made you, Villalobos. Your line was ours before it was anyone's. We have the contracts."

"Contracts," I repeated.

"The vessels' families swore service. Blood oaths. Binding on heirs." She folded her hands. "You are an heir."

I looked at the vellum. Somewhere behind my eyes, the memory of the tasters' guild ledger rose up — fifteen years of entries in handwriting that was mine and wasn't, someone managing me, someone keeping accounts. It seemed I had always been an entry in somebody's book. The usurper's book. The old priest's book. Now a cantor's book, three centuries old, waiting patiently for me to grow up into it.

"Did the contract say," I asked, "what happens if the instrument objects?"

Something flickered across her face — the same flicker Anselm had shown, that brief animal surprise of believers meeting a tool that talks back. "The covenant does not provide for objection," she said.

"No," I agreed. "It never does."

* * *

The lawyers came from Vellharbor council, and they were almost a relief, because at least they were honest about wanting to own me the way you own a lighthouse: publicly, expensively, and for the good of shipping. Magistrate Ferren herself led them. Over tea — which I tasted carefully out of habit, and which was merely expensive — she laid out the council's position with the serenity of a woman reading a weather report.

"The plain hosts four armies, Mr. Villalobos. Three of them are here for you. The fourth—" she paused delicately "—is here for you too, though their masters prefer not to advertise. If you attach yourself to any one faction, the others will move. If you attach yourself to none, they may move anyway. The council proposes neutrality under guarantee. You remain in Vellharbor, under our protection, as a civic trust. Neither god nor crown nor temple holds you. The city does."

"A civic trust," I said. "Like a bridge."

"If you like."

"And when the war comes — and it's coming, magistrate, you can smell it on those four camps the way you smell rain — whose side does the bridge stand on?"

Ferren sipped her tea. "Bridges," she said, "stand where they're built. Armies cross them or burn them. That is not the bridge's affair."

I had to give her points for candor. I even liked her, in the way you can like someone who would sell you without malice, the way a tide comes in without malice. But walking back through my shuttered house afterward, past the twelve guards who guarded me from my own choices, I finally put a name to the feeling that had been sitting on my chest since the Salt Gate.

It wasn't fear. Fear I knew; fear was an old colleague. This was worse. This was *claustrophobia* — the specific horror of a man who has spent his whole life being small enough to slip through cracks, discovering that destiny is a room, and the room is shrinking, and every wall is painted with his own name by people who each believe they painted it first.

Every faction wanted the same thing. That was the joke of it. Anselm wanted a weapon for his god. Ott wanted an heirloom for her tradition. Ferren wanted an asset for her city. The grey banners wanted — something quieter and worse. Even Ash, even the cult that had brewed nightshade into a king's breakfast, had wanted the same thing: *him, aimed.* Point me at an enemy and let go. Nobody, in four armies and five cities and three hundred years of contracts, had ever once asked what I wanted to be when I was not being fired at anything.

Except, I realized, standing alone in the shuttered dark, one party to these negotiations.

And it lived under my ribs, and it was leaning toward the armies, and I was afraid to ask it why.

* * *

On the fifth night, the grey banners finally sent someone.

She came without ceremony — a knock at the kitchen door, which amused me; after all the front-door diplomacy, the quiet people knew to use the servants' entrance. She was old, grey-cloaked, with a face like a dried riverbed and eyes that had done a great deal of watching. She set a single object on my kitchen table: a clay lamp, unlit, of a style I recognized with a jolt that went through me like cold water.

Hollow work. Sunken-quarter pottery, pre-Godfall, the kind Ines had shown me sketches of in another life, in another city, before everything.

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

"We dig," she said simply. "We have always dug. We are the ones who stayed near the buried places when the temples moved to the hills and the kings moved to the palaces. We keep the doors that nobody prays at anymore." She turned the lamp slowly. "Three weeks ago, the doors began to hum. All of them. Every buried thing in every buried place, humming like a struck bell that won't finish ringing." Her riverbed face tilted up at me. "That was you, wasn't it. Or in you. Or through you."

I said nothing. My silence said plenty.

"The temples think you're a sword," she said. "The cities think you're a treasury. The boy at the gate thinks you're a saint." She rose, stiff-backed, and gathered her cloak. "We dug up enough of the old world to know what vessels were for, Villalobos. We dug up the instructions. So before you let any of them aim you — before you let *it* lean you anywhere — you might want to read them."

"What instructions?" I said. "What are you talking about?"

She paused at the kitchen door, and for a moment the lamplight caught something in her expression that I have spent my life learning to read in other people's faces, because reading faces was how a forgettable man survived a poisoner's court.

Pity.

"Ask it," she said. "Not what it wants you to do. Ask it what it wants *done with it.* There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and none of those armies out there knows it exists." She pulled her hood up. "Read the instructions, vessel. Before you become the weapon they were written to prevent — or the one they were written to make."

Then she was gone, into the shuttered dark of Lanternmere Square, and I stood alone in my kitchen with a dead lamp from a drowned quarter of the world, listening to four armies breathe beyond the walls and one god breathe beneath my ribs.

The warmth stirred. Patient. Leaning.

"All right," I whispered to it, to the hearth-fire voice that had called me *vessel* in the dark beneath the Hollow. "No more armies. No more contracts. No more priests."

I put my hand flat on the table, over the hollow clay lamp, and closed my eyes.

"Tell me what you actually want."

The fire under my skin drew breath — and began, at last, to answer.
