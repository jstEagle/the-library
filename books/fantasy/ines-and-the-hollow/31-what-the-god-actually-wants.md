## 31. What the God Actually Wants

The free cities called it a sanctuary. It was a room with a bed in it, and a lock on the outside of the door.

I want to be fair to them. The bed was good—goose down, clean linen, the first mattress I'd slept on since leaving the capital that didn't smell of river or fear. There was a window, high and narrow, that looked out on a bell tower and a slice of sky the color of pewter. Someone had left a carafe of watered wine and a plate of figs, which was either hospitality or a bribe, and after thirty years at court I'd learned there's rarely a difference.

But the lock was on the outside, and I had counted four men passing my door in the first hour, and when I'd asked the young priest who brought my supper what my schedule would be tomorrow, he'd said "the Synod will inform you," in the tone of a man informing a cask of gunpowder of its schedule.

So: alone at last. Which is what I'd been asking for, in the way you ask for things by being so relentlessly pleasant that people give you a locked room just to be rid of you.

I sat on the edge of the excellent bed, ate two figs because even prisoners should eat well when the prison provides, and said, out loud, to the empty air:

"Right. You and I need to talk."

* * *

You should understand something about carrying a god for six months. It's not like carrying a passenger. It's not even like carrying a tenant. It's like living next to a cathedral organ that someone is always, gently, playing—one low note, held so long you stop hearing it, until the night everything goes quiet and you realize the silence is louder than the note ever was.

That's how I knew something had changed. Since the Hollow. Since Ash poured his half into me—or into the space between us, I still don't have the vocabulary—and since I'd walked out of that burning orchard with an army's worth of divinity folded up inside my ribs like a letter I couldn't read, the note had been constant. A warmth behind my sternum. A pressure, patient as tide.

Tonight the pressure was different. Not gone. *Waiting.*

"Everyone wants something from me," I told the empty room. "The Synod wants a weapon. The priests want a relic they can argue over. The envoy wanted a bomb—I know, she told me herself, bless her honesty, it's the only honesty I've gotten in weeks. The usurper wanted a dynasty. Ash wanted a god-king. My old master wanted—" I stopped. Swallowed. "My old master wanted a vessel kept safe until the hour came. Fifteen years of small kindnesses, all of it logistics."

The warmth behind my sternum did nothing dramatic. It never did anything dramatic. That was the thing nobody understood about it—not the Synod, not the prophets crowding the streets below my window, not the armies mustering in the fields outside the walls. They all assumed a waking god would be thunder. Fire from heaven, voices in whirlwinds, the whole theatrical repertoire.

But I'd tasted this god's attention every morning for fifteen years without knowing it, the way you taste almond in a sauce and think *marzipan*. It didn't thunder. It *simmered*. It was hearth-fire, banked coals, bread rising in a warm kitchen. When it spoke—and it spoke now, not in words exactly, but in the way a memory of your grandmother's house speaks, all at once and everywhere—

*I am listening.*

I flinched anyway. Thirty years of practice and I still flinched. But then I made myself sit still, hands flat on my knees like a man at confession, which, in fairness, is what this was.

"I'm going to ask you a question," I said. "And I've been a professional liar for most of my life, so believe me when I say I'll know if you dodge."

*Ask.*

"The free cities are going to march me to the capital. Or try to. Ash is already marching, with half of you in him and an army under him, and cities are burning where prayers go unanswered, and every mile he walks he's doing it in your name. The Synod down the hall thinks if they aim me correctly—at the usurper, at his stored weapons, at whatever's left of the Godfall arsenal—they can end the war in an afternoon and start a thousand years of blessed empire." I took a breath. "So here's the question. What do you actually *want?*"

Silence. The real kind, the kind with weight.

"Not what do the prophets say you want," I pressed. "Not what the covenant says, not what the vessels were bred for, not what anyone's ledger says. You. The thing under the city. What. Do. You. Want."

And the god of the Hollow—the old one, the buried one, the one whose shrines Ines had mapped in footpaths and moss and heartbeats under stone—that god answered me.

Not in words. Gods don't use words; words are a human invention, like taxes and betrayal. It answered in images, poured straight into the back of my eyes, and I sat very still on my excellent bed and let it show me, because I had asked, and because some part of me had been asking my whole life without knowing it.

* * *

It showed me the Hollow before it was the Hollow.

A valley. Green, unremarkable, the kind of place you'd walk through without remembering. And in the valley, a fire—a small one, a cookfire, and around it people. Not worshippers. Neighbors. They were arguing about a fence line, laughing about a bad harvest, setting out bowls. And the god—it wasn't a god then, or it was, but it was small, the way a hearth is small—sat among them the way heat sits in a room. Present. Useful. *Home.*

It showed me centuries of that. Bowls set out. Prayers said the way you talk to family—casually, constantly, without ceremony. *Warm the bread. Keep the child through the fever. Let the rain come gentle.* Small prayers, and small answers, and no one kneeling, because you don't kneel to your own hearth.

Then it showed me the Godfall.

I won't describe it fully. Some things shouldn't be carried secondhand. But I saw the sky crack, and I saw gods die or flee or be *hunted*, and I saw the small hearth-god of an unremarkable valley make the only choice left to it: it went down. Into the earth. Under its own valley, folding itself small, banking itself like a fire against a long night, and it sealed the way behind it and waited for the world above to forget.

And the world forgot. Mostly. Except that forgetting leaves scars, and scars leave footpaths, and footpaths lead cartographers somewhere, but that's Ines's part of the story.

Here is the part that matters. As it sank, as it buried itself alive beneath the stones of its own valley, the god did one more thing. It reached out—not upward, not to the sky where the hunters were, but outward, thin and desperate, into the blood of the people who had set out bowls for it. And it asked them:

*Carry me. Not my power. Just the memory of me. Carry the shape of me in your blood, so that someday, when it's safe, I can find my way home through you.*

And the people—who were grieving, who were terrified, who loved their hearth-god the way you love a grandmother—said yes. They didn't understand what they were agreeing to. They thought they were agreeing to remember. But a covenant made in desperation is a blunt instrument, and over generations the *remembering* became a *carrying*, and the carrying became a *breeding*, and somewhere along the way the descendants of those grieving farmers started being born with hollows inside them shaped exactly like a god, and priests—first kind ones, then ambitious ones, then the taster-master who bought a poison-proof farm boy off a cart—started managing those children like vaults.

"They turned a rescue into a weapon rack," I said aloud. My voice cracked on it. "Fifteen generations of children, bred to be doors."

*Yes,* said the god, and the grief in it was the grief of a hearth watching its own family burn the house down to forge swords.

"So when Ash says you should rule—"

*He carries my power. He does not carry my memory. There is a difference, and it is the width of the world.*

"And when the Synod says you should fight—"

*They carry ambition. They do not carry me at all.*

I sat with that. Down the hall, a door opened and closed; footsteps passed; the lock on the outside of my door rattled once, checking. I waited for it to move on.

"Then tell me plainly," I said. "No images. If you could have one thing—one—what is it?"

And the god, the old god of the Hollow, the last god of the Godfall, the thing three armies and two empires and one very tired food-taster were currently fighting over, answered with the plainest want I had ever heard in my life. I've heard a lot of wants. Kings' wants, priests' wants, desperate washerwomen's wants. I know wants the way a sommelier knows vintages.

It wanted to go home. To be carried home—to the sunken quarter under the capital, to the valley that had become a district that had become a grave—and to be *laid down*. Set into the earth the way you lay a tired child into bed. Not risen. Not enthroned. Not aimed. Laid down, and let to sleep, and let its warmth seep slow and gentle into the stone and the roots and the water table, so that for a thousand years afterward the wells would run sweet and the bread would rise well and the fevers would break, and no one would ever quite know why, and no one would ever have to kneel.

And it wanted the covenant ended. The breeding, the vessels, the children born with holes in them. It wanted the doors closed behind it. All of them. Including me.

"You want to stop existing," I said. "As a power. As a player. You want to become... weather. A blessing people inherit without knowing."

*A hearth does not rule the house,* it said. *It keeps the house. That was always all I was. Everything else was done in my name by people who mistook warmth for hunger.*

I laughed. I couldn't help it—it came out of me sideways, ragged, half sob. Because I'd spent six months assuming that everyone wanted the same thing, that wanting was universal, that the god underneath everything must want what kings want: more. More worship, more territory, more years. The usurper wanted eternity. The Synod wanted victory. Ash wanted purpose. The envoy wanted a detonation.

And the actual god wanted what a tired old servant wants at the end of a long shift. To put down the load. To go home. To sleep.

"Fifteen years," I said. "I tasted poison for a tyrant so I could fund a quiet life, and the whole time I was carrying a god who wanted the same thing I wanted. A quiet life. A warm kitchen. To be left alone." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand. "Do you know what they'll do when I tell them? The Synod? The generals?"

*They will not believe you.*

"No," I agreed. "Nobody believes the answer is 'nothing.' Nobody believes the treasure is a nap. Power always assumes power is the point." I stood up, because sitting felt suddenly unbearable, and paced the little room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the way I used to pace the tasters' quarters between courses. "There's a problem, though. A practical one. Laying you down—pouring you into the Hollow instead of into an army—that takes a vessel opening himself completely. Both halves. Me and Ash together, probably, since he's carrying the other portion and I doubt you split cleanly. And Ash doesn't want to lay you down. Ash wants to march you to a throne."

*Ash can be shown,* the god said, and there was something in it I hadn't heard before—something almost shy. *He has carried only my power, and power alone makes a poor counselor. But he was also bred, also emptied, also kept. He is lonelier than he is hungry. He simply has never been offered anything except thrones.*

"He was offered brotherhood," I said. "By me. He grieved instead of answering."

*Offer him the truth, then. Not brotherhood. An ending. No vessel who hears what you have just heard chooses the throne twice.*

I stopped pacing. Outside, the bell tower rang the third hour past midnight, and somewhere below, in the streets of the free cities, the faithful were holding candlelit vigils for a god who, if they got their way, would never be allowed to sleep again.

"One more question," I said. "The selfish one. If we pour you out—all of you, everything—what's left in the vessel?" I put my hand flat against my sternum, against the banked warmth that had kept me poison-proof and untouchable and *safe* for thirty years. "Because I know what's left. I've known since the orchard. Nothing. Mortal. Ordinary. The next cup of nightshade kills me like it kills anyone."

The god was quiet a long moment. Then, gently, the way you'd tell someone a hard truth across a kitchen table:

*Yes.*

"And you're asking anyway."

*I am not asking. I have never asked. That was the sin of the covenant—no one asked the farmers, no one asked the children, no one asked you.* The warmth behind my sternum shifted, and for the first time it felt less like a tenant and more like a hand resting briefly on my shoulder. *But I will tell you what I told no one else, in all the long years underground: the choice was always meant to be yours. That is what a vessel was supposed to be—not a door, not a weapon. A volunteer.*

I stood in my locked sanctuary in the free cities, mortal-to-be, hunted by everyone, in love with a woman a hundred leagues away in a tyrant's citadel, and I found—to my considerable surprise—that I wasn't afraid.

Well. I was afraid. I'm a coward by trade; fear is a professional tool, like a palate. But underneath the fear was something steadier. Because for thirty years my rule had been *be invisible*, and for six months my curse had been *be the most visible object in the world*, and here, finally, was an ending that matched the beginning: a quiet life. Not mine—a god's—but I'd spent long enough serving other people's quiet lives to know the work when I saw it.

"All right," I said. "Here's what happens tomorrow. I'm going to stand up in front of your Synod—your precious Synod, with their incense and their ledgers—and I'm going to tell them what you want. And when they don't believe me, and they won't, I'm going to walk out of their sanctuary, past their four guards, and I'm going to go collect the other half of you from a sad young man marching an army toward a throne. And then we're all going home to the Hollow."

*They will call it treason,* the god observed.

"They call everything treason. It's the cheapest word in politics." I picked up the last fig, considered it, and ate it. Even a man planning to spend his mortality deserves dessert. "One condition."

*Name it.*

"When it's done—when you're laid down and the wells run sweet and nobody remembers why—someone should keep a shop nearby. Something small. Charms, maybe. Legal ones." I smiled at the empty room, at the god, at the whole ridiculous arrangement of my life. "Someone should be there when the prayers come in. Slowly. By people helping people. Someone who knows the trade."

The warmth behind my sternum did something I'd never felt it do before.

It laughed.

* * *

I was still smiling when the key turned in the outside lock at dawn and the young priest came in with my breakfast tray and my schedule: an audience with the Synod at midmorning, the envoy attending, the generals attending, questions to be answered regarding my readiness.

"Before you go," I said pleasantly, taking the tray. "A theological question, free of charge. If a god told you it didn't want to win—would you believe it?"

The young priest blinked at me. "Gods want to be served," he said. "Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," I agreed, and tasted the porridge out of habit, and found nothing in it but oats and honey, and thought: *enjoy it, August. It's the last meal you'll ever taste safely either way.*

Because down the hall, raised voices had started—running feet, a bell ringing wrong, urgent and off-pattern—and through the god's senses, faint as smoke, I caught the flavor of the news arriving from the south.

Ash's army had taken the river crossing. The capital was three days away.

And I had two.
