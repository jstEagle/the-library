## 32. Ash Marches

*August*

I had been in the free cities for six days, and in that time I had been claimed by four different temples, two merchant guilds, one very persistent widow who ran a soup kitchen and an intelligence network out of the same building, and a man who insisted I was his cousin. I had been bathed, oiled, measured for robes I refused to wear, and prayed over so many times that I'd begun to develop a sympathy headache on behalf of whatever god was receiving all of it.

Nobody, in all of that, had asked me a single question worth answering.

They asked if I was the vessel. They asked if I could heal their grandmother's hip. They asked whether the prophecy favored the eastern districts or the western ones, as though divinity were a matter of municipal zoning. What nobody asked—what none of them wanted to hear—was what I intended to do, because the answer was *nothing you'd like*, and every faction in those cities had already decided what I was for. I was a key. A bomb. A banner. A cousin, apparently.

Ash arrived on the seventh day.

I should say something about how he looked, because everyone remembers how he looked. He came through the temple gates barefoot, in a plain gray coat, with ash—not metaphor, actual ash—ground into the creases of his knuckles like he'd been kneading burnt bread. He was my height. My build. Close enough in the face that a drunk man might've called us brothers and not entirely been wrong about why. And when the crowd saw him, they didn't cheer. They went quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone starts crying at a funeral. Recognition without joy.

"You're the other door," I said, when they finally let me near him.

"I'm the one who said yes," Ash said. His voice was gentle. That was the worst part. I'd braced for a fanatic's fire and gotten a bedside manner instead. "You're still deciding. I can hear it. The god can hear it. It's very patient with you." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it made my skin crawl. "It isn't patient with me."

Behind him, the armies were already moving. I don't mean that figuratively. Somewhere between his arrival and that sentence, the war councils of three free cities had stopped arguing about who owned me and started arguing about logistics, which is how you know a decision has been made without you. Banners were coming down off racks. Wagons were being loaded with grain and oil and, I noticed, a great many chains.

"Ash," I said. "The god doesn't want this. I've asked it. Directly. It wants to go home."

"It told you what it wanted you to hear," he said, not unkindly. "It tells each of us what we need. To you it says *carry me home*. To me it says *carry me forward*. We're both telling the truth." He looked past me at the mustering yards, at the thousands of men sharpening themselves into an argument, and his face did something complicated—grief wearing patience as a mask, or the reverse. "I spent twenty years in a cellar being emptied so this could happen. I'm not going to waste it on your hesitation."

"They'll burn," I said. "You know that. Every prayer you answer with fire, every city that resists—you'll give them exactly the god the usurper always warned them about."

"Yes," Ash said simply. "Probably." And then, softer, almost apologetic: "I'm sorry, brother. I truly am. But the prophecy doesn't have a verse where we all sit down and talk."

I want to be fair to him, even now. Especially now. He wasn't lying to himself the way fanatics usually are. He knew the cost down to the copper. He'd simply done arithmetic I couldn't follow—twenty years of emptiness balanced against everything—and come out believing the scales only tipped one way.

That night I stole a horse, a map, and a loaf of bread, in descending order of difficulty, and rode west toward the capital while behind me the free cities' army woke up and found itself a god.

* * *

*Ines*

In the capital, the prayers had started going unanswered, and the city was handling it about as well as you'd expect, which is to say badly, and then worse.

You have to understand what six months of answered prayers does to people. It spoils them. It spoiled *me*, if I'm honest—a woman who trusts nothing she can't draw to scale found herself lighting candles for safe roads and then, shamefully, checking whether the roads obliged. When the answers stopped, it wasn't like returning to normal. It was like a well running dry in a house that had already torn up its rain barrels.

And then the fires started.

Not everywhere. That was the horror of it—the selectivity. A granary in the Tanner's Ward burned to its foundations while the tannery beside it, full of more flammable material than any sane deity would tolerate, stood untouched. A magistrate's house. A witch-finder's barracks—I confess I read that report twice, and once more with wine. Always, afterward, someone from the neighborhood would swear they'd prayed for exactly this, weeks ago, some small bitter wish spoken into a cold hearth. *Let it burn.* The wishes had been filed away somewhere, and now they were being processed.

"He's answering the old debts," Serel Doss said to me. We stood on the archive balcony looking east, where the sky over the low hills held a smudge of orange that no sunset explains at midday. "Every prayer ever whispered to the Hollow god and ignored. He's collecting."

"Then he's not a god," I said. "He's a creditor."

Doss grunted. Coming from him, that was practically applause. Our relationship had evolved since the interrogation—evolved into something neither of us named, in which he brought me documents I shouldn't see and I drew him maps he shouldn't have, and we both pretended this was ordinary professional courtesy between a witch-finder and a cartographer rather than two people quietly defecting in opposite directions.

"The army's ten days out," he said. "Maybe eight. The usurper won't evacuate. He's opened the Godfall vaults."

My stomach dropped. Everyone in the palace service knew about the Godfall vaults the way children know about the thing under the bed—vaguely, completely, and with a firm policy of never discussing it. Weapons from the old war. Things built specifically to hurt gods, which meant things that would hurt anything carrying a god, which meant—

"August," I said.

Doss looked at me. For a long moment he said nothing, and I watched him decide something, the way I'd watched him decide things across a candle during my interrogation. "Your taster," he said finally. "Who walked out of the free cities four days ago, alone, ahead of the army. Yes. Him."

"You're tracking him."

"I track everything. It's my single redeeming feature." He turned back to the orange smudge on the horizon. "The usurper knows what he is. Has known, I think, longer than any of us. He doesn't want the vessel dead, Corvane. He wants it *caught*. A god in a bottle makes a fine dynasty." He paused. "And he's heard rumors about a certain cartographer who burns her own maps."

There it was. I had wondered how long my little bonfire of originals would stay secret. In a palace, secrets have the lifespan of milk.

"What happens to cartographers who burn maps?" I asked, keeping my voice level, keeping my hands flat on the railing the way you do when you're afraid they'll do something independent.

"Nothing good," Doss said. "Get out of the city. Tonight. I'll look the other way for exactly as long as it takes you to be gone, and not one minute of sentiment after." He straightened his coat, and for just a moment the machinery of the man showed through—the exhaustion, the disgust, the strange scrupulous honor of a hunter who'd finally seen what he was hunting *for*. "Go find your taster. One of you should survive this with clean hands, and it won't be me."

I should have gone that night. I want that recorded somewhere, in some honest ledger: I should have gone.

Instead I went down to the archive one last time, to the drawer where I kept the Hollow surveys, and I stood there with my hand on the handle like a woman saying goodbye to a house fire she'd started herself. And that was where the guards found me. Four of them, with the usurper's seal on their warrant and no interest whatsoever in my professional opinion.

They took the drawer keys first. Then me.

* * *

*August*

Riding west ahead of an army gives you a strange view of a war. You see it arrive in villages before it arrives anywhere official—as a change in the air, a quality of silence. The first village I came through had nailed its shutters closed against nothing. The second had painted the sigil on its well. By the third, the sigils were fresh, and the people were standing in their doorways watching the eastern sky, waiting for their miracle or their funeral, whichever the god felt like delivering.

I stopped at the fourth village because the church was on fire and nobody was putting it out.

Not the village. Just the church. The villagers stood in a loose ring around it, watching it burn with expressions I couldn't parse until I got closer and realized they weren't horrified. They were *satisfied*. An old woman caught my eye and nodded at the flames, companionable, as though we were both enjoying a parade.

"They preached the Godfall was a mercy," she told me. "Said the old god was rightly put down. Well." She gestured at the fire with enormous contentment. "Somebody upstairs disagrees."

"Did anyone pray for this?" I asked.

"Oh, years ago. Decades. Father Aldous burned a shrine-book when I was a girl. We all wished—" she shrugged, "—you know. Wishes." She patted my arm. "The new prophet keeps excellent records."

The new prophet. Ash. Keeping excellent records. Collecting decades of petty grudges and paying them out in arson, and calling it justice, and the terrible thing—the thing I carried west with me like a stone in my chest—was that every wish he answered had genuinely been wished. He wasn't inventing the cruelty. He was just the first entity cruel enough, or honest enough, to honor it.

The god stirred inside me as I rode, and I felt it straining east toward its other half the way a dog strains at a leash, and I understood suddenly, viscerally, what Ash had that I didn't: he'd let go of the leash. Whatever he was carrying pulled him, and he went. I was still hauling backward on mine, and my arms were tired, and the country between me and the capital was filling up with smoke.

Near the border, I passed a column of refugees heading the other way, and one of them—a boy of maybe ten, walking with his family's entire household on a handcart—looked at me and said, with the flat certainty of children stating facts, "You're the other one."

"How would you know that?"

"You're walking like you're sorry," he said, and went on.

I rode through the night after that. Not because I believed I could outrun a prophecy. Because somewhere behind me was an army with my brother's face on it, and somewhere ahead of me was a city with Ines in it, and I had learned enough arithmetic from Ash to know which of those I could still do something about.

* * *

*Ines*

The cell they gave me was better than I expected, which in the usurper's palace is its own kind of message. A desk. A lamp. My confiscated drawing instruments, laid out with insulting neatness. A window, barred, facing east.

He came at dusk—the usurper himself, no herald, no ceremony, just a quiet man in quiet clothes with eyes like a ledger's final column. I had seen him at banquets, at a distance, the way you see weather. Up close he was smaller and much worse.

"Mistress Corvane." He sat across the desk from me, uninvited, and folded his hands. "You've been very busy. Doctored maps. Burned archives. A friendship with a food-taster who cannot die." He smiled faintly. "And drawings. May I?"

He slid one of my own sheets across the desk—the Hollow overlay, the sigil formed from converging footpaths, the mark I'd first seen burned into August's palm at a banquet two years ago. He looked at it the way a jeweler looks at a stone.

"Fifteen years I've fed that man poison-proof and invisible," the usurper said conversationally. "Do you know how difficult it is to keep a weapon hidden in a pantry? And now the free cities have dug him up and aimed him at me, and my witch-finders keep losing their nerve, and here you sit—the one person in my capital who understands what he actually is." He looked up. "So tell me, Mistress Corvane. When the god comes over those hills wearing your taster's brother—what will you have drawn for me by then?"

Outside the barred window, far to the east, the sky had begun to glow.

I thought of August riding through the smoke. I thought of Doss looking the other way for exactly one night's worth of minutes. I thought of the sigil, and the heartbeat under the Hollow, and a hearth-fire voice asking a farm boy *will you carry me*, and I picked up my pen.

"Give me paper," I said, "and I'll show you exactly what's coming."

Because here is what I knew that the usurper didn't, what no ledger-eyed tyrant sitting safely behind his walls could know: the prophecy wasn't fulfilling itself. Prophecies don't fulfill themselves. Somebody carries them, mile by mile, prayer by prayer, fire by fire—and I intended to be holding the map when it arrived.

Even if I had to draw it for the devil first.
