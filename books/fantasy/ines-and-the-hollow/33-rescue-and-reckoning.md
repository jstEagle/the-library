## 33. Rescue and Reckoning

The candle in my cell had burned down to a thumb's width of tallow, and I had spent the last hour doing what cartographers do when the map runs out: drawing one anyway. In the dust on the floor, with a splinter of straw, I had charted the citadel as best I remembered it—three gates, two stairwells I'd never seen, and one large blank space where Doss had loosened my chains and said nothing at all.

I was adding a speculative corridor when the wall exhaled.

Not a figure of speech. The stone actually breathed—a long, warm sigh that stirred the straw, guttered the candle, and left behind it the smell of hearth smoke and rain on old moss. The god. August carried it like a man carries a fever he's stopped apologizing for, and lately the whole citadel smelled faintly of it, which was how I'd known, three days ago, before anyone told me, that he had come back for me.

Then the wall did something ruder than breathing. It opened.

Not dramatically—no thunder, no shattering. A section of masonry simply became a doorway the way a word becomes a sentence, and there he stood in the gap, soot-streaked, hollow-eyed, wearing a guard's cloak over a shirt that had been white in some previous life.

"You're drawing maps on the floor," he said.

"I'm planning an escape."

"With a straw."

"It's a very good straw."

He crossed the cell in four steps and took my face in his hands, and for a moment neither of us managed any wit at all. His palms were hot—god-hot, furnace-under-the-skin hot—and trembling. He kissed me once, briefly, like a man checking a door is locked, then pulled back to look at me properly.

"They hurt you."

"They asked questions. I answered them beautifully and lied with every syllable." I stood; my legs had opinions about this after six days on a stone bench, but they cooperated. "Doss loosened my chains yesterday. He didn't say why. I assumed a trap."

"Was it?"

"Everything is a trap. The question is whose." I nodded past him into the dark of the passage. "Where's Ash?"

"Marching. Two days out, maybe less. Which means we have—" he glanced at the candle stub, "—no time at all, and I intend to spend all of it getting you down the mountain."

"The usurper?"

"Undone by his own weapon's backlash, if the screaming we heard on the way in meant anything. Half the citadel thinks the world is ending. The other half is looting." He almost smiled. "It's the best chaos we'll ever get. Come on."

* * *

We went up, not down—which surprised me until he explained, in the terse grammar of a man conserving breath for stairs, that the lower gates were choked with fleeing soldiers and the upper postern, the one only the witch-finders used, would be watched by exactly no one who still remembered his orders.

The corridors were lit by emergency lamps and empty in the way of a house after a funeral. Twice we passed bodies. Once we passed a priest of the new rite sitting against a wall, weeping into his hands, and August stopped—actually stopped—and pressed two fingers to the man's brow. The weeping stopped too. The man looked up with the dazed peace of someone whose prayer has just been answered by accident.

"What did you do?" I whispered as we moved on.

"Gave him permission to grieve instead of fear. It's all most people need." He said it lightly, but his hand shook afterward, and I pretended not to see, because pretending not to see things is a skill I have refined to a court art.

At the third landing, a voice came out of an alcove like a knife from a sleeve.

"Corvane. And the vessel. How touching."

Serel Doss stepped into the lamplight, and I saw in one glance what I should have seen days ago: he was dying. Not wounded-dying—there wasn't a mark on him—but dying the way a lamp dies when the oil runs low. Grey at the lips. Steady at the eyes.

"Doss," I said carefully. "You loosened my chains."

"I did."

"Why?"

He looked at August for a long moment—the way a man looks at a fire he has spent his life trying to put out and finally, grudgingly, decided to sit beside.

"Because I interrogated you," he said, "and you lied to me with maps. Perfect lies. Every coordinate true, every conclusion false. And I sat there thinking: here is a woman who has been lying her whole life to protect something, and I have spent mine hunting people to protect nothing, because the thing I protected turned out to be a man who wants to bottle gods like wine." He spat, delicately, toward the wall. "I checked his dream, Corvane. Before the backlash. I walked through what he dreams. Corpses of gods stacked like cordwood, and his dynasty fat and shining on top. I have hanged women for lesser heresies than what he believes."

"So you freed me," I said.

"So I freed *you*," he corrected. "You were incidental. I freed the truth, and you happened to be carrying it." He unslung a satchel from his shoulder and pushed it into August's arms. "Water, bread, a writ of passage under a dead man's name, and these." Inside, glinting: a dozen small vials. "Witch-finder tools. Counter-agents to the anti-divine residues. If your god-friend here walks anywhere near the Godfall stores, he'll need them."

August stared at him. "You're coming with us."

"No." Doss smiled, and it was the first genuinely kind expression I had ever seen him wear, which was somehow more frightening than all his others. "Someone has to hold the postern stair when Ash's vanguard arrives, and convince them the vessel went east. Someone has to be found dead—or better, alive and wrong—in the records. Someone has to buy you the hour." He rolled his shoulders like a man settling into a familiar chair. "I've bought hours for worse men than you, vessel. This will be a pleasure."

"You'll die," I said.

"Everyone does. Most of us do it badly and for nothing." He drew his blade and tested its weight. "I get to do it well and for something. Go. Through the cistern gallery, then the goat track west. Don't argue with me, Corvane—I've heard you argue, and I refuse to die mid-lecture."

I argued anyway. Briefly. He let me, the way you let a child finish a tantrum, and then he put one grey hand on my shoulder—gently, the hand that had signed death warrants, resting on me like a bird—and said:

"You kept the names of the dead from the shrine fires. All of them. I read your interrogation transcripts; you recited them like a litany rather than give me one living name." His voice roughened. "Say mine too, when you say theirs. That's all. Say mine too."

And then he turned and walked up the stair toward the sound of distant war drums, and I did not watch him go, because watching would have made it real, and I needed it not to be real for perhaps another hour.

* * *

The cistern gallery ran cold and dripping under the citadel, our footsteps echoing off black water. We walked close enough to touch without touching, which is how we walked everywhere now, and somewhere around the second archway the silence between us got heavy enough that I broke it, because silence and I have always had a difficult relationship.

"When I met you," I said, "at the survey office. Eight years ago. You asked me for a map of the river districts and I gave you the wrong one on purpose."

"I know."

"You—what?"

"I know," August said. "The ink was smudged where you'd changed it. You were a terrible forger then. You've improved."

"That isn't the point." I stopped walking. The water dripped. "The point is that I noticed you that day. The food-taster nobody notices. And I thought: here is a man who vanishes on a schedule, and vanished men are either criminals or spies, and either way they're worth mapping. I started tracking your disappearances before I knew what I was tracking. And when I figured out what you were—or half of it—I sat down at my desk and I wrote a report."

The words came out of me the way a splinter comes out: slowly, painfully, and leaving a hole.

"A full report. Your name, your pattern, the shrine network, everything I'd guessed. Sealed it. Addressed it to the Office of Inquisition." My voice went flat, the way it does when I'm lying or confessing, which are apparently the same muscle. "I never sent it. But I wrote it, August. I planned to sell you. Before I loved you, before the Hollow, before any of it—I looked at you and saw a promotion."

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough for the dripping to become unbearable.

"Did you burn it?" he asked finally.

"After the archive confrontation. After you told me the truth. I burned it in the map-drawer lamp."

"And the copies?"

"There were no copies. I wanted there to be leverage, but I couldn't make myself make copies. Even then, some part of me—" I gestured vaguely at my own chest, at a loss for the vocabulary of conscience. "Some part of me already knew I'd rather lose the crown than spend you."

August leaned against the wet stone and looked at me, and I braced for the wound, because I had earned a wound.

"Ines," he said, "when the taster-master bought me, I was eleven years old, and the first thing he taught me was to lie about what I tasted. The second thing was to sell charms to desperate people. The third thing was that everyone who loves you is either using you or being used, and the trick is knowing which." He pushed off the wall and came closer. "So when I found out you'd been mapping me—yes, I knew, I've always known, forgettable men notice everything—it didn't even surprise me. What surprised me was chapter seventeen."

"Chapter what?"

"The shrine fire. Doss's burning. You kept the names. You could have traded those names for your own safety a dozen times since, and you didn't. You burned your maps instead. You gave Doss a false trail north." He took my hands, and his heat soaked into my cold fingers like sun into winter ground. "Ines, everyone in my life planned to use me. My master used me. The god uses me. The free cities want to detonate me. Ash wants to rule beside me. You are the only person who ever held the weapon I am in her hands, examined it, and chose to set it down." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "That's not betrayal. That's the opposite of everything else I've ever been given."

"It doesn't feel like the opposite. It feels like a thing I did."

"Yes," he said. "That's what forgiveness is. It isn't a feeling. It's a thing you do, and then it becomes a feeling later, the way a map becomes a country." He kissed my knuckles, each one, absurdly formal, like sealing a treaty. "I forgive you. Not because it costs nothing—it costs something, and I pay it gladly—but because I would rather love a woman who almost sold me and chose not to than trust a woman who never had the chance to choose at all."

I cried, a little, in the dark of the cistern, standing in drip-water and god-warmth, and I am recording that here because I promised myself I would keep honest accounts from now on. Honest accounts. It's the least a liar can do.

* * *

The goat track west was everything Doss had promised: steep, invisible, and mercifully empty. We climbed out of the citadel's shadow as the sun went down behind us, and below, the capital was burning in patches—not the whole-city conflagration of prophecy, but scattered fires, each one a prayer answered too hard, and between the fires, small lights moving. People. Carrying water, carrying each other.

August stopped at the ridge and looked back, and I felt the god in him lean toward the city like a plant toward light.

"She's waking faster," he murmured. "Every prayer feeds her, and every fire makes more prayers. By tomorrow—"

"By tomorrow we'll be on smugglers' roads," I said, "doing what you taught the villages to do. Praying her down gently. One well-bucket at a time."

"One well-bucket at a time," he agreed, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before—not hope exactly, but the place hope comes from.

We made camp in a shepherd's hut as full dark fell, sharing bread and Doss's water, and I laid out the counter-agent vials and studied their labels by candlelight, because studying things is how I pray. August watched me work with an expression I decided not to name yet.

"Ines," he said, when the candles were low. "There's something I haven't told you. About what the god showed me, alone, when I asked what she actually wants."

I looked up.

"She doesn't want war. She never did. She wants to be carried home and laid down—to end the covenant, end the vessels, end all of it. And Ash is marching on the capital carrying his half of her like a torch, and the free cities' army is marching behind him, and the usurper's weapons are waiting, and none of them know." He turned his hands over, palm-up, the sigil scars pale in the candlelight. "Two days, Ines. Maybe three. And we are the only two people in the world who know how this ends gently instead of in ash."

Outside, far to the east, the horizon flickered—not lightning. Something steadier. Something marching.

I folded the map I'd been sketching of the road ahead, and reached for a fresh sheet.

"Then we'd better start drawing," I said, "the fastest way home."
