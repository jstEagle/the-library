## 34. The Race to the Hollow

*August*

Here is what nobody tells you about racing an army: the army doesn't have to hurry.

Ash had a hundred thousand faithful and forty leagues of open road. We had a smuggler's cart, a mule named Duchess who hated us both on principle, and Ines's genius for finding roads that officially did not exist. The army went south by the King's Way, banners up, singing hymns that made the hedgerows smoke. We went north through the hills, where the roads were more suggestion than fact and the tolls were paid in silence and small favors.

"Smugglers' road," Ines said, easing the cart around a boulder that had no business being in the middle of anything. "Every kingdom has them. The crown pretends they don't exist because pretending is cheaper than policing."

"And you know them how?"

"I map things for a living, August. Including the things I'm not supposed to." She glanced at me sideways, that look she'd perfected somewhere between the archive and the burning of her life's work — half apology, half dare. "You were never supposed to see those maps either."

"No," I said. "But you showed me anyway."

"You were already on fire at the time. It seemed rude not to."

That was our whole courtship, more or less. Catastrophe, then courtesy.

Behind us — two days back now, maybe less, Ash moved fast when his god leaned on him — the world was burning in the shape of answered prayers. Villages where the crops had come in overnight. Wells that healed. A town called Brassen where every prayer shouted skyward came back as flame, because Ash carried half of something vast and it leaked out of him wherever faith pooled too deep. The free cities' priests called it harvest. From the hills, it looked like a fever moving down a body.

And here was my problem, the one I chewed on all day and most of the night: every fire we passed was partly mine. Not Ash's half. Mine. Because I had run. Because I'd taken the god's true wish — *carry me home, lay me down* — and turned it into a race instead of a surrender, and a god split between two doors who wanted different things does what any desperate creature does. It pulls. Both directions. And things tear.

"We can't outrun him," I said on the third morning, watching smoke stand up over the valley like slow gray trees. "We're not trying to beat him to the capital. We're trying to get there first enough to be standing in the right place when he arrives."

"That's still a race."

"It's a race where the prize is letting go of everything you are. Very popular event. Long waiting lists."

Ines almost smiled. She'd been rationing her smiles since Doss died; each one cost her something, and she spent them carefully, like coins sewn into a hem. "Then teach me," she said. "What you've been doing in the villages. I want to understand it before we need it."

So I told her. Or tried to.

* * *

The village was called Nether Fen, which is the kind of name you give a place when your ancestors looked at a swamp and decided honesty was the better part of ambition. Forty houses, a shrine to nothing in particular — the old god's mark worn to a dimple in the stone — and a fire in the barley field that had been burning for six days without consuming anything, which sounds miraculous until you realize it was also growing.

The villagers met us at the edge of it, pitchforks and prayer in equal measure. A woman with a burned shawl — burned, though the fire ate nothing else — stepped forward.

"You're him," she said. "The other one. The one the priests warn against."

"I'm frequently warned against," I said. "It's practically my coat of arms. August Villalobos, formerly poison-taster to the usurper, currently heretic, at your service."

"Why did your prayers do this?" she demanded, pointing at the field. "We only asked for rain."

Ah. There it was. The question underneath every question since the Hollow woke. I'd been asked it in nine villages now, and I'd finally stopped lying.

"Because you prayed to something that's been asleep for three hundred years," I said, "and it heard you the way a man hears after decades of silence — too loudly. You asked for rain. It gave you everything rain means. Growth, warmth, light, terror. It doesn't know how to be small yet. It's never been small. That's rather the point of gods."

"So we're cursed."

"You're loud," I said. "There's a difference, and I can teach it to you, if you'll put down the pitchfork. The tall one especially. I have a history with agricultural implements."

It took an hour to teach them what I'd learned from the god itself, there in the dark under the capital, when I asked it directly and it answered with the terrible simplicity of hearth-fire: *I want to be carried home and laid down.* The trick wasn't new words. The trick was quiet.

"Prayer isn't shouting," I told them, kneeling at the field's edge where the heat pressed against my face like a hand. "Shouting is fear wearing religion's clothes. Prayer is — think of it as setting a table. You don't scream at a guest. You make room. You say: here. Here is a place for you. Rest."

I put my palm flat to the earth. Somewhere far below, under three hundred years of stone and forgetting, something warm turned toward me like a sleeper toward a window.

*Down,* I said, not aloud, not entirely. *Gently. They didn't mean to wake you. They meant well, which is worse, but still.*

The fire sighed. I felt it go through the soles of my boots, this long exhalation of heat, and the flames in the barley folded themselves down like birds settling, smaller and smaller, until they were candle-height, then coal-glow, then a shimmer in the air and nothing more. The barley stood unburnt. Six days of miracle-fire, and the stalks were green.

Nobody spoke. Then the woman with the burned shawl sat down hard in the dirt and began to laugh, the ragged kind, the kind that lives next door to crying.

"How?" she managed.

"Practice," I said. "That's the whole secret, and I'm sorry it's disappointing. Hope isn't a feeling. Feelings are weather. Hope is a thing you do with your hands, every day, badly, until your hands learn it. You prayed like people drowning. Pray like people setting a table instead. It takes practice. Do it every evening. Small prayers. Quiet ones. 'Thank you.' 'Rest well.' 'We remember you.' That's all. The god will learn gentleness from you faster than it will ever learn it from me."

"Why you, then?" the tall man with the pitchfork asked. "If they can learn it from us?"

Because I'm a door, I didn't say. Because half a god lives behind my ribs and uses my mouth sometimes. Because I was bred for this in a cellar by men who are mostly dead now, and the breeding took.

"Because someone has to carry the message," I said instead, "and I'm apparently very good at carrying things I never asked for."

* * *

*Ines*

He made it sound simple, teaching villages to pray. It was not simple. I watched him do it four times in five days — Nether Fen, Coldharbour, a place whose name I never learned because its signpost had been used for firewood — and every time, the pattern held: fear first, then the pitchforks, then August saying something disarming and slightly self-mocking, then the long patient work of showing frightened people how to be quiet together.

What struck me, mapping it afterward in my head the way I mapped everything, was that he never performed hope. He practiced it. I could see the seams. Some nights, after the villagers slept and the fires lay down, he would sit alone at the edge of whatever miracle we'd calmed, and his shoulders would shake, and I understood that the calm he sold these people cost him something to manufacture. He was a taster. His whole life had been swallowing terrible things and showing no expression. Now he swallowed gods and grief in the same throat, and smiled anyway, because the smile worked.

On the fifth night I sat down next to him at the edge of Coldharbour's stilled fields and didn't say anything for a while. That's another thing I learned from him. Silence, done properly, is a kind of company.

"The army's a day behind us now," I said eventually. "Maybe less. Ash isn't stopping to teach anyone anything."

"No. He stops only long enough to take what faith is offered, and it feeds him, and he moves on." August turned a pebble over in his fingers. "He thinks he's saving them."

"He thinks he's saving everyone. That's what makes him dangerous. Fanatics with doubts are just sad men. Fanatics without them burn provinces."

"You've been reading Doss's books."

"Some of them survived the interrogation," I said, and we let that lie there, because some things deserved a moment of respect even when the man they honored had been our enemy for most of his life. Serel Doss, witch-finder, loosened our chains with his last act and bought us an hour with his last breath. I had hated him efficiently for years. Grief is embarrassing like that. It ignores all your careful bookkeeping.

"August," I said. "When we reach the Hollow. When you do — whatever it is you're going to do. Pouring yourself out. Laying the god down." I had rehearsed this sentence eleven times and it still came out crooked. "Ash dies doing it. Empty and grateful, you said. What happens to you?"

He was quiet long enough that Duchess, tied nearby, snored judgmentally.

"Honestly? I assumed I'd die," he said at last, in the mild tone he used for facts that should have been screams. "Ash was raised for it — emptied out, made into a clean vessel. I'm full of fifteen years of contraband charms and stubbornness and one extremely specific immunity. Pouring a god through me should be like pouring a river through a teacup. The teacup loses."

"But?"

"But the god keeps saying something strange when I listen. It says *both doors*. Not *one door*. I don't know if that's mercy or mathematics or a god being vague the way gods are." He looked at me then, and the firelight did something unkind to the shadows under his eyes. "Ines, if I pour myself out and there's nothing left — no magic, no immunity, no vessel, just a middle-aged former food-taster with bad knees — will that be enough? For you? For whatever this is?"

I could have lied. I'm good at it; I'd lied to an inquisitor with cartographic precision and won. But he'd taught me something on this road too, watching him practice hope with his hands: some things you do badly every day until they become true.

"August Villalobos," I said, "I fell in love with a man who sold illegal fertility charms out of a coat pocket and apologized to a mule. The god was always temporary. You were the plan."

Duchess snorted. August laughed — really laughed, the first time since Doss died — and it cracked something open in the night, and for a little while neither of us was running from anything.

Which is, of course, precisely when the rider found us.

* * *

*August*

She came out of the dark on a lathered horse, no banner, no escort, and I knew before she spoke that she was from the free cities, because only their envoys traveled with that particular combination of exhaustion and entitlement. Ines had her knife out before the horse finished stopping. Old habits.

"August Villalobos," the rider said. Young. Sharp-faced. The kind of earnest that gets people killed. "Thank all the sleeping ones. The council thought you'd be further along."

"The council," I said, "should stop thinking about me entirely. It's bad for their health and worse for mine."

"They sent me to tell you the army crossed the Sere ford this morning. Three days from the capital. Maybe two, if Ash pushes them, and he pushes them." She swung down, staggering, and thrust a sealed letter at me as if it were a torch and I a cold hearth. "They also sent terms. Read them. Please. Everything depends—"

"I know what everything depends on," I said, and broke the seal, because refusing to read a letter is a luxury for people not being chased by an army.

The free cities write beautifully. It's how they hide the knives. This one was four paragraphs of elegant prose that reduced, once you scraped off the varnish, to the following: *Carry the god to the capital. Ascend the walls or the towers or whatever high place offers itself. Open yourself above the usurper's host. The council will honor your sacrifice forever.*

Forever, in diplomatic correspondence, means *as long as it's useful*.

"They want me to be a bomb," I said. "A holy bomb. Detonate myself over the army and call it liberation."

"They want the war ended—"

"The war ends either way," Ines said quietly, stepping up beside me, and I felt her shoulder settle against mine like a keystone finding its arch. "The only question is whether anything is left standing after. Including him."

The envoy looked from her to me and back, and I watched her recalculate — watched the earnestness curdle into assessment, the way it always does when idealists meet the objects of their ideals and find them inconveniently alive. "The prophecy says a vessel will end the tyranny," she said. "It doesn't specify gently."

"No," I agreed. "Prophecies rarely specify anything usefully. That's how they stay in business." I handed the letter back. "Tell your council I'm grateful, I'm flattered, and I decline. I'm not carrying the god to a battlefield. I'm carrying it home. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and if they can't see it from their safe beautiful distance, that's a failure of their eyesight, not my obedience."

"They'll send others," she warned.

"They usually do."

She remounted and rode back into the dark toward the war, and Ines and I stood in the road listening to the hoofbeats fade, and neither of us said what we were both thinking, which was: the free cities want me aimed east, Ash wants himself aimed west, the usurper wants the god bottled, and somewhere beneath all of it, the only party with a modest, reasonable request — *take me home and let me sleep* — was the one nobody had consulted.

"Well," I said. "Everyone wants a piece of me. It's the charm trade all over again, just with higher stakes and worse margins."

"Two days," Ines said, looking south, where the horizon had begun to glow again — not dawn, dawn was hours off, but the light of a city-sized prayer going wrong. "The Hollow opens ahead of him. Can you feel it?"

I could. Under my feet, faint as a pulse through a wrist, the ground was beating faster. The god felt Ash coming, its other half, its torn twin, and it was rising to meet itself whether I consented or not. Streets in the lower city had cracked overnight, according to the last refugees we'd passed. Warm water rising in cellars. Moss blooming on marble. The Hollow, waking up around its own heartbeat.

"Ines," I said. "When we get there, I'm going to have to open the door from this side before Ash opens it from his. And I don't know what comes through a door opened in a hurry."

"Then we'd better not be in a hurry," she said, and turned Duchess north, into the hills, toward the smoking skyline of the only home either of us had left, and I climbed up beside her and put my hand flat against the cart's boards, against the earth underneath, and began, quietly, to set the table.

*Down*, I told the warm thing below. *Slowly. We're coming. Hold on.*

It held. For now.

But the horizon glowed brighter with every mile, and the pulse under my palm quickened like a heart climbing stairs, and somewhere ahead, in the sunken quarter where the moss grew on dead stone, something vast was counting the hours until its halves were whole again — and I had two days to teach a city of strangers how to whisper.
