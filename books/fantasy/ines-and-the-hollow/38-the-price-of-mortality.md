## 38. The Price of Mortality

I have drawn maps of disasters I never expected to see. Flood plains, fire breaks, the projected blast radius of a Godfall engine going critical beneath its own city. I drew them because drawing them was my job, and because a map is a way of looking at something terrible without flinching.

None of them prepared me for standing on the Grand Processional with ash in my hair, watching the sky over the Hollow glow like a forge door left open, and realizing that every line I had ever inked was now wrong.

The usurper's weapon had fired at last — the great anti-divine lance they'd kept buried under the citadel since the Godfall, the one he'd promised would end the war in a single stroke. It had struck the two vessels above the sunken quarter, or it had struck the god wearing them, and the distinction no longer mattered. The backlash came down the lance's spine like lightning running a bell-rope, and when it reached the mechanism that aimed it, the mechanism remembered, all at once, everything it had been built to do to gods, and did a version of it to itself.

I was three streets away when the citadel's eastern face folded. Not exploded — *folded*, the way a map folds, which is a sentence I will never say aloud again because it sounds like madness. Stone went soft and then went gone. Dust rolled out in a grey tide, and under it, faintly, I heard cheering from the direction of the walls, where Ash's army had breached, and screaming from the direction of the palace, where nobody had anyone left to obey.

The usurper died in that folding. I know this because I saw it, or enough of it: a figure on the high balcony where he had stood to watch his miracle work, silhouetted against the light, and then not silhouetted, and then not there. He had spent thirty years building an empire out of other people's silence, and in the end the silence fell on him. There was something almost tidy about it. I hated how almost-tidy it was. I wanted him to have suffered more, and then I wanted to stop wanting that, and then I stopped having time for either feeling, because a thought arrived in me like a thrown knife:

*August was in the Hollow.*

*August was directly beneath the light.*

I ran. I am a cartographer; I walk distances for a living and consider myself hardy. But I ran the way people run in stories, the undignified, lung-tearing run, down the Processional steps two at a time, through the smoke, past a shrine-front that had split open and was bleeding warm golden light into the street like a cracked lantern. Nobody stopped me. There was no one left with the authority to stop anyone. The whole city had become, briefly, a place without borders, which as a professional I can tell you is the most frightening thing a place can be.

* *

The Hollow district had been buried for two hundred years. I knew its geography better than anyone alive — better than August, who only knew it from underneath, in the dark, listening. I knew where the old stairways were, the ones my faked maps had politely marked *collapsed* so that Serel Doss would never send anyone down them. Doss. Who had loosened my chains four nights ago with hands that shook, and said, "You were the best liar I ever interrogated, Corvane. Try to be worth it," and then stayed behind in the dark so we could go up into the light.

I descended the stairway behind the tannery, the one that smelled of wet stone and old tallow, and the light got stranger with every step. Not firelight. Not godlight either, exactly — by then the pouring was nearly done; I could feel it in my teeth the way you feel a storm leaving, a great pressure lifting off the world. What remained was ordinary light, torchlight, hearthlight, coming from somewhere ahead, and a silence that felt less like absence and more like someone finally sleeping well.

I found August at the bottom of the final stair, at the edge of the sealed wellspring, on his back in the moss that grew on dead stone.

For one merciful second I thought he was asleep. He looked like a man asleep after a long day — sprawled, boneless, his chest moving. Then I knelt and saw his lips.

They were grey. Not pale — grey, the specific blue-grey of nightshade, the color I had seen once before on a kitchen boy in my first year at court, a boy who had licked a finger that had touched the wrong berries, and who had been dead before the physician finished walking across the room.

"No," I said, out loud, to him, to the god, to whatever was left listening. "No, you don't get to do this. You survived fifteen years of being poisoned professionally. You do not die of it *after*."

His eyes opened. That was worse, somehow. They found me and focused with effort, and he smiled — the small, wry, corner-of-the-mouth smile I had fallen for across a banquet table full of lies, and he said, in a voice like paper:

"Huh. So this is what it feels like."

"Don't talk."

"Seems unfair," he went on, because he was August, and talking was the last thing he would ever surrender, "to spend my whole life immune and then get killed by the very first—"

"*Don't talk.*" My voice cracked on the second word. I put my hand flat on his chest, over his heart, feeling for what Doss had taught me to feel for. His skin was cold and clammy and his pulse was there but wrong — fast and shallow, fluttering like a moth in a jar. "Pupils," I muttered, tilting his head toward the light. Blown wide, slow to contract. Dry mouth — his lips were cracked. Nausea, obviously; there was bile at the corner of his mouth. Heart arrhythmic. Nightshade, classic presentation, the same poison that had come on the usurper's breakfast fifteen years ago, the poison his blood had laughed at every single morning since.

Except his blood wasn't laughing anymore. Whatever had made him poison-proof had gone into the ground with the god. He had poured out everything, including the thing that had kept him alive through a career of tasting death for breakfast, and the nightshade — patient, faithful nightshade, waiting in him all this time like a debt — had finally come to collect.

"Antidote," I said. "There'll be antidote in the physickers' stores, in the palace—"

"Ines." He caught my wrist. His grip was weak. It frightened me more than anything else had. "Palace is dust. Physickers are—" He coughed. "You know the city better than the city does. You always did."

He was right, and I hated him for making me be right too. The palace physickers' hall was under the collapsed wing or buried in rubble or trampled by an army. The guild halls were locked and burning candles over their ledgers, deciding whose side the new world was on. And somewhere above us, two armies were learning that their war had ended without their permission, which historically is when the looting starts.

So it would be me. It would be what I knew.

Doss had taught me poisons the way he taught me maps — not as lists but as terrain. "Every toxin is a country," he'd said, leaning over the interrogation table with two cups of tea he'd laced differently to make his point. "It enters here, wants to go there, gets blocked along the way. Your job is never to memorize the poison. Your job is to know the roads." Belladonna's roads: the alkaloids race to the heart and the lungs and the sweat glands, blocking, inflaming, racing the pulse until it tears itself apart. The counter-roads: physostigmine, if you had it. Pilocarpus, if you had it. If you had neither —

If you had neither, you had vinegar and charcoal and tannin and cold water and your own two hands, and you fought the poison road by road, hour by hour, and mostly you lost. Doss had told me that too, cheerfully, over the second cup. "Most of the time you lose, Corvane. You do it anyway. That's the whole profession. That's most professions, if we're honest."

* *

I got him off the floor. That was the first battle. He weighed nothing, which terrified me — August had always been deceptively solid, a man built like a locked door — and I hauled him up the stairs in stages, stopping at each landing to check his pulse and hate the number it gave me. Above us, the city was settling into its new shape. Fires burned in the distance. Somewhere a crowd was singing, badly, some hymn to the god below, and I wanted to scream at them that their god had just cost me the only thing I'd ever chosen for myself.

No. That's not fair, and I promised myself I would be fair in this account, because maps are no good if you flatter the terrain.

Their god had cost them fifteen years of unanswered prayers and given them back a wellspring. It had cost August everything, and he had chosen it, and the choosing didn't make it hurt less but it did make it *his*, which mattered. Even then, half-conscious on my shoulder, he murmured: "Did it work? Is it — is it sleeping?"

"It's sleeping."

"Good." A pause. "Tell it — no. Never mind. It knows."

"What were you going to say?"

"That I'm sorry I'm late for dinner." His laugh turned into a cough, and the cough had a wet sound in it that I refused to think about yet.

I got him up through the tannery and out into a city gone strange. Fires, singing, rubble, and — this was the part I hadn't predicted — people kneeling in the streets, faces upturned, praying. Not frantically. Gently. The way August had taught the villages on our road south, the way he'd taught me on the smugglers' road, laughing at how bad I was at it: *you don't shout at it, love, you ask. It's been asleep two hundred years. Would you want to be shouted awake?*

A woman rose from her knees as I carried him past, took one look at his grey lips, and pressed her own water flask into my free hand without a word. Another pointed me toward a side street she swore was clear of fires. The city, in its first hour of freedom, was already doing what the god had done: answering, slowly, person to person. I filed that away the way I file everything, even while my arms burned and my heart hammered worse than his, because filing things is how I keep from falling apart, and I could not fall apart. Falling apart was a luxury for people whose person was breathing easily.

* *

The physickers' guild hall on Tanner's Row still stood. Its doors were barred. I kicked them, which accomplished nothing except hurting my foot, and then I did what Doss would have done and what August would have teased me forever for: I lied.

"I'm Ines Corvane, royal cartographer, acting under emergency authority of the crown, which has just changed hands and hasn't issued new paperwork yet, so you'll have to take my word, and if my word isn't good enough, consider that I am holding a dying man and I have nothing left to lose, which makes me the most dangerous person in this city."

A slot slid open. An ancient physicker with flour-white eyebrows looked at August, then at me, then sighed the sigh of a man whose night had already been long and was about to get longer. "Bring him in. Put him by the stove. And Corvane—" he squinted, "you're the one whose maps got Doss executed."

"Doss isn't executed."

"So I've heard." The slot closed. The bar scraped. "Come in, then. Mind the floor, someone spilled emetic everywhere, it's been that kind of week."

What followed was nine hours that I remember in pieces, the way you remember a storm — flashes and noise and one long continuous effort underneath. Charcoal, ground fine, mixed with water, poured into a mouth that didn't want it. Vinegar, weak, sipped, to slow what absorption remained. Cold cloths for the fever that came at hour three. His hands twitching; mine holding them still. At hour five his heart stuttered badly enough that the old physicker — Havel, his name was Havel, I learned it between crises — looked at me over August's body and said, quietly, "You may want to prepare yourself," and I said, "No," with such force that a younger physicker dropped a tray, and Havel shrugged and reached for the pilocarpus tincture he'd been hoarding under the floorboards since the Godfall, saved, he said, for someone worth saving, and apparently a food-taster who had unmade a dynasty qualified.

At hour seven, August's fever broke. At hour eight, his pulse steadied into a rhythm I could bear to listen to. At hour nine, he opened his eyes, found me asleep sitting up with my hand still wrapped around his wrist, and had the audacity to whisper, "You're drooling on our patient records."

I woke fully, instantly, the way you do when the person you've been fighting for all night speaks. "You're supposed to be unconscious."

"I'm a very bad patient. Ask anyone. Ask the entire medical establishment of this kingdom." He tried to prop himself up, failed, settled for turning his head. In the stove-light he looked hollowed out, older, mortal in a way I was still learning to look at directly. "Ines. The nightshade. Did it—"

"You're alive. Barely. Havel says another hour of delay and no tincture in the world would have mattered." I swallowed. My throat was wrecked from smoke and shouting. "Fifteen years, August. Fifteen years of swallowing that stuff every morning, and it just — waited?"

"It's punctual," he said. "Poison. Always was my most reliable colleague." But his eyes weren't joking. They were doing the thing they did in the Hollow, when he listened to the god — going somewhere inward and vast. "It's strange. I keep reaching for it. The immunity. Like a tongue reaching for a missing tooth. All those mornings I tasted the cup and felt nothing, and I thought that was *me*. Being strong. Being careful." His hand found mine on the blanket. "It was never me. It was a leash. And now it's off, and I'm just — a man who can die of berries."

"A man who can die of berries," I agreed, "who unmade a two-hundred-year covenant, ended a war, and put a god to bed. On balance I'd say the berries should be nervous."

That got a real laugh out of him, weak and rusty, and then a wince, and then Havel appeared out of nowhere with a glare that could have curdled milk and ordered us both into silence, and for a while there was only the stove ticking and the distant, muffled singing of the city and his heartbeat under my fingers, steady, steady, steady, the most beautiful map I had ever read.

* *

Near dawn, Havel sent me out to fetch more charcoal from the storeroom, which was code, I understood immediately, for *give the poor woman five minutes alone.* I stood among the shelves of jars and powders, each neatly labeled in a dead physicker's handwriting, and let myself shake.

Because here is what I had not said to August, and might never say, and had to at least say to the dark: I had planned, once, to sell him. Early on — before the archive, before the confrontation among the map drawers, before any of it — I had sat at my desk with Doss's list of names in front of me and calculated exactly what naming August Villalobos would buy me. Safety. Standing. A life with the fear taken out of it. I had gotten as far as drafting the report. I had gotten as far as the second paragraph.

And I had burned it, eventually, along with the original maps, in a brazier in my office while Doss's men walked the corridor outside. But burning a thing is not the same as never having written it. I knew that better than anyone; I was a cartographer. The territory remembers what the map erases.

"He'd forgive you," said a voice from the doorway, and I spun, and it was Havel, holding two cups of something hot. "The taster. Men like that collect secrets the way I collect jars. He already knows. Trust me — a man doesn't survive fifteen years at that court without knowing exactly who drafted which reports." He handed me a cup. Willow bark and honey. "The question was never whether he'd forgive you. It's whether you'll forgive yourself. That one takes longer. Drink your tea."

I drank my tea. Through the storeroom's high window, the sky over the Hollow was turning the color of a clean page, and the city below was quiet in a way it had not been quiet in thirty years — not the silence of fear, but the silence of a place that has finally set down something heavy.

When I went back in, August was awake, watching the same window, and he smiled at me without any irony in it at all, which from August was practically a marriage proposal.

"The new government will need maps," he said. "Of the wellsprings. Of everything. People will want to know what's under them now."

"They'll need a cartographer who marks nothing sacred as ruined," I said.

"They'll need you." He shifted, winced, gripped my hand. "And I'll need — well. Apparently I'll need someone to read labels for me for the rest of my life. Poison-proof no more. First dose in fifteen years and it nearly finished me."

"Second dose," I corrected gently. "Havel counted. There was residue from years ago in your hair and nails. You've been marinating in it since chapter one, so to speak."

"Marinating." He considered this. "Like a charm ingredient."

"Don't."

"Too late. I'm opening a shop, you know. Legal charms. Honest ones. *This bundle of dried lavender will not fix your marriage but smells nice.*" His voice was fading; sleep was taking him back, and this time it looked like the good kind. His eyes closed. Then, at the very edge of it, barely audible:

"Ines. Stay till I'm asleep? Old habit. Taster's habit. Someone always watched me sleep, in case the poison came late."

"Someone's watching," I said, and meant: *always, from now on, whichever of us goes first.*

He slept. Outside, the sun came up on a capital without a tyrant, a god without a war, and a taster without his immunity — and on me, sitting vigil with charcoal dust to my elbows, learning the shape of the only country I had never mapped: the one where the person you love can die, and didn't, and every ordinary morning afterward is a border crossing you get to make again.

Somewhere downstairs, a messenger was pounding on the guild door with news from the north — the envoy, the free cities, the armies still mustering around a rumor of a vessel who no longer existed. But that knock belonged to tomorrow.

Today, I watched him breathe.
