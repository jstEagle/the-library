## 40. Ines and the Hollow

**August**

A year ago I would have told you that the most dangerous thing a man could swallow was the usurper's breakfast. I'd have been wrong by a wide margin. The most dangerous thing a man can swallow is hope—specifically, someone else's hope, ladled out generously, with the expectation that you'll carry it up three flights of stairs without spilling.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. It's autumn again. The chestnuts along the Processional are dropping their spiny little bombs on the heads of diplomats, which is the only violence anyone commits in the capital these days, and I have flowers in my hands and a wife-shaped shadow at my shoulder and a hole in the ground at my feet that used to be the end of the world.

"Are you going to stand there admiring them all morning," Ines said, "or are we going down?"

"They're marigolds," I said. "The god liked marigolds. Or it liked *me* liking marigolds. Honestly, after everything, I've stopped trying to distinguish."

"You're stalling."

"I'm savoring. There's a difference. Stalling is what cowards do; savoring is what men do who have earned the right to be slow." I turned the paper cones of flowers over in my hands. My fingers ached at the knuckles these days, ordinary ache, ordinary cold. A year of being mortal and I still hadn't made peace with how much things weigh. Flowers weigh nothing. They weigh everything. You understand.

The stair into the Hollow had been rebuilt—or rather, unburied. After the Laying Down, when the streets had finished cracking and knitting themselves closed again like a scab over a wound, the masons found the old processional stair beneath six feet of rubble and ash, and it went down farther than any of them were comfortable with, and so naturally they sent me first. Old habits. The poison-proof man goes through the door. Only now I was neither poison-proof nor particularly brave, and I went anyway, because the alternative was letting someone else's child go first, and I had spent fifteen years learning exactly what that kind of arithmetic costs.

The lamps along the stair burned low and gold. Not oil—I'd checked, the first dozen descents, because a man who spent his life detecting hidden things doesn't stop detecting just because the hidden things have been declared friendly. The light came up out of the stone itself, faint as breath on a window, warm as a kitchen at dusk.

"It's stronger this month," Ines said behind me. She always walked half a step back on the descent and half a step ahead on the climb, an arrangement we had never discussed and never needed to. "I can feel it through my boots."

"That's the wellspring rising toward winter," I said. "It does that. Gathers itself in the cold months, spends itself in the spring. Like a farmer. Like a sensible creature, unlike every god humanity ever invented for itself."

We passed the third landing, where the moss grew thick and green on stone that had been dead for two hundred years. I put my palm flat against it, out of habit, the way you touch a doorpost you've touched ten thousand times. The warmth came up to meet me. Not a voice—the god didn't speak anymore, not in words, not since Ash and I had poured it home—but something better than a voice. Something like being recognized. Like walking into your mother's kitchen after years away and having her say *there you are* without turning from the stove.

*There you are.*

"Hello, old thing," I murmured. "We brought flowers."

Ines's hand found the small of my back. She never touched me during the greeting—she said it was between me and it, and she was right, she was usually right about boundaries in a way that would have been insufferable in anyone less kind—and then her hand was there, and we went down together into the warm dark where the world kept its heart.

* * *

**Ines**

Let me tell you what nobody tells you about treason: it gets easier. That's the horror of it. The first map I burned, my hands shook for an hour. By the fifth I was humming. And now here I am, one year later, descending into a sealed god's bedroom carrying contraband cartography, and my only anxiety is whether the ink will smudge in the damp.

The new map is folded in the inner pocket of my coat, against my heart, which is where August says I keep everything that matters and everything I'm ashamed of, and he's right, and I've stopped arguing. It is the finest piece of work I have ever done and no academy will ever see it. It shows the Hollow as it is now: the stair, the landings, the chambers where the roots of the old shrines reach down like the roots of a tree reaching for water. Every shrine is marked the same way—a small circle, open, unclosed. In cartography an open circle means *inhabited*. A closed circle means *ruined*. There is not one closed circle on this map. There never will be again.

Above us, in the marble rooms where the chestnut bombs fall on important heads, the free cities' envoys and the capital's ministers are negotiating the Treaty of the Two Banks, which will decide who owns the water that rises sweet from the Hollow now, and who owns the grain it grows, and who owns the prayers people still whisper over their stoves. They have been negotiating for eleven months. They have produced four hundred pages and no treaty. Meanwhile the water rises for everyone regardless of paperwork, because the god beneath us never learned to read a deed, and I find this the single most comforting fact of my life.

August thinks I don't know he listens to the negotiations. He sits in his charm shop—legal now, licensed, the sign painted by children from the Hollow district who charged him two sweets apiece—and he serves tea to clerks and washerwomen and the occasional guilt-ridden minister, and he hears everything, because a man who spent fifteen years being invisible learns to keep the ears for it even after the invisibility wears off. Then he comes home and tells me everything in the order he thinks I can bear it, which is not the order it happened in, and I let him, because marriage is mostly about letting people arrange the truth gently.

"The envoy asked about you again," he'd said last night, over the stew. "Madame Osk. She wanted to know if the vessel retains any residual capacity."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her the vessel retains a residual capacity for stubbornness and an excellent recipe for barley soup." He'd stirred the pot. His hand had trembled slightly—not fear, never fear anymore, just the mortal shakiness of a body that feels its own weight—and I had watched the tremble the way I once watched footpaths converge on a ruined shrine: reading a pattern, knowing what it meant. "She'll be back. They're always back. Everyone wants a piece of the miracle."

"And what do you want?"

He'd looked at me across the stew, and the lamplight had done that thing it does in our kitchen, pooling in the hollows of his face, making him look both older and younger than his years, a man twice rebuilt. "Soup," he'd said. "And you. In reverse order of difficulty."

So: the map. The flowers. The descent. We reached the bottom chamber at midmorning, though you couldn't have told it from the light, which is perpetual dusk down there, golden and sourceless, and the moss glowed faintly underfoot like a floor of crushed emeralds, and the air tasted of bread baking somewhere very far away.

August knelt first. He always kneels first. He set his marigolds down on the stone lip of the wellspring—that round mouth of living rock where the warmth breathes out in slow tides, in and out, in and out, the breathing of something enormous and content—and he said the words he always says, which are not a prayer, whatever the priests who come sniffing around might wish. They're just words. *Here. For you. From us.*

Then it was my turn, and I unfolded the map.

* * *

**August**

I have watched Ines do many remarkable things. I watched her lie to Serel Doss with a straight face while Doss held the evidence of her perjury in his own gloved hands. I watched her hold my head in her lap while the nightshade did its year-late work and talk me through the dying the way you talk someone down a ladder. But nothing she does undoes me quite like the way she presents a map to the god.

She holds it open with both hands, arms straight, the way you'd present a petition to a queen, and she reads it aloud—not the labels, but the meaning. "Shrine of the Nine Sisters: inhabited. Shrine of the Deep Well: inhabited. Processional stair: in use." Her voice carries in that chamber the way voices do nowhere else on earth, clear and small and utterly certain. "Nothing sacred is marked ruined. Nothing will be. As long as I'm alive to draw it."

And the wellspring breathes out, warm, and the moss brightens, and the whole chamber does the thing I can only describe as *leaning in*, and I stand there holding my hat like a farm boy at a funeral, undone for the ten-thousandth time.

Afterward we sit, as we always sit, on the bench the masons left—an old slab of Godfall stone worn smooth, facing the wellspring—and we eat the bread and cheese we smuggled down in Ines's satchel, because the god has made it very clear over the past year that it prefers company to offerings, presence to prostration, lunch to liturgy. Ash would have loved this, I think. Ash, my brother-in-everything-but-blood, who died empty and grateful with his hand in mine, who wanted the god to rule through us and learned instead that the god only wanted to go home. Sometimes I bring a second cone of flowers for him. Today I forgot, and I felt bad about it, and then I thought: no. Ash wouldn't want flowers. Ash would want me to eat the damn cheese and stop performing grief. So I ate the cheese.

"Do you ever miss it?" Ines asked. She asks this maybe once a season, always casually, always watching my hands instead of my face, because my face lies beautifully and my hands never learned.

"Miss what? The immunity?" I rolled a crumb between my fingers. "You know what I miss? Not tasting anything. Fifteen years, everything I ate arrived pre-filtered through dread. Now a strawberry is just a strawberry. It's astonishing. Strawberries are astonishing. Nobody told me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest answer I have." I leaned back against the stone, and the warmth came up through my spine like a hand pressed flat between my shoulders. "Do you know what I feel, down here? Nothing. That's the miracle. No voice, no pull, no door swinging open in my chest. Just warmth. Whatever it needed me for, it's done needing. I'm not a vessel anymore. I'm a visitor." I paused. "A regular visitor. The kind who brings flowers and gets fed."

"And the dreams?"

Ah. The dreams. Once a month or so, I dream of a hearth fire in a dark kitchen, and it doesn't speak, but I wake with the sense of having been asked a question and answered correctly. I told her about those, early on, because we had sworn off secrets the way other couples swear off drink—one day at a time, relapse forgiven, total honesty the only policy that survived the war.

"I still have them," I said. "But they've changed. It used to feel like standing at a door. Now it feels like sitting by the fire. Somebody's house. Somebody glad I came." I looked at her. "That's all it ever wanted, I think. To be carried home and sat down by its own hearth. Everything else—the vessels, the covenant, the war—it was all just the long road home, and everyone who walked it got broken on the way."

"Except you."

"Except me." I took her hand. Her fingers were ink-stained, as always; she'd been drawing before dawn, I could tell, the little compulsive survey maps she makes of our own street, our own kitchen, the garden she's planning. Mapping is her prayer. I've made my peace with that. "Except me, and only because of you. You know that. The nightshade should have taken me. You chose wrong, by every law you were raised under, and I'm alive because of it."

"I didn't choose wrong," she said, with the mild ferocity she reserves for errors in her own favor. "I chose correctly. The throne was wrong. I just took fourteen years to check my work." She squeezed my hand. "Cartographer's habit. Never trust the first draft."

* * *

**Ines**

On the climb up, August walks behind me, and I feel him there the way sailors feel a lighthouse—not looking at it, steering by it. Halfway up the third flight he stopped, the way he sometimes stops, palm against the wall, listening to something I can't hear and no longer need to. I waited. The lamp-glow moved gently on the stone. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of city, a cart rattled past, and a vendor cried plums, plums, sweet autumn plums, and the sound fell down the stairwell to us like a leaf falling down a well.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Nothing," he said, and smiled, and for once nothing was the truth. "It's just saying hello. Or goodbye. Or *see you next month*. It's hard to parse. Warmth doesn't have grammar." He caught up to me, and we climbed.

At the top, the capital received us the way it receives everyone: with noise, and chestnuts, and the smell of bread, and the distant murmur of men in marble rooms failing to finish a treaty. August squinted up at the sun like a man surfacing from deep water. I checked my map pocket—habit, comfort, prayer—and found it lighter by one map and heavier by one certainty, which is the correct exchange rate.

They will finish the treaty eventually. Madame Osk will get her water rights and the ministers will get their tariffs and somewhere in the margins, if I have anything to do with it—and I do, because they've asked me to draw the boundary maps, officially, publicly, the traitor-turned-surveyor-of-record, which is either irony or grace and I've stopped asking which—they will write into law that the wellsprings belong to no crown and no league, but to the ground itself, and to whoever lives above it. It's a strange clause. Lawyers hate it. I drew it anyway, in the shape of a small open circle.

August offered me his arm at the top of the stair, formal as a courtier, ridiculous as ever. "Madame Corvane-Villalobos. Your chariot awaits. It's a fruit stall, but the plums look honest."

"How can you tell?"

"Fifteen years of professional training. If they were poisoned, I'd know." He grinned, and I laughed, and we went up into the ordinary daylight together, two liars at one table, two carriers of one secret, except the secret isn't heavy anymore. That's the inheritance, in the end. Not the bloodline, not the power, not the maps or the memories. Just this: that what we carry now weighs nothing at all, because we carry it together, and the thing below us sleeps warm, and above us the plums are sweet, and nothing sacred is marked ruined, and nothing ever will be again.
