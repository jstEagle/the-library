## 1. The Quiet Trade

The bone told him everything before the widow finished speaking.

Marguerite Coldharbour kept his eyes down on the workbench, his knife moving in slow, patient curls through the antler blank, while Maren Ostrow sat across from him wringing a handkerchief that had long since stopped being dry. She was describing her husband, Tam, dead these six weeks—drowned in the millrace, pulled out blue and swollen—and she was describing him in the way widows did: too much detail about the wrong things. The way he whistled off-key. The fact that he'd never once remembered to bring in firewood without being asked twice.

What she didn't know was that the antler already knew all of it. Had known it since Marguerite had first laid his palm against the raw material that morning and felt the hum of it—the particular vibration that meant this piece had been near death, had drunk something of it. Antler cut from a stag found at the river's edge, sold to him by a tanner who thought himself lucky. The bone remembered the water. And through the water, faint as a voice heard through a wall, it remembered Tam Ostrow's last minute of life.

*Cold,* the bone said, in a register no ear could catch but his. *So cold, and the wheel just there, just out of reach, and I thought—foolish—I thought of her hands in flour.*

Marguerite's knife did not slip. Ten years of practice had taught his hands to be traitors to his face. He carved, and he listened, and he said nothing.

"Everyone says he went quick," Maren was saying. "The miller's boy says he didn't even cry out. But I lie awake thinking—is that true? Did he suffer? A person wants to know. A person needs to know, don't they? Even if it's bad. Especially if it's bad."

"It's a mourning charm," Marguerite said, because that was what he always said, in the same level craftsman's tone he used for everything. "I'll carve his initials into the crown of it, and a millwheel beneath. You hang it by the door. It holds the memory of him. That's what people say, anyway." He smiled the small smile that made customers trust him. "Bone keeps what we give it."

That much was even true. Just not in the direction anyone imagined.

He worked in silence for a while, letting her grieve in the particular quiet that mourners needed, and the bone murmured against his fingertips like a sleeping animal. *Her hands in flour.* It was such a small thing to die holding. Marguerite had heard dying men recite debts, curse their brothers, call for mothers forty years in the ground. Once—a memory he kept locked in a room at the back of his mind and visited only when he hated himself enough to need it—he had heard a man die whispering the name of a woman who was not his wife, and had carved that widow her charm anyway, and had lied to her with his whole gentle face.

"You've a gift for it," Maren said suddenly. "Everyone says so. The way your pieces seem to—catch them. Catch the person. Old Hessa bought one for her mother and swore she could feel her in it."

"People say that," Marguerite agreed mildly.

"No, but—" She leaned forward, and for a moment her red-rimmed eyes had something sharper in them, something almost hungry. "It's not just likeness, is it. There's something in your work. Like the piece is listening."

The knife paused for exactly half a heartbeat.

"Lucky carving," he said. "And good bone. I'm choosy about my suppliers."

She laughed then, wetly, apologetically, and the moment passed the way such moments always passed—because Vellamark was a town where people had learned, over ten years, not to look too closely at anything. The proclamations had started mild. *Report unlicensed practitioners.* Then the ash marks began appearing on doors. Then last spring, in the capital, they had burned a hedge-witch in the market square while children threw stones, and after that nobody in Vellamark looked closely at anything ever again.

Maren counted out the coin onto the counter—nine coppers and a worn silver bit, more than she should have spent, and both of them knew it, and neither said so. Marguerite swept it into his palm with the practiced motion of a hundred identical transactions.

"For the charm itself, four coppers would do," she said. "But for—"

"For catching him," Marguerite finished quietly. "I know."

He wanted, as he always wanted, to tell her the truth. *Your husband died cold and afraid but thinking of you, and his last thought was tender, and he did not call out because there was no one to call to, and none of it was your fault.* He wanted to press the words into her like pressing flowers, so she could keep them flat and safe forever. Instead he wrapped the finished charm in cloth, tied it with string, and handed it over with both hands, which was the closest his craft came to honesty.

"He loved you," Marguerite said. "If it helps. They usually do."

Maren's face crumpled and reassembled itself, and she clutched the little bundle to her chest like an infant, and she thanked him three times on her way out the door, and then the shop was empty and the bell above the door had stopped swinging and Marguerite stood alone in the grey afternoon light with nine coppers and a silver bit and the taste of other people's grief in his mouth.

* * *

He put the coin in the chipped crock behind the counter and did not count it, because counting it made the guilt arithmetic and arithmetic made it worse. Four coppers' worth of carving. Five coppers and a silver bit's worth of theft—not of money, never of money, but of the thing underneath the money. Of the words he heard and sold back to the bereaved in the currency of vague comfort. *They loved you. They went quick. They weren't angry with you.* Nine times out of ten it was true; the bones rarely lied about love. But he decided which truths to tell, and that deciding was the crime, and he committed it daily, cheerfully, with excellent craftsmanship.

His father would have called it a sin. His father had been a lay-brother of the old Order, back when the Order still existed to be lay-brothered in, and had taught Marguerite two things before the purges took everything: how to read grain in bone, and that a gift used in hiding curdles. *You'll become a merchant of the dead,* his father had said, in the last winter, in the cellar, in the candlelight. *You'll hear their voices and sell them by the inch.* And Marguerite, fifteen years old and furious with fear, had said, "Better than burning," and had meant it, and had never once in ten years stopped meaning it, and had never once in ten years stopped being ashamed of meaning it.

He cleaned the bench. He oiled the knives. He hung the day's finished pieces on the display rack by the window: a birthing charm for the midwife, a luck-piece for the carter whose son was joining the army, a set of buttons for the cooper's wife who liked pretty things and suspected nothing. Ordinary work. Safe work. Work that any honest carver in any honest town might have done, if honest carvers were allowed to exist in a kingdom where the new king's Hunt rode from village to village with their ledgers and their ash-pots and their tidy lists of names.

Through the window he could see the evening street of Vellamark doing its evening business: lamplighters, a fish cart, children running a hoop past the fountain. Someone had chalked a little whale on the cobbles outside the tanner's—some child's game—and it hadn't been rained away yet. Small domestic life, going on. Going on and on and on, thought Marguerite, while I sit in here with a head full of the drowned.

Because the antler was still on the bench. He should have set it aside with the offcuts for the stove. Instead he picked it up again, and pressed his thumb into the raw crown of it, and let Tam Ostrow's last minute wash over him one more time—not for the widow's sake now, but for his own, though he couldn't have said what he got from it. Warmth, maybe. Company. The dead were better company than the living, in a way; they never asked why he lived alone above his shop, never wondered aloud why a man of thirty with kind eyes and steady hands had no wife, no partner, no close friend closer than arm's length. The dead simply spoke, and he listened, and between them they maintained a silence that felt almost like companionship.

*Just there,* the bone murmured, the millrace's last moment looping its loop. *The wheel just there, and her hands in flour, and the water coming in cold—*

"I know," Marguerite said softly, to the empty shop. "I know. I'm sorry. I gave her what I could."

The bell over the door rang.

He set the antler down fast, too fast, and turned with his craftsman's smile already fixed in place—but it was only Brammel the baker's boy, come for the buttons his mother had ordered, and Marguerite sold them to him with small talk about the weather, and the boy left, and the bell rang again, and the shop settled back into its dusk quiet.

His heart took longer than the shop did.

This was the shape of his life, and he knew its shape the way he knew grain: mornings of ordinary trade, afternoons of the quiet crime, evenings alone with the voices, nights of dreamless exhausted sleep broken sometimes by the sense—never more than a sense—that somewhere far off, someone was keeping a list, and his name was on it, and the ink was drying.

He banked the stove. He lit the lamp over the workbench, because he always carved a little after closing, work with no customer attached, work that belonged only to him. Tonight it was a piece of whalebone he'd had for years without knowing why he'd kept it—dense, creamy, old beyond guessing, bought in a job lot from a peddler who'd swindled himself selling it. It never spoke to him. That was the strange thing, the thing he returned to the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth: every bone he'd ever touched carried some murmur of the lives near it, except this one, which was silent as snowfall. And yet his hands went to it night after night. His knife knew things about it his mind didn't.

He was setting the first curl of it free when he heard, distantly, through the walls of the world, the sound that had been coming closer for ten years: drums, or hooves, or nothing at all—hard to say, hard to be sure, easy to dismiss.

Marguerite kept carving.

Outside, the last light went off the rooftops of Vellamark, and somewhere in the dark east along the king's road, something was riding toward the town with a list in its saddlebag, and the first name on the list belonged to an apothecary two streets over, and the second name had not yet been written.

But it would be.
