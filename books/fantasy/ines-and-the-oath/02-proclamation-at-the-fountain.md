## 2. Proclamation at the Fountain

The criers came at midmorning, which was unusual. In Vellamark, official business kept banker's hours—proclamations read at noon when the square was fullest and the wine sellers had begun their second round of the day. Midmorning meant the crown wanted the crowd sober.

Marguerite was in the square because of a hinge. Old Tam Wicker's shop door had been groaning for a week, and Marguerite had promised to look at it, and looking at hinges was the kind of errand that let him feel like an ordinary man with ordinary hands. He had the small brass fitting wrapped in cloth in his coat pocket, and he was thinking about whether Tam would want it oiled or replaced when the first bell rang from the courthouse tower—not the hour bell, but the flat, quick toll they used for royal decrees.

The crowd gathered the way crowds do: reluctantly at first, then all at once, drawn by the gravity of other people stopping. Marguerite stopped too. He told himself he was simply curious, a craftsman with ten minutes to spare, and he believed himself for about as long as it took the first crier to climb the fountain's rim.

There were three of them, in the grey-and-gold livery of the crown, and behind them stood two soldiers in the new black tabards—the ones with the white circle stitched on the chest, the sigil nobody in Vellamark had a name for yet except *the mark*. The fountain itself was dry; it had been dry since summer, its stone mermaid holding her empty urn over nothing, and the crier stood on its lip with one boot on the mermaid's tail as if she were a stepstool rather than the last piece of beauty anyone had bothered to carve in this city for fifty years.

"By order of King Aldric, sovereign of the realm," the man began, and his voice carried the flat practiced music of someone who had said these words in six towns already this month, "and by counsel of the Lord Protector's court, be it known—"

Marguerite's hands went still inside his pockets.

He knew what was coming. He had known since the edicts started appearing on church doors in autumn, each one a little more specific than the last, each one worded so carefully that a reasonable man could pretend not to be included. *Practitioners of unlicensed arts.* *Concealers thereof.* *Aiders and abettors.* The words had been climbing toward him all season like water rising in a cellar, and he had watched them come and told himself the same thing every time: *they mean hedge-witches, they mean the wild ones out past the fens, they don't mean a carver who makes pretty trinkets.*

"—that a special commission is hereby established, to be called the Hunt, charged with the finding, examination, and judgment of those who practice forbidden arts within the realm."

*Finding, examination, and judgment.* Three clean words doing the work of one dirty one.

"The Hunt shall hold authority in all cities, towns, and hamlets of the crown, answerable to no magistrate but the crown itself." The crier unrolled a second parchment, though Marguerite suspected it was blank or near enough; the performance mattered more than the text. "Persons possessing knowledge of such practitioners are bound by duty and law to report them. Persons sheltering them share their guilt. Persons who come forward freely, before inquiry reaches them, may expect the crown's mercy."

Someone near the front asked what mercy meant. The crier didn't answer. One of the black-tabarded soldiers did, though not with words—he simply looked at the questioner until the man found something fascinating to study in the cobblestones.

Marguerite stayed. That was the strange part, the part he would turn over later that night and hate himself for. He could have walked away. He should have. Instead he stood in the cold with his neighbor's shoulder against his and listened to the whole of it, every clause, the way a man reads his own name in a list of the dead.

The second crier took up the recitation: penalties. He heard them all. For practice of the art: fire. Not imprisonment, not branding—fire, in the market square of the offender's own town, "that the people may see justice done." For concealment: the king's prisons. For aiding: fines that would strip a family to the walls. And for those who came forward and named names—a reward. A substantial one. Enough coin to buy a shop outright. Enough to buy a life twice over.

Marguerite looked at the faces around him. That was the worst of it, worse than the fire clause, worse even than the soldier's stare. Because the faces weren't horrified. They were *interested*. A woman beside him had gone very still in the way of someone doing arithmetic. Two boys near the fountain were whispering, and one of them glanced at the other with an expression Marguerite recognized from his own childhood—the look of a boy who has just learned that some people are worth more caught than free. An old fishmonger crossed his arms and nodded slowly, once, as though a long-awaited bill had finally come due.

They don't know, Marguerite told himself. They're thinking of strangers. Of fen-witches and curse-mongers and the things mothers warn children about. Not of the man who fixed their doors and carved their mourning charms and never once, not in ten years, did harm with what he had.

But then the third crier stepped forward, and he wasn't reading anymore. He was holding up a jar of something white, and the crowd quieted on its own.

"Ash," the crier said. "Marked and mixed by the Hunt's own hand. Where the gifted dwell, the mark will be set upon their door, that all may know and none may claim ignorance."

He climbed down. The soldiers began to move through the crowd, unhurried, and the crowd moved apart for them the way water parts for a keel, and Marguerite understood with a cold drop in his stomach that the proclamation was not the announcement. It was the alibi. The real work had been happening for days, quietly, door by door, while the city slept and argued about grain prices.

The soldiers walked to the far side of the square. To Cordery Lane.

To the apothecary.

* * *

Maren Ostler's shop sat on the corner where Cordery Lane met the square, under a sign painted with a mortar and pestle and, beneath it in smaller letters, *Remedies Honest & True*. Maren herself was a round, brisk woman of sixty who smelled of camphor and honey and who had once, three winters ago, sold Marguerite willow bark for his knees without asking why a young man's knees ached like an old man's. She had winked when she handed him the packet. He had never decided what the wink meant.

Now two soldiers stood at her door, and one of them held the jar.

Marguerite watched from thirty feet away, from the shadow of Tam Wicker's awning, and he did not move and he did not breathe and he hated himself for both.

Maren came out when they knocked. She was wearing her apron. She had flour on her hands, or powder—something white, something innocent—and she stood on her own threshold in the morning light and listened to whatever the soldier said, and then she laughed.

It was a short laugh, disbelieving, the laugh of a woman being told a joke in poor taste. Then the soldier dipped his hand in the jar and pressed it flat against her doorframe, and drew it down, and the laugh stopped.

The mark was a handprint. That was all. A white handprint on dark wood, five fingers, smeared slightly at the heel of the palm. But the crowd around the square had drifted toward it now, the way crowds drift toward anything, and Marguerite heard the murmur go through them like wind through wheat—that soft collective intake, that rustle of a hundred private calculations beginning at once.

Maren said something. Her voice didn't carry, but her posture did: chin up, hands still dusty, planted on her own step like a tree that intended to remain a tree. The soldier said something back. She shook her head. And then, in a gesture Marguerite would see every time he closed his eyes for months afterward, she reached up and unpinned the little bundle of dried lavender that hung beside her door—for luck, for sweetness, for no reason at all—and she dropped it into the gutter.

So they would have nothing of hers to burn, Marguerite thought wildly. So the smoke wouldn't smell like her.

The soldiers left the mark and moved on. Maren stood in her doorway a moment longer, looking out at the street—at her customers, her neighbors, the people whose fevers she had cooled and whose babies she had delivered—and the street looked back, and the street did not step forward, and after a long moment Maren Ostler went inside and closed her marked door behind her.

The crowd dispersed the way crowds do, in twos and threes, already talking. Marguerite heard fragments as they passed him. *"Always thought there was something off about her tinctures."* *"My cousin swears the woman cured his gout in a day, and how's that natural?"* *"Well. Someone has to say something to the magistrates, don't they? If it's true."*

*If it's true.* As if the mark itself were not the verdict. As if a white handprint required corroborating testimony.

Tam Wicker appeared at his elbow, hinge forgotten, his old face gray. "You hear that?" he muttered. "Fire. In the square. Here." He spat, but carefully, to the side, where no soldier could see. "My grandfather told me they did this in the south, forty years back, after the Order fell. Thought it was finished. Thought we'd got past it." He rubbed his jaw. "Maren Ostler. What in God's name has Maren Ostler ever done?"

Nothing, Marguerite thought. Or everything, depending on which side of a handprint you stand. Maybe she'd eased a birth-pain with a charm-word her grandmother taught her. Maybe she'd whispered over a fever. Maybe she'd only winked at the wrong customer, and someone with a grudge and a memory had written her name on a list.

Maybe lists worked that way. He wouldn't know. He'd made sure never to know.

"I'd best get home," he heard himself say. His voice sounded fine. Steady. Ten years of practice, steady as a plane across oak. "Work waiting."

Tam clapped his shoulder. "Mind yourself, lad. Days like this—" He didn't finish. Nobody finished sentences anymore. They just left them hanging in the cold air like laundry that would never come in dry.

* * *

He took the long way home. He told himself it was to avoid the square, and it was, partly. But the long way ran down Chandler's Row, and Chandler's Row was where the shops were, and the shops—

The shops had his work in their windows.

He hadn't thought about it in years. That was the trick of a double life lived long enough: it stopped feeling doubled. He was Marguerite Coldharbour, carver, and his pieces hung in four windows along this street because his work was good and his prices fair and he delivered when he said he would. Little charms, mostly. Whistles and birds and knotted hearts, tokens for sweethearts and christenings. Safe things. Things he made with the gift folded up small inside him like a moth in a closed fist, so that the wood sang only to itself and no one else.

Except.

He stopped outside Grell's Dry Goods, where a row of his carved birds hung on ribbons in the window, turning slowly in the draft from the door. Five wrens and a swallow. He remembered making them in an afternoon last spring, remembering nothing about the afternoon except that it had been a good one, the knives moving easy, the light coming gold through his workshop window.

And he remembered—now, standing in the street with his heart going hard against his ribs—that the swallow had taken him an extra hour. Because he'd liked it. Because somewhere between the first cut and the last, without deciding to, without even noticing, he had listened to it. Just a little. Just to hear what the bone of the wood wanted to become. And the swallow in the window was smoother than the wrens, more alive, and any carver in the city would call it his finest piece, and any hunter in the city, if hunters knew what to look for, might call it evidence.

How many? he thought. How many pieces in how many windows, in how many houses, on how many necks, carrying that little fold of listening inside them like seeds in a coat pocket? Ten years of work. Ten years of being careful ninety-nine times out of a hundred, and that hundredth time—the swallow, the christening cup for the Harrow girl that he'd sung strength into when the child was sick, the mourning whistle he'd made for the widow just yesterday, God help him, *yesterday*, with the dead man's last words still living in the grain—

His hands were shaking. He looked down at them and watched them shake, and felt absurdly distant from them, as if they belonged to another man who had done reckless things for a decade and left the reckoning to fall due today.

He walked on. Past Grell's, past the stationer's with his carved letter-knives, past the chandler's where a mother was lifting her toddler up to touch one of his knotted-heart tokens, the child crowning with delight, the mother smiling, saying something like *isn't it lovely, isn't the carver clever.*

Every piece a confession. Every window a witness stand. He had hung his life's work in public view like a man nailing his sins to a church door, and he had called it commerce, and the city had called it craft, and now the crown had arrived to translate.

By the time he reached his own street his breath was coming short and his palms were slick inside his gloves. His house sat where it always sat, narrow and slate-roofed, wedged between the cooper's and the widow Anselm's, indistinguishable from a hundred others. Smokeless chimney. Shutters latched. Nothing about it said *here lives a criminal.* Nothing about it ever had.

That, he understood now, climbing his stairs two at a time, had been the whole of his strategy for ten years: *nothing.* Be nothing. Carve nothing true. Want nothing visible. And it had worked, it had worked beautifully, right up until the morning the crown started paying neighbors to notice things.

Inside, he barred the door and stood in his own workshop with his back against the shelves, breathing.

The shelves. God. The shelves.

Forty years of tradition said a carver kept his masters—every significant piece, every commission, every experiment, logged and retained, proof of a lifetime's honest trade. His shelf held ten years of them. Charms and vessels and little bone boxes, and among them, tucked at the back where he'd told himself they were merely keepsakes, the ones that hummed. The ones he'd listened through. The widow's husband's voice, still warm in yesterday's whalebone. A dozen others. Twenty.

Evidence. A shelf full of it, arranged alphabetically by client.

He pushed off from the wall and stood in the middle of the room, and the fear that had been walking beside him all morning finally caught up and laid its hand on his shoulder, and Marguerite Coldharbour—who had buried a gift, buried a past, buried a name, and built a quiet respectable nothing on top of the graves—understood that the digging-out had to start tonight.

All of it. Everything that hummed. Into the hearth, into the fire, down to ash, before the ash came looking for him.

He was reaching for the first box when someone knocked at the front door.
