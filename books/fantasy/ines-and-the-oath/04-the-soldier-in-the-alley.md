## 4. The Soldier in the Alley

The charcoal burner's hut had been empty for three years, and Marguerite had been its only tenant for two of them.

It sat at the ragged edge of Vellamark, where the cobbled streets gave up and became mud, where the tannery stink thinned into woodsmoke and pine. The roof leaked in exactly one place, over the bed he'd built from shipping crates, so he'd learned to sleep curled at the foot like a dog avoiding the rain. There was a hearth that drew poorly. A window with a shutter that didn't latch. A door with a bar that would stop a drunk but not a determined man.

He'd chosen it for all of these reasons. Nobody came to the charcoal burner's hut unless they were lost, dying, or looking for him—and he'd spent ten years making sure the third category stayed empty.

The night after he burned his life's work, he sat on the crate-bed with the whalebone piece in his palm and tried to understand why he hadn't fed it to the fire with the rest.

It was small. A curl of white bone no longer than his thumb, carved into something between a wave and a sleeping face. He had made it—he was almost sure he had made it—but he couldn't remember when, or for whom, or what voice had come up through it while his knife worked. Every piece he'd ever carved left an echo in him, the way a bell leaves a hum in the air. This one was silent. It was as if the bone had swallowed its own sound.

*Keep me,* it seemed to say, without saying anything at all.

Outside, the wind moved through the pines with a sound like slow breathing. Somewhere far off, toward the city, a dog barked itself hoarse and quit. Marguerite turned the whalebone over once more, tucked it into the inner pocket of his coat—against his heart, though he'd have denied that if asked—and lay down to sleep the sleep of a man who had done the sensible thing and hated himself for it.

He woke because the door was opening.

Not knocked. Not forced. Just opening, slowly, with the particular care of someone who has spent the last hour dragging themselves across half a mile of dark ground and has no strength left for drama. The bar he'd rigged groaned, lifted, fell aside.

Marguerite was on his feet before his mind caught up, knife already in his hand—the good knife, the carving knife, which was the only weapon he owned and the only one he'd ever wanted. The hearth had burned down to a red eye of coals. In its light he saw the door swing inward and a shape fill it, then fail to hold the shape, then collapse against the frame.

"Light," the shape said. "Please. I need to see your hands."

*Your hands.* Not *your face.* Not *your name.* Whoever this was had come looking for a carver, and there was only one reason a man came looking for a carver in the middle of the night, bleeding, in the year of the Hunt.

"I have nothing worth taking," Marguerite said, and heard how thin his own voice sounded.

"I'm not here to take." The shape slid down the doorframe until it sat on the floor, one leg thrust out at an angle legs weren't meant to hold. "I'm here to give."

Marguerite should have barred the door and gone out the window. He had gone out windows before; he had a whole philosophy of windows. Instead he took the candle stub from the shelf, lit it at the coals, and brought it closer, and hated himself a little more for every step.

The soldier was young. That was the first thing. Younger than Marguerite by five years at least, with the kind of open, honest face that armies love and graveyards collect. Royal blue coat, torn open from collar to navel where someone had worked a blade in and twisted. His hands were pressed flat against his own belly, and between his fingers the dark came out not in pulses but in a long, patient welling, the way water rises through sand.

He'd been gutted hours ago. He should have been dead twice over. Men did not walk a mile with wounds like that; men did not *find* things with wounds like that. Which meant whatever had kept him moving was stronger than blood, and Marguerite had lived long enough among bones to know that strength had a name.

"Who cut you?" Marguerite asked. Stupid question. Useless question. But his mouth needed to say something ordinary or it was going to start screaming.

"Doesn't matter." The soldier's smile was gray around the edges. "They'll say I deserted. Let them." He held out his free hand, palm up. "You're Coldharbour. The carver. They said—the reliquary man said—you could hear them."

*They said.* Marguerite's stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept going. He had burned every vessel he owned. He had scrubbed the ash from his hearth with his own hands. And still his name traveled through the dark markets of Vellamark like smoke through cloth.

"You've been misinformed," he said. "I make charms. Mourning pieces. Pretty things for sad people. I don't hear anything."

The soldier looked at him. Just looked, with those honest young eyes, and there was no accusation in it, only a terrible patience, the patience of a man who had carried something too far to put it down now.

"The king is not the king," he said.

The words went through Marguerite like cold water through cloth.

"What?"

"King Aldric." The soldier's breath was shortening; Marguerite could hear it, the wet whistle underneath each inhale. "At the winter review, I stood guard. Three paces from him. He looked at me—looked *through* me—and said, in a voice that wasn't his, *the crown is hungry.*" The soldier laughed, and the laugh cost him; he bent forward over his ruined belly and had to wait for the pain to pass before he could speak again. "I reported it. You know what happens to guards who report things about the king? I was reassigned. To the night watch. To the places where men go missing."

"They cut you for it."

"They cut me for asking questions." He nodded, slow, as if even nodding had to be budgeted now. "But I asked the right ones before they caught up. There's an old thing. From before the Order fell. The oath-bone. It binds the king to—it binds—" His hand slipped against his wound and came away black in the candlelight. "It knows. The bone knows what he is. And it can't stay in the palace, they're hunting it too, they're hunting everything now, they've gone mad, the whole city's gone mad—"

"Stop." Marguerite crouched beside him, hands hovering uselessly. He knew wounds. He had carved memorials for enough dead men to know exactly how this ended, and knowing made it worse, because there was nothing in his skill that could turn any part of it. "You need a surgeon. There's a woman in Tanners' Row—"

"There's no time." The soldier caught his wrist. His grip was astonishing, iron and ice together, the grip of a man spending his last coin. "Listen to me. Listen. I'm going to give you something and you're going to hate it and you're going to keep it anyway. That's the whole of my plan. That's all I've got."

From inside his coat he drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, stained dark along one edge. He pushed it into Marguerite's hands.

It was heavier than it looked. It was warm. Those were the two facts Marguerite's mind seized on, the craftsman's facts, weight and temperature, because every other faculty he possessed had begun to recoil.

"Open it," the soldier whispered.

Marguerite untied the cord with fingers that did not feel like his own.

Inside the oilcloth lay a bone. Long as a forearm, curved like a crescent moon, white as the whalebone charm in his pocket and yet utterly unlike it—this was old, old past reckoning, its surface worked with lines so fine they might have been written by a spider, spiraling inward toward a hollow at the center. It was beautiful the way deep water is beautiful. It was beautiful the way a wound is beautiful.

And it was *loud.*

Marguerite had spent ten years learning to keep his gift behind a wall—to let voices arrive and pass through him like weather, to nod and carve and take the coin and never, never listen too closely. He had built that wall stone by stone until even he couldn't hear over it.

The oath-bone went through the wall like it was made of paper.

It didn't speak. It *screamed.* Dozens of voices, scores, layered one atop another like strata in cut earth—men's voices, women's, some human and some that had only ever pretended to be, all of them speaking at once, all of them saying the same thing in different tongues and different centuries: *bound, bound, we are bound, the crown is hungry, the king is not the king, unbind us, unbind us, unbind—*

Marguerite cried out and nearly dropped it. The candle guttered. The coals in the hearth flared as if something had breathed on them.

"It does that," the soldier said quietly. "Gets louder near the palace. Quieter if you wrap it. But it never stops." He watched Marguerite with those honest eyes, and now there was something else in them—relief, vast and terrible, the relief of a courier seeing his letter delivered. "You can hear it. Thank the dead. You can actually hear it."

"I can't do this." Marguerite tried to push the bundle back. The bone's voices poured through his skull, through his teeth, pooling at the base of his tongue like molten metal. "Whatever you think I am—I carve trinkets for widows. I'm nobody. Find someone else. Find the Order—"

"The Order is ash." Each word cost the soldier visibly now; his sentences were coming shorter, gaps widening between them. "The vessels are scattered. The rite is lost. Except—" his hand found Marguerite's sleeve, gripped, "—except the man who hears. The reliquary man swore it. One listener left in Vellamark. One pair of ears the bone will speak to."

"He lied."

"Maybe." The ghost of a grin. "But you're holding it, aren't you? And it's singing to you. I can see it in your face. I've watched men hear bells and angels and their mothers' ghosts, and they all make that same face."

Marguerite wanted to deny it. The bone chose that moment to shift its scream, and beneath the hundred voices, distinct as a finger drawn through fog, he heard something new—a single voice, young and frightened and familiar, and it took him three full seconds to understand why it was familiar.

It was the soldier's voice. Speaking from inside the bone. Saying words the soldier had not yet said:

*"Tell him the truth. Tell him I chose this. Tell him it isn't his fault."*

Marguerite looked up. The soldier was watching him with an expression of dawning wonder, as if he'd felt something pass between himself and the bone, some thread drawing taut.

"It talks about me," the soldier said softly. "Doesn't it."

"You're dying," Marguerite said. "People say strange things when they're dying."

"That's not what it says." The soldier's eyes were bright now, feverish, fixed on some middle distance where the future was being arranged. "What does it say? Tell me. I want to know what I'm going to be."

And God help him, Marguerite told him. Word for word. Because he had spent ten years refusing to carry other people's messages, and in the end he couldn't refuse a dying man the one thing he had to give.

The soldier listened all the way through. Then he nodded, satisfied, like a man hearing his own eulogy read and finding it accurate.

"Good," he said. "Good. Then it's true, and I get to choose it." He sagged back against the doorframe. Blood had stopped welling now; there wasn't enough left. His skin had gone the color of the candle wax. "Listen. The bone needs three vessels. Old things. Bone-vessels, from the Order's vaults. Scattered when the Order fell—I found one reference, one, in a sealed archive, before they caught me. The rite won't work without them. And the rite has to happen at the Renewal. Winter coronation. When the king swears again, the binding is thinnest, and—" his breath hitched, wet, long, "—and so is the spirit's grip. One night. One chance. Then it wears him through, and whatever's under the crown stands up wearing Aldric's body, and this kingdom learns what hungry means."

"I don't know anything about rites. I don't know anything about spirits. I carve *charms.*"

"Then find someone who does." The soldier's hand crawled across the floorboards, leaving a dark trail, and closed over Marguerite's ankle—not gripping now, just resting, a weight going out. "There are others. The Order had keepers. Some survived. Some ran. Ask in the whisper-markets. Ask for the Broken Order. Someone will find you, because the bone wants to be found, and things the bone wants—" his voice was fraying at the edges, words coming loose like stitching, "—things the bone wants, happen."

His eyes had drifted half-closed. Marguerite knelt there, holding a screaming relic in one hand and a stranger's ankle in the other, and understood with perfect clarity that he was watching a man die, and that he would be the last person to ever hear this man's voice, and that in ten years of hearing the dead he had somehow never learned what to do for the living.

"What's your name?" he asked. "If I'm going to carry this. If I'm going to remember tonight. Tell me your name."

The soldier smiled. It was a good smile. It was the kind of smile that made armies love him and graveyards collect him.

"Dannel," he said. "Corporal Dannel Ferro. Fourth—"

The rest never came. Not out loud.

Because the bone screamed it instead. All at once, in Dannel Ferro's own voice, in a chorus of a hundred borrowed voices underneath, the bone spoke the words he would never finish: *Fourth Regiment, honor guard, eleven years' service, mother in Corrow Vale, never married, afraid of deep water, kind to horses, died carrying the truth to a stranger in a charcoal burner's hut and did not regret one step of the road.*

Every detail. Everything. The whole small, bright, ordinary catastrophe of a human life, recited by a relic that had apparently been listening to him since the moment he touched it.

Marguerite bowed his head over the bone and wept, silently, the way he'd taught himself to weep years ago—no sound, no shaking, just salt running off his jaw onto the oilcloth. Because he had heard thousands of last words, and they were always small. *Tell my wife the blue dress. Bury me facing the sea. Who will feed the cat.* He had never once heard a whole life spoken entire, and it was the most terrible and holy thing he had ever witnessed, and it was happening in his filthy hut at the edge of Vellamark while the city slept and the Hunt sharpened its knives.

When he looked up, Corporal Dannel Ferro was gone. His eyes were open. His hand was still resting on Marguerite's ankle, light as a bird's foot.

Marguerite eased the hand away, laid it on the man's chest, folded the other one over it. He did what he'd done for strangers before, in other years, other huts: he closed the eyes, straightened the coat as best he could over the wound, turned the face slightly toward the window so it caught the first gray suggestion of dawn.

Then he sat back on his heels, in the silence, except that there was no silence, because the bone was still murmuring in his hands—quieter now, almost gentle, a hundred voices settled into something like sleep.

*Unbind us,* they whispered. *Unbind us, listener.*

"No," Marguerite said aloud, to a dead man and a haunted relic and the empty room. "No. I didn't ask for this. I kept my head down for ten years. I burned everything. I'm *nobody.*"

The bone said nothing. It didn't have to. It simply grew warmer against his palms, patient as a seed, patient as winter, and somewhere in the layering of its voices he heard—faintly, unmistakably—Dannel Ferro's laugh.

Marguerite wrapped the oath-bone back in its oilcloth, careful as a midwife, and held it against his chest, and felt it beating there like a second heart that was not his and never would be.

Outside, the sky was turning the color of ash. Somewhere between here and the city, someone would notice a corporal missing. Someone would retrace his bloody mile. Someone with a captain's insignia and a ledger full of names would stand in this doorway within a day, maybe within hours, and look at the hearth, and look at the floor, and find the dark stain that no amount of scrubbing would ever fully lift.

He had until dawn to be gone. Maybe less.

Marguerite packed the way he'd practiced in his head for ten years: one bag, tools, dry bread, the whalebone charm from his coat pocket—he hesitated over it, that silent little piece that wouldn't burn, wouldn't explain itself, and put it back against his heart anyway. He banked the coals. He dragged the pallet to cover the worst of the stain, knowing it was useless, knowing he was leaving a signature.

At the door he stopped. Looked back once at Corporal Dannel Ferro, lying straight and quiet in the gray light, wearing the expression of a man who had finished his errand.

"I'll carry it," Marguerite said. "I don't know how, and I'll probably die badly, and I resent every minute of it. But I'll carry it."

The room, naturally, said nothing. Rooms always do.

But the bone, against his chest, pulsed once—warm, brief, unmistakable—like a hand squeezing his heart from the inside, like something ancient and starving and patient settling into step beside him.

Marguerite barred the door behind him out of habit, absurdly, protecting an empty hut, and walked east into the pines as the dawn came up bloodless and cold.

Behind him, three miles back toward the city, a rider on the north road paused at a fork, consulted a scrap of paper covered in a precise, disciplined hand, and turned her horse toward the charcoal district.

She was ahead of schedule. She was always ahead of schedule.

And she was looking for a carver.
