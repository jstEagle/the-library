## 5. Ash on the Wind

The rain had stopped by the time Serane Voss reached the alley behind the tanners' row, and she was grateful for it. Rain made corpses lie about themselves. It washed away the small honest details—a drag mark in the dust, the direction blood had spattered, the way a man's fingers curled when he died reaching for something or reaching away from it. She had learned to read bodies the way other people learned to read ledgers, and she did not like her ledgers smudged.

She crouched beside the soldier and did not touch him yet.

Young. Twenty-two, perhaps twenty-three. Royal livery under a borrowed cloak, which meant he'd been trying not to be seen wearing his allegiance, which meant he'd been running from someone specific and not merely from danger in general. The gut wound was a sword wound—entered left of the navel, angled upward, delivered by someone taller or standing above him. He'd been on his knees, or he'd been brought to them.

"Name?" she asked the corpse, as she always did. It was a habit her lieutenants had learned not to mock. The dead answered her more honestly than the living did, if you knew how to listen—not with magic, never with that, but with patience and attention and the willingness to hear things you didn't want to hear.

The dead soldier answered with his hands. They were empty, but the right one had a smear of tallow on the palm, and the fingers were curled around the memory of an object. Something he'd held hard, and recently, and had let go of only at the end. Serane turned his hand over gently. Tallow, and beneath it, a fine powder. She touched it to her tongue. Bone dust.

She sat back on her heels and let the alley tell her the rest.

A struggle, first—scuffed boot-heels near the wall, a smear of blood where someone had braced a shoulder. Then the soldier had been dragged two paces toward the mouth of the alley and left. Not hidden. Left deliberately, the way you'd leave a message. And whatever he'd been carrying had been taken, but not by whoever cut him open. The drag marks went one way. The blood trail went another. Two visitors, then, or three. A dying man in an alley in Vellamark, and everyone who came to him wanted something from his hands.

"Captain." Lieutenant Brakke stood at the alley's mouth, holding his helmet under his arm like a schoolboy who'd lost his slate. He was a good soldier and a poor liar, and right now his face said he had news he'd already decided she wouldn't like. "Coroner's man says he's Third Company. Corporal Edmun Hale. Reported absent from the garrison four days ago."

"Four days." Serane stood, knees cracking—she was thirty-six and her body had begun filing complaints. "And no one reported it to the Hunt?"

"The garrison commander says he assumed desertion. Said he'd have filed it by week's end."

"By week's end." She looked down at Hale. Deserters didn't gut themselves in alleys holding bone dust. "The garrison commander is a fool, Brakke. Note that I said that, in the report, in those words. He can take it up with me."

"Yes, Captain."

"Who found the body?"

"Charcoal burner's boy, on his way to market. Ran straight to the watch, and the watch sent for us within the hour. No one's touched anything but the boy, and he swears he only looked."

"Swearing is what people do instead of telling the truth." But she said it without heat, and Brakke relaxed a fraction. She crouched again, working her way down the body methodically. Boots—good ones, worn at the outside edge, a man who walked a lot on hard roads. Belt—buckle scratched, standard issue, nothing hidden in the folds. Under the cloak, against his chest, a leather cord with nothing on it. The cord was important. Cords like that held things, and the skin beneath it was chafed in a ring, which meant whatever it had held had hung there long enough to leave a mark, and had been removed recently enough that the mark was still angry and red.

Something he wore against his heart. Something he'd given away, or had taken, in the last hour of his life.

"Brakke. When did the boy find him?"

"Half past the morning bell."

"And the coroner estimates death?"

"Sometime in the night. He won't be more precise without—" Brakke stopped himself. He knew better than to finish that sentence. *Without magic.* Half the old coroner's craft had been the quiet gift of listening to wounds, and the Hunt's edict had cut that out of the trade like a tumor, and now everyone guessed and no one knew.

"In the night," Serane said. "So he lay here bleeding for hours, and in all that time, someone came to him. Someone he trusted enough to give his last possession to." She looked at the tallow and bone dust again. "Or someone he begged."

She rose and walked the alley end to end, slowly, the way her father had taught her to walk a field after a bad harvest—looking not for what was there but for what was missing. Her father had been a farmer before the fire. He'd taught her that loss leaves a shape, if you look for the shape and not the thing.

What was missing here: a relic. She didn't know its name or its nature yet, but she knew its weight. The chafed cord, the bone dust, the curled fingers, the way the dying man had been positioned facing the street—*here I am, come find what I gave away.* Corporal Edmun Hale had not been a deserter. He had been a courier, or a thief, or a penitent, and he had died carrying something the crown would very much want back.

And someone had beaten the Hunt to it.

* * *

The Hunt's headquarters had been a granary in its previous life, and it still smelled faintly of rye, which Serane had decided long ago was better than most buildings deserved. The great floor had been divided into offices with walls of planed pine that didn't reach the ceiling, so that every interrogation, every argument, every weeping informant carried to every other room. Serane had put a stop to the torture chambers in her first month—not out of mercy, whatever her detractors said, but out of arithmetic. Torture produced confessions, and confessions were cheap, and cheap evidence rotted a case from the inside. She preferred patience. She preferred the long ledger.

Her office held a desk, two chairs, a map of Vellamark province with pins in it, and a shelf of ledgers in her own cramped hand. On the wall behind the desk, in a plain frame she'd made herself, hung a charcoal drawing of a farmhouse. It was the only decoration she permitted, and no one in the granary asked about it, because everyone in the granary knew.

Her father. Her mother. Her brother Tam, who'd been nine. The artist had worked from Serane's description, because there had been nothing left to draw from. The fire had taken the house and the orchard and the well and the four of them inside it, and it had been set by a hedge-witch named Maren Ostell, who had been paid in eggs and mutton by a neighbor over a boundary dispute, and who had not, by all accounts, thought of it as murder at all. *A working,* she'd called it, weeping in her cell. *A small working. I didn't know fire could be so greedy.*

Serane had been seventeen. She'd been at market. She'd come home to a field of ash and a smell she still caught sometimes in her sleep, in bakeries, in forges, in the sweet curl of any smoke that was too thick and too sweet.

She did not hate the gifted. That was the thing her enemies got wrong, the lie they told about her in the taverns of the old quarter—*Voss the witch-burner, Voss who loves the fire.* She did not love the fire. She had seen what the fire did when it was asked, politely, in the old words, by a woman who meant no harm. Power did not need malice to kill. It needed only carelessness, and the gifted were the most careless people in the world, because the law had made them liars and liars made mistakes, and their mistakes burned houses down around sleeping children.

So she hunted them, and she did it cleanly, and she did it by the book she had written herself, and if the book was more merciful than the mob wanted and less merciful than the gifted deserved, that was the nature of books. You wrote the best one you could and then you lived inside it.

Brakke knocked and entered with the morning's file. "Coroner's preliminary on Hale. Gut wound, single stroke, blade of good steel, and—" he hesitated, "—he says the wound was dressed."

"Dressed?"

"Bandaged. Crude, but someone tried. Strips of linen, packed with moss. The coroner thinks it was done within minutes of the wounding. Whoever cut him stayed long enough to try to save him."

Serane turned that over. A killer who dressed his victim's wound. A soldier who'd been absent four days, moving through the city in a borrowed cloak, wearing a relic against his heart. "Get me Hale's service record. Every posting, every commendation, every reprimand. And find out where he was born and whether he has family in Vellamark."

"He has a mother in the Weavers' District. Watch brought her in this morning. She's in the front room, Captain. She's—" Brakke's jaw worked. "She's asking to see her son."

Serane closed her eyes for one breath. This was the part of the work that never got easier, and she had made a rule about it in her first week, and she had never once broken the rule: the Hunt did not send clerks to do this. The captain went.

* * *

Hale's mother was a small woman with her son's same brown eyes and a shawl she was gripping the way drowning people grip wreckage. She stood when Serane entered, which was wrong, which was everything wrong with the world in a single gesture—a mother standing for a captain because the captain's office had the power to tell her how her boy had died.

"Sit, please," Serane said. "I'm Captain Voss. I'm sorry for your loss."

"You're the witch-hunter." The old woman's voice was steady. Steadier than Serane's would have been. "They said you'd come. They say you always come yourself."

"I do."

"Is it true what they say elsewise? That you're fair?"

"I try to be."

"Then be fair to me, Captain, and tell me plain. Was it the gifted that killed my Edmun?"

The question sat in the room like a stone. Serane had been asked versions of it a hundred times—*was it one of them, was it their kind*—and she had learned what the question really meant. It meant *give me someone to hate, because the grief needs somewhere to go.*

"I don't know yet," Serane said. "I know he was wounded with a sword by someone who regretted it enough to try to save him. I know he'd been away from his company four days, moving carefully, and I know he was carrying something he valued. I'm going to find out what, and who, and why. That's what I can give you now. I won't give you a villain before I've earned one."

The old woman studied her for a long moment. Then, quietly: "Edmun wrote me a letter, three weeks back. I wasn't going to say anything, because he'd have been furious, but—" she fumbled in her shawl and produced a folded paper, worn soft at the creases. "You can read it. I've read it so many times I've near worn through it. I don't understand half of it. Maybe you will."

Serane took the letter with both hands, the way you take something that might be evidence and might be a dying woman's last hold on her son.

The hand was a soldier's hand, blocky and careful.

*Mother — Do not be afraid for me. I have been given a charge that weighs more than I thought anything could weigh, and I am going to set it down where it can do some good instead of the harm it has done in my keeping. There is a man in Vellamark who can hear the dead in what he carves. I know how that sounds. I did not believe it either, until I heard it myself. If I do not come home, do not let them tell you I was a deserter. I was the opposite of a deserter. I was the only one who stayed true to the oath that matters. Burn this.*

He had not burned it. Mothers didn't.

Serane read it twice. The granary's rye-smell seemed suddenly very loud, and behind it, faintly, she caught the sweet-thick ghost of smoke that wasn't there.

*A man who can hear the dead in what he carves.* A bone-carver. A gifted man, unregistered, unburned, hiding in her city for gods knew how long—she'd have felt it, the way you felt a draft under a door. And a soldier of the crown who had decided, in the space of some revelation Serane could not yet imagine, that this criminal was the only man who could be trusted with a charge *that had done harm in my keeping.*

She thought of the chafed cord. The bone dust. The relic, gone.

"Captain?" the mother said. "Is it bad, what he did?"

Serane looked at the woman who had given her the one thing she hadn't had to take, and made a decision she would think about for a long time afterward.

"What he did," she said, "was keep an oath. I'm going to find out to whom."

* * *

That evening she stood at her map with a cup of cold tea going colder, and she pinned a new marker into the Weavers' District, and another at the tanners' row, and she drew a slow circle with her finger around the whole of the low city, where the shops were and the workshops were, where a bone-carver could hang his charms in windows and be beloved and be invisible all at once.

Brakke appeared at her door. "The garrison commander sent over Hale's record, Captain. And—" he paused, and she could hear him choosing, which was unlike him. "And there's a requisition in the file. Hale drew equipment four days ago. Rations for a week, a mule, and a map. The map's still checked out. It's not with his body."

"Where was the requisition for?"

"That's the thing, Captain. The route marked on the requisition—it's redacted. Sealed. The order came from above the garrison. Above the provincial command." Brakke lowered his voice as though the pine walls might lean in. "It came from the palace, Captain. Whoever sent Corporal Hale out with a sealed route and a relic around his neck wasn't garrison. That's crown work."

Serane set down her tea. Outside, the lamps of the granary yard were being lit one by one, and the wind had come up from the river, carrying the tanneries' stink and, beneath it, the first woodsmoke of the season, and she stood very still and let herself think the thought she had been refusing all day.

A soldier with a palace secret. A relic of bone. A dying man's whisper about a carver who heard the dead. And somewhere in her city, a gifted man who was about to discover that the crown's own secrets had teeth.

"Brakke," she said. "I want a likeness drawn of the man we're looking for. The carver. Pull every guild register, every market toll list, every shop license in the low city. Someone has seen his face."

"And when we find him, Captain?"

Serane looked at the charcoal farmhouse on her wall. Her father had taught her that loss leaves a shape. She was beginning to suspect that this shape—the one Hale had died leaving—was not the shape she'd been told to hunt.

"When we find him," she said, "we find out what the palace is afraid of."
