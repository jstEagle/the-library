## 6. Ines of the Broken Order

Marguerite did not sleep.

He sat in the charcoal burner's abandoned hut with the oath-bone wrapped in three layers of linen and shoved beneath a loose floorboard, and he told himself that distance was safety, that bone was only bone, that the screaming he'd heard in the alley had been blood loss and terror playing tricks on a dying man's witness. He told himself these things the way a man tells himself the water rising around his ankles is only rain.

The bone did not scream now. That was worse. It *waited*. He could feel it through the floorboards, through his boots, through the soles of his feet—a low patient hum, like a hive thinking. Like something with too many mouths holding its breath.

Near dawn he gave up on sleep and took it out.

Unwrapped, in the grey light leaking through the shutter slats, the oath-bone looked almost ordinary: a forearm's length of yellowed femur, dense as ivory, banded with old gold at either end. The gold was worked into script so fine it might have been stitching. He turned it in his hands and the hum rose to meet him, and this time—because he was tired, because he was alone, because his gift had never once asked his permission—he listened.

Voices. Not layered this time but braided, wound tight as rope, and every strand was an oath. *I swear by crown and marrow. I swear my breath to your breath. I swear until the bone forgets me.* Dozens of them, hundreds, generations deep, all spoken into this one length of dead king's leg—or whatever it was—and all of them still burning. Oaths didn't die when their speakers died. He'd known that since he was nine years old and carved his first charm from his grandmother's knucklebone and heard her say *mind the stove* as clear as if she stood behind him. But these oaths were different. These had been fed. Kept warm. Renewed, year after year, by someone who needed them alive.

And underneath them, deeper than the rest, something else. A voice like cold water moving under ice. It said nothing. It breathed.

Marguerite set the bone down on the floor as gently as if it were a sleeping animal and wiped his palms on his knees.

"That's far enough."

The voice came from the doorway, and it was not a voice he knew, and it was armed.

He turned slowly. A woman stood in the gap of the door with the dawn behind her, which meant she had chosen her position deliberately, which meant she had been there long enough for the light to move. Cloaked, hood back, dark hair cut short and practical, a sword at her hip worn the way soldiers wore tools rather than ornaments. She was perhaps forty. Her face had the particular economy of someone who had stopped spending expressions on things that didn't pay.

Her eyes went to the bone on the floor and something moved through them—hunger, grief, recognition—all of it banked fast, like a flame snuffed under a lid.

"Pick it up," she said. "Slowly. And give it to me."

"I don't know what you think—"

"You're the carver." She stepped inside and closed the door behind her without looking at it. "Vellamark. Charms in six shop windows, mourning pieces for half the widows on Tanner's Row, and not one signature, because signed work can be traced and you are not a stupid man. The soldier who died in Cooper's Alley two nights ago crawled four hundred yards on a opened belly to reach the one man in this city who could hear what that bone is saying." Her gaze flicked to the floorboard he'd pried up, the linen wrappings. "I watched you carry it out of that alley. I've been following you since."

"Then you saw him give it to me," Marguerite said. His mouth was dry but his voice held, mostly. "Dying men don't bequeath things to thieves."

"Dying men give things to whoever's closest." She held out her hand. Gloved leather, fingers steady. "The bone. Now. I'm not going to ask again, and I want you to understand that everything I've said so far has been the polite version."

He should have given it to her. Every sensible instinct he owned—the ones that had kept him alive and unburned for ten years—screamed at him to pick it up, place it in her gloved hand, thank her, walk away, go home, carve something harmless, be nobody again. That was the whole architecture of his life. Give dangerous things to dangerous people and stay small.

But his hands did not move. And he understood, with a strange floating clarity, why: the bone was humming against the floorboards, and the hum had changed, and it was reaching toward this woman the way iron filings reach toward a lodestone. The oaths inside it knew her. Whatever she was, the bone had been waiting for her longer than it had been waiting for him.

"You're of the Order," he said.

The steadiness in her face cracked—one hairline fracture, quickly plastered. "There is no Order."

"No," he agreed. "There isn't. It fell eleven years ago, its members scattered or hanged, and the crown pretends it never existed, except once a year at the Renewal when the king swears his oath on bones no one will explain." He had learned that much in ten years of listening to the dead, who gossiped worse than fishmongers. "But you stand like a keeper. Left foot forward, weight even, hands where they can be seen. My grandmother was Vellamark-born; the old keepers trained here before the motherhouse closed. She showed me the stance when I was a boy. Said it was how honest people stood."

"And what do you know about honest people?" she said softly.

"Nothing. I carve charms for grieving women and pocket coin for hearing their husbands' last words. I'm the least honest man you'll meet today." He picked up the bone. Held it between them, not offering, not withholding. "So we're both liars. Which means you'll appreciate that I don't believe you'll kill me for it. If you wanted the bone badly enough to kill, you'd have done it while I slept. You wanted me awake. You wanted me to *choose*."

Something shifted in her posture—not softening exactly. Recalculating.

"Ines," she said, as if paying a toll. "Ines Marchena. Formerly First Oath-Keeper to the household of the crown. Formerly. The word does a great deal of work."

"Marguerite Coldharbour. Formerly nobody."

"The bone," Ines said. "Please."

"Why?" And then, because lying to her felt suddenly both pointless and dangerous: "What is it? What did that soldier die carrying?"

For a long moment he thought she would simply draw her sword and take it. He watched her decide against it, watched the decision cost her something, and understood that she was a woman who had run out of allies and could not afford to make enemies of the ones fate dropped in her path, even reluctant ones.

"It's a king's oath-bone," she said. "One of seven. They were made forty years ago when the Order bound a guardian spirit to the crown of Aldric's line—to the *crown itself*, not the man, so that the protection would pass to each heir. Seven bones, seven anchors, each sworn by a different generation of keepers. This one was kept in the palace reliquary under triple seal." Her jaw tightened. "Three nights ago it left the palace in the hands of a dying guardsman, which means someone inside those walls wanted it gone, which means—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "It means the binding is failing. Or being failed. And if the anchors scatter, the spirit comes loose, and what happens next will not have a name anyone survives using."

"The soldier said something." Marguerite heard his own voice go flat and careful. "Before he died. He said—"

The window exploded.

Not the glass—the glass had been gone for years—but the shutter, burst inward off its hinges in a spray of rotten slats, and with it came lantern-light and mail and a voice like a whip: *"Hunt! Nobody moves!"*

Later, Marguerite would reconstruct it in fragments. The red-and-black livery filling the doorway. The crossbow already leveled. The sergeant's face, young and frightened under his helm, shouting something about the relic, about orders from Captain Voss, about the carver of Vellamark whose likeness was already being inked onto a hundred posters. He remembered thinking, absurdly, that he had never been drawn before, that some clerk was at this very moment sketching his face from a description, giving him features in charcoal that would outlive him.

Mostly he remembered Ines moving.

She moved the way water moves when a dam goes—not fast so much as inevitable. One moment she was standing beside him negotiating for a bone; the next she had taken the shutter-slat from the floor and put it through the sergeant's wrist with a snap he felt in his own teeth, caught the falling crossbow before it discharged, and driven her shoulder into the second soldier's sternum hard enough to fold him over the windowsill and out into the mud. The third man in the yard got his blade half-drawn. Ines's boot found his knee, then his temple, and he went down among the chickens, who objected loudly.

"Back door," she said, already sheathing nothing, unarmed again except for borrowed violence. "Now. Take the bone."

"They'll have the street covered—"

"They'll have the *front* covered. Sergeants are predictable. Move."

He moved. Through the lean-to, past a rusted cauldron, out into the grey morning and the strip of scrubland behind the hut, the oath-bone clutched to his chest like an infant. Behind him he heard a shout, then a whistle—two notes, ascending, the Hunt's signal for reinforcement—and then Ines beside him, running low and silent, and the strange discovery that she was not running *away* from the hut but *around* it, along a game trail he'd never noticed, downhill toward the stream bed.

"They'll expect flight," she said between breaths. "Flight means roads. Roads mean checkpoints. So we don't take roads."

"We?"

She didn't answer. The stream bed took them a half-mile through alder and standing fog, and twice they froze as riders passed above on the bank, close enough that Marguerite could hear the horses breathing, and both times Ines put a hand flat against his chest and pressed him down into the wet stones, and both times the pressure of her glove burned through his shirt like a brand, and he hated himself a little for noticing.

They surfaced, eventually, in the root-cellar of a ruined millhouse a full mile from the hut. By then the sun was properly up and the fog was burning off and somewhere behind them dogs were barking at the wrong coordinates. Marguerite sat down against the stone wall, discovered his hands were shaking, and watched Ines bar the cellar door with a beam older than both of them.

"You saved me," he said.

"Don't make it strange."

"You had the chance to let them take me and lift the bone off my corpse. It would have been cleaner. Quieter. No witnesses." He turned the oath-bone over in his lap. The hum had settled since they'd run—almost, he thought, contentedly. "Instead you spent yourself saving a stranger. So either you're a fool, or you need me alive for something more than courier duty."

Ines leaned against the door, arms crossed, and studied him for a long time. Outside, distantly, the dogs gave up.

"You heard the bone," she said finally. "Don't lie to me—I watched your face change in that alley. You heard it."

"Yes."

"Then you know it isn't just an anchor. It's a record. Every oath ever sworn into it, every renewal, every ceremony, forty years of them, all still sounding." She pushed off the door and crouched in front of him, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the exhaustion under her eyes, the kind that had stopped being temporary years ago. "I helped forge that binding, Coldharbour. I was nineteen years old and I believed every word my teachers told me, and I stitched my own oath into that bone alongside everyone else's. When the Order fell, I ran. Eleven years I've run. And three days ago a guardsman I've never met died in an alley getting this thing to the only man in Vellamark who could hear it, and I have to believe that wasn't chance, because the alternative is that it was, and I can't—" She stopped. Breathed. "You're not a courier. Couriers deliver. You're a listener. And right now you are the only person alive who can tell me what that bone is saying, and whether I'm too late."

"Too late for what?"

Ines looked at the bone in his hands the way people look at graves.

"For the king," she said. "Tell me what you hear. All of it. Even the parts that don't make sense. *Especially* those."

Marguerite looked down at the oath-bone, at the fine gold script, at the braid of burning voices, and beneath them the cold water moving under ice, breathing, waiting, patient as winter.

"It doesn't sound like a guardian," he said quietly. "It sounds like something being *fed*."

Ines closed her eyes. And when she opened them again, whatever she had been holding back for eleven years came up behind them like a tide, and she said, "Then it's started, and we have maybe a month. Sit down, Coldharbour. I'm going to tell you what we bound to that crown—and then you're going to tell me why the dead won't stop talking about you."

Outside, the hunt horns sounded—far off, searching the wrong hills—and inside the ruined mill, over the murmur of a bone full of kings, the liar and the keeper began, at last, to trade truths.
