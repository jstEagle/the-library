## 7. The History of the Binding

The safehouse was a tanner's loft above a shuttered dye-works, and it smelled like a hundred years of secrets soaked into the beams. Marguerite sat on an upturned crate with the oath-bone wrapped in his coat on his knees, and he did not let go of it, even when Ines paced close enough to take it.

She had tried twice already. Both times, his hands had closed around the bundle before she'd finished reaching, quick as a trap snapping shut. The second time she'd stopped, looked at him with something that might have been respect, and gone back to pacing.

"You should sleep," she said.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep well in rooms where people are lying to me." She stopped at the narrow window, where grey dawn was leaking through a crack in the boards. Below, Vellamark's eastern quarter was waking—the fish carts, the gulls, the ordinary machinery of a city that had no idea what was sitting in its attic. "You heard it. In the alley. Don't tell me you didn't."

"I don't know what I heard."

"Then let me tell you what you heard." She turned. Up close, in this light, he could see how tired she actually was—not soft-tired but worn-through tired, the kind that lives in the bones. He noticed himself thinking *in the bones* and almost laughed. Everything came back to bone, these days. "But first you put that thing down."

"No."

"Marguerite."

"You know my name now," he said. "That's twice you've used it. That means we're past the part where you pretend you'll walk away if I don't cooperate."

Something flickered across her face—annoyance, or maybe the ghost of amusement, quickly killed. She dragged a second crate over and sat, elbows on her knees, close enough that he could see the faded scar through her left eyebrow, old and white and deliberate-looking.

"The Order of the Kept Oath," she said. "Ever heard of it?"

"Everyone's heard of it. They burned the records when I was a boy. My master told me they were sorcerers who got above themselves."

"Your master was half right." Her mouth twisted. "We were sorcerers. We didn't get above ourselves—we were put there. By the crown. Forty years ago, King Aldric's father, Osric the Fourth, was dying of something no physician could name. Wasting. Wandering. Talking to empty corners. And a woman named Halvard—first keeper of the Order, my teacher's teacher—came to court with a proposal."

Marguerite's hands tightened on the bundle without his permission. Through the wool, faint as a pulse under frost, he felt the oath-bone respond to her voice. It knew her. That was the worst part. It had been screaming since the alley, a layered chorus of voices he couldn't separate, but whenever Ines spoke near it, one thread went quiet and attentive. Listening.

"What kind of proposal?" he asked.

"A binding." Ines leaned forward. "There are old spirits in this world, carver. Older than the kingdom, older than the crowns. Most of them don't care about us at all—one more mayfly dynasty, one more crop of short-lived things scrabbling in the dirt. But some can be *interested*. Called. Given a shape to wear and a purpose to hold. Halvard proposed calling one such spirit—a guardian, a warden-thing, patient and vast—and binding it to the crown itself. Not the king. The *crown*. So that whoever wore it would be protected. Shielded from assassination, from illness, from the wasting that was killing Osric. A king who could not be killed by any mortal hand."

"And the price?"

Ines was quiet for a moment. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"There's always a price," she said finally. "The spirit doesn't work for free. It feeds. Not on blood—that would have been simple, honest, refuseable. It feeds on *memory*. On the self. Slowly, so slowly no one notices at first. A king forgets a face. Misplaces a year. Starts saying strange things in his sleep. And the binding was built to last—it holds the spirit in place, keeps it fed and settled, generation after generation. Every Renewal, every winter coronation ceremony, the king re-swears the oath and the binding is renewed and the feeding continues."

"That's monstrous."

"It was *necessary*." She said it fast, sharp, like a door slammed on something behind it. Then, quieter: "It was necessary. Osric recovered. He ruled eleven more years, lucid, strong. His son Aldric took the crown and the same protection. Two kings saved. That was the arithmetic Halvard taught us: one slow drain against sudden death, against civil war every time a monarch dropped. Any keeper would make that trade again."

"But?"

"But the binding has soured." Ines looked down at her hands. They were scarred too, he saw—knuckles nicked and crosshatched, a working woman's hands, though he'd never learned what work. "What was meant to be a sip became a draught. Something changed—I don't know what, not yet—and the spirit stopped rationing itself. Aldric's grandfather forgot his own son's name two years before he died. Aldric's father lasted less than a decade on the throne before they started keeping him from public view. And now Aldric himself—" She stopped. Started again. "Aldric was crowned at nineteen. He's thirty-four now. Fifteen years of feeding, and the last three have been a flood. There are days he doesn't know where he is. Days he doesn't know *what* he is. And the spirit grows stronger with everything it takes, and the binding that should hold it in check is instead holding the doors open."

Through the coat, the oath-bone shivered. Marguerite realized he could feel it the way you feel a dog trembling against your leg in thunder. He unwrapped one corner of it despite himself—a sliver of pale bone, long as a finger, carved all over with script so fine it might have been etched by a needle of light. Even half-wrapped, it made the air around it feel thin.

"This was the soldier's," he said. "He called it an oath-bone."

"It's a key." Ines stared at it like it was a snake she'd once been fond of. "Every keeper carried one. It's cut from the spine of the first vessel—the anchor-bone, we called it. It attunes to the binding. Lets a keeper approach the crown-spirit without being... consumed. Without it, anyone who tries to unbind the spirit will simply be emptied first. Another meal."

"And you want it because—"

"Because I helped forge the binding." The words came out flat and hard, like stones laid in a row. "Not the original. The renewal. Twelve years ago, when Aldric was crowned, I was the junior keeper who inscribed the new oath-cycle. My hand wrote half those lines. I trusted my mentor, Edran Vael, who told me the rite was unchanged, the safeguards intact. They weren't. He'd loosened them. Whether he was bribed or bewitched or simply believed the spirit's promises, I never learned—he vanished the night the binding sealed, and I was left holding a forged rite and a young king who began to starve from the inside out." Her jaw worked. "When the Order fell—when the Hunt burned the motherhouse and scattered us—I ran. I've been running for twelve years. And now a soldier dies in an alley pressing a key into a stranger's hands, and the only person alive who knows how to use it is me."

Marguerite sat with that. The gulls cried. Somewhere below, the dye-works' old kettle ticked as it cooled, a small mechanical heartbeat.

"Why him?" he asked finally. "Why give it to me? He was dying. He could have brought it to you—to anyone in the old Order."

"He couldn't find anyone in the old Order. We're dead men walking, most of us. And he wasn't choosing you, carver. He was choosing the nearest person who could *hear* it." She nodded at his hands. "Yes. I know what you are. I've known since the fountain square, watching you flinch at the criers. You hear the dead through the pieces you carve. Don't insult us both by denying it."

The cold that went through him then was an old cold, familiar as his own heartbeat. Ten years of hiding, of carving charms that whispered to him alone, of smiling at customers while their dead husbands murmured their last words through the whalebone. One sentence from this woman and it was all ash.

"I could report you," he said. "Right now. Walk to the garrison and say a fugitive oath-keeper is squatting over a tannery. They'd burn you in the market square by evening."

"Yes," Ines said. "And then you'd be the man who handed over the only key to saving a king being eaten alive, because you were frightened. You'd live with that the way I live with the rest." She held his gaze. "I'm not threatening you, Marguerite. I'm telling you what we both already know: we're tied together now. You can't unhear what's in that bone. I can't undo what I carved. The question isn't whether we trust each other. The question is whether we're going to be useful to each other before the Hunt finds us."

He looked down at the oath-bone. The voices inside it had gone quiet—not silent, never silent, but hushed, the way a room goes hushed when the important conversation starts. He thought of the soldier's last breath, the wet rasp of it, the weight of the bone pressed into his palms like a newborn child being handed over.

*"The king is not the king."*

"What do you want from me?" he asked.

"Three things." Ines held up fingers, one by one. "Three vessels. Bone-vessels, made by Halvard herself when the binding was first forged—containers shaped to hold the spirit if it ever had to be drawn out. Anchor, chalice, and reliquary. When the motherhouse burned, they were scattered. Hidden by keepers who died before they could pass on where. I know fragments. Rumors. A drowned chapel south of the city. A hermit in the western hills who was once one of us. A third place I won't speak of until we've survived the first two." She folded her fingers closed. "Bring the three together, bring the key, stand before the king at the winter Renewal, and perform the unbinding rite. Draw the spirit out of Aldric and into the vessels. Seal them. End it."

"And if we fail?"

"Then the spirit finishes eating him, wears him whole, and rules this kingdom wearing a dead man's smile until someone burns the palace down around it. Which is exactly what the Hunt is for, incidentally—did you never wonder why a crown that outlawed magic employs so much of it?" She stood, restless again, moving to the window. "They know. Someone at court knows what's happening to the king, and rather than fix it, they're burning every gifted soul in the realm who might stumble onto the truth. You're not prey, Marguerite. You're loose ends."

The word landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He thought of the apothecary's shop marked with ash. Of his own charms hanging in shop windows, little pieces of himself displayed to every passing constable. Of ten years of careful, cowardly quiet.

"How long?" he asked. "Until the spirit finishes with Aldric."

"Months. Perhaps weeks. The Renewal is in—" she counted silently, lips moving— "eleven weeks. At the Renewal the binding is at its strongest, and so is the spirit. If we unbind him there, in the heart of the rite, we have a chance. Miss that window and the next one is a year away. He won't last a year."

Eleven weeks. Marguerite looked at the oath-bone, at the fine bright script, at the voices sleeping inside it. He had spent a decade perfecting the art of not being involved. Of carving grief for other people and feeling none of it himself, or telling himself he felt none of it. And now a dying man had put a kingdom in his hands because his ears worked wrong.

"If I do this," he said slowly, "I want something in return."

Ines turned from the window. Wariness sharpened her face. "Name it."

"When it's done—when the king is free—you clear my name. Whatever records the Order kept, whatever the Hunt has on me, I want it gone. I want to carve charms in daylight and hang them in my own window and not flinch when a crier opens his mouth. I've been a criminal for ten years for the crime of listening. I want it to end."

For a long moment she just looked at him. Then something in her shoulders eased—not softening, exactly. Recognition.

"That," she said, "is the first honest thing either of us has said all night. Done. My name and yours, cleared together, or neither." She crossed the loft and held out her hand—scarred knuckles, working woman's grip. "Partners, then. Reluctant ones."

He took it. Her hand was warm and rough and it shook, very slightly, and he pretended not to notice, the way she was pretending not to notice that his did too.

"One condition of mine," she added, releasing him. "The oath-bone stays with me. It's a keeper's tool. You wouldn't hand a surgeon's knife to—"

"No."

"Marguerite—"

"It chose me." He hadn't known he was going to say it until the words were out, and once out, they felt true in a way that scared him more than anything she'd described. "In the alley. Out of everyone it could have gone to, a dying man put it in *my* hands, and it hasn't screamed since—not the way it did at first. It talks to me. Quietly. Like it's glad someone's listening." He met her eyes. "You had your twelve years with this world, Ines. This part is mine."

She stared at him, and he watched her wrestle with it—watched the keeper's territorial instinct grind against something else, something that looked almost like relief. The bone had been hers to carry alone for twelve years, insofar as a bone could belong to anyone. Maybe it was a kindness, having someone else hold the screaming thing.

Maybe it was just surrender.

"Fine," she said at last. "You carry it. But you don't listen to it alone. Ever. Whatever it says to you, whatever the voices show you—you come to me first. Promise me that."

"Why?"

"Because the last man who listened to it alone was Edran Vael," she said quietly, "and he's the reason a king is being eaten alive."

The loft went very still. Below them the city went on waking, fish carts and gulls and church bells, oblivious. Marguerite looked at the oath-bone in his lap—at the fine bright script, at the voices sleeping inside it—and understood, finally, the true shape of what he was holding. Not a relic. A confession. Forty years of good intentions curdling into something hungry, and every person who'd touched it along the way leaving fingerprints.

"Ines," he said. "The third vessel. The place you won't speak of. Why not?"

Her face did something complicated—grief and fear fighting for the same ground.

"Because it's in the motherhouse ruins," she said. "Where I was made. Where the Order died. And because the ward on it opens to an oath-keeper's blood, and I am the last oath-keeper there is." She picked up her pack, slung it over one shoulder, businesslike again, walls back up. "We leave within the hour, before the markets fill and someone remembers your face from the fountain square. South first—the drowned chapel. Sleep if you can, carver. You'll want your strength."

She went down the ladder ahead of him, and Marguerite followed, the oath-bone cradled against his chest, warm as a living thing.

As he reached the bottom rung, the voices stirred—not the chorus, not the scream, but one voice, low and familiar, the soldier's voice, worn smooth as river stone:

*South,* it said. *Hurry.*

*She lies about one thing. Ask her what she buried.*
