## 8. Market of Whispers

The tanneries of Vellamark announced themselves long before we reached them—a stench of lime and rot and rendered fat that clung to the back of the throat like a second tongue. Ines walked ahead of me through the crooked lanes with her hood up and her stride too confident for a woman who hadn't set foot in this quarter in ten years.

"You're sure about this," I said, not for the first time.

"I'm sure about nothing," she said. "That's why we're here."

The tannery district sat along the eastern wall of the city where the river ran slow and grey, and the people who worked it had long ago stopped pretending the smell was temporary. They wore it like a uniform. And like any district that respectable Vellamark preferred not to think about, it had grown its own economy beneath the economy—cellars and culverts and rooms that appeared on no tax roll, where a person could buy things the crown had decided no one should want.

Including, apparently, bones.

"The reliquary is called the Hollow Shelf," Ines said. "It was a wine cellar before the Order fell. The man who runs it now—Corvo—was a lay brother. He kept his vows about as well as I kept mine."

"And he'll just tell us where the vessel is?"

"He'll tell us what it costs." She glanced back at me, and in the flat grey light her face looked older than it had by firelight. "Everything down there has a price, Marguerite. That's the bargain of places like this. No one pretends otherwise."

I touched my satchel strap, feeling the shape of the carving wrapped inside it. A raven, life-sized, carved from elk antler over three sleepless nights—the finest thing my hands had made since I'd burned the rest. It had felt like a betrayal to carve it, and stranger still, it had felt like a homecoming.

We descended by way of a drainage stair behind the third tannery yard, past a door that looked like it hadn't opened in years but swung silently at Ines's knock—three short, one long. A boy of perhaps twelve stood in the dark beyond it, holding a lamp, studying us both with the professional blankness of someone paid to notice everything and report selectively.

"Business or sanctuary?" the boy asked.

"Business," Ines said.

"Arms?"

Ines hesitated. I didn't. I unbuckled the knife at my belt and handed it over hilt-first, and the boy weighed it in his palm like a grocer judging fruit before passing it to a shadow behind him.

"Follow the lamps," he said. "Don't touch the shelves."

* * *

The Hollow Shelf was not what I expected, though I couldn't have said what I'd expected. Not a den, not a vault. Something closer to a chapel that had given up on God and taken up commerce instead.

Wine racks still lined the walls, but the bottles were gone. In their place: relics. Hundreds of them. Finger bones in silver cases. Splinters of crosses so numerous they could have rebuilt a forest. Dried saints' hands, cracked marriage crowns, funeral rings threaded onto cords, votive plaques scratched with names of the dead whose graves had been robbed to feed this place. Lamps burned low and amber everywhere, and the air was thick with tallow smoke and old dust and—beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat—

Voices.

I stopped two steps inside the door.

Not the dead themselves. Most of these relics held nothing; bone only speaks when it's been worked, when hands have shaped it with intent or love or grief. But some of them had been loved once, prayed over, carried against skin through plagues and weddings and wars, and those ones murmured. A wedding ring whispering a bride's name, decades stale. A finger bone reciting a prayer in a language half-eroded from memory. A child's votive plaque humming a lullaby no adult had sung in forty years.

They weren't loud. But they were *everywhere*, layered like sediment, and standing among them was like standing at the bottom of a well with rain falling into it.

"Marguerite." Ines's hand closed on my arm. "Your face."

"I'm fine." I wasn't, quite. My gift had been a private thing for ten years—one voice at a time, in the quiet of my workshop, each dead soul a visitor received alone. This was a crowd. This was a market square of the dead, all of them talking at once, none of them knowing I could hear.

And underneath the overwhelm, treacherous and warm, something in my chest unclenched.

"Corvo," Ines called, her voice carrying the flat authority of a woman who had once worn an order's colors. "We've come to trade."

Racks shifted at the far end of the cellar. A man emerged who might have been sixty or ninety—he was bald as an egg and built like a barrel, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of weak tea. He moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who owned every shadow in the room.

"Ines of the Broken Order." He said it without malice, tasting the words. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell you a great many things, Corvo. How many were true?"

He smiled, showing teeth stained brown by decades of cheap wine he no longer drank. "Fewer every year." His gaze slid to me, and I felt it like a physical weight—not hostile, but thorough, the way a butcher appraises a carcass. "And this?"

"A carver," Ines said.

"Is he." Corvo drifted closer. "A carver of what, I wonder."

I should have stayed quiet. Ten years of discipline said stay quiet, be dull, be furniture. But the voices were all around me, and one of them—a small clay medal of some forgotten martyr hanging at Corvo's own throat—was murmuring, and I heard myself say:

"Your medal misses its maker."

Corvo went very still.

"It was pressed by a woman named Odile," I said, the words coming up through me like water finding a crack. "Her brother cast the molds. She died before she could fire the last batch, and he fired them anyway and never made another. He gave you that one because you were kind to him the winter his shop flooded. He's been dead eleven years. He says—" and here my voice caught, because the dead are rarely eloquent, they simply mean well— "he says thank you for keeping it. Nobody keeps things anymore."

Silence. Even the lamps seemed to lean in.

Then Corvo laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, rough as gravel poured into a bucket. "God's teeth. *There's* one. Ines, you brought a listener into my house."

"I brought a carver," Ines said carefully. "Who listens."

"Same animal." Corvo circled me slowly, and I let him, because there was nowhere to hide and—I realized with a lurch I didn't fully understand—no particular wish to. "Do you know how many listeners come through that door? None. Do you know how many *claim* to? A dozen a year, all frauds, all selling luck-charms to frightened fools. And here stands one who reads a pressed clay medal like a letter from home." He stopped in front of me. "What do you want, listener?"

"The first vessel," Ines said. "Of the three. Bone-vessel, Order work, scattered after the fall. We believe it passed through this market."

Corvo's smile thinned but did not vanish. "You believe correctly. I've held it. Held it twice, in fact—once eight years ago, once four months back, when a scavenger pulled it out of a river muck near Greyford and didn't know what he had." He spread his ink-stained hands. "It's gone now. Sold on. But I know where it went, and knowledge, as ever, has a price."

"We brought payment," I said, and unwrapped the raven.

* * *

I had carved it in two nights of feverish focus while Ines slept badly by the fire, and even as my hands worked I'd known I was carving it for an audience I couldn't name. Now I understood. Some pieces want to go out into the world. This one had wanted Corvo's shelf the way a river wants the sea.

I peeled back the cloth and the antler raven sat in the lamplight, wings folded, head cocked, every feather bar rendered in strokes fine as hair. Around us, faintly, the honest relics went quiet—the way a room quiets when something true enters it.

Corvo did not reach for it. That was the mark of the man. He bent close instead, hands clasped behind his back like a man at a museum, and studied it for a long time.

"Elk antler," he murmured. "Old stock. You don't see antler anymore—everyone's frightened of horn and bone since the proclamations." His tea-colored eyes lifted to mine. "You carved this knowing what it was. Knowing what it would cost you if the wrong man saw your hands' work."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The question deserved honesty, and honesty came strangely easily in that cellar, surrounded by murmuring dead and a man who dealt in them openly. "Because hiding nearly killed me slower than the pyre would have," I said. "Because a thing made well is a prayer whether or not anyone's listening. Because—" I faltered, then finished anyway— "because tonight is the first time in ten years I've said what I am out loud, and I find I don't want to go back to lying."

Corvo straightened. He looked at Ines, and something passed between them—old recognition, old disappointment, maybe old affection.

"You always did collect interesting sinners," he said to her.

"You were one of them."

"Mm." He picked up the raven at last, turning it in the light, and I watched his thumbs find the wing joints, the neck feathers, reading the piece the way I read bone. Whatever he found satisfied him. He set it gently on an empty shelf space between a saint's knuckle and a cracked reliquary cross, adjusting it twice until it faced the door.

"For that," he said, "you'd have gotten the name of the street. For the story about Odile's brother—" he tapped his own medal through his shirt— "you get the whole road." He led us deeper into the cellar, past racks of the murmuring dead, to a table strewn with maps and manifests written in a cipher of his own devising. "The vessel went south. Downriver, past Greyford, to a chapel that drowned when they cut the new millrace forty years ago. Saint Aldhelm's-in-the-Water, the old-timers call it. The scavenger sold it to a diver named Brannoc who works the flooded crypts for grave-goods, and Brannoc doesn't sell. He hoards. Superstitious as a cat—says the drowned chapel whispers to him in his sleep and he likes the company."

My skin prickled. "It does whisper. If he's got an Order vessel down there, it's been speaking to him for months."

"Has it." Corvo's gaze sharpened. "And what does it say?"

I thought of the oath-bone hidden in my satchel, wrapped in wool, silent unless I dared touch it bare-handed—and I hadn't dared, not since the soldier died holding it, not since the layered voices had screamed through me like a struck bell. "Warnings," I said. "Vessels remember what they were made to hold. This one remembers a king."

Corvo absorbed that with a slow blink. Then he drew a scrap of paper, wrote directions in a quick crabbed hand, and sealed it with a dab of candle wax rather than a signet—an anonymity I suspected was habit rather than caution.

"Brannoc drinks at the Anchorstone in Greyford, most nights, worst nights especially. Tell him the Hollow Shelf sent you and that the whisper in his crypt is about to get much louder if he holds on to what isn't his." He pushed the paper across the table. "And listener—"

"Marguerite," I said.

"Marguerite." He pronounced it carefully, filing it away. "When the Hunt comes asking—and they will come asking, because a man who trades a masterwork for directions is a man running from or toward something enormous—know that no one in this market will give you up quickly. We protect our own. But we also survive, and survival makes liars of better people than us. Move fast."

"We intend to," Ines said.

"One more thing." Corvo's voice dropped, and for the first time the merchant fell away and the lay brother showed through—someone who had once believed in something larger than inventory. "You said the vessel remembers a king. I've heard things too, Ines. Things from the palace side of the river. Servants who talk too much when they're frightened. There's a wrongness up on that hill that predates the Hunt, predates the proclamations, and the clever ones have known it for years and said nothing because saying nothing kept them breathing." His eyes moved between us. "Whatever you two are doing—it's bigger than a relic hunt, isn't it."

Ines said nothing. I said nothing. The silence answered for us.

Corvo nodded slowly, as if we'd confessed. "Then take the long way to Greyford. And Marguerite—" he picked the raven off the shelf one last time, held it up to the lamp, and set it back facing outward, proud, visible, "—come back someday and carve me another. It's been a long time since anything true hung on my walls."

* * *

We climbed back into the grey afternoon smelling of tallow and dust, and I was halfway up the drainage stairs before I noticed that I was smiling.

"What?" Ines said, catching it.

"Nothing."

"You're smiling like a man who's just gotten away with something."

"I have." I reached the street and breathed the terrible tannery air like it was perfume. "Ten years, Ines. Ten years of being the dull craftsman, the safe man, the one who pockets coins for mourning charms and hates himself quietly in the dark. And tonight a stranger looked at me—*saw* me, saw exactly what I am, the criminal thing, the outlawed thing—and he didn't flinch. He put my work on his shelf next to his saints." I shook my head. "I keep waiting to feel filthy. I feel *found*."

Ines studied me for a moment, and there was something complicated in her face—warning and envy and a tenderness she probably didn't know was showing. "That feeling," she said quietly, "is exactly what the Hunt counts on. People like Corvo, people like you, finding each other in cellars. It's easier to burn a movement than a man. So they make sure the movements stay small and scared and scattered." She pulled her hood lower. "Enjoy belonging, Marguerite. But belong with your eyes open."

"I will."

But walking back through the crooked lanes toward the river gate, past tannery workers who nodded to us as if we were already regulars, past a doorway where someone had chalked a small spiral sigil—the old Order mark, defaced but recognizable—I let myself feel it anyway. Dangerous and sweet, like stolen wine. For the first time since the soldier died in my arms, the oath-bone in my satchel felt less like a curse and more like a commission.

Greyford was two days south. The drowned chapel waited beneath the water with its whispering dead, and somewhere in the dark a diver named Brannoc was sleeping badly beside a piece of the king's salvation.

We bought passage on a coal barge before sundown.

We were through the river gate and a mile downstream, city smoke fading astern, when Ines went rigid at the rail.

Behind us, on the eastern heights above the tanneries, a column of the Hunt's riders had crested the ridge—six of them, torches lit though the light hadn't failed yet, moving with the unhurried purpose of people following a scent rather than a rumor. As I watched, one rider broke from the column and descended toward the drainage stair behind the third tannery yard.

Toward the Hollow Shelf.

"Told you they'd come asking," Ines said grimly. "Question is who talked—and whether Corvo sold us with his dying breath or bought us time with it."

The rider disappeared below. The torches on the ridge did not move on.

They waited.
