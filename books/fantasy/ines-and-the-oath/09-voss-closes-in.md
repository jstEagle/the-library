## 9. Voss Closes In

The tanneries stank the way they always did in late autumn—rendered fat and lime and something older underneath, like the ground itself had been pickling for a century. Serane Voss had grown up two streets from this smell. She had spent eleven years learning to stop noticing it, and now she noticed it again, deliberately, because noticing was her trade.

She rode into Tanner's Row with six riders and no banners. The Hunt's writ gave her authority to kick down any door in Vellamark, but she had learned early that a door kicked open tells you only what the owner wanted you to find after he'd had time to tidy. A door opened politely told you what he hadn't.

So she dismounted at the mouth of the row, handed her reins to Corporal Ives, and walked the last hundred yards on foot, alone, in a plain grey coat with her captain's pin tucked in her pocket.

The reliquary was behind the third tannery yard, down a stair that smelled of urine and old tallow, behind a door painted the same grey as every other door on the row. She'd had it described to her twice before she came: once by a paid informant who talked too much about the quality of the candles inside, and once by a dead man's ledger.

The soldier's name had been Dannel Ferro. Twelve years in the king's livery. Found in an alley off Cordwainer Street with a gut wound that had been dressed badly and too late, his hands folded over his chest like a man laid out for viewing. No weapon taken. No coin taken. His oath-ring still on his finger, which meant whoever killed him either didn't know what it was or didn't care—and both possibilities had kept Serane awake for two nights running.

What *had* been taken was the thing clenched under his folded hands, according to the physician who turned the body: a bone, pale, carved, the size of a man's thumb. The physician described it in the flat voice physicians used for things they didn't want to think about. He said the dead man's fingers had been broken, all ten, bent backward at the moment of death, as if he had been made to hold the bone while someone else decided whether to take it.

Serane did not believe in omens. She believed in ledgers, in patterns, in the arithmetic of evidence. But the arithmetic here was wrong. A royal courier murdered in a back alley, a relic stolen, and within a day, whispers moving through the tanneries like water finding a crack: *the Order's bones are surfacing. Someone is gathering them.*

Someone had gotten there first. That was the part she hated. For nine years the Hunt had been the fastest thing in Vellamark—faster than rumor, faster than guilt, faster than mercy. Now something was outrunning her, and it had a head start measured in days.

She knocked on the grey door.

* * *

The man who opened it was called Oskell, though everyone on the row knew him by three other names depending on what he was selling them. He was sixty-odd, soft-bodied, with the fine tremor of a man who drank to steady nerves that drinking had ruined. His eyes went from her face to the empty street behind her and back, doing the arithmetic of how loud a scream would have to be to reach anyone.

"Captain Voss," he said. "I'm honored."

"You're terrified," she said. "That's better. Honored makes people stupid." She stepped past him into the room.

It was dimmer than she expected, and warmer. Shelves lined every wall, and on the shelves sat candle stubs, cracked icons, dried herbs in paper twists, and bones—hundreds of them, sorted into shallow trays like a chandler's stock. Chicken bones, pig bones, a ribcage of something large enough that she chose not to identify it. The air smelled of beeswax and dust and, faintly, underneath everything, that tannery reek that had soaked into the walls of this whole district since before her grandmother was born.

"Nice collection," she said.

"It's folk art," Oskell said. "Souvenirs. Nothing with any—" He caught her expression and stopped.

"Sit down, Oskell."

He sat. There was one chair in the room, a dealer's chair behind a counter scarred with knife-marks, and he lowered himself into it like a man lowering himself into cold water. She remained standing, which was not cruelty; it was simply that she thought better on her feet, and she wanted to think clearly for what came next.

"I'm going to ask you questions," she said. "You're going to answer them quickly and completely, and then I'm going to leave, and your shelves are going to stay exactly where they are. Do you know why?"

"Because I've got nothing worth burning."

"No," Serane said. "Because I don't burn shopkeepers. I burn the gifted, when I find them, because the law says so and because I've seen what happens when nobody does. You're not gifted. You're a fence with a taste for morbid inventory. Which means you're useful to me alive." She let that sit. "But usefulness is a perishable good, Oskell. It spoils fast."

He wiped his palms on his trousers. "Ask."

"Three nights ago, someone came in here. Someone sold or traded something, or asked about something. A bone. Carved. Old."

Oskell's face did something complicated—a whole small war fought in the muscles around his mouth. Serane watched it with professional interest. Guilt, fear, greed, and the particular vanity of a man who knows something valuable and wants credit for knowing it.

"That's not a question," he said finally.

"No. It's a summary. Here's the question: describe them."

Silence. Somewhere above them, through the floorboards, the tannery vats turned over with a wet, industrial groan. Oskell looked at his shelves, at his trays of little clean bones, at everything he'd built out of other people's superstitions, and Serane could see him weighing it—the cost of talking against the cost of not talking, the way men always weighed it, as if silence were a currency that held its value.

It never did. Silence just spent slower.

"They were two," he said.

That landed harder than she expected. She had come prepared for one. One stranger, one relic, one trail. Two meant a conspiracy, or at least a partnership, and partnerships left more fingerprints.

"Go on."

"A woman first. Came in the front, regular hours, bold as brass. Older, maybe forty. Kept her hood up but she had a way of standing—straight, like she was used to rooms with better floors. Knew what she was looking at. Asked me about vessels. Not charms, not keepsakes—*vessels*. Said it like a word with a capital letter in it." He swallowed. "I told her I didn't deal in that. She put a coin on the counter. Old mint. Royal seal, pre-civil-war strike. Worth more than everything on my shelves together, probably, and she treated it like it was nothing."

"What did you tell her?"

"What I tell everyone. Nothing." Oskell's eyes flicked sideways, toward a curtain at the back of the room, and Serane noted it without turning her head. "But I keep a book. Names, faces, what was asked. Habit from the old days, when the Order's people used to come through. If the wrong buyer asks me later what passed through my hands, I need to be able to say I never touched it."

"The book," Serane said. "Now."

He hesitated—one last, doomed instinct toward self-preservation—and then reached beneath the counter and produced a ledger bound in oiled leather, its pages soft as cloth from handling. She took it and read by the light of his own cheap candles, turning pages with two fingers, careful not to smudge whatever might be written in margins.

Most of it was exactly what he'd said: buyers, sellers, dates, descriptions in a cramped hand. And there, four days old, the entry.

*Woman, hooded, upright carriage, scarred left hand (burn?). Asked after vessels by name. Paid in old-mint crownpiece. Referred to second party waiting outside.*

Serane read it twice. *Scarred left hand.* She had a file, thick as a psalter, on every surviving member of the Order of the Broken Oath—the order that had been dissolved, scattered, and hunted piecemeal for a decade. There were perhaps thirty names on her list of the still-living. She mentally turned pages of her own memory until she found the hand: Ines Maraval, former First Keeper's aide, court records sealed by special dispensation, presumed fled north after the purges. Burn scar, left palm, documented at her tribunal.

Ines Maraval. In Vellamark. Asking after vessels.

Her superiors would want to know that before sundown. Her superiors would also want to know it quietly, because the sealed tribunal records meant someone high up had buried this woman once already, and men who bury things get nervous when the ground starts moving. Serane closed the ledger on that thought and looked up.

"And the second party?"

Oskell's war started again, fought across his face, and this time she watched him lose it faster.

"A man," he said. "Came in the back way, after she'd gone. Nervous. Younger—youngish, anyway. Kept his hands in his pockets like he was hiding burns of his own." He paused, and something shifted in his voice, some note she couldn't immediately classify. "He wasn't a buyer. He was a carver."

"How do you know?"

"Because he looked at my stock the way a smith looks at another man's forge. Not covetous. *Critical.* He picked up one of my pieces—a charm, cheap work, chicken bone—and turned it over twice and put it down, and I swear on my mother he was being polite about it. You could see him deciding not to say anything." Oskell rubbed his jaw. "I've sold to carvers before. They look with their hands. This one looked with his hands and flinched."

"What did he want?"

"To know if anyone had been asking after vessels. Same question, coming the other direction. Like they were circling each other and using me for a mirror." He laughed, short and unhappy. "I told him the same thing I told her. Nothing. And then—"

"Then?"

"He bought a carving off me. Traded, really—one of his own for one of mine, plus coin. Fair trade, generous even. And the piece he gave me—" Oskell stopped. His tremor got worse. "Captain, I've handled bones for thirty years. Dead things don't bother me. But that piece hummed. Not sound. More like... like holding a bird that's thinking about flying. I wrapped it in wool and put it in a box and I still feel it sometimes, at night, through the shelf."

"Where is it?"

"Sold it yesterday. To a collector out of Greyford, passing through. It'll be on the north road by now." He saw her expression change and added hastily, "I can write down the buyer—"

"Do that." She watched him find paper, dip a pen with his shaking hand. "Describe the carver. Everything. Face, build, clothes, voice, the way he moved. All of it."

Oskell wrote, and talked while he wrote, the way frightened men do when narration keeps them from thinking. Medium height. Lean, but not hard-lean—workshop-lean, the stoop of a man who bends over a bench. Dark hair, unremarkable. Clothes good but worn, mended well, the kind of poverty that used to be comfort. Soft-spoken. Watchful. Kept glancing at the door.

"And his face?" Serane said. "For the sketch."

Oskell set down the pen. He looked at her for a long moment, and she realized the strange note in his voice had been there since he started describing the man, and she still couldn't name it.

"That's the thing, Captain," he said. "I can give your artist the shape of him easy enough. Jaw, brow, all that. But if you're asking me what I remember most—" He rubbed his hands together, embarrassed. "Kind eyes. That's it. That's the whole truth of it. Man walks into a den like mine, in these times, hunting the same dangerous thing the woman was hunting, scared half out of his wits—and his eyes are kind. Like a healer's. Like he felt sorry for me, sitting in my own shop, and didn't even know he was doing it."

Serane said nothing.

She had hanged people with kind eyes. She had hanged a hedge-witch in Harrowgate whose last act had been to heal the constable's daughter of a fever on the walk to the scaffold, gratis, without being asked. Kindness proved nothing. Kindness was not evidence. She had built her entire career on the principle that what a person seemed to be was the least reliable information in the world—that the woman who sang in the temple choir could raise the dead, that the gentle schoolmaster could boil a river, that her own father, kind-eyed to the last morning of his life, had stood in their kitchen doorway while the thing he'd bargained with wore his body like a coat and smiled at her with his smile.

She knew better than anyone alive what kindness was worth.

"Write the description," she said. "All of it. Including the eyes."

* * *

The artist worked fast, in the guardhouse on Cordwainer Street, charcoal on heavy paper, working from Oskell's words and Serane's corrections. She stood over his shoulder for two hours and did not sit, and the guards learned not to offer her a chair.

The trouble with a verbal likeness was that it assembled itself out of averages. Give the artist *lean, dark-haired, watchful* and you got a hundred possible men, none of them real. So Serane fed him specifics. The stoop. The long fingers Oskell had described, ink-stained at the tips. The habit of keeping hands hidden. The jaw—narrow, she said, make it narrower, he's a man who goes unnoticed, build the face for going unnoticed.

And then, at the end, because Oskell had insisted and because she was, despite everything, a thorough officer: *kind eyes.*

The artist drew them. And the face changed.

It was a subtle thing, the difference between a face and a person. The same jaw, the same hair, the same watchful set of the shoulders—but with those eyes rendered honestly, the whole portrait tilted toward sympathy, the way a scale tilts when you add a single coin. The man in the sketch looked less like a criminal than like someone you'd ask to watch your child. He looked like a man you'd trust with a dying friend's last words.

Serane stared at it for a long time.

"Print fifty," she said finally. "Post them at every gate, every checkpoint, every market square from here to Greyford. Caption reads: wanted for questioning in the murder of a royal courier. Reward: twenty crowns, name withheld."

"Yes, Captain." The artist began cleaning his charcoal. Then, carefully, because artists were the one class of subject who could not be intimidated out of honesty: "He doesn't look like a murderer."

"He isn't," Serane said. "Probably. Murderers don't buy fair trades from fences and flinch at bad craftsmanship. He's something else. I don't know what yet." She picked up the master sketch and held it at arm's length, studying it the way she'd study a map of unfamiliar country. "Which is worse. Murderers I understand."

She rolled the sketch, sealed it, and carried it out into the street, where the wind had come up from the river and was stripping the last leaves off the plane trees along Cordwainer Street. Ash season, the papers were calling it—the criers' new term for the burning days, delivered with the bright brutality of official language. Somewhere east, a crowd was gathering for a scheduled cleansing; she could hear the bells that preceded it, and she made herself count them, all seven, because counting was discipline and discipline was the wall she had built between herself and the fire.

Twenty crowns, she thought. Fifty sketches. Every gate watched. If the carver was still in Vellamark, he was already in a cage of paper.

And yet.

She thought of Oskell's face as he'd said *kind eyes*, the embarrassment in it, the confession of it—as if the fence had betrayed something by remembering a stranger fondly. She thought of the dead courier's broken fingers, folded around a bone that hummed. She thought of Ines Maraval's sealed file, and the question she had not yet dared put in writing to her superiors: *who sealed it, and why?*

At the corner of Cordwainer and Bell, a crier was nailing her sketch to the fountain post, smoothing it flat with his palm. A small crowd gathered, as crowds did—curiosity being free even when bread was not. Serane slowed her horse to a walk and watched, from twenty feet, anonymous in her plain coat, as strangers studied the face she'd commissioned.

A baker's boy squinted at it. "Don't look like much," he said.

His mother looked longer. "Looks tired," she said. "Look at the eyes. Poor devil looks like he hasn't slept in a month."

*Poor devil.* Serane turned her horse and rode, and the words followed her the length of the street like a debt she couldn't locate the creditor for.

By nightfall the sketch hung in every gatehouse in the city. By the following noon, the first reports would come in—a carver matching the likeness seen near the fountain on proclamation day, a man of similar description renting a room, a face half-glimpsed at a window. The machine would do what machines did: grind rumor into shape, shape into pursuit, pursuit into a knock on a door.

She gave it three days, she estimated. Four at most.

What she did not estimate—what no ledger of hers accounted for—was the rider who came in from the north road at dusk, mud to the thighs, carrying word from Greyford: a collector robbed on the highway, his purchases scattered, among them a wrapped box containing a carved bone that, per the collector's sworn statement, had been *screaming* when they pried it open.

Serane read the dispatch twice by lamplight.

Then she reached for her cloak, and her horse, and the road north.
