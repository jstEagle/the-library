## 10. The Drowned Chapel

The chapel had been drowning for a hundred years, and it had done the work patiently.

We came down to it at dusk, when the marsh light went the color of old pewter and the reeds stopped whispering. The building sat in a shallow bowl of black water like a man who had lain down in a river and simply decided to stay. Its roof had fallen inward long ago; its bell tower leaned at an angle that suggested apology. Water stood waist-deep in what had once been the nave, and above the waterline the stone walls wore a tide-mark of pale mineral, a century of small tides writing their ledger in salt.

"The reliquary keeper said the crypt is beneath," Ines said. She stood on the chapel's lowest step, boots already ruined, hood back despite the cold. "Says the water comes up through the floor. Says no one goes down there."

"No one who can't hear them, you mean."

She looked at me. "Can you?"

I didn't answer that. I was listening already, the way you listen to a house at night—not for sounds but for the shape of silence, and whether anything moves inside it. And there was something moving inside it. Something vast and slow and layered, like a choir heard through three feet of stone and three feet more of standing water.

"They're all still here," I said. "The flood took the village in—" I did the arithmetic from the tide-marks, from the lean of the bell tower. "—ninety years, maybe ninety-five. The whole congregation. They were at evening service."

Ines went very still. "All of them."

"All of them." I tested the first step with my boot. It held. "The vessel will be in the crypt. Under the altar, if they followed the Order's custom. Which means I have to walk through the middle of their service to get to it."

"Marguerite." Her voice had changed—the flat command of it softened into something she probably hated using. "In the reliquary, you said listening to one bone was like pressing your ear to a door. What's it like with four hundred?"

I looked out over the black water, at the drowned roof beams just breaking the surface like the ribs of some enormous patient animal.

"I don't know," I said honestly. "No one's ever asked me to do this before."

* * *

We waded in at full dark, because Ines insisted dark would hide us from the road and I insisted nothing would hide us from what was inside, so we might as well be comfortable about one of those things. The water was colder than the night deserved. It climbed my thighs, my waist, found every seam in my coat and made itself at home. Ines moved ahead of me with a shuttered lantern, following the line of fallen pews that just broke the surface, and I watched her and thought, absurdly, that she walked through water the way she seemed to walk through everything: as though it had personally wronged her and she intended to make it regret that.

The altar platform rose ahead, dry-ish, a low island of stone furred with moss. Behind it, the crypt entrance—a stair going down into the flooded undercroft, its arch half-collapsed.

"There," Ines whispered.

And that was when I first heard them properly.

Not through a bone. Through the water itself. It came up my body like a struck bell, and every drop of it was carrying voices.

They weren't screaming. That was the thing I hadn't been ready for. I had braced for screaming—for the terror of a congregation watching black water climb the aisle while the priest shouted for God. There was some of that, near the top of the chorus, worn smooth by ninety years of repetition until it was less a memory than a groove. But underneath it, everywhere, was the rest of it. Hymns. Half-remembered hymns, sung by people whose mouths were full of water, sung without end because they had died mid-verse and could not find the end. Prayers. A woman counting her children's names over and over—there were six names, then five, then four, as the water rose, and she kept counting anyway. A man apologizing to his wife for something so small I couldn't catch it, apologizing across decades, his voice fraying down to a thread. A child asking whether the fish would bite.

Four hundred voices. Four hundred doors, all open at once, all of them opening onto me.

I put my hand on the altar stone to steady myself and the stone was worse. Stone remembers longer than water. The whole history of the place poured up my arm—the founding, the weddings, the funerals, the day the dam upstream failed and the deacon rang the bell until the water took the tower out from under him—and I heard myself make a sound I didn't recognize as mine.

"Marguerite." Ines's hand closed on my collar. "Look at me."

"I'm looking at you."

"You're not. Your eyes have gone somewhere else." She hauled me around to face her. Her face was lantern-lit from below, which made her look like something carved herself—all planes and hard edges and two fierce eyes. "Tell me what's happening."

"They're singing," I said. My own voice sounded far away. "They never stopped singing. Ninety years and they're still mid-hymn, and nobody told them the verse ended, and I can hear every single one of them, and Ines—they know I'm here."

Her grip tightened. "What do you mean, they know."

"The dead always know. When someone can hear them. Most of the time it's faint, it's like—like birds going quiet when you pass under the tree. But this many, this close—" I swallowed. The water around my waist had begun to move. Not current. Attention. Ripples spreading outward from nowhere, converging toward me, toward the sound of a living man who could listen. "They've been alone for ninety years. And I'm the first person in a century who can hear them."

The ripples reached the walls and came back.

"Say what you need," Ines said, very fast and very level. "Say it now, before I have to drag you out of here without the vessel."

"I need to go down the stairs. Into the crypt. Alone. And I need you to hold the rope, and count, and if I don't come up when you reach three hundred—if I stop answering—you pull. Don't argue with me about whether you can pull hard enough. Just pull."

"And if pulling isn't enough?"

"Then you run, and you tell no one what I am, and you find someone else to finish this." I said it lightly, and we both knew it was a lie, and neither of us wasted time on it.

She tied the rope around my chest herself, under my arms, with the quick brutal knots of a woman who had tied men to things before. At the top of the stairs she caught my wrist.

"Marguerite. One thing." For a moment the commander fell away and what was underneath was younger and much more frightened. "When my Order fell, I watched good keepers walk into places like this and not come back. Not because the dead hurt them. Because the dead *wanted* them. Do you understand? Grief is hungry. Four hundred griefs are a feast. Whatever they offer you down there—it isn't real. Nothing they offer is real."

"What do they usually offer?" I asked, because I genuinely wanted to know.

"Rest," she said. "They offer rest. To the listener, it feels like being forgiven for everything."

I went down the stairs.

* * *

The crypt was fully flooded. Of course it was. The water closed over my head and the world became pressure and cold and the lantern-light dying to a brown smear above me, and I swam down along the rope into the dark with my heart slamming and the voices rising around me like—

Like a city. That was the only way I could hold it in my mind. A city of the drowned, built in layers, street upon street, and I had descended from the rooftops into its streets and every window was open and every window was singing.

I found the crypt floor by touch. Bones everywhere—arranged, I realized dimly, arranged in rows, the congregation laid out by hands that must have come after the flood, neighbors or descendants or just the next village over, laying them out decently because no one else would. And against the far wall, behind the altar's twin, a niche sealed with a slab, and on the slab, cut deep and filled with lead, the sigil of the Order of Keepers: the circle broken by a straight line. The oath-sign. Ines wore it scarred into her palm.

The vessel was behind the slab. I knew it the way I knew the widow's husband's last words—by hearing. Even through stone and lead and ninety years of water, the bone-vessel sang a different song than the dead around it. Older. Colder. A note with no grief in it at all, which, after the last hour, was the most frightening sound in the room.

I got my knife into the lead seam and worked it, and the whole time the city pressed closer.

They weren't trying to hurt me. I want that written somewhere, if anything I say is ever written down: they were not cruel. They were lonely. Four hundred people had been singing to an empty church for ninety years, and a listener had finally come, and they gathered around me the way starving people gather around a lit window. They pressed in. They showed me things. A wedding, the bride's shoes muddy from the marsh path. A christening, the baby howling, everyone laughing. The last ordinary Tuesday, bread and argument and a dog stealing a ham. And threaded through all of it, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck: *stay. Stay and hear us. No one has heard us. You could hear us forever. You wouldn't have to be alone either—we know about you, carver, we know you eat supper in silence, we know you talk to your carvings because there's no one else—*

That one went in deep. That one found the exact seam.

Because it was true. Ten years in Vellamark, ten years of being beloved and unknown, of pocketing coins with guilt in his throat, of hearing the dead's last words and giving them to strangers who wept and thanked him and never knew what it cost him to carry each one home alone. I had built a life that was a locked room with a very nice front counter.

*Stay,* said the drowned city, kindly. *Here you are known.*

My hands stopped working the lead. Or rather, my hands kept working—I could feel them, distantly, patiently prying—but *I* had begun to drift upward out of them, out of the cold and the burning lungs, into the warm layered dark where four hundred voices made a place for me. It didn't feel like drowning. That was the trap of it. It felt like coming home to a house I'd never lived in, where everyone knew my name and no one needed anything explained. Somewhere very far away a rope was tied around my chest and a woman was counting, and the number meant nothing, numbers meant nothing, time meant nothing here, here there was only the hymn, only the verse that never ended, and I could simply add my voice to it, I could simply—

Something struck the water above me like a hammer blow.

Sound, in that drowned world, travels strangely, but pain travels perfectly. The shockwave of it hit me as a physical thing, and in the same instant a hand closed in my collar and hauled, and the rope went taut, and the city screamed—not in anger, in loss, four hundred voices crying *no, no, stay, please*—and I came up out of the crypt stair into air and lantern-light, vomiting water, alive, my ears ringing with the sudden thinness of a world that contained only one voice.

Ines's voice.

"—with me, carver, *look at me*, breathe, damn you, breathe—"

I breathed. It hurt. Everything hurt. My lungs, my head, my hands—which were still clenched around something, I realized, fingers frozen into hooks around a length of pale bone no bigger than my forearm, dense and yellowed and humming with that cold noteless song.

The first vessel. I had it. I had gone down and gotten it and I had almost no memory of doing it.

"How long?" I croaked.

"Forty counts past three hundred." She was soaked to the shoulders—she'd come down the stairs after me, rope or no rope, and the fury on her face was mostly fear wearing armor. "You stopped answering at two-forty. I gave you sixty counts because you asked for patience, and then I came down, and you were kneeling on the crypt floor with your eyes open, smiling, and Marguerite—" Her voice cracked, just once, and she killed the crack immediately, strangled it like an intruder. "You were smiling like a man being welcomed home. I have seen that smile before. On better keepers than me."

I looked down at the bone in my hands. Then at the black water, where the ripples had stilled. Where four hundred voices waited, politely, terribly, for me to come back.

"They're still singing," I said.

"Yes."

"When we take the vessel, does it—do they stop?"

Ines was quiet for a moment. Water ran off her chin. "I don't know," she admitted. "The vessels were made to hold power, not to free the dead. But—" and here she did something I hadn't seen her do yet: she hesitated, and let me see her hesitate. "If the binding comes undone, everything the Order made comes undone with it. Maybe the wards too. Maybe the ones holding this place shut."

"Maybe."

"Maybe," she agreed. "It's the best word I have. I've been living on it for ten years."

We climbed out of the chapel together, hauling ourselves up the fallen pew-line with the vessel wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, and at the doorway I stopped. Just once. I turned and looked back at the drowned nave, at the moon lying on the black water in a long silver aisle, and I listened—one last deliberate listening, the way you'd tip your hat to a stranger at a funeral.

*I'll come back,* I didn't say aloud, because promising things to the dead is how carvers go mad. But I thought it, hard, and I felt the water receive it, and from under the surface, faint as a hymn through a wall, four hundred voices sang one verse together—complete, resolved, the ending they'd been missing for ninety years—and then, for the first time since the flood, the chapel was silent.

Ines watched my face. "What was that?"

"I think," I said slowly, "they just finished the song."

* * *

We made two miles before dawn, east along the marsh causeway with the vessel knocking gently against my sternum at every stride, and Ines talking strategy in a low rapid voice—roads, papers, the hermit who held the second vessel, the weeks we didn't have. I answered maybe a third of what she said. The rest of me was still down in the dark, in the warm layered city, and part of me—I'll be honest, since honesty is the one luxury this story affords—part of me missed it.

"Stop walking," Ines said suddenly.

I stopped. We stood on the causeway with the marsh sighing on both sides and the sky going grey at the edges, and she studied my face with the expression of a woman reading a document she suspected had been forged.

"You left something down there," she said. "Not the vessel. Something of you. I've seen it happen. The ones who come back from the deep listens always leave a little blood in the water, so to speak. So I'm asking plainly, and I want the plain answer: are you still ours, or are you half theirs?"

"Half theirs," I said. "But the half that's ours is the half that walks."

Something moved behind her eyes—approval, or disappointment, or the exhaustion of a woman deciding that half a man was better than none. She turned to keep walking.

And that was when we both saw it: far back along the causeway, at the edge of the marsh road, a lantern. Not moving. Standing. Watching. And beside it, catching the light, the dull gleam of a Hunt officer's pauldron, and the tall unhurried silhouette of someone who had clearly been waiting for us to notice her, and who made no move at all to follow.

"She's letting us see her," Ines breathed. "Why would she let us see her?"

I touched the vessel through the oilcloth, and it hummed its cold noteless note against my palm, and I thought of a corpse in an alley with a missing relic, and a captain examining the scene, and a description posted on market walls.

"Because," I said, "she doesn't need to follow us anymore. She knows exactly where we're going."

The lantern went out.
