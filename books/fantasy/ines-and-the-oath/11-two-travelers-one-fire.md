## 11. Two Travelers, One Fire

They made camp in the hollow of a fallen oak, where the roots had torn up a wall of earth against the north wind. Ines built the fire small and disciplined, feeding it like she fed everything—sparingly, with an eye on what it might cost later. Marguerite watched her arrange the kindling in a lattice and thought, not for the first time, that she did even the smallest things like a woman who had once been trusted with kingdoms.

The first vessel sat between them, wrapped in oilcloth. The drowned chapel's relic had been dry for two days now, but Marguerite still felt it humming against his hip like a struck bell that refused to finish its note. He'd taken to sleeping with it apart from him, on the far side of whatever shelter they found, because at night it whispered. Not words. Just the memory of water, and the pressure of all those drowned voices he had waded through to reach it.

"You keep looking at it," Ines said.

"I keep listening to it." He turned his hands toward the fire. "It's a habit I can't break. Everything carved, everything old—I listen. Most things say nothing. Bones are honest that way. They've finished their arguments." He nodded at the wrapped vessel. "That one hasn't finished."

"Then don't listen."

"That," said Marguerite, "is like telling the sea not to be wet."

Ines almost smiled. It was a narrow thing, gone as soon as it arrived, but he had begun collecting her almost-smiles the way other men collected coins. She unwrapped a heel of bread and some hard cheese from her pack and divided it with the same precision she gave to everything—two portions, measured, equal, no favoritism even here.

"Eat," she said. "Tomorrow we cross the Marrow Hills, and there's nothing kind in them."

He ate. The cheese was terrible. The fire cracked and spat, and somewhere above them an owl asked its one question of the dark and got no answer.

* * *

The silence between them had changed over the past days. At first it had been the silence of two people bound by necessity, each counting the hours until they could be rid of the other. Now it was something else—a silence with furniture in it, comfortable enough to sit inside. Marguerite noticed the change the way he noticed changes in bone: gradually, then all at once.

"Ines," he said, because the fire made him brave, "why does a disgraced oath-keeper spend four years running from the crown and then walk straight back toward it?"

She didn't answer immediately. She fed a stick to the flames and watched it catch.

"Because I made the thing we're trying to undo," she said. "Not alone. But my hands were on it. My blood was in the wax. When the Order fell, everyone scattered or burned, and I ran like the rest. And every year since, another king has gone into that crown whole and come out of it hollow, and I have told myself I couldn't have known. That I was young. That Merrin—" Her jaw tightened around the name. "That Merrin deceived me."

"Merrin," Marguerite repeated. "The mentor."

"The man who taught me the craft of oaths." She said it flatly, the way you'd recite a confession learned by rote. "I was nineteen when the Order took me. Do you know what it is to be nineteen and chosen? To have the greatest oath-keeper in a generation look at your work and say, *this one has the ear for it*? I would have walked into fire for him. In a sense, I did."

Marguerite said nothing. He had spent ten years listening to the dead, and he knew better than most that the living needed less interruption and more room.

"I forged the binding myself," Ines went on. "My hands. I inscribed the crown-vow that anchors the spirit to the throne. I was so proud of it. Do you understand that? I was *proud*. Forty years ago, the realm was bleeding—border wars, succession riots, a king assassinated in his own hall. The Order proposed a guardian: a spirit bound to the crown itself, sworn to protect the sovereign, to steady his judgment, to warn him of treachery. A shepherd for kings. And I stood in the motherhouse with the wax warm under my stylus, and I wrote the vow that would hold it, and I believed—gods help me, I *believed*—that I was writing mercy."

"What went wrong?" Marguerite asked quietly.

"The vow was perfect." Her voice did something strange then, thinned to a wire. "That's what no one understands. The binding didn't fail. It worked exactly as written. But a vow is a door, and doors don't choose what walks through them. Merrin altered the invocation—the summoning, not the binding. One line. One line I never saw, added after my part was done, sealed under his authority. Where the rite should have called a guardian willing to serve, it called something hungry instead, and dressed it in guardianship. The spirit protects the king the way a farmer protects his cattle."

The owl called again. The fire settled.

"He used you," Marguerite said.

"He used all of us. But I'm the one who wrote the door." She looked up, and the firelight showed him her face stripped of all its keeper's composure—just a woman, aging, tired, carrying forty years of a crime she hadn't quite committed and couldn't stop claiming. "Four kings, carver. Four kings have worn that crown since the binding. The first lasted nine years before he started talking to empty chairs. The second drowned himself in a lake that came up to his waist. The third—Aldric's father—died convinced his own shadow was plotting against him. And now Aldric, who was the best of them, who was *kind*, is clawing at his own head in front of mirrors. Every one of them devoured a little more each year, and every year I told myself the same lie: next spring. Next spring I'll find the vessels. Next spring I'll make it right."

"And now?"

"Now the soldier died in an alley pressing an oath-bone into a stranger's hands, and the spring has run out." She straightened, and he watched her gather her composure back around herself like a cloak. "So that's my history, carver. Treason by negligence, written in wax at nineteen years old. You wanted to know why I walk back toward the crown. There's your answer. I'm not trying to save the king. I'm trying to save the girl who thought she was writing mercy."

* * *

Marguerite fed the fire a branch and considered how much of himself to give in return. Fairness demanded something. Trust demanded more. And there was a third thing, newer and less nameable, that demanded the most of all.

"My turn," he said. "You know what I am. You've seen what the bones do. But you don't know how it started."

"Tell me or don't."

"My mother sold charms at the Vellamark autumn fair. Nothing real—knots and feathers and painted stones, the kind of magic that works because people want it to. I was eight. I picked up a deer rib off the road, some dog's leftover, and I started carving it with my belt knife because I was bored, and the moment I finished the last cut, I heard a voice." He paused. "A woman's voice. Saying *tell Tomas the river took the ring, not the thief.*"

Ines had gone very still.

"I didn't know what it meant. I still don't, entirely. But I brought the rib home and my mother—she was sharp, she was frightened, she'd buried two husbands—she looked at it and went white and said, *that's old Marta's voice, and Marta's been dead six years.*" He laughed, a small sound without much humor in it. "We burned it. We burned every piece I ever touched after that, for three years. And then my mother got sick, the slow kind, and I sat by her bed with nothing to carve and nothing to hear, and I realized the silence was worse than the voices. Isn't that a filthy thing to learn about yourself? That you'd rather hear ghosts than sit quiet?"

"No," Ines said. "That's the truest thing I've heard all year."

"She died in autumn. And the first thing I did after the funeral was pick up a knife and a bone, because I wanted—if there was anything left of her anywhere, any echo, any scrap—I wanted to hear it. And do you know what happened?" He stared into the fire. "Nothing. Silence. Whatever gift I have, it doesn't reach the ones I need. Ten years in Vellamark, carving mourning charms for strangers, hearing their husbands and daughters and mothers say their last words through my hands—and my own mother says nothing. The dead talk to me all day long, and the one voice I want is the one I can't have."

The wind moved through the bare branches overhead. Ines reached across the fire—not far, just to the edge of the warmth—and set her hand briefly over his wrist. Her grip was dry and strong and gone again in a moment, but the place where it had been stayed warm longer than it should have.

"That's why you hide it," she said. "Not just the Hunt."

"Partly the Hunt." He shrugged. "Mostly it's this: every charm I sell, I take coin for a grief that isn't mine, using a voice that isn't mine either. The widow pays me three coppers and gets her husband's last words, and I stand there smiling like a tradesman while a dead man speaks through my carving. It feels like theft. It has felt like theft for ten years, and I've done it anyway, because a man has to eat, and because—" He stopped. Started again. "And because when the widow leaves, she isn't crying anymore. She knows something true. And for about a minute, watching her go, I feel like what I do matters. Then the guilt comes back and I lock the shop."

"You give them their dead," Ines said. "There are worse crimes."

"There are worse crimes," he agreed. "But there are better lives."

* * *

The fire burned down to a red heart. Ines took first watch, as she always insisted, and Marguerite lay with his blanket around his shoulders and did not sleep, because the vessel was whispering and because his mind kept turning over the shape of her story the way his thumb turned over the grain of good bone.

At some point he realized she was speaking again, very quietly, half to herself and half to him.

"When I was young in the Order," she said, "Merrin told me that an oath-keeper's whole art was knowing which promises deserve to outlive the people who made them. That we were gardeners of vows. Pruning, tending, deciding what grew." A pause. "I believed that too. And then I watched him prune a kingdom down to a stump, one line of hidden text at a time, and I understood that he never tended anything. He only ever planted hooks."

"Do you hate him?" Marguerite asked the darkness.

"He's dead. Died the winter the Order fell—some say by his own hand, some say the spirit got tired of him." She was quiet a moment. "I don't hate him. That's the worst of it. I miss him. Forty years, and some stubborn corner of me still wants his approval, still hears his voice saying *well done, child* every time I do the work well. He put that in me the way you put a voice in a bone. And I can't burn it out, because unlike you, I can't hear it and hold it at arm's length. It's just... mine."

Marguerite lay looking at her across the embers—her silhouette straight-backed even sitting down, her hands folded like a woman at prayer—and felt something shift in his chest that had no business shifting on the second week of knowing someone.

"We're both keeping dead men's voices," he said. "Yours just doesn't need bone to speak."

"No," she said. "Mine needs forgiveness. Which is harder to carve."

He smiled at the dark, where she couldn't see it, and let the silence close over them again—but it was a different silence now, the kind two people share when each has handed the other something breakable and neither has dropped it yet.

Sleep took him eventually, shallow and full of water sounds. Near dawn he surfaced once, briefly, and saw Ines still awake, still watching the east, and beside her knee the wrapped vessel, and beyond it the black line of the Marrow Hills waiting against the graying sky.

"Ines," he murmured, half-dreaming.

"Yes, carver."

"The second vessel. The one in the hills. Whose keeps it?"

She was silent long enough that he thought she'd gone back to her watch. Then:

"A hermit," she said. "A blind old man who lives above the tree line. He was the Order's finest vessel-smith, once." Another silence, longer. "And he was Merrin's closest friend."

Marguerite woke fully at that, but the dawn was already coming, gray and cold, and Ines was already rolling the blankets, already armored again, already three steps ahead of the conversation.

"Get up, carver," she said, not turning around. "We've a hard climb ahead. And I have a debt to collect from a man who's owed me an apology for forty years."

The fire was ash. The hills were waiting. And somewhere behind them, faint on the morning wind, Marguerite thought he could smell smoke that wasn't theirs—though he told himself it was nothing, and shouldered his pack, and climbed.
