## 12. The King's Public Face

Lord Emmerich Vael had attended the winter councils of Vellamark for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things worth knowing: never sit within arm's reach of Duke Corvane when the wine was poured, never repeat aloud what the Chancellor muttered into his sleeve, and never — under any circumstance — look directly at the King for longer than courtesy allowed.

The first two rules kept a man comfortable. The third kept him alive.

He sat now at his usual place, third from the end of the long oak table in the Amber Chamber, where morning light came through windows of honey-colored glass and made everything it touched look warmer than it was. The chamber smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke and, faintly, of the dried yarrow the King's physicians burned to "clear his humors." Emmerich had long suspected the yarrow was for the courtiers rather than the King. A smell to steady the nerves of men who had learned to be afraid.

The King entered at the ninth bell, precisely as he always did.

Aldric IV of Vellamark was fifty-three years old and had worn the crown for thirty-one of them. He was a tall man gone slightly stooped, with his mother's grey eyes and his father's heavy jaw, and he moved down the length of the chamber with the unhurried certainty of a man who had never once doubted that every room belonged to him. His beard was trimmed. His robes were immaculate — deep blue velvet, the collar worked with silver thread in the pattern of interlocking rings that was the royal sigil. On his head, as required by law during all formal sessions, sat the crown itself: an old thing, older than the dynasty, iron beneath its gold leaf, set with garnets the color of dried blood.

"Good morrow, my lords," the King said, and took his chair.

And there it was — the thing that had made Emmerich's hands unsteady around his breakfast cup for the past month. The voice was *right*. Warm, measured, faintly amused, the voice of a king greeting his council on an ordinary day. It was the voice Emmerich had heard across nineteen years of winters. If a man closed his eyes, he would have sworn nothing in the world had changed.

But Emmerich had not been able to close his eyes lately. He had been paid very well not to.

*Watch him,* the carver had said, pressing the purse of silver into Emmerich's palm in the shadow of the tannery wall, where respectable men did not go and could not later claim to have been. *Not when everyone watches him. Watch him when they don't.*

*Why me?* Emmerich had asked. *There are a dozen lords closer to the throne.*

*Because you're nobody important,* the carver had answered, with the blunt honesty Emmerich found himself oddly unable to resent. *Nobody watches you. That's your whole value.*

It was, Emmerich reflected, the most accurate assessment of his life anyone had ever offered. Forty-four years old, second son of a second son, holding a minor estate in the eastern fens and a minor seat at a council where major men decided everything. He had spent his whole career being overlooked, and now, at last, it was a marketable skill.

"The matter of the grain tariffs for the northern provinces," the Chancellor began, unrolling a document, and the council settled into its familiar rhythms.

Emmerich watched the King the way he had taught himself to watch over these past weeks: sideways, in glances, in the reflections of the polished ewers and the dark glass of the windows. Never directly. Directly was dangerous — not because the King would notice, but because *others* might notice Emmerich noticing. Captain Serane Voss of the Hunt missed nothing. Her soldiers stood along the chamber walls even now, four of them, motionless as furniture, their ash-grey cloaks making the room feel colder than the fire could mend.

For the first hour, there was nothing to see. The King listened to the tariff dispute with visible attention, asked two sharp questions that flustered the northern delegates, and ruled with a compromise so cleanly balanced that both sides left the table half-satisfied and half-annoyed, which was the mark of good statecraft. He joked — dryly, briefly — about the Chancellor's handwriting. He drank one cup of warmed wine and no more. He remembered the name of the youngest delegate's newborn daughter and asked after her health, and the young man went pink with pleasure.

A lucid morning. A good morning. Emmerich let his shoulders loosen a fraction.

Then the council turned to the matter of the Hunt's winter requisitions, and Captain Voss herself stepped forward to speak, and Emmerich — watching the reflection in the window glass, out of habit rather than intent — saw the King's hand rise toward his crown.

It was a small movement. Almost nothing. The fingers of the right hand lifted from the armrest, drifted up along the jaw, and touched the iron-and-gold band just above the left ear — lightly, the way a man touches an ache, or an itch beneath a bandage. The fingers pressed. Pressed harder. The knuckles whitened.

And the King's face did not change at all.

That was the part that had woken Emmerich at night. Not the clawing — he had seen the clawing before, in the private moments, the moments he'd been paid to find. What woke him was the stillness above it. The face serene, attentive, nodding along to Captain Voss's report on requisition schedules while the hand at the crown dug in like a drowning man's grip on a rock. Two things happening in one body, and only one of them aware of the other. Or — and this was the thought Emmerich could not make go away — only one of them *belonging* to the same person.

The hand withdrew. Smoothed the King's beard once, casually, as if it had merely been adjusting it. Returned to the armrest.

Emmerich looked down at his notes and wrote something meaningless, because his own hands wanted to shake and idle hands shook visibly.

* * *

The council broke at midday for the meal, and this was where Emmerich earned his silver.

Meals were served in the adjoining hall, but the King customarily took his alone, or with one or two chosen companions, and the courtiers dispersed into clusters of gossip and maneuvering. Emmerich had arranged — through a cousin who supplied the palace kitchens — to be the one who carried the King's afternoon tonic from the physician's tower to the royal apartments. It was a servant's errand, technically beneath a lord, but Emmerich had cultivated a reputation for harmless eagerness, and the physician's assistant was lazy, and no one questioned a man whose whole talent was being unremarkable.

He climbed the north stair with the covered cup in both hands, past guards who nodded to him without interest, along the gallery hung with portraits of dead kings. In the third portrait — Aldric's grandfather — the crown sat differently on the painted brow. Smaller. Emmerich had checked the portraits weeks ago, one slow afternoon, comparing them. The crown in the old paintings was lighter. Thinner. Sometime in the last forty years, the crown had changed — grown heavier, darker, its garnets deeper red. He had mentioned this to the carver, and the carver had gone very quiet and asked him, twice, if he was certain.

He was certain. He had counted the garnets. Nine in the paintings. Eleven now.

Two new stones, in a crown that was supposed to be a thousand years old.

The door to the King's private study stood ajar. Emmerich knocked, announced himself, and entered — and stopped.

The King was standing at the window with his back turned, and he was gripping the crown with both hands.

Not touching it. Not adjusting it. *Gripping* it, both hands, knuckles bloodless, hauling at it as though it were a vice screwed onto his skull, as though he meant to tear it free — and the crown did not move. It sat on his head like it had grown there. And from between the King's teeth came a sound Emmerich would hear for the rest of his life: not a cry, not a word. A low, grinding hum, the sound of a man swallowing a scream whole.

"My lord?" Emmerich said carefully. "Your tonic."

The sound stopped. The hands dropped. When the King turned, his face was composed, pleasant, faintly questioning — a man interrupted at his window, mildly curious about the interruption.

"Ah. Vael." The grey eyes were clear. Clearer than clear; there was something almost luminous in them this close, something that made Emmerich think of ice over deep water. "Set it on the desk. And tell me — how does the fen country fare this winter? Your marshes. I remember the marshes."

"They fare well, Majesty. The eel harvest was good."

"The eels." The King smiled, and it reached his eyes, and Emmerich felt the smile land on him like a hand on the back of the neck. "I stood in those marshes when I was nineteen. My father sent me to survey the drainage works. I was miserable the entire time." A pause. "Wasn't I?"

"Yes, Majesty. So the records say."

"So the records say." The King repeated it softly, tasting the words. He turned back to the window. Below, in the inner courtyard, Hunt soldiers were drilling, their movements precise and silent. "Vael. Do you ever have the feeling that you are remembering your life rather than living it? As though the days were written down somewhere, long ago, and someone is reading them back to you — mostly correctly, but with small errors? With... additions?"

Emmerich's mouth had gone dry. "I — can't say that I have, Majesty."

"No." The King's reflection regarded him from the dark glass, and for a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — the reflection seemed to smile before the man did. "No, I imagine you haven't. Thank you for the tonic. You may go."

Emmerich bowed and walked backward to the door, because one did not turn one's back on the King, and because he did not want to stop watching the window until the last possible moment.

At the threshold, the King spoke again, without turning.

"Vael. Who pays you?"

The words dropped through Emmerich like stones through water. "Majesty, I don't—"

"You've been watching me for six weeks. Carrying tonics you needn't carry. Sitting where you always sit, looking everywhere except at me, which is how people look at things they're paid to look at." The King's voice remained mild, conversational, terribly gentle. "I am not angry. I am asking a question, and I find I very much want the answer. Who pays you?"

Emmerich stood in the doorway with his heart slamming against his ribs, and understood that he was standing on a knife's edge with darkness on either side. Tell the truth, and whatever wore the King's face would know a bone-carver in the low city was asking questions about the crown. Lie, and lie badly, to a creature that had just demonstrated it read men like open books.

"I'm paid by my conscience, Majesty," he said at last. "I'm paid to see that the realm has a king."

A long silence. The drill-yard sounds rose faintly through the glass. Then the King laughed — a real laugh, short and startled, and for one moment the face was entirely, unmistakably human, tired and amused and frightened underneath.

"Well answered," Aldric said. "Get out, Vael, before I decide to ask again."

Emmerich got out.

* * *

He did not go home. He walked, instead, the long way down through the city, past the fountain square where the criers had proclaimed the Hunt in autumn, past shops where his neighbors' charms still hung in windows — little carved protections, horse-brasses and door-tokens, the work of a craftsman whose name Emmerich had extracted from the tanner's clerk for another purse of silver. Marguerite Coldharbour. Bone-carver. The man who had promised Emmerich's family debt erased in exchange for eyes inside the palace, and who had, Emmerich admitted, paid promptly and asked reasonable questions and never once threatened him, which put him well ahead of most men Emmerich dealt with.

He found the workshop's back entrance by the smell of bone dust and lamp oil, and knocked the pattern he'd been given.

The carver opened the door himself. Up close, Marguerite Coldharbour was younger than his reputation, with ink-stained fingers and the wary, tired eyes of a man who slept badly. Behind him, in the lamplight of the workroom, a woman stood half-shadowed by a shelf of carved pieces — sharp-featured, armed, moving with the coiled readiness of a soldier. She looked at Emmerich the way a hawk looks at a field mouse deciding whether it's worth the dive.

"Well?" Coldharbour said.

"He knows," Emmerich said. "Or *it* knows. I don't know which, and that's the whole horror of it. This morning at council he was perfect — better than perfect, sharper than he's been in years. And all the while his hand was at the crown, digging at it like he was trying to get it off, and his face never moved. Then this afternoon, alone—" Emmerich stopped, steadied himself. "Alone, he tried to tear it off with both hands. It wouldn't come. It *wouldn't come*, do you understand? Like it's fastened to the skull. And then he knew I was watching. Knew it to the minute. Asked me who paid me, calm as you please."

The woman stepped forward. "What did you tell him?"

"That my conscience paid me." Emmerich gave a shaky laugh. "He laughed. Gods help me, he laughed like a man — and then he told me to go before he decided to ask again. Which means next time he won't ask. Next time he'll simply have me taken up, and no one will question it, because I'm nobody."

Coldharbour had gone pale. He looked at the woman — Ines, the disgraced oath-keeper, though Emmerich only knew her as "the lady" — and something passed between them, some grim arithmetic completing itself.

"It's nearly done," she said quietly. "The wearing-through. When it can use his mind that clearly and hold his body that tightly, we have weeks. Perhaps less. The Renewal—" She stopped herself, but Emmerich saw the word land on the carver like a blow.

"What renewal?" Emmerich asked. "What happens at the Renewal?"

Neither of them answered him. Coldharbour had picked up a scrap of whalebone from his bench without seeming to notice he'd done it, turning it over and over in his fingers, and his face was the face of a man reading a sentence he wished he hadn't started.

"Lord Vael," he said at last, without looking up. "One more errand. The last one, I swear it. There's a ceremony at midwinter — the coronation renewal, where the King re-swears his oath before the whole realm. I need to know everything about it. The order of the rite. Who stands where. How the crown is handled. Every detail, however small."

"And why would a bone-carver need the ceremony's choreography?"

Coldharbour finally looked up, and Emmerich saw that the man's hands had gone perfectly still around the bit of whalebone — stiller than any living hands had a right to be.

"Because," he said, "in a few weeks' time, either I'm going to save the King's soul in front of the entire court — or the thing wearing his crown is going to add mine to its collection. And I'd very much like to know the ground before I stand on it."

Outside, somewhere across the rooftops, a bell rang the hour, and Emmerich Vael — nobody important, overlooked all his life, and now the most frightened man in Vellamark — held out his hand for another purse of silver.
