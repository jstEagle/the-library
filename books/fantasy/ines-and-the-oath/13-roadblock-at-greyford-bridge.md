## 13. Roadblock at Greyford Bridge

They saw the smoke before they saw the bridge.

It rose off the road in a thin grey column, unhurried, the way smoke rises when nobody is in a hurry to put it out. Marguerite reined the mule to a stop and squinted down the long descent toward the river. Greyford sat in its shallow bowl of fields like a stone in a spoon—rooftops, a mill wheel turning slow, and beyond it the bridge itself, wide and old and clogged with carts.

And at both ends of the bridge, banners.

"The Hunt," Ines said. She said it flatly, the way you'd say *rain coming*.

"I can see that."

"No, look." She stood in the stirrups, one hand shading her eyes. "Two checkpoints. One on each side. They're not searching travelers—they're sealing the crossing. Nobody goes east without papers, and I'd wager my sword arm nobody's papers are good enough today."

Marguerite counted the figures at the near checkpoint. Eight soldiers, maybe ten, a sergeant pacing, a brazier burning despite the mild afternoon because braziers were theater and the Hunt understood theater. A queue of farmers and peddlers stretched back from the barrier, and every so often a soldier would wave someone through or turn them around, and the ones turned around would stand there arguing until another soldier made them stop arguing.

"They've sealed the whole valley road," he said. "There isn't another crossing for thirty miles."

"There's a ferry."

"A ferry is a man who asks questions."

Ines dropped back into her saddle and looked at him, and there was something almost amused in the set of her mouth, which was rare enough that he noticed it the way you notice sun through cloud. "You charmed a tanner's clerk out of a reliquary address with a carved fish," she said. "You can charm a sergeant."

"That was different. The clerk wanted to be charmed. He was bored and lonely and his wife didn't laugh at his jokes."

"And sergeants aren't bored?"

"Sergeants are bored and *suspicious*. It's a professional condition."

She snorted—a small, undignified sound she'd have denied making—and turned her horse back onto the road. "Then we don't charm him. We give him nothing to be suspicious about. Papers, a story, and a boring afternoon. Come on. We lose the light if we sit here admiring the view."

They left the mule and the horses with a widow who kept a hay barn half a mile back and asked no questions because Ines paid her a silver piece and silver pieces were better than questions. Then they walked, because travelers with money rode and travelers with nothing walked, and today they were two pilgrims going east to a shrine, poor as dirt and glad of it.

Ines had spent the previous evening hunched over the inn room table by candlelight, doing something with wax and a brass stamp she'd carried sewn into her coat lining for eleven years. Marguerite had watched her work without pretending not to—the way her thumb pressed the seal into the cooling wax, the little breath she held, the way she checked each impression twice against a memory only she could see. Her old Order seal. Forged copies of a thing that no longer legally existed, stamped onto travel writs written in a hand so perfectly bureaucratic it made his eyes ache.

"Where did you learn to write like a customs clerk?" he'd asked.

"Where did you learn to carve grief into whalebone?" she'd answered, and that had been the end of the conversation.

Now the writs sat inside his coat, against his chest, and felt heavier than paper should. Two pilgrims, bound for the shrine of Saint Vessia at Aldenmere, carrying letters of passage issued by the provost's office at Cael Harrow. The ink was right. The seals were right. The only question was whether the sergeant at Greyford Bridge was the kind of man who read documents or the kind who read people.

Marguerite had been reading people his whole life, usually through bone, sometimes through their faces, and he did not like the odds.

* * *

The queue moved at the speed of suspicion.

They joined it behind a tinker with a cart full of kettles and ahead of a family of five whose youngest child was eating an apple with the total commitment of the very young. The soldiers worked in pairs—one took papers, one looked at faces—and between them they had developed a rhythm: hold out your hand, state your business, wait while a man with a bored mouth read what you'd written.

The tinker went through in under a minute. The family took longer; the soldier made the father open every bundle, found nothing but clothes and bread, and waved them on with the expression of a man disappointed not to find sin.

Then it was their turn.

The soldier who took their papers was young—younger than Marguerite had expected, with the kind of face that hadn't yet decided what it thought about anything. His partner was older and stood slightly apart, arms crossed, watching the crowd rather than the documents. That was bad. The young one could be managed. The old one was the kind who'd been a soldier long enough to smell a story that had been rehearsed.

"Purpose of travel," the young soldier said. Not even a question. A slot to fill.

"Pilgrimage," Ines said. "Saint Vessia at Aldenmere. My brother and I go every year at this season. Our mother's vow."

Marguerite kept his eyes down and his hands still. *Brother.* They'd agreed on it the night before—siblings traveled together, siblings didn't invite questions the way a man and woman sharing a road did—but hearing her say it so easily, with that small tired warmth women used for family obligations, he felt something twist in his chest that had no business twisting.

The young soldier held the writ up to the light. Turned it over. Marguerite watched him look at the seal and saw the exact moment the boy realized he didn't actually know what the seal was supposed to look like, and the moment after that when he decided it probably didn't matter, and the moment after *that* when his eyes flicked sideways to his partner, asking permission to not care.

The old soldier came over anyway.

He took the writ without apology, read it top to bottom, and then did the thing Marguerite dreaded most: he looked up. Not at Ines—at Marguerite. A long, level, unhurried look, the look of a man cataloguing.

"Vessia's shrine," the old soldier said. "Long walk for a vow."

"Our mother made us promise," Ines said. "Before she died. You know how mothers are."

Something crossed the old soldier's face—not softening exactly, but recognition. He'd had a mother once. Everyone had. It was the thinnest of levers and Ines had found it in three seconds flat.

"She's dead?" the young soldier asked, and immediately looked embarrassed.

"Eleven years," Ines said, which was true, though not of any mother.

The old soldier handed the writ back. Then his eyes went to Marguerite again, and stayed there, and Marguerite understood with cold clarity that this man was going to ask one more question, and that the question would be the wrong one, and that Ines's perfect forgery was about to die on this bridge for no reason except that old soldiers got paid either way.

"You," the old soldier said. "You've got carver's hands. I noticed at the barrier. Calluses here—" he tapped the heel of his own palm, "—and here. Knife work. What do you carve?"

The queue shifted behind them. Somewhere a cart driver swore at a mule. Marguerite felt Ines go very still beside him, the stillness of a drawn blade deciding.

And he heard himself say, easily, warmly, in a voice he barely recognized as his own: "Saints, mostly. Little ones. For the shrine stalls at Aldenmere—there's a fellow there buys them by the dozen, sells them to pilgrims who forgot to bring their own. I'm not good enough for churches, mind. Just good enough for tourists." He smiled, and it was astonishing how easy the smile was, how it sat on his face like it lived there. "Would you like to see? I've got one nearly finished."

He did not have one nearly finished. He had, in the inner pocket of his coat, wrapped in cloth, a small oakwood token he'd carved three nights ago at the inn fire because his hands had needed something to do—a fox, curled nose-to-tail, the grain worked so the animal seemed mid-breath. He had carved it for no reason. He had kept it for no reason. And now his fingers were already bringing it out, and some calm, distant part of his mind observed that he was about to gamble their lives on a whim of wood, and that the whim felt more honest than the forged papers ever had.

The old soldier took the fox and turned it over once, twice. His thumb found the curve of the spine. And Marguerite watched something happen in the man's face—the boredom draining out of it, replaced first by surprise and then by something older and hungrier, the look of a man holding a small beautiful thing in a life that contained very few of them.

"My daughter," the old soldier said, and stopped, and started again. "My girl's six. She—" He cleared his throat. "What would you want for it?"

"It's yours," Marguerite said. "A gift. For letting a pilgrim keep his feet warm on a long road."

"I can't take—"

"You can and you will, or I'll be obliged to carve you a second one out of spite, and spite makes my work ugly." He said it lightly, grinning, and the young soldier laughed, and even the old soldier's mouth twitched at the corner.

The old soldier closed his fist around the fox like a man closing a gate against the wind. "Go on, then," he said gruffly. "Mind the bridge boards, middle section's rotten. And tell your sister—" he glanced at Ines, embarrassed again, "—safe roads."

They walked. Across the planks, past the second checkpoint where a different soldier glanced at the same writ for half the time, off the bridge and onto the east road, and Marguerite did not let his shoulders drop until the bridge was a hundred paces behind them and the fields had swallowed the banners from view.

Then Ines stopped walking.

She turned and looked at him with an expression he couldn't parse—something between exasperation and wonder, lit from underneath by reluctant delight.

"You gave away the fox," she said.

"He liked the fox."

"You gave away the fox we— you *carved that for yourself*. I watched you make it. You sat up half the night sanding its ears."

"It was going to sit in my pocket being admired by no one. Now it sits on a soldier's mantel being adored by a six-year-old. That's a promotion." He said it lightly, but his heart was hammering in a way that had nothing to do with the checkpoint, and he wasn't sure the lightness was fooling her.

"You realize," she said slowly, "that the papers might have failed. That man was going to ask you something else. I saw it. I was reaching for the knife under my coat."

"I know."

"And instead you—" She gestured at him, at all of him, as if he were a phenomenon she was trying to classify. "You just *gave* him a piece of your work. Like it cost you nothing."

"It didn't cost me anything."

"Marguerite." She said his name the way she said few things: plainly, without armor. "I watched you burn ten years of carvings in a hearth rather than let the Hunt find them. I watched you flinch when a customer complimented a mourning charm. Everything you make costs you something. I've seen it. So don't stand there and tell me giving that fox away was nothing."

The east road ran empty ahead of them, hedgerows gold in the late light, and Marguerite found that he had no clever answer prepared, which was unlike him, and that he didn't especially mind, which was more unlike him still.

"It was a good fox," he said finally. "It deserved better than my pocket."

Ines looked at him a moment longer. Then she turned and started walking, and said, over her shoulder, in a voice carefully stripped of everything: "Keep up, brother."

But she was smiling. He caught it in profile before she turned away, quick as a fish under water, and he walked after her through the golden evening thinking that he had faced down the Hunt's checkpoint with less fear than he now felt looking at the back of her head, and that he would follow that head—those shoulders, that stride, that buried smile—down any road in the world, across any bridge, into any fire, and call it a reasonable errand.

*Oh*, he thought, with the mild detached horror of a man diagnosing himself. *Oh, that's what this is.*

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath a rowan tree, off the road far enough that a passing patrol would need luck and a lantern to find them. No fire. Cold bread, hard cheese, water from a skin. Ines took first watch, sitting with her back against the rowan's trunk, knees up, sword across them, watching the dark field the way cats watch mouse holes.

Marguerite lay on his bedroll and did not sleep.

He turned the day over in his mind the way he'd turn a carving, looking for flaws. The checkpoint had gone well—better than well, it had gone *easily*, and ease was a thing he'd learned to distrust. But it wasn't the checkpoint that kept him awake. It was the moment afterward, the moment on the road, when she'd said his name without armor and he'd had nothing to hide behind.

Ten years, he had built his life out of concealment. Carve true, live false. Give the widow her husband's last words and pocket the coin and never let anyone see what the hearing cost him. It had worked. It had kept him alive through proclamations and pyres and neighbors marked with ash. And then a disgraced oath-keeper with a forged seal and a soldier's stride had walked into his hiding place four weeks ago, and in four weeks she had dismantled more of his concealment than a decade of fear had built.

She knew what his gift cost. She knew he burned his work. She knew he'd given away the fox because the fox deserved better than a pocket, and she knew that meant something, and she'd said his name like it meant something too, and then she'd armored up again so fast he could have imagined the whole exchange.

Except he'd seen the smile. He'd stake his hands on the smile.

He rolled onto his side. Across the hollow, Ines sat her watch, moonlit, motionless, her face turned toward the dark fields and whatever threats the morning would bring. The second vessel rode in her pack next to the first, wrapped in oilcloth, two-thirds of a rite that would unbind a king neither of them had met. Ahead of them lay the motherhouse ruins, the third vessel, the Hunt, Serane Voss and her patient ledger, and a coronation renewal that was coming whether they were ready or not.

Ahead of them lay every reason in the world to keep his distance.

"Your watch," Ines said quietly, some hours later, and he rose and took her place against the rowan, and she lay down where he'd lain, and within minutes her breathing slowed into sleep—which meant she trusted him, or meant she was exhausted, and he suspected the second and chose to believe the first.

He sat with the sword across his knees, badly, the way a carver holds a sword, and watched the stars wheel over Greyford's fields, and admitted it to himself at last, plainly, in the privacy of his own skull where no one could hear and no one could burn for it:

He would follow her anywhere. Over any bridge. Through any checkpoint. Into the motherhouse, into the palace, into the teeth of the Hunt itself. Not for the king, who was a stranger. Not for the oath, which wasn't his. For her—for the woman who forged lies by candlelight and said *brother* like a wound and smiled like a fish surfacing.

Which was, he reflected, watching the eastern sky begin its slow grey turn, an extremely inconvenient thing to realize about oneself in the middle of a manhunt.

Toward dawn, hoofbeats sounded on the far road—many hooves, moving fast, riding west toward Greyford Bridge. He counted them out of habit and stopped counting somewhere past twenty. A column. A pursuit, or a reinforcement, or a net drawing tighter around a valley they'd only just escaped.

He woke Ines with a hand on her shoulder, and she came awake instantly, fully, eyes sharp in the dark.

"Riders," he whispered. "Westbound. A lot of them."

Ines listened to the fading drum of hooves, and he saw her do the same arithmetic he'd done, and arrive at the same sum.

"They're not chasing us," she said slowly. "They're heading *toward* the bridge. Toward Greyford." Her jaw tightened. "Someone told them two pilgrims crossed east last night. Someone's describing us right now."

She was already rolling her bedroll, already moving, and Marguerite reached for his pack and followed her into the pre-dawn dark—down the east road, away from the riders, toward the ruined motherhouse and the third vessel, following her anywhere, exactly as promised, and wondering how long a description could travel faster than two people on foot.
