## 15. Serane's Ledger

The archive beneath the Hall of Records smelled of dust and tallow and old decisions.

Serane Voss had been coming down here for three nights now, after her captains went home, after the lamps in the upper halls guttered out one by one. She'd told the night clerk she was auditing Hunt expenditures. He hadn't believed her—no one audited anything at midnight—but he'd stamped her pass anyway, because Captain Voss did not ask twice for things.

She sat alone at a scarred oak table with a single oil lamp and a ledger older than her grandmother. The binding records of the Order of the Oath. Forty years of them, transferred to crown custody when the Order fell, catalogued by clerks who had never understood what they were holding and shelved accordingly: misfiled, water-stained, forgotten.

That was why no one had found this before. Or perhaps that wasn't why at all. Perhaps someone had found it, and buried it deeper than a shelf.

She turned another page with two fingers, careful as handling a wound.

*Year Thirty-One of the Binding. Renewal rite performed. His Majesty's humors noted as melancholic; spirit-response within tolerances.*

Tolerances. As if a king's mind were a barrel to be gauged.

She had grown up on stories of the binding. Every child in Vellamark had. A protective spirit, called up by the Order in the kingdom's darkest hour, bound into the crown itself so that no invader, no plague-year, no famine could break the realm while its king wore Aldric's circlet. The spirit watched. The spirit warned. The spirit held the walls of Greyford when the border lords rebelled, and every schoolchild could recite the names of the keepers who had forged it.

Serane had believed in it the way she believed in stone walls and winter: something solid, something that held.

Then she had joined the Hunt, and learned what else the crown asked her to believe.

* * *

It was the third ledger that broke her.

She almost missed it—a marginal note in a hand she recognized from earlier pages, the senior keeper's script, cramped and certain. Dated eleven years ago. The year King Aldric's father began to fail.

*Renewal deferred by order of Lord Chancellor Merevane. Keeper Halvard objects. Note appended:*

*Halvard claims the deferral will starve the vessel-bound and force it to feed elsewhere. I have reminded him whose treasury funds his cloister. The spirit's hunger is not the crown's concern so long as it remains hungry. A hungry thing obeys.*

Serane read it four times.

*A hungry thing obeys.*

She set down the page. Her hands were steady—that was her reputation, steady hands, steady voice—and she made herself look at the lamp flame until her breathing slowed.

Because there was a second note beneath it, in a different ink, added later. Halvard's hand again, but ragged now, pressed hard enough to tear:

*They are not starving it. They are shaping it. Deferral is not neglect—it is training. Merevane wants a spirit that serves the throne rather than the realm, and he has found the method: starve the bond, and the bond turns to its bearer alone. It will love whoever feeds it. It will devour whoever does not. This is not protection anymore. This is a leash, and we tied it around a living god.*

Below that, one final line, smaller, as if written in shame or haste:

*I have moved the third vessel. If any keeper reads this: the rite cannot be undone without all three. Forgive us. We built the cage, and then they taught the animal to love the bars.*

Serane sat back in the dark.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, a door closed, footsteps crossed the hall, faded. The ordinary sounds of a kingdom going about its business atop a secret like a rotten floor.

She thought about the soldier.

Corporal Dennet Harrow, twenty-six years old, found dead in an alley off Tanner's Row with a gut wound and no relic. She had stood over his body in the rain and felt the shape of the story before she had any of its pieces: a man who had seen something in the palace, who had run, who had died carrying something worth killing for. She had posted Marguerite Coldharbour's likeness within a day—the reliquary owner's description, kind eyes and clever hands—and told herself it was procedure.

But she had also pulled Harrow's service record, quietly, and found he'd been assigned to the king's private guard six months ago. And she had pulled the duty rosters for those six months, and found that three other men from that rotation had requested transfer, and one had resigned outright, and one—

One had drowned. In a cistern. Inside the palace.

Accident, said the report. Serane had read accidents before. They always sounded exactly like this one.

* * *

She worked until the fourth bell, copying pages by lamplight into her own hand, because she trusted no clerk and no scribe and, increasingly, no institution that employed either. Her father had taught her that much: if a truth matters, write it yourself, seal it yourself, hide it where only you can find it. He'd been a customs officer in Greyford, a man of ledgers himself, murdered along with her mother and her sister in the plague-year when the hedge-witch's fever-charms failed—or so the story went, though Serane had been nine, and what she remembered was not the witch failing but the witch screaming that her charms had been *cut*, deliberately, by men in crown livery who wanted the quarantine district emptied.

She had spent her whole life choosing not to remember that part clearly.

She copied Halvard's notes word for word. She copied the deferral orders—there were seven of them across eleven years, each signed by Merevane or his successors, each stretching the interval between renewals past every tolerance the Order had ever recorded. She copied the expenditure entries showing payments from the privy purse to unnamed recipients in the years the vessels went missing. Three vessels, scattered after the Order's fall, the histories said. Fallen. Scattered. As if by storm.

The ledgers said otherwise. The vessels hadn't scattered. They'd been *sent*—paid couriers, sealed instructions, and then, once delivered, the couriers themselves appear in the mortality rolls. Fever. Accident. Drowning.

Someone had hidden the unbinding rite so thoroughly that even the crown's own archives forgot it existed. And someone had done it knowing that the thing in the crown would grow hungrier and stranger with every deferred renewal, would eat its way through one king's mind and be leashed onto the next, generation after generation, a dynasty passing down a parasite like an heirloom.

And the Hunt—her Hunt—had been burning gifted men and women in market squares for two years to protect that inheritance.

Her pen stopped.

Ink pooled under the nib, a small black bloom spreading through the fibers. She watched it spread and thought: *this is what I've been. Ink on a page, doing what the hand commands.*

* * *

Dawn found her climbing the stairs to the Hall of Records proper, her copies sewn into the lining of her coat, and the hall already awake with clerks and the smell of fresh bread from the vendors outside. She had a summons waiting at her office. Of course she did. Midnight visits to restricted archives do not go unnoticed in a building full of people paid to notice things.

Lord Chancellor Merevane received her at the ninth bell.

He was not what strangers expected. Too young for the office, too pleasant, silver-haired at forty in a way that looked distinguished rather than aged, and he greeted her with genuine warmth—she had never doubted the warmth was genuine, which was somehow worse. He poured wine without asking. He asked after her captains by name. He had attended her mother's memorial service eleven years ago, standing solemn at the back, and had sent a letter afterward saying the realm owed her family more than grief.

"Captain Voss." He settled behind his desk, folding his hands. "You've been busy."

"The Hunt is busy, my lord."

"Mm. And the Hunt's captain has been reading things that aren't hers." No anger in it. That was the frightening part—there never was. "I don't mean the expenditures audit. That was well done; you found irregularities we've chased for a year. I mean the Order ledgers."

Serane kept her face still. "I review all historical material relevant to active investigations."

"Do you." He smiled, faintly, and turned a sheet of paper on his desk toward her. Her own access log, transcribed in a clerk's neat hand. Third ledger, restricted shelf, four hours. "You're the best officer the Hunt has ever produced, Serane. Do you know why I say that? Not your arrest record—though it's exemplary. It's that you believe. Most men join the Hunt for wages or spite. You joined it because magic took your family, and you have never once let that grief curdle into cruelty. You burn the guilty and shelter the frightened and you sleep at night. I have watched you do it for two years." He leaned forward. "So I'm going to treat you like the officer you are, and tell you plainly: whatever you think you found down there, put it back."

"My lord—"

"Aledric's grandfather bound a spirit to save us all. Something has gone wrong with it—I won't insult you by pretending otherwise; you've stood near the king, you've seen his hands shake. The Order tried to mend it and failed, and their failure nearly broke the realm, and the crown absorbed the wreckage and has managed it, imperfectly, painfully, ever since. That is the whole of it. There is no conspiracy. There is maintenance." He picked up his wine, drank, set it down. "What there is, is a war. The gifted are rising—the dead man in Tanner's Row was carrying stolen state secrets for them, did you know? And right now, today, the only thing standing between this kingdom and open sorcery is the Hunt, and the Hunt is you. I need you steady, Captain. Not reading ghosts in forty-year-old margins."

"The ledgers say the deferrals were deliberate," Serane said. "Eleven years of them. Signed orders."

Something moved behind Merevane's eyes—not guilt, she realized. Patience. The patience of a man watching a child reach for a stove.

"The ledgers say a great many things," he said gently. "Dead men wrote them. Dead men are wrong constantly. Here is what is alive: the coronation renewal is in five weeks. The realm will gather. The king will swear his oath before ten thousand witnesses, and the spirit will be renewed, and whatever imperfection exists in the binding will be corrected by keepers better trained than Halvard ever was. Your task between now and then is simple. Find the bone-carver. Find the relic he carries. Bring me both, and let the adults manage the rest." He rose, signaling the audience over, and came around the desk to stand close—close enough that she caught the scent of cedar oil in his hair. "You lost everything to the gifted once, Serane. Don't let them take your purpose too. It's the last thing they'll try to steal."

He walked her to the door himself. At the threshold, his hand light on her shoulder, he added, almost kindly:

"The copies you made. My clerk will collect them this afternoon. Consider it tidiness, not suspicion."

* * *

She gave up the copies. Refusal would have ended her command by sundown, and a captain without command helps no one—she had learned that arithmetic long ago, in the quarantine district, at nine years old, hiding in a cellar while men in livery burned charms in the street.

But she kept one thing. One page, torn from the third ledger days before her final visit, folded flat against her ribs inside her coat, where the sewn linings of her copies had been. Halvard's last line, in his shamed, hurried hand:

*If any keeper reads this: the rite cannot be undone without all three.*

Three vessels. The bone-carver carried the oath-bone, which meant he knew—or would soon know—what the vessels were for. Which meant the carver was not fleeing the Hunt at all.

He was gathering them.

She stood at her office window that evening, watching the city lights come on below, the lamplighters moving through the dusk like slow fireflies. On her desk lay two papers. The first was the warrant for Marguerite Coldharbour's arrest, renewed, current, bearing her own signature. The second was a blank sheet, and she had been looking at it for an hour.

Five weeks to the renewal. Merevane wanted the carver brought in before then—which meant Merevane feared what the carver might do at the ceremony, which meant the chancellor knew exactly how fragile the binding was, which meant everything he had said in that warm, reasonable room was a lie wearing a uniform of truth.

Or she was wrong. She might be wrong. Grief made conspirators of us all; she had preached it to her own lieutenants a hundred times.

She thought of Corporal Harrow dying in the rain, pressing something into a stranger's hands. *The king is not the king.* She'd read it in the patrol report—witnesses heard the dying man say it. She'd filed it as delirium.

She picked up the pen. Her hands were steady. They were always steady.

She wrote a single name on the blank sheet—the name of the one person in the palace who had served the king longer than Merevane had served the chancellery, an old chamberlain half-retired, half-forgotten, who had been a junior clerk in the Hall of Records forty years ago.

Then she rang for her horse.

There was a man in Vellamark with kind eyes and clever hands, and tomorrow morning his likeness would come down off every post in the city—officially recalled for revision, a clerical error, nothing more. And Serane Voss would follow him instead of hunt him, quietly, alone, without warrant or sanction, because the ledger in her coat had cracked something open that warm words could not reseal.

She had spent her life believing the crown was the wall that held.

Tonight, for the first time, she wondered what it was a wall against—and which side of it she'd been standing on all along.
