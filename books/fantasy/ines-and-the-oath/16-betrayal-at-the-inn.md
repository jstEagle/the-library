## 16. Betrayal at the Inn

The inn was called the Drowned Lamb, which Marguerite thought was either a very bad omen or a very honest one, and after three days on the road he had stopped caring which.

It sat where the Greyford road bent toward the river flats, all sagging beams and peat smoke, the kind of place that asked no questions because it didn't want any asked of itself. The common room smelled of tallow and wet wool. A one-eyed dog slept under the settle. The innkeeper, a broad woman named Hesta with forearms like a farrier's, took their coin without looking at their faces, which was exactly the service they were paying for.

"Two nights," Ines said. "Maybe three."

Hesta counted the silver. "Back room's free. Cold, but the chimney draws."

"It's always cold," Ines said.

Marguerite carried their packs up himself, though Ines watched him do it with that particular set of her jaw that meant she thought he was being foolish and had decided not to say so. The back room held a bed too short for either of them, a table scarred by decades of knives, and a window that looked out on the yard and the woodpile and, beyond the fence, the flat grey country running toward the hills.

He set the two vessels on the table between them. Wrapped in wool, they looked like nothing—two lumps of old bone, one the size of a fist, one smaller, curved like a cup. He had carried them for weeks now, through checkpoints and flooded crypts and one memorable night hiding in a drainage culvert while a Hunt patrol argued about dice on the road above his head. He still caught himself listening to them sometimes, the way you'd press an ear to a door. They murmured. They never said anything useful.

"We should keep moving," Ines said. She stood at the window, one hand resting on the frame, watching the yard. She always stood like that now—at windows, at doorways, with her back to the wall and her eyes on the exits. He'd started to think of it as her natural posture, the way other people slouched or folded their arms. "Greyford's sealed. Voss will have riders on every crossroad between here and the motherhouse."

"So we rest tonight and move at first light," Marguerite said. "You haven't slept properly in four days. You fell asleep standing up at supper."

"I did not fall asleep."

"You leaned against the doorpost and your eyes closed and you made a small sound like a kettle deciding whether to boil."

"That was tactical rest."

"It was snoring."

She turned from the window and gave him the look—the one that had stopped terrifying him somewhere around the second week and had started doing something else entirely, something he was careful not to name even in the privacy of his own skull. "One night," she said. "Then we're gone before the fog lifts."

"One night," he agreed.

* * *

He woke once in the dark, certain he'd heard something.

That was the trouble with his gift, or one of the troubles: silence was never silent. Every room held its residue. The Drowned Lamb's walls had soaked up forty years of arguments and griefs and fumbled couplings, and if he let himself listen he could hear all of it layered like sediment—a man weeping over debts in some winter long dead, a girl laughing on the stairs, Hesta's own voice repeating *out, out, I'll not bury another* to someone who never left. He kept his gift leashed tight these days, a habit ten years in the making, but in strange rooms it leaked. The bones in the world wouldn't hush just because he wanted quiet.

What woke him wasn't the room's residue, though. It was the dog. The one-eyed dog under the settle had started up in the common room below, barking—not the lazy alarm-bark of a stranger at the door but something higher, faster, frightened.

Marguerite lay still and listened past the dog, past the settling timbers, down into the marrow of the house.

Boots. Many boots. Not arriving—arrived. Already in the yard, already at the doors, distributed around the building with the unhurried competence of people who had done this many times before and expected no resistance worth planning for.

He was out of bed and across the room before the thought finished forming. His hand found Ines's shoulder and she came awake the way she always did—all at once, silently, her knife already in her hand as her eyes opened, and he would have marveled at it any other time.

"How many?" she breathed.

"I don't know. Eight? Ten? They're in the yard. Some at the front. Someone's at the stable side." He pressed his palm flat against the floorboards, letting the house speak through its old bones. "Two moving toward the kitchen door. One on the roof side—I can hear him shifting weight on the—"

The window exploded inward.

Glass, then a boot, then a Hunt soldier in half-armor coming through it like the wall had insulted him, lantern-light swinging wild. Ines moved—not away from the man but *into* him, low and fast, and there was a wet sound and the soldier folded sideways into the table, taking it down with him, the vessels skidding across the floorboards.

Marguerite dove for them. Got one under his hand, the big one, felt its murmur spike against his palm like a trapped bird.

The door burst open behind them. Two more soldiers, swords out, and behind them—unhurried, lamplight catching the pale line of a scar along her jaw—Captain Serane Voss.

"Ines of the Broken Order," Voss said. Her voice was level, almost tired. "And the carver. You've made this easy. I want to thank whoever sold you to me."

"Later," Ines said, and threw the table.

Not the whole table—he'd remember afterward the absurd detail that it was only half of it, wrenched up by one edge and hurled with a strength that had nothing to do with her size—and the two soldiers flinched back into each other and the doorway, and Ines grabbed Marguerite by the collar and hauled him toward the window.

"The vessels—" he gasped.

"You have one. Go!"

"I'm not leaving—"

"*Go.*" She shoved him onto the sill. Below, the yard was chaos—lanterns, shouting, the dog going mad—and beyond the fence the woodpile, and beyond the woodpile the dark shoulder of the river flats, and beyond that the hills. "First light," she said, fast, gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise. "The old drover's track, north. If I'm not there by midday, don't wait. Take them to the motherhouse yourself. You know what they look like now. You know what to listen for."

"Ines—"

"Marguerite." For one moment, in the middle of all the noise and the splintering wood and the boots on the stairs, she looked at him with something naked in her face, something she'd been keeping behind the jaw-set and the tactics for weeks. "I forged this binding. It's mine to end. That was always the truth of it. Go be the man who carries the bones."

Then the door gave way entirely, and she turned to meet it, and Marguerite went out the window.

* * *

He didn't run for the fence. He ran for the stable.

It was instinct, or spite, or the bone-sense that had kept him alive for ten years—later he couldn't have said—but he knew the yard was covered, knew the fence line was where they'd expect a runner to go, so he went through the stable instead, among the terrified horses, and out the gap where the rail had rotted away, and dropped into the ditch behind the farrier's shed with the vessel clutched to his chest like a child.

Behind him the inn burned bright with lanterns and voices. He heard Voss's voice, clear and cold across the yard: "*Alive. I want her alive. Anyone cuts her and answers to me.*"

He heard Ines answer something—he couldn't make out the words, but he knew her tone, the dry contempt she saved for people she considered beneath argument—and then the sound of steel, several blades at once, and a body hitting the ground that wasn't hers, and then more boots than he could count converging on one point of light.

He should have gone back.

He knew that even as he crawled along the ditch, mud soaking through his sleeves, the vessel's murmur frantic against his ribs. He knew it the way you know a debt is coming due. There were perhaps ten of them. She had taken down two already. She was the finest fighter he had ever seen and she had told him to go and he was going, and every yard of distance he put between himself and the Drowned Lamb felt like a stone added to a pack he would carry the rest of his life.

At the treeline he stopped. Just once. Turned back.

The inn's windows glowed. Figures moved across the light—one of them limping, one of them being supported between two others. And then a woman's silhouette in the doorway, tall, arms bound or held, head high, and even at that distance, even in silhouette, he knew the set of her shoulders. Not dragged. *Escorted.* Walking out on her own feet because she refused to give them the sight of anything else.

They put her in a wagon.

Marguerite stood in the dark with the bone pressed against his heart and hated himself with a completeness he had never managed before, not in ten years of hidden gifts and guilty coins. He had faced the drowned dead in a flooded crypt and kept his mind. He had lied to sergeants and charmed patrols and walked past ash-marked doors with steady hands. None of it had ever required him to walk away from her.

"If I'm not there by midday, don't wait."

He waited until the wagon's lantern shrank to a spark on the Greyford road. Then he turned north, into the hills, and ran.

* * *

The old drover's track was exactly where she'd said it would be, a thread of a path climbing through gorse and scree, invisible from the road unless you knew to look. He followed it upward until the inn's glow vanished behind the ridge, until the only sounds were his own breath and wind in the gorse and, faintly, ceaselessly, the murmur of the vessel.

He didn't stop at midday.

Midday came and went and he sat on a stone above the valley with the track empty behind him, north and south, no figure climbing, no signal, nothing, and he told himself she'd told him not to wait, and he stayed anyway, and the sun crossed the sky and touched the western ridges, and he stayed.

Toward dusk he heard footsteps on the path below and his heart lurched so hard it hurt—and then he heard the rhythm of them, wrong, too heavy, two sets moving in step, and he flattened behind the stone with the vessel silent in his grip because he had learned, in the last hour, how to ask it for silence, and it obeyed, and he wondered distantly when that had started happening.

Two Hunt soldiers climbed past his hiding place, talking low about the prisoner, about the captain, about how she'd taken three of them down before they got the irons on her and Voss had docked nobody's pay for it, which everyone agreed was strange.

"They're taking her to the palace," one said. "Fast horses, straight through the night."

"Not executing her?"

"Captain's orders. Nobody knows why."

Marguerite lay against the cold stone until their footsteps faded, and the stars came out, and the vessel murmured against his chest like something grieving.

*To the palace,* he thought. *They're taking her to the palace.*

And underneath the self-hatred, underneath the fear, something else began to turn over—slow, cold, and clear. Because the palace meant the king. The king meant the spirit. And Serane Voss, who hunted the gifted with a ledger full of doubts, had ordered a captured oath-keeper kept alive and rushed to the crown's own seat without explanation.

Which meant either the captain served the thing wearing Aldric's face—

—or she was starting to suspect it existed.

Either way, the road ahead ran through the one place in Vellamark he least wanted to go, carrying bones that whispered, toward a prison he couldn't breach and a king who wasn't a king—and the only person who knew the way was locked in a wagon rolling through the dark toward it.

Marguerite got up, settled the vessel inside his coat, and started walking north in the dark, alone, hating every step and taking them anyway.
