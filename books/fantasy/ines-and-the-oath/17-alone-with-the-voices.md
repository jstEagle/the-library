## 17. Alone with the Voices

The charcoal burner's hut sat at the edge of a clearing where the pines grew thin and crooked, as if even the trees had given up on that patch of earth. Marguerite had found it three days ago, or maybe four—the days had gone soft at the edges since Greyford, since the inn, since Ines's hands shoving him through the kitchen window while soldiers poured in the front.

*Run,* she'd said. *Don't look back. Don't you dare look back.*

He had looked back. Of course he had. He'd seen her go down under two men in Hunt grey, seen the captain—Voss, the name was Voss—shout something sharp, and then he'd run, because Ines had told him to and because running was the only thing his body knew how to do anymore. He'd run through the wood with the two bone-vessels wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, one from the drowned chapel, one from the blind hermit's keeping, and he hadn't stopped until his lungs burned and his legs gave out beneath him here, in this hut that smelled of ash and old smoke and someone else's abandoned life.

Now he sat on the floor with his back against the cold stone hearth, and he did not move.

There was food. The burner had left dried apples and a sack of barley and half a wheel of cheese gone hard at the rind. There was water, a rain barrel outside catching the thin drizzle that had started this morning. There was even wood, stacked neat as an argument beside the door. Everything a man needed to survive.

Marguerite ate because his body demanded it, mechanically, without tasting anything. Then he sat some more.

The vessels lay on the floor between his knees like sleeping animals. He could feel them even when he wasn't touching them—a faint warmth through the oilcloth, a hum like a struck string still ringing somewhere below hearing. Two of the three. Two-thirds of a miracle, and no third vessel, no Ines, no plan, and no idea how to get into a palace he'd never seen except in a woodcut on a broadsheet.

North, then. That was the thought that kept circling back to him, patient as a vulture. *Go north.* Past Vellamark's borders, past the Hunt's reach, up into the freeholds where magic was just weather and nobody burned for it. He'd heard stories. A carver could live there. A carver who heard the dead could even be *respected* there, sought after, paid well to listen to what grieving families couldn't say themselves. He could set up shop in some mountain town with a good view and bad wine, and in twenty years the memory of all this would wear smooth as river stone.

Ines would rot in a cell. Or worse. The Hunt didn't execute oath-breakers quickly; they wanted names first, and Ines knew every name the old Order had ever kept.

But she'd told him to run. She'd made him promise nothing, which was somehow worse—if she'd made him promise, he could at least hate himself for breaking it. Instead she'd simply looked at him through the window, blood already at her temple, and said *run*, and now every hour he stayed in this hut was an hour he was choosing himself over her, and every hour he spent imagining the north road was another brick in the wall of that choice.

He got up. He paced. The hut was eight steps long; he measured them out like a sentence being counted.

On the fourth pass, the oath-bone spoke.

It had been quiet since the inn—quiet since the alley, really, if he was honest, quiet in the way a wound is quiet before it festers. But tonight, alone in the dark with the drizzle ticking on the roof, it came alive against his chest where he wore it strung on cord, and the voice that came through it was one he knew.

*Please.*

The soldier's voice. The man in the alley with his guts opened and his hands shaking, pressing the bone into Marguerite's palms with the last strength he had. *The king is not the king.* Ten words and a death, and Marguerite had carried both ever since.

"I know," Marguerite said aloud, to the empty hut, feeling foolish and not caring. "I know. I'm trying."

*You're sitting,* the voice said, and there was something almost wry in it, the ghost of a soldier's gallows humor. *I've been dead for weeks and I can tell the difference.*

Marguerite laughed. It came out cracked and wrong, half sob, but it was a laugh. "You don't understand. They took her. If I go to the capital, they'll take me too, and then there's no one. No one at all. The whole thing dies in a cell."

*Then it dies anyway,* the soldier said. *Slowly. While you carve pretty trinkets in the mountains and pretend you never heard me die.*

"That's not fair."

*Nothing about this is fair. I had a daughter.* The voice steadied, and Marguerite felt the weight of it settle over him like a hand on his shoulder. *Her name is Maren. She's nine. I pressed this bone into your hands because I was dying and you were the only warm thing in that alley, and I have watched you—yes, we watch, we're not proud of it—every step since. I watched you burn your carvings rather than let the Hunt find them. I watched you listen to drowned men for hours when any sane man would have fled. I watched you hold her hand across a campfire like it was the most natural thing in the world.*

"Stop."

*I watched you run,* the soldier said, gently now. *And I'm asking you to stop.*

Marguerite sank down against the hearth again. The drizzle thickened to proper rain, drumming on the roof, and in the sound he could almost hear the crowd noise of the market square, the criers, the crackle of the pyres they built for people like him.

"The spirit promised me things," he said quietly. It had marked him in the throne room; its touch still itched along his forearm like a scar forming. "When I refused, it let me go so it could hunt me at leisure. If I walk into the capital, I walk into its mouth."

*Yes.*

"And Ines—" His throat closed. He tried again. "She forged the binding. Forty years ago, young and trusting and wrong. Everything that's happened—the kings it devoured, the Order falling, the Hunt burning children in squares—it traces back to her mistake. She's spent her whole life trying to unmake it. And I was going to let it end with her in a cage."

*Were you?*

"No." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "No. I wasn't."

*Then stand up,* said the dead soldier, *and tell me what you need.*

What he needed was impossible, so he said it anyway, laying the pieces out like a carver laying out tools: the third vessel, in the ruined motherhouse of the Order, warded behind blood and worthiness. Ines, in the cells beneath the Hunt's garrison—or the palace; he didn't know, and not knowing was its own torture. Serane Voss, who had taken Ines alive and might be the only person in Vellamark with both access to prisoners and a conscience left to scratch. And the king, hollowing by the day, the coronation renewal coming with the winter solstice like a tide that wouldn't wait.

"I can't do any of it," he said. "Not alone. Not without her."

*No,* the soldier agreed. *But 'not alone' isn't the same as 'not at all.' You have two vessels and a gift the spirit itself fears enough to mark you. You have a captain who looked at a prisoner and asked questions instead of drawing blood—I've been dead, carver, but the walls of garrisons talk more than the living do. And you have me.* A pause, and when the voice came again it was softer. *You have every dead soul whose last words passed through your hands. Do you think we gave you those words so you could keep them in a box?*

Marguerite looked at the two vessels on the floor. Through the oilcloth, faint as breath on winter glass, they glowed.

"I'm afraid," he said. It was the truest thing he'd said in days.

*Good,* said the soldier. *Fear means you finally understand the size of it. Now pick up your bones, carver. Winter is coming, and the king is running out of time—and so, I think, is she.*

---

He slept, after that—not well, but truly, dreamless as a stone. When he woke, the rain had stopped and the light through the shutters was the pale gold of late morning, and the despair that had sat on his chest for four days had shifted into something else. Not hope exactly. Hope was too clean a word for it. Purpose, maybe. The difference between drowning and swimming toward shore in the dark: same water, same exhaustion, different direction.

He packed the burner's remaining food into a satchel. He wrapped the vessels carefully and slung them across his chest where they beat against his heart like a second pulse. He took up his carving knife, and then—he didn't know why, except that his hands insisted—he sat down at the hearth one last time and picked up a scrap of antler left over from some long-dead project.

Twenty minutes later he had a small figure: a woman standing, arms crossed, weight on one hip, chin lifted in an expression he knew too well. He turned it in his fingers. He didn't remember making the decision to carve her. His hands had simply done it, the way they always did, holding what his mind refused to drop.

"You'd laugh at this," he told it. "Sentimental rubbish."

He put it in his pocket, next to the small whalebone charm he still couldn't explain, and stepped out into the cold.

The road south to the capital ran past the charcoal camp a mile down, and beyond it the hills folded green and gray toward Greyford Bridge, toward the checkpoints, toward everything he'd sworn off four days ago. Somewhere ahead of him were garrison walls and a woman in a cell and a captain with kind eyes in a wanted-sketch and a king eating himself alive from the inside.

Somewhere behind him was the north, and safety, and a life half-lived.

Marguerite Coldharbour stood at the treeline for a long moment, breathing the wet pine air, listening—to the wind, to the silence, and beneath it all, faint and steady as a heartbeat in the bones against his chest, to the dead who had not given up on him yet.

"All right," he said, to the soldier, to Ines, to himself. "All right. I'm coming."

He turned south, and walked.
