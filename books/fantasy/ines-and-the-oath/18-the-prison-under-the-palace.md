## 18. The Prison Under the Palace

They took her boots first.

Ines had expected the manacles, the hood, the rough hands at her elbows—those were ritual, almost courteous in their predictability. But the boots they took with a strange deliberateness, a sergeant crouching to unlace them while she stood bound between two guards, and she understood: this was not cruelty. This was procedure. The Hunt had learned that prisoners escaped through small permissions. Bare feet on stone could not run far.

The cell was beneath the palace, which surprised her until it didn't. She had helped design its security forty years ago—or rather, she had carried the wax and ink for those who did, a young keeper's apprentice trailing her mentor through corridors that smelled of tallow and cold granite. The Order had built these cells for gifted prisoners, back when the crown wanted such people *questioned* rather than burned. The irony was not lost on her that she now occupied one.

They left her a blanket, a bucket, a candle stub that was replaced each dawn by a hand thrust through the door slot without a face attached. No window. Time moved in meals: gruel at what she guessed was morning, bread and thin soup at evening. She counted nine meals before anyone came to speak to her.

Nine meals of listening to the stone.

That was the thing about prisons built by oath-keepers—the old ones, the true ones—they remembered their makers. The walls here had been warded with spoken vows, and vows left an echo the way footprints left dust. Ines sat with her back against the granite and let the dead language hum against her spine. *We hold what must be held. We speak what must be spoken.* Words she had once believed with her whole chest. Words her mentor had taught her, smiling, while he threaded rot into the crown-binding like a worm into fruit.

She had told Marguerite none of it. Not all of it. He knew she'd forged the binding; he didn't know how deliberately it had been soured, or by whose hand, or that she had signed the witness line with a steady hand because she'd been too young and too loyal to look closely at what she was witnessing.

Nine meals gave a person time to rehearse confessions.

On the tenth day—the day she'd decided would be her last alone, because after ten days either they came for her or she started screaming at the door slot—a key turned in the lock, and Captain Serane Voss stepped inside with a lantern and two wooden cups.

No guards. That was the first strange thing. The second was that she set one of the cups down within Ines's reach before saying a word.

"Water," Serane said. "Not wine. I'm not stupid."

"No," Ines agreed. "You're not."

Serane lowered herself onto the cell's single stool with the economy of a woman who had spent years sitting wherever she could, whenever she could, and never quite relaxing. She was out of uniform—plain grey wool, no insignia—and her hair was tied back plainly, and Ines understood that this visit did not officially exist.

"You've been quiet," Serane said.

"I've been counting your mistakes."

Something flickered across the captain's face—not anger. Interest. "Such as?"

"Keeping me alive." Ines lifted the cup, drank. The water was clean, cold, palace-cold. "You had orders. Prisoners taken in aid of a fugitive go to interrogation within three days. I've read the protocols. I helped write some of them, in another life. It's been ten days, Captain. Either you're defying orders, or someone above you is very interested in why I know things."

Serane said nothing for a long moment. The lantern threw her shadow long against the ward-humming wall.

"Interrogation happened," she said finally. "A man named Corvel. You wouldn't have met him; he works quickly and quietly and he's very good at making people say things they don't mean." Her jaw tightened. "He got nothing from you because there was nothing to get. You gave him your name, your rank, your date of birth, and then you went somewhere he couldn't follow."

"I went still," Ines said. "Old keeper discipline. We trained to hold silence under questioning. It's not magic. It's just—" she almost smiled— "stubbornness with structure."

"And Corvel reported you as 'resistant but empty.' His word. Empty." Serane turned the cup in her hands. "I've read a hundred of his reports. Guilty people aren't empty. Guilty people are full of fear and lies and they leak. You're not leaking anything. Which means either you genuinely have nothing to hide—"

"—or I'm hiding something so large it doesn't fit through Corvel's questions."

"Yes." Serane looked up. "Which is it?"

And there it was. The question, laid on the floor between them like a drawn blade.

Ines set down her cup. She thought about Marguerite, somewhere north or east or hidden, carrying two bone-vessels and a guilt he hadn't deserved, running because she'd shoved him out a window and stayed behind to buy his escape with her own capture. She thought about whether he'd gotten clear. Whether he hated her. Whether he was, at this moment, deciding to flee to the coast and forget her name—which would be the sensible thing, the survivable thing, and she found she almost hoped for it.

Then she thought about the ledger.

Because that was the other reason she'd gone still under Corvel's needles-and-questions routine. Not just discipline. A gamble. A wager placed in the dark: that Serane Voss was not the woman her reputation claimed. That a captain who kept a prisoner fed and unburned past every deadline, who visited without guards and brought water instead of irons, who asked *which is it* instead of *confess*—that captain was looking for a door out of something.

"You first," Ines said.

Serane's eyebrows rose. "Excuse me?"

"A trade. You want truth from me? Give me truth first. One piece. Something real." Ines leaned forward despite the manacles' weight. "Why does the Hunt's own captain keep a traitor alive past her orders' expiration?"

For a moment Ines thought she'd overplayed it. Serane's face closed like a shutter, and the lantern flame seemed to shrink, and the cell got colder. Then the captain exhaled through her nose—a short, controlled breath, the sound of a woman managing herself—and reached into her coat.

What she drew out was not a weapon. It was a folded paper, soft at the creases, handled many times.

"My family," Serane said. "Kettlewick village. You may have heard of it. Or rather—you may have heard of what happened at Kettlewick, eleven years ago. The official account says a hedge-witch's working went wrong. A binding misfired. Nine dead, including my parents and my sister." She unfolded the paper with fingers that were perfectly steady, which somehow made it worse. "I was fourteen. I was at market when it happened. I came home to a crater where our house had been, and ash, and the smell."

Ines said nothing. There was nothing to say yet.

"The Hunt recruited me off that ash heap. Told me the gifted were a disease and I was the cure, and I believed them, because believing was easier than grieving." Serane's voice stayed flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who had told this story only to themselves for years. "I rose fast. I'm good at this work. And then, four months ago, I pulled the records on Kettlewick for a routine audit, and I found the original field report." She held the paper out. "Read it."

Ines took it. The handwriting was cramped, official, decades of bureaucratic habit pressed into every letter. She read it twice.

"It wasn't a hedge-witch," she said quietly.

"No. It was a licensed court sorcerer performing an unsanctioned binding on behalf of a treasury official who wanted a rival's land. The binding failed because he was drunk and rushed and *licensed*, and the crown buried the report and blamed a dead woman who couldn't defend herself." Serane took the paper back, folded it along its worn creases. "Magic didn't kill my family, Captain Voss. A man with a crown seal killed my family. And I have spent eleven years burning people who are, at worst, guilty of being born different."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere far above, boots crossed stone.

"Why show me this?" Ines asked. "You don't know me. For all you can prove, I'm exactly what the Hunt says I am."

"Because you're *empty*," Serane said. "Corvel couldn't crack you, and I've watched you through that slot for ten days, and do you know what I've seen? A woman doing penance arithmetic. Counting meals. Testing the walls. Not planning escape—planning *confession*. You've been rehearsing something, oath-keeper. I want to know if it's something I should help you say."

Ines felt the gamble tilt beneath her, the way ice tilts before it holds or breaks.

She could give him up. Marguerite—the carver, the gentle criminal, the man who heard the dead through whalebone and wept over widows' charms. She could trade his location for her freedom, walk out of this cell, and spend the rest of her life knowing she'd fed the last honest pair of hands in Vellamark to the fire. Or she could keep holding silence, and Corvel would come back with sharper tools, and eventually even stubbornness with structure fails.

Or she could bet everything on a grieving woman with a forged report in her pocket.

Ines chose.

"My name is Ines Maraval," she said. "Formerly Third Keeper of the Royal Order of Oath-Wrights, dissolved by decree twenty-two years ago. Twenty-six years ago, I assisted in forging the binding that ties the crown-spirit to King Aldric. And I am telling you, Captain, with the last honesty I possess: the king is not the king. The spirit we bound to protect the realm has turned. It devours him. It has devoured the kings before him, slowly, and it is close now to wearing Aldric entirely, and when it finishes, whatever sits on that throne will not be a man. It will be a hunger with a crown."

Serane had gone very still. Not disbelieving-still. *Listening*-still, the stillness of a soldier receiving a report too important to interrupt.

"Unbinding requires three vessels," Ines continued. "Bone-vessels, made by the Order, scattered when the Order fell. Your prisoner has two of them. The third is in the motherhouse ruins at Cairnlow. There is a rite. There is a cost. And there is a clock, because the Renewal is in five weeks, and at the Renewal the spirit feeds deepest."

Silence. Long enough that Ines heard her own heartbeat.

"That's a lot of treason," Serane said at last. "Spoken freely. To the captain of the Hunt."

"You showed me yours."

"One dead village and a buried report." Serane stood, and the stool scraped. "Versus the throne itself."

"Yes," Ines said. "I know how it sounds. I know what you have to weigh it against. Eleven years of doctrine. A career. Your own neck." She looked up at the woman towering over her, and something in her chest ached with recognition—this was what Marguerite must have felt, offering truth to strangers and waiting to see if it would be returned or used as a blade. "But I watched your face when you said *a man with a crown seal*. You already know the crown rots things, Captain. You've known since Kettlewick. I'm only telling you how deep the rot goes."

Serane gathered the lantern. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, not turning around.

"The soldier," she said. "Dorn Meric. He was mine. Two years under my command. They found him in an alley with his guts opened and a relic missing, and everyone assumed he'd stolen it and died for it." Her knuckles whitened on the doorframe. "He didn't steal it, did he. He was carrying it *to* someone."

"He was carrying it to a bone-carver in Vellamark," Ines said softly. "Because Dorn figured out what I've told you, and he knew the carver could hear the dead, and he was dying anyway, so he made his death into a message." She hesitated. "His last words were about the king. He loved you, Captain. He spoke of you in the bone. I heard it secondhand, but the carver—wept over it."

Serane stood motionless for the space of three breaths. When she finally spoke, her voice was level in the way that cost the most.

"If I act on what you've told me and I'm wrong, I hang beside you."

"If you don't act and I'm right," Ines said, "there won't be a kingdom left to hang anyone in."

Another pause. Then, quietly, almost to herself:

"He always said the king's eyes had gone wrong. Wouldn't stand guard at the privy chamber anymore. Requested transfer twice." She shook her head once, sharply, like a woman clearing water from her ears. "Eat your soup, oath-keeper. I'll decide what I am by tomorrow."

The lock turned. The boots faded. Ines sat alone with the humming walls and the two cups—one hers, one abandoned on the stool like a promise or a threat—and realized her hands were shaking.

She had given away everything. The Order's secrets, the rite, the vessels, Marguerite's role in all of it. If Serane walked out of that corridor and reported the conversation verbatim, Ines would die in the square within the month, and worse, Marguerite would be hunted with a precision no fugitive had ever survived.

But if she'd read the woman correctly—if grief and a buried report had done their slow work—then somewhere above her, Captain Serane Voss was standing in the dark, holding the truth like a live coal, deciding which hand to pass it to.

Ines lay back on the pallet and stared at the ceiling where the ward-words hummed, old vows from better keepers.

*We hold what must be held.*

"Hold," she whispered to the stone. "Just a little longer."

Above her, muffled by granite and distance, she heard the palace bells begin to mark the hour—and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, a sound she had hoped never to hear again: a long, low note of the crown-spirit's hunger, resonating up through the bones of the mountain like something vast turning over in its sleep.

Five weeks. Perhaps fewer.

Somewhere in the dark, Serane Voss was making her choice.
