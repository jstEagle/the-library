## 19. Bargaining with the Captain

The Hunt's headquarters in Vellamark had once been a customs house, back when the river trade was honest and the crown taxed wool instead of souls. Marguerite knew the building the way he knew most old buildings—by its bones. He could see them in his mind's eye: the oak frame beneath the whitewash, the great beams salvaged from ships, the cellar arches that had held casks of brandy and now, he suspected, held people.

He stood across the street in the grey light before dawn, watching the sentries change watch, and tried to think of himself as a man walking toward something rather than into it.

There was a difference. He needed there to be a difference.

Three days since the inn. Three days since Ines had shoved the satchel with the two vessels into his arms and turned to face the door as it came down, her knife already drawn, her voice steady as she told him *run*. He had run. He had run through the kitchen, through the yard, over a wall he still bore splinters from, and every step of it had felt like tearing something out of himself along the grain.

He'd hidden the vessels in the charcoal burner's hut, wrapped in oilcloth under the floor stones, and then he had sat in the dark listening to the oath-bone whisper its dead soldier's litany—*the king is not the king, the king is not the king*—until the words stopped sounding like horror and started sounding like instructions.

You couldn't unbind a king from a cell you weren't standing in. And Ines was in a cell. He'd confirmed it yesterday at cost: a fishwife bribed with a carved brooch worth ten times the fish, who'd seen the prisoner brought up from Greyford under guard—a woman, dark-haired, wrists bound with iron wire because rope wasn't good enough for her.

So here he was. A man with nothing left to lose except the two things he'd die before surrendering, both of them buried under a charcoal burner's cold hearth, and one thing he could offer that no one else in Vellamark could.

Proof.

He crossed the street before he could reconsider. The sentry at the door was young, chapped-lipped, bored in the way of men who had stood too many watches without anything happening. Marguerite stopped an arm's length away and put his hands where they could be seen.

"I'm here to give myself up," he said. "My name is Marguerite Coldharbour. I'm the carver your captain is looking for."

The young man blinked. Looked him up and down—the plain wool coat, the travel-worn boots, the face that half the shopkeepers on Tanner's Row would vouch for. "You're having me on."

"I'm not. There's a sketch of me posted on the fountain square. Kind eyes, I'm told." He kept his voice level, almost pleasant, because he had learned long ago that fear traveled fastest through a shaking voice. "Go fetch whoever wakes your captain. Tell her the man from the sketch walked here on his own feet, and that if she sends soldiers to arrest him instead of coming herself, he'll change his mind about what he knows."

The sentry hesitated. Marguerite watched him weigh it—the absurdity against the possibility, the punishment for waking Serane Voss over nothing against the punishment for turning away exactly the man whose likeness hung in three public squares.

"Wait here," the sentry said finally, and went inside, and did not lock the door behind him, which Marguerite chose to take as a good sign.

* * *

They made him wait in a room with no chairs, which he understood to be a message. Two hours by the church bells. He spent them cataloguing the sounds of the building—the muffled rhythm of boots overhead, a door somewhere below opening and closing, and beneath it all, faint but unmistakable to ears like his, the murmur of the dead.

The customs house had its own quiet congregation. Men who had died in the cells, or in the yard, or in the market square with their names read aloud first. Their voices were thin, worn smooth like sea glass, and they said very little anymore. Mostly they repeated the last things they'd heard. One of them, somewhere below his feet, murmured the words of a confession over and over in a voice cracked with age, and Marguerite pressed his palms against his thighs and did not listen, because listening down here, in this building, felt like giving something away.

When the door opened at last, it was not soldiers who came through it. It was Serane Voss alone, and she closed the door behind her.

Up close she looked worse than the sketch suggested—not older, exactly, but more carried. There were shadows under her eyes the color of iron filings, and her braid had come loose on one side, though her uniform was immaculate. She looked at him the way she must have looked at ledgers: checking the sum against the entry.

"Marguerite Coldharbour," she said. "Carver of Tanner's Row. Ten years in Vellamark, no incidents, no reports, no complaints. Beloved by widows." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Do you know how that reads, in my files? *Beloved by widows.* It reads like a man who has found the perfect cover, or a man who genuinely spends his life easing grief. I've never been able to decide which."

"Both," Marguerite said. "It's usually both."

Something flickered in her face—recognition, maybe, of a fellow professional. Then it hardened. "You killed Corporal Dennet."

"No."

"His body was found in the alley off Cooper's Lane with your carving knife's width of wound in him, and the oath-bone missing from his kit, and you vanished the same week the reliquary keeper described a man matching you buying passage to questions about old Order relics."

"I found him dying," Marguerite said. "I knelt in that alley and held him while the life went out of him, and I heard every word he said, Captain, because that is my crime. Not murder. Hearing. If I'd wanted him dead I'd have walked past, the way any sensible criminal walks past a dying soldier of the Hunt."

Serane studied him for a long moment. "And what did he say? Before he died?"

"He said the king is not the king."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere above them a chair scraped; the building went on being a building. But between the two of them, something shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond before it decides whether to hold.

"You're lying," she said, but her voice had changed register. It was flatter now. Careful.

"Am I?" Marguerite reached slowly—she watched his hands the whole way—into his coat, and drew out the oath-bone.

He had wrapped it in linen, and even so, holding it made his teeth ache. It was a long bone, yellowed, drilled at both ends with the socket-holes of old fittings, and it hummed against his palm like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. Layered voices moved inside it. He had learned to keep them at arm's length; now, deliberately, he let one surface.

"Listen," he said, and spoke the words the soldier had spoken, in the cadence the soldier had used, the last breath of him still folded into the sound: "*Captain—if I don't make the ferry—it's in the bone—the king is not the king—tell her the binding's gone rotten—*"

Serane had gone very still. "Those are Dennet's exact words. The ferryman reported them. They're in my report." Her eyes fixed on the bone. "What is that?"

"This is an oath-bone. One of nine forged by the royal Order of Keepers, forty years ago, when the crown was bound to a protective spirit. You've heard the stories. The king's guardian. The reason Aldric's grandfather survived the plague years and the border war." He turned the bone slowly in his fingers. "But bindings rot, Captain. And this spirit has soured. It doesn't protect the kings anymore. It devours them—from inside. It wears them. And King Aldric is nearly worn through."

"That's sedition," she said automatically. "That's—" She stopped. He watched her stop, watched the trained reflex collide with something she'd been carrying privately for weeks. He thought of chapter and ledger, of evidence ordered buried. He didn't know about that yet, but he knew the look of a person whose faith had begun to itch.

"Is it?" he said gently. "Then answer me one question, Captain, and I'll let you call it sedition with a clean heart. When you've stood close to the king—at reviews, at ceremonies—have you ever seen him claw at his crown? Just for a moment, when he thinks no one watches. Like a man trying to take off a ring that's grown into the finger."

Her face told him everything before her discipline erased it.

"I've seen it," she said quietly. "Twice. I filed it as illness."

"It isn't illness. Illness doesn't speak through its host. Illness doesn't promise people their heart's desire in a voice that comes out of someone else's mouth." He set the oath-bone down on the floor between them, wrapped in its linen, and stepped back from it with his empty hands open. "I can prove the rest. I have proof. But proof does you no good locked in a cell, and it does the kingdom no good at all, because there is exactly one person in Vellamark who can undo this binding—and your Hunt arrested her three days ago."

"Ines Maravel," Serane said.

The name landed between them. Marguerite hadn't said it. His surprise must have shown, because something dry moved at the corner of her mouth.

"She was interrogated yesterday. By me. She gave me nothing but her name and rank and a great deal of insolence." Serane crouched, not touching the bone, studying it the way she'd study a suspicious seal. "She claims the Order was corrupt. That the binding was a leash disguised as a blessing. She says she helped forge it when she was nineteen years old and has spent fifteen years trying to undo it."

"She's telling the truth."

"They all tell the truth," Serane said. "That's what makes this work unbearable. Every gifted soul I've burned or hanged or shipped north told me the truth, eventually, in the end, when there was nothing left to protect. The truth is cheap at the end. What I want to know is whether it's true at the beginning."

"Test it, then." Marguerite met her eyes. "Give me one night with the king. One hour, even. Let me stand in front of Aldric and do what I do. If I'm lying, you'll know within minutes—you'll have caught the most wanted man in Vellamark attempting sorcery against the king's person, and you can burn me in the square with everyone's blessing. Your career survives. Your conscience survives. But if I'm right—if the king begs you, lucid, for freedom—then you'll have to decide what kind of officer you are."

"And why would I gamble my commission on a carver's story?"

"Because you already have." He nodded at the bone on the floor. "You didn't call your guards when you read my name. You came alone. You closed the door. Whatever your superiors have told you to bury, Captain, you dug it up and kept it. You're already halfway to traitor. I'm offering you the chance to be a patriot instead."

The words hung there. He saw her jaw tighten, saw the calculation move behind her eyes like water finding its level—and underneath the calculation, faint but real, the grief. He had heard enough grief in ten years of bones to know its shape from the outside too. Someone she'd lost. Something magic had done to her family, if the rumors that reached even Tanner's Row were true. She hunted the gifted because she believed the gift was a knife, and here was a knife asking to be trusted.

"One night," she said at last.

Marguerite's breath went out of him.

"Not for you." She rose, straightening her coat with a small precise motion, and he understood that the motion was how she reassembled herself. "For the kingdom. If you're wrong, you hang at dawn and I sleep fine. If you're right—" She looked at the wrapped bone a moment longer. "If you're right, then I've been burning innocent people for two years while a parasite ate my king, and I will need something to do with that knowledge. Getting the truth in front of Aldric before the Renewal is something to do with it."

"The Renewal," Marguerite said. "Nine days."

"Eight now." She moved to the door, then paused with her hand on the latch. "Coldharbour. If this is some scheme to reach the prisoner—"

"It is," Marguerite said. "Entirely. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. I want to see her, and I want her free, and I want the king unbound, and I suspect those three wants are the same want wearing different coats. But I'm offering you the only currency I have, and it's real. Test it."

A long pause. Then, unexpectedly: "Dennet was a good boy. Young. He wrote letters home to his sister every week, terrible letters, full of weather." Her hand tightened on the latch. "If your bone really holds his last words, then something of him survived the alley, and I find I want that to mean something other than horror."

"It means what you make it mean," Marguerite said. "That's all any of the dead ask."

She opened the door. Two guards waited in the corridor, and at her nod they took Marguerite's arms—not roughly, but firmly, the grip of men following orders they didn't understand.

"Search him," Serane said. "Take his tools, his knife, everything sharp. Put him in the east cell—the dry one." She met his eyes one last time, and in them he saw the question she wouldn't ask aloud: *what if you're right, and what does that make me?*

"One night, Coldharbour," she said. "At dawn, I bring you to the palace. At dawn tomorrow, we learn what kind of kingdom this is."

The door closed. The locks turned, three of them, deep and deliberate.

Marguerite sat down on the cell's narrow bench and let his hands shake now that no one could see. Below his feet, the old dead murmured their worn-smooth fragments, and somewhere deeper, behind stone and iron, a living woman sat in the dark not knowing that dawn was bringing her a chance—or bringing her captor a carver with kind eyes and a liar's nerve.

He pressed his palms flat against the wall. The stone was cold, and honest, and said nothing at all.

One night. Eight days until the Renewal.

He began, quietly, to rehearse what he would say to a king.
