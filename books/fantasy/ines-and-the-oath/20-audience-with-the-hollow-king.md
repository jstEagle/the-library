## 20. Audience with the Hollow King

They came for him an hour before dawn, when the palace corridors were lit by tallow candles and the air smelled of cold stone and old incense.

Marguerite had not slept. He had given his word to Serane at dusk—*one night*, she'd said, and her eyes had said *and if you waste it, I will burn you myself*—and now two guards in plain livery walked him down stair after stair, deeper into the palace than he had imagined it went. His wrists were bound in front of him with soft cord, almost courteous. Almost kind.

The oath-bone was gone from him. They had taken it at the gate, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax, and its absence ached like a pulled tooth. Ten years he had carried voices in his pockets, in his sleeves, in the quiet of his own skull, and only now, walking into the dark beneath the crown's seat, did he understand how much of his silence had been company.

*You are doing this for Ines,* he told himself. *For the vessels. For the king.*

But that was only half true, and he was tired of lying to himself in the dark. The other half was simpler and worse: the dying soldier's voice still lived somewhere behind his sternum, saying *the king is not the king*, and Marguerite had never once, in all his careful criminal years, walked away from a voice that needed hearing.

The stairs ended at a door banded in iron so old it had gone green. One guard knocked. A voice on the other side—dry, papery, unremarkable—said, "Send him alone."

The cord was cut from his wrists. The door opened on darkness and the smell of beeswax and something else beneath it, something sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting in a locked room.

He went in.

* * *

The chamber had been a chapel once. He could read it in the bones of the place: the arched windows bricked over, the altar stripped and pushed against a wall, the faint ghost-lines on the floor where pews had stood for a century before someone decided a king should not pray where his people could see him do it. Now there was a bed, vast and curtained, and a writing desk buried under papers no one had moved in months, and a fire burning too hot in a hearth built for a colder faith.

King Aldric sat in a chair beside the fire, dressed in a plain wool robe, his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a physician.

He looked smaller than his coinage. On the silver marks stamped in Vellamark, Aldric's face was broad and certain, jaw like a cliff, eyes fixed on some horizon of permanent prosperity. The man in the chair was thin in the way of things being slowly emptied. His hair had gone white at the temples though he could not have been past fifty. His hands were clean, immaculately kept, and they trembled—not constantly, but in small storms that rose and passed like weather.

"Bone-carver," the king said. His voice was level. Almost warm. "Serane Voss tells me you turned yourself in to reach me. That you gave up your freedom, your relics, and very likely your life, for one night in my company." He smiled, and it was a good smile, worn smooth by decades of use. "I confess I don't understand it. Most men run from me lately."

"They should," Marguerite said.

The smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the first wrong thing—the smile not moving, not flickering, held like a mask held by a hand behind the face. "You're braver than my council. Or stupider. I've found it difficult to tell the difference for some years now."

"I've come to ask you questions," Marguerite said. "About the binding."

"Ask, then." The king gestured with one trembling hand at the chair opposite. "Sit. It's been an age since anyone asked me anything except how I slept."

Marguerite sat. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, the palace went about its midnight business—a door, footsteps, nothing.

"The Order bound a spirit to your crown," Marguerite said. "Forty years ago. To protect the realm."

"Yes."

"It has soured."

"Yes," the king said again, in the same pleasant tone, and Marguerite felt the hair rise on his arms, because the man was agreeing to his own devouring the way one agrees that the weather is fine.

"And you want it removed."

Something changed. It was small—a catch in the breathing, a stillness in the trembling hands—and then King Aldric looked up, and his eyes were different. Not different in color or shape. Different in the way a house is different when the lamps are lit inside versus when the windows only reflect the street. For the first time since Marguerite had entered, someone was looking out of that face.

"You came," the king whispered. "Oh, thank every saint dead and nameless—you actually came."

Marguerite's mouth went dry. "Your Majesty—"

"Don't." The word tore. Aldric leaned forward, gripping the arms of his chair, and the tremors in his hands became shaking, full and helpless. "Don't kneel, don't title me, don't waste a second of whatever you bought with your night on ceremony. How long do we have? How long does this last? When I'm—when it lets me up for air, it's minutes. Sometimes less. Tell me what you need."

"I need—" Marguerite fumbled. He had rehearsed this. He had lain awake rehearsing it, the questions in order, the evidence laid out like wares on a stall. "The oath-bone. The soldier who carried it. Do you know how he died?"

"Aldric's man. Corven." The king said the name like a prayer. "He found something. He was trying to reach the old Order records—he got as far as Greyford before they ran him down. I felt it when he died." His face twisted. "That's the worst of it, carver. I feel everything it does. Every person it turns away from my door. Every order given in my voice that I never gave. I am a passenger in my own skull, and it makes me watch."

"How long?" Marguerite asked quietly.

"Four years since I noticed the gaps. Two since I understood them. This year—" Aldric laughed, and it was a horrible sound, wet and short. "This year I woke on the renewal dais with my mouth tasting of iron and forty thousand people cheering, and I could not remember the words of my own oath. I knew the words. I've sworn them twenty times. But *I* hadn't said them. Something had said them wearing me." He gripped Marguerite's sleeve, and his strength was sudden and desperate. "Can you unbind it? Voss says you can. She says you carry the bone and you hear the dead—is that true? Is any of it true?"

"It's true," Marguerite said. "And yes. There's a rite. We have the vessels. Two of three."

"Then take the third and do it. Tonight. Now. I'll sign whatever—"

"It needs the Renewal," Marguerite said gently. "The moment the oath is re-sworn. It's the spirit's greatest hold on you—and its greatest exposure. We have until winter coronation eve. Eleven days."

Aldric stared at him. And Marguerite watched hope arrive in the man's face like light arriving in a room, slow and total, and watched what it cost him, because hope was clearly a thing the king had trained himself out of the way one trains oneself out of crying.

"Eleven days," Aldric repeated. "I can last eleven days. I've lasted—" He stopped. His grip on Marguerite's sleeve tightened. "Listen to me. There are things you must know before it comes back. The third vessel—it's at the motherhouse, yes? The ruins in the Greymarsh?"

"You know where it is?"

"I know what it cost. Ines—" The king's voice caught. "Ines Vale is alive? Voss wouldn't say."

"She's alive. She's captured. I traded myself for a night with you; she doesn't know I've done it."

"She'll be furious," the king said, and smiled—a real smile this time, crooked and human and gone in a heartbeat. "She was always furious on my behalf. My father's keeper, then mine. The best of them. If she's come back for this, then—" He stopped again. His head tilted, sharply, like a man hearing a call from another room.

"No," he breathed. "No, not yet, I need more time—"

"Your Majesty?"

Aldric's hand released Marguerite's sleeve. The king sat back. And Marguerite saw it happen—saw the light go out of the windows of the face, saw the shoulders settle, the hands fold, the pleasant mask rise like water filling a mold—and when Aldric looked up again, he was smiling the same good smile as before, and the fire seemed suddenly much colder.

"Forgive me," the king said lightly. "I drift. Old men drift." He rose, unhurried, and walked to the desk, trailing one finger along its dusty edge. "Where were we? Ah. The binding. You wanted to know whether I wished it removed."

Marguerite did not move. Every instinct he owned was screaming at once, the way the bones screamed, the way the drowned dead had screamed in the flooded crypt—a chorus of animal certainty with no words in it.

"I never asked Your Majesty's wishes," he said carefully. "I stated them."

"So you did." Aldric turned. The firelight caught his face, and for just a moment the shadow under his cheekbones fell wrong, fell like a shadow cast by a different light source than the fire, and Marguerite's skin crawled. "Let us speak plainly, then, bone-carver, since plainness seems to be your trade. You have two vessels. You intend to find a third. You intend, at the Renewal, before the assembled realm, to pour me out like dishwater and call it mercy."

"That is the intent."

"Mm." The king examined his immaculate fingernails. "And the woman. Ines Vale. You'd free her for it, of course. Voss has her in the cells under the east wing. You turned yourself in for one night with me hoping to buy her freedom too—I admire that, by the way. Sentiment in a craftsman is rare. Usually they carve what's paid for."

The cold went through Marguerite like a blade finding the gap in armor. "How do you—"

"I know what you dreamed last night," the king said pleasantly. "Shall I tell you? No—of course not. Vulgar. But I could. I know the shape of your gift better than you do, Marguerite Coldharbour. I have worn four kings. I have listened to their hearts the way you listen to your little bones. Do you imagine there is anything in you I haven't heard?"

* * *

The spirit did not change the king's posture. That was the horror of it. It stood where Aldric stood, folded its borrowed hands where Aldric folded them, and spoke through a mouth that had learned, over forty years, to be a king's mouth. Only the eyes were wrong. Only the patience was wrong.

"Here is what puzzles me," it said, conversational, circling toward the fire. "You could walk away. Consider it honestly. You are a criminal in a kingdom that burns your kind; you have evaded justice for ten years through diligence and small virtues. You owe this crown nothing. The soldier who gave you the bone was a stranger. The woman is—what, precisely? You've known her a fortnight. Shared one fire. Fled together through one checkpoint. And yet here you stand, having sold yourself to the Hunt for a single hour in this room." It clicked its tongue softly. "Love makes such wasteful arithmetic of people."

"You know nothing about it."

"I know everything about it." The spirit turned, and smiled with Aldric's mouth, and the smile was the worst thing yet because it was gentle. "That is rather my point. Shall I demonstrate? Your mother died of the lung-rot when you were nineteen, in a rented room that smelled of tallow, and her last word was your name, and you weren't there. You were selling a charm to a merchant's wife two streets away, haggling over three coppers, while she died saying *Marguerite*. You have carved her face eleven times since. You keep none of them. You give them away, because keeping one would mean admitting you're sorry, and you have built ten years of quiet competence on top of that apology like planks over a well."

Marguerite's breath had stopped somewhere in the middle of it. He made himself breathe.

"There," the spirit said softly. "Do you see? I don't guess, carver. I listen. Everything you have ever poured into bone, every voice you've carried, every secret you've sold back to grieving widows at fair prices—all of it passes through hands that touch the same world I live in. The dead are my countrymen. You've been corresponding with my kingdom for a decade and never knew it." It stepped closer. The fire threw Aldric's shadow long across the floor, and the shadow bent the wrong way. "So let me make you an offer, since you came all this way."

"No."

"You haven't heard it."

"I've heard enough."

"The woman," the spirit said. "Ines Vale. You love her. Don't flinch; it's not an accusation, it's simply a fact, the way her pulse is a fact, the way yours is. You love her clumsily and too fast and you haven't said it because you're a coward about living things—you carve the dead because the dead can't leave." The king's head tilted. "Give me the vessels. Give me the rite, torn up, forgotten. Walk north with her tonight—Voss will release you both; I'll see to it; I wear this captain's superiors like gloves. Open a shop somewhere green. Carve whatever you like. No Hunt. No hiding. No more listening to strangers' dead for coin while your own hands shake with loneliness." The spirit paused, savoring. "And she will stay. That's the part you don't believe, isn't it? That's the splinter in you—that everyone leaves, that she'll finish the quest and be restored and ride back to her Order and you'll be a footnote in her rehabilitation. I can promise you otherwise. I can make it otherwise. I have worn kings, carver. Do you think holding one woman's heart is beyond me?"

The silence stretched. The fire cracked. Marguerite discovered he was gripping the back of the chair hard enough to hurt his fingers, and made himself let go, one finger at a time.

Because here was the thing. Here was the rotten, unspeakable thing, the thing he would carry to his grave even if the grave came tonight: for one full heartbeat—one—he had wanted it. Not the manipulation of it, not the spirit's greasy promise, but the picture itself. The shop. The green country. Ines across a workbench, arguing about grain direction, alive and staying. It had risen in him whole and warm before his mind could brand it as counterfeit, and the spirit had seen it, of course, had tasted it off the air like smoke.

"That's how you feed," Marguerite said. His voice came out rough. "Isn't it. Not fear. Not worship. Wanting. You wear kings because kings are wanting machines—everything they touch turns to appetite. And you sit inside the hunger and eat."

The spirit regarded him for a long moment. Then it laughed—Aldric's laugh, rich and kingly, a laugh that had charmed councils and courted queens—and the laughter cut off mid-note like a snapped string.

"Clever," it said. "Voss said you were clever. Cleverness won't save the woman, though. She sits in a cell eleven floors above us, and her trial is scheduled for the day after the Renewal, and if the rite fails—if you falter, if you die, if you get clever at the wrong moment—she hangs as a traitor to a crown I intend to keep wearing for a very long time. So weigh your cleverness carefully, Marguerite Coldharbour. I have given you my offer. You have until the Renewal to accept it. After that, I stop being generous."

It sat back down in the chair. Folded the hands. Became, in the space of a breath, a tired middle-aged king beside a fire.

"Guards," Aldric said mildly, to the door. "The carver is finished. See him back to his cell."

The door opened. Torchlight spilled in. Marguerite walked toward it on legs that felt borrowed, and at the threshold he stopped, because there was one question left and he would hate himself forever if he didn't spend it.

"Your Majesty," he said, without turning. "When you begged me—those few minutes. Was that real?"

Behind him, the silence lasted three heartbeats. Four.

"He is always real," the spirit said, and the voice was neither Aldric's nor the pleasant mask but something older, layered, many-mouthed, the sound of the oath-bone given a throat. "That is what none of your Order ever understood. I do not replace the men I wear. I *keep* them. Every king I have ever devoured is still in here, carver, still watching, still begging. You aren't saving one man at the Renewal." The torchlight guttered. "You're deciding what to do with four."

The door closed behind him. And as the guards led him down into the dark, Marguerite finally understood what the dying soldier had felt in his last moments, pressing the bone into a stranger's hands: not duty. Dread. The specific dread of a man who has learned exactly what sits on the throne, and knows that knowing it changes nothing unless someone is brave enough, and foolish enough, to do the unbearable thing about it.

Eleven days. One vessel. One rite. And a cell upstairs where a woman he loved was counting hours she didn't know were numbered.

He had come for answers. He left with a war.
