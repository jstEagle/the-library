## 21. The Spirit's Offer

The guards did not take me back to a cell.

They took me up.

I had expected stone and darkness, the cold arithmetic of the prison under the palace where Ines sat somewhere below my feet. Instead the corridor climbed, torchlight giving way to grey morning through narrow windows, and at the end of it stood a door I had only ever seen in other people's memories—the carved recollections of courtiers whose trinkets I had made, the dying whispers of old soldiers who had once guarded it.

The king's private chapel.

The guards stopped at the threshold. One of them looked at me with something that was not quite pity and not quite fear, and said, "His Majesty will see you now," in the flat voice of a man reciting words he did not understand.

The door opened on its own weight.

Inside, the chapel was small and beautiful and wrong. Sunlight came through glass painted with saints whose faces had been replaced over the centuries with the faces of kings—Aldric's face on four of them already, though he had reigned only nine years. Candles burned in ranks along the walls, hundreds of them, and not one guttered. The air was perfectly still, as if the room were holding its breath.

And there, kneeling at the altar rail with his back to me, was King Aldric Vane.

He looked smaller than his coinage. A broad-shouldered man gone soft at the edges, grey threading his hair, dressed not in ceremony but in a plain wool robe belted with rope. He might have been any penitent lord at any country shrine. For a long moment he did not move, and I stood in the doorway like a fool, my wrists still bound before me with silk cord—the courtesy of a palace that had decided I was more useful unbruised—and waited.

Then he turned, and I saw his eyes.

One eye was Aldric's: tired, human, wet with something that had been held back for years. The other was not. It was the same brown, the same shape, but it *looked* differently—the way a window looks different when something stands behind the curtain. It focused on me with an attention that had nothing sleepy or sorrowful in it, an attention like a hook.

"Marguerite Coldharbour," it said, using the king's mouth. "Bone-carver of Vellamark. The man who hears the dead." The mouth smiled, and it was Aldric's smile, warm and crooked, the smile from the coins, and that was the worst thing yet—to see a face I knew from a hundred pockets of change wearing an expression its owner could not have chosen. "You turned yourself in. For her. How wonderfully stupid."

"I want to see Ines."

"You want many things. Sit." The king gestured to a pew, and my legs obeyed before my pride could object. The spirit watched me settle with what I can only describe as appetite. "You have been listening to bones since you were a boy. Do you know what that makes you, little carver? Not a criminal. Not a witch. A *door*. And doors are so rarely thanked for what walks through them."

"The soldier said the king is not the king," I said. "I'm looking at proof."

"Aldric is here." Something flickered across the human eye—a spasm, a drowning man's hand breaking water. "Sleeping. Dreaming. He dreams of his coronation, over and over. It is kinder than waking would be." The spirit tilted the king's head, studying me the way I studied bone before the knife touched it. "You felt him, didn't you? Last night, in your cell. You put your hands against the wall and you listened down through the stone, and you heard him weeping."

I said nothing. My silence confirmed it, and the spirit laughed—a sound like coins spilled on marble.

"Here is what I admire about you, Coldharbour. You have spent ten years pretending to be a carpenter of trinkets, and every day you have opened yourself to the voices of the dead anyway, because you cannot bear not to. You are the least dishonest liar I have ever worn a face at." It rose. The king's body moved stiffly, joints protesting, and I understood suddenly how much effort the wearing cost—that beneath the stillness of the candles and the perfect composure, this thing was *laboring* inside a body that did not fit it. "So I will be equally honest with you. I know why you came. You came to trade yourself for the oath-keeper. Noble. Predictable. Unnecessary."

"Serane gave me until dawn—"

"Serane Voss is a soldier playing at politics, and she has already lost control of this board, though she doesn't know it. No." The spirit walked slowly down the aisle between the pews, and I pressed back into the wood without meaning to. "I am offering you something better than a trade. I am offering you everything."

* * *

It did not touch me. That was the first mercy, or the first cruelty—it never laid a finger on the king's skin against mine. It simply leaned close, close enough that I could smell candlewax and underneath it something older, dry as a crypt, and it said:

"Look."

And the chapel went away.

I was standing in my workshop in Vellamark. Morning light through the south window, dust turning gold above the bench, the smell of whalebone and linseed oil. My hands—my hands, scarred and steady—were carving, and the piece under the knife was good, the best thing I'd ever made, a charm shaped like a wing mid-beat.

Behind me, someone hummed.

I knew the voice before I turned. Of course I knew it. Ines stood in my doorway with snow melting in her dark hair, wearing no uniform, no broken Order's colors, just a traveler's coat and an expression I had seen only in fragments by the fire on the road—an expression with all the guilt burned out of it, leaving warmth. She was carrying bread. She set it on my bench among the shavings and kissed my cheek and said, "You were up all night again," in the tone of a woman who intended to do something about that.

"This isn't real," I said.

The vision ignored me. Time lurched forward the way dreams lurch, and it was summer, and we were walking the sea-cliffs north of Greyford, and her hand fit in mine as if the fit had been measured. Then it was winter, and there was a small stone house with smoke rising, and she was arguing with me about where to hang a shelf, laughing, alive, *unburdened*—no fallen Order, no name to clear, no mentor's betrayal sitting in her chest like swallowed glass. Then years. Many years. Her hair going grey at the temples the way mine already was. Her hands learning my craft until she could carve a passable wren. An old woman and an old man on a bench outside the shop, watching the market, her head on my shoulder, and the whole street knowing them, greeting them, *us*, plain and public and safe—

"Do you see?" The voice came from everywhere, from the painted saints, from the dust, from my own mouth. "This costs you nothing. Walk away. Take your vessels—keep them, bury them, I don't care; I will take them from you later regardless, but you needn't know that part. Go home. Open your shop. She will find you, or you will find her, and none of it will have been paid for. No drowned dead clawing at your mind. No rite. No burning. Just this."

The vision held. The old Ines on the bench turned and looked at me with eyes full of forty years of love, and said, "Come inside, love. It's getting cold."

And God forgive me—I wanted it. I wanted it with a hunger that felt like illness. Every lonely night behind my shuttered windows, every coin taken with practiced guilt, every year of being beloved and unknown at once—all of it reached toward that bench like a starving man reaches toward bread.

That was how I knew it was a lie. Not because the details were wrong—they were perfect, heartbreakingly perfect—but because I had heard enough of the dead to know what longing sounds like when it comes from inside a person, and this came from outside. This was bait on a hook, dressed in the shape of my own wanting.

"No," I said.

The vision held a moment longer. Old Ines smiled at me, patient, certain I would come inside.

"You're not her," I said, and my voice cracked, and I hated that it cracked. "She'd never say it's getting cold. She'd say the cold doesn't matter if the company's right. She told me that at Greyford Bridge, when my fingers were blue. You don't know the small things. You only know the big ones, the ones I've thought about too loudly. You're reading me, not remembering her. And she—" I swallowed. "She wouldn't want this version either. She'd rather earn her life than be handed a counterfeit."

* * *

The chapel snapped back around me like a trap closing. Candles. Saints. The still air.

The spirit regarded me through the king's mismatched eyes, and for a moment—just a moment—the human eye and the other one agreed on an expression, and it was disappointment.

"They always refuse the first time," it said. "Did you know that? In forty years, every gifted one I have shown the door has refused it first. Pride, mostly. Or love, which is pride wearing better clothes." It began to walk again, slow, the king's slippers whispering on stone. "But they come back to me, Coldharbour. In their sleep. In their grief. When the world grinds them small enough. The offer does not expire. It ripens."

"It's not an offer. It's a leash with a longer rope."

"Everything is a leash. The question is only who holds it." The spirit stopped before me, close, and the king's breath stirred the hair at my temple. "Let me tell you what happens instead, then, since you prefer truth. Tomorrow night is the Renewal. The king will kneel before the realm and swear his oath, and I will drink that oath the way I have drunk it nine times before, and I will grow, and he will grow smaller, and within the year there will be no Aldric left to dream. And you—" the hook-attention fastened on me, "—you will spend that year running. With your two vessels and your broken oath-keeper and whatever scraps of hope Serane Voss lets you carry. And I will hunt you. Slowly. I am very good at slow."

"Then why let me leave?"

"Because," the spirit said, and here it did something terrible: it smiled with genuine pleasure, the king's warm smile, "hunted things run toward their own destruction. Your third vessel is in the motherhouse ruins, is it not? Behind the blood-ward. You'll go there. You can't help yourselves. And the motherhouse ground is *mine*—consecrated to the binding, soaked in forty years of oath. If you survive the ward, you will walk out carrying the last piece of my cage directly to my doorstep, in time for my feast." It straightened. "I don't need to catch you, carver. I only need to let you run true."

My stomach dropped through the floor of the world. Because it was right. That was the obscene thing—we had planned exactly that route, Ines and I, huddled over stolen maps in a cell visitation room, and every step of our clever path led, at the end, precisely where the spirit wanted us.

Unless.

"Then kill me now," I said. "If I'm so useful to your plan, blind me. Burn my hands. Take the gift that lets me hear the vessels. You're wearing a king; you can order anything."

The spirit was quiet for a long moment. The candles did not flicker. Somewhere far off in the palace, a bell rang the hour, muffled and ordinary, absurdly human.

"No," it said finally, and there was something almost like respect in it. "Because you might be lying. Because there is a version of tomorrow where you stand at my altar and pour your ghosts into my cage, and I want to watch you try. Hope makes better theater than despair." It lifted the king's hand—two fingers, extended, the gesture of a blessing—and before I could flinch, touched them to my forehead.

Cold went in. Not pain—cold, a point of frost sinking through skin and skull and settling somewhere behind my eyes, and I heard myself make a sound I had never made before, and the painted saints blurred, and for one instant I heard—not the dead, not the spirit, but *everything*, every voice I had ever channeled, layered and screaming and singing, all of it compressed into a single syllable of sound—

Then it was done. The fingers withdrew. The chapel was warm again, ordinary again, except for the frost-point behind my eyes, which did not melt.

"There," the spirit said softly. "Now I will always know where you are. Now, when you lie awake at night wondering whether the dead are speaking or whether it is only you, you will wonder if it is me instead. That is the mark, Coldharbour. Not a brand. A *doubt*. I plant it in hunters' minds and it grows better than any wound." The king stepped back, and the human eye glistened, and I would have sworn on any bone that for one heartbeat Aldric himself looked out of it—a man buried alive in his own face, mouthing something soundless. *Run,* the shape of the lips said. Or perhaps *sorry.* Perhaps both.

"Guards," the spirit called, in the king's clear carrying voice, and the doors opened. "See the carver to the west postern. He is released, per Captain Voss's bargain. And gentlemen—" the mismatched eyes found mine one last time, "—tell the captain His Majesty looks forward to seeing her at the Renewal."

* * *

They walked me down through corridors going gold with late afternoon, past servants who did not look at me, past windows where the city spread out below—Vellamark in winter, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, the cathedral dome already scaffolded with garlands for tomorrow's ceremony. My wrists were untied at the postern gate. A guard muttered that my effects had been returned to Captain Voss, that I should collect them at the guardhouse, that I should count myself fortunate.

Fortunate. I stood in the gateway with the frost-point aching behind my eyes and laughed until a passing sergeant gave me a wide berth.

Ines was waiting three streets away, in the shadow of a dyer's awning, exactly where we had agreed she would wait if I walked out—which neither of us had truly believed I would. When she saw me she crossed the distance in six strides and gripped my arms hard enough to bruise, searching my face, my hands, my eyes.

"You're free," she said. "What did it cost?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's what frightens me."

I told her all of it on the way to the safehouse—the visions, the offer, the refusal, the mark. I told her about the old woman on the bench, and she went very still when I described her, and I did not ask what she was thinking, because I already knew: she was imagining herself at forty years, and wondering which of them the vision had flattered.

When I got to the part about the motherhouse, about the spirit letting us run true, she stopped walking altogether.

"It's lying," she said. "Or bluffing. Spirits of the crown-rite can't touch the motherhouse ward—it was built against them."

"Can't they?" I touched the place behind my eyes. "Ines, it knew about Greyford. It knew what you said to me at the bridge. It knew things I haven't told anyone, maybe things I haven't told *myself*. Whatever rules used to bind it, the binding is souring. The cage is rusting through. We have three days, and every road we can take leads where it wants us."

"Then we take no road it expects." She was already moving again, faster, thinking aloud the way she did when fear sharpened her instead of dulling her. "Not the motherhouse. Not yet. There's another way to open a blood-ward—there has to be—the Order kept contingencies—"

"And if there isn't?"

She didn't answer. Above us, bells began to ring vespers, and the sound rolled down over the city like a countdown. One day of preparation. One night. Then the Renewal, and a king kneeling before his realm with a devourer smiling through his teeth.

Behind my eyes, faint as a splinter of ice, the mark pulsed once—as if the thing that placed it had felt me think of tomorrow, and was pleased.

I followed Ines into the gathering dark, hunted and marked and free, running true.
