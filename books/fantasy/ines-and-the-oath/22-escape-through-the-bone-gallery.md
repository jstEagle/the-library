## 22. Escape Through the Bone Gallery

The key came wrapped in a rag of altar linen, tucked beneath a loose stone in the third alcove of the pilgrims' cloister, exactly where Serane's note had said it would be.

*Ossuary gate. Third bell. Bring nothing that burns.*

Marguerite read it twice by the light of a shuttered lantern, then fed the note to his own breath and let the ash sift between his fingers. *Bring nothing that burns.* As if he carried anything else. As if he were not, at this moment, a man made almost entirely of things that burned—warrants, sketches, a name shouted in three market towns.

He pressed his palm flat against the cold cloister wall and listened.

The palace dead were loud tonight. He'd felt them since the moment he passed under the outer gate in a relic-bearer's gray hood, a crate of votive bones on his shoulder and a forged chit sweating against his ribs. The palace sat on its dead like a man sitting on a chest to keep it shut. Beneath the flagstones, beneath the cellars, beneath even the black cells where they kept people like Ines—there they were, thousands of them, murmuring in the dark of the ossuary galleries where the old kings' bones had been stacked when the crypts filled up and no one wanted to dig anymore.

They knew he was here. The dead always knew.

*Carver,* they whispered, in voices like wind through rib slats. *Carver, carver, come down, come down.*

"Not yet," he breathed, and shouldered the crate, and walked toward the third bell.

* * *

Serane Voss met him at the ossuary gate with a torch in one hand and her captain's baton in the other, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Torchlight moved across her face and made her look older than the sketch on the warrants—the sketch with kind eyes, which she had drawn herself, or ordered drawn, and which had hung in every square from here to Greyford Bridge.

"You came," she said.

"You left the key."

"I left a test." She turned, boots ringing on stone. "If you'd sold it, if you'd run, I'd have slept better. Follow me, and keep your voice down. The night guard rotates at third bell. We have the length of one rotation."

"How long is that?"

"Long enough if you don't ask questions."

She led him down a stair that narrowed as it descended, the walls closing in until his shoulders brushed both sides at once. The air changed first—cooler, then not cooler but *thicker*, the particular density of air that has been breathed by the dead for centuries. Then the smell: dust and calcium and something older underneath, sweetish, like a chapel after rain.

The gallery opened around him all at once.

Bones. Bones from floor to vaulted ceiling, arranged with a terrible loving care—femurs fanned into arches, skulls mortared into pillars, vertebrae strung like garlands along the cornice. Ten generations of palace dead, exhumed to make room for newer kings and stacked here in patterns meant to be beautiful. Candles guttered in niches every twenty paces. Somewhere water dripped with the patience of geology.

Marguerite stopped walking. The murmur rose to meet him like heat off a summer road.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you're *loud*."

"Most people find it grim," Serane said.

"Most people can't hear them." He turned slowly, taking it in, and the dead took him in turn—a thousand attentionless attentions swiveling toward the living man who could hear. "Captain Voss. Your family. Are they—"

"North field cemetery." Her voice didn't change at all, which was itself an answer. "My father wanted the ossuary. Said it was an honor." She looked up at the fan of femurs overhead. "I was eleven. I thought so too."

"What changed?"

"The same thing that changes everyone. Keep moving."

* * *

The cells proper began past the gallery's far arch—a corridor of iron doors set into living rock, each with a sliding grate and a number stenciled in white. Serane walked it like a woman walking through her own house, counting under her breath.

"She'll be in the deep row," she said. "Not the common cells. The Order trained her against interrogation; my interrogators have been getting nowhere for six days, and I told them to stop trying because a prisoner who talks too easily is a prisoner who's lying." She paused at a door with no number at all, only a chalk mark. "This one."

Through the grate: darkness, and the small sound of someone breathing in a controlled, deliberate rhythm—the breathing of a person keeping herself together by main force.

"Ines," Marguerite whispered.

The breathing stopped. Then, low and flat, in the voice of someone who has decided not to hope: "If this is another of the captain's games, tell her she's wasting her craft. I've given her everything true twice over."

"It's me."

Silence. Long enough that he heard his own heart. Then the scrape of a body rising off stone, and her face appeared at the grate, half-lit, thinner than he remembered, a bruise gone yellow along her jaw.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You should see the other cells." His hands were shaking so badly the key chattered against the lock. "Hold still. It's an old key, it sticks—"

"Marguerite." Quiet. Serious. "Whose idea is this?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. If it's yours, you're braver than you are sensible. If it's hers—" her eyes went past him, down the corridor, to where Serane stood with her back turned, watching the stair, "—then something has broken in this palace, and I need to know what, before I walk out of this cell and make it worse."

Serane answered without turning around. "The records room, oath-keeper. Shelf forty-one. A ledger from the year of the second Renewal, with your mentor's marginalia in it. I've read it four times. My superiors have ordered me to bury it." Now she did turn, and the torch threw her shadow huge against the bone-pillars. "I have spent nine years hunting your kind because magic killed my family, and I will not spend nine more serving men who knew what was eating their king and chose to feed it. That's what broke. Open the door, carver."

The key turned. The lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and Ines stepped out into the torchlight, blinking, swaying once before she caught herself against the doorframe.

"Can you walk?" Marguerite asked.

"I can run." She rolled her shoulders; something in her back crackled. "Where are we going?"

"Through the dead," Marguerite said. "It's the only way out Serane can give us that doesn't end with her hanging."

* * *

They went back through the gallery fast, Serane leading, Ines limping only slightly, Marguerite last with the lantern shuttered to a thread. And it was there, among the arches and garlands of the palace dead, that the plan nearly died—not by blade or alarm, but by Marguerite's own gift turning traitor.

Because the dead of the ossuary had understood, finally, who he was and why he'd come, and they did not want to be passed by.

*Carver,* they said, louder now, pressing in on him like a crowd at a fair. *Carver, we have words. We have words unspoken forty years, sixty years, we have names nobody carved, we have—*

He staggered. The lantern swung. Ines caught his elbow.

"What is it?"

"They're talking. All of them. At once." He gritted his teeth. "It's usually a murmur. This is—" A skull at his eye level seemed to lean toward him, jaw agape in its mortar bed, and inside it a voice said *my daughter's name was Bess, tell the north field, tell them Bess lived—* and another said *the king before the king, I carried his litter, I know what sits in the crown, boy, I KNOW WHAT SITS IN THE CROWN—*

Marguerite froze.

"What?" Ines said. "What did you hear?"

But Serane had heard the other thing—the thing that mattered. From somewhere above, muffled through floors of stone: a door. Voices. The rotation coming early, or a patrol doubling back, or—

"Torch out," Serane snapped, and killed it with her bare hand, cupping the flame dead. Darkness fell total and soft. "Stair. Move. Now."

They moved blind, Serane counting steps she clearly knew by heart, Ines gripping Marguerite's sleeve, and the dead still pouring their words into him in the dark—*Bess, Bess lived, the crown, the crown, boy, listen, LISTEN*—until he could bear it no longer and he stopped, one hand flat against a pillar of skulls, and said aloud, in a whisper that carried anyway:

"One at a time. Please. One at a time."

And the ossuary—impossibly, wonderfully—went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. The way a room goes quiet when a storyteller lifts a hand. And then, out of the hush, one voice, older than the others, dry as paper: *Follow the drip. Forty paces past the drip, the old sally passage. It opens in the tanner's yard. The guards forgot it exists because the guards are young. We are not young. We remember.*

"The drip," Marguerite breathed. "There's a sally passage. Forty paces past the water drip."

"There's no sally passage on any plan of this palace," Serane said sharply.

"The plans are younger than the dead." He was already moving, following the sound of the drip through the black, counting. "Your architects forgot. They didn't."

Behind him he heard Ines exhale—half laugh, half prayer. "Of course they didn't."

Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty. His fingers found it: a seam in the stone, hairline, then wider, then a door that swung inward on hinges someone had oiled within living memory—which meant someone alive knew, which meant luck, real luck, for the first time since the inn.

Light spilled in behind them. Shouts above, boots on stairs.

"Go," Serane said. "Both of you. Go now."

Ines went through. Marguerite paused at the threshold and turned.

"Come with us."

Something crossed Serane's face in the torchlight rising up the stair behind her—something that had been crossing it for days, maybe years, arriving at last. She looked back once, up the stair, at the light and the shouting and the career and the country she had served with both hands.

"No," she said. "Someone stays to be found innocent. And someone—" she pulled her baton from her belt and settled her grip, facing the light, "—has to explain to the night guard why the prisoners escaped. Thoroughly. At length. With paperwork." She glanced at him, and for just a moment the corner of her mouth moved. "Tell the oath-keeper: shelf forty-one wasn't the only ledger. There's more. When you're ready to come back for it, leave word at the tanneries. Same way you got the key."

Above her, the first guard rounded the stair, torch high, and saw his captain standing alone in the bone gallery with her weapon drawn and two fugitives vanishing into a wall.

"Captain—!"

"Arrest me in the morning, Halden," Serane Voss said, in the clear carrying voice of a woman signing her own name to something permanent. "Tonight, you're going to listen."

Marguerite went through the door, and pulled it shut behind him, and the last thing he heard through the stone was the captain of the Hunt beginning, calmly and precisely, to lie.

* * *

The sally passage ran forty feet and opened, as promised, into the reeking dark of the tanner's yard, between vats that breathed their sour stink over them like a blessing. Ines leaned against the wall and laughed until it turned into something else, and Marguerite held her while she shook, and neither of them said anything about how long he held her, or how tightly.

"We're out," she said finally, into his shoulder. "We're actually out. How?"

"The dead showed us the way."

"Then we owe them."

"We do." He thought of the skull with the daughter's name. "One debt in particular. There's a grave at the north field cemetery. A girl named Bess. Someone needs to carve that she lived."

Ines pulled back and looked at him in the dark, and he felt her looking, the way he was learning to feel most things about her—directly, like weather.

"First the vessels," she said gently. "Then every debt in this kingdom. I promise you."

From the direction of the palace, faint but unmistakable, a bell began to ring—not the third bell, but the alarm, rolling out over the sleeping city stroke after stroke after stroke. Somewhere in the Bone Gallery, Captain Serane Voss was being taken into custody, or was not, and either way the Hunt would wake by dawn with a new shape and a new fury, and the likeness on the walls would have a second face beside it now.

Ines took his hand.

"Run first," she said. "Worry after."

They ran.
