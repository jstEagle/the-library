## 23. Fugitives No More

The hills above Greyford were the color of old iron in the last light, all bracken and bare thorn, and the wind came off them smelling of frost and distant snow. Marguerite's legs ached from the climb. His lungs burned. He had not slept properly in four days—first the inn, then the running, then the long cold hours of hiding in a culvert while Hunt riders passed close enough that he could hear their horses chewing their bits—and now, finally, they had stopped.

Serane Voss chose the campsite, because of course she did. She moved through the dusk like someone reading a document: here, a hollow backed by rock so no firelight would show; there, a spring seeping from under a stone, sweet enough to drink; there, a stand of dead gorse to break the wind. She set the camp the way she had once set ambushes, and she did it without being asked, and she did it well, and Marguerite watched her do it and thought, not for the first time since the ossuary, *I have thrown my lot in with a woman who could kill us both before we finished our supper.*

She didn't, though. She built a small fire instead—a real one, unhidden, which felt almost obscene after days of smokeless cooking and sleeping in ditches—and she fed it carefully, stick by stick, until it made a circle of warmth no wider than a blanket.

Ines sat down across from her without a word. The two women regarded each other over the flames. They had been fugitives together for two days now, and in that time they had exchanged perhaps forty sentences, most of them about water, distance, and the location of patrols. It was the politest hatred Marguerite had ever witnessed.

He sat down between them because someone had to, and began dividing the bread.

* * *

They ate in silence for a while. The fire cracked. Somewhere below, a fox screamed once and went quiet.

It was Serane who broke first, which surprised him. She sat with her knees drawn up, her sword across her lap where she always kept it, even now—even here, on a hillside with two people who were, by every law she'd spent nine years enforcing, criminals. She was turning something over in her fingers. A strip of leather from her bracer. She worried it the way other people worried prayer beads.

"You want to ask me," she said at last. "Both of you. You've wanted to ask me since the palace."

Marguerite said nothing. Ines said nothing. The fire popped.

"Why I turned." Serane's voice was flat, the voice she used for reports. "Why the Captain of the Hunt is sitting on a hillside eating stolen bread with a bone-witch and a disgraced oath-keeper."

"I wasn't going to ask," Marguerite said.

"Yes, you were. You've been looking at me sideways for two days like I'm a trap that hasn't sprung yet." She almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, thin as a knife's edge. "Fair enough. Here is your answer, then, so you can stop flinching when I move too fast."

She threw the leather scrap into the fire and watched it curl.

"I had a brother," she said. "Tomas. And a sister, Liss. And a mother who sang badly and a father who mended nets in Saltmere, down on the coast, before the coast road existed properly. We were nobody. Salt-smelling nobodies. And when I was eleven years old, a storm drove a ship onto the rocks below our village, and one of the survivors came up out of the surf half-drowned, and he was gifted."

The firelight moved on her face. Marguerite found he couldn't look away.

"He didn't mean any harm. That's the part no one believes, so I'll say it twice: he didn't mean any harm. He was a weather-worker, a hedge-craft man, the kind who calms squalls for fishermen and gets paid in fish heads. He'd been at sea three weeks. He was freezing and mad with saltwater and his gift came out of him sideways, the way gifts do when the body's failing. He reached for the storm to push it back off the shore." Her jaw tightened. "And he pulled it down on us instead. All of it. Every ounce of that storm came inland like it had been called by name. Took the roof off our house. Took Tomas. Took Liss. My father lived another six years and never once said my mother's name again, because she died reaching for them through the floodwater and he saw her face when the wave took her, and some things a man cannot carry."

Silence. Even the wind seemed to hold itself.

"The crown sent investigators," Serane went on. "They wrote it down as an act of the gods. Storm-taken. Fourteen dead in Saltmere, storm-taken, condolences of the court. No one told my father that a man had done it. No one told *me*. I found out eight years later, when I joined the constabulary, digging through the same coastal records for a different case. One line in a magistrate's private ledger. *Survivor apprehended, gifted, drowned attempting escape.*" She looked into the fire. "I joined the Hunt the month it formed. I told myself we would find the ones like him—the ones whose gift comes out sideways, the ones who pull storms onto villages—and we would stop them before they killed fourteen more somebodies' children."

"And instead?" Ines asked quietly.

"Instead I burned apothecaries." Serane's voice didn't waver, but her hands had gone still, absolutely still, in the way of a person holding themselves together by main force. "A woman in Vellamark who could ease childbirth pain. A boy in Corrow who glowed in the dark when he slept. An old man who talked to bees. Nine years, and the list of people I've put to the pyre is longer than the list of people my gift-hunter ever saved, which is zero, because the ones like the Saltmere man are one in ten thousand and the rest are just people. Just frightened people hiding small mercies." She exhaled slowly. "Then I pulled a dead soldier out of an alley in Vellamark, and his relic was gone, and I followed the trail of a bone-carver with kind eyes, and somewhere along that road I started finding papers I wasn't supposed to find. Records of the binding. Orders to bury them." She turned her head and looked at Ines directly. "Your Order didn't fall, oath-keeper. It was *felled*. And the axe is still swinging in the palace, and it's wearing the king's arm."

Ines had gone very pale. "You knew. In the cell—you already knew."

"I suspected. You confirmed it." Serane shrugged, a soldier's shrug, putting the weight down. "I'm not asking your forgiveness. I'm telling you why I'm here, so you know what you're sleeping next to. I am not a good woman. But I am done burning people for a crown that eats its own kings."

* * *

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time anyone spoke again. Marguerite had taken out the oath-bone—not to use it, just to hold it, the way a man holds a stone he's decided to carry—and it lay quiet in his palm, its layered voices murmuring at the edge of hearing like a sea heard through a wall.

It was Ines who spoke next. She had been quiet so long he'd assumed she wouldn't speak at all tonight.

"My mentor's name was Ossian Vael," she said.

Serane looked up. Marguerite went still.

"I've never said it aloud," Ines continued, "since the Order fell. Not once. Eight years, and I have told myself a hundred stories—that he died in the purge, that he was coerced, that the corruption crept into the binding without his knowledge, that he was a good man caught in a bad machine. I have built him a shrine of excuses in my head and prayed to it every night." She laughed, short and bitter, a sound with no humor in it anywhere. "He taught me the oath-keeping forms when I was sixteen. He used to say, *Ines, a vow is a bridge. Build it true, or everything you send across it falls.* He held my hands steady the first time I cut a binding-glyph. He told me I had the truest ear for oath-language he'd heard in thirty years of teaching. And I loved him. That's the worst of it. I loved him the way you love the person who tells you that you matter."

The firelight caught the scar on her palm—the ward-cut from the motherhouse, still healing, a raw pink seam across the lines of her hand.

"When the king began to fail—Aldric's father, then Aldric—I brought the records to him. The vessels' resonance readings. The spirit's growth curve. I laid it all out and said, *Master, the binding has soured, we must unbind it,* and he looked at me with those kind gray eyes and he said—" her voice caught, steadied— "he said, *Yes, child. I know. I designed it that way.*

"He bound the devouring into it deliberately. Forty years ago. The Order believed they were binding a guardian, but Ossian had decided that a kingdom needed a king who could be *steered*, and what better steersman than a spirit that wears each monarch from within? He built the flaw into the rite and hid it inside the true work, and he let three generations of keepers maintain his machine believing they were maintaining a shield." She was crying now, silently, the tears simply present on her face, unacknowledged. "And when I confronted him, he didn't deny it. He said the realm was safer with a controllable crown. He said I'd understand when I was older. And then the purge came, and he vanished, and I let myself believe he'd died, because a dead traitor is easier to carry than a living one."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, like a soldier.

"There. Named. Out loud. Ossian Vael betrayed his order, his king, and me, and I spent eight years making excuses for him because the truth meant admitting that the person who taught me everything worth knowing was the rot at the center of it." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce through the wet. "I named it. Are you satisfied?"

"No," Marguerite said quietly.

Her head snapped toward him.

"But you will be," he said. "Eventually. Maybe. Naming it doesn't fix anything tonight, but tomorrow it weighs less. I know because I did the same thing—for ten years I told myself I hid my gift to protect my neighbors, that staying silent was a kind of loyalty. It was cowardice wearing loyalty's coat. The day I called it cowardice, out loud, alone, in my own workshop—it didn't make me brave. But it made me honest. And honest is where you start from."

Ines stared at him. Something in her face shifted—not softening exactly, but settling, like silt finding the riverbed.

* * *

Later, when the fire had sunk to embers and Serane had taken first watch at the lip of the hollow, Marguerite lay awake between the bedrolls, the oath-bone under his hand, listening to the hillside breathe.

He should sleep. Tomorrow they would go north, to the motherhouse ruins, to the third vessel and whatever price it demanded. He needed rest the way a drowning man needs air.

But he lay listening instead—to Serane's boots shifting on the frost-stiff grass, patient as a metronome; to Ines's breathing, finally deep and even, the first true sleep he'd heard in her since the inn; to the wind combing the bracken; to the faint, far chorus the bones always carried, the dead murmuring their endless unfinished business.

And beneath all of it, a new sound. Small. Unfamiliar. Three people who had every reason to betray each other, lying in the dark within arm's reach, keeping watch in turns. It wasn't trust. Trust took longer. But it was the ground trust grew in, and he recognized it with a start because he hadn't felt it in ten years: he was not alone, and he was not hiding, and tomorrow he would walk toward the thing he feared in company.

*We are fugitives no more,* he thought, and then corrected himself, smiling at the dark. *No. We are fugitives exactly as much as we were this morning. But we are fugitives together.*

From the embers, the oath-bone stirred. One voice rose out of the layered murmur, clearer than it had ever been—the dying soldier, the one who had pressed the relic into his hands in the alley, the one whose blood had started all of this. The voice was calm now, almost gentle, and it spoke words Marguerite had never heard from it before:

*"The motherhouse keeps more than the vessel, carver. Ask the keeper what Ossian left behind in the west crypt. Ask her why the ward drinks blood."*

Marguerite sat up slowly, his heart beginning to pound.

Across the ashes, Ines opened her eyes.
