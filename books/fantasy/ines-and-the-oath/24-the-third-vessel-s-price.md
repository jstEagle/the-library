## 24. The Third Vessel's Price

The motherhouse had been burning for three days before anyone thought to save it, and by then the fire had eaten everything worth keeping except the stone.

Ines stood at the edge of the courtyard where she'd once drilled with wooden practice-staves at dawn, her breath fogging in the grey morning air. The ruin rose around her like a ribcage picked clean—arches without roofs, corridors open to the sky, ivy threading through the bones of the place that had made her. Snow lay in the hollows of the shattered flagstones, undisturbed for years.

"You grew up here," Marguerite said quietly. He came to stand beside her, his pack heavy on his shoulders, two bone-vessels wrapped in wool against his chest. He'd been quiet since Greyford, since the bridge, since the checkpoint sergeant had smiled at his carved token and waved them through. Quiet in the way he got when he was listening to something the rest of them couldn't hear.

"I trained here." She corrected him out of habit. "Grew up is generous. I arrived at nine years old and they spent ten years carving the girl out of me and fitting an oath-keeper into the space."

"Did it hurt?"

She looked at him. He asked questions like that—flat, sincere, terrible questions no one else would think to ask.

"Everything worth doing hurts," she said. "Come on. It's at the center."

* * *

They crossed the courtyard single file, Serane last, her hand never far from her sword even though the Hunt was a hundred miles behind them and officially dissolved in their absence—or so the rumors said, though rumors were cheap and Serane trusted none of them. The captain moved through ruins like a woman reading a battlefield: eyes up, eyes down, checking sightlines, counting exits.

"The ward will know we're coming," Ines said. "It's been waiting forty years."

"Wards wait?" Marguerite asked.

"Good ones do." She stopped before the great doors of the sanctum—the inner heart of the motherhouse, where only oath-keepers of the third rank had been permitted. The doors themselves had survived the fire better than anything else: black oak banded with iron, unburned, which should have been impossible and was its own kind of warning. Carved into them, worn shallow by weather but still legible, ran the words of the First Oath.

Ines hadn't read them aloud since she was nineteen years old.

*We swear our blood to the crown's keeping. We swear our memory to the realm's service. We swear our names to the silence between.*

Her mouth went dry. She'd sworn all three. And the Order had fallen anyway, scattered and disgraced, its name dragged through mud by the very crown it had died protecting.

"It's warded against everyone but you," Marguerite said. Not a question. He'd heard it from the dead somewhere along the road; he'd stopped distinguishing lately between what he learned from living mouths and what the bones told him.

"From my bloodline of keepers, yes. The warding was laid by the founders. It drinks oath-keeper blood and judges what it finds." She drew her belt knife. "If I'm found wanting, it won't just refuse us. It'll take the blood and give nothing back."

"And if you're not wanting?"

"Then we get the vessel."

Serane shifted behind them. "And if it kills you?"

"Then you have two vessels and a problem," Ines said, and put the knife to her left palm before either of them could argue further.

The cut was deep—deeper than it needed to be, because she remembered the old texts, remembered the warning scrawled in her mentor's cramped hand: *the ward takes what it is offered and measures the offering*. A shallow cut was a shallow faith. She pressed her bleeding hand flat against the iron band of the door.

For a moment, nothing. The cold bit into her wound, and her blood ran warm down her wrist and dripped onto the snow, each drop a small red flower blooming and fading.

Then the door drank.

There was no other word for it. The blood lifted off her skin in a slow red film, drawn inward through the iron as if the metal had become porous, and the pain changed—not worse, but stranger, a pulling sensation running up her arm and into her chest, as though something behind the door had hooked a finger into her and begun reeling.

"Ines—" Marguerite's hand closed on her shoulder.

"Don't break the contact," she managed. "It's judging."

"Judging what?"

"Whether I'm still an oath-keeper." She laughed, breathless, the sound tearing out of her. "Forty years after the Order fell. Whether I'm still *worthy*."

The pulling deepened. Her knees buckled. Marguerite caught her weight against his shoulder, and the last thing she saw before the world folded shut around her was the black oak doors swinging silently inward—and beyond them, not the sanctum she remembered, but a corridor lit with golden afternoon light that did not exist in winter.

* * *

She was nineteen again.

She knew it immediately, the way you know a dream—the body remembers its own ages. Her hands were smooth, unscarred. Her hair was the brown it had been before grey came into it. She stood in the novices' cloister of the motherhouse, whole and unburned, and the bells of morning prayer were ringing, and someone was calling her name.

"Ines! You'll be late for your swearing!"

Maren. Maren, who had died at the fall of the Order, who had taken a crossbow bolt meant for the reliquary and bled out in Ines's arms on these very stones. Maren came around the corner young and laughing and alive, her novice's robes crooked as always, and Ines's heart stopped and restarted wrong.

"This isn't real," Ines said.

"Isn't what?" Maren frowned, that particular frown, head tilted. "You're strange this morning. Come on—the prelate wants the candidates assembled early. Today's the day, Ines. Your swearing-in. Third rank. Everything you wanted."

Everything she wanted. Yes. That was the shape of the trap, and she could see its edges now that she looked—the light too golden, the bells too sweet, the snow absent though it had snowed on her swearing day, she remembered that, she'd remember that forever, the snow catching in Maren's eyelashes while they shivered in the courtyard—

But oh, it was hard. Oh, it was so hard. Maren stood there breathing, and her crooked collar was exactly right, and Ines had spent twenty years carrying the weight of that death, the specific guilt of surviving when the better keeper hadn't, and here was the wound offered back to her healed.

"If I stay," Ines said slowly, "the vessel stays lost. The king stays hollowed. The spirit wins."

"What are you talking about?" Maren laughed. "You're talking like one of the mystics. Come *on*. They're waiting."

"They waited forty years," Ines said. "They can wait a moment longer." She reached out and took Maren's hands—young hands, warm hands, hands that had gone cold in her grip once already—and held them. "I'm sorry I couldn't save you. I've been sorry every day since. But I won't trade the living for the dead. Not even for you. Especially not for you."

Something flickered across Maren's face—grief, or pride, or the ward itself acknowledging the answer—and the cloister began to dissolve like sugar in water.

"No," Maren said, and her voice was suddenly older, suddenly layered, suddenly the ward speaking through the beloved dead. "That is the first answer. There are others. The blood has paid. Now the name must."

The world turned over.

* * *

The second illusion was crueler, because it wasn't painful at all.

She stood in a small house in the river country—a house she had imagined building a hundred times during dull hours of drill and doctrine. Two rooms, a garden, a loom by the window. A woman came in from the garden with dirt on her hands and a tomato in each fist, complaining cheerfully about the blight, and the woman was Ines herself, older, soft-bodied, content, and there were children's voices outside calling *Mother*, and none of it had ever happened because she had joined the Order at nine years old and given that life away before she was old enough to understand what she was trading.

"You could have had this," the ward said, in her own voice now, from everywhere. "This is what you refused. Look how happy she is. Look how little she regrets."

"I don't regret it," Ines said, and was surprised to find it true. "I grieve it. Those aren't the same thing."

"She has children. You have scars."

"She has a garden. I kept a king alive for twenty years." Ines walked through the illusion-house, touching nothing, cataloguing its perfections the way she'd once catalogued the flaws in forged seals. "I chose the flawed truth. I choose it again. Show me the third test or let me pass."

The house sighed away like breath on glass.

* * *

The third was Marguerite.

He sat in her quarters in the palace, in the life that might have been if the Order had never fallen—if she had risen to prelate, if she had found him decades ago instead of weeks. He was carving something small and white, whalebone by the look of it, and he looked up at her with those kind eyes and said, "You're home early."

And Ines understood, finally, completely, what the ward was doing. It wasn't tempting her with lies. It was showing her truths—true griefs, true hungers, true loves—and asking whether any of them outweighed the oath.

She knelt down in front of him, in the illusion that knew her too well, and said: "He doesn't know me yet. Not fully. What I feel for him is real, but it's new, and new things can be rebuilt. The oath cannot. If I abandon it now, everything he suffered to get here becomes waste." She touched the illusion's face, gently, apologizing to it. "I love him. And I will still walk past him to finish this. That's what an oath-keeper is. That's what I am."

The false Marguerite smiled—sadly, she thought, or perhaps that was her own reflection in the ward's long patience—and set down his carving.

"Three answers given," the ward said, in a voice that was now every voice at once: Maren's, her own, his, and beneath them all something older, mineral, patient as stone. "Blood freely spilled. Grief refused over comfort. Love weighed and not found heavier than duty." A pause, vast as a turning season. "The founders would have called you worthy. The founders were harder judges than I. Enter, Ines of the Broken Order. Take what your Order left sleeping. And know this: the vessel you seek does not sleep peacefully. It has been listening to the dark for forty years, and it has opinions."

The corridor of golden light collapsed.

* * *

She woke on cold stone with Marguerite's hands under her shoulders and Serane standing guard over them both, sword drawn, facing a doorway that now stood open onto genuine darkness.

"—back," Marguerite was saying, urgent, low. "Ines. Ines, come back. Your eyes were open but you weren't *in* there—"

"How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe ten. You bled a lot." His jaw was tight. "The door opened on its own and you dropped. I couldn't wake you. I thought—" He didn't finish it.

"I passed." She sat up, swaying. Her left palm had sealed itself into a thin white line, as if years had passed since the cutting. "Three tests. It showed me the dead, the life I never lived, and you."

"Me?"

"You'll be flattered later. Help me up."

The sanctum beyond the doors was exactly as the fire had left it: scorched benches, a shattered altar, ash drifted like grey snow across everything. But at the center, on a plinth of black stone that the flames had never touched, something glowed faintly.

The third vessel.

It was larger than the other two—a great knuckle-bone of some beast Ines didn't recognize, mounted upright in a cradle of silver that had gone black with age. As they approached, Marguerite stopped walking.

"Do you hear that?" he whispered.

"Hear what?"

"It's not silent." His face had gone pale in the gloom. "The other two hummed, when we got close. This one—" He swallowed. "This one is *talking*. It's been talking to itself for forty years. Ines, it's been alone in the dark with nothing to listen to except its own voice, and it's—" He looked at her, and she saw real fear in him, the first she'd seen since the drowned chapel. "It's gone strange. Like a person locked too long in a cell."

Serane's hand tightened on her sword. "Can it be used?"

"It can be used." Marguerite stepped forward, slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal. "But someone will have to listen to it. All the way. Before the rite. Someone has to hear what forty years of solitude made it say, or it won't sit still in the pattern." He glanced back at her, and despite the fear there was something else in his face too—that stubborn, terrible compassion of his. "I think it has to be me. I'm the only one who listens for a living."

Before Ines could answer, before she could tell him what it might cost him this time, the vessel's glow pulsed once—like a heartbeat, like a knock at a door—and from the bone came a sound neither whisper nor scream but something between:

*a voice, hoarse from forty years of talking to no one.*

*"Oh," it said. "Oh. Visitors. At last. At last, at last, at last—"*

Marguerite reached out and lifted it from its cradle, and the darkness of the ruined sanctum seemed to lean in around them, listening.

* * *

Outside, the snow had begun again. Serane took first watch without being asked, and Ines sat beside Marguerite at the edge of the courtyard while he wrapped the third vessel in wool with hands that were not quite steady.

"It talked to you," she said. "While you carried it out. I saw your face."

"It talks constantly." He gave a short laugh with no humor in it. "Mostly it asks questions. Who I am. Whether I'm real. Whether *it's* real. Forty years is a long time to be certain of nothing." He tied the wrapping off and looked at her, and in the falling snow his eyes were very clear. "Ines. When it asked who I was—I told it the truth. And then it asked who *you* were. And I realized I didn't know how to answer. Not the name. The name's easy. It asked what you are *to me*."

The snow settled on her shoulders. Somewhere below them, in the valley, the lights of the capital were coming on one by one, and the coronation renewal was four days away, and the king was running out of time.

"What did you tell it?" she asked.

"That I didn't know yet," Marguerite said. "And that I intended to find out."
