## 25. Illusions of the Motherhouse

The ward took Ines's blood the way a dry field takes rain—greedily, without thanks. It ran along the ruined doorway in channels that had been carved for exactly this purpose forty years ago, and then the motherhouse opened.

Not opened, exactly. *Unfolded.* The great doors, which had stood warped and rotting since the Order's fall, swung inward on silent hinges, and beyond them was not ruin at all.

Beyond them was candlelight.

Ines stopped so hard that Marguerite nearly walked into her back. "That's not possible," she said. "I've been here. Three years ago. The roof is gone. The nave is a bird roost. I saw it myself."

"I believe you," Marguerite said quietly. He could feel it already—the hum beneath his feet, the pressure behind his eyes that meant he was standing inside something made of listening. The whole building was bone to him now, or something like it: dense with intention, saturated with old vows. "But I don't think it's trying to convince us it's real."

"Then what is it doing?"

"Testing whether we'd rather it were."

They stepped through together, because there was no sense putting it off, and the doors closed behind them with the soft finality of a held breath.

* * *

The nave stretched ahead of them, whole and golden and impossible. Candles burned in their hundreds along walls that had never been rebuilt, and the air smelled of beeswax and cold stone and—Ines's stomach turned at this—the specific tallow-and-ink smell of the scriptorium where she had spent six years of her life copying oaths she barely understood.

"It knows us," she whispered. "It's reading us."

"Then let's not give it anything good to read." But his voice wasn't steady either, and she heard it.

They walked. The floor was clean where it should have been choked with rubble. Somewhere ahead, voices—the murmur of the Order at morning prayer, dozens of keepers chanting the Litanies of Binding in unison, a sound Ines had not heard since she was twenty-three years old and standing in this very nave while her world began to end.

She should have known better than to look up.

He was standing at the altar rail. Master Corvane. Her mentor. The man whose teachings she had carried for two decades, whose betrayal she still could not say aloud even after the hermit—after everything. He looked as he had on her first day in the motherhouse: silver at the temples, kind around the eyes, patient as stone. The face she had trusted more than her own judgment.

"Ines," he said warmly. "You're late. We're about to begin."

She knew it was false. She knew it the way you know a dream is a dream, in the part of the mind that stays awake while the rest of you wanders. And yet her feet moved. That was the horror of it—not deception but momentum, the body's old obedience to a voice it had spent years learning to follow.

"You died," she said. Her voice came out flat. "Or you vanished. You soured the binding and you vanished, and I spent twenty years paying for it."

Corvane tilted his head, sorrowful. "Did I? Or did you simply need someone to blame? Come here, child. Look at what we built. Look at what it could have been."

And the nave changed around her—or she noticed it changing, which amounted to the same thing. The candles multiplied. The chant swelled. And through the great windows poured light that fell across rows of faces: keepers, hundreds of them, alive and robed and singing, an Order that had never fallen, a world where the binding had held pure and no king had ever begun to claw at his crown.

A world where she had never failed.

"There," Corvane said gently. "Isn't this what you wanted? Not your name cleared. Something better than cleared. A name that never needed clearing. Stay. Teach. Forgive me—it costs you nothing here, forgiveness. Nothing here costs anything."

Marguerite's hand found her shoulder. Just that. Warm weight, real weight, the hand of a man who smelled of wood shavings and hearth smoke and the road, who had blisters from walking and fear-sweat he didn't bother hiding. The illusion smelled of nothing at all. Illusions have no smell; she realized it only now, standing in one.

"That's how you know," she said aloud—to Marguerite, or to herself, or to the ward itself. "None of it stinks. Twenty years of my life and none of it stinks of anything."

She looked at Corvane—at the lie wearing her mentor's kindness—and found, to her surprise, that she was not angry. Grief, yes. Old grief, worn smooth as river stone. But the anger had finally burned down to something she could hold.

"No," she said. "You taught me that an oath is a thing you keep when keeping it costs. This—" she gestured at the shining nave, the singing dead-or-never-were, "—costs nothing. So it isn't an oath. It isn't even a life. It's a shop window."

Corvane's face flickered. Just once. Like a candle guttering.

"And I don't forgive you here," Ines said. "Because here it would be worthless. If I ever forgive you, it will be out there, in the cold, where it actually means something. Where it *stinks*."

She turned her back on him. The nave held its breath—and then, softly, like snow sliding off a roof, the whole golden edifice sighed and slumped. The candles went gray. The chant thinned to wind through broken rafters. When she looked again, the motherhouse was ruin again, honest ruin, and Corvane was a shadow of shadow dissolving against the far wall.

Marguerite exhaled beside her. "Well done."

"Don't praise me yet." She gripped his arm. "It's not finished with us. It reads everyone. It'll read you next."

"I know." His jaw was tight. "I felt it start before we even came through the door."

* * *

It took him differently. Of course it did. The ward was not cruel; it was thorough.

Marguerite blinked, and he was in his workshop.

His workshop—but not the cramped, careful room above the chandler's in Vellamark. This one was wide-windowed, sunlit, set on a street where people passed openly carrying charms, where a carved whalebone pendant hung in a shop window with a price tag and no shame attached. Through the glass he could see a market square where a crier called out festival days, not burnings. No ash marks on any door.

There was a sign over his bench. *M. Coldharbour, Bone-Carver. Hearing Given Freely.*

He picked it up with hands that shook. Ten years. Ten years of carving with the shutters closed, of pocketing coins with practiced guilt, of hearing widows' dead husbands whisper through the grain and pretending to be merely skilled. Ten years of flinching at boots on stairs.

Here, a woman waited by the counter with a mourning charm request, and she said, "My husband's last words—I want them cut into the piece, all of them, even the ugly ones," and no one lowered their voice. A child pressed her nose to the display case and pointed at a rattle carved with whales, and her mother smiled instead of steering her away.

No Hunt. No Voss. No oath-bone screaming in his pack. No dying soldier in an alley pressing destiny into his hands.

He understood, with terrible clarity, what the ward was offering. Not power. Not wealth. Just the ordinary life he might have had if he had never learned to be afraid—if the world had never given him reason. A life where his gift was a trade instead of a crime. Where loneliness was a choice rather than armor.

He could feel the illusion studying him, waiting to see if he would take it.

And God help him, some part of him wanted to. Some tired, starved part of him that had spent a decade eating guilt for breakfast wanted to sit down at that sunlit bench and carve until he forgot the drowned chapel, forgot the soldier's blood going tacky under his fingers, forgot the way Ines had looked covering his escape—

Ines.

The thought arrived like a hook behind the sternum. Here, in this warm bright shop, there was no Ines. There was no reason for an Ines. A Marguerite who never hid was a Marguerite who never fled, who never met a disgraced oath-keeper in the dark, who never sat across a fire watching her laugh at something he'd said and feeling his chest do something complicated and new.

The ward offered him ten years of honesty.

It asked, in exchange, for the last three weeks. For her.

He set the sign down very carefully, the way you put down something breakable.

"No," he said.

The shop did not argue. That was somehow worse. It simply continued—the woman waiting patiently, sunlight lying warm on the bench, the whole comfortable world humming its invitation—and he understood that it would continue forever, patiently, if he let it. Comfort doesn't fight you. It just waits.

"The truth is uglier," he told the empty middle distance, addressing whatever intelligence lived in the ward. "The truth has ash on the wind and checkpoints on the roads and a woman I—"

He stopped. Swallowed.

"A woman I care about waiting in a ruin full of ghosts, and a king being eaten alive, and every reason to run north and not one good one to stay except that staying is right." His voice roughened. "That's the life I choose. The one that costs. You taught me nothing—I learned that on my own, in an alley, holding a dying man's hand. An oath is a thing you keep when keeping it costs."

Where had he learned that phrase? It felt borrowed. It felt like hers.

The workshop dimmed like a lamp running dry. The woman by the counter blurred, smiled sadly, and became motes of dust drifting through a shaft of genuine gray light. The bench under his hands went cold, then rough, then real—old wood, water-stained, abandoned for decades.

* * *

He came back to himself on his knees in the ruined nave, gasping, and Ines was gripping both his arms, saying his name with a rawness she would deny later.

"How long?"

"An hour, maybe. For me it was—" She stopped. Shook her head. "Doesn't matter. You came back. That's what matters."

"You?" He searched her face. "What did it show you?"

For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then, quietly: "A world where I never joined the Order. Where I never had anything to be disgraced from." A thin, humorless smile. "It almost worked. It showed me my mentor, alive and kind, and an Order that never fell, and a version of me who had never failed anyone." Her grip tightened on his arms. "It was perfect. That's how I knew. Nothing real is perfect, Marguerite. Real things are scarred and they stink and they cost, and I'd rather have one true hour of this wreck than a lifetime of that window-display world."

He laughed—a cracked, exhausted sound—and hauled himself upright using her shoulders. They stood facing each other in the honest ruin, dust sifting down through the broken roof, and something passed between them that neither of them named. Something that had been growing since a firelit camp and a forged seal and a blind hermit's forgiveness, and which both of them were now carefully, deliberately not looking at directly.

Because looking at it directly would mean admitting what it was. And admitting what it was would mean imagining losing it. And they were about to walk deeper into a place built to take things from people.

"Come on," Ines said at last, turning toward the inner sanctum, where the third vessel waited behind whatever trials remained. "We're not done. The ward tests worthiness, and worthiness isn't proven by surviving one illusion."

"Then what proves it?"

She glanced back at him, and in the gray light her eyes were fierce and tired and entirely real.

"Choosing again," she said. "Every time it asks. Choosing the flawed truth over the comfortable lie, over and over, until it believes us."

Ahead of them, deep in the motherhouse, something ancient stirred—a sound like a great door unlocking, or a vow remembering itself—and the dust in the air began, very slowly, to glow.
