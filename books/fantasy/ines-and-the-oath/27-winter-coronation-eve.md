## 27. Winter Coronation Eve

The city of Vellamark had never been quiet, but Marguerite had never heard it like this.

He stood at the window of a rented room above a chandler's shop on Candlemakers' Row, and the noise below was not the ordinary commerce of a capital morning. It was the sound of a whole realm arriving at once—cart wheels grinding on frost-slick cobbles, heralds shouting the names of distant provinces, ten thousand voices braided into one long hum that rose through the floorboards and settled in his teeth. He could feel it in his bones. Literally, he thought, and almost laughed. In the whalebone charm in his pocket, faint as breath on glass, the dead were murmuring too. Even they wanted to see what happened tomorrow.

*Tomorrow.*

The word sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Below, workmen were stringing garlands of winter pine along the eaves of the Row, and a boy ran past under them carrying a bundle of white candles nearly as tall as himself. Every window on the street held light already, though it was barely midday—the Renewal Eve custom, Ines had explained, one candle lit for every year of the king's reign, so that Aldric's forty years burned in every home whether the household loved him or not. Marguerite counted the flames across the rooftops until counting became useless. Forty years. Forty candles. And somewhere beneath all that borrowed light, a man who was not entirely a man was preparing to be re-sworn to the thing that ate him.

"You'll wear a hole in the floor," Ines said behind him.

She was sitting cross-legged on the bed with the three bone-vessels laid out before her on a square of black cloth, as she had been for most of two days, checking and rechecking the fittings, the seals, the hairline channels carved into each vessel's throat where the rite would need to breathe. She did this the way she did everything: precisely, silently, with the particular stillness of someone who had once made a catastrophic mistake and intended never to make another. Her oath-keeper's hands—scarred at the knuckles from forty years of other people's bindings—moved over the bones without quite touching them.

"I'm not wearing holes," Marguerite said. "I'm listening."

"To what?"

"The city." He turned from the window. "It sounds different when it's afraid and happy at the same time. Like a crowd before a hanging and a wedding rolled into one."

Ines looked up at him. Candlelight from the sill caught the grey at her temple, the line of an old scar along her jaw that he had stopped seeing weeks ago because it was simply part of her face now, the way the river was part of Vellamark. "That's exactly what tomorrow is," she said. "Both at once. Depending on how it goes."

He crossed the room and sat down opposite her, the vessels between them like a border. The first, from the drowned chapel, still smelled faintly of deep water no matter how many times she wiped it clean. The second, the hermit's, was warm to the touch in a way bone should not be. The third had cost Ines blood and something worse than blood, and she had not spoken of the motherhouse illusions since, and he had not asked, because he knew what waited inside trials like that, and some doors you did not reopen for the people you—

For the people you traveled with, he corrected himself, in the privacy of his own skull, where apparently even now he could not say the truer thing aloud.

"Say it again," he said instead. "The order of it. I want it in my bones."

"You have enough in your bones," Ines said dryly, but she obliged, because she always obliged when he asked; it was the one tenderness they permitted themselves, this exchange of necessary things. "Midnight toll, the procession enters through the West Door. The king re-swears the Oath of Aldric at the high altar—that's the moment the spirit is most present, because the oath is its anchor and its meal. It has fed on forty renewals. Tomorrow it expects a forty-first." Her finger traced the air above the vessels without landing. "We are positioned among the relic-bearers—I've the credentials, Serane has the guard rotation. When the king begins the swearing, we move to the altar steps. You open the vessels. I hold the frame of the rite. And then—"

"And then we pour in a memory of true love," Marguerite said, "willingly given, and the spirit starves, and the king goes free."

"Yes."

They had known this for six days. They had said these words to each other perhaps fifty times, in barns and ditches and the back of a charcoal wagon, and every time, Marguerite watched for it—the flicker in her eyes, the hesitation, any sign that she understood what the requirement meant for *him*, that she had done the arithmetic he had done alone in the dark a hundred times. And every time, her face gave him nothing but resolve, and he told himself: *she doesn't know. She hasn't worked it out. There is still time to make sure she never has to.*

Because there was only one memory in either of their lives that fit the requirement. He had known it since the night by the fire when she'd told him about the mentor, about the binding, about the young keeper who'd trusted the wrong man—and he had felt the knowledge settle over him like snowfall, soft and total and cold. The rite would take it. All of it. The fire, the bridge, the alley where she'd saved him from the patrol, the way she said his name when she was annoyed, which was often, the way she'd looked at him in the drowned chapel when she pulled him back from the chorus of the dead. Everything. He would stand up from the altar with the kingdom saved and look at Ines of the Broken Order and see a stranger.

And she would remember everything, and grieve alone, and never know why he had done it.

That was the shape of it. That was the price he had chosen without telling her, because if he told her she would find a way to stop him, or worse, offer to do it herself, and he had decided—one decision, made quietly, the way he made all his real decisions—that this was the one thing in his life he would not let be taken out of his hands.

"Marguerite." Ines was watching him. "You went somewhere."

"Just counting candles," he said.

* * *

Serane Voss stood at the barracks window overlooking Coronation Square and read the crowd the way other officers read dispatches.

The Hunt had stood down at dawn. By ancient charter, no officer of the crown might exercise the power of arrest between sunset on Renewal Eve and the completion of the king's oath—a courtesy older than anyone could justify, a relic of the years when the ceremony had involved actual danger, when kings had died at their own coronations and the realm had agreed, once a year, to lower its weapons and pray. Serane had always found the tradition quaint. Today she found it unbearable. Somewhere in that sea of banners and fur hoods and provincial lords, three fugitives were walking freely toward the cathedral, and her warrant for them was worth less than the paper it was written on until midnight passed.

Not that she intended to use the warrant.

She turned back to her desk, where the evidence lay in its folder like a wound she kept choosing to touch: the Order's records, the correspondence proving the binding had been corrupted deliberately, the orders from her superiors to bury it, delivered in her superior's own hand with his own seal. *For the stability of the realm.* As though the realm were a patient to be kept sedated rather than a body to be healed.

On the corner of the desk, weighted under an inkwell, sat a charcoal sketch of a bone-carver's face. Kind eyes, the artist had managed, despite working from a reliquary owner's frightened description. Serane had looked at those eyes more times in the past month than she cared to admit.

A knock. "Captain. The delegation from Greyford is asking again about seating."

"Tell them the cathedral assigns seats, not the Hunt," she called, and when the footsteps faded she allowed herself, briefly, to close her eyes.

Tomorrow—or tonight, at the tolling—she would either be the woman who saved the king or the woman who helped unmake the monarchy, and there was a decent chance history would not distinguish between the two. She thought of her family the way she thought of them every day now, but differently lately: not as the reason for the Hunt, but as people her father would have wanted protected, and her father would not have wanted protection bought with lies. He'd been a ferryman. He'd believed in crossing water honestly.

*One night,* she had given the carver. One night of access, in exchange for proof, and the proof had been worse than anything she'd imagined, and now here she stood, captain of the Hunt, standing down her own hunters, guarding the door against herself.

She picked up the sketch, considered it a last time, and slid it into the folder with the rest of the treason.

* * *

By late afternoon the crowd outside the cathedral had grown thick enough that moving through it meant surrendering to it.

They went in separately, as planned—Ines first with the relic-bearers' credentials, hooded, carrying the vessels in a pilgrim's litter-box slung from her shoulder; Marguerite twenty minutes later with the chandler's cart, delivering the Renewal candles themselves, four hundred white tapers for the nave, and no one looked twice at a tradesman doing trade on the holiest errand of the year. The sergeant at the West Door waved the cart through with the glazed boredom of a man who had checked nine hundred carts already and would check nine hundred more.

Inside, the cathedral was a country unto itself.

Candles everywhere, thousands, their light climbing the pillars and drowning in the vaulted dark overhead. Choirs rehearsing in overlapping fragments, one voice starting where another broke off, so that the stone itself seemed to be humming. Nobles in furs drifting through the transept like weather. And beneath it all, under the incense and the wax and the cold breath of ten thousand bodies, Marguerite heard the other congregation: the dead in the crypt below, generations of them, murmuring in their sleep of stone. He had learned, in the ossuary, to navigate by that sound. Now it rose around him like a tide coming in, and somewhere far beneath it, deeper than the crypt, deeper than the foundations, something else stirred and turned over in its long sleep and *noticed* him.

The mark on his forearm woke.

It had been quiet for days—since the palace, since the spirit had let him walk away with its brand burning under his skin like a leash it could pull whenever it pleased. Now it flared, sudden and specific, a hot wire drawn from wrist to elbow, and Marguerite stopped so abruptly that the chandler behind him swore and steered the cart around.

*There you are,* said something that was not a voice, in the place where voices live. *Come to watch me eat.*

He kept walking. He set his face into the mild blankness of a tired workman and kept walking, one foot, then the other, past the pillar where Ines stood in her pilgrim's hood pretending to pray, past the roped-off aisle where the relic-bearers were assembling in their order of march, past a cluster of Hunt officers in ceremonial dress who stood down and unarmed and restless as dogs forbidden to bark. His arm burned. He did not scratch it. He did not run. He had learned, in the drowned chapel, the difference between hearing the dead and being heard by them, and right now he was being heard, and the only defense was to be nothing, nobody, a man with candles to deliver.

But as he turned toward the sacristy with his cart, the pressure lifted—slowly, almost lazily, the way a cat releases a caught bird to play with it longer—and in the space it left, the dead in the crypt shifted, and one voice near the top of the tide came clear for half a heartbeat:

*...the soldier... tell him the soldier said...*

Then the choir swelled, and it was gone.

Marguerite stood very still among his candles. He knew that voice. He had carried it out of an alley with blood soaking his sleeves—the royal soldier, the dying man, the oath-bone pressed into his hands with the last strength in a failing body. *The king is not the king.* The soldier had been buried weeks ago in a pauper's trench outside the city walls.

And yet here he was, in the cathedral crypt, on Renewal Eve, saying—what? *Tell him the soldier said*—said *what?*

"Master carver." A sacristan, brisk, impatient, holding out his hands for the candle boxes. "You can leave those. The bearers will distribute them."

"Of course," Marguerite said, and handed over the Renewal light of the realm, and walked back out into the crowd with his arm still humming and a dead man's unfinished sentence lodged in his ear like a splinter.

* * *

They met at dusk in the shadow of the buttresses, where the relic-bearers gathered to don their robes, and for a few minutes they simply stood together, two figures among dozens, invisible, saying nothing.

Ines broke the silence first. "It knows you're here."

"It's known since the palace. It let me go on purpose." He flexed his marked arm inside the sleeve. "It wants me close. I think—" He hesitated. "I think it thinks I'm already beaten. Or that I'm useful. It offered me things, Ines. It knew what I wanted before I did."

"That's what it does. That's how it got forty kings." She adjusted the litter-box strap on her shoulder, and her hand, passing, touched his—briefly, accidentally, or in the way that nothing between them had been accidental for a long time. "When it speaks through him tomorrow, whatever it shows you, whatever it promises—it will be tailored. Made to fit you perfectly. Remember that perfection is the tell. Nothing true fits that well."

"Nothing true fits that well," he repeated, and filed it away next to the soldier's unfinished sentence, in the growing inventory of things he meant to survive long enough to understand.

Across the square, the great bells began their rehearsal—a single deep note, struck and held, struck and held, testing the hour mechanism for midnight. The crowd hushed instinctively at the first strike, ten thousand faces turning toward the towers, and in that hush Marguerite felt the whole city lean, the living and the dead together, all of it balanced on the edge of a single night.

"One more thing," Ines said quietly. She was looking at the cathedral doors, not at him, and her voice had the careful flatness she used when feeling was too dangerous to load into words. "Whatever happens at that altar. Whatever the rite asks. We finish it. Both of us. Sworn?"

He looked at her profile against the candlelight—at the scar, the grey, the mouth that had said his name in anger and in fear and once, only once, by the fire, in something softer that he had replayed every night since—and he thought: *you have no idea what I've sworn already.*

"Sworn," he said.

The bells struck again. Somewhere below their feet, the dead began, very softly, to sing.
