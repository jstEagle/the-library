## 28. Infiltrating the Procession

The relic-bearers' robes smelled of camphor and old incense, and they were a size too small for Marguerite in the shoulders. He tugged the hood forward as the seventh bell rang across Vellamark, and tried to breathe like a man who had never once broken the law.

"Stop fidgeting," Serane murmured beside him. She wore the grey of a lesser order, her captain's bearing folded away beneath a stoop she'd practiced all morning. Even hunched, even anonymous, she moved like a blade in a cloth sheath.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You've adjusted your hood eleven times since the bell. I counted." She didn't look at him. None of them looked at each other; that was part of it. Relic-bearers walked alone with their burdens, eyes down, minds on holy things. "Ines is two rows ahead. Watch her back, not your hood."

He found Ines by the set of her shoulders—straighter than any penitent's, though she'd argued for an hour that she could make herself small. She carried the reliquary box on a padded yoke, one of forty such bearers winding down the Processional Way toward the Cathedral of the Renewal, each box holding something old and officially sacred: a saint's fingerbone, a martyr's kneecap, splinters of the First Oak. Theirs held three bone-vessels and the oath-bone wrapped in linen against Marguerite's chest, and if anyone opened it, they would all burn before the bells finished ringing.

The city had turned out for the Renewal the way water turns out of a burst dam. Every window along the Way hung with white banners. Children perched on fathers' shoulders. Roasted chestnuts and mulled wine perfumed the cold air, and above it all hung the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but meant to. The Hunt stood down for the ceremony—that had been law since Aldric's grandfather—and their absence was its own kind of presence. Marguerite kept catching himself looking for grey cloaks in the crowd, finding none, and not believing it.

"They're not gone," Serane said quietly, reading him. "They're inside already. Securing the cathedral. My lieutenants will be at every door." Her jaw tightened around the word *my*. "Which means I know exactly which doors to avoid, and so do you."

"And the king?"

"You'll see him soon enough." A pause. "Pray you see the man and not the thing wearing him."

The Processional Way narrowed at the Fountain of the Ninefold Blessing—the same fountain where, months ago and a lifetime ago, criers had read the names of the condemned while ash from the apothecary's shop drifted like dirty snow. Marguerite had stood in this exact spot with his hands shaking. Now he stood here again in stolen vestments with outlawed relics strapped to his chest, and his hands were steady, and somehow that frightened him more.

The column of bearers funneled through the fountain square, past the great bronze basin where coins glittered under ice skin. A priest-warden stood at the archway beyond, checking bearer after bearer with a bored flick of the eyes. Not searching. Counting. The Renewal was ritual, and ritual did not tolerate surprises, which was precisely why Ines had chosen it.

"Names?" the warden asked.

Serane answered for them, low and flat, reciting the three dead bearers whose places they'd bought from a man in the Tanner's Quarter who sold everything except remorse. The warden checked his ledger, waved them through, and never saw their faces at all. That was the beauty of ritual, Marguerite thought. It made people stop looking.

Then they were inside the archway, and the cathedral rose before them.

He had seen it his whole life from a distance—the spire like a bone needle stitching sky to earth—but never from the Processional Way itself, never as one of the anointed. Up close it was less a building than a ribcage, vaulted and vast, its pale stone quarried from the same cliffs as whale-fall bone. His fingers itched. Stone remembered too, in its slow way. This place remembered four hundred years of coronations, and beneath the incense he could almost hear it: a low murmur like surf, the accumulated weight of ten thousand oaths sworn and re-sworn under this roof.

*Careful,* said a voice that wasn't a voice, deep in the oath-bone against his chest. Or perhaps deep in himself. Lately he couldn't always tell.

The bearers filed down the side aisle to the reliquary alcoves, setting their burdens into niches carved with saints' names. Ines placed theirs last, in the shadowed niche of Saint Verany the Forgotten—a deliberate choice; forgotten saints got no visitors. Her hands were steady as she laid the box in, and only Marguerite, who knew her hands now the way he knew his own chisels, saw the tremor when she withdrew them.

"It's done," she breathed, passing him without turning her head. "Vessels are placed. Now we wait for the ceremony, and we pray the spirit doesn't look too closely at what it's about to eat."

Marguerite took his position among the bearers along the north wall, head bowed, hands folded. Serane melted toward the guard corridor she'd walked a hundred times, a ghost returning to haunt its own house.

And then, as the great doors boomed open for the nobility, the mark on Marguerite's forearm woke up.

It began as warmth. He'd been carrying the spirit's brand since the audience—the black sigil it had pressed into his skin like a seal into wax, a hunter's tag, a promise. Usually it slept, a dull ache he could ignore. But here, in this house of oaths, with the Renewal hours away and the crown-spirit gathering itself inside the king like a fist closing, the mark *opened*.

Heat lanced up his arm. He bit the inside of his cheek and kept his head bowed. Under his sleeve, the sigil was glowing faintly through the fabric—not light exactly, more a darkening, the way a bruise surfaces. Around him, forty relic-bearers swayed through the opening liturgy, and not one of them glanced at the man whose arm was burning like a brand iron.

*I know you're here,* the mark said. Not in words. In pressure. In the sensation of a thumb pressing slowly, exploratively, against the inside of his skull. *Little carver. Little thief of voices.*

Marguerite breathed. In through the nose, out through the teeth, the way Ines had taught him on the road when the drowned dead got loud. The spirit couldn't find him—not precisely, not yet. It was stretched thin, poured through a king's crumbling mind and a cathedral full of expectant faith, and its attention was a searchlight sweeping a field. He had to be a stone when it passed over him. A bone. An unremarkable thing.

He thought about whalebone. About the small piece he'd saved from his own hearth-fire without knowing why. He made himself smooth and dense and silent, and the searchlight swept past—

—and caught, at the edge, like a fishhook in a sleeve.

*There you are.*

Cold flooded him from scalp to sole. Across the nave, beneath the rising anthem of the choir, King Aldric sat enthroned on the dais in cloth-of-gold, and though the distance was fifty yards and a thousand candles, the king's head turned. Slowly. Mechanically. The crowned face swung toward the north wall like a weathervane finding wind, and the eyes—lucid eyes, Marguerite would have sworn they were lucid, a man drowning behind glass—fixed on the row of bowed relic-bearers and traveled down it, one by one, until they reached him.

The king smiled.

It was Aldric's smile, worn like a borrowed coat, and underneath it something else smiled too, something with far too many years in it. The monarch's lips moved, shaping words no one could hear at that distance, and Marguerite's blood went to ice because he could hear them anyway, delivered directly into the marrow of his burning arm:

*Come to the altar, little carver. I've left a seat for you.*

The king turned back to the ceremony. The anthem swelled. Candles guttered in a draft no window admitted.

Marguerite discovered he had stopped breathing, and started again, carefully, the way a man moves when he's standing on lake ice and has just heard it speak.

* * *

The hours between the bearers' dismissal and the Renewal itself were the longest of Marguerite's life, and he had once spent a night listening to a chapel full of the drowned.

They regrouped in the ambulatory behind Saint Verany's niche, three shadows among pillars, speaking in the sub-vocal shorthand they'd drilled on the road. Ines laid out the sequence one final time, tracing it on the flagstones with one finger: vessels retrieved during the Litany of Remembrance, when every eye would be on the king; positions taken at the altar rail during the Renewal Oath; the rite begun the moment the crown touched the king's brow—the instant of greatest binding, and therefore greatest vulnerability.

"And if it goes wrong?" Serane asked.

"Then it goes wrong loudly," Ines said, "and you get us out, or die trying, or both. Same as the inn."

"That was different. At the inn I only lost you." Serane's mouth twisted. "Now I'm losing a career, a name, and quite possibly a neck, and I find I mind less than I should. That's your fault, both of you."

"We're very corrupting," Marguerite said.

The joke landed wrong and they all heard it land wrong, because the mark on his arm still throbbed, and because none of them had slept, and because tomorrow—today, now, within hours—one of three things would happen: a king freed, three fugitives dead, or a kingdom swallowed from the inside by a hunger older than its dynasty.

Ines looked at him for a long moment. In the pillar-shadow her face was half candlelight, half dark, and he thought—not for the first time, but with new urgency—that whatever happened at that altar, he wanted the shape of her face stored somewhere the spirit couldn't reach. He wanted to carve it later, from memory, in the good whalebone, and get the stubborn line of her jaw exactly right.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. Memorizing."

Her expression flickered—something warm and alarmed at once—and then the bells changed, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

The Renewal began at dusk, when the cathedral's thousand candles were lit and the winter dark pressed against the windows like a crowd at a door.

The Litany of Remembrance came first: a slow procession of the high clergy bearing the relics of the realm's founding, each named aloud in a voice like tolling stone. This was their moment. While the archdeacon intoned the name of the First Oak and four hundred throats sang the response, Ines slipped from the ambulatory and lifted their box from Saint Verany's niche as smoothly as a pickpocket working a festival crowd. She divided the vessels—two to Marguerite, sewn into the lining of his robe over the oath-bone, one to herself—and by the time the response ended, three relic-bearers were again bowing innocently along the walls, now armed with the means to unmake a god.

Marguerite's mark had gone quiet. Too quiet. It lay against his arm like a held breath, and he understood: the spirit knew he was here, had known since the king's smile, and had chosen not to strike. *Let him come,* the silence said. *I want him close.*

The Renewal Oath was announced. The nobility rose in a rustle of silk and fur. Down the nave came the crown of Vellamark on its cushion of white velvet, borne by the Lord Chamberlain, followed by King Aldric descending from his throne to kneel at the altar.

And Marguerite, watching from thirty feet away, saw what the whole adoring court could not—or rather, saw what they saw and refused to understand. The king moved beautifully. That was the horror of it. Whatever wore Aldric had perfected him, smoothed every tremor, straightened every stoop, and the result was a monarch so composed he seemed less a man than a portrait of one, rendered by an artist who had never actually met a living face. When he knelt, he knelt like a marionette settling onto its strings.

*Almost time.* Ines drifted to his side, her lips barely moving. "On the crowning. Don't look at me, don't look at the altar. Just walk when I walk."

"I remember the plan."

"You remember nothing lately," she whispered, and there was an ache in it she probably didn't intend, and he had no time to answer because the archbishop lifted the crown.

The cathedral fell silent. Four hundred nobles, fifty clergy, a thousand commoners packed into the transept galleries—all of them holding one breath between them. Snow began outside the high windows, fat flakes drifting past the glass like slow white petals, and inside, the archbishop raised the crown of Vellamark over the kneeling king and spoke the ancient words:

*"As the year dies and is reborn, so swears the king—"*

Marguerite felt it happen. Felt the whole cathedral's attention narrow to a single point, felt ten thousand gathered oaths lean inward like wheat toward sun, felt the crown descend inch by inch toward the gold hair of a hollow man—and felt, beneath his own skin, the mark ignite from ache to agony to something beyond pain, a door swinging wide, because this was the moment the spirit had been waiting for, the annual feast, the re-swearing that fed it for another year—

The crown touched the king's brow.

Aldric screamed.

It was not a kingly sound. It was not a human sound. It was the sound of a man feeling, for one naked instant, the full weight of the thing inside him—and the scream cracked across the cathedral like ice splitting on a pond, and every candle flame bent sideways though no door had opened, and in the ringing silence that followed, the king rose to his feet.

Too smoothly. Far too smoothly. And when he lifted his face to his assembled kingdom, the smile he wore was the one from the north wall, the borrowed coat, the thing beneath it grinning through.

*"My people,"* the king said, and the voice was Aldric's voice pitched slightly wrong, like a song played a half-tone flat, *"how long you have waited. How patient you have been."*

Beside Marguerite, Ines went rigid. On his arm, the mark blazed like a coal. And in the galleries above, four hundred of the most powerful people in Vellamark watched their king with expressions of serene, glassy delight, nodding along to words that had begun to curdle, and Marguerite understood with sickening clarity that the spirit had decided to stop pretending—that the Renewal was not its vulnerability at all, but its coronation, and they were all of them, the whole realm, standing inside its open mouth.

Ines's hand found his wrist in the dark of the pillar's shadow. Squeezed once.

"Now," she breathed. "God help us. *Now.*"
