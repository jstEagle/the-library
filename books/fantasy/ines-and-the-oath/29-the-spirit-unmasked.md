## 29. The Spirit Unmasked

The cathedral of Saint Vellum had been built to hold ten thousand voices, and on the morning of the Renewal it held every one of them.

Marguerite stood among the relic-bearers in the third rank of the procession, close enough to the altar that he could see the king's face, far enough that his borrowed vestments might pass a casual glance. The disguise was good. Ines had seen to that—she'd dressed him herself, her fingers steady at his collar, her eyes saying what her voice could not: *if this goes wrong, run.*

He was not going to run.

The incense was heavy, a resinous sweetness that sat at the back of the throat like a held breath. Candles by the thousand turned the vaulted dark above into a field of small trembling stars. And beneath it all, under the singing and the shuffling and the rustle of ten thousand bodies, Marguerite heard the other thing.

He had heard it since he crossed the threshold. A hum in the stones. Not the dead—the cathedral dead were quiet, respectful, murmuring their old prayers in the marrow of the walls—but something else. Something vast and patient and *fed*, coiled around the throne like a serpent grown fat on forty years of kings.

It knew he was there. His marked skin had burned since dawn, a slow brand spreading from his breastbone outward, and now, as the choir swelled toward the moment of the oath, the burn sharpened into something almost sweet. Almost welcoming.

*Come closer,* the burning said. *I have been waiting for you, carver.*

Marguerite fixed his eyes on the back of the relic-bearer ahead of him and breathed through his teeth.

Ahead, King Aldric knelt at the altar steps in cloth-of-gold, the crown of Vellamark lifted above him by the Archkeeper's ceremonial pincers. The crown was old iron and older bone, and Marguerite could hear it too—a thin, continuous keening, like wind through a ribcage. The oath-bone in the satchel against his chest answered it, vibrating faintly, two instruments tuned to the same terrible note.

Beside him, disguised as a relic-bearer of the second rank, Ines went very still.

"He's fighting it," she breathed. It wasn't meant for him; it was meant for her own courage. "Look at his hands."

Marguerite looked. The king's hands, spread flat on the altar steps in submission, were trembling. Not with age or awe. With resistance. The fingers curled once, twice, as if gripping an invisible edge—as if some part of Aldric, deep down where the spirit had not yet finished its meal, was clawing at the walls of himself.

*Hold on,* Marguerite thought, absurdly, uselessly. *We're here. Hold on.*

The Archkeeper began the Litany of Renewal. Ten thousand voices took it up, the ancient call-and-response that bound king to crown and crown to kingdom, and Marguerite felt the spirit lean into the words like a drunkard leaning into a doorway. Every syllable fed it. Every vow renewed the channels it had spent four decades carving through Aldric's mind.

Ines's hand found his sleeve. "When the crown touches his head," she whispered, "the binding opens. That's our moment. We move for the altar on the third response."

"The guards—"

"Serane's people are on the north doors. She'll manage the rest." A pause. "She'd better."

Below them, the Archkeeper lowered the crown.

And the world broke.

* * *

Later, Marguerite would try to describe it and fail, because what happened next did not happen in any order the mind could keep.

The crown touched iron to brow. The Litany reached its peak. And the spirit stopped pretending.

It did not seize the king so much as *pour itself forward*, the way water finds a hole—and Aldric's body straightened from its kneel with a smoothness no kneeling man should possess, joints unfolding like something remembering a different shape. He rose. He stood taller than he had stood in years. And when he opened his eyes, they were not the clouded, flickering eyes of a man being eaten from within.

They were gold. Both of them, iris and sclera alike, molten and calm, and they swept the cathedral the way a landlord surveys a property.

The choir faltered. One voice, then ten, then the whole soaring structure of the Litany collapsing into confused silence.

King Aldric smiled.

"My children," he said, and his voice carried to every corner without effort, without echo, as if the stones themselves were repeating it. "You have sworn to me forty times. Today I will swear to you—truly—for the first time."

Marguerite's blood went cold. Beside him, Ines whispered, "No. No, no—he's not supposed to speak until—"

"Kneel," the king said gently.

The word moved through the cathedral like a physical thing. Marguerite watched it travel—saw the Grand Duke of Harrowmere sway, stiffen, and drop to one knee with a face gone smooth as river stone. Saw the merchant princes fold. Saw the Archkeeper himself, crown still in his pincers, sink down with the blank serenity of a man in a dream. One after another after another, ten thousand wills guttering out like candles pinched between wet fingers, until the only people left standing in the cathedral of Saint Vellum were three relic-bearers who did not belong there, a handful of guards whose oaths ran deeper than compulsion, and the thing wearing the king.

"What is it doing?" Marguerite hissed.

"It's not compelling them." Ines's face had gone the color of tallow. "Compulsion breaks. I've seen it break. This is—it's *taking*. It's hollowing them the way it hollowed—" She stopped. Her hand closed hard on his wrist. "Marguerite. It's making vessels. Hundreds of them. This is what it does at every Renewal, this is how it holds the court, and we never—we never understood—"

The golden eyes found them.

Across two hundred feet of marble and candlelight, across ten thousand bowed heads, the thing in the king looked directly at the third rank of relic-bearers and smiled wider.

"There you are," it said fondly. "My little thieves. My bone-whisperer and my broken keeper. Did you truly think I would not know my own rite when it walked into my house?"

Every bowed head in the cathedral turned.

Not the people—the heads alone, rotating with mechanical unity, ten thousand faces fixing on Marguerite and Ines with identical blank devotion, and the sound that came out of them was not a gasp but a single harmonized breath, drawn in unison, held, released.

"*Run,*" Ines said, and shoved him.

* * *

They ran for the north transept, and the cathedral woke around them like a body discovering pain.

The compelled nobility rose—not quickly, nothing so human as quick, but with the terrible coordinated smoothness of puppets on a single string—and began to walk. Not chase. *Walk.* Ten thousand people converging with perfect patience, closing aisles, sealing exits, filling the nave wall to wall with serene upturned faces.

"Ines!" Marguerite caught her arm and hauled her left, away from the transept, toward the ambulatory. "North door's cut off—"

"I can see that!"

"The ossuary—"

"Too far—"

Then the bells began.

Not the cathedral bells. The alarm bells of the palace guard, and through the great south doors burst the loyalist guard—two hundred men in royal blue, swords drawn, come to defend their king from the intruders who had dared disrupt the Renewal. Their captain's voice rang out over the chaos: "Seize the relic-bearers! Protect His Majesty!"

And between the loyalists and the altar, drawing her sword in a single bright arc, stood Captain Serane Voss.

* * *

Serane had known, from the moment the king's eyes turned gold, exactly which side of this she was standing on.

It was a strange relief, after months of ledger-entries and buried evidence and orders she'd swallowed like stones. There was no more ambiguity. There was only the thing on the altar wearing her sovereign's face, and the two hundred men behind her who hadn't yet understood what they were looking at.

"Lieutenant Corvane!" she shouted. "Look at the king! *Look at him!*"

Corvane looked. To his credit, he hesitated—two full seconds, his sword point wavering, his eyes moving from the molten-gold gaze to the stone-smooth faces of the assembled dukes and back again. Two seconds was longer than most men would have managed.

Then the king pointed one jeweled finger at Serane and said, in a voice like warm honey poured over a blade: "That woman is a traitor. She has conspired with sorcerers to murder your monarch. Kill her, and be rewarded."

The compulsion hit the loyalists like a wave. Serane saw it take them—their faces smoothing, their eyes glazing, two hundred trained soldiers turning as one toward her with the unhurried certainty of tide.

"Corvane," she said quietly. "Fight it."

For one heartbeat, Lieutenant Corvane fought it. She saw it happening behind his eyes, saw his jaw clench, his sword arm shake, his mouth form the words *Captain, I can't—I can't—*

Then his face went smooth, and he came at her with his sword.

After that there was no time for grief. There was steel, and marble, and the endless patient press of bodies that did not tire and did not fear and did not stop. Serane gave ground step by step, using the pillars, using the ranks of frozen nobility as cover the compelled guards wouldn't strike through, cutting hamstrings and throats with the economy of a woman who had hunted dangerous things for six years and never once enjoyed it.

"Voss!" A whistle from the west gallery. Three of her own—Hale, Brandt, and young Ostrey, the ones she'd briefed at dawn, the ones whose oaths she'd re-sworn personally with the old words, the words from before the Order fell. They came down the gallery stairs in a wedge, breaking through to her.

"How many?" she shouted.

"Thirty fighting free! The rest are taken!"

"Then thirty is an army. Form on me!"

They formed on her. And Captain Serane Voss, traitor to a crown that had never deserved her, began the work of holding the line.

* * *

Through the ambulatory, past the chapel of the Nine Sorrows, over the backs of slow-turning congregants who grasped at their cloaks with gentle, terrible hands—Marguerite ran with the satchel bouncing against his ribs and Ines's grip locked on his wrist, and everywhere he heard the dead of the cathedral screaming.

That was the worst of it. Not the puppets, not the golden eyes. The dead. The thousands interred in the walls and floors of Saint Vellum, kings and archkeepers and nameless choristers, and every one of them awake now, shrieking through the stone: *it is loose, it is loose, it wears him, it wears us all—*

"They're terrified," he gasped. "Ines, the dead in these walls—they've been watching it feed for forty years and they're *terrified*—"

"Then use them!" She wrenched him around a corner. Ahead, the ambulatory forked—left to the sacristy, right to the stair down. "You hear the dead? Then make them useful! Give me a path!"

He closed his eyes for half a stride, half a prayer, and listened.

Beneath the terror, beneath the forty years of muffled witness, the cathedral dead still knew their house. They knew which stones were thin, which doors were barred, which passages the living had forgotten. And they wanted, with a ferocity that shook him, to hurt the thing that had made their resting place a feeding pen.

*Left,* they said, a hundred voices braided into one. *Left and down. The old way. We will hold the doors.*

"Left and down!" He pulled her. "The dead are helping us—there's a passage under the reliquary—"

They took the left fork. Behind them, the double doors of the ambulatory groaned, then boomed, then held—held against a pressure on the other side that made the hinges scream, though nothing visible struck them. The dead, keeping their word.

The stair was narrow, dark, smelled of centuries. Halfway down, Ines grabbed his shoulder.

"Marguerite. Stop. Listen."

He listened. Below them, in the dark, something was breathing. Many somethings. The slow, synchronized respiration of people standing very still in a confined space.

"The crypt garrison," Ines whispered. "Ceremonial honor guard. Twelve men. If they've been taken—"

"If they've been taken, we're dead in this stairwell." Marguerite's heart slammed. His skin burned so badly now that he could hardly breathe. And through the burn, through the fear, the spirit's voice slid into his mind like a hand into a glove.

*Carver,* it said, almost tenderly. *Why do you run? I told you I would give you everything. I showed you her. I showed you the life you could have had if you had never hidden. I am showing it to you still—look—*

And he saw it. Saw it vivid as sunlight: a workshop with open shutters, morning light, Ines at the bench beside him learning the grain of whalebone, laughing at something he'd said. Years of it. Decades. A whole warm life laid out before him like a banquet, and all he had to do was stop. Put down the satchel. Walk up the stairs and kneel.

"No," Marguerite said aloud, to the dark, to the thing, to himself. "That life isn't real. You don't make things. You only eat them."

*All love is eaten eventually,* the spirit said. *I merely skip the waiting.*

"Marguerite." Ines's whisper cut through. Her hand found his in the dark and gripped it, hard, real, callused, alive. "Whatever it's showing you—it lies with true things. That's what makes it strong. Don't drink."

He squeezed her hand once. Then he drew his carving knife—the small one, the honest one—and led her down into the dark.

* * *

The twelve men of the crypt garrison stood in a perfect ring around the passage door, swords drawn, faces smooth.

Twelve against two, in a corridor barely wide enough for three.

Ines exhaled slowly. "Well," she said. "I always assumed I'd die somewhere with better lighting."

But Marguerite was already stepping forward, satchel open, and what he drew out was not a weapon. It was the oath-bone.

The relic screamed the moment it met open air—that layered chorus of every voice it had ever held, the soldier's dying whisper loudest of all: *the king is not the king, the king is not the king*—and the sound rolled down the crypt corridor like a shockwave.

The twelve guards staggered. Four of them dropped their swords outright, clutching their ears, and in two of those four faces the smoothness cracked—cracked like ice on a thawing pond—and underneath it, blinking, gasping, human, were men waking from a dream they could not remember.

"It fights compulsion," Marguerite breathed. "The old oath—it *fights* it—"

"Then give them more!" Ines was already moving, kicking a fallen sword aside, pulling the nearest waking guard upright by his collar. "Soldier! Look at me. Oath-keeper Ines Vale, Order of the Crown, and I am telling you your king is possessed and your captain is holding the north line. Choose."

The guard stared at her. At the bone. At the dark behind her where his eleven brothers stood swaying between two masters.

"Voss," he rasped. "Captain Voss said—she said to trust the bearers. She said whatever happens at the ceremony—" His jaw set. He bent, picked up his sword, and turned to face the eight men still smooth-faced behind him. "Sergeant Dree. I'm sorry about this."

What followed was short, brutal, and nothing like a battle—eight compelled men do not fight, they only advance, and seven waking men with a cause can hold a corridor forever. By the time it ended, the crypt garrison was five wounded, two dead, and five free, and the passage door stood open onto the undercroft stairs that climbed toward the altar's shadow.

Marguerite stood shaking in the aftermath, the oath-bone keening in his hands, and looked at Ines.

"This is it," he said. "The binding opens at the altar. The vessels, the rite—you anchor, I channel. Everything we planned."

"Yes."

"And the spirit knows we're coming. It's been inviting us."

"Yes." Ines drew a long breath, and for a moment—just a moment—her composure slipped, and he saw underneath it the disgraced girl who had forged this binding at nineteen and spent twenty years paying for it. "Then we stop planning," she said, "and we go."

Above them, muffled through sixty feet of stone, the bells of the cathedral began to swing again—all of them at once, wild and discordant, and threaded through the bronze clangor came a sound that froze the blood of everyone in the crypt:

Ten thousand voices, singing in perfect unison. A hymn no living congregation knew. A welcome.

The spirit was singing them home.

Marguerite took the first stair.
