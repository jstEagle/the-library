## 30. The Rite Begins

The cathedral shook with the spirit's voice, and Marguerite ran toward it.

Chaos poured down the nave in both directions—nobles fleeing, guards shouting, a censer swinging loose on its chain and scattering coals across the flagstones like dying stars. Somewhere behind him Serane was holding the west doors with a handful of turncoat soldiers and a voice like a drawn blade: *"Hold this line! No one enters, no one leaves!"* He did not look back. Looking back was how people died in stories, and he had no intention of becoming one.

Ines was already at the altar.

She had fought her way through the procession ahead of him, relic-bearer's robes torn from her shoulder, her hair coming loose in dark ribbons, and she stood now before the high altar with the three bone-vessels laid out in a triangle at her feet—the drowned chapel's salt-white cradle, the hermit's knucklebone box, the motherhouse vessel that had drunk her blood and deemed her worthy. They glowed faintly, each one, like embers wrapped in linen.

"Ines!" He reached her, grabbed her arm. Her sleeve was wet. Blood, his mind supplied uselessly, cataloguing the wound he couldn't see. Later. Everything later.

"The vessels are set," she said. Her voice was steady in the way a rope bridge is steady—holding, but only just. "But I can't open them alone. The rite needs a counter-song. It needs *voices*, Marguerite. The spirit feeds on memory and obedience. We starve it with everything it isn't."

"And you know this because—"

"Because I helped write the binding." She met his eyes. "Forty years ago, in this room, kneeling where we're standing. I remember the shape of the rite even if I was too young to understand what I was sealing. The vessels are anchors. But anchors need a tide." She pressed her palm flat against his chest, over his heart, over the mark the spirit had burned into his skin two nights ago—a brand shaped like an open eye that had been aching since he entered the cathedral. "You're the tide. You've always been the tide."

Above the altar, King Aldric hung in the air.

That was the only word for it. The old man's body floated three feet above the consecrated stone, arms slack, crown blazing white-hot on his brow, and around him the air rippled like heat off a forge. His face was wrong—not possessed, not twisted, but *smoothed*, as if something were wearing him the way a hand wears a glove, and the glove was beginning to fit too well. When he spoke, his mouth moved out of time with the words.

"*Little carver.*" The voice came from everywhere—from the pillars, from the bones of the saints beneath the floor, from Marguerite's own marked skin. "*I offered you everything. Your heart's desire, walking beside you. And still you crawl back to me.*"

"You offered me a lie," Marguerite said.

"I offered you the only thing mortals ever want. A past worth keeping." The king's head turned with terrible slowness. "Look at your oath-keeper. Look at her hands shaking. She knows what the rite costs. Ask her why she didn't tell you."

"Don't listen." Ines's grip tightened on his wrist. "It speaks in truths bent into knives. That's all it is. That's all it's ever been."

Marguerite looked at her—at the woman whose name he'd known for one season, whose laugh he knew, whose nightmares he knew, whose favorite way to hold a cup he knew—and felt the eye-shaped brand pulse once, hungrily. *Ask her,* the spirit whispered inside his skull. *Ask her what she held back.*

"I will ask her," he said aloud, to the spirit, to Ines, to himself. "After. Whatever it is—I'll hear it after. Right now I have work to do."

Something flickered across Ines's face—grief and gratitude tangled so tightly they were indistinguishable. Then she nodded, once, and turned to the vessels.

"How do I start?"

"You don't start." She knelt at the triangle's southern point and drew a bone-handled knife from her belt. "You *open*. Like a door. Like a window in winter." She cut her palm without flinching and pressed the blood to the first vessel. "The drowned chapel answers to water and grief. The hermit's vessel answers to mercy. The motherhouse answers to blood and worthiness. But none of them sing by themselves. They need a voice that can hear the dead." She looked up at him, blood dripping between her fingers. "They need yours."

* * *

He had heard voices through bone since he was nine years old.

He had spent ten years pretending he hadn't. Ten years carving charms for grieving widows and slipping their husbands' last words into the grain of the whalebone like contraband, ten years flinching from every carved surface in Vellamark because the dead clung to their likenesses the way ivy clung to walls. He had thought of his gift as a sickness. A burden. A crime.

Kneeling at the altar of the cathedral of Vellamark, with a devouring god wearing his king above him and the Hunt fighting for its life at the west doors, Marguerite Coldharbour finally understood what his gift actually was.

It was a door. And tonight, every dead soul he had ever listened to was on the other side of it, waiting.

He put his hands flat on the cold stone and opened.

It hit him like the flooded chapel had hit him—like drowning in reverse, like being pulled up through black water into light—but a hundredfold, a thousandfold. The drowned dead came first, hundreds of them, the congregation who had gone down with their chapel when the river took the valley, and they were singing. They had been singing for forty years in the dark and the silt, and no one had heard them until now, and their song was low and patient and vast as the riverbed itself.

Then the hermit—old Corvess, who had soured the binding and spent a lifetime paying for it, whose last words to Ines had been a plea for forgiveness she couldn't give. His voice was small and dry and steady as a candle flame: *I am sorry. I am sorry. Let my vessel be of use. Let my mistake be unmade.*

Then the soldier. The gut-wounded royal soldier who had crawled through the snow to die at Marguerite's hiding place, pressing the oath-bone into his hands with the last of his strength. *The king is not the king. The king is not the king.* Except now, in the chorus, the words resolved into what they had always been underneath: *Save him. Please. Save him. He was kind to me once. Save him.*

And then—every voice he had ever heard through every piece he had ever carved. The widow's husband saying *tell her the garden will keep*. The child's charm whispering a lullaby its mother had sung over the grave. The old ferryman, the young bride, the nameless bones in the market reliquary, the paupers in the potter's field, the dead of Vellamark layered generation upon generation like rings in a tree. All of them. All of them, rising.

Marguerite opened his mouth and gave them his voice to pour through.

What came out was not a sound he could have made alone. It was a counter-song built from forty years of stolen memories and ten years of listening—a great slow chord of grief and love and unfinished business, rising through the cathedral's pillars, meeting the spirit's voice in the air above the altar and *pushing against it*. Where the spirit's presence was smoothness, the song was texture. Where it was obedience, the song was every stubborn, contradictory, unquiet soul who had ever refused to be forgotten.

The floating king convulsed.

"*Stop.*" The spirit's voice cracked like ice on a pond. "*You are one man. I am a kingdom's devotion. Forty years of oaths. Forty years of kneeling. You cannot—*"

"He's not one man," Ines shouted over the roar, both hands pressed to the second vessel now, her blood soaking into the ancient bone. "He never was! That's the whole point of him!"

* * *

Later, Marguerite would remember it in fragments, the way you remember a storm.

He remembered the vessels lighting one by one—the drowned chapel's cradle glowing blue as river-water, the hermit's box glowing gold as lamplight, the motherhouse vessel burning red as the blood that had opened it—and the triangle of light they made on the altar floor, a net woven from three kinds of sorrow.

He remembered Ines moving between the anchor points, chanting the old words of the binding in reverse, her voice raw by the third repetition, her wounded hand leaving prints on the stone like a signature written again and again: *I was there when you were bound. I unbind you now. I was there when you were bound. I unbind you now.*

He remembered the spirit fighting back. Of course it fought back. It reached into him with fingers made of memory, showing him his mother's kitchen, the smell of bread, the first piece he'd ever carved, offering him his whole life back if he would only stop, stop, *stop*—and he sang through it, because the dead at his back were singing through it too, and grief, he discovered, is the one thing that cannot be bribed.

He remembered the west doors bursting, remembered loyalist guards in white-and-gold flooding up the nave with drawn steel, remembered Serane's voice cutting through everything—"They're mine! They answer to *me*!"—remembered the clash of blades somewhere far away, at the edge of the world, while he knelt in the center of it and sang.

He remembered Aldric.

For one moment—one clear, crystalline moment in the middle of the maelstrom—the smoothing left the king's face. The old man looked down at Marguerite from where he hung, and his eyes were his own, terrified and exhausted and ninety percent gone, and his mouth moved in time with his words for the first time since the ceremony began.

"*Carver,*" the king rasped. "*Whatever it takes. Whatever it costs you. Don't stop.*"

Then the spirit slammed back down over him like a lid, and the king's face smoothed again, and the voice that came out was furious now, genuinely afraid for the first time in forty years.

"*I kept them safe!*" it shrieked through Aldric's throat, and the pillars wept dust. "*Through plague and war and famine, I kept the throne steady, I kept the realm whole—is that nothing? Is forty years of order NOTHING?*"

"It's not nothing," Marguerite sang, and meant it, and the dead behind his voice meant it too. "But you were meant to guard the king, not wear him. You were meant to hold the crown, not eat the head beneath it. You've forgotten what you were. So we're going to remind you."

He poured more in. The drowned congregation swelled their line. The hermit added his thin thread of penitence. The soldier's plea rose over all of it—*save him, save him, save him*—and Marguerite realized with a jolt that the soldier had been Aldric's man, had loved his king the simple way soldiers love, and that love was in the song now, and the spirit had nowhere to hide from it.

The vessels began to pull.

He felt it through the stone, through his knees, through his teeth—a tidal drag, the triangle of light tightening like a snare drawing closed. The spirit's presence in the air thinned, stretched, shrieked. Aldric's body jerked downward an inch, then another, the invisible hand lowering him toward the altar, toward the vessels, toward the emptied bones waiting to be filled.

"It's working!" Ines cried. "Marguerite—it's working—keep singing—"

He kept singing.

* * *

But the rite was not finished. He understood that the way you understand a storm has another wall behind the wall that hit you.

The counter-song was starving the spirit, yes. The vessels were pulling it down, yes. But a binding this old, fed for forty years on coronation oaths and renewal rites and the kneeling devotion of an entire realm, did not simply come loose. It had roots. It had grown into the crown the way roots grow into a foundation, and pulling it free would tear something loose with it—something it had been feeding on all along.

Memory. It fed on memory. And the rite required a replacement—a payment, a filling of the hollow it would leave behind.

He had known since the motherhouse. Since the three vessels lay assembled on that ruined table and the old words surfaced in Ines's voice, trembling: *the rite requires a memory of true love, poured willingly into the bones.* They had looked at each other across those bones and neither of them had said the rest aloud, because the rest was unbearable: that they each held such memories, and that only one of them needed to pay, and that each of them had decided, silently, in the privacy of their own heart, that it would be themselves.

Now there was no more privacy. Now there was only the song, and the song was running out of road.

The spirit knew it too. He could feel it grinning through the king's stretched face, feel it gathering itself for a final bargain even as the vessels dragged it down. It had one card left to play, and Marguerite knew with sick certainty exactly which card it was.

"*Carver,*" it said, and its voice had gone soft now, intimate, almost gentle, the voice of something that had watched ten thousand mortals kneel and knew precisely where the cracks in them ran. "*You are pouring yourself empty for a woman you have known one season. One season. I can give you fifty years. I can give you the wedding, the grey hairs, the grandchildren carving at your bench. Put down the song, and I will give you the whole life you are about to throw into these bones—*"

"No," Marguerite said.

"—you don't even know what she withheld from you—"

"*No.*"

"—ask her, carver, ASK HER—"

"I'll ask her *after*," Marguerite roared, and the dead roared with him, hundreds of voices, thousands, the drowned and the buried and the burned and the forgotten all rising through his throat in a single wave of sound that struck the floating king like a hammer—

—and the spirit screamed, and the crown blazed white, and the vessels pulled, and the rite stood balanced on its knife-edge, one breath from done and one breath from failing, and Marguerite understood with perfect clarity what remained.

The song had brought it to the brink. But the hollow had to be filled. The price had to be paid. And there was only one coin in his possession heavy enough to weigh down that scale.

Every moment of Ines. The fountain where they'd argued. The fire where she'd told him about her mentor. Greyford Bridge and her forged seals. The inn where she'd been captured covering his escape. Her hand on his chest five minutes ago, telling him he was the tide.

All of it. Poured willingly into the bones.

He looked at her—at Ines, anchoring the vessels, bleeding, shouting the old words, believing he didn't yet know what the rite would take—and he saw that she had made the same silent decision he had. She was going to pay it herself. She was going to give him up without telling him, the way he was going to give her up without telling her, two people racing each other to the same sacrifice, neither willing to let the other burn alone.

*Not her,* he thought. *Whatever else happens. Not her.*

The spirit felt the decision form in him the way a shark feels blood.

"*There you are,*" it whispered, almost tenderly, as the song crested and the vessels blazed and the king's body sank toward the altar stone. "*There is the coin. Spend it, then, little carver. Spend it all.*"

Marguerite Coldharbor closed his eyes, held the last of the counter-song in his chest like a drawn breath, and began to reach for the memories—the fountain, the fire, the bridge, her name—to pour them out.

Behind him, at the shattered west doors, Serane Voss went down under a loyalist blade, and Ines screamed his name, and the rite hung suspended between salvation and ruin, waiting to learn what a memory of true love was truly worth.
