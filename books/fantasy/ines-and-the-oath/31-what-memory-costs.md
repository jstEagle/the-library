## 31. What Memory Costs

The counter-song held for eleven breaths.

Marguerite counted them the way he'd once counted heartbeats in a dying man's wrist—because counting was all that kept him from screaming. The dead of the drowned chapel sang through him, three hundred voices braided into one long note that ran up his spine and out through his hands and into the vessels at the altar. The hermit's vessel hummed. The chapel's vessel hummed. The third—the motherhouse vessel, still wet with Ines's blood—hummed loudest of all.

And then, on the twelfth breath, the note cracked.

It was not a sound so much as an absence, a hole torn in the middle of the song, and Marguerite felt it like a tooth breaking. The vessels shuddered on their stone rests. Above them, King Aldric hung in the spirit's grip like a puppet with its strings half-cut, his body arched, his mouth open around a voice that was not his own, and the spirit *laughed*—a sound like frost spreading across a window, patient and crystalline and vast.

*You cannot unmake what you cannot replace,* it said, and every candle in the cathedral guttered. *The binding was made of love, little carver. Forty years ago a young woman gave her whole heart to these bones. What will you give?*

Marguerite's knees hit the altar steps. Blood ran from his nose—he could feel it, warm and absurd, dripping onto the white stone—and somewhere behind him Serane was shouting, steel ringing against steel as the loyalist guards broke against her line. He heard Ines's boots on the steps beside him, heard her breath tearing in and out, felt her hand close over his shoulder.

"It's stalling," she said. "Why is it stalling?"

"Because I don't have enough." His voice came out shredded. "The dead aren't enough. It wants something living. Something—" He stopped.

He knew. He had known since the motherhouse, when the vessels had assembled themselves in his hands and shown him the rite whole: *three vessels, one rite, and a memory of true love poured willingly into the bones.* He had known for six days, and he had told her nothing, because telling her would have changed nothing except to break her heart six days early.

Ines's grip tightened. "Something what, Marguerite?"

He looked up at Aldric. At the thing wearing him. The king's face was slick with sweat, and beneath the spirit's possession his eyes were still moving, still human, still *begging*—the same look the soldier in the alley had given him, the same look every dying man gave the person holding their hand. *The king is not the king.* Finish it. Finish it.

The spirit leaned its borrowed face down toward him, and its smile was almost gentle.

*I know your heart,* it whispered, so only he could hear, though the words went through him like cold water through cloth. *I have worn forty years of hearts. I know what you keep folded away like a letter you're afraid to open. Give me that, and this ends. Keep it, and I will wear this king until his bones are dust, and then I will find another crown, and another, and somewhere in that long procession of years I will find her too.*

Marguerite closed his eyes.

He thought about the fire at the camp outside Greyford, how she'd sat with her boots off for the first time since he'd known her, wiggling her toes like a girl, embarrassed when she caught him watching. He thought about the bridge checkpoint, the bored sergeant, the way she'd laughed—actually laughed, helplessly, into her sleeve—when Marguerite had charmed the man with a carved duck of all things, a *duck*, and the sergeant had waved them through grinning like they were old friends. He thought about her hands teaching his the old oath-grips in the ruined chapel, correcting his fingers one by one, and how he had deliberately gotten the fourth grip wrong twice just so she would touch his hand again.

He thought about the whalebone charm he had never been able to explain, the one he'd saved from his own bonfire ten years ago without knowing why, and understood suddenly, completely, that some part of him had carved it for her before he'd ever met her—that the bone had known first, the way bone always did.

"I have it," he said aloud. "I have what it needs."

"Don't." Ines's voice had gone very quiet. She was looking at the vessels, at the rite laid bare in the motherhouse script, and he saw the moment she read the last line—the moment her face went white under the candlelight. "Marguerite. Don't you dare."

"The rite needs a memory of true love," he said. "Poured willingly. All of it."

"Then we find another way—"

"There is no other way. There's a king up there drowning, and there's a spirit that will outlive us both if we fail, and there is no other way." He took her hand off his shoulder and held it, briefly, pressing it flat between both of his, the way he'd press a carving to check its grain. Her fingers were ice. "I'm sorry. I should have told you sooner. But if I'd told you, you'd have tried to stop me, and I couldn't—I couldn't spend our last days fighting you instead of loving you."

"Marguerite—"

"Ines." He smiled at her. He made himself smile, made it steady, because if he didn't she would see what it was costing and she would fight him, and he could not survive being fought by her. Not now. Not with so little left. "Listen to me. Whatever happens in the next minute—whatever you feel leave the room, whatever goes out of your own chest—remember that it was mine to give. You taught me that. An oath-keeper's gift is hers to spend. You said that on the road, the night you told me about the binding. You said a keeper gives what the oath requires."

"That was different," she whispered. "That was *my* oath. This is—this is *us*. This is everything we—"

"Yes," he said. "It is. That's why it has to be this. Nothing less would work. Nothing less would be worth it."

Above them the spirit screamed—not in triumph but in hunger, a vast hollow wanting, and the candles flared blue-white, and Marguerite knew the choice was being taken from him if he waited any longer.

He turned back to the vessels. He put his hands over the third one, the motherhouse vessel, warm with Ines's blood, and he began.

* * *

Memory does not pour like water. It pours like blood from a wound you can't bind—one pulse at a time, each one taking more than you thought you had.

The first thing to go was small, and that was the cruelest part. The duck. Just the duck—the sergeant's red face, Ines's laughter muffled in her sleeve, the absurd little carved bird sitting on the checkpoint table while a Hunt officer examined their forged papers. He felt it lift out of him like a splinter drawn clean, and he felt the space where it had been, smooth and empty, and he wanted to weep already and he had barely begun.

The vessels drank. The counter-song swelled.

He gave them the fire. Both fires—the first one, the night she'd told him about the Order's fall, her face lit orange and her guilt laid out between them like an opened wound; and the second, the quiet night, the boots off, the stars so thick they looked like spilled salt. He gave them the drowned chapel, her arms locked around his chest, hauling him back from three hundred voices, shouting his name into his ear, and the strange holy shock of realizing someone had come in after him.

He gave them Greyford Bridge. The inn before the raid. Her hand on his jaw in the charcoal burner's hut, the night he'd nearly fled north, when she'd found him and said nothing at all, just sat beside him until dawn, shoulder to shoulder, while the soldier's voice begged in the bone.

Each memory left the same way: a bright pull behind his eyes, a cold rush down his arms, and then—a gap. A smooth place. He could feel the shape of what had been there, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth, and he was losing teeth faster now, faster, the rite accelerating as it fed—

"No." Ines's voice, raw. "No, stop, you're—you're forgetting me, I can see it, look at me, *look at me*—"

He looked at her. And for one terrible half-second he did not know her name.

Her face was a stranger's face—beautiful, urgent, tear-streaked, familiar in the way a portrait is familiar—and the knowledge of her arrived a heartbeat late, dragged up from somewhere deeper than memory, from the marrow itself. *Ines*, his bones said. *Ines of the Broken Order. Yours.* But the surface was going, the topsoil washing away, and he watched her understand it in real time, watched her read the blankness in his eyes, and the sound she made was not a word in any language.

She seized his wrists.

"You stubborn, self-sacrificing *fool*," she said, and her tears fell on his hands. "You were going to do this alone. You were going to let me stand here and watch you empty yourself and not even—" Her voice broke. She pressed her forehead to his knuckles. "I helped forge the binding, Marguerite. My hands made this cage. If love's memory is the price, then it is *mine* to pay, and you had no right—no *right*—to spend yours trying to spare me."

"Ines—"

"And I am not," she said, lifting her head, and her eyes were blazing, wet, magnificent, "going to let you be the only one who loved enough to lose something."

She put her hands on the vessel beside his.

"Ines, *don't*—"

"*Together*," she said. "Or not at all. Those were the terms, weren't they? On the road. Wary alliance, mutual leverage. Well, carver—here is my leverage."

And she began to pour.

* * *

Later—if there was a later; time had gone strange, stretched thin as wire—Marguerite would remember none of what happened next, and would spend the rest of his life feeling the absence of not remembering it.

But the bones remembered. The bones always remembered.

They poured together, two streams into one channel, and the counter-song rose until the cathedral's windows hummed in their lead frames. He gave her the bridge and she gave him the motherhouse courtyard where she'd first sworn her oaths at seventeen, terrified and certain. He gave her the tannery market and she gave him the night she'd decided to trust him—really trust him—watching him listen to a dead woman's charm and weep for a stranger's grief. He gave her the prison under the palace, the desperate plan, the ossuary full of murmuring dead lighting their way like drowned lanterns; she gave him her mentor's face, the forgiveness she couldn't speak and the mercy Marguerite had spoken in her place, and how that mercy had cracked something open in her that had been sealed for twenty years.

The spirit shrieked. It thrashed inside its stolen king, and the throne dais split, and the stone-faced nobility swayed like a field of wheat in wind—but the vessels were filling, filling, drinking down forty years' worth of love compressed into minutes, and the binding's old knot was loosening thread by thread.

*Stop,* the spirit said, and for the first time its voice was afraid. *Stop, you are unmaking yourselves—*

"Yes," Ines said, laughing and crying at once, her hands burning on the bone. "Now you understand what an oath costs."

Marguerite was past speech. He was mostly past *self*. He clung to one image the way a drowning man clings to a spar: her face across a fire, boots off, laughing at a carved duck. He poured it out last, deliberately, saving the best for the end, and as it left him he felt it go all the way through—out of his mind, down his arms, into the bone—and he heard, distantly, Ines give up her own final memory, whatever it was, whatever secret treasury she had kept for this exact moment, and the two gifts met somewhere in the middle of the vessels and *ignited*.

Light filled the cathedral. Not candlelight—bone-light, white and total, pouring out of the three vessels like water from a broken dam.

The spirit came apart.

There was no other word for it. It tore loose from Aldric with a sound like a mountain of ice calving into the sea, a formless hunger made briefly visible—a shape of shadow and want, vast and starving—and the vessels caught it, drew it in, sealed it, the way a fist closes around a moth. The king dropped like a cut rope. Serane caught him—Marguerite saw that much, saw the captain drop her sword and lunge, catching the falling monarch before his head struck the dais—and then the light peaked, and screamed, and went out.

Silence.

Candles relit themselves one by one, ordinary yellow, trembling. Somewhere a noblewoman was sobbing. Somewhere Serane was saying, "Breathe, Your Majesty, breathe," in a voice stripped of all command, just a woman holding another human being.

And on the altar steps, Marguerite Coldharbour knelt beside a woman he did not know.

He knew that he knew her. That was the worst of it—the knowledge was structural, load-bearing, built into him below the level of memory. He knew her the way he knew his own hands. But when he turned to look at her, her face was a stranger's face, lovely and wrecked and searching his eyes with the same blank terror he could feel rising in his own chest.

"Do you—" she started, and stopped, and tried again. "Do you remember my name?"

He reached for it. He reached with everything he had.

Nothing came.

"No," he said, and his voice sounded very far away. "I'm sorry. I don't—there was something, there was *us*, I can feel the shape of it, I can feel—"

"Marguerite." She said his name like a lifeline thrown across dark water, and he watched her realize, even as she said it, that she was testing whether his name still meant anything to her either—and watched her find the same emptiness. Her hand rose halfway toward his face and stopped there, hovering, belonging to a stranger. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, it took everything. It took *everything*."

Below them, the king stirred in Serane's arms and whispered something—free, or forgiven, or merely alive. The vessels sat cold and dark on the altar, sealed, silent, heavier than bone should be.

Ines looked at Marguerite for a long moment. Two strangers on the steps of a saved kingdom, kneeling in each other's blood and candlelight, holding hands they did not remember holding before.

"We won," she said at last, wonderingly, as if reminding herself. "We actually—we won."

"Yes," Marguerite said. And then, because his hands had always known things before his mind did, he heard himself add, quietly, to this woman whose name lived somewhere in him beyond reach:

"I think I loved you."

Her breath caught. For a moment something moved behind her eyes—grief, recognition, a door opening onto a room she could no longer enter—and then she closed her fingers around his hand, tight, deliberate, a keeper sealing an oath.

"Then," she said, "we begin again."

Far above them, through the shattered rose window, the first snow of winter began to fall into the cathedral, settling on the altar, on the vessels, on the hollowed king—white flakes drifting down through the candlelight like ash, like blessing, like pages torn from a book neither of them could read anymore.
