## 32. Unbinding

The counter-song held.

Marguerite could feel it the way a man on a frozen lake feels the ice holding—one tremor from catastrophe, and yet holding. The dead of the drowned chapel sang through him, hundreds of voices braided into a single low note that filled the cathedral like water filling a hull. The hermit's voice was in it too, and the soldier's, and every whisper he had ever pulled from bone and ivory and the cheap river-clay charms of his early years. They poured through his chest and out through his hands and into the three vessels arranged at the altar's foot, and the vessels drank, and the song went on.

Ines knelt opposite him, both hands pressed flat to the largest vessel, blood still seeping from the cut she'd opened at the motherhouse. Her lips moved without sound—she was anchoring, holding the geometry of the rite in place the way a keel holds a ship true—and between them, on the altar steps, King Aldric convulsed.

The spirit did not want to leave.

It had worn this man for forty years. It had eaten his childhood memories first, then his loves, then his griefs, working inward like rot through timber until what remained was a hollowed frame dressed in royal silk. And now, with its food cut off—the memories poured away, the vessels drinking everything it reached for—it was thrashing.

Aldric's body arched. His mouth opened and what came out was not one voice but many, layered and wrong, a choir squeezed through a single throat.

*YOU WOULD UNMAKE ME WITH SONGS?*

The words struck the cathedral like a physical blow. Candles guttered along fifty feet of altar rail. Somewhere behind him Marguerite heard Serane shouting—*hold the line, hold it, don't let them through*—and the clash of steel as loyalist guards met the reformed Hunt's shields. He didn't look. Looking cost him the song.

*I KNOW WHAT YOU POURED OUT, BONE-CARVER,* the spirit said, and its voice slid inside his skull now, intimate as a hand on the back of his neck. *I TASTED IT AS IT PASSED THROUGH YOU. HER LAUGH AT THE FIRE. THE BRIDGE AT GREYFORD. I COULD GIVE THEM BACK. I COULD GIVE YOU MORE.*

Marguerite kept singing.

That was the thing about pouring yourself into bone: once given, you knew exactly what was gone, even if you couldn't see its shape anymore. He felt the absence where Ines should be—a hole in his chest shaped precisely like a woman kneeling across from him, blood on her hands, eyes fixed on his with an expression he no longer had the history to read. The spirit offered to fill that hole. The spirit offered everything.

He sang louder.

The dead helped. That was the secret no one had ever understood about his gift—the dead were not echoes, not residue. They were people, still, in the way a banked fire is still fire. And they hated this thing. Whatever the crown-spirit had been when forty young keepers bound it with hope and bad counsel, it had spent four decades feeding on kings, and the dead recognized a devourer the way sheep recognize a wolf. They leaned into the song. They made it heavier.

The vessels began to glow.

Not gold—bone-white, a light like moonlight through ice, rising up the sides of the three carved vessels until Marguerite had to squint against it. Ines gasped. Her hands were shaking against the largest one, and he saw why: the vessel was pulling now, not waiting to be fed. It had tasted the spirit's attention and it wanted more, and it was drawing through her arms, through her blood, through whatever thread of the old Order still lived in her.

"It knows," she said aloud, the first words anyone had spoken in the rite since it began. "Marguerite—it knows what we're doing. It's going to—"

The king stopped convulsing.

That was worse. Aldric lay suddenly still on the altar steps, an old man in ceremonial white, chest rising and falling with terrible calm. His eyes opened. They were clear. They were the clearest things in the cathedral.

And then the clarity folded inward, and something looked out through them that was not Aldric and had never been Aldric, and it regarded the two of them kneeling over its prison door with the patience of a thing that had waited forty years and could wait forty more.

*CLEVER LITTLE KEEPERS,* it said, and its voice was almost gentle now. *BUT A DOOR SWINGS BOTH WAYS. YOU HAVE OPENED HIM TO ME ENTIRELY. THERE IS NOTHING LEFT OF YOUR RITE BETWEEN US BUT HIS LAST MEMORY, AND I HAVE BEEN EATING THAT ONE SLOWLY FOR YEARS, SAVORING IT.*

"What is it?" Ines whispered. "What memory?"

Marguerite already knew. He could feel it through the bone under his hands—the oath-bone, warm as a living thing against his palms. The last unswallowed piece of Aldric Vane. Not a coronation. Not a crown.

*A wedding,* the dead murmured through him. *A garden. A woman with dirt on her hands.*

"His wife," Marguerite said. "The queen. The one who died before his reign began."

Ines's face went white. "If it takes that—"

"There'll be nothing left to free."

The spirit smiled with the king's mouth. It was a horrible thing, that smile—too wide, too knowing, wearing a face it had no right to.

*GIVE ME THE WOMAN'S MEMORY AND I WILL LEAVE THIS BODY QUIETLY. HE WILL LIVE. DIM, PERHAPS. EMPTY, CERTAINLY. BUT ALIVE, AND YOU TWO CAN GO BACK TO YOUR LITTLE LIVES AND YOUR LITTLE BONES, AND IN TIME YOU MIGHT EVEN POUR SOMETHING NEW INTO THE HOLE YOU'VE MADE.*

Marguerite looked at Ines.

She was looking back at him, and here was the cruelty of it: he could not remember why her face mattered to him. He knew it mattered—he knew it the way an amputee knows the missing limb, a constant cold ache with no source—but the knowledge was all he had. She might have been a stranger. She was a stranger. And still some animal part of him, some part deeper than memory, wanted to cross the altar and put his hand over hers and tell her they would finish this together or not at all.

He didn't move. The song needed his hands.

"We refuse," Ines said.

Her voice cracked across the nave like a whip. She lifted her chin, and Marguerite saw the oath-keeper come up in her spine, the woman who had been forged in an order of vows and broken ones, and she said it again, louder, for the whole cathedral:

"We refuse. Take nothing else from him. You've eaten enough kings. You've eaten enough of *this* king. It ends tonight."

*THEN IT ENDS WITH HIM HOLLOW,* the spirit said, and lunged.

There was no other word for it. Aldric's body went rigid, and the presence inside him surged toward that last protected memory like floodwater toward a final room, and Marguerite felt the rite shudder—the vessels' light flickering, the dead's song fraying at the edges as the spirit's will bore down on it.

He did the only thing left.

He let go of the song entirely and threw himself into the silence after it.

Later—insofar as there was a later—he would try to describe it and fail. But in the moment it was simple: the spirit was a devourer, and devourers feed, and Marguerite Coldharbour had spent ten years learning how to make bone hold a voice. So he took the emptiness where his memories of Ines had been—that clean, screaming void—and he offered it.

*Here,* he said, without sound, without song, straight down the bone. *Feed on this.*

The spirit struck at it like a pike at a lure.

For one instant it poured itself out of the dying king and into the shape of an absence, into the hollow where love had been, trying to swallow a thing that was not there—and the vessels closed around it like a fist.

Ines slammed her palms down on all three at once. Blood, sweat, tears—whatever the ward at the motherhouse had left in her, she gave it, and the bone-vessels flared white, and the spirit screamed.

It screamed with a hundred stolen voices. It screamed in Aldric's voice, begging; in the soldier's voice, cursing; in voices Marguerite recognized from drowned throats in a flooded crypt, from hermit caves, from every charm he'd ever carved for grieving families. It screamed as it was torn—truly torn, root and branch—out of the body it had worn for forty years, and the sound of its leaving shook dust from the cathedral rafters and cracked the great rose window in a spiderweb of silver lines.

Then it was in the vessels. All of it. Coiled and furious and small, a darkness the size of a heartbeat sealed in three pieces of carved bone, and the vessels went dark, and the silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

King Aldric Vane collapsed onto the altar steps like a coat slipping off a hook.

* * *

Marguerite got to him first—or his knees did, carrying him the last few feet before his mind caught up. The king was breathing. That was the first thing. Shallow, ragged, but real breath, moving a real chest, and when Marguerite rolled him gently onto his back the face beneath was just a face: lined, exhausted, seventy years old and looking every one of them, and utterly, blessedly empty of anything that wasn't Aldric.

Except it wasn't empty. That was the horror of it. The spirit had lied about that too, or half-lied. What remained wasn't nothing.

"Your Majesty." Ines was beside him, pressing two fingers to the king's throat, counting a pulse. "Aldric. Can you hear me?"

The king's eyes opened. They wandered the vaulted ceiling, the shattered window, the two strangers leaning over him, and Marguerite watched him try to assemble the world from pieces, like a man waking in a house he'd been told was his.

"The garden," Aldric whispered.

"Your Majesty—"

"She was planting the winter roses." His voice was thin and wondering, a child's voice in an old man's throat. "I can't—I can't find her name. I know there was a garden. I know I loved—" His hand rose, trembling, and closed on air. "Where did she go? Where did everything go? Who are you?"

"I'm Ines." Her voice broke on her own name, which told Marguerite something, though he couldn't have said what. "Ines of the Order. I helped bind you, forty years ago. I'm so sorry. We came to take it back."

The king looked at her for a long moment. Then at Marguerite. Something moved behind his eyes—not recognition, not quite. Recognition's ghost.

"You gave something," Aldric said slowly. "Both of you. I felt it go past me. Like—like coins dropped through my fingers on the way down." His gaze fixed on Marguerite with sudden, terrible lucidity. "You paid more than she did. Don't tell me you didn't. I heard it land."

Marguerite had no answer. He looked down at his own hands, stained with bone-dust and the king's ceremonial oil, and they were shaking, and he did not know if it was exhaustion or grief, because grief required a memory and he had none.

Around them, the cathedral was settling into its new shape. The clash of steel had stopped; through the broken doors he could see Serane standing over a knot of disarmed loyalist guards, her sword point-down, her face gray with fatigue and something else—something that looked, from this distance, like the beginning of belief. The nobility in their stalls were stirring out of the spirit's paralysis one by one, blinking, touching their own faces, murmuring to each other in the hushed tones of people checking whether the world was still real.

The three vessels sat at the altar's foot. Dark. Quiet. Heavy.

Ines gathered them into her arms one at a time, handling each like a sleeping infant, and when she stood, she swayed, and Marguerite caught her elbow without thinking. She looked at his hand on her arm. He looked at it too. Neither of them moved it, and neither of them knew why.

"Is it done?" she asked quietly. "Do you feel it? Is it finished?"

He searched himself the way he'd search a carving for flaws—running thumb and fingers over the interior of his own mind, feeling for gaps. There was one. Of course there was one. A Marguerite-shaped absence running straight through the middle of the last season of his life, smooth-walled and clean as a socket, and somewhere at the bottom of it, faint as heat off a stone at dusk, the sense that something wonderful had been taken out.

"It's done," he said.

She nodded. She didn't ask what it had cost him, and he realized she was asking with her face anyway, and he realized he was answering with his, and neither of them could read the other anymore. Two fluent speakers of a language that had ceased to exist.

The king stirred. They turned back to him, and found him watching them with an expression of dawning, exhausted comprehension—the look of a man who has been locked in his own house for forty years and has just seen, through a newly opened door, the size of the world outside.

"Help me stand," Aldric said. "There are laws to break and a Hunt to dissolve, and I have lost everything except the will to do it. That will have to be enough." He gripped the altar rail, hauled himself upright, and stood swaying in his ruined ceremonial white, an emptied king in a cracked cathedral, alive.

Ines shifted the vessels in her arms and stepped forward to offer her shoulder.

And Marguerite, following, caught sight of them reflected together in the dark glass of the shattered rose window—an oath-keeper, a carver, a king—and thought, with a pang whose origin he could not name: *I would swear I've stood beside her before.*

Below, in the square, the crowd had begun to sing.

* * *

They came down the cathedral steps into a city holding its breath.

Word had spread the way word does—through servants and soldiers and the ever-hungry mouths of rumor—until the whole of the coronation crowd knew only this: something had happened in the cathedral, something vast, and the king had walked out onto the steps alive when everyone had whispered for a year that he was dead behind his eyes.

Aldric raised one hand. The square went silent enough to hear the snow.

"The Hunt is dissolved," he said, and his voice carried, thin but unbroken, over ten thousand heads. "Every warrant burned. Every prisoner freed. And the Order of Keepers—" his eyes found Ines, and something in his ravaged face softened, "—is restored, with its last true keeper at its head. There will be reckoning. There will be rebuilding. But tonight—" he swayed, and steadied himself on the stone balustrade, and Marguerite saw Ines tense to catch him, "—tonight, go home. Go home to your children. The thing that fed on us is caged."

The roar that answered shook snow from the rooftops.

Serane appeared at Marguerite's shoulder as the crowd surged forward with torches and tears and questions. She looked at him sideways, and there was no suspicion in it anymore—only a tired, wry appraisal, the look of a woman recalculating a ledger she'd kept wrong for years.

"The soldier who died in your alley," she said. "He'd have liked this."

"You never knew him."

"No." She glanced toward Ines, standing a few steps below with the vessels cradled against her chest, lit orange by a hundred torches. "But I know the type. The kind that dies passing the weight to someone who'll carry it." She paused. "You look like a man carrying something heavy, carver."

"I wouldn't know what," Marguerite said honestly.

Something flickered across her face—pity, maybe, or understanding—and then she was gone, into the crowd, a captain with no hunt left to lead.

Ines climbed back up to him. The noise of the celebration wrapped around them like weather, and for a moment they simply stood in it, two strangers at the top of the world, holding between them three dark bones and a silence full of everything they'd lost.

"Tomorrow," Ines said at last, "the Order will need to decide what to do with these." She touched the largest vessel, lightly, the way you'd touch a grave-marker. "And I'll need—" She stopped. Started again, differently, softer. "There's a workshop on Cordwainer Lane. I'm told the carver there is the best in Vellamark. I'll need someone who understands what these bones remember. To guard them. To study them. To make sure nothing like this ever—"

"Yes," Marguerite said.

She blinked. "You haven't asked what it pays."

"I don't care what it pays." He frowned at himself, at the certainty in his own voice, arriving from nowhere like a letter postmarked years ago. "I don't—know why I said yes. I mean, I do, obviously, it's important work, but it's more than that. Isn't it?" He looked at her helplessly. "Tell me it isn't just me."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Torchlight moved in her eyes, and behind it, somewhere far down, something grieved and hoped at the same time.

"No," she said quietly. "It isn't just you."

Above them, the cracked rose window caught the dawn's first light and threw it down in fractured color across the steps—across the king, across the vessels, across the two of them standing in the wreckage of a victory that had cost them the only thing either of them would have refused to pay.

Marguerite held out his hand.

Ines looked at it. Looked at him. And took it.

Her grip was warm, and steady, and completely unfamiliar, and neither of them let go.
