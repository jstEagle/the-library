## 34. Strangers Who Remember Nothing

The walk home took three hours because he kept stopping.

Not from weakness—though his legs had every right to give out beneath him after what they'd done in the cathedral—but because Vellamark kept insisting on being beautiful at the worst possible moment. Frost silvered the eaves of the chandler's row. A dog chased its own breath in clouds across the fountain square where, a lifetime ago it seemed, criers had announced the Hunt and a neighbor's door had worn the ash-mark like a brand. Children were scraping ice off the fountain's rim and eating it, shrieking, and somewhere a baker was pulling loaves, and the whole city smelled of woodsmoke and rye and the particular cold-clean scent of snow that hadn't yet decided to fall.

Marguerite stood in the square with his hands in his pockets and felt like a man reading a letter addressed to someone else.

He knew this city. He knew it the way he knew his own hands—the shortcut behind the tanneries, the tavern with the crooked sign, which cobblestones rocked loose in spring thaw. But knowing wasn't the same as belonging, and there was a hollow space inside him shaped like something he couldn't name, the way you don't notice a missing tooth until your tongue goes looking for it. His tongue went looking all the way home.

*You gave it away,* he thought. *Whatever it was. You poured it into the bones.*

He remembered the rite in fragments: the altar, the three vessels singing, Ines's hands steady on the bone while his own voice cracked apart under the weight of every dead soul he'd ever carried. He remembered the spirit tearing free of King Aldric like a root ripped from wet earth, screaming in a hundred borrowed voices. He remembered the silence afterward, vast and ringing, and the king weeping on the marble like a man pulled out of deep water.

He did not remember her.

That was the strangeness of it. He could stand in the cathedral's aftermath and know, intellectually, that the woman anchoring the vessels had been the center of everything—that she'd been captured for him, freed for him, that she'd cut her palm open on a ward and walked through illusions to reach the third vessel. Serane had told him some of it on the road back, in her flat captain's voice, watching him carefully the way you watch a man with a head wound. *Ines. Oath-keeper. She forged the binding when she was young and spent ten years trying to undo it. You loved her. From what I saw, she loved you.*

*I loved her.* The words sat in him like stones in a riverbed, smooth and foreign. He believed them the way you believe a map of a country you've never visited.

And then she had stood in front of him outside the cathedral, snow beginning to fall in earnest, and looked at him with an expression he had no key for—grief and tenderness and something almost like gratitude—and said, "It was worth it," and he had said, "I'm sorry, I wish I—" and she had pressed two fingers briefly to his lips and said, "Don't. Don't apologize for saving a kingdom." And then she had walked away into the white falling quiet, upright as a blade, and he had watched her go and felt nothing but a terrible, sourceless ache, like a bell struck in an empty house.

Now the bell wouldn't stop ringing.

* * *

His workshop door stuck the way it always stuck—he knew that without knowing how he knew it—and he shouldered it open into cold air and dust and the smell of shavings and linseed oil that hit him like a mother's voice.

Home. Whatever else had been poured out of him, this remained. The workbench with its worn hollows where his elbows rested. The rack of chisels sorted by fineness, each handle polished dark by years of his grip. The little stove, dead now, its chimney breathing winter down at him. Shelves of finished charms waiting for customers who would come again now that the Hunt was dissolved and a carver who heard the dead could hang his sign openly—which he supposed he'd have to do, eventually, when he understood what kind of man he was now.

Because that was the thing. He didn't. He knew he was a bone-carver. He knew he heard the dead through what he made—knew it the way he knew arithmetic, a fact without a history attached. But the *man* who had lived here, who had hidden for ten years, who had burned a decade of vessels in his hearth and kept one piece anyway—who was he? What had he been afraid of? What had he loved? The shape of his life was there, all around him, and the life itself was gone, and he moved through his own workshop like a stranger in a museum dedicated to himself.

He built up the stove fire first. His hands did it without consulting him—kindling, coal, the iron poker turned just so—and he watched them work and felt the eerie comfort of a body that remembered more than its owner.

Then he began to clean.

It was something to do. It was better than standing in the middle of the room feeling the hollow ache expand. He swept shavings. He wiped down the bench. He sorted the odds and ends in the drawer by the window—wax stubs, sandpaper gone soft with use, a tin of pins, a small brass hinge he had no memory of buying—and it was going fine, it was almost soothing, right up until he opened the cabinet above the bench.

The whalebone charm sat on the top shelf, wrapped in a scrap of blue cloth.

He knew, immediately, that it didn't belong with the others. It wasn't stock. It wasn't a commission awaiting pickup. It had been *kept*—wrapped carefully, set deliberately where fire and damp and accident couldn't touch it, the way you keep a letter or a lock of hair. Someone had put it there on purpose. Someone had chosen this one object, out of everything, to survive.

He had burned ten years of carved vessels in his hearth once—Serane had mentioned that too, or maybe Ines had, before the end; the memories people kept offering him were secondhand coins, worn smooth—and he had kept this one piece he couldn't explain his attachment to. That was what they'd said. *He kept one small whalebone piece he cannot explain his attachment to.*

Well. Now neither could he.

He unwrapped the cloth with hands that had begun, very slightly, to shake.

It was small—a palm-length sliver of whalebone, carved into the shape of a wave curling over, and nested inside the wave's curve, so small you'd miss it if you weren't looking, a figure. A woman. Standing at the wave's crest with her arms open, riding it rather than fleeing it, her face tipped up toward something above the carving's edge. The work was fine. Better than fine. It was the kind of work a man does only once or twice in a life, when the knife stops being a tool and becomes a nerve.

He turned it over. On the underside, half-hidden in the whorl of the bone, a single word was incised in letters so small they might have been accidental:

*stay*

He read it four times. The word did not become less strange.

"Who are you?" he asked the little figure aloud, and his voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "Who was she?"

The bone said nothing. Bone never spoke unless it held a death—and this piece held none, he realized, listening with the sense he couldn't turn off, the way you can't unhear a room. It was empty. Quiet as a stone. Whatever voices it might once have carried, whatever words had lived in it, they had been given away along with everything else, poured into the unbinding rite with the rest of him.

Except it hadn't been emptied entirely. Because as he sat there on his stool in the warming room, turning the wave-woman in his fingers, the grief arrived.

It came the way weather comes—not gradually but all at once, a front rolling over the hills—and it had no name and no story and no face, and it was enormous. It was grief with the memory scooped out of it, a wound with the arrow withdrawn, and it went through him like cold water through a net. He was crying before he understood he was crying, great silent helpless tears running into his beard, his shoulders shaking around the small careful object he held as gently as an egg, and he could not have told anyone—and least of all himself—what he was mourning. A woman? A life? Ten years of a self he'd surrendered willingly, gladly, to save a king?

None of it. All of it. There was no answer. There was just the ache, and the ache knew things he didn't. The ache recognized the curve of the wave. The ache knew why the woman's face was tipped upward, and toward whom, and what word was hidden under the bone, and it would not tell him. Grief without a name is the cruelest kind, because you cannot even hate what caused it. You can only sit inside it, in your own warm workshop, in your own saved kingdom, and weep at a piece of whalebone like a fool.

He wept until there was nothing left, and then he sat a while longer, watching the stove breathe, holding the charm against his sternum as if it could hear a heartbeat and take notes.

*Stay,* the bone said, under his fingers, meaning nothing, meaning everything.

"I don't remember you," he told the little woman in the wave, and his voice cracked on it. "I don't remember any of it. I'm sorry. Whoever you were—I hope it was good. I hope I was good."

The fire popped. Outside, the snow was coming down thick and soundless now, erasing the street, the square, the whole wounded city, laying a clean page over everything.

He wrapped the charm back in its blue cloth. Then, instead of returning it to the shelf, he set it on the workbench where he could see it—propped against the oilstone, facing the room, the way you'd face a photograph of family. He didn't examine the choice. Some choices you make below the level of the mind, in the place where the hands remember.

* * *

Sleep came late and left early. He dreamed of water—not drowning, just water, green-lit and patient, and a voice moving through it saying his name in a tone he couldn't hold onto, the way you can't hold a fish. He woke before dawn with wet eyes and no memory of why, which was becoming, he suspected, simply the condition of his life.

He got up. He fed the stove. He did what he knew how to do.

The days that followed arranged themselves into a rhythm that was almost a life. Word had spread, the way word does, that the carver on Chandler's Row was the one who'd unbound the king, and Vellamark being Vellamark, half the town wanted to look at him and the other half wanted something carved. They came shyly at first—an old woman wanting a memorial for a son lost at Greyford Bridge, a tanner with a limp asking, red-faced, if the stories about hearing the dead were true. Marguerite found, to his surprise, that he didn't mind. The work was the one country he still had a passport to. When the knife was in his hand and the bone was speaking, he was not a hollowed man; he was a craftsman, whole and occupied, and the ache receded to a hum beneath the noise of making.

But every evening, when the light went blue and the customers thinned, he would find himself at the workbench with the whalebone charm propped in front of him, carving without quite deciding to carve. His hands made things his mind didn't order. A bridge, rendered over and over in scraps of antler and sheep bone, always the same bridge, always with two figures on it whose faces he smoothed away before he understood he was doing it. A campfire, tiny and perfect, with a kettle hung on a green stick. A woman's profile emerging from a knuckle of elk bone one night—strong jaw, mouth set in a line that was half discipline and half laughter—and he stared at it when it was done and felt the hum swell toward a roar and had to put it facedown in a drawer.

He told himself it was practice. He told himself a carver's hands keep their habits even when the mind lets go, the way a sailor's legs keep the sea. Both things were true. Neither was the truth.

On the ninth day, the customer came.

He heard the door stick and shoulder open the way his own did—it always stuck; he'd meant to plane the edge for years, apparently—and looked up expecting another widow or wonder-seeker. Instead a woman stood in the doorway shaking snow from a traveling cloak, and something in his chest lurched so hard and so suddenly that he actually gripped the edge of the bench.

She was tall. Dark-haired, shot through with early grey she hadn't bothered to dye. She carried herself like a drawn bow, and her eyes—dark, level, tired in a way that went deeper than sleep—moved over the workshop with the quick inventory of someone who has learned never to enter a room unarmed. A long scar marked the back of one hand.

He had never seen her in his life.

And yet his heart was slamming like a fist on a door, and the hollow ache that had lived in him since the cathedral turned over once, like a sleeper, like something that had heard its name.

"Master Coldharbour?" she said. Her voice was low and even, sanded smooth by command. "I'm told you take commissions now. Openly."

"I do," he managed. "Please—sit. Forgive the mess."

She didn't sit. She came forward to the bench, and her gaze snagged—for half a heartbeat, no more—on the whalebone charm propped against the oilstone. Something crossed her face, fast as a bird's shadow, and was gone.

"I need a memorial carved," she said. "Bone, if you work in it. Whalebone, if you have it." Her eyes came up and met his, and held, and there was a challenge in them, or a plea, or both wearing the same coat. "For someone I lost."

"Of course," Marguerite said. His mouth was dry. "May I ask—what form? A figure? An emblem?"

She looked at the wave-woman on the bench again, longer this time, and when she answered, her voice had gone quiet enough that the snow outside seemed loud.

"No," she said. "Something new. Something that hasn't happened yet."
