## 35. The Body Remembers

The Order's new headquarters had been a granary, once, before the fire took the roof and the crown took the land. Ines had chosen it deliberately. A building that had already burned could not be threatened with burning, and the Order of Keepers—rebuilt from three surviving members, a stolen ledger, and her own name cleared by royal decree—needed to begin from ash. That was honest work. Everything else felt like pretending.

She stood at the window of what would become the scriptorium and watched the frost climb the glass, and she did not let herself think about the shop on Candlemakers' Row.

She thought about it constantly.

*You are the First Keeper now,* she reminded herself, the way one reminds oneself of a debt. *You have oaths to reforge, vessels to guard, a king's broken mind to tend through his recovery. You have no time for—*

For what? She didn't have a word for it. Grief without a death. Mourning for a man who was alive and well and carving charms forty paces from where she stood, who smiled at his customers and remembered nothing of her, nothing of the drowned chapel or the firelit camp or the way he had poured himself out like water into bone so that a kingdom might keep breathing.

He had done it without telling her. That was the part she returned to in the dark hours, turning it over like a stone with a sharp edge. He had known the cost. He had paid it alone, silently, while she anchored the vessels and sang the counter-song and understood only too late what was leaving him. She had added her own memories after—every moment she could seize, flung into the rite like coins after a thief—but the bones had already drunk deep of him, and what remained between them was this: two strangers in a cathedral, standing over emptied vessels, feeling as though they had lost something they could not name.

The healers said the memories were not destroyed. Poured out, not burned. They lived now in the whalebone charm he'd kept—the one he couldn't explain keeping, all those years ago—and in whatever strange country the rite had made of them. But no one knew how to pour them back. The dead did not return what they were given. Ines had asked every scholar, every remnant of the old Order, every voice Marguerite's gift could reach through the relics she brought him under pretext of study.

Nothing.

So she did the only thing left. She led. She rebuilt. And once a month, on some invented pretext—a commission, an authentication, a question about provenance—she walked down Candlemakers' Row and pushed open the door of his shop, and let the bell above the door say what she could not.

* * *

Today the pretext was a memorial. Forty-one names, the fallen of the night the spirit unmasked itself in the cathedral—guards, bystanders, two relic-bearers whose deaths still sat in her chest like swallowed stones. The Order owed them permanence. Bone was permanent. And there was only one carver in Vellamark whose work the families would trust.

She told herself this was true. It was mostly true. It was true enough to carry her through the door.

The bell rang. Warm air met her, scented with shavings and linseed oil and something faintly mineral, like the inside of a cave. The shop had changed since magic was decriminalized—since Aldric, lucid and hollow-eyed, had stood before the assembled court and dissolved the Hunt with three sentences that Serane Voss delivered to the streets herself. Charms hung openly in the windows now, little wards and blessings and mourning-pieces, swinging gently in the draft. No more ash-marked doorways. No more neighbors vanishing in the night. Marguerite Coldharbour's name was spoken with respect in half the districts of the city.

He was at his bench with his back to her, sleeves rolled, a burin moving in small precise strokes. Winter light came through the south window and caught the dust motes around him, and for a moment—involuntary, treacherous—Ines saw another fireside, another winter, a man telling her about his mother's hands and how he'd learned to listen to bone before he learned to read.

She hadn't seen that. She couldn't have. She had no memory of it. The knowing arrived anyway, whole and warm and sourceless, the way you know the shape of your own hand in the dark.

"Be with you in a moment," he said, not turning. "Just finishing the—"

He stopped. His shoulders changed. Not tensed—something gentler than that, something like recognition arriving ahead of thought. He set down the burin and turned, and when he saw her his face did the thing it always did: surprise first, then a kind of helpless softening, then a quick recovery into craftsman's courtesy that fooled no one, least of all her.

"Keeper Ashgrove." He used her title the way other people used a shield. "I mean—Ines. Sorry. Habit."

"No apology needed." Her own voice sounded strange to her, pitched carefully neutral. "I've come to commission a memorial. Forty-one names. I'm told you're the only carver in Vellamark who can make bone sing."

"I'm told that too." A smile flickered, uncertain of itself. "Mostly by people who want a discount."

* * *

They talked terms at the bench because neither of them suggested anywhere else. He spread sketches; she leaned over them; their heads bent close enough that she could smell the oil in his hair and see the fine scar along his jaw that no story of his life accounted for. He explained grain and density, which bones held a name longest—she watched his hands move and thought, absurdly, *I know those hands*, and hated herself for the thought, and could not stop it.

"The femur holds best," he was saying. "But for a public piece, we'll want something that reads at distance. Whalebone, probably. I have a source up the coast who—"

He stopped again. Mid-sentence, mid-gesture, the way he sometimes did, as if a door had opened somewhere behind his eyes and shown him a room he didn't recognize. Ines had learned to wait through these moments without comment. The scholars called them echoes. Marguerite called them *weather*.

"You okay?" she asked, when it stretched too long.

"Yeah." He rubbed his thumb across his fingers, absently, as though feeling something that wasn't there. "It's just—you do that. Lean in like that. When you're reading something." His ears went faintly red. "Forget it. Whalebone. I'll send to my contact up the coast."

"Ines," she said.

"What?"

"My name. You said it right the first time. I'd rather you use it."

Something moved through his face like wind through wheat. "Right," he said quietly. "Ines."

And then, reaching together for the same sketch, their hands brushed.

It was nothing. A grazing of knuckles, half a second, the sort of accident that happens a hundred times in any workshop. Ines had been cut, burned, struck, bound; she had a high tolerance for sensation and a professional's contempt for drama.

Her whole arm lit up.

Not pain. Something older than pain, something that bypassed skin entirely and went straight into whatever part of a person remembers being alive. Lightning with no thunder. She heard herself draw breath, sharply, and saw his hand freeze where it lay, and saw him staring at the point of contact as though the touch had left a visible mark.

Neither of them pulled away. That was the strange thing. Half a second became a full second, became two, their hands resting side by side on the sketch of forty-one names, and Ines felt her heartbeat in her fingertips and thought: *this is what drowning feels like, if drowning were kind.*

"I—" he started.

"Don't," she said, but softly.

He withdrew his hand. Flexed it. Looked at it as though it belonged to someone else. "That happens," he said finally. "Around you. I don't—I've tried to work out why. I thought maybe it was the oath-bone residue, or—" He laughed, short and unhappy. "There's no reason. There's no reason at all. My hands remember things I don't. Some days I carve faces I've never seen and wake up with songs in my mouth I never learned. The healers say the mind heals forward. Nobody tells you the body heals sideways."

"The body keeps what the mind loses," Ines said. "My teachers taught me that. Oaths live in the marrow, not the memory. That's why we swore on bone." She heard her own voice steady itself around the words. "Maybe love is an oath too."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Outside, a cart rattled past; somewhere a child shouted; the ordinary city went on being ordinary, indifferent to the two people standing in a workshop over a sheet of sketches, both of them gripping the edge of something enormous and refusing to look directly at it.

"I should go," Ines said.

"You just got here."

"I know."

Neither of them moved.

* * *

She stayed another hour. She told herself it was due diligence—selecting bone samples, confirming dimensions, discussing the mounting frame. He made tea in a chipped pot and she drank it and the tea tasted like a campfire she had never attended and could not stop missing.

At the bench, working through the name list, he paused at one entry. "Serane Voss is paying for two of these herself. Out of pocket. She wouldn't say whose."

"She wouldn't." Ines turned the teacup. "Two guards died holding the cathedral doors. They were hers. She takes that personally."

"She seems like a woman who takes everything personally and apologizes for none of it."

"That's exactly who she is." And then, because the warmth in the room had grown dangerous, Ines reached for the safe subject, the one that always worked. "Show me what you're carving. The piece on the bench."

He hesitated—she saw the hesitation, the instinct toward privacy—and then, with a shrug that was mostly surrender, turned the workcloth back.

Faces. Dozens of them, small studies in scrap bone, arranged like a family of strangers. A bridge in miniature. A fire ringed with stones. A woman's profile rendered over and over, each attempt slightly different, each one unmistakably—

Hers.

Ines looked at the small bone face looking back at her and felt the floor tilt beneath her certainties. "Who is she?" she asked, though her voice came out wrong, thin.

"I don't know." He said it simply, without embarrassment, which somehow made it worse. "She keeps showing up. Every piece I start turns into her eventually. My customers think I'm in love with an imaginary woman." He smiled, and the smile cracked in the middle. "Maybe I am. Maybe that's the joke the universe is playing on me. I carve her and I feel like I'm homesick for a place I've never been."

Ines set down the teacup very carefully, because her hands were not entirely her own.

"Marguerite," she said. "What would you say if I told you—"

The bell over the door rang.

A customer, bundled against the cold, wanting a mourning charm for a father. Ordinary life crashing in like weather. Marguerite turned to serve him, and the moment folded itself up and put itself away, and Ines stood among the hanging charms with her heart hammering and the unfinished sentence dying on her tongue.

*What would you tell him?* she asked herself. *That he loved you once? That you can prove it? That the proof changes nothing, because knowing is not remembering, and he deserves better than a history handed to him like a bill?*

She had sworn oaths her whole life. Speak truth. Keep faith. Bear witness. None of them covered this.

When the customer left, Marguerite walked her to the door. At the threshold they stopped, and the space between them filled with everything unsaid, dense as smoke.

"The memorial will take six weeks," he said. "Maybe seven. I'll need to consult with you on the inscription. Several times. Probably." His eyes were careful, hopeful, terrified. "If the Order can spare you."

"The Order," Ines said, "can spare me whenever I decide it can."

His breath caught—she saw it, small and unmistakable, hope flickering in the draft from the open door like a candle deciding whether to stay lit.

"Good," he said. "Then. Six weeks. Several consultations."

"Several."

She stepped out into the cold. Behind her the bell rang once, bright and final, and she heard him stand there a long moment before the sound of his footsteps returned to the bench.

* * *

She made it four streets before she stopped, in the shadow of the old fountain where criers had once proclaimed the Hunt, and pressed her hand flat against a wall to steady herself.

Her palm still buzzed where their hands had touched. Not fading. Growing, if anything, like a bell that had been struck and would not finish sounding. She thought of the scholars' theories, the healers' cautious optimism, the dead who kept what they were given. She thought of a whalebone charm on a shelf in a workshop, holding forty years of poured-out love like a cup holds rain.

And she thought of what Marguerite had said, laughing at himself: *my hands remember things I don't.*

The body keeps what the mind loses.

Ines lifted her hand and looked at it—her oath-keeper's hand, scarred, capable, sworn to truth above all things—and made herself a new vow, silent, unwitnessed, binding as any carved in bone:

*I will not hand him his past like a ledger. But I will build him a present worth remembering. And if the body remembers—if anything in him reaches for me across that emptied space—then I will be there when it does.*

Six weeks. Several consultations.

She turned toward the granary and the waiting work of the rebuilt Order, and she was almost smiling when the runner found her—an Order novice, breathless, snow in her hair, holding out a sealed letter with shaking hands.

"First Keeper. From the palace. It's marked urgent—it concerns the vessels."

Ines broke the seal. Read it once. Read it again.

The whalebone charm—the one Marguerite had kept all those years without knowing why, the vessel that held everything they had poured into the rite—had begun, according to the palace's bone-listeners, to *sing*.
