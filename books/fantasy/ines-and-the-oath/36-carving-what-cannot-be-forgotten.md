## 36. Carving What Cannot Be Forgotten

The hands knew things the mind did not.

Marguerite discovered this on the third morning after the woman in grey left his shop, when he woke with his fingers aching and found shavings scattered across his workbench like snow that had fallen indoors. He had no memory of working through the night. He remembered going to bed — remembered blowing out the lamp, remembered the particular creak of the third stair — and yet there on the bench sat a half-finished piece he did not recognize: a small figure of a woman standing at a bridge railing, her cloak carved to look as though wind were pulling it sideways.

He turned it in the lamplight. The face was unfinished, blank as an egg, but the posture was so specific it made his chest hurt. She wasn't looking at the water. She was looking back over her shoulder, at something behind her — at someone behind her — and the whole body was coiled around that glance like a spring.

"Who are you?" he asked the little figure.

The bone said nothing. That was the strange part, the part that kept him up after that. Marguerite Coldharbour had heard the dead speak through bone since he was nine years old; it was the reason he'd spent ten years hiding, the reason he'd burned a decade of carvings in his hearth, the reason he now lived openly as a bone-carver in a kingdom where magic had been decriminalized by a king who owed him everything and didn't know his face. Bone always spoke. Even old bone, even boiled and bleached bone, carried some murmur, some residue of the creature it had held together.

This piece was silent. Carved from a rib of elk he'd bought from a tanner's cart two weeks ago, ordinary as bread, and silent as stone.

He set it down and told himself he would not carve again that day.

By noon he had begun a second figure.

* * *

It went on like that for eleven days.

He stopped taking commissions, which was unlike him; he had built his reputation in Vellamark on reliability, on delivering mourning charms and memorial plaques exactly when promised, because a man with a secret could not afford to be unreliable in any visible way. Now he let orders pile up unanswered. He ate standing at the bench. He slept four hours and woke with his hands already moving, already reaching for the knife before his eyes were fully open.

The pieces came out of him like water from a cracked jug — steadily, without his consent, faster than he could account for.

A firelit camp: three figures seated around a ring of stones rendered in amber resin he didn't remember buying, their faces smooth and unfinished, but the composition perfect, intimate, one figure leaning toward another as if mid-laugh. A bridge — Greyford Bridge, though he only learned its name later, when a traveling merchant saw the piece and exclaimed over it — rendered in grey slate-bone with such precision that the merchant swore Marguerite must have stood on it recently, and Marguerite had no memory of ever crossing it at all. A woman's hands holding a broken seal, wax dripping from her fingers. A cell door, very small, carved from a sliver of black oak, with a keyhole worn bright as if many fingers had touched it.

A chapel underwater. This one frightened him. He carved it in a single sitting of fourteen hours and came out of the trance with his eyes streaming and no idea why, the little flooded nave sitting in his palm, tiny fish frozen in the arches, light falling in shafts through water he had somehow made translucent from solid material — a technique he did not know, had never learned, and could not repeat when he tried.

His neighbors noticed. Old Marta from the dye-house knocked to ask if he was ill, because she'd seen his lamp burning every night till dawn. The baker's boy reported that Master Coldharbour had shouted at him — actually shouted — for ringing the shop bell twice, and the boy's mother wanted to know if the master was quite well.

"I'm well," Marguerite told them all, and mostly believed it, because the truth was stranger than illness: he was happy. Not comfortable, not content — *happy*, in a raw, exposed way, like skin newly grown. Every hour at the bench felt like walking through a house he'd once lived in, touching walls he recognized while unable to say why. Something had been taken out of him. He knew this the way you know a room is missing furniture — by the shape of the absence. And his hands were rebuilding it, piece by piece, in bone.

He just couldn't remember what the original had looked like.

* * *

On the twelfth day, the pieces began to have faces.

Until then the figures had been anonymous, their features smoothed away — and he'd assumed this was style, some aesthetic choice his unconscious had made. But on the twelfth day he woke from a nap he hadn't meant to take and found a finished head resting beside his elbow, no larger than a walnut, carved from whalebone.

The same whalebone. He was almost sure of it. The small piece he'd kept through the burning, the one he couldn't explain, the one that had survived his hearth ten years ago because his hands had refused to feed it to the flames — he'd carved into it at last, or part of it, and the result was a woman's face with high cheekbones and a mouth set in a line that was neither smile nor severity but the exact tension between them. Someone who laughed easily and forgave slowly. Someone who had decided, at some cost, to trust.

He knew her.

That was the terrible thing. He didn't remember her — there was no memory attached, no name, no scene, nothing he could reach for — and yet he knew her the way you know your own handwriting. The face was familiar down to the grain. When he looked at it too long, something behind his sternum pulled tight, like a knot cinching.

He put it in his pocket. He carried it around the shop all day. Whenever the silence in the flat above got too loud, he took it out and held it, and the knot pulled, and somehow that was better than the silence.

"You're being ridiculous," he told himself aloud, in the empty shop, and heard how unconvincing he sounded.

* * *

The customer came on the seventeenth day, near closing, when the light through the front windows had gone the color of honey left too long in the sun.

She was perhaps sixty, dressed in the good wool of a merchant widow, and she browsed the shelves with the unhurried air of someone who intended to buy something whether or not she liked it, because she had come downtown specifically and would not be cheated of her errand. Marguerite wiped his hands on his apron and prepared his sales voice.

Then she stopped at the display table — the one where he'd arranged the past fortnight's output without quite meaning to, the camp and the bridge and the chapel and the figures, a museum of his own amnesia — and went very still.

"Oh," she said.

"Madam?"

She didn't answer him. She picked up the firelit camp, turned it, set it down, and picked up instead the figure of the woman at the bridge railing — the first one, the one with the unfinished face — and then, setting that down too, she reached for the walnut-sized head. The whalebone face. Her thumb moved across the cheekbone with a gentleness that made Marguerite's throat close for no reason he could name.

"This is Ines," the widow said.

The name landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the splash. It never came. Just the falling, and the falling, and the dark.

"I'm sorry?" he managed.

"Ines." The widow looked up at him, puzzled by his puzzlement. "Ines Ashgrove — no, wait, that's not right, she wouldn't have taken the name. Ines... oh, I've met her, surely I've met her. At the Renewal, last winter. Everyone in the cathedral saw her. She anchored the rite." The widow's eyes widened as the memory assembled itself. "They say she gave her memories to free King Aldric. Both of them did — her and the carver. There were ballads about it for a month, until the ballads got it wrong enough times that people stopped singing them." She turned the little face toward him. "But this is her. This is exactly her. Whoever carved this loved her."

The bell over the door seemed very loud in the quiet that followed.

"Madam," Marguerite said carefully, "I carved that."

"Yes," she said, "I know. You're the carver. The other one. The one from the ballads." She said it the way people say *you're the ghost*, with a flicker of fear under the wonder. Then, being a practical woman, she rallied. "How much for the face? I'll take the face. And the camp, if it's for sale. My husband and I used to travel; I like the fire."

"They're not—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "They're not finished."

"The face is finished," she said. "Believe me, dear, I've spent sixty years looking at faces. That one is finished."

He named a price at random. She paid it without haggling, which told him he'd named it too low, and she wrapped the whalebone head herself, in her own handkerchief, handling it like a relic. At the door she paused.

"You don't remember her at all, do you?" she asked, not unkindly.

"No," Marguerite said. "Should I?"

The widow studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw made her expression soften into something between pity and awe.

"You gave it away," she said. "For the king. For all of us, they say. And here you are, still making her." She lifted the wrapped bundle slightly. "You can't stop making her. Look at this table. Look at what your hands have been doing for a fortnight while you slept." She shook her head slowly. "Memory lives in more places than the mind, Master Coldharbour. Whoever took yours didn't get all of it. They didn't get your hands."

She left. The bell rang. Marguerite stood alone among seventeen days of shavings, surrounded by a bridge he'd never crossed and a campfire he'd never sat beside and a chapel he'd never drowned in, and understood that he had just received the most important commission of his life — from a dead man's memory of himself, delivered by a stranger's mouth:

Finish the face. Find the name. Remember her.

* * *

He worked through the night, and this time he stayed awake for it, deliberately, watching his own hands the way a man watches a sleepwalker — ready to intervene, unable to look away.

The hands did not need him. That was the humbling truth of it. He sat at his bench with his mind wide open, cataloguing every motion, and his fingers moved with a fluency that belonged to someone else, someone who had spent years learning this face from every angle — laughing, angry, asleep, wet from rain, lit by firelight, older by sorrow than her years. He carved variations without deciding to. A second face, younger, the trust not yet decided. A third, mid-laugh, caught so perfectly that he laughed too, alone at midnight, startled by joy coming out of his own knife.

And the bone — the bone had started whispering.

Not words. Not yet. But the silence that had clung to these pieces was breaking up, softening, like ice going out of a river. When he held the newest face to his ear — an absurd gesture, a madman's gesture, and he made it anyway — he heard something underneath the stillness. A murmur. The sense of a voice about to arrive, the held breath before speech.

His gift was finding its way back along a road his mind had forgotten existed.

Near dawn, he carved the last piece without understanding why until it was done: a pair of hands, life-size, rendered in pale birchwood-bone, open and cupped as if offering something. He set them on the table facing the door.

Then he slept, dreamlessly, for the first time in weeks.

* * *

He woke to knocking.

Not the shop bell — knocking, hard and urgent, at the street door, and gray daylight was already well up, which meant he'd slept past opening, which meant customers were getting impatient, except that whoever this was knocked like the law, three sharp blows and a pause, three blows and a pause.

Marguerite came down the stairs with his heart doing something complicated and opened the door on a woman in the grey travel-cloak of the restored Order, hood back, hair tied plainly, a face he knew the way you know a word that's been on the tip of your tongue for a month.

She was carrying the whalebone head. His widow's handkerchief was gone; the piece rode bare in her palm, and she was looking at it — at her own face — with an expression he recognized from his own mirror these past seventeen days: recognition without recollection, the knot pulling tight.

"I bought this from a widow in the market," Ines said. "She told me where she got it. She told me who made it." Her voice was steady the way a rope is steady — bearing enormous weight, quivering somewhere along its length. "Seventeen pieces, the shopkeeper says. Seventeen, in seventeen days. Faces and bridges and a chapel under water." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry and furious and terrified, all three at once, and he thought: *I know that look. I have never seen this woman in my life, and I know that look.*

"I dreamed about you," she said. "Every night since the rite. I wake up with your name in my mouth and it means nothing. I lead the Order now. I rebuilt it from ash. And every night I lose a dream I can't keep, and every morning I think — *go to Vellamark, go to the carver's shop, go and see* — and I tell myself it's duty. Oversight. The Order should keep accounts with the man who paid its founding price." She stepped closer. The whalebone face sat in her palm between them like a small sun. "It isn't duty. Is it?"

"No," Marguerite said. His voice came out rough. "It isn't."

"My hands remember you," she said. "That's the worst of it. I shake hands with ambassadors and my hands are disappointed. I hold my oath-staff and my grip is wrong, too careful, like it's used to holding something else. Something smaller. Warmer." She closed her fist around the little face. "What did we do to ourselves, Marguerite?"

Hearing her say his name went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor. He hadn't told her his name. The widow must have. Or the shopkeeper. Or — it didn't matter. It fit in his ears like a key.

"There's a place," he heard himself say. "I don't remember it, but my hands do. A chapel. Flooded. I've carved it four times." He was reaching for his coat before he'd decided to move, seventeen days of compulsion suddenly resolving into direction. "The dead there — if anywhere, the dead there will still have us. What we poured out went somewhere. Memory doesn't burn. It's only ever given, or taken, or—"

"*Kept,*" Ines finished, and the word hung between them, and neither of them knew which of them had said it first, which was its own kind of answer.

She pulled up her hood. He banked the lamp, took his knives, took the coat that smelled of shavings and birch dust, and locked the door of a shop full of faces he was finally beginning to recognize — hers, all of them, always hers.

The drowned chapel was three days' ride north.

Neither of them looked back.
