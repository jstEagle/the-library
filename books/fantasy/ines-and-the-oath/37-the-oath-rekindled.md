## 37. The Oath Rekindled

The chapel was smaller than he remembered it—or rather, than his hands remembered it. That was the thing about the drowned place now: the water had been drained in spring, by order of the new king, and the crypt stood open to the air for the first time in a century. Sunlight fell through the broken roof in pale columns and lit the green-black stain on every wall where the flood had lived so long.

Marguerite stopped at the threshold.

He didn't know why he'd come. Ines had asked, and he had said yes, and that was all—except it wasn't all, because his body had gone strange on the road here, his pulse climbing with every mile as if it knew something his mind did not.

"You don't have to," Ines said beside him. She wore her oath-keeper's grey again, the rebuilt Order's first true uniform, and she kept her hands folded like a woman holding herself together at the seams. "I told you what I hoped. I shouldn't have hoped out loud."

"You hoped the dead would give back what we gave them." His voice came out flat, careful. "You said it on the road. You've been saying it for three weeks."

"I know what I said."

"Then let me say what I know." He stepped into the chapel. The light took him. Dust moved around him like slow snow. "The dead don't give things back, Ines. I've listened to them my whole life. They keep. They don't return."

She followed him down the nave, past the pews furred with moss, to the crypt stair. The water line still showed on the stone, a dark tide-mark at shoulder height, and below it everything was clean the way drowned things are clean—scoured, pale, honest.

"I brought you here anyway," she said.

"I know."

"Because I thought—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because when you poured yourself into the rite, you were standing in this room. Part of you went into the bones of this place. I thought maybe the chapel held the shape of it. Maybe if you stood where you stood, something would... settle."

"Settle," he repeated.

"Like sediment." A ghost of a smile. "Like silt after a flood. You're a carver. You know how a piece settles when you set it down right."

He did know that. He turned a slow circle in the crypt, looking at the niches where the drowned dead had lain, now empty—the Order had seen them properly buried, named, sung over. He had helped carry some of them himself, in the weeks after the unbinding, though he could not have told anyone why he volunteered. His hands had known. His hands always knew.

Nothing settled. Nothing stirred. For the first time since the rite, he heard nothing at all from the dead—not even a murmur. The chapel was silent as a held breath.

"It's empty," he said. "Whatever was here, we spent it. All those voices, poured through me into the vessels." He looked at his palms, the fine white scars the rite had left there, like the marks of holding something too hot too long. "There's nothing left to listen to."

Ines's face did something complicated. Hope going out of it by degrees, the way a fire dies when no one feeds it.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That was selfish. Dragging you out here because I can't stand—"

"Don't apologize for wanting it back." He said it harder than he meant to. "Gods, Ines. Don't ever apologize for that. I want it back too. I just don't know how to want a thing I can't remember."

They stood in the silence then, two strangers in a drained chapel, and Marguerite became aware of the sound of their breathing, how close they were standing without meaning to, how his body had angled toward hers like a compass finding north. He watched her notice it too. Watched her step back, deliberately, and hated the space she made.

"The commission," she said, businesslike, retreating into formality the way she retreated into everything. "The memorial. That's why I really came, under the pretext. The Order wants a piece for the cathedral. Names of the dead. I thought you might—"

"I already started it."

Her head came up. "What?"

"At the shop. Before you asked." He turned his hands over, showing her the fresh nicks on his fingers, the splinters under the nails. "I wake up and there's whalebone dust on the bench and I don't remember sitting down. I've carved four faces I don't recognize. A bridge. A fire. Two cups." He paused. "A woman's hands, folded. Grey sleeves."

Ines looked at her own hands. Folded. Grey sleeves.

"That's not a commission," she said softly. "That's not memory either. What is that?"

"I don't know." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "My mind is a house with the furniture gone. But my hands keep setting the table. Every morning. Like they're expecting someone."

* * *

They climbed out of the crypt into the nave, and the light had shifted, gone gold and low, evening coming early through the winter trees beyond the broken roof. Ines walked the aisle slowly, trailing her fingers along the backs of the pews, and Marguerite watched her and understood suddenly that she wasn't touching the wood. She was walking a route she'd walked before. Here, with him. Some other day, some other light.

"You've been here since," he said. "After the rite."

"Twice." She didn't turn around. "Three times. I keep coming back like a dog to a door that's been shut." Now she turned, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I led the rebuilding of the Order, Marguerite. I restored the archives. I testified before the king about the binding, about Master Corvane, about all of it, and everyone says how strong I am, how composed, and none of them know that every night I lie awake trying to hold on to moments that only one side of me remembers. You poured your half into the bones. Mine is still here." She pressed her fist against her sternum. "It's still *here*, and it's the loneliest thing in the world, remembering alone."

"Ines—"

"And the worst part," she went on, voice fraying, "the truly unforgivable part, is that I asked you here hoping for a miracle, knowing there wouldn't be one, because I wanted one more hour of standing near you. Even like this. Even as strangers. Because strangers is more than I get anywhere else."

The words hung in the gold light between them.

Marguerite found that his heart was doing something foolish and steady, beating like a drum in a procession, like a heartbeat that had somewhere to go.

"Come here," he said.

She didn't move. "Why?"

"Because I'm going to tell you something, and I'd rather say it close."

She crossed the nave slowly, boots crunching on fallen plaster, and stopped an arm's length away, chin up, braced for grief. He had learned that about her in these three weeks: she braced for grief the way soldiers braced for arrows. Someone had taught her to expect the worst and call it wisdom.

He reached out and took her hand. Her fingers were cold. His were warm from the walk, and he felt her whole arm tremble at the contact, a shiver running through her like wind through wheat.

"I don't remember you," he said.

"I know."

"Listen to all of it before you brace." He tightened his grip gently. "I don't remember you. Not your name before you told me. Not your face before the mirror in my shop. Not Greyford Bridge, not the fire you talk about in your sleep sometimes when you doze off in my back room—I hear you, by the way, you snore like a small determined animal—"

That startled a wet laugh out of her.

"—not any of it. It's gone. Truly gone. I checked. I've sat up nights turning over every stone in my own skull, and there's just... smooth ground where you should be." He drew a breath. "But I think I loved you."

Her breath caught audibly.

"I think I loved you," he said again, slower, tasting each word, testing them against the evidence of his own body, "because when you walk into a room, something in my chest turns over like a key. Because I carve your hands without deciding to. Because when Serane mentioned your name last week I felt proud, actually proud, like a man hearing good news about family. Because I stood in this chapel just now and my heart was pounding before I ever reached the door, like it recognized the address." His voice dropped. "Because every night I grieve someone I can't picture, and every morning I wake up lighter for no reason, and the only variable in either is you."

The light was almost gone now. Blue dusk filled the chapel, and the first stars showed through the broken roof, cold and precise.

Ines was crying without any noise at all, tears sliding down her face in the dark, and she was smiling, which broke him a little, that combination.

"That's not love," she whispered. "That's the shape love left."

"Yes," he said. "And I've decided the shape is enough to build on. I'm a carver, Ines. I work from shapes all the time. Give me the outline of a thing and my hands will find the rest." He lifted her hand, the cold one, and pressed it flat against his chest, over the turning-over thing. "So here is my proposal. Not a miracle. Not the dead giving anything back. Just this: I don't remember you, but I think I loved you. And I would like to find out if I was right."

For a long moment she said nothing. He felt her pulse through her wrist, fast as a bird's.

Then: "Then love me again."

It came out steady. It came out like an oath—which, he supposed, was the only way she knew how to say anything that mattered.

"Say it properly," he managed, because his throat had gone tight. "Oath-keepers have forms. I've read the archives you sent me. There's a right way to swear a thing."

"There is." She wiped her face with her free hand, laughed at herself, and squared her shoulders, and for a moment she was every inch the woman who had once bound a spirit to a crown and later unbound it, who had carried a kingdom's guilt for twenty years and set it down. "Repeat after me, then, carver. This is the oldest form we have. Older than the Order. Older than the crowns."

"I'm listening."

"'What was given, I give again.'"

His heart slammed. "*What was given, I give again.*"

"'Not from memory, but from choice.'"

"Not from memory, but from choice."

"'Not because I must, but because I would.'"

"Not because I must," he said, and his voice cracked on it, "but because I would."

She stepped closer, closing the arm's length entirely, and put her other hand against his cheek. Her palm was warming now. "And the seal," she whispered, "in the old form, is—"

"I know the seal." He did know it; his hands knew it; they rose without instruction and framed her face the way they had been framing it in whalebone for three weeks, thumbs at her cheekbones, fingers in her hair. "Every carving I've made of you ends at this exact moment. My hands keep stopping here. I think they were waiting for permission."

"Granted," she said.

He kissed her, and the world did not split open, and the dead did not murmur, and no memory came flooding back through the ruined doors of his mind. None of the miracles happened. Instead something much stranger occurred: the turning-over thing in his chest went quiet. Settled. Like a piece set down right. Like silt after a flood.

When they broke apart, foreheads touching, her breath mingling with his in the cold blue dark, Ines made a small sound that was half laugh, half sob.

"What?" he asked.

"The chapel," she said. "Listen."

He listened. Old habit, old gift—and there, beneath the silence, beneath the emptiness where the drowned chorus used to be, he caught it: faint as a thread, warm as banked coals. Not the old dead. Something new. Something that had just arrived.

Voices. Faint, formless, unborn—but present. The chapel was beginning to gather echoes again, the way a shell gathers the sea. And underneath them, unmistakable, his own voice—his own voice from some hour he couldn't reach—saying a name.

*Ines.*

"It's not giving anything back," he murmured against her hair. "But it's keeping. It keeps everything, eventually. Maybe someday—"

"Someday," she agreed, and kissed him again, briefly, decisively, the way she did everything. Then she pulled back, eyes bright, and the strategist surfaced through the woman, the planner through the mourner. "All right. If we're doing this—if we're swearing oaths—we do it properly. No more of this circling-each-other-in-workshops nonsense. You'll come to Vellamark. Formally. There's a guest house by the Order hall, and Serane has been threatening to inspect your security for a month anyway, and—"

"Ines."

"—and the memorial commission will take weeks, months, you'll need to study the faces of the dead, it's practically a professional obligation—"

"*Ines.*"

She stopped. "What?"

"Ask me plainly," he said. "No commissions. No pretexts. You taught me that tonight—no more bracing, no more excuses. Ask me the way you swore just now."

She looked at him for a long moment in the starlight. Then she smiled—slow, real, unarmored, a smile he suspected very few people alive had ever seen—and said:

"Stay with me. Not as a stranger learning a stranger. As a man courting a woman. Badly, probably. With arguments and terrible poetry and Serane glaring at us across dinner tables. Stay, and fall in love with me a second time, and let me spend the rest of my life telling you the story of the first."

Outside, far off down the valley road, a lantern bobbed—Serane's escort, patient, punctual, waiting to walk them home. The chapel held its new small silence around them, keeping, always keeping.

Marguerite laced his fingers through hers, and found that his hands, at last, had stopped carving.

"Yes," he said. "Now show me the way out of here. I've been lost in this chapel twice today, and I refuse to begin our second life by sleeping among the dead."

Ines laughed—a real one, loud, echoing off the drained walls, adding itself to whatever the chapel was learning to keep—and pulled him toward the door, and the light, and the long road home.
