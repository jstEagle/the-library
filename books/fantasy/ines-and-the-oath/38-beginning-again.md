## 38. Beginning Again

The first thing Marguerite learned about courting a woman he had already loved and could not remember loving was that it required paperwork.

Not literal papers—though Ines, being Ines, had actually suggested they draw up terms, the way the old Order formalized oaths. She'd been half joking. He'd caught her sharpening a quill before she admitted it.

No, the paperwork was internal. Every time she laughed, some part of him braced against the joy, as if joy were a debt he'd already paid once and been robbed of. Every time her hand found his across a table, he felt the ghost of a gesture—familiar, expected—and then the hollow where its history should have been. He was learning to live in that hollow. To furnish it instead of mourning it.

"You're staring at my knuckles again," Ines said.

They sat in his workshop on a grey March morning, rain ticking against the shutters, two cups of barley tea steaming between them. She'd taken to coming three mornings a week, ostensibly to commission pieces for the rebuilt Order's memorial hall. Ostensibly. The commissions were real—she paid honestly, overpaid, and he'd stopped arguing—but the work was finished now, and still she came.

"I'm trying to memorize them," he said. "In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case I lose this too." He turned her hand over, studying the calluses: sword callus on the palm heel, quill callus on the middle finger, an old burn scar along the thumb he'd asked about twice because he kept forgetting the answer. "Tell me again about the burn."

"Training yard, age nineteen. I grabbed a practice blade someone had left too near the brazier because I thought it was mine." She watched his face while he listened, and he knew she was doing what he did—checking for cracks, for signs that the story landed somewhere or nowhere. "You carved me a salve dish for it. Willow bark and tallow. You said my hands were worth more than any oath I'd sworn."

The words went through him like water through sand. Nothing held. But something *warmed*—a heat without shape, grief without content.

"That sounds like something I'd say," he managed.

"It is," she said softly. "It was."

* * *

They had rules. They'd made them in the drowned chapel, walking back through the rain with his confession hanging in the air between them like breath in cold weather. *I don't remember you, but I think I loved you.* *Then love me again.*

Rule one: no pretending. They would not perform a marriage they couldn't recall. They would build a new one from timber anyone could inspect.

Rule two: tell everything. The stories of what they lost belonged to both of them now, and hoarding them would only make shrines. So they told each other everything—the firelit camp in the hills, the bridge at Greyford, the way she'd hummed when she thought he slept, the way he'd talked to bones when he thought she wasn't listening.

Rule three, Ines's rule, delivered flatly over their first shared supper: "We do not rush. Whatever we were, we died in that rite. What grows now gets to grow at its own speed."

Marguerite had agreed to all of it. What he hadn't said—what he was only beginning to understand himself—was that the regrowth was already happening in ways neither of them had planned.

* * *

He noticed it first with small things.

A customer came in on a wet Tuesday wanting a charm for her son's first voyage, and Marguerite reached for whalebone without thinking, and as the knife moved, he heard—not the dead, not this time—*himself*. His own voice, faint as a memory of a memory: *She likes the north light. Put the bench by the north window.*

He set down the knife. Stood very still. The workshop was quiet except for rain and the sea-sound of his own blood.

There was no bench by the north window. There never had been. But the voice had spoken with such certainty, such *affection*, that for one vertiginous moment he'd lived inside a fact that had never existed.

That night he told Ines, expecting her to be alarmed. Instead she gripped his arm hard enough to bruise.

"New memories," she said. "Growing where the old ones were poured out. Like—" She stopped, jaw working. "Like bone knitting. The body remembers the shape of what was broken and builds around it."

"Or it's rot," he said. "Or the spirit left something behind. Or I'm making up ghosts because I can't stand the quiet."

"Or," Ines said, and her eyes were bright and fierce and afraid all at once, "you are the same man who heard a king's death in a soldier's dying hand, and your gift doesn't distinguish between the dead and the lost. Maybe the memories aren't gone, Marguerite. Maybe they're just buried so deep even you have to carve down to reach them."

He wanted to believe her. He made himself not believe her, because hope that sharp cuts going in as well as coming out. But that night, alone, he took the whalebone charm from its shelf—the one he'd wept over without knowing why—and held it to his ear the way he'd hold any listening-piece.

Nothing. Just the sea trapped in old bone, the sound every whalebone keeps.

But when he set it down, his hand knew exactly where to place it. Center of the shelf. Where it would catch the morning light.

* * *

Spring came to Vellamark the way forgiveness comes—slowly, then all at once, then with mud everywhere.

They walked. That was the whole courtship, some weeks: walking. Along the harbor wall where the Hunt's pyres had once been built and where now children flew kites made from torn proclamation paper. Up through the terraces to the old watchtower. Out the Greyford road as far as the bridge, which they always stopped short of crossing, both of them, without discussing why.

At the bridge's near end there was a milestone worn smooth by centuries of travelers' hands. Ines touched it every time.

"Sergeant Brannic," she said, on the fourth such visit. "You charmed him with a token—a little fox, you told me. Carved it in under a minute while I forged our papers. He let us through and asked if you'd take a commission for his wife's name-day."

"A fox." Marguerite looked at the milestone, at the brown water sliding beneath the arches. "Why a fox?"

"Because you'd seen one on the road that morning and it had looked at you like it knew you were lying." She smiled at the river. "You said everything that survives out here is a good liar. Foxes, rivers, us."

He crouched, put his palm flat on the milestone, and waited. This was new, this practice of his—listening to places the way he listened to bone. Stone remembered differently. Slower. Dumber, almost, like a faithful dog that knew only that many feet had passed.

But beneath the dumbness: a flicker. Cold morning. A bored sergeant. A scrap of carved wood changing hands, and laughter—his own laughter, hers—and the specific weightless terror-joy of getting away with something together.

It wasn't memory. It was less than memory, a stain rather than a picture. But it was *theirs*, embedded in the world itself, outside his skull where the rite couldn't touch it.

"The world remembers us," he said, standing. "Even when I don't."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Then she stepped close, took his face in both hands—the sword-callused palm, the quill callus, the burn scar he finally, finally remembered the origin of without asking—and kissed him.

It was their third kiss since the chapel. The first had been an accident of relief. The second, an experiment. This one was a decision, deliberate as her signature, and it lasted until the river had said several things worth hearing.

When they broke apart, she rested her forehead against his. "New rule," she murmured. "We add to the record. Everywhere we go, we leave something for the stone to keep."

* * *

So they did.

A carved sparrow left on the windowsill of the inn where she'd been captured and he'd fled—an apology to a place that had held their worst night, and a thank-you, because the innkeeper's daughter had smuggled bread to Ines in her cell and never once asked why. A pair of names scratched inside the bell tower at the drowned chapel, high up where the flood line couldn't reach: not their true names, but the names they'd traveled under, MARGUERITE GREY and INES OF THE BROKEN ORDER, because the false names had carried more truth than the real ones ever had.

And in his workshop, on evenings when the tea went cold and the candles burned to stubs, they told the stories again. Not because he needed them repeated—he'd heard each one five times now—but because telling had become its own kind of rite, and because sometimes, in the telling, something surfaced.

"Tell me about the motherhouse," he said one night in April. He'd been avoiding that one; the illusions there had shown him a life where he'd never hidden, and the shape of that lie still itched under his skin.

Ines turned her cup slowly. "You want the trial part?"

"All of it."

"There was a corridor lined with the portraits of every keeper who'd ever served. In my illusion, my portrait hung among them. First woman to lead the Order unbroken." Her mouth twisted. "Forty years of service. A wing of the motherhouse named for me. And my mentor—old Maester Corvess, the one who soured the binding—alive, honored, forgiven. Everyone I'd failed, safe. Everything I'd broken, mended. All I had to do was stay."

"And you didn't stay."

"No." She met his eyes. "Because I looked at that perfect life and felt nothing. And I thought—if this is what I get for abandoning the truth, then the truth must be worth more than this. Then I heard you screaming somewhere below me, in your own illusion, and I chose the screaming." A pause. "What did yours show you?"

"A shop with a sign in the window. *Bone-carver, licensed.* No hearth full of ash, no back window worn smooth from climbing out of it. My mother alive, my father proud, customers who knew exactly what I was and applauded anyway." He laughed, but it came out wrong. "Ten years of hiding, undone. And do you know what I felt? Not joy. *Vertigo.* Like standing on a cliff that turns out to be painted on canvas. Because every good thing in it was built on the lie that hiding had never cost me anything."

"And you rejected it."

"I chose the flawed truth." He reached across the table and took her hand. "I seem to keep choosing it. It has better company."

* * *

In May, the new memories began arriving faster.

He'd be planing a plank of yew and suddenly know—in his hands, in his spine—that Ines hated the smell of yew shavings, said it reminded her of the tanneries. He'd never been told that. Or had he? He couldn't find the seam between learned and recovered anymore, and eventually he stopped looking, because it didn't matter. The knowledge was true either way. She did hate yew. She confirmed it, astonished, and then sat down hard on his work stool and cried for a minute, quietly, the way she did everything administrative.

"They're coming back," she whispered.

"Or new ones are growing."

"Does it matter?" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, impatient with her own tears. "Marguerite, does it actually matter whether these are the old memories resurrected or new ones born? Either way, they're ours. Either way, they're *real*."

He thought about it for a long time, turning a scrap of boxwood over and over in his fingers.

"When I was young," he said finally, "my master told me that bone doesn't heal. That a fracture stays a fracture forever—you just grow strong around it. I spent years thinking that was a sad thing to learn." He set the boxwood down. "But look at any old bone under a lens. The healed break is the strongest part. Densest. The body remembers where it failed and builds armor there."

Ines stared at him. "Did you just compare our love to a healed fracture?"

"I'm a bone-carver. It's how I say romantic things."

She laughed—full, unguarded, the laugh he'd been collecting like coins—and threw a yew shaving at his head, and he caught her wrist mid-second-throw, and the afternoon dissolved into the kind of wrestling that wasn't, and later wasn't, and the workshop smelled of shavings and candle smoke and the particular human warmth of two people rebuilding a house on foundations they could finally see.

* * *

On the last night of May, she stayed.

Not the way the town gossips would eventually decide she'd stayed—though that happened too, and neither of them apologized for it, and Serane, visiting with a basket of pears and her reformed guard's insignia newly pinned, took one look at the two teacups and said, "Finally," with the satisfaction of a woman closing a ledger.

No. Before that, Ines stayed because of the storm.

It came off the sea at dusk, a proper spring gale, and she'd have had to walk the harbor road in it to reach the Order's lodgings, and Marguerite said "stay" the way he'd once said it to a dying soldier's bone: simply, and meaning it completely. She stayed. They ate bread and honey by the hearth—the same hearth where he'd burned ten years of vessels, though neither of them mentioned that—and when the wind screamed against the shutters, she flinched, and he saw her go somewhere else entirely: a cell under the palace, iron door booming, alone with a captor's questions and her own gambled truth.

"Hey," he said. "The storm's outside. We're inside. That's the whole arrangement."

"I know where I am." But her hands were white around the cup.

So he did the thing his hands had been aching to do for weeks. He took the small piece of whalebone he'd been carving in secret—finished that afternoon, hidden in his apron pocket through supper—and pressed it into her palms.

It was a sparrow. Not the harbor sparrows, scrappy and grey, but the bird from the inn windowsill, rendered finer, wings folded, head tilted as if listening. And inside, where no eye would ever see, he'd carved words so small they were more intention than inscription:

*I heard you before I knew you. I will hear you again.*

Ines turned it over twice. Three times. Then she looked up at him with an expression he'd only ever seen her wear once before—in a flooded crypt, pulling him back from a chorus of the dead, refusing to let the dark have him.

"You carved this for me."

"For the woman I'm falling in love with the second time." He swallowed. "Which is either easier or harder than the first, and I intend to find out."

She kissed him then, storm and all, and the whalebone lay warm between their hands, and if it whispered anything—to him, to her, to the listening dark—neither of them said so aloud.

* * *

June arrived. The Order's memorial hall opened with Ines at its head, and Marguerite stood in the crowd watching her speak, and thought: *I am building something with this woman, plank by plank, and this time I will be present for every nail.*

Walking home through the evening market, past stalls selling early strawberries and charms that were merely decorative now, legally, wonderfully merely decorative, he passed a stall of secondhand goods and stopped.

On a blanket between a cracked mirror and a bundle of candlesticks sat a wooden box. Small. Dark oak, brass fittings gone green. And his hands—his traitor, faithful, remembering hands—knew it before his mind did.

He'd owned that box. He'd *made* that box. And inside it—

His heart slammed. He paid the vendor without haggling, which the vendor clearly found alarming, and carried the box home through streets gone soft with dusk, and set it on his workbench, and stood over it for a long time, breathing.

Inside, he was nearly certain, were the things he'd saved from the hearth-fire ten years ago—or rather, the one thing. The whalebone piece he'd kept when he burned everything else. The original. The one whose twin now lived on Ines's shelf.

He opened the brass clasp.

The box was empty except for a slip of paper, folded small, yellowed at the creases. With hands that had steadied themselves over dying soldiers and living kings, Marguerite unfolded it.

One line, in his own handwriting, in ink he didn't remember buying:

*If you are reading this, you gave her everything once. Don't be afraid to give her less than everything this time. Less than everything is enough to build on.*

Below it, smaller, almost erased:

*Her name is Ines. Say it every day.*

He read it four times. Then he lit a candle, sat down at his bench, and picked up a fresh piece of whalebone—for tomorrow was the anniversary of nothing he could remember and everything he needed to keep—and began, carefully, to carve.
