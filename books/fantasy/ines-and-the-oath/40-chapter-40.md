## 40. Chapter 40

The frost came early that year, silvering the shutters of the carver's shop on Tanner's Row before the bells had finished ringing in the hour. Marguerite Coldharbour stood at his workbench with a whalebone blank in his hands and did not begin.

He had learned, over twelve months of relearning everything, that bone could not be hurried. That was the first lesson the dead had ever taught him, back when he was a boy pressing his ear to a sheep's knuckle and hearing his grandmother hum. Bone kept its own time. It remembered at its own pace. And lately—wonderfully, terrifyingly—it seemed to remember things he himself had forgotten.

The whalebone blank was warm. It was always warm. He had stopped questioning that around midsummer.

Outside, Vellamark woke the way it always did: cart wheels on cobbles, the fishwives arguing prices at the fountain, a child shrieking with delight about something no adult could see. A year ago those sounds had been cover for him, camouflage for a man walking through his own life with his collar up. Now they were just sounds. Now when the neighbor's boy banged on his door demanding a carved wolf for his sister's name-day, Marguerite opened it without checking the street first, and the boy barged past him into the shop like he owned the place, because in every way that mattered, he did.

"Master Coldharbour! Master Coldharbour, is it done?"

"It's done when it's done, Tam."

"You said three days ago it was nearly done."

"I said it was nearly begun." But he was already reaching under the counter, already drawing out the little wolf he'd carved from antler—running pose, ears back, jaw open on a silent laugh—and Tam received it with both hands and the solemnity of a knight accepting a sword.

"Tell your mother the coin can wait until the thaw," Marguerite said. "Or don't tell her at all."

Tam grinned, pocketed nothing, and thundered out. Through the window Marguerite watched him go, weaving between market stalls, holding the wolf up to the grey light. On the corner post of the apothecary across the lane—rebuilt now, repainted, its ash-scarred timbers planed smooth—the notice board held no likeness of anyone. It held a playbill, and a proclamation about drainage works, and a child's drawing of the king that made Aldric look like a turnip with a crown. Nobody had taken it down. The king, they said, had laughed at it himself.

Marguerite turned the whalebone blank over in his hands.

He still didn't know what it was going to be. That was new for him. For ten years in hiding, every piece he carved had been commissioned, discreet, deniable—a mourning charm here, a luck-piece there, each one a small crime committed so smoothly it barely felt like one. Now the crimes were lawful and the commissions came openly, and yet this piece had come to him unbidden, the way the old gift came: not as an idea but as an ache. His hands wanted to carve something and would not tell him what.

He set it down. There were orders to fill. There was always orders to fill; half the city seemed to want a piece of bone now that owning one wouldn't get you burned. He took up a commission for the harbor guild—a ship's figurehead charm, no larger than a fist—and let the work carry him the way water carries a leaf, out past thought, into the murmuring dark where the dead lived in their ivory cities.

The harbor guild's dead had little to say. Sailors' bones mostly remembered weather. He listened anyway, out of habit, out of love, while his knife whispered along the grain.

* * *

Serane Voss arrived at midday, which meant she arrived quietly, which meant she was off duty.

A year ago the captain of the Hunt entering his shop would have been the last sound Marguerite ever heard. Now she pushed open the door with her shoulder, ducked under the hanging charms—he'd strung them across the whole ceiling, dozens of them, a small wooden and bone sky—and dropped onto the stool by the stove like a woman who had been doing it all her life, because she had been doing it for most of a year.

"Your kettle's cold," she said.

"Your boots are wet."

"The river path flooded again. The city fathers promised drainage works." She nodded at the playbill direction. "I've seen the proclamation. Very reassuring."

He put the kettle on. She watched him with the particular attention she gave everything—not suspicion anymore, or not only, but the habit of a woman who had spent years reading rooms for danger and couldn't fully unlearn it. Her hair was shorter than it had been. There was a scar along her jaw he didn't remember her earning, though he supposed a great many things had happened to all of them that he only knew secondhand, through other people's telling, through bones.

"Reformed Guard business?" he asked.

"Day off."

"Captain Voss doesn't take days off."

"Commander Voss," she corrected mildly, "has learned to delegate." She flexed her fingers toward the stove's warmth. "I came to ask you something, actually. Not a commission."

"Then you're in the wrong shop."

"A memorial." Her voice changed, the way it always did when it went near that country. "For the drowned chapel dead. The city finally voted the funds. They want to raise the crypt, drain it proper, give them names on stone. Four hundred names. We have perhaps sixty."

The kettle began its low climb toward singing. Marguerite sat down across from her.

"You're asking me to listen," he said.

"I'm asking if it's possible. Sixty families waiting a year already. If there's a way to give them even thirty more—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "You told me once the chapel chorus nearly took you under. I haven't forgotten that. I won't order it. I'm asking."

He looked at his hands. Knife-callused, ink-stained at the edges, steady now in a way they hadn't been for ten frightened years. He thought of the flooded dark, the hundreds of voices braided together, the pull of it, like sinking into warm water that wanted to keep you. He thought of Ines's hand closing on his wrist in that black water, hauling him back to air and self.

"It's possible," he said. "Not alone. I'll want the Order's anchor-keepers with me. And I'll want—" He paused, and Serane's mouth curved very slightly, because she knew exactly what he'd say next.

"Ines is due back Thursday," she said. "The northern circuit ended early. Good news out of Greyford, for once. The border villages signed the accord."

"Greyford," Marguerite said, and felt something move in him that had no name attached to it, a warmth with no memory behind it, like sunlight falling on a scar. "I keep almost remembering Greyford."

"Don't force it." Serane said it gently, and it was strange to hear gentleness in that voice, stranger still to know how earned it was. "That's what the healers at the motherhouse say. What's poured out isn't gone. It's *elsewhere*. You don't dig for it. You leave the door open."

"And meanwhile my hands remember everything and my head remembers nothing."

"Your hands are wiser than your head. Most people's are." She stood, took her coat, paused at the door under the little bone sky. "Thursday," she said. "She'll come straight here. She always does."

The door closed. The kettle sang. Marguerite poured the water over the leaves and drank tea he didn't taste, looking at the whalebone blank on his bench, which was warm, which was waiting.

* * *

Wednesday night he dreamed of a bridge.

Not Greyford Bridge itself—he'd seen sketches of it, heard it described—but the dream version, the one his sleeping mind kept building from materials he didn't know he possessed. In the dream it was dusk and there was a checkpoint and a bored sergeant, and someone beside him was laughing behind her hand at the outrageous lie he was telling, and the sergeant was turning the little carved token over in his fingers, and the token was warm—

He woke with his heart going hard and his cheeks wet, and lay in the dark of the room above the shop trying to hold the pieces. A fire. Two travelers. Her voice saying *you magnificent liar* in a tone that was not reproach at all. It dissolved even as he reached for it, the way dreams do, leaving only the temperature of it. Warmth. Firelight. The sense—unbearable, specific—that he had once been happier than he currently knew how to be.

He got up and went down to the workshop, because sleep was finished with him, and lit the lamp, and picked up the whalebone blank.

It fit his palm like it had been cast for it. Under the lamp it showed faint whorls, the long gray-gold grain of a leviathan's jaw, salvaged decades ago from some beach far north. He'd owned it since before he could remember why. Chapter three of his life, Ines called it—no. Ines didn't call it anything. He'd read that phrase somewhere, or dreamed it. The past kept doing that: offering itself to him in borrowed voices.

His knife found the grain. He didn't decide anything. He never decided anything with this piece; the deciding had been done long ago by whatever part of him had refused, ten years back, to feed this one bone to the hearth-fire with all the others.

The shape came slowly. A curve first, like a wave, or a hill. Then two figures rising out of it—or maybe they'd been in it all along and he was only uncovering them, which was how carving always felt to him, less making than listening. Two figures seated close. Between them, a small flame. He carved the flame last and left it rough, unfinished, because something in the bone said *not yet*, and he had learned to obey that voice above all others.

It was nearly dawn when he set the knife down. The little campfire scene sat in his palm, no bigger than a heart, and he looked at it and wept—briefly, hopelessly, the way he sometimes did over pieces like this, grief arriving without an address, love arriving without a face.

"Who are you?" he asked the bone, as he'd asked it a hundred times.

The bone, being bone, said nothing.

Yet.

* * *

Thursday came up bright and hard, frost burning off by nine into a blue winter sky. Marguerite worked the counter all morning—name-day charms, a widow's mourning piece he took with genuine care, a merchant wanting a ship's blessing—and kept finding reasons to glance at the clock, and despised himself mildly for it, and kept glancing anyway.

She came at the hour when the light goes gold. He heard the door and looked up and there she was, travel-dusted, cloak still bearing the smell of cold road, her oath-keeper's badge—new-made, the Order restored, the sigil redrawn without the corruption the old masters had folded into it—catching the light at her throat.

"You're back early," he said, and heard how his voice changed, and didn't try to hide it anymore. They had agreed, months ago, not to hide things from each other. It was the only vow either of them trusted.

"The roads were kind." She unwound her scarf. Her eyes went around the shop the way they always did—the hanging charms, the bench, the little campfire scene sitting where he'd left it—and stopped there.

She crossed the room without a word and picked it up.

He watched her hold it. Watched her thumb find the two figures, the small rough flame. Watched something cross her face that he had seen before, on the night she'd first stood in this shop, on the nights after, whenever his hands produced something his mind had lost: recognition moving through her like weather.

"This is new," she said carefully.

"Finished it this morning. Or it finished itself. I don't—I don't know what it is."

"It's us." She said it simply, without drama, the way you'd report the time. "Greyford Bridge. No—the hills after. Our first camp. The night you told me about your grandmother humming through the sheep bone, and I told you I'd never heard the dead at all, not once, in twenty years of keeping oaths. You said that was its own kind of deafness and I threw a pinecone at you."

Marguerite's breath had gone somewhere and stayed there.

"I threw a pinecone at you," she repeated, softer, watching his face. "And you laughed. And I remember thinking—" She stopped. Set the carving down with exaggerated care, the way people handle things when their hands aren't entirely their own. "I'm sorry. I do this every time. The healers said—"

"Leave the door open," he said. "Don't dig. I know."

They stood in the gold light with the little campfire between them, and neither of them moved, and outside a cart rattled past and the world went on being ordinary, which was the miracle none of them had ever gotten used to: that the world had gone on, that the kingdom had been saved, that the Hunt was a bad memory and the spirit slept sealed in its vessels beneath the motherhouse and the two of them were allowed to simply stand in a shop and hurt gently, yearningly, without any of it being the end of anything.

"I brought you something," Ines said at last, and her voice was steadier. "From the north. From Greyford itself."

She took it from her pack: a small flat stone, river-smoothed, with a hole worn through its center. "The bridge keeper's daughter sells them. She says the river gives one back to the bridge every spring, like a tooth. I thought—" A small shrug, defenseless. "I thought you should have a piece of the place your hands keep dreaming about."

He took it. It was cold from her pack and then it wasn't.

"Thank you," he said. "Ines."

"Yes?"

He had practiced this. He'd practiced it for weeks, in the evenings, saying it to the empty shop: *I don't remember you, but I think I loved you.* He'd said it to her once already, months ago, in the drowned chapel with the water whispering below, and she had answered *then love me again*, and they had been doing exactly that, deliberately, tenderly, two people courting across a gap where a shared history used to be—telling each other the stories, learning each other the second time, discovering that the second time had its own textures, its own surprises, that loving someone twice is not repetition but deepening.

But tonight the practiced words wouldn't come. Something else did instead.

"Show me the story again," he said. "The pinecone. All of it. Start from the checkpoint."

Her whole face opened. "You hate that story."

"I know I hate that story. Tell it anyway."

So she sat on the stool by the stove, the same stool Serane had warmed yesterday, and began—*there was a checkpoint at Greyford Bridge, and a bored sergeant, and a carver with honest eyes and a criminal's hands*—and Marguerite listened the way he listened to bone: completely, patiently, letting the story sink into the grain of him, trusting that what was poured out was elsewhere, that doors left open eventually admit the light.

* * *

She stayed until the lamps needed lighting, and then she stayed past that, because the Order could survive one more evening without her and they both pretended not to count the cost and counted it fondly. When she finally rose to go, he walked her to the door, and in the doorway—with the frost coming down again outside, silvering the street, the whole quiet city settling into winter—they did the thing they always did now, the thing that had started as accident and become ritual: their hands brushed.

Lightning. Every time. Neither of them flinched from it anymore. They just stood a moment in the doorway letting it pass through them, current without source, memory without content, the body's stubborn testimony that something true had happened here once and would happen again.

"Thursday," she said, though it was Thursday.

"Every day," he said, which was the answer they'd settled on.

He watched her go down Tanner's Row until the dark took her, then closed the door and stood a while with his palm against the wood.

* * *

The shop was cold and full of small shadows. He banked the stove, trimmed the lamp, and then—because the day had cracked something open in him, because the pinecone story was lodged in his chest like a splinter of light—he went to the bench and took up the whalebone carving one more time.

The little campfire scene. Two figures, a flame left rough.

He held it the way he'd held bones all his life, the way he'd held the dying soldier's oath-bone in an alley that he knew only from other people's retellings, the way he'd held the first vessel in black water while four hundred drowned voices sang. He held it and he listened.

And the bone spoke.

Not the dead. Not the harbor sailors, not the chapel chorus, not any of the thousand strangers whose last words had passed through his hands across two lifetimes. The voice that rose out of the whalebone—faint, warm, firelit, unmistakable—was his own.

*"...and that's why I'll never carve anything for anyone I don't—"* a laugh, his own laugh, younger somehow, lighter, *"—Ines. Ines, look at me. Whatever happens at the coronation tomorrow, whatever this rite takes—knowing you was the best—"*

The voice frayed. The recording of it, pressed into the bone's grain a year ago by hands that had known what they were about to spend—it thinned like smoke, like a fire settling, and the last thing that came through, so soft he had to close his eyes and hold the piece to his ear like a boy with a sheep's knuckle, was his own voice saying her name. Just her name. *Ines.* Said the way you say the one word that contains all the others.

Then silence. Warm, complete, ordinary silence.

Marguerite stood in the lamplight with the carving against his cheek, tears running freely down his face, and he was laughing too, he discovered, laughing and weeping in equal measure, because the dead had given back a living voice, because his past self had hidden a message in the one object he'd never been able to burn, because the bone had waited a full year for exactly this night to speak.

*I loved her,* he thought, and the thought had no memories attached, and it was the truest thing in him. *I loved her, and I said so, and the bone kept it.*

He set the little campfire scene on the shelf above the bench—in the place of honor, where the light would find it first each morning—and beside it he placed the river-stone from Greyford, cold and smooth and holed like a listening ear.

Tomorrow he would tell her. Tomorrow he would press the whalebone into her hands and let her hear his voice say her name, and watch her face do whatever it did, and they would add this night to the growing ledger of second-time stories, the ones they were writing now to replace the ones that were elsewhere.

Tonight he trimmed the lamp, and touched the carving once, and felt it—still warm, always warm, warm as a firelit camp in the hills, warm as a hand brushing his in a doorway.

Outside, the frost came down on Vellamark like a blessing, and somewhere across the sleeping city an oath-keeper was walking home with lightning still in her fingertips, and the bone on the shelf held its small fire, patient as bone always was, keeping its own time, remembering at its own pace—

—and in the deepest grain of the whalebone, so faint that only a listener who loved it could ever find it, another voice waited still. Hers. Pressed there a year ago by her own hands, in the hour after his, when she had come to the shop alone and held this same piece and poured her own farewell into it, believing no one would ever hear.

It was waiting for her to come back.

It would not have to wait long.
