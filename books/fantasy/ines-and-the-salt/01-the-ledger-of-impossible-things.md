## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with impossible things, Edmund Halloran had learned in his nine years at Corvane & Sons, was that they were never as quiet as the ledgers promised.

He sat at the third counting desk from the window, where the afternoon light came through glass thick with salt-frost and fell across his columns like a pale hand. The guildhouse smelled the way it always did on ledger day: tallow, ink, and the faint mineral bite that clung to everything they traded. Impossible things left a residue. Bottled storms sweated brine through their stoppers no matter how well they were waxed. Caged echoes shed their sound slowly, so that a crate marked *one (1) echo, weeping woman, gently used* would whisper at you from the shelf for years after the sale.

Edmund entered figures. He was good at figures. Figures asked nothing of him except patience, and patience he had in the way that a stone has stillness — not a virtue, exactly, but a shape he'd grown into.

*Item forty-one,* he wrote. *Bottled storm, Aeolian class, minor hail content. Sold to the Widow Anser of Quay Street. Condition: restless.*

The storm in question had been restless for three days now, rattling its bottle on the high shelf above the sample room like a fist against a door. Master Corvane said storms settled eventually, once they forgot the sky. Edmund knew better. He could hear it — not the rattle, which anyone could hear, but the thing underneath the rattle: a low, grinding mutter, wind talking to itself about the long grey miles it used to own.

That was the part he hid. That was the part he had always hidden.

In the cellar beneath the counting floor, the beasts were talking again.

There were only four of them down there this season — a marsh-ox the guild had bought from a debt-broken farmer, two lantern-moths in a wire globe, and an old dog-thing called a gravehound whose trade value had collapsed when it stopped being frightening. The ox complained about its knees. The moths argued, endlessly, sweetly, about light. And the gravehound — the gravehound was telling the same story it had told every afternoon for a week, in a voice like dry leaves dragged over stone.

*They are coming,* the gravehound said. *The ones who smell of salt and paper. They are coming, and they will ask for what is not theirs.*

Edmund's pen paused above the page.

He had learned young that hearing beasts was a death sentence in a guild like Corvane & Sons. Not because the gift was rare — the Wardens' manuals suggested otherwise — but because a man who could hear what a caged echo wanted, or what a bottled storm remembered, was a man who might one day ask inconvenient questions about where these things came from and whether they ought to be sold at all. Worse, he was a man the Salt Wardens called *unregistered*, and unregistered gifts went into the white rooms under the Warden-house and did not come out. What came out wore grey and answered to a number.

So Edmund Halloran balanced accounts and kept his ears shut in public and never, ever looked at the cellar grate when something spoke to him.

*Boy,* the gravehound said anyway, because old hounds have no manners. *Boy with the listening face. Someone is walking up your street who does not cast a shadow.*

"That's nonsense," Edmund murmured into his collar, quiet enough that the clerk at the next desk didn't stir. "Everything casts a shadow."

*Not everything,* said the gravehound, satisfied, and lay back down on its cold stones.

Edmund told himself the beast was lying. Beasts lied the way children lied — for attention, for drama, for the pleasure of being believed. He dipped his pen. He wrote *item forty-two*. He got as far as *caged echo, male voice, recites*—

The door of the guildhouse opened without knocking.

*

The stranger came in sideways, the way weather comes in. One moment the entry hall was empty; the next there was a man leaning against the doorframe as though the building had been holding him up all along and had only just decided to admit it.

He was wrong in every particular. Too tall for the stoop he'd been forced into. Too thin for the coat he wore, which hung off him like sailcloth off a wrecked mast. His skin had the grey-gold tint of old parchment, and when he lifted his head Edmund saw eyes the color of shallow water over sand — pale, fixed, and looking directly at him across the whole length of the counting floor.

"Closed," said Master Corvane's nephew from the front desk, without looking up from his newspaper. "Ledger day. Come back Thursday."

The stranger took one step. Then another. His boots left prints on the clean boards, and Edmund, who noticed such things because noticing was how you stayed alive in a place like this, saw that the prints were wet. Wet, and white-rimmed. Salt. As if the man had waded here through the sea, though the sea was four days' ride east and everyone in the room knew it.

"Thursday," the nephew repeated, louder, and then seemed to lose interest entirely, folding his newspaper as if the stranger had become too boring to look at. That was the other thing Edmund noticed, and it raised the hair on his arms: nobody else was looking. A dying man was crossing their counting floor and the clerks bent to their columns like wheat bending away from wind, and Master Corvane himself, passing with a tray of invoices, stepped around the stranger without a flicker, as though the space he occupied were simply empty.

Only Edmund saw him. Only Edmund heard the gravehound below begin, softly, to sing.

It was not a song any kennel-master would have recognized. It was a keening, low and rising, the sound hounds make over the dead — and it was aimed upward, through the floorboards, straight at the man who cast no shadow.

"No," Edmund whispered. "Don't."

But the stranger had reached him. He folded down across the counting desk like a puppet with cut strings, and his hands — cold, crusted white at the knuckles, the hands of someone who had held salt too long — came down on either side of Edmund's ledger and pinned it there.

"You balance," the man said. His voice was a ruin, all gravel and tide. "You count what cannot be counted. Good. Good boy. Then count this."

From inside his ruined coat he drew a folded square of leather, cracked and stiff, stained across one whole edge with a crust of salt so thick it might have been frost or bone. He pressed it into Edmund's hands and closed Edmund's fingers over it with his own, and his grip, for all his dying, was strong as a trap.

"It doesn't open for eyes," the man said. "It opens for a mouth. For a name spoken by someone it remembers." His shallow-water eyes searched Edmund's face with terrible urgency. "Do you understand? It remembers. Even when the world forgot. Even when I—" He stopped. Something moved behind his face like a fish under ice. "Even when I paid it not to."

"I don't know what you're—"

"You do." The man smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was kind. "You've known since I walked in. You have the listening face. Vell always liked the listeners."

The word went through Edmund like a nail through cloth.

*Vell.*

He had never heard it. He was certain he had never heard it — and yet it arrived in his mind already furnished, already worn smooth with use, the way a word you dreamed arrives feeling borrowed from somewhere real. Vell. White streets. A harbor that filled twice a day with water that came from nowhere. His mother's voice saying it over a stove, saying it like a prayer, saying it—

He had no mother's voice saying it. He had been raised by the guild. He had no memory of any stove, any street, any harbor. And yet the name fit somewhere in him like a key fits a lock that has been painted over, and the knowing of it made him sick with a dread he could not source.

"Who are you?" Edmund managed.

"A cartographer," the man said, "of the last country in the world." Blood showed suddenly at the corner of his mouth, thin and bright. "And you are going to be my heir, whether you consent to it or not. Listen. The map needs four readings. Four doors. Each reading costs the reader—" his breath caught, rattled, went on — "costs more than you'll want to pay. Don't read it alone. Whatever you do, don't read it alone. Find the others. Find—"

"What others? Whose name is on it? Who are you?"

The man's grip tightened. His lips shaped a word — gave it breath, gave it weight, spent the last of whatever was holding him upright on it —

"—Ilse," he said. "Tell Ilse her father didn't sell her country. Tell her he only ever tried to buy it back."

Then the light went out of him between one heartbeat and the next, the way a lamp goes out when the oil is gone, and he slid from the desk onto the counting floor of Corvane & Sons and lay there among the ink-stains and the salt, dead as an entry in a ledger.

*

What happened next happened quickly and strangely, and Edmund would spend many sleepless nights afterward trying to decide whether it had happened at all.

Master Corvane appeared with a cloth and began wiping the boards around the body without touching the body. Two porters came and stood at a respectful distance, faces blank as unsold stock. The nephew rang a small brass bell twice — the signal, Edmund knew, for *guild property, deceased, inventory pending* — and somewhere below, the gravehound stopped singing and began instead to laugh, a dry, awful, leaf-scrape sound.

Nobody looked at Edmund. Nobody looked at the map.

Which meant, Edmund understood with a clarity that felt like falling, that as far as the guild was concerned, nothing had changed hands. Which meant the map in his closed fist was either invisible to them or — worse — already accounted for. Already entered somewhere. *Item forty-three: one (1) inheritance, misdelivered.*

He slid the leather square into the hollow spine of his ledger, between the pages for the spring quarter, and closed the book over it the way you close a hand over a coal.

Outside, the light was going amber and long. Somewhere up the street, boots were ringing on cobbles in a rhythm Edmund knew too well — the paired, unhurried tread of Salt Wardens walking their evening rounds, coming toward the guildhouse with the easy confidence of men who had never once been told no.

The gravehound's voice rose through the floorboards one last time, soft and certain as a closing door:

*I told you, boy. They smell of salt and paper. Count fast.*
