## 2. What the Rats Remember

The cellar of Corvane & Sons smelled of brine and old paper, which was fitting, because everything the guild traded came from one or the other. Edmund Halloran descended the stairs with a lantern in one hand and a ledger in the other, and before his boots touched the last step, the rats began to talk.

*The tall one. The tall one who listens.*

He had learned young that listening was the dangerous part. Anyone could hear a dog bark; only Edmund heard what the bark *meant*, and worse — he heard the things beasts said when they weren't barking at all. In Corvane's cellar, where the guild kept its stock of impossible things between sales, the animals talked constantly. A caged echo in a brass bell muttered fragments of other people's arguments. Two bottled storms rolled against each other on their shelf like quarrelsome brothers. And the rats — the rats were philosophers.

"Good evening," Edmund said quietly, setting down the lantern.

*Good evening,* answered the eldest rat, a scarred female who lived beneath the salt crates and had once told him she was older than the building. She spoke in the way rats do, in whisker-twitches and scent-words that arrived in his mind already translated, though no one had ever taught him the grammar. *You come at a strange hour. You come with your face doing something it does not usually do.*

"My face is fine."

*Your face looks like a man who has been handed a map by a dying stranger and has not slept.*

Edmund crouched beside her crate. Above him, through the floorboards, the guildhouse ticked and settled, and somewhere a clerk was still scratching at figures, but down here the dark felt private. He had come for exactly this privacy. He had not slept. The map was folded inside his coat, against his ribs, and it had been there long enough that he could feel its edges through the linen like a second heartbeat.

"I need to ask you something strange," he said.

*All your questions are strange. That is why we answer them.*

"The name Vell." He said it carefully, the way you set down something that might be hot. "Do you know it?"

The silence that followed had weight. The bottled storms went quiet. Even the echo in the bell stopped muttering mid-syllable, as if it too were holding its breath.

Then the old rat said: *Salt-white country. Harbor of nine mouths. The city with the bell tower that leaned like a drunk uncle.* Her whiskers trembled. *We remember Vell.*

Edmund's mouth went dry. "Humans don't."

*Humans don't remember anything longer than they have to,* she said, with rodent contempt. *But we carried it. Rats ride ships, tall one. We rode out of Vell when Vell ended, and we carried the memory in our teeth the way we carry grain. Ask any rat in any port from here to the ice. They all know the word. They just don't know why they know it.*

"Why did it end?"

*It didn't end. It was ended. There is a difference, and the difference has a smell.* She turned her blunt face up toward him. *Why do you carry a dead man's map against your heart?*

"How do you know he's dead?"

*Because you smell like grief and salt, and because you asked about Vell instead of asking about the ledger you brought down here to pretend to check.* Her black eyes glittered. *We saw him die on your counting desk. We were in the walls. We heard what he whispered.*

Edmund's hand rose without permission to his coat. "Tell me."

*His name was Aldric Vane. Last cartographer of Vell. He spent forty years walking a country that was being unwritten, drawing it faster than they could erase it, so that somewhere, someday, someone could find the way back in.* The rat groomed her paws, unhurried, savoring the telling. *And then he walked into a guildhouse full of men who trade in impossible things, and he gave the map to the only person in the room who flinched when a moth died against the lamp glass.*

"I didn't flinch—"

*You grieved. For a moth. That is how we knew you.* She paused. *There are Salt Wardens asking questions upstairs tonight, tall one. We heard them through the floor an hour ago. They used the word 'property.'*

The cold that went through Edmund then had nothing to do with the cellar. "How many?"

*Three. Grey coats. Boots that don't squeak even when wet. One of them smells of the sea in a way that isn't right — like the sea after something bad happened in it.* She looked at him for a long moment, and then she did something no rat had ever done for him. She pressed her small head briefly against his knuckle. *Run, listener. And take the map out of the ledger binding where you hid it this afternoon, because Wardens know ledgers better than they know their own mothers.*

* * *

He made it four steps up the stairs before the lie began forming, and it was a bad lie, and he knew it was a bad lie, and he told it anyway.

The three grey-coated figures stood in the counting room under the gas lamps, and the middle one — a woman with iron-colored hair and eyes the pale green of shallow water over sand — was turning the dead man's hat over in her hands.

"You're the junior keeper of accounts," she said. It wasn't a question. "Halloran."

"Yes." His voice came out half a tone too high. "Edmund Halloran. Can I — help you?"

"Aldric Vane collapsed at this desk this morning. He was carrying certain documents belonging to the Crown Office of Boundary Maintenance. Guild property, recovered property, whatever phrasing your masters prefer." She set the hat down with terrible gentleness. "We'll need to see whatever he was carrying."

"He wasn't carrying anything." Too fast. Far too fast. "That is — he had nothing on him. When he — when it happened. I checked. Standard procedure. After. When people collapse."

The warden looked at him the way a cat looks at a hole in the baseboard. Behind her, one of her companions drifted toward the shelves of ledgers, trailing one finger along their spines, and Edmund's stomach dropped through every floor of the building.

"Standard procedure," the woman repeated.

"Yes."

"For a man dying on your desk, your first act was to inventory his pockets."

"It's a guildhouse," Edmund said. "Everything is inventory."

Something flickered across her face — amusement, maybe, or appetite. The finger reached the shelf where the current year's ledgers stood, and Edmund understood with perfect clarity that he was going to be arrested in the next thirty seconds, and that the map would go into a grey coat, and that Vell — whatever Vell was, whatever the rats remembered, whatever the dying man had seen in his face — would be unwritten twice.

Then the front door banged open and Sefa came in like weather.

She was seventeen, small, quick-fingered, and technically employed by the guild as a runner, which meant she ran messages and also occasionally removed coins from the purses of clients too distracted to notice. She burst into the counting room shouting about a fire in the tannery district, a real fire, three streets over, smoke visible from the clock tower, and the junior wardens moved toward the windows exactly as long as it took Edmund to slide the map from its hiding place inside the day-ledger and drop it down the back of his own collar, flat against his spine, where it lay like a slab of winter.

By the time the wardens turned back, satisfied there was no fire worth their attention and no document worth their search, Sefa was leaning against the doorframe whistling, and Edmund was smiling at the iron-haired woman with a face he hoped resembled innocence.

"We'll return," she said. "Guildhouses keep poor secrets, Mr. Halloran. So do clerks."

When the door closed behind them, Edmund sat down hard on the counting stool, and Sefa looked at him, and he looked at her, and neither of them said *thank you*, because saying it would make it a thing that had happened, and things that had happened could be punished.

"You owe me," she said finally.

"I know."

"And whatever's down the back of your shirt, I want ten percent."

"Sefa—"

"Fifteen, for the lying. Yours was terrible. I've heard better lies from geese."

* * *

They found Brannoc where Brannoc always was after sundown: the drowned-corner tavern off Rope Lane, drinking alone at the end of the bar with the posture of a man who had once stood very straight and never fully forgiven himself for slouching. He had been a Warden once — eleven years, grey coat, salt-ring, the whole inheritance — until the night he'd refused an order at the Nine Mouths incident and been stripped of his ring in front of the entire chapter house. Nobody in the trade trusted him now, which was precisely why Edmund trusted him.

Brannoc listened to the whole story without interrupting, which took a while, because Edmund told it badly, out of order, twice. Then the big man turned his cup slowly on the counter.

"You understand what they'll do to you if they find it," he said. "Not arrest. Not trial. The salt burns the truth out of a man, Halloran. I've watched it done. It takes hours, and afterward the man confesses to things he never did just to make it stop, and then they take him out behind the boundary posts."

"Then don't help me," Edmund said. "I'm not asking. I'm telling you because you're the only person I know who's seen what they do and walked away from it, and I thought—" He stopped. He had not thought past this sentence. He had no ending for it.

"You thought what?"

"I don't know. That you might want to walk toward something for once. Instead of away."

Sefa, wedged between them on the bench, snorted into her cider. But Brannoc didn't laugh. He looked into his cup for a long time, and when he raised his head his eyes were wet in the way that large quiet men's eyes get wet, briefly and furiously.

"Vane," he said. "Aldric Vane. I knew that name once. Eleven years I wore the ring, and there was a file that passed my desk in my third year — a country that didn't exist, erased from every record, and one man's name redacted so hard it tore the page." He drained the cup and set it down like a decision. "I've spent twenty years wondering what was under that redaction."

"So you'll help?"

"I'll help." He stood, and the bench sighed with relief. "But we leave tonight, and we go south, because the confluence at Marrowford is where fresh water meets the tide, and if that map of yours needs reading anywhere, it's there." He pulled his old grey coat from under the bar, where the tavern-keeper had kept it for him these six years out of pity or habit. "And Halloran — whatever you're hiding besides the map, whatever it is you can do that makes stable horses calm when you pass and makes the rats follow you around like a pied piper —"

Edmund went still. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Brannoc agreed grimly, shrugging into the coat. "You lie badly. It's going to be a long road."

Outside, the fog had come up off the harbor, thick and white, and somewhere in it — moving without squeaking, even on wet stones — were boots. Three sets of them, closing from the north.

Sefa felt it first, the way she felt pickings: a prickle at the neck. "Company," she breathed.

Brannoc put a hand on each of their shoulders, turned them south into the fog, and said the words that Edmund would remember for the rest of his life, however many or few those turned out to be:

"Run now. Ask questions in Marrowford."
