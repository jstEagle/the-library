## 3. A Daughter Made of Ash

Sefa had stolen from kings, priests, and one very angry bear-trainer, so she knew the shape of a pursuer before she ever saw a face. It was in the rhythm of footsteps behind them on the canal road — too steady, too patient, the walk of someone who did not need to hurry because they already knew where you were going.

She said nothing for half a mile. She let Edmund chatter about ledger columns and let Brannoc grunt his way through a debate with himself about which of them was least fit to lead, and all the while she counted the footfalls the way other people counted coins.

Three days out of Corvane's guildhouse, and the Salt Wardens had not found them. That was the problem. Wardens found you fast or not at all, and this walker was neither warden nor soldier. This walker had been following them since the ferry at Marn, and she — because Sefa had finally caught the silhouette against the dawn and it was a woman, tall, wrapped in a grey traveling cloak gone stiff with old salt — she walked like grief walks. Like someone carrying a coffin they hadn't finished building.

"She's back," Sefa said at last.

Edmund went pale in that particular way he had, as if blood were something he'd read about in a book. "The warden?"

"No." Sefa glanced over her shoulder. The woman had closed the distance to fifty paces and made no effort to hide it now. Grey cloak, iron-grey braid, a face like a door someone had nailed shut from the inside. "The daughter."

Brannoc stopped walking. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, a disgraced warden who carried his shame the way mules carry loads — without complaint and without setting it down. His hand drifted toward the short sword he wasn't supposed to have anymore. "Ilse Vantre? The cartographer's girl?"

"The cartographer's *daughter*," Sefa corrected. "And she doesn't look like a girl."

The woman stopped when they stopped. Fifty paces of empty road between them, white dust and brown grass and the slow green slide of the canal. She stood very still, and Sefa felt the hair rise on her arms, because stillness like that was worse than shouting. Stillness meant someone had finished deciding things.

"Ilse Vantre," Brannoc called, and his voice had the careful flatness of a man reading charges. "Your father died in the guildhouse of Corvane & Sons. We know. We were there."

"I know where my father died." Her voice carried across the distance without effort, low and even, ash settling on a fire. "I was two streets away. I arrived while they were still wiping his blood off your counting desk, Halloran. I watched the wardens carry him out in a sack."

Edmund flinched. Sefa watched him flinch and filed it away. Three days he'd been carrying that dying man's map sewn into the spine of a false ledger, and three days he'd been walking like a man with a knife between his shoulders, waiting to learn whose hand had put it there.

"He gave me this," Edmund called back. "He pressed it into my hands. I didn't take anything—"

"You took everything." Ilse Vantre began to walk again, unhurried, and the three of them stood there like fools watching her come. "Twenty-two years he worked on that map. Twenty-two years he drew and redrew a country no one else believed in, and I ground his pigments and stretched his paper and kept us fed by mending nets while he chased a door out of the world. And at the end, dying in a stranger's guildhouse, he gives it—" her voice cracked, just once, like ice underfoot, "—gives it to a *clerk*. A clerk who can't even look me in the eye."

She stopped ten paces away. Up close she smelled of salt and lamp-oil and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sea cave. Her eyes were the color of shallow water over sand, and they went from Edmund to Brannoc to Sefa with the cold arithmetic of an appraiser.

"Give it here," she said. "The map. Walk away, all of you, and live. The Wardens want the clerk most of all. They'll follow him like gulls follow a fishing boat. You two are just gulls."

Sefa laughed. She couldn't help it; it came out of her the way laughter always did, sideways, at the worst possible moment. "You've got it backwards, love. We're not following him. We're *with* him."

"With him." Ilse looked at Sefa the way you'd look at a stain. "You've known him four days."

"And I've known you five minutes," Sefa said, "and I already like you better than most people I've known five years. But the map stays with him."

Something moved behind Ilse's eyes — not softness, nothing so useful as softness. Calculation. Then, briefly, beneath it, a rawness so sudden and so deep that Sefa understood, with the particular clarity of a thief reading a stranger's house, that this woman had not slept since her father died. That the fury was load-bearing. Take it away and the whole structure came down.

"You don't even know what it is," Ilse said quietly. "Do you, clerk? You're carrying it around like a stolen purse. Do you know what my father called it?"

"A map," Edmund said. His voice was thin but it held. "To Vell."

The name dropped onto the road between them like a stone into still water. Sefa saw it land on all of them at once — saw Brannoc's jaw tighten, saw Edmund's hands curl at his sides, saw something flicker across Ilse's face that was almost pain, almost hunger.

"You've heard of it," Ilse said slowly. "That's impossible. No one has heard of it."

"He says it like he remembers it," Brannoc rumbled. "I've watched him say it. Like a man remembering home."

"My father said the country forgets people," Edmund went on, and now he was talking fast, the way he talked when the beasts in the cellar talked and he needed to fill the silence before anyone noticed he was answering questions no one had asked aloud. "Or people forget it. He wrote it in the margin — I've read the margins a hundred times. *Vell is not hidden. Vell is forgotten. There is a difference.*"

Ilse stared at him. For a long moment the only sound was the canal lapping at its banks and somewhere inland a dog barking itself hoarse.

"That's his handwriting," she said at last, and her voice had changed, dropped its armor a finger's width. "You've been reading his margins."

"He's been sleeping with the thing under his head," Sefa offered. "Like a pillow. It's honestly a bit sad."

"Sefa," Edmund said.

"What? It is."

But Ilse wasn't listening anymore. She was looking at Edmund with a new expression, one Sefa recognized from card tables and market squares: the look of a gambler who has just realized the game isn't what she thought it was. "My father spent twenty years looking for someone Vell still remembered. Someone the country would answer to. He interviewed madmen and mediums and one man who claimed to be a hundred and forty years old. And then he dies and hands the map to the first clerk who happens along?" She stepped closer. "Unless he didn't happen along. Unless he *found* you."

"I don't know what he found," Edmund said. "I never met him before that night."

"No," Ilse agreed softly. "But maybe Vell did."

Nobody spoke. The dog inland had given up. Sefa shifted her weight and became aware, with professional unease, that they were standing in open road having a conversation about forbidden countries while somewhere behind them the guild's wardens were sharpening whatever they sharpened.

"We can't do this here," she said. "There's a culvert a mile ahead where the drainage channel meets the canal. Walls, shade, one way in unless you swim. I say we argue there."

"Why there?" Brannoc asked.

Sefa grinned. "Because if we're going to be murdered, I'd rather be murdered sitting down."

* * *

The culvert was old imperial work, brick vaulted and cool, where a stream of fresh water ran chattering down from the hills and spilled into the canal's sluggish brine. They sat in the green half-light with bread and hard cheese, and Ilse Vantre sat apart from them, exactly far enough away to make the point that she was not one of them, and close enough to make the point that she wasn't leaving either.

It was Edmund, of course, who broke first. Sefa could have predicted it to the minute. Four days she'd known him and already she could chart his guilt like a tide table.

"I'll give it back," he said. "If it's truly yours. Your father's work, your inheritance. I only wanted—" He stopped. Started again. "I only wanted to know why he chose me."

Ilse unwrapped her own bread without eating it. "Why does it matter?"

"Because I've spent my whole life being chosen for things I didn't ask for," Edmund said, "and it would be nice, just once, to understand one of them."

The fresh water ran. The salt water slid. Where they met there was a long ragged line of foam, arguing, folding into itself, neither yielding.

Sefa saw it before anyone else did — saw Edmund see it a heartbeat later, his eyes going to that seam of foam and staying there, his face going strange.

"Where salt water meets fresh," he said.

"What?" Brannoc asked.

"The clue. In the border of the map — there's lettering worked into the waves, tiny, I thought it was decoration. My father wrote it in a cipher of tides," Ilse said sharply, sitting forward despite herself. "It took me two years to break half of it. What does it say?"

"*The door answers where the waters argue,*" Edmund recited. "*Bring the reader to the confluence. Let the reader speak what Vell remembers.*" He looked up, and his eyes were bright and frightened. "We've been sitting next to it all afternoon."

They all turned to look at the seam of foam.

"It can't be that simple," Brannoc said.

"Nothing about this has been simple," Sefa said. "But also, yes, it absolutely could be. Cursed things love a pun."

"Then we test it," Ilse said, rising, brushing crumbs from her cloak with sharp economical movements. "Here. Now. Read it, clerk."

"Not here." Edmund hadn't moved. "Not yet. You heard what the margins said. *Let the reader speak.* If I'm wrong about what I am — if I'm wrong about what this costs —"

"If you're wrong, nothing happens," Ilse said. "If you're right, my father's life's work takes its first breath in twenty-two years. Read it."

"Or," Sefa said pleasantly, "we wait until morning, sleep on it, and read it somewhere the Wardens aren't currently triangulating. Because I promise you, the moment that map gets spoken aloud, every salt-blessed ear in three counties is going to hear it like a dinner bell."

"She's right," Brannoc said. "First rule of running: never ring the bell until you've chosen the door."

Ilse looked at each of them in turn, and Sefa watched her do the arithmetic again — three strangers, one inheritance, no allies worth the name. Watched her arrive at the only sum available.

"Fine," Ilse said. "Tomorrow. But understand me, all of you." She picked up her pack, slung it, and stood with the seam of foam at her back like a blade laid on a table. "I'm coming with you. Not because I trust the clerk. Because the map is mine, and I will take it back, and until I do, I want to know exactly how many people I'll have to go through."

"Two," Sefa said cheerfully. "Possibly three, if the big one's feeling loyal."

"Sefa," Edmund said again.

But Ilse almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, thin as paper, but it was the first one, and Sefa counted it the way she counted everything: an asset, entered in the ledger, interest accruing.

* * *

They made camp above the culvert, in the lee of a fallen wall, and Sefa took first watch because she always did, because sleep was a debt she'd never trusted herself to owe.

From the dark she watched them. Edmund curled around the false ledger like a child around a doll. Brannoc flat on his back, snoring like a landslide. And Ilse Vantre awake, sitting up, staring at the sleeping clerk with an expression Sefa had seen once before — on her own face, years ago, in a canal-side window, the night before she'd decided whether to rob the man who'd raised her or forgive him.

Grief and greed, wearing each other's clothes.

Down in the culvert, the waters argued on, salt against sweet, neither winning, the foam whispering something in a language made entirely of borders.

And Sefa, who trusted nothing, felt the first cold thread of certainty slide down her spine: tomorrow, when Edmund opened his mouth and read, something on the other side of that foam was going to open its mouth too.

She reached for a pebble and held it, just to have something in her hand that was real.

The pebble was damp.

Everything near the water was damp.

Everything near the water tasted, faintly and unmistakably, of salt.
