## 4. The Market of Borrowed Names

The market at Grayharbor did not deal in goods. It dealt in *who*.

Brannoc had known that for thirty years, back when he'd worn the salt-grey coat of a Warden and come here to buy informants the way other men bought boots. The stalls along the canal sold names stitched into cloth, faces folded into paper lanterns, whole lives pressed flat and sealed in wax like preserves. A farmer's memory of his first harvest: three coppers. A smuggler's entire childhood, guilt-free and portable: a silver. Once, he had watched a woman sell her own wedding day to pay a debt, then stand blinking at her own husband like a stranger, apologizing politely for the inconvenience.

He had never bought anything here before tonight. He had only ever been the one selling.

Now he moved through the lantern-light with his hood up and his old Warden's ring turned inward against his palm, feeling every eye on him the way a scarred dog feels weather coming. Beside him, Sefa drifted through the crowd like smoke given opinions, lifting nothing — she had sworn it, hand on heart, with a grin that suggested the oath was technical rather than sincere — but marking everything. Edmund walked between them with the ledger clutched to his chest like a breastplate, and Brannoc kept catching himself watching the boy instead of the crowd.

That was the trouble with loyalty. It started as a stone in the boot and ended as a stone in the chest.

* * *

Old Maren kept her stall where the fresh water of the canal met the brackish push of the tide, because she claimed the mixing waters made people honest. She was ninety if she was a day, blind in one eye, and she had sold information to both sides of every conflict in living memory, which was why she was still alive.

She looked up as Brannoc approached, and her good eye went straight past his hood to his face, the way it always had.

"Well," she said. "The drowned man walks."

"Maren."

"They fished you out of the Salt Cells, I hear. Or you climbed out. Either way, you're dead on the rolls. Dead men get better prices." She gestured with a chin like a dock piling. "Sit. Buy something small so we look busy."

He sat on the upturned crate across from her stall. Behind him, Sefa melted into the market noise; Edmund stood too close, breathing like a man trying not to drown quietly.

"I need to know what the Wardens are saying," Brannoc said. "About us."

"About *you*, you mean." Maren picked grit from beneath her fingernails with a bone knife, unhurried. "Corvane & Sons posted notice this morning. Three heads. The pickpocket girl, two hundred crowns. You, four hundred — deserter, traitor, thief of guild property." Her good eye lifted. "And the quiet one with the ledger. Five hundred."

Edmund made a small sound, like a hinge.

"For a clerk?" Brannoc said.

"For a clerk who can do what clerks can't." Maren set down the bone knife. "There's a word going around the Wardens' barracks, Brannoc. They're calling him a *beast-speaker*. They say the rats in the Corvane cellar went strange the night the cartographer died. They say a hound on the quay turned on its handler two nights past, for no reason anyone could name — except that a pale young man was standing near." She leaned forward. "They haven't proven it. But they've stopped looking for a thief and started looking for an abomination. That's a different kind of hunt. That one doesn't end at the border."

Brannoc felt the cold arrive in his hands first, then his gut. He had spent eleven years in the grey coat. He knew what happened to beast-speakers when the Wardens caught them. There was a salt room beneath the guildhouse with a drain in the floor, and he had signed the requisitions for its supplies without ever once asking himself what the salt was *for*.

He had asked now. Late. Too late by one dead cartographer and one ruined life.

"You knew him before," Maren said, watching him. "Halloran. You brought him in once, years ago. A boy caught talking to the fishmonger's cat."

"I remember."

"You reported him?"

"I wrote it up as vagrancy." His voice came out flat as slate. "I buried the truth in the ledger because burying things was my job. And then I spent eleven years walking past him in the corridors pretending I hadn't."

Maren snorted. "And now?"

"Now I'm four hundred crowns of dead man standing between him and the people who taught me how to make salt burn."

The old woman studied him for a long moment. Then she reached under her counter and came up with a twist of oilcloth, which she pushed across the boards.

"Free of charge, which should frighten you. The Wardens have a reader working for them now. A map-reader. They know the cartographer's map needs reading aloud, and they know it answers to certain voices. They'll be checking every crossing where salt meets fresh." Her good eye flicked, deliberate and slow, toward the canal behind Brannoc — toward the mingling waters lapping at the pilings. "Starting with convenient ones."

Brannoc was on his feet before she finished. "How long?"

"A day. Perhaps less. They travel fast when they smell abomination." She waved him off with the bone knife. "Go. And Brannoc —" her voice dropped, and for one moment the broker fell away and something older looked out of her ruined eye — "the boy doesn't know what you did to him. Don't tell him on the road. Tell him after. If there is an after."

* * *

He lied to Edmund within the hour, and it was the easiest lie he had ever told, and it shamed him more than any truth.

"There's a checkpoint forming at the tidal gate," he said, crouched in the shadow of a net-drying rack while Sefa scouted ahead. "We go around, through the marsh road. It adds half a day."

Edmund nodded, trusting, tired, hugging the ledger. Sefa raised one skeptical eyebrow but said nothing, which from Sefa was a sermon on faith.

Half a day. It would put them at the confluence a night behind the Wardens' sweep instead of square in front of it. It would cost them time they might not have, and it might save Edmund's neck, and Brannoc told himself the lie was strategy, not penance. He told himself that all the way out of Grayharbor, along the stinking margin where the town gave up and the marsh began, while the tide breathed behind them and the lanterns of the name-market shrank to embers.

It was Sefa who broke him, because of course it was.

She fell into step beside him when Edmund wandered ahead to look at something — a heron, standing in the shallows like a folded letter — and she said, without preamble: "You lied back there."

"I don't know what—"

"You got the price wrong. On purpose." She picked her way over a tangle of root, light as a bird. "Maren quoted you four hundred. You told Edmund five. You wanted him to think the hunt was about the map, not about him." She glanced sideways. "Why?"

Brannoc said nothing for twenty paces. The marsh sucked at their boots. Somewhere ahead, Edmund laughed at the heron — a startled, unguarded sound, the laugh of a man who had never been allowed many.

"Because I knew what he was when he was nine years old," Brannoc said finally, "and I chose the ledger over the boy. Because I sat in a warm room signing papers while they built a room downstairs with a drain in the floor. Because everything that's hunting him now, I helped build the machinery of." He stared at the dark water. "I'm not covering for him because he's innocent. I'm covering for him because I'm not."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. Then: "He'd forgive you."

"That's what frightens me."

Ahead, Edmund turned and beckoned — actually beckoned, waving them forward like children called to supper, pointing at the heron as though it were a marvel worth sharing. As though the four-hundred-crown traitor and the two-hundred-crown thief were simply his people, and this was simply a walk, and the world had not posted a price on all their heads.

Brannoc felt something shift in his chest, some load-bearing guilt giving way to something else — not lighter, but differently placed. Guilt looked backward. This looked forward, at a pale young man waving from a marsh, and it said: *not again. Whatever it costs. Not again.*

He lengthened his stride to catch up.

Behind them, far back along the black water, a lantern kindled at the tidal gate — then another, and another, a string of lights dropping into place like beads on a wire. The Wardens had reached Grayharbor early.

And in the reeds beside the path, close enough to touch, a dog began to howl — low, rhythmic, deliberate — and Edmund stopped, head tilting, listening to a language no one else could hear.

"They know we're here," he said softly. "Not the Wardens. *Them.* The hounds. They're saying—" He frowned, translating. "They're saying the white door has already been found."
