## 5. Where the Rivers Argue

The rivers met a mile past the last farm, and they did not meet quietly.

Edmund heard them arguing before he saw the water — two voices braided together in the way that water speaks to those who listen, which was no one, he reminded himself, no one but him. The Vellin came down from the north carrying snowmelt and old stone, cold and certain of itself. The Marrow came up from the south brown and slow and heavy with silt, muttering like an old woman who had carried too many things too far. Where they collided, the current churned white and furious, each refusing to become the other for a hundred yards downstream until, defeated by distance, they finally mingled into one gray-green river with no name either of them would claim.

"Charming spot," Sefa said. She stood at the bank's edge with her arms crossed, boots planted on mud the color of old bruises. "Smells like fish dying."

"It smells like salt," Edmund said.

That stopped her. He watched her test the air herself, nostrils flaring, and watched the suspicion settle deeper into her face. They were forty miles from any sea. But the salt was there — faint, mineral, unmistakable — rising off the confluence like breath from an open mouth.

Brannoc said nothing. He had said very little since the market, since he had lied to a Warden's face about what Edmund was without being asked, and Edmund still hadn't decided what to make of it. The disgraced warden stood apart, hand resting on the axe at his belt out of habit rather than threat, watching the treeline the way men watch treelines when they have been hunted and expect to be again. His left eye still bore the yellowing shadow of Ilse's fist.

Ilse knelt at the water's edge with the map spread across her knees.

They had argued about who would carry it the whole way here. She had lost the argument, technically, because the map had settled the matter itself: whenever Sefa touched it, the ink crawled like insects disturbed under bark; when Brannoc held it, the lines went gray and dead as ash. Only in Edmund's hands did the salt-stained vellum lie quiet, the faded roads holding their shape, the single door-marked place at the center pulsing faintly when his thumb passed over it, slow as a sleeping pulse.

*It knows you,* Ilse had said, and there had been something terrible in the way she said it — not accusation anymore, not quite. Something closer to hunger.

Now she looked up at him across the map, and her face was raw from three days of wind and grief she refused to name. "Read it," she said. "Or give it back to me and I'll read it."

"You can't." It wasn't cruelty. It was arithmetic. "It won't let you."

"My father bled over that thing for thirty years—"

"And it went dead in his hands, didn't it?" Edmund heard his own voice come out harder than he intended. He softened nothing. "You told us. Thirty years, and every reading he attempted came out as noise. Because the map doesn't want a reader who *wants* something. It wants—" He stopped. He didn't know how to finish it. He didn't know how to say *it wants someone it remembers* without hearing how mad it sounded.

Sefa saved him the trouble. "It wants you," she said flatly. "Which is exactly what worries me."

The river argued on. Edmund crouched beside Ilse, careful not to touch the map, and looked at the place where fresh water fought salt-borne memory, and felt the dread he had been carrying since the counting desk finally take a shape he could look at directly.

He could hear the rivers. That was the thing none of them understood, the thing he could never say aloud in full: the world talked to him constantly, ceaselessly, and he had spent thirty years learning to hold the door shut on all of it. Rats in cellars. Horses on the road. Moths against lantern glass, complaining in voices like torn silk. And now two rivers quarreling about whose water this was, and beneath their argument — faint, rhythmic, patient — a third voice he could not place, saying something over and over like a lullaby half-remembered.

"Stand back," he said. "All of you. Whatever happens, don't touch me while I'm reading. I don't know what it does yet."

"That's reassuring," Sefa muttered, but she moved.

Ilse did not move. For a long moment she knelt there, staring at him with her father's gray eyes, and Edmund braced himself for fury, for another round of *my inheritance, my birthright.* Instead she folded the map once, twice, with the tenderness of someone shrouding a body, and held it out to him handle-first, the way one offers a knife.

"He waited his whole life for this," she said quietly. "Don't waste it being afraid."

"I'm extremely afraid."

"Good." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might someday live again. "Then you'll be careful."

Edmund took the map and walked into the shallows.

The water seized his ankles immediately, cold enough to ache, and both rivers began talking at once — the Vellin sharp and glacial, the Marrow thick with complaint — and beneath them, louder now, that third voice, patient as tide. He waded to the exact center of the confluence, where the currents crossed and canceled and the water went strangely, unnaturally still around his waist, a circle of calm in all that violence, as if the river were holding its breath.

He unfolded the map.

Up close, in daylight, he could see what he had missed: the ink was not ink. It was salt, ground fine and mixed with something that gleamed — egg-white, maybe, or tears; cartographers' lore said both. The lines of roads and rivers were written in it, and the words along the borders were written in it, and the words were in no alphabet he knew, and yet—

And yet he could read them.

That was the horror of it. Not gibberish, not guesswork. The syllables assembled themselves in his mouth the way a sneeze assembles, inevitable, foreign, *known*. His lips began to move before he decided to let them.

"*Vell-in-arda. Vell-in-ostia. Kel-varren, kel-vane.*"

The rivers stopped arguing.

Not quieted — *stopped*, mid-current, the white water at the seam going smooth as poured glass, and the silence underneath the world opened like a trapdoor. In that silence Edmund heard the third voice clearly for the first time, and his knees nearly went out from under him, because he knew it.

It was his mother's voice.

She had died when he was six. He remembered almost nothing — a smell of bread, a hand on his hair, a song hummed low that he had spent his whole life unable to recall past its first three notes. Now the voice rose out of the still water and out of the salt lines on the page and spoke the next line of the map in her throat, her cadence, the slight catch she'd had on the word for *door*:

"*...and the door remembers what the country forgot, and the country remembers what the reader lost.*"

"Mother?" His voice cracked like a boy's. Water shivered outward from him in rings though he hadn't moved. "Mother, I don't— I don't remember you. I don't remember *Vell*. Why do you know me? Why do I know your voice and not your face?"

The map answered, because of course it did; because the map was not a map at all but a mouth, and mouths answer.

"*Because forgetting has a direction, little one. You did not forget Vell. Vell was taken out of the world — and everything it held was taken with it, except what hid inside living flesh. You are a room it hid in. I am the voice the room kept.*"

The air above the water split.

There was no better word for it. A seam opened in the morning light the way a seam opens in cloth — a thin white line, then a widening, and through it Edmund saw a doorway standing free of any wall: white stone, white frame, a whiteness beyond it that was not blankness but *distance*, hills and terraced fields and a sky the color of unspooled wool, a country seen through the wrong end of a spyglass, beautiful and unbearably far away. Wind came through the gap and it smelled of bread. Of bread and salt and a garden after rain.

His mother's voice was singing now. Three notes. Then a fourth he had never been able to reach.

Behind him he heard Sefa swear, heard Brannoc's axe come free of his belt, heard Ilse whisper — "*Father*" — in a voice broken open down the middle.

Edmund reached toward the light.

The seam snapped shut.

It closed like a wound closing, without sound, and the recoil came back through his arm and up into his skull, and Edmund Halloran learned in one white-hot instant what the reading cost.

It took the taste of bread.

Not the memory of bread — the *taste*. He groped for it frantically, the warm crust, the steam, the specific sweetness of his mother's kitchen, and found a hole where it had been, a smooth absence, a tooth missing from the row. He knew bread existed. He knew he had loved it. He could not remember what it tasted like, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning, that the map had chosen the smallest thing first. A mercy. An appetizer.

*Three more readings,* the stillness said, in no voice at all. *Each costs more than the last.*

He staggered. The rivers resumed their argument as if nothing had happened, and perhaps for them nothing had. Hands caught him — Sefa on one side, Ilse on the other, hauling him up the bank, and he realized distantly that he was shaking, that his lips were moving, that he was saying "it took the bread, it took how bread tastes" over and over like a man reporting a theft.

"The door," Ilse was saying, gripping his shoulder hard enough to bruise. "You opened it. I *saw* it. Edmund. Look at me. How many times?"

"Four readings." His teeth wanted to chatter and wouldn't. "One done. Each one takes..." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Each one takes a memory. It took the smallest one first. Like a moneylender. Like a guild ledger." He looked up at them, three faces blurred by the bright water-light, and made himself say the rest. "The next ones won't be small. It'll want bigger things. First loves. Names. Faces."

Silence, except the rivers, arguing.

Sefa sat down heavily in the mud. "So we're robbing you," she said. "This map is robbing you, one pocket at a time, and we're standing here helping it."

"We could stop," Brannoc said. It was the longest sentence he'd offered in two days. He said it looking at the water, not at Edmund. "Walk away. Let Vell stay forgotten. Some doors are shut for reasons."

"No," Ilse said.

Everyone looked at her. She was still staring at the empty air where the doorway had been, and her eyes were wet, and her jaw was set in the expression Edmund was beginning to know the way he knew weather coming.

"My father spent thirty years and died in a gutter for that door," she said. "I am not letting it close because the price got honest." She turned to Edmund, and what was in her face frightened him more than the Wardens did — gratitude, kinship, the terrible tenderness of the grieving. "We find the next confluence. We keep going."

"And what does it take from him next?" Sefa demanded. "His name? His mother's face? At what point do we stop and ask what's left of him at the end?"

Edmund opened his mouth to answer — to say something brave, something ledger-balanced, something worthy of the family he seemed to be acquiring against all sense —

—and stopped.

Because the map, lying in the mud between them, had begun to glow. Faintly. Patiently. And new lines were surfacing through the salt like veins rising under skin: a road heading south, and beside it, in his mother's voice that only he could hear, a single instruction, gentle as a hand on his hair:

"*Come home, little one. Bring the girl who carries my ashes. There is so little time, and so much of you left to spend.*"

Edmund looked at Ilse, kneeling in the mud with her father's grief and her father's gray eyes, and understood suddenly why the map had gone quiet in her hands.

It hadn't rejected her.

It was saving her.

* * *

They walked south as the light went long and gold, four figures on a road that no map but theirs admitted existed, and behind them the rivers went on arguing, as rivers do, about whose water it was — while ahead, somewhere past the darkening hills, the second gate waited, patient as tide, hungry as a ledger, already choosing what it would take.
