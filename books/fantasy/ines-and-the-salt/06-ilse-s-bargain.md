## 6. Ilse's Bargain

Ilse had learned to sleep with one ear open in her father's house, back when the house still stood and the nights were full of his coughing and the scratch of his pen. She slept that way now, on a bedroll of borrowed wool in the ruin above the river, listening to the others breathe: Edmund's slow and careful rhythm, Sefa's quick shallow flutter like a bird dreaming of flight, Brannoc's great bellows snore that shook dust from the rafters.

She waited until Brannoc's snore reached its deepest register. Then she rose.

The map lay where Edmund always kept it, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false spine of a ledger — *Guild property, Corvane & Sons*, stamped in faded gilt on the cover, as if bureaucracy could protect anything from what it held. Ilse lifted it out without a sound. She had spent three weeks learning where he put things. Three weeks of watching him with hatred sharpened into study, because hatred was easier than grief and study was useful either way.

The map was hers. Her father had drawn it with hands that shook too badly to hold a stylus straight, and he had given it to a stranger at a counting desk instead of sending for his own daughter. She would take it back. That had been the whole of her plan when she'd caught them at the ford outside Marrow's Crossing, knife drawn, voice shaking with fury she hadn't known she carried. And then she had looked at Edmund Halloran's face — tired, guilty, *kind* — and heard herself say, "I'm coming with you," and hadn't understood why.

She understood now. Or she was beginning to.

Outside, the moon sat low over the confluence country, silvering the two rivers where they ran side by side before their argument. The seam had opened there once already; Edmund had shown them, or tried to. A white doorway hanging in the air like a tear in wet paper, and then gone, sealed, waiting for more readings. Three more, the map had said, in letters that moved when no one looked directly at them. Each reading cost the reader a memory. Edmund wouldn't say which memory his had taken. He went quiet whenever Sefa asked, and Sefa, for all her thieving fingers, had learned not to push.

Ilse knelt at the water's edge where fresh met salt, where the grey river braided itself into the tide. She unwrapped the map.

It smelled of her father. That was the cruelest thing about it — not the salt stains, not the impossible geography, but the smell of tallow and ink and the particular soap he'd used all her life, rising off the vellum as if he'd only just set it down. She pressed it flat against her knees and read the second gate's inscription by moonlight, the words swimming, rearranging themselves into shapes she almost recognized.

*Read me aloud,* the map said, in the way maps do, which is to say silently, insistently, the way a locked door says it. *I answer only those I remember.*

"You remember me," she whispered. "You have to. He drew you. Everything he touched remembered me."

Her father had talked about Vell in the last years, though he'd never called it by name. He'd talk around it — a country shaped like a cupped hand, he'd say, holding water that tasted of iron and honey. Streets paved with mother-of-pearl. A harbor where the fish came when you whistled. Ilse had thought these were the fancies of a dying man. Now she knew they were memories leaking out of a wound in the world, and that he had been its last cartographer, the last man alive who could draw a place that every other human mind had agreed to forget.

And she was forgetting it too. That was the thing she couldn't say aloud. Every year, her father's stories grew fainter in her — she could no longer picture the cupped-hand shape of the coast, could no longer hear the whistle-song he'd taught her for calling fish. Vell was letting go of her, or she of it, and soon there would be nothing left but a woman with an inheritance she couldn't read.

Unless she read it first. Unless she paid the price while there was still something of her left to pay with.

"Ilse."

She didn't startle. She'd heard him coming — Edmund walked like a man who'd spent years apologizing to floors. He stood behind her in the moonlight, arms crossed against the cold, and she watched him understand, all at once, what she held and what she meant to do.

"Give it here," he said quietly.

"No."

"It takes a memory, Ilse. You don't know what it'll—"

"It took yours." She turned to face him. "What did it take? Tell me that much."

A long silence. The rivers argued below them, fresh against salt, the sound like two voices that loved each other poorly.

"A name," he said finally. "My mother used to sing a song with a name in it. I can't remember the name. Or the song. Or—" His jaw tightened. "Or whether my mother sang at all. I only know that someone did, once, and that it mattered."

"And you'd let us go on paying that price one by one? Sefa next? Brannoc?" She stood, the map clutched to her chest. "You're all so busy protecting each other that none of you will admit what this door is. It's a toll road, Edmund. And we are the coin."

"It should be me. Vell remembers me. It costs me less than—"

"It doesn't cost you *less*." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "That's what none of you understand. It costs me everything, because I'm running out. My father is dead and Vell is forgetting me and if I don't do this now, there won't be enough of me left that remembers it to pay with at all. This isn't sacrifice. This is arithmetic."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment. Moonlight made him look younger than he was, and sadder. Then he did the last thing she expected.

He nodded.

"The reading has to be true," he said. "Whatever you speak, it has to be yours. Not your father's words. Yours."

He stayed while she read. She hadn't asked him to stay, and he hadn't offered, and somehow they both simply knew.

She began with the inscription, the old words rising off the vellum like heat off summer stone. But halfway through, her own voice took over — she couldn't have stopped it any more than she could stop bleeding. What came out was not cartography. It was a girl's voice, telling a country about her father:

*He drew you until his hands failed. He named your streets in his sleep. He kept a whistle on a cord around his neck for forty years for fish that no longer existed anywhere but in him. I hated you, sometimes, for taking him. I don't hate you anymore. Take what you need from me. Only leave me enough to find you.*

The air split.

Not violently — that was the terrible part. The seam opened the way a curtain parts, gently, and beyond it was white. Not blankness. *Whiteness.* Light with texture to it, light like pearl, like the inside of a shell held to the sun. And through it, faintly, Ilse heard water that tasted of iron and honey, and a whistle-note, high and sweet, that made her sob without knowing why.

Then it closed. The night rushed back in, ordinary and dark, and Ilse was on her knees at the river's edge with salt water soaking through her skirts.

She felt lighter. That was her first thought, and it horrified her — she felt *lighter*, as if something heavy had been lifted, and she scrabbled inward through her own mind looking for the wound.

She found it quickly. She reached for her father's face and found a smooth white space where it had been.

She knew he had a face. She knew he had been tall, stooped at the end, that his hair had gone white in patches like frost on a windowpane. She knew he'd had a scar over one eyebrow from a fall she'd witnessed. But the face itself — the particular architecture of nose and mouth and eyes that she had loved across thirty years of mornings — was gone. In its place was a description. A list of facts wearing the shape of a man.

She sat at the edge of the water and cried, not loudly, not dramatically, but the way rain falls — steadily, without performance, until it stops.

Edmund crouched beside her. He didn't touch her. He seemed to know that touch would be unbearable just now, that grief needed its borders respected.

"He gave you the map," she said at last, "because he knew you'd open the door. And he never sent for me because—" She stopped. Breathed. "Because he knew I'd try to follow, and he wanted me safe. And I followed anyway, and it cost me his face, and I would do it again tonight. Do you understand what that means?"

"That you're stubborn?"

She laughed — a broken, startled sound, half sob. "That I'm *Vellish*. Whatever that is. Whatever's left of it." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "I spent three weeks planning how to steal this map from you. I had a knife. I had routes planned. I told myself you were a thief and a liar and that I'd take back what was mine and vanish."

"And now?"

"Now I've paid the toll, and the gate opened, and I heard—" Her voice failed, steadied. "I heard the water, Edmund. Iron and honey. Exactly as he said. Which means he wasn't mad, and it means the door is real, and it means every step closer is a step toward him." She turned and looked at Edmund directly, and something in her gaze had changed — the flint knocked out of it, or ground down into something finer. "You're not a thief. You're the only other person alive who grieves a country neither of us can picture. So we do this together. All the way. And when the last gate opens, I walk through it, and I find whatever's left of him on the other side."

Edmund exhaled slowly. "Sefa's going to be insufferable about this. 'The ash daughter smiled,' she'll say. 'Mark the date.'"

"Let her." Ilse gathered the map, wrapped it, and — deliberately, watching his face — handed it back to him. Trust, offered like a blade returned hilt-first. "But Edmund. One thing."

"Anything."

"When we reach the final gate — the one that needs someone Vell fully remembers —" she saw him flinch, saw that he'd already done the same arithmetic she had, "—we find another way. We *find* one. Because I've lost a face tonight. I am not losing the rest of you."

Before he could answer, a whistle cut the night — two notes, sharp, urgent, the signal Sefa had invented for *trouble, run, don't ask*. Downriver, torchlight bloomed among the reeds like a crop of burning flowers, and beneath the rivers' argument rose a new sound: the baying of hounds, and the thin cold call of Salt Wardens' horns.

They were out of time. The third gate would have to wait — but the Wardens, it seemed, had decided it wouldn't wait for them.
