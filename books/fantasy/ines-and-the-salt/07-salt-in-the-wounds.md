## 7. Salt in the Wounds

The road to the third gate ran through a country that had forgotten how to be anything but salt.

Edmund felt it in his teeth first — that faint mineral ache, like biting a coin — and then in the ledger strapped beneath his coat, where the map of Vell lay folded against his ribs. It had grown heavier since Ilse's reading. Heavier, and warmer, as though it were learning to beat.

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a fallen waystone, one of the old boundary markers whose carved letters had been scraped smooth by hands no one remembered. Sefa built a fire small enough to hide inside a cupped palm. Brannoc took the watch without being asked, standing at the edge of the light with his back to them, the way he always did, as if he hadn't yet decided whether he belonged to the fire or the dark.

"You're limping," Edmund said to him quietly, when he brought him a strip of dried eel.

"I'm walking."

"Brannoc."

The big man's jaw worked. "Twisted it on the scree this morning. It's nothing." But he let Edmund look at it, and he didn't flinch when Edmund's fingers found the swelling, and when Edmund murmured something low under his breath — not words, exactly, more the shape of a word — Brannoc pretended not to hear, which was its own kind of mercy.

Above them, on the ridge, a nightjar called three times. Then again. Then a fourth time, which was wrong, because nightjars never gave a fourth call unless they meant it as a warning.

Edmund went very still.

*Four,* said the bird, in the thin silver voice all birds used inside his skull. *Four men. Two dogs. Coming down the watercourse. They smell of iron and they do not sleep.*

"Wardens," Edmund breathed. He was moving before the word finished leaving him, kicking dirt over Sefa's fire, hauling her up by the wrist. "Ilse — wake her — we have to run, now, they've found us—"

Sefa was already awake; she was always already awake. She had the map-ledger out of his coat and into her shirt before he'd finished speaking, and Ilse came up out of her blankets with her father's compass already in her fist, her eyes clear and cold and utterly changed from the woman who had joined them hating Edmund Halloran.

"The dogs will find us wherever we go," Ilse said. "You know that. Salt-hounds don't lose a scent."

"Then we split the scent." Edmund's mind was racing through the cellar-rat grammar of escape routes, the badger-logic of false trails. "Brannoc, can you run?"

Brannoc looked at his ankle. Looked at Edmund. And lied, badly, with a ghost of a smile: "I can fly."

* * *

They did not fly. They ran, down through the black gorse and the shingle flats where the river argued with itself before giving up and becoming estuary, and the whole time Edmund listened to the dogs behind them — their hearts, their breath, the dumb devoted certainty of animals who had been told that what they hunted was evil. That was the worst part. Not the pursuit. The *certainty*. The hounds had been raised on warden sermons, fed on salted meat and righteous fear, and there was nothing in their simple minds but the shape of the command: *find the forbidden thing, find the forbidden thing.*

"They're closing," Sefa gasped. She could run for hours, that girl, born to alleys, but even she was flagging now, and Ilse had gone grey-lipped, and Brannoc —

Brannoc had stopped running.

Edmund turned. The ex-warden stood on the shingle with his arms slightly away from his body, the universal posture of surrender, and beyond him four lanterns bobbed down the watercourse like a string of drowned stars.

"No," Edmund said. "No, you get up, you come *on*—"

"Somebody has to be caught," Brannoc said calmly. "Or nobody escapes. Go east along the flats. There's an old oyster shed at the point — I stocked it myself, years ago, when I still thought I'd need somewhere to hide from them." His eyes found Edmund's, and held them, and there was no fear in them at all, only a terrible settled patience. "Whatever they ask me, I'll give them nothing. I've had practice lying to the guild. You taught me better ones."

*He is buying us our lives,* said the nightjar, from somewhere overhead. *Do not waste the purchase.*

The lanterns reached him. Salt-white cloaks. Iron-tipped staves. And between them, straining at chains, the two hounds — great grey beasts with pale eyes, bred in kennels that smelled of brine and scripture.

A warden's voice, flat as slate: "On your knees, traitor."

And Edmund, hidden in the gorse with Sefa's hand crushing his sleeve and Ilse's breath ragged against his ear, watched Brannoc kneel in the wet shingle like a man kneeling at an altar he no longer believed in.

* * *

They took him to the oyster shed themselves. Of course they did — it was the only roof for a mile, and wardens trusted roofs the way other men trusted gods. Edmund heard it all from the darkness behind the drying racks, pressed shoulder to shoulder with Sefa and Ilse, listening through the wall with the whole of his unwanted gift stretched wide open.

They chained Brannoc to a beam. They built a brazier. And then the senior warden — a woman named Corvel, whose voice Edmund knew from guild proclamations, patient and pleasant as a knife being sharpened — opened a small clay jar and began, gently, to talk.

"This is blessed salt, Brannoc. Old recipe. Guild recipe. You remember." She took a pinch of it, white as teeth. "It doesn't hurt truth. Truth passes through it like water through a net. Only lies burn. So we're going to have a very honest conversation, you and I, about a clerk named Edmund Halloran."

"Don't listen," Sefa whispered, her face buried against her knees. "Don't listen, don't listen—"

But Edmund listened. He couldn't stop listening. He heard the first hiss of salt meeting skin, and he heard Brannoc's breath go ragged, and he heard — worse than the screaming, which came later and briefly — the sound of a man fighting to keep his silences honest.

"Where is the map?"

Silence. Burn.

"Where is Halloran?"

Silence. Burn.

"Is he one of *them*? Does he speak to beasts? Is the gift alive in him?"

And here Brannoc laughed — a cracked, bloody, magnificent laugh — and said: "Burn me all night. Every lie I tell you costs me skin. So I'll tell you none. Do your worst arithmetic, madam. I won't help you count."

Edmund closed his eyes. Behind them, in the dark of his own skull, he saw the counting desk at Corvane & Sons, the neat columns of impossible things, and understood at last which column he himself had been entered under all along.

There were two roads out of this shed. In one, he walked through the door and gave himself up, and the map died in a warden's evidence locker, and Vell finished forgetting itself with Ilse and Sefa safe and hollow-eyed forever after. In the other—

In the other, he did the thing he had spent his whole life hiding. Here. Now. Before witnesses. Before the guild.

His hands were shaking. Sefa saw them shaking and gripped one, hard, and said nothing, because she understood before he said it — she had always understood faster than anyone — and Ilse looked at him across the dark and nodded once, slowly, like a queen granting a terrible knighthood.

Edmund stepped out from behind the drying racks into the firelight.

* * *

Later, he would never quite remember deciding. He remembered the cold air of the shed, and the smell of burning, and the hounds' heads coming up like compass needles finding north — because they had smelled him too, all along, the forbidden thing, and had been *confused*, poor faithful creatures, torn between the command and the truth of him.

He knelt in front of them. He let the gift rise in his throat, unhidden, unashamed, in a voice that was not entirely a human voice.

*I am not your quarry,* he said, in the tongue that needed no mouth. *Look at me. Smell me. I am the one who speaks. I am the one who hears. No one here has commanded you truly — they have only frightened you, and fear is not a master.*

The hounds' pale eyes fixed on him. The shed went silent except for the brazier's crackle and Brannoc's broken breathing.

"Halloran." Corvel's voice, still pleasant, still flat. "Step away from the animals."

*Your masters burned your kennel-brother last spring for going deaf,* Edmund went on, softer. *I heard him cry for three days. I am sorry no one else did.* He held out both hands, palms open. *Come to me. Or stay. But choose. You were never given a choice before.*

For a long moment, nothing. The fire spat. A warden shifted his weight.

Then the nearer hound — the older one, the one with the scarred muzzle — turned. Not lunged, not fled. *Turned*, deliberate as a door swinging shut, and sat down at Edmund's knee like a dog coming home. The second followed. And the shed full of salt-white terror broke apart into shouting and staves and Corvel's voice cracking at last into something human and furious:

"Forbidden! The gift is *forbidden* — seize him — mark him—"

What happened next happened fast and ugly. Sefa came off the racks like a thrown knife, cutting lantern cords, plunging the shed into strobing dark. Ilse's knife found the chain above Brannoc's wrists — three blows, precise, paid for with a gash across her forearm. Edmund ran with a hound on either side of him, huge and warm and finally, finally *his*, and behind him the wardens' shouts twisted from commands into panic as the beasts they had trusted wheeled against them.

They gained the flats. They gained the dark. Behind them, Corvel's voice rose over the shingle, no longer pleasant at all, calling out the words Edmund had dreaded all his life and now wore like a brand:

"*Marked!* Halloran is marked! Forbidden gift, openly wrought — every warden, every hound, every eye of the guild — bring me his heart in a jar!"

They ran until the estuary became marsh and the marsh became reed-beds whispering with things that whispered back to Edmund, *this way, this way, brother, this way.* At some point Brannoc stopped being carried and started walking. At some point Ilse bound her arm without complaint. At some point Sefa fell into step beside him and said, quietly, in the dark:

"You knew what it would cost."

"Yes."

"And you did it anyway. For him. For us."

Edmund looked down at the hounds pacing beside him, at the family stumbling around him through the reeds, and found that he was smiling, though his eyes burned. "I've balanced accounts my whole life," he said. "This one was easy."

Ahead, faint as a memory, the reed-beds opened onto water — black water, wide and still, and rising from its far shore a seam of white light, thin as a paper cut in the skin of the world.

The third gate. Waiting.

And Edmund understood, with a cold settling in his gut deeper than any warden's mark, why the map had grown so heavy against his ribs — and who, this time, it would ask to pay.
