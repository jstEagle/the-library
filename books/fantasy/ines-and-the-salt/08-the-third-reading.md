## 8. The Third Reading

Sefa had stolen a great many things in her life — purses, reputations, a silver reliquary once, from under the nose of a bishop who was still preaching when she'd pawned it. She had never before volunteered to be robbed.

She stood at the edge of the third gate with her hands jammed in her pockets and watched the seam of white light shiver in the air above the salt flats like a wound that hadn't decided whether to bleed. Two readings had already been paid. Edmund's memory of his mother's voice, spent at the confluence where the rivers argued. Ilse's memory of her father's face, given up in the ruins of the old customs house, after which she had come out of the light weeping and gentle and calling Sefa *sister* in a voice that no longer sounded like vengeance.

Two gates down. One between them and the door.

And the map, spread on Brannoc's knees as he sat on a boulder of white stone, said what it always said now, in letters that rearranged themselves each time one of them looked away: *the third gate asks for a reader who owes Vell nothing and chooses it anyway.*

That was the part Sefa kept coming back to. *Chooses it anyway.*

Ilse owed Vell her blood. Edmund owed it something older and stranger, some debt written into him before birth. But Sefa? Sefa had been born in the gutter behind the Fishmonger's Guild in Corvane, raised by a woman called Mother Hatch who collected orphans the way other people collected debts, taught to pick locks before she could write her own name. Vell had never done anything for her. Vell didn't even exist, as far as the world was concerned. It was a country erased so thoroughly that even its name felt like a coin from a dead currency.

Which meant she was exactly what the gate wanted.

"I'll do it," she said.

The word came out smaller than she intended. She cleared her throat and said it again, louder, the way she'd learned to say everything — loud enough that nobody could look too closely at what was underneath.

Nobody looked up right away. That was the thing about this family she'd fallen into: they were all terrible listeners when it mattered most. Brannoc was still turning the map over, hunting for a loophole in its ink. Edmund stood at the flat's edge talking to a gull — actually talking, head cocked, listening to the answer — because they were three days past pretending he didn't do that. Only Ilse heard her. Ilse turned, and her ash-grey eyes went soft in a way that would have made Sefa's skin crawl two weeks ago.

"Sefa—"

"It wants someone who doesn't owe it anything," Sefa said. "Someone who picks it free of charge. That's me. I'm the only one here whose whole life is worthless real estate." She grinned, because grinning was armor and she owned a full set. "Besides, what's it going to take? A memory? I've got plenty I'd pay to lose."

It wasn't true and everyone knew it. Her memories were the only property she'd ever guarded. The smell of tar on the Corvane docks. Mother Hatch's voice calling the children home by banging a spoon against a pot. The first purse she ever lifted, fat and warm as a sleeping bird. Those streets had raised her mean and quick and alive, and she loved them the way you love a scar — without gratitude, but completely.

Edmund came back from the gull with salt wind in his hair and that look on his face, the one that made him seem younger than all of them and older than the country itself. "You don't have to prove anything."

"Course I don't," Sefa said. "I want to."

"That's worse," he said quietly. "Wanting to prove you belong is how the map gets you. It can smell it."

"Well then it should love me," Sefa said. "Because I reek of it."

* * *

They argued until dark. That was what families did, she was learning. They sat around a fire of driftwood that burned green and strange, and Brannoc laid out reasons like a man laying out cards he knew were losing: the readings cost more each time; the third gate stood closest to whatever heart Vell still had; the price might not stop at one memory. Ilse said little, but when she spoke it was with the new gentleness that frightened Sefa more than any threat. "I lost my father's face," Ilse said. "Do you understand? I remember that I loved him. I remember that he died holding your hand, Edmund, and that I hated you for it. But his face — it's gone, and there's a hole in me shaped exactly like it, and I would not wish that hole on anyone. Not even for a door."

But Sefa had watched them all week. Watched Brannoc flinch at every horn on the wind, waiting for the Wardens to come finish what the salt interrogation started. Watched Edmund grow quieter, thinner, as if the country were already drinking him through a straw. Watched Ilse touch the folded map at night like a daughter touching a coffin.

They needed the door open. And none of them could afford to pay for it except her.

"I've got nothing ahead of me either," she said, when the arguments wore down to embers. "No trade, no name, no country that knows I exist. You're all going through that door into ordinary lives. Safe lives. Somebody has to buy the ticket, and I'm the only one who can pay in small change."

Brannoc put his big scarred hand over hers, careful as a man handling glass. "Your memories aren't small change, girl."

"No," she agreed. "They're the only fortune I've got. So I'm spending it on family."

Nobody said anything after that. The fire popped. Out on the flats, the seam of white light breathed in and out, patient as a creditor.

* * *

They walked out at dawn, when the salt was pink as the inside of a shell and the light hung low enough to touch. The third gate rose before them — not a doorway this time but an arch, thin as paper, humming. Up close Sefa could hear it. Not sound exactly. More like standing next to a wall and knowing there were people on the other side, going about their lives, separated from you by something you couldn't see through.

Edmund took her hands. His palms were cold and cracked from the salt air. "When it takes it," he said, "don't fight it. Fighting makes it take more. Ilse fought."

"I don't fight fair," Sefa said. "Never have."

"Then fight dirty," he said, and almost smiled. "Come back mean. Come back yourself. Just come back."

She squeezed his hands once, hard, because if she held on longer she'd start saying things that belonged in no ledger anywhere, and then she stepped forward into the arch.

The light took her like water takes a dropped coin.

There was no pain. That was the obscenity of it. There was warmth, and a voice — not her mother's, not anyone's, a voice like the country itself remembering aloud — and it asked her, gently, terribly: *what will you give?*

"The streets," Sefa said. "Corvane. The docks and the gutters and Mother Hatch and all of it. Take the whole city. It's yours."

And the country, courteous as any thief, obliged.

She felt them go. Not ripped — *lifted*, one by one, like coins drawn from her pocket by a pickpocket better than she'd ever be. The tar-smell of the docks went first, and she reached for it and found smooth blankness where it had been. Then the spoon banging the pot. Then Mother Hatch's face — she clawed at that one, she *fought*, just as Edmund had begged her not to, and she felt the country pause, almost sorry, and take it gently anyway, and take a little extra for the trouble: the name of the street she'd slept on, gone. The color of the sky over Corvane harbor, gone. Gone, gone, gone, each loss painless and total, holes opening in her that didn't hurt at all, which was somehow the worst thing she had ever known.

She came out of the light on her knees on the pink salt, and the arch behind her rang like a struck bell and dissolved into morning air.

Three gates. Paid.

"Sefa?" Edmund was crouched in front of her, gripping her shoulders. "Sefa, look at me. What's your name?"

"Sefa," she said. That one remained. Thank every god, that one remained.

"And where are you from?"

She opened her mouth.

Nothing came. There was a shape where the answer should have been — a warm, crowded, tar-and-onion shape, a whole childhood sitting right there — and when she reached for it, it was like reaching into a pocket she'd already been picked. She remembered *that she had* a home. She remembered that she had been loved, badly and fiercely, by a woman with a spoon. But the streets themselves, the names, the smells, the map of her own beginning — blank. Blank as fresh paper.

"I don't know," she said, and heard her own voice go strange. "I don't know where I'm from. Edmund, I don't—" She stopped, because crying was for people with somewhere to cry about, and she refused. She refused. "Ask me something else. Ask me anything else."

He asked her nothing. He just held her while the others gathered round, and over his shoulder she saw the map lying on the white stone, and the ink moving.

The letters were rearranging themselves faster than she'd ever seen, urgent now, almost frantic, and Brannoc read them aloud in a voice that went hoarse halfway through:

*Three gates are paid. The fourth opens onto the world. But the fourth gate does not ask for a gift. It asks for the reader entire. It requires one whom Vell remembers wholly — body, blood, and birthright — and it will consume all that he is: every memory, every year, every name. The reader will remain, and the reader will be no one.*

Silence, except the wind moving over the salt.

Sefa pulled back from Edmund's arms and looked at his face, and understood, with the clarity of someone who had just lost everything and therefore had nothing left to cloud her sight, why he had gone quiet all week. Why he'd grown thin. Why he talked to gulls and touched the ground like a man greeting a relative.

Vell remembered him. Wholly. It always had.

"There's no fourth volunteer," she said. Her voice came out flat and hard, gutter-hard, the one thing the country hadn't managed to steal. "Is there. It was never going to be any of us."

Edmund wouldn't meet her eyes. He was looking at the horizon, where — far off across the white flats — a line of black coats had appeared, moving fast, and the wind carried the thin high cry of Warden hounds.

"They found us," Ilse whispered.

"No," Sefa said, standing, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears, furious at the country, furious at the man who had known all along and let her spend her city thinking it would save him. "They found *him*. And he was planning to let them."

She grabbed Edmund by the collar and made him look at her at last.

"You don't get to be no one," she said. "I didn't pay a whole city for that."
