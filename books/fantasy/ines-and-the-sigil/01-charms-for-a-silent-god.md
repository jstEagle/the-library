## 1. Charms for a Silent God

The rain came down on Corvane like a debt being collected.

Orsen Vane stood beneath the sagging awning of the Saltwife's Rest and watched it hammer the harbor into gray lace. Three days of it. The gutters ran brown with ash from the foundries upstream, and the whole city smelled like wet iron and drowned things — which was fitting, she supposed, because Corvane was a city built on both. It had been outlawed eleven years now, struck from every map the Ministry kept, its name spoken only in the way people spoke of diseases they'd survived. Officially, no one lived here. Unofficially, forty thousand people did, and every one of them needed something the law said they couldn't have.

Which was why Orsen was here, at dawn, in the rain, with a dead god's mark burning cold under her sleeve and nine luck-charms sewn into the lining of her coat.

The fisherman had been waiting since before light. He sat on an overturned crate by the tavern's back door, hat in his hands, turning it around and around like a man winding a rope he meant to hang himself with. Young. Maybe twenty-five. Hands like knotted rope themselves, salt-scoured, the nails cracked. A herring-boat man, or he had been before the Ministry's quotas gutted the fleet; now probably he poached the shallows at night and prayed the patrol boats were lazy.

They usually weren't.

"You're the charm-wright." Not a question. His voice had the flat carefulness of a man who'd rehearsed this in front of a mirror and hated how it sounded. "The woman at the fish market said. Said you could—" He stopped. Started again. "My daughter's sick."

Orsen didn't move. "Sick how?"

"Fever. Four days. The herb-wife gave her willow-bark and it's not—" His hands stopped their turning. "It's not enough. I need luck. Real luck. Enough that whatever's got her lets go."

"Charms aren't medicine," Orsen said. She'd said it ten thousand times and would say it ten thousand more, because it was true and because saying it kept her honest in the one place she still had any honesty left to keep. "A luck-charm tips odds. It doesn't work miracles. If your girl needs a physician—"

"Physicians cost more than my boat." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "I don't have my boat anymore. They took it Tuesday. Quota violation." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were the eyes she saw in every mirror: a man standing at the edge of something with nothing left to hold onto. "I heard you're fair. I heard you don't cheat people who've got nothing. Please. Just tell me what you want for it."

Orsen looked out at the rain. Somewhere across the harbor a Ministry bell tolled the hour — six slow strokes, bronze-deep, the sound the occupation had given the city instead of a name. Five years ago that bell had made her flinch. Now it was just weather, like the rain.

"Two silver for the charm," she said. "And you tell me your name so I know who I'm cursing if you ever sell me out."

Something loosened in his face. Not quite a smile. "Denn. Denn Marlowe."

"Denn Marlowe." She crouched, opened her coat just far enough, and worked one of the charms free of its lining. It was a small thing — a disc of river-clay no bigger than a coin, fired hard, stamped with a spiral that meant *turning* in a language nobody spoke anymore. She'd pressed the spiral herself, three nights ago, in a rented room with the shutters drawn, and she'd done it the old way: thumb in the clay, breath held, and behind her teeth the first line of a prayer to a god who hadn't answered in five years.

She said the prayer anyway. Habit, or faith, or just the stubbornness of a woman who had nothing else to say with her mouth.

The clay stayed cold. It always stayed cold.

But she'd learned, over five years of selling silence to people drowning in it, that cold clay still worked if you believed hard enough that it might. That was the dirty secret of the charm trade, the one the Ministry had never understood when they burned the temples and salted the altars: the gods had gone quiet, but the shapes of them remained in human hands. A spiral pressed with intent still turned a fever's odds, a little. A knot tied in red thread still held a door shut against bad news, mostly. It wasn't divinity. It was carpentry. And Orsen Vane was, if nothing else, a very good carpenter.

She pressed the charm into Denn Marlowe's palm and folded his fingers over it.

"Sew it into her pillowcase. North corner. Don't tell her it's there — luck you can see is just pressure." She took his two silver coins, weighed them out of habit, and nodded. "And Denn. If the fever breaks, you never met me. If anyone asks, you bought the charm off a dead man in the Drownmarket. There's always dead men in the Drownmarket."

"Thank you." His voice cracked on the second word. "Thank you, I—"

"Don't." She stood, knees popping, thirty-four years old and feeling sixty in the rain. "Go home to your daughter."

He went. She watched him run through the downpour, coatless, holding the little clay disc against his chest like a coal he was carrying through a storm, and she felt the familiar thing move through her — the thing that wasn't quite warmth and wasn't quite grief, the thing that was the closest she came, these days, to praying.

*Let it be enough. Let the shape of the turning be enough.*

Under her sleeve, against the inside of her left wrist, the sigil lay silent as it had lain silent for eighteen hundred and twenty-seven days. She knew the count without counting. You always know the count of the thing that killed you.

* * *

The sigil had been beautiful once.

She remembered it that way — remembered everything that way, which was the trap the past set for people like her. Ossuin's mark, inked into her skin at seventeen by a priest whose name she'd also buried: a stylized blade crossed with a river, the god's two natures, war and water, judgment and mercy. When it was alive it had moved. That was the part nobody believed unless they'd seen it. The ink had flowed like current under her skin, brightening when she prayed, cooling when she sinned, and once — one time, on the field at Harrow Ford, with the enemy line breaking and Ossuin's voice like a bell inside her skull — it had blazed white enough to light the mud.

Five years ago it had gone dark. Not faded. Dark. Like a river frozen mid-flow, like a blade quenched in black ice. The priests called it the Silence. The Ministry called it proof. The theologians of the surviving underground called it the Question, and argued about the answer in cellars over candle stubs, and got arrested for it, and died for it, and the Silence went on.

Orsen had her own theory. She kept it where she kept the count: somewhere below thought, in the flooded cellar of herself where she put things too heavy to carry in daylight.

Her theory was that gods didn't die of neglect. Her theory was that something had happened to them — something sudden, something with hands — and that the whole country was walking around inside the aftermath of a crime, calling the empty sky peace.

She kept that theory to herself. Theories like that got you a black hood and a shallow grave outside the walls.

She was three streets from the Saltwife's Rest, hood up, charms secure, thinking about breakfast and whether Tallow would actually pay what he owed her or whether today was another day of eating trust, when she heard the whistles.

Ministry whistles. Three short, one long. Sweep pattern.

The street changed around her the way water changes around a dropped stone. A woman gathering washing pulled it down in one motion and vanished indoors. A boy selling eels flipped his basket shut and became very interested in a wall. Two men who'd been talking stepped apart and walked in opposite directions without finishing their sentence. Nobody screamed. Nobody ran. Running was what the whistles were for. Everyone in outlawed Corvane knew that the Ministry didn't chase you — they arranged the street so that running was the only stupid thing left to do, and then they shot the stupid.

Orsen did what everyone else did: she became furniture. She turned toward a shuttered stall, hunched her shoulders, and let her face go slack and bored, the face of a woman waiting out the rain, because that was exactly what she appeared to be. Under her sleeve, her hand found the edge of her cuff and held it down over the sigil. Not that the mark showed — five years dark, it was just ink now, just a scar with ambitions — but habits were armor, and hers were worn thin as anyone's.

They came around the corner four abreast: gray coats, black masks, the lacquered silence-sigil of the Ministry gleaming dully on their breasts. Behind them walked the one they all orbited — an Inquisitor, third rank by the braid, a narrow woman with a ledger chained to her belt and a censor's wand in her gloved hand. The wand was the thing that made Orsen's stomach drop. Censor's wands didn't find weapons. They found resonance. Prayer, devotion, active charms — anything that hummed at the frequency of the divine.

Anything like nine luck-charms sewn into a coat lining.

*Don't run,* she told herself. *You're a woman waiting out the rain. You are rain-furniture. You have been furniture for five years and you are very good at it.*

The sweep came down the street at parade pace, unhurried, thorough, terrifying in its boredom. The Inquisitor touched her wand to the eel-boy's basket. Nothing. To the shutters of the stall beside Orsen. Nothing. Ten feet away now. Eight. Orsen breathed the way she'd been taught at seventeen, the way Ossuin had taught her — slow in through the nose, out through the mouth, heart quiet, mind quiet, *quiet as a closed door* —

The wand swung toward her.

And stopped.

The Inquisitor frowned. Orsen watched the frown happen from very far away, from the flooded cellar, from behind the closed door. The wand's tip trembled — a fine vibration, like a tuning fork remembering a note — and the Inquisitor raised it closer to her own ear, listening, and Orsen thought with perfect clarity: *the charms. The clay is resonating. Why is the clay resonating, it has never resonated, it is cold clay and dead craft and—*

"Ma'am?" One of the graycoats. "Problem?"

The Inquisitor lowered the wand. Looked at it. Looked at Orsen — a long, flat, inventorying look, the look of a woman pricing a horse — and then, slowly, shook her head.

"Residue," she said. "Someone prayed here recently. Move on."

They moved on. All four graycoats and the Inquisitor with her trembling wand, down the street, around the corner, whistles going again three short one long, and the street exhaled, and the washing came back out, and the eel-boy reopened his basket, and Orsen Vane stood in the rain with her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out of a locked room.

*Someone prayed here recently.*

Nobody had prayed on this street in years. Nobody prayed anywhere in Corvane except in whispers, in cellars, in the sealed dark behind their own teeth. But the wand had sung. She'd seen it. Cold clay, dead craft, and the wand had *sung*—

She flexed her left hand. Under the cuff, the sigil lay dark and cold as ever.

Probably nothing, she told herself. Residue drifts. Pilgrims pass through. The wands misfire; they're cheap Ministry make, half of them haunted by nothing but shoddy craftsmanship.

She told herself that all the way to the Drownmarket, and almost believed it, right up until Tallow ruined her morning.

* * *

Tallow kept an office above a chandlery that smelled of tallow, unsurprisingly, and secrets, less unsurprisingly. He was a fat, soft-spoken man with the face of a disappointed uncle and the memory of a customs ledger, and he owed Orsen money, which was the foundation of their entire friendship.

"You look terrible," he said, pouring tea she didn't want into a cup that didn't look clean.

"It's raining."

"It's always raining. That's not what I asked about." He settled into his chair, which groaned the way furniture groans when it knows what's coming. "Word from the north road, Vane. Word you'll want to sit for."

"I'm sitting."

"Sit better." He drank his own tea, watching her over the rim, milking it, because Tallow milked everything, including cows he didn't own. "Pilgrim traffic's up. Way up. Caravans going north out of the lowlands, past the treaty line, into the hill country where even we don't have ears. And they're not going to shrines — the shrines are ash, you and I both know it. They're going to *places*. Crossroads. Wells. A hillside outside a village called Merrow's End where, allegedly—" he set the cup down with a click, "—a deaf child heard bells last month. And a well at Thornhollow that came up sweet after six years of brackish. And a widow in the fens whose dead husband's boat came home with the catch already salted."

Orsen said nothing. The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below them the chandlery boy was singing a smuggling song about a girl with two lovers, one of whom was the sea.

"Tavern talk," she said finally. "Grief makes miracles. Grief and cheap wine."

"Mm." Tallow produced, from somewhere in the depths of his coat, a scrap of paper — actual paper, expensive, not the greasy rag-pulp the city used — and unfolded it on the desk between them. On it, in a clerk's precise hand, was a list. Names. Places. Dates. "Copied this off a Ministry courier who won't be needing it. Know what it is? It's a tally. Every reported answer to prayer in the last six weeks, logged by the Office of Silence. Forty-one entries, Vane. Six weeks ago it was zero. Zero for five years, and then forty-one." He tapped the page. "The Ministry isn't logging rumors. They log *threats*. Which means somewhere in a gray office, someone has decided the gods are coming back. And you know what the Ministry does with threats."

"They burn them."

"They burn them." He leaned back. "So. Two pieces of news, and you get to choose which order. News one: there's a caravan forming at the Northgate, pilgrims and freight, heading up the old salt road toward the Door of Ash. They need guards. Pay's poor, company's worse, but it's movement north, and movement north is worth more than pay right now, if you take my meaning."

"And news two?"

Tallow's face did something complicated. For a moment the disappointed uncle fell away and underneath him was just an old smuggler, tired, in a city that officially didn't exist, telling the truth because he'd run out of lies that helped.

"News two," he said quietly, "is that three nights ago, in a room above the Saltwife's Rest, somebody pressed a luck-charm the old way. Thumb in the clay. Breath held. And the charm *took*, Vane. Took like the old days. My cousin's wife bought it off a fisherman this morning — fever broke overnight, whole street's talking. And the description of the seller matches a woman I've known for years, who I have never once known to lie to me." He folded his hands on his belly. "So which is it, Vane? Is the country waking up? Or is it just you?"

Orsen looked at the rain on the window. Under her sleeve, cold and dark, the sigil of a silent god lay against her pulse, and for the first time in five years she let herself ask the question she'd kept locked in the flooded cellar, the one with hands on it:

*If the gods are coming back — what happens to the person who's still wearing one?*

"The caravan," she said. "When does it leave?"

Tallow smiled, sad and knowing. "Tomorrow, dawn. Better decide fast."
