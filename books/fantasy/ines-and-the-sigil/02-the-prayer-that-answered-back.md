## 2. The Prayer That Answered Back

The knock came at the hour when even the lamps in Corvane's lower streets had given up pretending.

Orsen heard it through the wall of her rented room — three sharp raps, then a pause, then a fourth, softer, almost apologetic. The kind of knock that meant the person on the other side had stood in the rain deciding whether to knock at all. She knew it well. She'd answered a hundred of them from behind a hundred doors, and she knew what they wanted before she opened: not her, exactly. Not Orsen Vane with her cracked boots and her ledger of small debts. They wanted the sleeve. The thing beneath it. The dead god's mark she wore like a scar that had never quite finished scarring.

She almost didn't answer. The Ministry sweep two nights ago had left her nerves raw as stripped wire; she could still smell the ash-paste they'd smeared on doorframes to reveal hidden sigils, still hear the boots in the stairwell of the tenement where she'd been sleeping, climbing floor by floor while she crouched on a roof beam with her heart going like a drum in a funeral march. Tallow's warning sat in her chest too — *prayers being answered somewhere north* — and hope was a dangerous thing to carry into an open doorway.

The knock came again. Weaker now.

"Damn you," she said quietly, to no one in particular — to the city, to the night, to whatever god had once owned her and then gone quiet — and she opened the door.

The woman on the landing was soaked through, gray-faced, maybe thirty but aged past it by something worse than weather. She held a bundle against her chest wrapped in a man's coat, and the bundle was breathing. Orsen could hear it — the wet, rattling drag of lungs working too hard, each breath a small war.

"Please." The woman's voice came out shredded. "They said — the fisherman, Doss, he said you sold him a charm, a luck-charm, and it *worked*, he swore it worked, and I know luck-charms are nothing, I know they're tricks and old wives' nonsense, but my boy—" Her voice broke apart and reassembled itself around the word. "My boy can't breathe."

Orsen didn't move. Every instinct she'd sharpened over five years told her to close the door, to say she was a mender of kettles, a seller of buttons, anything but what she was. The Ministry paid informants in bread. Half of Corvane would sell their mothers for a sack of flour, and here was a woman standing in the open asking for outlawed work by name.

But the bundle shifted, and under the coat's edge Orsen saw a child's face — bluish at the lips, sweat-slick, eyes half-open and unfocused, chest pulling so hard against the ribs that she could count the effort. Seven years old, maybe eight. She'd seen men die with less fight in them.

"How long?" she asked.

"Since yesterday. The fever first, then his chest. The physicker wants four marks just to look at him and I have—" The woman fumbled at her neck, dragged out a cord, held it up. Three coins and a tin whistle on a string. A soldier's whistle, worn smooth. "It's everything. It's all we have."

Orsen looked at the whistle a long moment. Then she stepped back from the doorway.

"Inside. Quickly."

* * *

Her room was one room, and it lied about its purposes. A cot, a stool, a cold stove, a table where she carved charms from riverwood and bone and painted them with dyes that were only dyes — pigment and oil and prayer-shaped geometry, nothing more. For five years that was all the magic in them: the shape of belief, sold cheap to people who needed to believe something. She'd told herself it wasn't fraud. The shapes were true once. The words she whispered over them had been true once. If a luck-charm steadied a fisherman's hands because he believed, then the belief did the work, and she was only a carpenter of hope.

She lit the stove. Set water on. And while the woman — Marette, her name was, widow of a soldier, tenant of a cellar room off Tanner's Row — sat holding her son upright so he could struggle air into himself, Orsen took down the box.

Not the selling-box. The other one. Under the floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing she hadn't touched in months except to check that it was still there, still hers, still silent: a blank charm of white birchwood, unfinished, waiting for a mark. And beneath her left sleeve, the sigil itself.

She rolled the sleeve back.

The sigil sat on her forearm like a brand long healed — Ossuin's mark, the closed eye crossed by a blade, inked into her skin at nineteen by a priest who'd wept while he did it. Five years ago it had been warm. It had *sung*. When she'd sworn her oaths and taken her power, it had glowed like a coal under her flesh, and every charm she marked had carried a sliver of that fire out into the world. Then the Silence came — the day the gods stopped answering, all of them, everywhere, all at once — and the sigil had cooled to nothing. Dead ink. A tattoo of a life she used to have.

She pressed her thumb to it, the way she always did. Ritual without faith. Checking a grave.

And stopped.

It was warm.

Not body-warm. Not fever-warm. Warm the way a stone is warm hours after the sun has left it — faintly, impossibly, as if something on the far side of the world had remembered it existed. Orsen's breath went shallow. Tallow's voice crawled up out of her memory: *prayers being answered somewhere north.* She'd filed it away as smuggler's talk, drunk hope, rumor wearing rumor's clothes.

She pressed again. The warmth deepened. Somewhere beneath her skin, deeper than blood, something turned over in its sleep.

"No," she said aloud. "No, you're dead. You've been dead five years."

Marette looked up sharply. "What? What's wrong?"

"Nothing." Orsen pulled the sleeve down. Her hands were shaking, and she hated them for it. "Your son. Has he been bled?"

"The physicker bled him this morning. It made it worse."

Of course it did. Orsen knelt by the boy — Tam, Marette said his name was, Tam, wake up, Tam — and laid her palm flat on his chest. The ribs moved like a bird's, frantic and failing. Wet sounds in the lungs, deep ones. Pneumonia, or its uglier cousin. In the old days — in the days when her god answered — she had healed wounds like this with a word and a touch and never thought twice. She had walked through the plague camps of Vessarin with light pouring off her hands. That woman was five years buried.

All she had now was craft. Herb-craft, charm-craft, the honest medicine of the desperate. She set the water boiling with willow bark and lungwort, propped the boy upright, and worked. And while she worked, some part of her mind — the part that never stopped, the part that had kept her alive through purges and betrayals and the slow death of everything holy — kept circling back to the warmth in her arm like a tongue to a broken tooth.

It could be nothing. Bodies ran warm; fear ran warmer. It could be the beginning of a sickness of her own. It could be—

Tam coughed, a deep tearing cough, and brought up blood-flecked mucus, and Marette made a sound like something breaking, and Orsen stopped thinking and started doing.

When the herbs had done what herbs could do — which was little, which was slow, which might not be enough — she sat back on her heels and looked at the blank birch charm in her hand. Healing charms were the most expensive thing she sold and the least often bought, because everyone knew they were theater. She'd carved this one weeks ago and never marked it, because marking it felt like lying to someone dying.

She should carve a new one. She should do the ordinary thing, the safe thing, the thing that had kept her head attached to her neck for five years.

Instead she heard herself say, "Marette. Put your hand on his forehead. Keep it there."

"Why?"

"Because if this works, I want a witness. And if it doesn't—" She exhaled. "If it doesn't, I want you to remember I tried everything."

She pulled back her sleeve.

The sigil lay on her forearm in the lamplight, and it was glowing. Faintly — barely — a dull ember-light in the lines of the closed eye, so faint she might have imagined it, except Marette saw it too and went rigid, and the whistle slipped from her fingers and rang against the floorboards, one thin bright note that seemed very loud in the small room.

"What is that," Marette whispered. "What *is* that."

"A scar," Orsen said. "Hush now."

She took up the stylus — bone-tipped, worn smooth by years of marks — and dipped it in the ink she mixed for real work, iron-gall and salt and a drop of her own blood, because that much had always been true even when the rest was theater. She set the tip to the birch charm. And she spoke the words.

Not the market words. Not the mumbled blessing-for-coin she gave strangers. The *real* words, the oath-language, the tongue she'd sworn in at nineteen and never spoken aloud since, because speaking them felt like shouting into a house where everyone she loved had died. They came up out of her rough and rusted, half-swallowed, and she got through the first line and faltered and hated herself for faltering, and then —

— the sigil *flared*.

Light like a struck match behind her skin. Heat racing up her arm, across her shoulder, down into her hand, and the stylus moved without her, or she moved without thinking, and the mark that bloomed across the birchwood was not the simple healing-knot she'd intended but something older, denser, a design she hadn't drawn in five years and her hand had not forgotten. The lines drank the ink and shone. The whole charm lit like a lantern with a soul inside it.

Orsen couldn't breathe. The last time she had felt this, she had been standing in a circle of seven, and the world had been full of gods, and she had been young and certain and armed.

"*Ossuin,*" she said — involuntarily, a name escaping a locked room — and the light pulsed at the sound of it, once, like a heartbeat answering.

Then she pressed the charm to the boy's chest.

The effect was not gentle. Tam arched, gasping, and the rattle in his lungs turned to something else — a rushing, a draining, the sound of water finding its way out — and the blue drained from his lips and the fever-light from his eyes, and he breathed. One breath, whole and deep and easy. Another. His eyes focused, found his mother's face, and filled with tears, and he said, in a voice scraped raw but *his own*: "Mama? I'm hungry."

Marette made no sound at all. She folded over her son like a closing door, and her shoulders shook, and the only noise she made was the smallest possible keening, the sound people make when joy arrives too large for the container of their bodies.

Orsen sat back against the table leg and discovered she was on the floor. She didn't remember getting there. Her arm throbbed — the sigil burned like a fresh brand now, vivid, awake, *alive* — and terror and wonder were tangled in her chest so tightly she couldn't tell which was which, couldn't find the seam between *it worked* and *oh gods, it worked, and now what.*

Because this was not luck-charm theater. This was not belief doing the heavy lifting. Something had come through her — something vast and starving and unmistakably *present* — and it had used her arm as a door.

Five years of silence. Five years of dead ink. And tonight, in a rented room above a shuttered chandlery in outlawed Corvane, her god had answered a prayer.

Or something wearing his voice had.

* * *

She made Marette take the coins back. All but one — "for the wood and the herbs, and that's charity enough" — and Marette wept over that too, and promised silence, and Orsen looked at the woman's shining face and knew with cold certainty that the promise was worthless. Not because Marette was false. Because joy like that doesn't fit inside a person. It leaks. It spills. By morning it would be in the wine-shops and the fish-market and the queue outside the bread ovens, growing details as it traveled: the witch of Tanner's Row, the saint of the chandlery, the woman whose arm *glowed*.

By morning, half of Corvane would know. Including the parts of Corvane that reported to the Ministry of Silence.

"You have to go," Orsen said, when Tam had eaten broth and fallen into true sleep, the easy sleep of the healed. "Tonight. Now. Don't tell anyone where you got the medicine. Say the physicker relented. Say anything."

"But—"

"Marette." Orsen took her by both shoulders. "Do you know what the Ministry does to vessels? To people who carry living divinity?" The woman's face said she didn't, and Orsen almost envied her. "They don't ask questions first anymore. That's the questions. Understand me? You were never here. Your son was sick and then he wasn't. Go."

Marette went, wrapping Tam in the coat, pausing at the door to look back with an expression Orsen recognized and dreaded — reverence, raw and unearned, aimed straight at her. Then the stairs swallowed them, and the room was quiet, and Orsen stood alone in the lamplight with her arm burning.

She unwrapped the sleeve and looked at the sigil.

It glowed steadily now. Not flickering — steady, patient, like a lamp left burning in a window for someone expected home. The closed eye. The crossed blade. Five years of dust blown off it in a single night.

She should scrape it out. She'd thought about it before, in the early years — knife, or acid, or the Ministry's own ash-paste, anything to be rid of the mark that marked her. She'd never done it. Cowardice, she'd called it. Tonight she understood it was something else: you do not dig out your own grave while you're still using it.

"Talk to me," she whispered to it. "Five years of nothing, and now this? Why now? Why a dying boy in Corvane? Where were you for the plague camps, for the purges, for—" Her voice caught on names she didn't let out. "Where were you?"

The sigil glowed. It did not answer. But beneath the glow, faint as a pulse under a wrist, she felt it: attention. Something on the other side of the silence, aware of her, straining toward her, the way a drowning man strains toward light through water.

And underneath the wonder — underneath the wild, shameful, singing joy of feeling her god's power move through her hands again after five years of amputation — the terror sat where it always sat, patient as winter:

*Answered prayers are evidence.*

Somewhere north, prayers were being answered, and Tallow had heard rumors. Somewhere in Corvane, a woman was carrying home a miracle and a story. And somewhere in the Ministry's black towers, clerks kept ledgers of rumor the way merchants kept ledgers of debt, and a name was already being written — *vessel, female, lower city, healing-work* — in a hand that did not press hard, because names in that book rarely lived long enough to need erasing.

Orsen banked the stove, packed her selling-box, checked her escape routes out of habit — window to the roofline, stairs to the yard, yard to the drainage cut — and sat by the dead fire until dawn, watching the sigil glow, unable to decide whether she was looking at a miracle or a target.

She was still awake when the knocking started.

Not one knock. Many. From the street below, rising up — voices, too, hushed and urgent, multiplying. She went to the shutters and put her eye to the gap.

In the gray light of dawn, in the mud of the lane below her window, people were gathering. Fishwives. Dockhands. An old soldier with half his face gone to scars. A girl holding a baby with bandaged eyes. A dozen of them, then more, coming up the lane through the drizzle, and every one of them looking up at her shutters with the same expression Marette had worn — that terrible, hungry reverence.

The girl with the baby called up, in a voice that carried and cracked: "They said you healed Tam of Tanner's Row. They said the gods are waking. Please — please, lady — my daughter's eyes—"

Behind her, the crowd murmured agreement, and the murmur spread outward down the lane like fire finding straw, and Orsen stood in the dark behind her shutters with her burning arm pressed to her mouth, understanding that the choice had already been made for her.

Word spreads overnight. And it had wings.
