## 4. Tallow's Map

The rain had turned Corvane's lower streets into a network of brown veins, and Orsen waded through them with her collar up and her hood down, because hoods marked you as someone with something to hide, and in the Salt Quarter that was an invitation. Three days since the boy. Three days since the sigil had burned like a struck match under her skin and a child who should have drowned in his own fever had sat up in bed and asked for bread.

Three days of not sleeping.

Word traveled the way water did in Corvane — downhill, fast, and into every cellar. She'd heard her own name whispered at the tannery stalls, seen chalk marks on doorposts she recognized as the old warning signs: *miracle-worker here, or liar here, or bait here.* The Ministry didn't need to post notices anymore. The city posted them for free.

She took the long way to Tallow's, doubling back twice along the canal, watching the reflections for grey coats. Nothing. Only rain, and the gaslights drowning in it, and somewhere a dog barking itself hoarse at nothing.

Tallow kept his shop behind a chandler's on Rope Walk, down a stair that smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone. The sign above the door read *Candles & Sundries* in letters so faded they were more suggestion than text. Inside, the shelves were crowded with the honest junk of a dishonest trade: candle stubs by the pound, rope ends, lamp oil, bundles of dried herbs that were exactly what they appeared to be, which was the best camouflage anything in Corvane could have.

Tallow himself sat behind the counter, a broad soft man with a face like rising dough and small quick eyes that missed nothing. He was rendering wax when she came in — she could smell it, the sweet animal reek of it — and he didn't look up until the bell over the door had finished its sentence.

"Closed," he said.

"It's raining."

"Then you'll get wet going home." But his eyes flicked past her shoulder to the street, held there a moment, and then he reached over and turned the lock with a click that sounded louder than it was. "Get down here where the light doesn't reach, girl. You've got a face on you like a wanted poster."

"That's because I am one." She came around the counter into the back room, where the light was one oil lamp and the walls were stacked with crates that had never held candles in their lives. "Or I will be by morning."

"By morning," Tallow agreed, wiping his hands on his apron. "You want tea? It's terrible."

"I want what you promised me at the fish market. Before Rell's people started pulling carts apart on Bridge Street."

He poured two cups anyway, from a pot that had been sitting on the stove long enough to develop opinions. The tea was terrible. She drank it anyway, because her hands wanted something to hold.

"You've heard it too," she said.

"Heard it?" Tallow lowered himself onto a crate with the groan of a man whose knees had their own weather. "Girl, I've got cousins in Harrow Fen, up north past the marshes. My cousin Maret wrote me — actual ink on paper, which already tells you how frightened he is, because nobody writes anymore unless they're scared or dying. He says in the fen villages they're praying again. Openly. On the roads. And the prayers are being *answered.* Crops coming in through blight. Wells that went brackish running sweet. A woman in Marsh End raised her husband out of a fever that had already stopped his breath." He shook his head slowly. "Five years of nothing, and now this. Like somebody opened a door somewhere and forgot to close it."

"Or remembered how."

Tallow looked at her then, really looked, the way he did when he was pricing something. His gaze dropped to her left sleeve, where the sigil slept under three layers of linen and binding-salve, quiet now but present, the way a scar is present. "How is it?"

"How's what?"

"Don't insult us both."

Orsen turned the cup in her hands. "Warm," she said finally. "It's been warm for three days. Not burning. Just... warm. Like something breathing under a blanket."

"And before?"

"Cold. Cold for five years. Cold as a coin on a dead man's eye."

Tallow nodded slowly, and for once he didn't have a price ready. That frightened her more than the Ministry did. Tallow without a price was a man thinking about survival instead of profit, and survival made people do strange arithmetic.

"There's a way out," he said at last. "There's only one way out, and you know what it is."

"The Door of Ash."

"The Door of Ash." He said it the way you'd say the name of a dead friend — carefully, with respect for what it had cost. "Officially, of course, there's no such thing. Officially, Corvane doesn't exist. Officially, none of this exists — the country signed itself out of the world six years ago with the Treaty, and the world was polite enough to agree. No borders. No embassies. No legal way in or out for anyone with a name and a face." He smiled without humor. "But paper is paper and ground is ground, and the ground doesn't care what the Treaty says. There's a pass in the northern mountains, past the fen country, where the old road runs up to a gate cut into the rock. Volcanic country. The stone there is black and always warm, and the gate stands in it like a keyhole with no key. On the far side: the borderlands, the camps, the world. People still call it the Door of Ash because of what the mountain did the year it opened last — spat ash for nine days, buried the road, closed itself. Nobody's been through since."

"Why not?"

"Because the Ministry holds the lowlands between here and the fens, and the pass itself has gone strange. Weather comes down off that mountain wrong. Snow in summer, fog that eats sound. And because—" he hesitated, and Orsen felt the shape of the hesitation, the weight of the thing he wasn't saying yet, "—because getting there costs money. Real money. Smugglers' money. Guides who know the fen paths, bribes at the toll-camps, papers forged well enough to survive a lantern test. For one person, traveling lean and mean and willing to sleep in ditches? Forty crowns. Maybe fifty, if the toll-keepers are feeling pious."

Orsen laughed, short and bitter. "I have eleven."

"Eleven." Tallow turned his cup, considering. "And your stock's burned. You can't sell charms in the markets anymore — every fence in the Quarter knows your face now, and half of them would sell you to Rell for the reward and call it patriotism. So you can't earn it. And you can't stay, because Rell let you go, and men like Rell don't let things go. They *loosen* them. Like a snare. You walk easy for a day, two days, and then the wire pulls tight."

"I know how snares work, Tallow."

"Do you?" He set down his cup. "Then tell me why he let you walk out of that room."

She couldn't. She'd turned it over for three nights, lying on a pallet in a safehouse that smelled of mildew, listening to the rain and the rats and her own heart. Inquisitor Halvard Rell had sat across from her with a file full of her life and asked his questions in a voice like a man reading scripture, and when she'd given him nothing — when she'd given him *less* than nothing — he had stood, straightened his coat, and told her she was free to go. Free. As if freedom were a thing he carried in his pocket and dispensed at whim.

"Bait," she said quietly. "I'm bait. He wants to see who comes to me. Who I run to. Where I go."

"Yes." Tallow didn't soften it. "So whatever you do next, you do it knowing he's watching the road behind you. Which means the Door of Ash isn't just expensive. It means moving fast enough and careful enough that a man with the whole Ministry behind him loses the thread." He rubbed his jaw. "Forty crowns, Orsen. Fifty if we're unlucky. Eleven in your purse."

"So I'm staying."

"So you're *stuck,*" he corrected, "which is worse. Staying is a choice. Stuck is just waiting for the trap." He was quiet a moment, and she watched him wrestle with something — she'd known him eight years, since she'd come to Corvane with dead eyes and a dead god and a bag of charms to sell, and in eight years she'd learned all his tells. This was the tell of a man about to say something he'd sworn not to say.

"But," he said. "There's a job."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"Tallow. Every job you've ever offered me has ended with me bleeding in a ditch or running from the Watch, and you taking your cut off the top."

"This one pays forty crowns."

That stopped her mouth. Forty crowns was passage money. Forty crowns was the Door of Ash with change left over for bread on the far side. She sat very still and let him talk, because she already knew, in the place where she kept her oldest instincts, that she was going to hate it.

"A caravan," Tallow said. "Pilgrims. They're gathering already — you've seen them, haven't you? On the north roads. People walking toward the fens because the prayers are answered there, because the gods are waking there, and half of them want to touch the miracle and half of them just want to be near it when it happens. There's a woman organizing a proper caravan out of the Weepers' Yard — pilgrims, refugees, a few merchants' families who've decided the Ministry's purges are closer than the winter. Two hundred souls, maybe more. Wagons, livestock, the whole slow river of it."

"They'll never make it. The Ministry burns congregations. You know what happened at Saint Verre's."

"I know." His voice went flat. "I know exactly what happened at Saint Verre's. Which is why they're paying for guards. Real ones. The organizer — a woman called Mother Cassia, used to be something high in the old church before the Treaty dissolved it — she's hiring anyone with a blade and a clean-enough record, and paying forty crowns a head for the road to the fens, and from the fens there are guides who'll take pilgrims up to the pass for another fee, because where there's faith there's always someone selling passage to it." He leaned forward. "Forty crowns, Orsen. One job. Guard a caravan of farmers and children north through country where nobody in their right mind travels. Then take your pay and your eleven crowns and buy your way through the Door of Ash, and I will personally drink to your health in a country that exists."

"And if the Ministry catches the caravan?"

"Then you'll be standing in the middle of it," Tallow said, "with a blade in your hand and a choice to make. Same as always."

Orsen looked into her cup. The tea had gone cold and bitter, a little pond of it, and her reflection floated there broken by the ripples of her own trembling hands. Two hundred souls. Wagons and children and old women, walking north into the teeth of a Ministry that executed vessels and burned congregations, toward gods that were waking underneath the world like something stirring in a grave. And her, planted in the middle of it, wearing a sigil that was getting warmer by the day, carrying a name that Rell already knew.

It was a trap. Not Rell's trap — Tallow's, or fate's, or the simple trap of being the kind of person who couldn't watch a caravan of children walk into fire. Tallow knew that. He'd priced it accordingly.

"When does it leave?"

"Four days. Weepers' Yard, dawn." Tallow's shoulders came down half an inch; he'd known the answer before he asked, same as she had. "You'll want new papers. I've a man who does faces — not good faces, but faces that survive a lantern test at ten paces in bad light. Cost you three of your eleven."

"Of course it does."

"Everything costs, girl. That's the whole of the wisdom I've got." He rose, groaning, and began rummaging in a crate, and came up with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth which he pushed across the counter. "Blade oil, salve for the sigil — the good kind, the kind that actually binds — and a map."

She unwrapped it. The map was hand-drawn on waxed linen, the ink fine and sure: the city, the north roads, the fen country rendered in careful crosshatching, and beyond it the mountains, and there, in the black rock, a single mark like a keyhole, annotated in Tallow's cramped hand: *warm stone. nine days' ash. ask for Yara at the last waystation.*

"Yara?"

"Runs the waystation below the pass. Ex-Ministry, if you can believe it. Broke away years back, reasons she doesn't discuss. She gets pilgrims through sometimes — for a price." He tapped the map. "Don't show her the sigil until you have to. Don't show *anyone* the sigil until you have to. And Orsen—" he caught her eye, and for a moment the merchant fell away and there was just an old man, tired and fond and frightened for her, "—if the gods are truly waking, and your god is among them, then the moment that sigil stops being warm and starts being *loud*, you don't wait for the caravan, you don't wait for the pay, you don't wait for anything. You run for that door and you go through it, and you don't look back at whatever's chasing you. Promise me."

She looked at the map. At the little keyhole drawn in black rock, the only exit from a country that had erased itself. Five years ago she'd have laughed at the idea of running. Five years ago she'd been Vessa, blade-sworn, certain, armored in purpose, and gods had been things you served, not things that chased you.

Now she was Orsen Vane, charm-seller, and her god was a warmth under her skin like a fever deciding whether to break.

"I promise," she said, because Tallow needed to hear it, and because promises were cheap and she was suddenly, desperately poor.

* * *

She spent the four days preparing the way she prepared for everything now: quietly, in pieces, trusting no single errand to reveal the whole.

The papers cost three crowns and gave her the face of Marta Ilves, a widowed laundress from the canal districts, thirty-four years old, no gifts, no gods, no history worth a lantern test. The face-man worked out of a barber's back room and talked constantly while he inked, about his daughter, about the price of coal, about rumors from the north — wells running sweet, a saint in Marsh End, soldiers deserting rather than burn another village. Everyone was talking about the north now. The whole city was turning its face toward it like a field of flowers finding the sun, and the Ministry could arrest talkers faster than talk spread, but not much faster.

She sold what remained of her stock — honestly, this last time, telling each buyer plainly that the charms were cold, that whatever luck they carried was only craft and hope, no god in it. Some still bought. An old dockhand pressed a coin into her hand and said, "Craft and hope's more than most of us ever had," and she thought about that for a long time afterward.

On the third night she went to the safehouse and packed: the blade, the salve, the map, dry socks, the eleven minus three minus the bribes she'd need just to reach the Weepers' Yard alive. Under the floorboard, beneath everything, she left the one thing she couldn't carry and couldn't destroy — a child's wooden bird, painted blue, bought in another life by a woman named Vessa for a reason she no longer let herself remember. She put the board back over it and told herself the house would probably burn anyway, and that it was better this way.

On the fourth night, she didn't sleep. She sat by the window of the safehouse with the sigil warm under her sleeve and watched the city breathe. Toward dawn the rain stopped, and the clouds tore open over the northern rooftops, and for a moment — a moment only — she saw stars, sharp and cold and many, and under her skin the sigil pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat remembering itself.

Like something turning over in its sleep.

* * *

The Weepers' Yard at dawn was a tide of humanity that had pooled in a graveyard and overflowed its banks. The old church that gave the yard its name had been stripped to its bones by the Ministry years ago — roofless, windowless, its saints chiseled blank — but its graveyard wall made a corral, and inside the corral the caravan was assembling itself out of fear and hope and whatever people had managed to load in the night.

Two hundred was an underestimate. Orsen stood at the gate and counted wagons until counting became pointless: covered carts, market drays, a gilded funeral coach with its plates painted over, handcarts, wheelbarrows, ponies, oxen, goats on ropes, cages of chickens swinging from axles. Children everywhere, wrapped in every scrap of cloth their families owned, eyes enormous above the mufflers. Old people. Pregnant women. A man carrying a loom strapped to his back like a crucifixion. The smell was woodsmoke and wet wool and animal dung and, underneath it, faint and unmistakable, incense — someone had brought incense, in defiance of every law left in the country.

Guards moved along the wagon line: maybe thirty of them, hired blades in mismatched armor, checking wheels and axles and water barrels. Orsen joined the line at the pay-table without letting herself think about it, because thinking was how courage died.

Mother Cassia sat behind the table like a fortress. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a door, with a face that had been handsome the way a cliff is handsome and eyes that weighed souls and found most of them light. A ledger lay open before her. A strongbox sat at her feet with a bored mercenary's boot resting on it.

"Name."

"Marta Ilves. Laundress." Orsen slid the papers across. "Guard work. Blade and bow."

Cassia read the papers the way she presumably read scripture — thoroughly, hunting heresy. "Canal districts. Widowed."

"Six years."

"Who did you lose?"

"The war," Orsen said, which was true in a way Cassia wouldn't check and couldn't imagine.

Cassia looked up. Her eyes were pale grey and utterly without mercy, and they stayed on Orsen's face for three seconds longer than comfort allowed. "I've hired forty blades this week, Marta Ilves. Half of them are liars and a quarter of them are Ministry plants, and I know which is which more often than not, because I was reading faces before you were born. So understand me." She leaned forward. "Whatever you're running from, it stays behind this wall. Whatever you're running *toward*, you leave at the border. These people"—she gestured at the yard, the wagons, the children, the whole fragile ark of them—"are not cargo and they are not shields. They are the last congregation this country has. You guard them because they are worth guarding. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Mother."

"Forty crowns at the fens. Twenty now, twenty on delivery. You abandon the caravan, I'll have your description in every market from here to the mountains before your footprints cool." She pushed across a leather pouch and a wooden tally-stick stamped with the caravan's seal. "Wagons seven through twelve. You answer to a man called Doss — big fellow, grey beard, used to be a soldier. Report to him."

Orsen took the pouch. Twenty crowns, real weight, real metal. More money than she'd held in two years. It should have felt like salvation. It felt like a leash, and she supposed both were made of the same leather.

She found Doss at wagon nine, a mountain of a man mending a harness with fingers surprisingly gentle, and he looked her over once, grunted, and pointed her to the rear flank without a single question, which she was grateful for and slightly suspicious of.

The sun cleared the rooftops. Somewhere a horn blew — three notes, ragged but determined — and the caravan began to move, groaning out of the Weepers' Yard into streets lined with silent watchers. People came out of doorways to see it pass. Some wept. Some knelt, right there in the mud, and made the old signs, the outlawed ones, quick and furtive as thieves. A child broke from the crowd and ran alongside the wagons until her mother caught her and pulled her back, and the child's wailing followed the caravan for three streets.

North, they went. Out through the tannery gates, past the last toll-post where grey-coated Ministry clerks checked papers with deliberate slowness and let them pass — *bait*, Orsen thought, *he's watching which way the fish swims* — and onto the north road, where the mud was frozen at the edges and the sky hung low and white as unwashed wool.

Orsen walked the rear flank with her hood back and her face open to the cold, Marta Ilves the laundress, widow, no gifts, no gods. Under her sleeve, against the binding-salve and the linen, the sigil lay warm as a sleeping animal.

By midday they had left the city's smoke behind. By evening the road climbed into scrub country, and the first hard frost silvered the wheel ruts, and the pilgrims sang — quietly at first, then less quietly, hymns that hadn't been sung aloud in six years, their breath steaming in the dark like incense.

Orsen walked at the back and did not sing, and watched the north, where the mountains waited under the stars like a row of locked doors.

And far off, at the edge of hearing — so far off she told herself she imagined it, told herself it was wind, told herself it was nothing — she heard singing too. Thin and high and wordless, coming down off the frozen hills, keeping pace with the caravan.

Keeping pace with *her.*

Under her sleeve, the sigil burned.
