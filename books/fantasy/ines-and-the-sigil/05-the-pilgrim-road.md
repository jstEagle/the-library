## 5. The Pilgrim Road

The caravan left Corvane before dawn, thirty-one souls and eleven carts, moving north through a country that pretended not to exist.

Orsen rode at the rear, where the dust was thickest and no one asked questions. The pilgrims were mostly poor — farmers whose fields had stopped growing anything but frost, fisherfolk whose nets came up empty or full of things that had no business being alive, mothers with children who woke in the night speaking words in languages that had died centuries ago. They carried what they could and burned what they couldn't, and they walked toward rumors the way drowning people swim toward light: not because they believed it would hold them, but because standing still was certain death.

Tallow had arranged it all with his usual talent for making other people's desperation profitable. Orsen's payment for escorting them was passage money she'd never have earned in ten years of selling charms — plus one condition she hadn't liked from the start.

"You'll travel with the girl," Tallow had said, jerking his chin toward a slight figure sitting motionless at the edge of the gathering crowd. "Sefa. She's blind. Her grandmother paid for her place and then died before the departure, so now she travels alone unless someone keeps an eye on her. You keep an eye on her."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only one here who doesn't want something from her." He'd paused, chewing on something. "Or because you're the only one who wants something *worse*. Either way, you'll do."

Now, three days out from Corvane, Orsen had learned exactly three things about Sefa. She ate little. She slept even less. And when the cart wheels hit a rut, she smiled — a small, private smile, like someone hearing a joke told just under the audible world.

The girl sat at the back of the third cart among sacks of dried lentils, her face turned up to the gray morning as if reading weather written somewhere above the clouds. Her eyes were the pale, unfocused blue of river ice, and they never moved, never tracked, never blinked at anything Orsen could see. A knotted cord hung around her neck, and her fingers worked its beads constantly, counting something only she could count.

"Your god is loud today," Sefa said, without turning her head.

Orsen's hand went to her sleeve before she could stop it. Beneath the wool, the sigil lay quiet — had lain quiet all morning, all week, since the boy in Corvane. But quiet wasn't silent. Quiet was a held breath.

"I don't have a god," Orsen said. "Mine went away."

"He didn't go anywhere." Sefa's fingers kept their counting. "He's folded very small. Like a letter in a pocket. Letters don't stop saying what they say just because nobody reads them."

The cart lurched over a stone. Somewhere ahead, a pilgrim began singing — one of the old road hymns, the kind the Ministry had outlawed for carrying too many names. The woman's voice cracked on every third note, and no one told her to stop.

"What do you hear?" Orsen asked. "Exactly."

Sefa turned her face toward her then, and those ice-pale eyes found Orsen with terrible accuracy, the way a compass finds north regardless of what stands between.

"A lot of talking," she said. "Most of it's old. Old talk sounds like water over stones — you stop noticing it. But there's one voice that isn't old. It's been getting louder since we left the city, and this morning it started saying a name over and over, and the name isn't mine." She tilted her head. "It's yours. But it isn't the name everyone uses."

The dust rose behind them. The hymn cracked onward. Orsen made herself breathe.

"People have a lot of names for me," she said. "Charm-witch. Smuggler. None of them worth keeping."

"This one's older than all of those." Sefa smiled again, that under-the-world smile. "It's buried deep. It has dirt on it. Someone put it in the ground on purpose and walked away, and now he's digging it up with both hands, and he's crying while he digs."

*He.* Orsen looked north, where the hills were still green-brown and ordinary, and felt the sigil shift beneath her sleeve like a sleeper turning over.

"Tell him," she said quietly, "to stop digging."

Sefa's fingers paused on the cord for the first time since Orsen had known her.

"He won't," she said gently. "That's the whole point of him."

* * *

Sefa heard the gods the way other people heard rain — constantly, in layers, some near and some far, some gentle and some with teeth.

She had been born this way, though it took her grandmother four years to understand what the child's babble actually was. Other children invented invisible friends; Sefa reported conversations. Her grandmother, who had worshipped in cellar congregations and buried three prayer-books under the floorboards, had gone white as ash the first time little Sefa repeated, word for word, a blessing spoken aloud by a priestess two hundred miles south who had died before Sefa was born.

"They're always talking," Sefa explained to the lentil sacks, to the road, to anyone who sat near her long enough. "Even when they were shut away. Especially then. Shut-away things talk the most. Prisoners sing."

She liked the pilgrims. They smelled of fear and hope boiled down together, which was, she'd decided, the smell of honest people. The soldiers who had come through her village last winter smelled of nothing at all, which was worse.

But the woman called Orsen — the one with the dead god folded small against her wrist — Sefa could not stop listening to. Not to her voice, which was careful and flat as a closed ledger, but to what moved underneath it. Most people rang like empty pots. Orsen rang like a well with something drowned at the bottom.

And the voice — the loud one, the digging one — had been saying her name for three days now.

Not *Orsen*. The voice didn't know that name, or cared nothing for it. The voice said the other one, the buried one, the one with dirt packed around it like a seed planted wrong-side-up. Each time it said the name, something in the woman flinched, and each flinch sent out a little ripple into the audible world, and Sefa heard the ripples the way she heard everything: as light.

On the fourth day, the voice changed its tune.

It stopped saying the name and started saying something else instead, over and over, patient as tide:

*Come and see. Come and see. Come and see.*

Sefa didn't tell the woman right away. She was learning, slowly, that sighted people needed things given to them in the right order, like medicine dosed out drop by drop. Too much truth at once and they did what the Ministry men had done in her village: they broke things to make the noise stop.

So she waited until evening, when the caravan made camp in the shadow of a ruined wayshrine — one of the old ones, its saint's face chiseled off during the Silence, leaving a smooth blank oval that caught the firelight like an egg — and the pilgrims were singing again, badly, beautifully.

The woman sat apart, as always, mending a strap with the focused violence of someone who would rather be doing anything else.

"He stopped saying your name today," Sefa said, settling onto the stone beside her.

Orsen's hands stilled. "Good."

"He says something else now. He says—" Sefa listened inward, tilting her head, letting the words come up through her like bubbles through dark water. "*Come and see.*"

The strap slipped from Orsen's fingers.

For a moment the firelight moved on her face and Sefa heard — actually heard, in the ringing way — the well-thing inside the woman turn over, huge and slow, and press its mouth against the underside of the surface.

"That's not possible," Orsen said. Her voice was steady. Everything else about her wasn't. "Whatever you're hearing, it isn't for me. I'm nobody's messenger. I'm nobody's anything."

"You're his," Sefa said simply. "You've been his a long time. That's why it hurts so much to listen to you. There's a rope in you, and it goes down and down, and at the end of it something is pulling."

"Girl—"

"Sefa."

"*Sefa.*" Orsen turned to face her fully, and up close her fear had a shape Sefa could almost see — a door held shut by someone leaning on it. "Five years ago my god went silent. Silent means dead, in my trade. Dead gods don't call people north. Dead gods don't dig up names. Whatever speaks to you, it's wearing his voice like a coat, and things that wear voices are the reason the Ministry burns villages. Do you understand? If you repeat this to the wrong ears—"

"To Rell's ears," Sefa said.

Orsen went very still. "What do you know about Rell?"

"He's coming." Sefa said it the way she'd report weather, or supper. "He's been coming since the city. He rides fast and he prays ugly. His prayers sound like knives being counted." She reached out and found Orsen's sleeve, the one with the secret folded inside it, and pressed two fingers there — lightly, the way you touch a wound to learn its depth. Under her fingertips the sigil pulsed once, warm as a swallowed coal. "He knows about the boy in Corvane. He knows about you. And the voice says if he catches you, he'll use you to hurt everything that's trying to wake up."

The campfire crackled. The hymn had ended. In the silence, Sefa heard the voice begin again, far off and patient, and saw — in the way she saw everything — the woman beside her go rigid as a snared animal that hasn't yet decided whether to fight the rope or run with it.

"Come and see," Sefa whispered. "That's all he says. He doesn't order. He doesn't promise. He just asks. Isn't that strange? All the old stories, the gods command and threaten and bargain. This one just asks."

Orsen pulled her sleeve free. When she spoke, her voice had rebuilt itself, flat and closed, the ledger shut again.

"Get some sleep, Sefa. We move at first light."

But she didn't sleep. Sefa heard her all night, sitting upright by the dead fire, and heard the sigil burning slow and low against her wrist like a letter finally being read, and heard — deeper than all of it, down where the rope ended — the thing at the bottom of the well saying the buried name one more time, soft as a hand laid on a grave.

*Vessa.*

* * *

They crossed into hill country on the sixth day, and the world began to behave strangely.

It started small. A spring beside the road that ran warm in the morning and cold at noon. Birds flying in circles, unhurried, as if waiting for instructions. Then, on the seventh day, the frost.

It came at midday, in summer, in a land where summer still had six weeks left in it. One moment the pilgrims were sweating under a mild sun; the next, breath bloomed white and silver lace crept across the cart planks. It lasted an hour and passed like a fever breaking, and the pilgrims laughed nervously and blamed mountain air, and only Sefa heard what underlay it: a groan from far below the ground, vast and muffled, like something enormous shifting in chains.

After that it happened twice more. Frost at noon. Warmth at midnight. The seasons stuttering like a lamp with a bad wick.

"The ground is sick," Sefa told Orsen on the eighth evening, as they stood watch together. Above them the stars were wrong — not missing, just dimmed, as if seen through smoked glass. "Under us. Something down there is hurting, and the hurting leaks upward. First the cold. Then worse things."

"Worse how?"

"I don't know yet. The voices are arguing about it." She frowned, fingers flying over her knot-cord. "Some of them want to be gentle. Some of them are so angry they've forgotten their own names. And one of them—" she stopped.

"One of them what?"

"One of them is closest to the surface. Right below us, maybe a mile down, maybe less. And he's not angry at all. He's afraid." She turned her ice-pale eyes up toward Orsen. "He's afraid of being found. He's afraid of being freed. And most of all, Orsen Vane, he's afraid of *you*."

The wind came down off the hills carrying the smell of snow that shouldn't exist. Below their feet, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the earth groaned again.

Orsen stood a long time without speaking. When she finally did, her voice was barely above the wind.

"What does he say? Exactly. Word for word."

Sefa listened. It took a while; the voice was deep, and tired, and it kept breaking off to listen itself — listening, Sefa realized with a chill, for footsteps.

"He says," she reported at last, "'If she comes, tell her I remember what she did. Tell her I forgive her anyway. And tell her—'" Sefa's breath caught, because the last part was not words at all but a feeling, poured straight up through a mile of stone, raw as an open vein: longing and terror braided into one unbearable cord. "Tell her he's glad she's coming. And he wishes to God she weren't."

Far to the south, so far no ordinary ear could catch it, Sefa heard hoofbeats. Many horses. Moving fast. Praying ugly.

Rell was coming.

And below their feet, patient as tide, the voice began again: *come and see, come and see, come and see.*
