## 6. What the River Remembers

The river appeared first as a smell — cold stone and old rot and something sweeter beneath it, like flowers drowned in wine.

Orsen felt it before she saw it, the way she'd once felt her god: a pressure behind the sternum, a low hum in the teeth. She reined her mule to a stop at the head of the column and stared down the long slope of gorse and broken terracing toward the water, and the water stared back.

It was too wide for a river. That was everyone's first thought, though no one said it aloud. It lay across the valley like spilled mercury, flat and silver-grey even under a churning sky, and it did not seem to move so much as breathe. The far shore was a rumor of hills. Between here and there, the water held its stillness with the patience of something that had learned to wait.

"That's not a river," said Doss, one of the drovers, spitting into the dirt. "That's a grave with delusions."

"It's the Vell," Tallow said. He had come up beside her mule without sound, which was his gift and his irritation both. He squinted at the water through fingers ringed with cheap tin bands. "Or what's left of it. Used to run fast and narrow through the middle of Corvane City, right past the Temple Quarter. When they drowned the capital, the river drowned with it. Spread out. Got fat on all that holy real estate." He smiled without humor. "Now it's less a river than a memory with a surface."

"The capital's a story," Orsen said. "A thing mothers tell children to keep them from wandering."

"Mothers tell true stories too." Tallow tapped his temple. "The Treaty didn't just dissolve the country on paper, Vane. They flooded it. Nine days of rain called down by men who knew how to ask, and the whole heart of the nation went under. Temples. Palaces. The Ministry's first seat. Everything that made Corvane *Corvane* is down there, under forty feet of standing water, and nobody fishes it because nothing that goes down comes back up."

Orsen looked at the water again. This time she let herself see what she'd been refusing to see since the ridge: shapes beneath the surface. Not fish-shapes. Architecture. A colonnade, pale and soft-focus, like something seen through frosted glass. A tower leaning at an angle no builder would choose. And — she blinked, and it was gone, or she made herself believe it was gone — a vast dark mass near the center, wider than any building had a right to be, sitting on the drowned ground like a sleeping animal.

Sefa had stopped singing.

Orsen turned. The blind girl stood three carts back with her face lifted, nostrils flared, her white eyes aimed at nothing and therefore at everything. Her hands hung open at her sides, palms forward, the way a person stands when listening with their whole skin.

"Sefa," Orsen said.

"They're quiet here," Sefa said. "Not silent. Quiet. Like people holding their breath in a room you're about to walk into." She tilted her head. "They know we're coming. They've known since the pass."

"You can't know that."

"I know it the way you know your own name." Sefa smiled faintly, and it was the smile that frightened Orsen most — serene, certain, utterly without fear. "You'll understand soon. The water remembers, and it likes to show off."

* * *

They crossed at dusk, because Tallow insisted dusk was when the ferryman came, and because arguing with Tallow about the ferryman was like arguing with the tide about schedules.

The ferryman was a woman built like a mooring post, grey-haired, grey-eyed, wearing oilskins older than Orsen. Her barge was broad and flat and scarred, and it sat so low in the water that the passengers' boots were wet within minutes of boarding. She took no coin. She never did, Tallow said; she took only answers.

"One question each," she rasped, poling them out from the reed-choked bank while the caravan's animals stamped and rolled their eyes on the bank above, refusing the water as one. "Answer honest or don't answer. The river knows a lie faster than I do."

The pilgrims went one by one. A farmer asked whether his orchards would bloom again. The woman asked if her dead husband waited for her somewhere. A boy asked how deep it was, and the ferryman said, "Deeper than it looks," which was somehow worse than any number.

Then it was Orsen's turn.

She hadn't planned a question. Questions were commitments, and she had spent five years committing to nothing but survival. But the ferryman's grey eyes settled on her and stayed, patient as the water, and Orsen heard herself say:

"What happened here?"

The pole stopped.

For a long moment the only sounds were water lapping the hull and the far-off complaint of a cart axle on the bank. Then the ferryman leaned on her pole and studied Orsen with an expression that was not unkind, which was somehow the cruelest expression available.

"Wrong question," she said. "But the river will take it."

She struck the water once, sharply, with the flat of the pole, and the world went white.

* * *

Not white. Silver. Everything silver, the way the underside of a wave looks from below, and Orsen was standing — standing where she should have been sitting — on dry cobbles beneath a sky the color of hammered tin, in a city so beautiful it hurt.

Corvane City. Alive. Whole. Towers of white stone and copper roofs gone green, banners snapping in a wind that smelled of salt and incense, crowds flowing through streets wide enough to hold their grief and their festivals at once. She knew it instantly, the way you know a face from a dream, and the knowing arrived with a name attached:

*Home.*

Except it wasn't hers. She was seeing it through someone else's memory, someone who had loved it, and the love was so large it crowded everything else out of the vision like light crowding out stars.

Then the rain began.

It did not fall so much as arrive — a wall of it, nine days compressed into nine breaths, and the city's beauty curdled into panic. Bells rang from every tower, wrong bells, alarm-bells, and the crowds stopped being crowds and became rivers themselves, pouring uphill, away from the water that rose in the streets like something summoned. Orsen watched a woman drop a basket of oranges and not stop for them. Watched a priest stand on temple steps screaming scripture at the sky until the water took him mid-word.

And through it all, seven figures walked against the current.

Orsen's breath stopped in her chest. She knew them. Not their faces — their faces were veiled in storm-light, shifting, more suggestion than form — but their *weight*. Seven presences moving through the drowning city like seven suns walking, each trailing power the way comets trail fire. Ossuin she knew by the cold iron feel of him, the god of oaths and edges, her god, her silent god, alive and terrible and *free*. Beside him moved six others: one like a green wind, one like a hearth-fire that wept, one whose footsteps bloomed flowers in floodwater, one made of ledger-lines and law, one that was mostly wings, and one that was only a voice, everywhere, saying something over and over that the vision refused to let her hear.

They were running. Gods, running. From what?

From the chains.

They came out of the rain like questions come out of a courtroom: men and women in grey, hundreds of them, bearing staves tipped with sigils Orsen recognized with a lurch — binding-sigils, outlawed now, the very charms she sold degraded copies of in Corvane's alleys. The Ministry of Silence, five years younger and five years fewer in numbers, marching on divinity itself.

And it should have been impossible. She watched it happen and it should have been impossible: mortal hands throwing lines of light around a god's throat. But the gods were weakened — she could see it now, see how each of them flickered like a candle in draft, how their steps dragged, how they turned to fight and found their own power answering the enemy's call instead of their own. The Ministry had not come to kill them. It had come having already poisoned the well.

*We asked them nicely first,* said a voice beside her, and Orsen turned.

The speaker was a woman in grey robes, rain streaming off her hood, and her face —

Her face was Orsen's face.

Younger. Unlined. Eyes bright with conviction instead of exhaustion. But the jaw, the scar over the left brow that Orsen had told a hundred customers was a fishing accident — it was all there, all hers, worn by a woman named someone else.

Vessa.

The vision-Vessa raised her hand, and in it was a stake of black iron longer than her arm, carved end to end with the binding-script, and it drank the light around it the way a wound drinks blood. Around her stood six others, six other blade-sworn, each carrying a stake, each chosen because a sworn blade's oath gave their hands authority over the god they served. The gods' own faith, turned into shackles. That was the genius of it. That was the obscenity of it.

"No," Orsen said aloud, in the silver nowhere, to no one. "No, that's not — I never —"

Vision-Vessa walked through floodwater to where Ossuin stood surrounded, his great veiled head bowed under lines of grey light, and Orsen screamed at her — at herself — to stop, and the vision did not pause, because visions never do, because memory is a country you cannot warn.

Ossuin turned his head. Through the veil, through five years of silence, through the impossible distance between then and now, he looked directly at her — at Orsen, the watcher, the ghost outside the memory — and his voice filled the drowned sky:

*You will understand someday, little blade. Forgive me for making sure of it.*

And Vessa drove the stake into the ground at his feet, and the world folded shut like a book.

* * *

Orsen came back to herself on her knees in the bilge water of the barge, someone's hands on her shoulders, Sefa's voice close and calm saying her name — her false name, her safe name — *Orsen, Orsen, come back.*

The crossing was half done. The far bank was a smudge of dark. The ferryman poled on as if nothing had happened, though her grey eyes flicked down once, briefly, with something that might have been pity.

"What did you see?" Sefa whispered. She alone had knelt beside her; the others kept their distance, uneasy, the way people get around a person the water has spoken to.

"Nothing," Orsen said. Her voice came out sanded raw. "An old story."

"Liar," Sefa said gently. "You saw yourself."

Orsen's whole body went still. Somewhere behind them, on the receding bank, the boy was asking his mother why the lady cried, and the mother was shushing him, and the river breathed beneath the hull, patient, vast, full of architecture and secrets and one small buried truth that had just grown teeth.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Orsen said.

Sefa reached out and found her hand with unerring accuracy, the way she found everything, and squeezed it. Her palm was warm. Her voice dropped to almost nothing, beneath the hearing of the others, meant for Orsen alone.

"When we reach the place where they're bound," the blind girl said, "you won't be able to lie anymore. Not to them. Not to yourself. So practice now, if you like. Practice all the way north." She smiled that serene, terrible smile. "But the water already knows your name. Both of them."

The barge slid on through the dark. Beneath the keel, forty feet down, in the drowned heart of a country that officially did not exist, something shifted in its sleep — and every soul aboard felt the hull tremble once, softly, like a hand knocking from the inside of a door.
