## 7. Ashes of the Congregation

Sefa heard the village before anyone else did.

Not the sound of it — the absence. For three days the road north had been full of voices for her: the low mutter of the river-god under the ford, the thin whistling of something old and patient in the mountains, the constant warm hum that lived in Orsen Vane's sleeve like a banked coal. The world had been loud since the gods began to wake, so loud some nights she pressed her palms against her ears and still heard them singing through the bone.

But here the noise simply stopped, as if someone had drawn a line across the land and said *no more*.

"Stop," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. "There's nothing ahead."

The caravan creaked to a halt on the frozen road — eleven wagons, forty-one souls, a hundred and six animals by Tallow's count, though he counted wrong when he was tired. Sefa stood in the bed of the lead wagon with her hands out like a woman testing rain. Cold air. Wood smoke, but old smoke, days old, the kind that had soaked into stone and would not leave until spring.

"There's a village," Master Halden said from the driver's bench. "Ashford Crossing. We're meant to water there."

"There's a village," Sefa agreed. "But it's quiet in the wrong way." She turned her face toward Orsen, who rode somewhere near the front with her hood up and her sleeve pulled down over the sigil she no longer bothered hiding from Sefa. "It's quiet like held breath."

She felt Orsen look at her. She had learned to feel it — the particular weight of that woman's attention, sharp as a needle through cloth.

"How far?" Orsen asked.

"Half a mile. Maybe less."

A long pause. Then Orsen's voice, flat and practical: "Tallow. Take three men and walk ahead. Nobody else moves until they wave us in."

Sefa heard the mercenary dismount, heard the soft crunch of boots on frost, and then she climbed down too, because staying in the wagon felt like hiding, and she had promised herself on the pilgrim road that she would not hide anymore. Her stick found the ruts of the road. Someone — she thought it was Marta, the tanner's wife who had taken to walking beside her since the waystation — put a hand briefly on her shoulder and then took it away, as if touching her required permission now.

They waited. The wind moved through bare birches with a sound like whispering, and beneath the whispering Sefa caught it again: that thread of song from the deep places, the one that had been growing louder every day, the one that made her teeth ache. But here it sounded different. Here it sounded strained. Like a voice singing through clenched teeth.

*They're hurting,* she thought, and didn't know how she knew, only that she did. *Whatever's under the ground, they're hurting worse here than anywhere.*

Tallow's whistle came back — two short notes, the all-clear, though his voice carried oddly behind it, tight and wrong. "Come on, then! Bring water skins!"

They brought the wagons in at a walk.

Sefa smelled it before they reached the square. Not smoke this time. Blood — old blood, blood gone sweet and rotten in the cold, the smell of a slaughterhouse left unattended. She stopped at the edge of what must have been the market square and stood very still while the caravan rolled past her into silence, and she listened with everything she had.

No dogs. No chickens. No children. A village should have been full of small sounds even in winter — axes, coughing, a baby crying somewhere. Ashford Crossing had none. It had wind, and the groan of a loose shutter, and underneath everything, faint as a pulse, the screaming-song of the buried gods.

"Sefa." Marta's hand again, gripping hard this time. "You shouldn't—"

"What happened here?"

Marta didn't answer. Sefa heard her turn away and be sick against a wall.

Someone led Sefa forward — she thought it was Orsen, because the hand on her arm was strong and impersonal and the sigil-sound flared warm when they touched, the god's ember answering its sworn blade even now. Orsen walked her slowly through the square, and narrated in a voice stripped of everything, a surveyor's voice:

"Eleven bodies in the square. Laid out in rows. Not killed where they fell — arranged. All of them face-down, hands bound behind them with wire. There are scorch marks on the stones. Ministry pattern — I've seen it before, in the border wars. They make the whole congregation kneel first." A pause. "There's a post in the center of the square. Charred. Someone was burned at it. Recently — two days, maybe three."

"The church?" Sefa asked.

Orsen was quiet a moment. "What's left of it is a shell. The doors were broken inward. There are more bodies inside. Dozens." Another pause, longer. "You don't need to hear the rest."

"Yes I do," Sefa said. "That's why I'm here. That's why any of us are here. Tell me the rest."

Orsen told her the rest. The bodies in the church had been arranged kneeling at the pews, as if forced to pray at gunpoint and then executed mid-prayer. The altar had been defaced — not smashed, which would have been rage, but carefully chiseled, every holy symbol planed flat, which was policy. On the altar's face, in letters cut deep enough to survive weather, someone had carved the Ministry's motto: *THE SILENCE IS MERCY.*

And there was a survivor.

* * *

He was in the bell tower, or what remained of him was, curled in the corner beneath the bell rope like a man hiding from his own shadow. He screamed when they found him — a high, tearing scream that went on and on until Orsen put a hand on his chest and spoke to him in a low, steady voice, the voice Sefa imagined she used on frightened horses, and gradually the screaming wound down into sobbing, and the sobbing into a kind of terrible quiet.

His name was Brother Anselm. He was maybe sixty. Most of his hair had been burned away on one side, and his hands were wrapped in filthy bandages, and when Sefa knelt beside him and took one of those ruined hands gently in hers, he flinched and then clung, grip astonishingly strong, like a drowning man.

"You're blind," he rasped. "Child, you're blind. You shouldn't see this. Blessed are you. Blessed are you, you shouldn't see this."

"I hear it instead," Sefa said. "Tell me what happened."

"They came at night." His voice was a ruin, scraped raw. "Rell's riders. The Inquisitor himself — I saw him, I saw his gray cloak, I saw the silver eye. They'd been lists, you understand? Lists of names. Who attended, who gave alms, who sang the old hymns. They read the names out in the square and made the whole village kneel and watch." His breath hitched. "They weren't angry. That's the worst of it, child. They weren't angry at all. It was bookkeeping. The Inquisitor walked the rows like a man inspecting a harvest."

Sefa's stomach turned over. She had grown up on stories of the purges — everyone had — but stories had edges, stories ended, stories let you sleep. This man's voice had no edges. It just kept going.

"They took the priest to the post. Father Colm. He wouldn't recant. They gave him three chances to recant." Anselm began to shake. "I was in the tower. I rang the alarm bell when they came, and then I couldn't stop ringing it, and they — they set the fire below and the smoke came up and I rang until my hands burned and the rope burned and then I fell, and I lay here, and I heard everything."

"I know," Sefa whispered. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

"And the worst part—" His grip crushed her fingers. "The worst part, child, the part they'll never write in their reports. At the end, when Father Colm was burning — the ground *answered*. Underneath us. The whole valley shook, and there was a sound, a sound like—" He made a noise that wasn't language. "Like something enormous turning over in its sleep. And the Inquisitor looked at the ground, and do you know what he did? He smiled. He smiled and he wrote something in his book, and he said, 'Confirmed,' and they rode out north. North. Toward the pass. Toward wherever you're all going."

Sefa felt the cold go through her like a blade sliding between ribs.

Because she knew what the ground had answered. She had been hearing it for weeks, growing stronger every day, and here in Ashford Crossing it was loudest of all — seven voices, or the ghosts of seven voices, screaming up through miles of stone and drowned dark, screaming because they felt their worshippers die. Every soul in that square, every kneeling body in that church — each death had struck the prison like a hammer, and the gods had *felt* it, and cried out, and could do nothing, nothing, nothing.

"They're screaming," she said aloud, without meaning to. "Can't you hear it? They're screaming underground. They felt every one of them die and they couldn't move, they couldn't help, they're chained in the dark under the drowned city and they're *screaming*—"

"Sefa." Orsen's hand on her shoulder, firm. "Breathe."

"They're in pain!" She was crying now, hot and fast, and she didn't care. "Everyone thinks the Silence is peace, everyone thinks the gods abandoned us, but they didn't abandon us, they've been *screaming* for five years and nobody could hear—"

Anselm was nodding, nodding, weeping. "Yes. Yes. That's it exactly. That's what I've been trying to say. They're screaming. God forgive us, they're screaming, and the Ministry calls it mercy—"

Behind them, in the square, the caravan had gone silent. Forty-one people listening to a blind girl and a burned priest say aloud the thing that had been growing in all their hearts since the miracles started: that the world's long quiet had never been peace. It had been a hand over a mouth.

* * *

They buried what they could before dark. There were too many for proper graves; they dug one long trench outside the village and laid the dead of the square in it, and said what words could be said, and Sefa stood at the head of the trench with snow beginning to fall and did the only thing she knew how to do.

She preached.

She hadn't planned to. The words simply rose in her, the way the gods' voices rose, and she opened her mouth and let them out: about a silence that was not peace, about prisoners who loved the ones who had forgotten them, about a country built on top of a scream and taught to call the scream quiet. She talked about the boy in Corvane whose fever broke. About the luck-charms working again. About how the gods were waking, and waking was agony after five years of chains, and how their pain was not a punishment but a birth-cry.

"We are not orphans," she heard herself say, her voice carrying strange and clear across the snow. "We are the children of prisoners. And the question of our lives is whether we will be the ones who keep the prison — or the ones who open the door."

When she finished, the silence afterward was different from the village's silence. Warmer. Forty-one people breathing together in falling snow, and more than half of them, she could feel it, had decided something in that moment. She heard Marta whisper an old hymn under her breath — one of the outlawed ones, the ones with names in them. She heard Halden join in on the second line, badly.

Only Orsen stood apart, arms crossed, saying nothing, and when Sefa reached toward her with her hearing she found that familiar banked-coal warmth — but around it, for the first time, something else. Something that felt, in the language of feelings rather than words, like a door held shut from the inside.

*You know more than you're telling me,* Sefa thought toward her. *You've known since the river. Since you saw whatever the river showed you. One day soon you'll have to tell me, Orsen Vane. Because they're calling your name down there. I hear it every night. And I don't think they're going to stop.*

* * *

The riders came an hour after full dark.

Sefa heard them first — always her, always first now — hooves on frozen road, many horses, moving at a canter with the disciplined spacing of soldiers. Eight. No, eleven. Behind them, wagon wheels: heavy ones, iron-rimmed.

"Riders," she said, standing up in the inn where they'd sheltered. "From the south. Eleven horses and a supply wagon. Moving fast."

The room erupted. Tallow was already moving, kicking the fire apart, hissing orders. Orsen appeared at Sefa's side as if summoned, one hand closing around her arm.

"How sure are you?"

"Certain. They'll be here in minutes."

"Ministry?" Tallow demanded.

"Who else rides eleven abreast in the dark?" Orsen said. "Out the back. Now. Leave the wagons — take what you can carry. Sefa, you're with me. Marta, get Anselm — he comes or he dies here, those are his choices."

They poured out the back of the inn into the snow, forty-one people becoming shadows among shadows, and behind them Sefa heard the riders enter the square at a trot and then halt, and a voice carry across the empty village — a voice she had never heard before and knew instantly, the way you know a shape from a story, cold and clear and unhurried, a voice that read names off lists and smiled at the ground.

"Inquisitor Rell," Orsen breathed beside her. Of course Orsen knew the voice too. Everyone did. "Of course it is. He let me go in Corvane because he wanted me to lead him somewhere. And here I am, leading him."

The voice in the square called out, pitched to carry, addressed to the darkness and the snow and the fleeing shapes beyond the lamplight:

"Pilgrims! You have witnessed a sanctioned purification. By decree of the Ministry of Silence, witnesses are remanded for testimony. Come forward and be processed, and you will be treated fairly." A pause, and then, softer, almost gentle, almost kind, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet: "Orsen Vane. I know you can hear me. I know the girl can hear me — yes, blind one, I know what you are. Walk away from her, Vane. Give me the sigil, and every soul in that caravan walks free. Keep it, and I burn them one congregation at a time, starting with whoever is slowest. You have until dawn. I'll be at the church."

Hooves wheeled. The riders withdrew to the square, and the snow fell, and the caravan stood in the treeline in the freezing dark, forty-one hearts hammering, and Sefa reached out with her hearing and found the thing she had been dreading: the deep song beneath the village had changed. The screaming had stopped.

In its place was something worse.

Listening. Seven vast attentions, pressed up against the underside of the world, waiting to hear what the small creatures in the snow would choose.

Beside her, Orsen Vane exhaled a long plume of white into the dark, and Sefa heard her say, quietly, to no one, to herself, to the god in her sleeve:

"Dawn, then."

Then her hand found Sefa's shoulder. "Get your coat, Sefa. Whatever happens at sunrise — I need you to promise me you won't give yourself up for me. Promise me first. Then we'll argue."

But Sefa had already heard the future in the god-song under the earth, and it did not sound like surrender, and it did not sound like sacrifice.

It sounded like a door, very far below, beginning to open.
