## 8. Bargain at the Waystation

The snow came down like a verdict.

Orsen felt it before she saw it — a change in the pressure behind her eyes, the sigil stirring under her sleeve like a cat that smells a storm. By the time the caravan reached the waystation, a squat stone box wedged into the shoulder of the Serpent's Tooth pass, the flakes were falling fat and deliberate, the kind of snow that intended to stay.

"Inside," Tallow said, and there was no argument in him left to spare. He'd been coughing since the burned village, and the cough had a rattle in it now that Orsen didn't like the sound of at all.

The waystation had been a toll house once, back when the pass had traffic and the country had a future. Now it was a shell: a great room with a dead hearth, a stable half-collapsed against the windward wall, and a keeper's loft that smelled of old smoke and older rats. The pilgrims crowded in with their bundles and their frostbitten faces, and Orsen counted them the way she counted everything now — as lives she was responsible for, and losing.

Eleven pilgrims. Sefa, sitting quietly by the window with her face turned toward the falling snow as if she were listening to it. Two of Tallow's drovers. The widow Maret and her sister. The boy Pellen, fourteen and stubborn, who had refused to turn back three days ago and hadn't spoken since they passed the ashes of Ashenford.

And Tallow, who was dying, though he'd deny it to his last rattling breath.

Orsen got the hearth lit with a charm she should not have used — a fire-knot, outlawed twice over, worth more than everything else in her pack combined. The wood caught with a sound like a sigh, and the pilgrims converged on the warmth with the slow, wordless gravity of the freezing.

That was when the door opened.

Not the front door. The stable door, the one facing the drifts, and the woman who came through it moved like weather — fast, silent, and inevitable. Snow in her grey hair, a Ministry longcoat with the insignia cut away but the tailoring unmistakable, and a crossbow held with the easy, terrible competence of someone who had never once needed a second bolt.

"Nobody reach for anything," the woman said. "I'm alone, I'm cold, and I'm better armed than all of you put together. Let's be civilized."

Orsen's hand was already inside her coat. The woman's eyes — pale, patient, the eyes of someone who read people the way clerks read ledgers — flicked to her and stayed.

"You're the charm-seller." Not a question. "Orsen Vane. The miracle of Corvane. The woman every broadsheet from here to the coast is calling either a saint or a plague." She smiled without warmth. "I've been following your legend north for two weeks. Imagine my delight at finding you in my waystation."

*Your* waystation. Orsen filed that away. "And you are?"

"Yara Sen." She said it plainly, the way you'd hand over a weapon for inspection. "Formerly First Auditor of the Ministry of Silence, Eastern Circuit. Currently nothing at all." She set the crossbow on a table, deliberately, and held out empty hands. "Currently, I'd like to trade."

* * *

They talked in the keeper's loft, away from the pilgrims, while the snow thickened against the shutters and the hearth below crackled its small defiance.

"You cut the insignia off," Orsen said. "But you kept the coat. That's not a defector's habit. That's a woman who wants to be recognized."

"I want to be *survivable*," Yara said. "There's a difference. A coat like this opens doors — and closes others. Right now, out there—" she nodded toward the window, toward the white silence swallowing the pass, "—the only thing more dangerous than a Ministry coat is a Ministry deserter without one. Rell's riders shoot us on sight. They shoot *everyone* on sight now. You saw Ashenford."

Orsen had seen Ashenford. She would see Ashenford every time she closed her eyes for the rest of her life, however long the Ministry allowed that to be.

"You said you wanted to trade," she said. "Trade what?"

Yara sat down on the keeper's stool, unhurried, and folded her hands. Everything about her was unhurried. It was the unhurried-ness of a knife, Orsen thought — a knife is never in a hurry, because the hand that holds it does all the rushing.

"Passage," Yara said. "I know this pass. I know the waystations, the caches, the old smuggler cuts that Tallow's map doesn't show because Tallow's map is fifteen years out of date and the Serpent's Tooth has moved twice since. I can take your caravan through the first snows to the high shelter, and from there to the Door of Ash, ahead of every rider Rell has. I can do it without losing a single one of your pilgrims." She paused. "In exchange, I want the sigil."

The word landed in the room like a dropped coin.

"No," Orsen said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to. No."

Yara's smile returned, thinner. "You're not a fool, so let me treat you like one anyway. Do you know what the Ministry calls people like you? *Vessels.* Containers. The gods are coming back, Vane, and they come back *into* people — into marks, into sigils, into scars left by old oaths. Rell has been executing vessels for a month. Quietly, at first. Now in batches. He burned a family of five in Vessic Row because the youngest drew a spiral in the frost that matched one of the Seven." Her voice didn't rise. That was the horror of it; it stayed level as a surveyor's line. "I audited the orders. I signed the summaries. For eleven years I made the numbers tidy, and then I read one number too many, and I left. So understand me: I am not your enemy. I am the only person on this mountain who is not your enemy."

"Then ask for something else."

"I'm asking for the only thing I want." Yara leaned forward. "The sigil, Vane. Cut out or given — the giving is cleaner, and I'm told it can be done with a knife and an hour of screaming, or with a god's permission and a moment of quiet. Your choice of methods. In exchange: your people live, you reach the Door, and I disappear into the world with the one object in this country that Rell cannot afford to lose."

Orsen stood very still. Under her sleeve, the sigil burned — not painfully, not yet, but with a low attention, the way it had been burning for weeks now, as if it were *listening*.

"Why?" she asked. "What do you want with it? You said you left the Ministry. You said the numbers made you sick. So why do you want the thing they're killing children for?"

For the first time, Yara's composure moved. Not broke — *moved*, like ice shifting on a river.

"Because I prayed," she said. "Five years ago, when the gods went silent, I prayed like everyone else. And nothing answered. And I did what the Ministry trained me to do — I stopped believing the silence was a mystery and started treating it as a fact. Facts can be managed. I managed the fact of a godless world for five years, Vane. I was *good* at it." She looked at her own hands. "And then the prayers started being answered, and I discovered that the fact I'd been managing was a lie, and the lie had been wearing my whole life like a coat. I want to know what's true. That's all. That's the whole of it. The sigil is the closest thing in this country to a true thing, and I want to hold it."

"You want to *hold* it," Orsen repeated. "Not destroy it. Not free anyone with it. Hold it."

"Knowledge first," Yara said. "Mercy after. I've learned the order matters."

* * *

Orsen went down to the great room and sat by the fire and did not bargain with anyone for a long while.

Sefa found her there. The blind girl navigated the crowded room without touching anyone, which Orsen had stopped finding strange and started finding quietly terrifying, and settled beside her with the unerring instinct of the young for the person least interested in company.

"She asked you for the sigil," Sefa said.

Orsen didn't bother asking how she knew. "You heard?"

"I hear everything now. It's getting worse." Sefa's face was turned to the fire, and the light moved on it like water. "What did you tell her?"

"Nothing yet."

"You'll tell her no."

"You sound very sure."

"You *are* very sure." Sefa reached out and touched Orsen's sleeve, right over the sigil, and Orsen flinched — and then didn't. The girl's fingers were warm as coals. "It's not yours to give, you know. That's the thing nobody understands. Not Rell, not her, not the ones who worship you. It was never a *gift*. Gifts can be handed over. It's a—" she searched, frowning, listening to something Orsen couldn't hear, "—a *debt*. A debt that got written on you. You can't pay a debt by handing the paper to someone else."

"Your god has opinions about my finances," Orsen said dryly.

"He's not my god." Sefa said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "None of them are. They're just *loud*. And one of them keeps saying your name. The real one." Her clouded eyes found Orsen's face with terrible accuracy. "The one you buried."

"Stop."

"I'm not doing it on purpose."

"I said *stop*." Orsen's voice came out harder than she intended, and the fire guttered, and every pilgrim in the room looked over, and Pellen's hand went to the knife at his belt — not to use it, she realized, but the way you touch a charm. For luck. Against her.

Sefa withdrew her hand. "I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and left Orsen alone with the fire and the debt written on her arm.

* * *

She climbed back to the loft near midnight, and Yara was awake, of course, sitting by the little lamp with her coat off and a map spread across her knees — hand-drawn, dense, annotated in a clerk's immaculate script. Orsen looked at it and understood, with a lurch, that it was better than Tallow's. Better than any map she'd ever seen of the pass. This woman had not defected on impulse. She had defected the way she did everything: thoroughly, and in advance.

"You've decided," Yara said, not looking up.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped you'd be stupid. Stupid would have been easier." Yara rolled the map and set it aside. "Sit down, Vane. Humor me. Tell me why. Not the pilgrims, not the morality — I've read the morality, I helped write the justifications for burning it. Tell me *why you won't give it up*. Because I've watched you for two weeks, and here's what I see: a woman who hates the thing on her arm. It hurts you. It marks you. It's the reason Rell wants you and the reason the faithful follow you and the reason you can't buy bread in half the towns in this country without someone falling to their knees or reaching for a knife. If I cut it off you tonight, you'd be free. So why?"

Orsen sat down. The lamp between them made a small, warm circle, and outside the snow pressed against the world like a thumb on a wound.

"Because of what it cost to put it there," she said.

"Tell me."

"You wouldn't—"

"I signed the summaries," Yara said. "Try me."

So Orsen told her — not everything, not the stake, not the drowned capital, not the name she'd buried with her old life, but the shape of it. That the silence five years ago had not been an accident. That the gods had not *left*. That someone had made them leave, had bound them, had driven something deep into the heart of the world to keep them there, and that the person who did it had been marked for the work — sworn, branded, forged into a blade and used as one. That the sigil was not a gift from a god. It was a tool left in her by the people who had done the binding, a key that had outlived its lock's purpose, and it had slept for five years the way a knife sleeps in a wound.

"And now it's waking up," Yara said quietly. "Because they're waking up."

"Because they're waking up." Orsen turned her sleeve back. In the lamplight the sigil showed its true self for once — not the faded grey knot the charm-buyers saw, but a living thing, seven-pointed, turning slowly beneath her skin like a star seen through deep water. "You want to hold this. Understand what you'd be holding. It's not power. Power is what it *gives* — I could burn your crossbow to slag from across this room, and I'd hate myself by morning. What it *is* is a leash. Whoever holds it holds the hand that made the prison. That's why Rell wants vessels executed and sigils *collected* — I've figured that much out, watching him work. He doesn't want the gods dead. He wants them *kept*. And this—" she covered the sigil with her sleeve, and the cold rushed back into the room as if it had been waiting, "—this is the keeping."

Yara was silent for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a pilgrim coughed, and Orsen thought of Tallow's rattle, and made herself keep talking.

"So no. Not because it's precious. Not because I'm faithful — I'm not, I haven't prayed honestly since before you signed your first summary. I won't give it to you because the price of this thing was already paid once, by people who thought they were saving the world, and the interest is coming due now, and I am *not* going to hand the bill to another stranger and walk away clean. That's how this all started. Someone decided a burden was transferable." She met Yara's pale eyes. "I'm done being the someone."

Yara looked at her for a long time. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, genuine, exhausted sound, the first honest thing Orsen had heard from her.

"Auditors," she said. "We spend our whole lives learning that everything has a price, and then we meet the one person who quotes us one we can't pay." She stood, took up the map, and held it out. "Take it. No charge. Consider it an apology for the offer."

"And your passage?"

"I'll walk with you as far as the high shelter. After that—" she shrugged, "—I'm a defector with a map and no destination. I'll decide at the top." She paused at the loft ladder, one hand on the rail, and did not look back. "For what it's worth, Vane — Rell isn't behind you anymore. He's ahead of you. He left Corvane nine days ago with two hundred riders, and he isn't hunting the pass. He's *holding* it. Whatever's at the Door of Ash, he means to be there when you arrive."

She went down into the dark, and Orsen sat alone with the lamp and the map and the slow-turning star under her skin.

Outside, the snow kept falling, soft as ash, and far beneath the mountain — she was almost sure of it, almost — something shifted in its sleep, and the sigil flared once, bright as a struck match, and went quiet.

The silence it left behind was worse than the burning.

It felt like holding its breath.
