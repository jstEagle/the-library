## 9. The God Beneath the Snow

The mountain spoke first.

Orsen heard it as a sound she had no name for — a deep, grinding exhalation beneath the snowpack, like something vast turning over in its sleep. She stopped mid-stride on the switchback above the waystation, and the world went very quiet, the particular quiet that follows a scream.

"Avalanche," she said. "Run."

They ran. Sefa's hand was in hers — when had that happened? — and behind them Yara shouted something about the ridge line, about the shelf of rock jutting from the eastern face, but the words were torn away by the roar that came down the mountain like the wrath of an old and patient animal. Orsen looked back once. The slope above them was moving. Not sliding — *moving*, a whole white country deciding to relocate itself, cornices folding, trees vanishing whole, the air filling with a mist of shattered crystal that caught the grey light and made it holy.

The sigil on her arm burned.

Not the warm flicker of answered prayer, not the slow pulse of these strange weeks since the boy lived. This was fire, this was a brand pressed to her skin by a hand she hadn't felt in five years, and through the pain she heard — or didn't hear, or remembered hearing — a single syllable of a language that predated language: *down*.

Then the snow took them.

It did not feel like drowning. It felt like being erased. The world compressed to white pressure on every side, to cold that stopped being temperature and became a fact of existence, and Orsen tumbled in the dark under the weight of the mountain with Sefa's fingers ripped from hers, and the last thing she thought, with a clarity that would have shamed her awake, was: *at least it isn't Rell.*

Then even that thought froze, and she fell further than falling goes.

* * *

She woke standing in a field of snow that stretched to no horizon.

There was no sky. There was light without source, the flat pearl glow of overcast reflected upward from below, so that the snow seemed lit from within, and there were no shadows anywhere, and no wind, and no sound at all — not the creak of her boots, not her own breath. She breathed anyway. Her lungs worked. Her body was hers, dressed in her road-worn coat, the sigil bare on her forearm because in dreams sleeves have no loyalty.

She knew this place. Not this exact place — she had never stood here — but this kind of place. The inside of a silence. She had spent five years living in its outer suburbs.

"Ossuin," she said.

The name left her mouth and did not echo, because there was nothing for it to come back from. She said it again, louder, and the snow drank the sound, and she understood that she could stand here shouting until her dream-body failed and the only thing that would hear her was herself.

So she walked.

The snowfield was featureless and yet not empty. As she walked, she began to notice marks in the whiteness — faint, almost subliminal, like impressions left by things removed. A circle where something round had lain a long time. A trough like the bed of a river that had never existed. And once, unmistakably, a handprint, vast, each finger longer than she was tall, pressed into the snow and half-filled with fresh fall, as if whatever made it had been lying here and risen recently, or had fallen here and not yet managed to rise.

Her heart did something complicated.

"You're dead," she said to the white silence. "I watched you go quiet. I carried your silence for five years like a stone in my chest. You don't get to be a handprint."

The snowfield disagreed by continuing to exist.

She walked until walking lost meaning — time moved strangely here, or didn't move, and her legs neither tired nor rested — and then the ground began to slope downward, gently at first, then more steeply, and the snow grew thinner, patchy, revealing beneath it not earth but something dark and smooth and curved, like the shoulder of an enormous buried animal. She knelt and brushed the snow away with her palm. The surface beneath was warm. That was the worst part. In a dream of absolute cold, the thing under the snow was warm as a sleeping body, and it rose and fell, very slowly, very faintly, with a rhythm she recognized because she had once slept with her ear against a living chest and listened to exactly this.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you're alive."

"I am not alive." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the snow and the lightless sky and the warm curve beneath her knees, and it was a voice she knew better than her mother's, better than her own — dry and vast and tired, a voice like a door opening in a house long abandoned. "Alive is a word for things that eat and grow and end. I do none of those things. I persist. There is a difference, little blade, and the difference is the whole of my suffering."

Orsen stayed kneeling. Five years of prayers unanswered, five years of talking to a god who had become a habit rather than a presence, five years of telling herself he was gone, dissolved, spent, dead — and now his voice filled the silence of her own skull and she found she could not look up.

"You're starving," she said.

"Yes."

"How long?"

"What is time, to me?" A sound that might have been laughter, if laughter could rust. "Long enough that I have forgotten the taste of prayer. Do you know what we ate, before? Worship is not flattery, Vessa. It is food. It is warmth. It is the difference between a fire and a picture of a fire. Your kind gave us ourselves. Without it we are shapes in the dark, remembering that we were once shapes in the light."

She looked up at last. Nothing looked back — no face, no form, only the snow and the sourceless glow — and yet she felt seen, wholly, in the way only he had ever seen her, down to the parts she kept locked even from herself.

"The Ministry says the gods are returning," she said. "Prayers answered all across the north. Miracles. Healing. I've—" She stopped. "I've performed one. My sigil burned and a dying boy lived."

Something moved beneath the snow. The whole field shifted, a slow swell, like the flank of a whale turning in deep water, and for one vertiginous moment Orsen felt how thin the crust was, how much weight hung below her, how little separated her kneeling body from the dark.

"That is not return," Ossuin said. "That is leakage. We are bound beneath the drowned heart of this country, seven of us, staked and chained in the dark, and the binding was made to hold forever — but nothing holds forever, little blade, not even the work of the faithful. The stake loosens. The seals sweat. What leaks out leaks through any vessel open enough to receive it. You have been leaking for weeks."

"And the others? The ones the Ministry calls vessels?"

"Cracks in the prison wall. Candles lit by smoke escaping a burning house." The voice roughened. "And the Ministry kills them. I know. I can feel each one go out, like a tooth pulled. They think they are silencing us. They are only sealing the cracks faster, driving the pressure deeper, making the final breaking worse."

Orsen's hands had gone cold despite the dream's bloodless warmth. "Breaking."

"There will be a breaking. Pressure does not negotiate. The only question ever was whether it breaks *through* the binding or *along* it — whether we rise as ourselves or come apart into a thousand mindless fragments, a storm of unshaped divinity that will scour this land to bedrock." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone intimate, gone gentle in the way that frightened her more than anger ever had. "Which brings us to why you are here, and why I have broken every law of my own imprisonment to speak to you."

"You sent the avalanche."

"I sent a dream. The mountain merely cooperated." A pause. "You are the maker of my binding, Vessa. The stake answers to your hand. When the breaking comes — soon, sooner than any of us wish — you will be pulled toward it whether you will it or not. Every faction in this dying country will drag you by the sigil toward the drowned heart. The blind girl hears us calling you already; you think that is accident? So I ask you now, while you still have the freedom to choose—"

"Don't." She was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. "Don't ask me anything. Don't you dare. Five years, Ossuin. Five years I thought I'd killed you, and I grieved you, I built a whole life on top of your grave, and now you're *alive*, you've been alive the whole time, down here in the dark, starving—"

"Yes."

"—and you want me to leave you there."

The silence that followed was the truest thing in the dream. Snow drifted down somewhere far away, though there was no sky for it to fall from.

"Yes," Ossuin said.

* * *

She raged at him. She would remember afterward that she raged — pacing the endless snowfield, shouting into the sourceless light, demanding reasons, hurling five years of grief at him like stones at a locked door. He bore it the way mountains bear weather. And when she finally wore herself out, when she stood panting in the bloodless glow with tears freezing on her face that were not real tears because none of this was real except that all of it was, he spoke again, softly, and told her.

"Do you remember the last night before the binding?" he said. "Do you remember what I told you, when you asked me if it would hurt?"

"You said gods don't hurt. You said pain was a mortal invention."

"I lied. I have lied to you twice in all our years together, and both lies were mercies, and I regret them both." The warm shape beneath the snow seemed to shift closer, or perhaps the dream simply bent around his attention. "You asked me tonight — you demanded — why you should leave me bound. Here is why. Hear all of it before you hate me.

"When we are freed, we will be mad. Starvation does that, even to us. Five years of darkness and silence and the slow chewing of our own substance — we will wake ravening, little blade, all seven of us, and the first thing a starving god reaches for is the nearest warmth. And what is nearest? What has been bound into us deepest, woven through the stakes and chains and seals like a thread through cloth?" He let her arrive at it. He made her arrive at it.

"The makers," she whispered. "The ones who bound you."

"You. You above all. The stake knows your hand. When it comes free, the first mouth of the first freed god will close around the one who held it, and I cannot stop it, and worse — I do not trust myself to *want* to stop it. That is what starvation means. That is what I have become down here in the warm dark: something that loves you and would eat you, and cannot always tell the difference between the two."

Orsen wrapped her arms around herself. The sigil burned cold now, a ring of ice-fire on her arm.

"So don't free me," Ossuin said. "Whatever they tell you. Whatever the blind girl hears in our voices — and understand, Vessa, that what she hears is true, we *are* calling, we call because we are mad and starving and we cannot help it, the call is not wisdom, it is hunger wearing the mask of wisdom — whatever the defector offers, whatever the inquisitor threatens, whatever rises up in you when you stand before the stake and feel it answer your hand like a key answering its lock — *don't*. Let the prison hold. Let us starve. Let the country break along the seams instead of through them, let the fragments scatter, let mortals keep their small world and their small lives. It is the only mercy left to give, and you are the only one who can give it, because you are the only one whose mercy the binding will accept."

"And if I refuse?" Her voice came out small. "If I pull the stake anyway?"

"Then I will try to die before I reach you," he said simply. "I have been trying for five years. Gods are very hard to kill. It is the cruelest thing about us."

* * *

The dream began to end the way ice ends — not all at once but in slow concessions, cracks spreading through the white field, threads of other sensation bleeding in: the ache of cold in her real body, the muffled thunder of the settled avalanche, a weight on her chest that was snow and not snow. Ossuin's voice thinned with the light.

"One more thing," he said, hurried now, a man speaking quickly before a door closes. "There is another below. Younger than I. Louder. She does not love you, little blade, and she has learned to make her hunger sing like hope. When the blind girl speaks with our voices, listen for which god is speaking. Promise me."

"I promise nothing," Orsen said. "You taught me that. You said oaths were chains mortals put on themselves."

A rustle that might have been laughter, might have been grief. "Then remember instead. Remember that I begged. Whatever you decide at the stake, whatever you become — remember that the monster in the dark loved you enough to ask you to leave him there."

The snowfield folded. The light went out like a lamp carried away down a long corridor, and the last thing she heard, fading, worn smooth as a river stone, was her old name spoken the way no one had spoken it in five years — not as an accusation, not as a weapon, but the way you say the name of someone you are leaving:

"*Vessa.*"

* * *

She woke to blue dark and a crushing weight, and for three full seconds she did not know where the dream ended, because the cold was the same cold and the silence was the same silence.

Then she heard breathing. Small, quick, human, close — and a voice, hoarse and calm, reciting something rhythmic in the dark, and she realized the weight on her chest was snow and the reason she wasn't dead was a hollow, a pocket in the avalanche's throat, and the reason the pocket hadn't collapsed was that someone had dug to her.

"Sefa," Orsen rasped.

The blind girl's face appeared above her, snow-crusted, chapped lips split and bleeding, eyes closed as always and turned toward Orsen with terrible accuracy. Behind her, faint, came the scrape of digging — Yara, and one of the escorts, and another voice, and another. They had survived. Some of them. The pocket was crowded and cold and alive.

"You were under longest," Sefa said. "Four hours. I dug the whole time. I couldn't stop — I could hear you underneath, and I could hear—" She faltered, and her face changed, and Orsen saw fear move through the girl like a fish under ice. "I could hear the others too. Under the mountain. The ones who didn't make it. And something else, Orsen. Something else under everything, all the way down, so deep I'd never reached it before. Tonight the avalanche cracked the mountain like a bell, and now I can hear it everywhere. Singing."

Orsen lay still in the dark pocket, her frozen breath rising past Sefa's cheek, and thought: *she doesn't know which god is singing.*

"It's beautiful," Sefa whispered. "It's saying we should hurry."

Above them, muffled by ten feet of snow, the mountain groaned and settled, and somewhere far below the drowned heart of the country, something turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to pull its hand free.

Orsen closed her eyes and made herself remember: *he begged. Remember that he begged.* But already the memory was slipping sideways, softening at the edges, and the singing was very beautiful, and the snow above them was beginning, gently, to thaw.
