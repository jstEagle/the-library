## 10. Sefa's Sermon

The snow came down like ash that had forgotten how to burn.

Sefa heard it first — the particular hush of flakes settling on wool and hide and frozen stone, a sound like the world holding its breath. She had learned to hear snow before she could hear anything else in this country of white silence; it was the sound of everything being slowly covered, gently erased. The Ministry had a word for what they did to villages. Sefa thought the snow did the same thing, only without the fire.

She sat near the waystation's great hearth, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of wet dog and woodsmoke, listening to the room.

There were forty-one people in the long hall. She knew them by their breathing: the shallow rattle of the old ferryman who'd come up from the river with frostbitten fingers; the steady, stubborn rhythm of Marta and her two daughters, huddled so close they sounded like one large animal; the nervous cough of the boy Pell, fourteen maybe, who kept touching the charm at his throat as if checking whether his luck was still there. And scattered among them, the six escorts Tallow had hired back at the pass — Brenner, the twins Osk and Ilse, old Havel with his bad knee, the quiet one called Dree, and Coram, who laughed too easily and slept with his knife unsheathed.

And Orsen Vane, three bodies away, whose breath made no sound at all when she wanted it not to. Sefa had never met anyone who could breathe quietly on purpose. It frightened her a little, and comforted her more.

The gods were loud tonight.

They had been louder every night since the pass, since the avalanche, since Orsen came out of the white silence with frost in her lashes and something changed behind her eyes that she would not speak of. The gods' voices used to be distant — bells rung underwater, hymns sung through walls. Now they pressed close, seven vast attentions leaning against the skin of the world, and Sefa could feel each one like a hand laid flat against her back.

One of them was screaming. That one she tried not to listen to.

But the others — the others were singing. And the song said: *tell them. Tell them we are coming back. Tell them it is mercy.*

Sefa stood up.

She didn't decide to stand. Her legs simply took her, the way water takes a channel. She heard Orsen's breath stop — deliberately, completely — and felt the woman's attention swing toward her like a lamp.

"Child," said old Havel, "sit down, you'll catch your death."

"The gods are awake," Sefa said.

Her voice came out smaller than the words deserved. She cleared her throat. Forty-one sets of breathing shifted; someone's spoon stopped mid-stir.

"They never left," she said. "Everyone thinks they left. Everyone thinks five years ago they went silent because they abandoned us, or died, or were never there at all. That's what the Ministry teaches. That silence is the natural state of heaven." She turned her blind face toward the hearth, letting its warmth map her cheeks. "It isn't. Silence is a wound. They are bound beneath the drowned city, all seven of them, chained under the river where the capital used to be, and they have been screaming for five years, and now—" her voice caught, because the song swelled as she spoke, harmonies stacking inside her skull like snow on a roof— "*now* the chains are breaking."

Silence in the hall. The good kind and the bad kind tangled together.

"That's madness," said one of the twins — Ilse, by the pitch. "The Ministry says—"

"The Ministry says," Sefa agreed gently, "that the gods are dead and that anyone who hears otherwise should be burned. The Ministry has been saying that while prayers started being answered. While charms started working. While the sick started waking whole." She turned toward the boy Pell, following the thread of his fear like a rope. "Your mother prayed over you every night for a year when the fever took your sister. Did you feel answered then?"

"No," Pell whispered.

"And last month? When the fever came back?"

His breathing went strange. "...Yes."

"Yes," Sefa said. "That wasn't luck. That wasn't a charm some smuggler sold you. That was a god with one hand free, reaching through a crack in the world to hold one small boy." She heard him start to cry, quietly, the way boys cry when they're trying not to. "They are coming back. Not as punishment. As mercy. They remember us. Every prayer we sent into the dark for five years — they heard all of them. They were *keeping* them."

She hadn't known she was going to say that last part until she said it, and the moment it left her mouth she felt it land in the room like a coal dropped in dry straw. A woman began to sob — Marta, the deep helpless sobs of someone who has been strong too long. The old ferryman was murmuring something, a prayer from before the Silence, half-remembered, stumbling over the words and weeping harder for the stumbling.

*Good,* sang the gods. *More. More.*

And Sefa felt — warm, lifted, carried — and underneath the warmth, faint as a hairline crack in ice, something else. A small cold voice of her own that said: *you didn't know that either. You're making it up as you go, and they're handing you the words.*

She stepped on that voice. It was so much easier to step on than the screaming god.

* * *

By morning she had a congregation.

Not that anyone called it that. They called it sitting near the girl while she listened. But they arranged themselves around her the way iron filings arrange around a lodestone, and when she spoke of what the gods told her, they stopped chewing, stopped shivering, stopped grieving — just for the length of her voice — and Sefa drank that stillness like water.

Orsen watched from the wall.

Sefa could feel her there the way you feel weather coming: a pressure, an attention, a held breath. All morning the woman stood apart with her arms crossed, and when Sefa paused between visions, she could hear Orsen's jaw working, the tiny grind of teeth, words being swallowed.

At midday she finally came close. Close enough that Sefa could smell woodsmoke and old leather and, beneath it, the scorched-metal scent that clung to her ever since the avalanche — the smell of the sigil, though Sefa had never seen it and never asked.

"We need to talk," Orsen said, low.

"You could sit and listen instead."

"I've been listening. That's the problem." A pause. Snow ticked against the shutters. "What you told them last night about the chains breaking — is that true, or is that what they told you to say?"

"What's the difference?"

"The difference," Orsen said, "is everything."

Sefa tilted her head, listening past the woman to the sevenfold chorus. "They say you know more about chains than anyone alive."

Orsen's breath stopped again. That deliberate, practiced nothing.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, and her heartbeat — Sefa could hear heartbeats, always had been able to, it was the first thing the gods ever gave her — Orsen's heartbeat did not change at all. Which was itself a kind of answer. Only people who had trained their hearts for years could lie to a listener.

"Ossuin says your name," Sefa said softly. "Every night. He says it like a man saying a prayer he's ashamed of."

"Stop." The word came out flat and hard as a slammed door. Then, quieter, almost gentle, which was worse: "Sefa. Listen to me. I've seen what happens when people start believing a girl who hears voices. I've seen what the Ministry does to congregations. There was a village two days south of here—"

"I know. I heard it burning."

"—and if you keep gathering these people around you and calling it mercy, you're painting a target on every one of them. Including you. *Especially* you." A pause, and when Orsen spoke again her voice had dropped into something rawer. "The gods aren't merciful. I served one for eleven years. They're *vast*. And vast things don't love you, Sefa. They use you. The kindest thing they ever do is ignore you."

For a moment Sefa let the silence stretch, feeling the woman's pulse finally, finally quicken — anger or fear, indistinguishable at this distance.

"You're wrong," Sefa said. "About one thing."

"Which thing?"

"They're not ignoring me." She smiled, and felt the smile do its work even unseen — she had been told once, by a temple matron before the purges, that her smile made people want to be better. "They chose me. Out of everyone in this freezing hall, out of everyone in this dying country, they gave their song to *me*. Do you know what that's worth after five years of nothing? After five years of being a blind beggar girl nobody prayed *for*, let alone *to*?" She reached out and found Orsen's sleeve, gripped it. "You think I'm painting a target on them. I'm giving them something to live for. That's not the same thing. Whatever happened to you — whatever made you hate them — that's yours. Don't put it on me."

She released the sleeve and turned back to her congregation, and felt Orsen stand there a moment longer, breathless, before walking away with steps too controlled to be calm.

*Well done,* sang the gods, warm as brandy.

And the small cold voice, quieter now, harder to hear: *you just used a blind girl's smile like a knife. Since when?*

Since they started answering, Sefa supposed. Since warmth came back into the world and she discovered how starved she'd been.

* * *

It was Coram who knelt first.

He came to her on the third evening, when the storm had pinned them all another day in the waystation and the food had gone down to broth and the mood with it. He knelt in the trampled snow by the woodpile, out of sight of the others, and took her hand in both of his — big calloused hands, a fighter's hands — and pressed his forehead to her knuckles.

"Bless me," he said. "I know you're not a priest. Bless me anyway. I haven't dared pray in five years. They told us praying was how the Ministry finds you." His voice shook. "I used to be a door-warden at a temple, before. I killed men for the Ministry during the purges. I need — I need someone to tell me it can be washed."

Sefa looked down at where his face must be. The gods were singing through her like wind through a flute, and she felt the enormous temptation of it, the gorgeous easy power of standing in for something infinite.

She also felt, distinctly, the shape of the lie available to her: *you are forgiven.* Three words, and this man would follow her off a cliff.

So she said instead: "I can't wash anything. I'm just a girl who listens. But they hear you. They've heard you all along, Coram. Ask *them*."

He wept into her hand, and the gods sang *yes, yes, this, more,* and Sefa stood in the falling snow and understood, with terrible clarity, that she had just become a priest whether she wanted it or not — and that some part of her, some part that had been hungry for five years, had wanted exactly this.

By the next dawn, four of the six escorts knelt with Coram. Even Ilse, the skeptic twin, hovered at the edge of the circle with her arms crossed and her doubt visibly losing weight. Marta brought Sefa the first share of the broth. Old Havel, who had no knees to kneel with, bowed his head from his bench and mouthed the old prayers along with her, tears freezing in the seams of his face.

They called her the Listener. They asked her to settle disputes — Osk and Dree argued over a knife and brought it to her like children. They asked her whether the gods could see them through the storm, and she said yes, and it was true, and the truth felt like a coin she hadn't minted but was spending freely.

Only Orsen stayed outside the circle. And only Orsen saw what Sefa could feel happening to herself and could not stop.

* * *

On the fifth day, the storm broke and the Ministry came.

Not riders — worse. A paper. Dree found it nailed to the waystation door at first light, weighted there sometime in the night, which meant someone had stood in the storm within arm's reach of them all and none of the listeners, none of the watchers, none of the gods singing in Sefa's skull had heard him.

A printed leaflet, passed hand to hand in the hall. Sefa couldn't read it, but she could read the room reading it: forty-one breaths changing at once, the specific quality of silence that follows shock.

"Well?" Orsen's voice, sharp. "Someone read it aloud."

Havel read it, in a voice that got slower and slower. It named the Listener of the pilgrim road. It named the miracle-worker of Corvane — a woman marked with a living sigil, an escaped vessel, a servant of the imprisoned powers. It offered silver for information, pardon for cooperation, and the pyre for shelterers. It described the sigil-bearer in detail: a woman of middle years, dark-haired, armed, traveling north with a blind child.

"*The blind child is an instrument of the bound gods,*" Havel read, and faltered. "It says — it says she is to be taken alive. Taken alive and delivered to the Ministry's northern house. It says the gods speak through her, and the Ministry has... questions."

Sefa stood very still in the center of her congregation and listened to them not look at her.

She heard it happen — the subtle re-weaving of attention, fear threading through devotion, devotion curdling toward calculation. Silver. Pardon. She heard Coram's hand find his knife and Ilse's breath go quick and Marta begin, softly, to pray — to *her*, of all things, praying to the Listener while the Listener's own price was read aloud above their heads.

And she heard Orsen Vane fold the leaflet closed with a small, precise sound, like a trap shutting.

"They know exactly where we are," Orsen said to the room, calm as deep water. "Which means the rider who delivered this already reported our numbers. Which means we leave within the hour, split into two groups, and anyone carrying this paper's offer in their heart can stay behind with a clear conscience. No shame in it. Just choose now."

Nobody moved. Nobody stayed. But Sefa counted heartbeats, and three of them were lying.

Afterward, packing in the grey light, Orsen came and stood beside her, shouldering her pack.

"You heard them," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. "The ones thinking about the silver."

"I heard them." Orsen tied a strap. "Welcome to having followers. They love you and they'd sell you for coin in the same breath, and both are honest." She paused. "You're different lately. Did you notice? Your voice has changed. You used to ask people things. Now you tell them."

"The gods tell me things. I pass them on."

"Mm," Orsen said, in a tone that filed the answer away somewhere permanent. She slung the pack. "One more thing. That leaflet says *taken alive*. Rell doesn't take vessels alive unless he needs something from them. Something only a listener can give." She looked north, toward the pass, toward the drowned country beyond it. "He's not hunting you, Sefa. He's *recruiting* you. And whatever he thinks you can hear for him — I promise you it's the one thing the gods will never tell you themselves."

She walked out into the snow.

Sefa stood alone in the emptying hall, the leaflet's words still hanging in the air like smoke, and reached for the seven voices the way she reached for a handrail in the dark.

They were singing. They were always singing.

But tonight, for the first time, she noticed the song had a shape she'd been too warm and too fed and too adored to hear before — a shape like a funnel, like a throat, like a road running straight and narrow toward the drowned heart of the country, with her name written at the bottom of it in letters of light.

*Come,* sang the gods, tender as a mother. *Come and see what we will make of you.*

And far off, beneath everything, the seventh voice — the screaming one — stopped screaming, just for a moment, and said something new.

It said: *run, little listener.*

It sounded exactly like Orsen Vane.
