## 11. Rell's Offer

He came to her in the hour before dawn, when the waystation was nothing but breath and embers and the small sounds of people trying to sleep through the cold.

Orsen woke because the dog didn't bark. That was the first wrong thing. The pilgrims' half-starved cur had barked at every shadow for three nights, and now it lay flat against the wall of the stable, ears back, trembling at a man who stood in the doorway with snow on his shoulders and his hands folded like a priest at prayer.

Inquisitor Halvard Rell did not enter. He simply waited, and the waiting was worse than a shout would have been.

She reached for the knife under her bedroll and found it gone.

"Yara took it," Rell said quietly. "Before she left. She said you'd understand." He tilted his head. "Do you?"

Orsen sat up slowly, keeping the blanket across her lap, keeping her breathing even. Around her, the waystation's single long room held its sleepers — Sefa curled near the fire with two of her new followers, the mason Brannic snoring with a hatchet under his hand, the others scattered like dropped coins. None of them stirred. She couldn't tell if that was mercy or craft on Rell's part, and she suspected craft. A man who could walk into a room full of desperate people without waking one of them had not come alone by accident.

"You burned a village two days south," she said.

"I burned a congregation that was feeding itself to something in a well." His voice stayed level, almost gentle, the voice of a man correcting a child's arithmetic. "You've seen what they were feeding. You heard it screaming under the ice. Don't make me the monster in this story, Vessa."

The name went through her like a nail through the palm.

She hadn't heard it spoken aloud in five years. She'd buried it so deep she sometimes convinced herself she'd invented it — that the woman who had carried it belonged to someone else entirely, some other life, some other war. And here was this gray-eyed man in a Ministry coat saying it the way you'd say *good morning*, saying it knowing exactly what it would do.

"My name is Orsen."

"It is now." He stepped inside at last, and the cold came in with him, and the fire guttered though there was no wind. He crouched by the hearth, unhurried, and held his hands out to the coals as if he owned them. "I've read your file. All of it. Do you know how rare that is? Most vessels get a page. You got a vault."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," he agreed. "But your hands do."

* * *

He laid the folder on the table between them like a man laying down cards he'd already won with.

It was Ministry-gray, wax-sealed, stamped with the eye-and-bar of the Office of Silence. He broke the seal without ceremony. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere above them the mountain groaned — that deep tectonic complaint Orsen had been hearing for days, the sound of a country shifting its weight on something buried.

"Five years ago," Rell said, "the gods were taken off the board. Not killed — we both know killing was never possible — but bound. Chained beneath the drowned capital by seven sworn blades, each of whom drove a binding-stake with their own hand. Seven stakes. Seven blades. The working required it. Willing hands, god-touched hands, hands that loved what they were destroying." He looked up at her. "Six of those seven are dead. They died badly, over years, in ways the Ministry recorded with great care. The seventh—"

"Don't."

"—disappeared the night the last stake went home. Carried no sigil afterward. No god answered her prayers. She surfaced eighteen months later selling charms in Corvane under a name that doesn't appear in any registry before that year." He turned the folder around and pushed it across the table. "Orsen Vane. Age twenty-six. Born nowhere. Sworn to no one. A woman made of paperwork, and I do admire craftsmanship, but I was trained by the same office that taught her how to build it."

Orsen looked down at the folder.

She told herself not to touch it. She told herself that whatever was in it, looking would change nothing, that the past was a country she'd burned behind her, that—

Her hand moved anyway.

The top sheet was a portrait sketch, charcoal on heavy stock, and it was her face — younger, harder, the eyes still lit from inside with something she hadn't felt since. Her hair was braided in the war-style of the Ossuin clergy, iron rings worked through it, and across the collar of the drawn coat sat the sigil: the broken wheel, the seven spokes, the hub like a closed eye.

Below the portrait, in a clerk's precise hand:

**VESSA OF THE NINTH CHOIR. Blade-sworn to Ossuin, the Wheel-Turner, God of Roads and Return. Stake-bearer, Seventh Position. Status: operational.**

There were pages beneath it. She knew better than to read them and read them anyway. Supply requisitions in her own handwriting — *Vessa* — signing for cold-iron chain by the ton. A map of the drowned capital with seven points marked, six crossed off in red ink, the seventh circled. And then a report, dated five years ago to the day, written by someone whose name had been struck through so hard the paper had torn:

*...the seventh blade reports the stake seated true. Subject (Ossuin) ceased vocalization at the moment of binding. Recommend immediate extraction of all personnel from the sunken quarter. Recommend the seventh blade be retired from service and observed. Note: subject's final vocalization, translated from the old tongue, was addressed to the seventh blade by name. Translation follows.*

And beneath that, in a different hand, smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages, a single line of the old tongue that Orsen did not need anyone to translate, because she had heard it every night for five years in the space between sleeping and waking, and had told herself it was only a dream:

*I will keep the road open for you.*

The folder trembled in her hands. She made it stop.

"This proves nothing," she said. "Anyone can forge—"

"Look at the sigil on the requisition stamps."

She looked. And there it was, pressed into the wax of every seal, the mark her own flesh had worn for eleven years before it went dark: not drawn, not stamped — *burned*. The Ministry had taken impressions of the living sigil, the way you'd take a print from a criminal, and the wax held the ghost of heat that no forgery could fake, because the heat had come from a god, and gods left evidence the way wounds left scars.

"The other six files look just like this one," Rell said. "Except theirs end with death certificates. Interesting pattern, if you're the sort who looks for patterns. The ones who resisted the binding longest died worst. The ones who accepted it—" he shrugged, elegant, terrible, "—lived longer. You accepted it most completely of all. You drove your stake while smiling, according to the observer's notes. Shall I read you the notes?"

The fire popped. Above them the mountain groaned again, longer this time, and a fine sift of dust came down between the roof beams, and none of the sleepers woke, and Orsen understood suddenly, with a lurch, that they weren't sleeping. That the room was wrapped in something. That Rell had come prepared to have this conversation privately, and privacy, in the Ministry's grammar, meant a room where no one else existed yet.

"What do you want?" she asked.

* * *

What he wanted, it turned out, was almost reasonable. That was the horror of it. He laid it out the way a merchant lays out fair terms, and every piece of it made sense, and every piece of it was a knife.

"The Ministry has known for weeks that the silence was ending," he said. "Prayers answered in the north. Charms working. Sigils waking. We executed forty-one vessels in the first month, and it accomplished nothing except teaching the rest of them to hide, so now we are being more... discerning." He folded his hands. "Here is what I know about you, Vessa. You built the prison. Which means you know where it is, how it works, and — critically — how it fails. The cracks are already open. Something under the ice is pushing, and the winter falling out of season is not weather. It is pressure. The prison is failing, and when it fails completely, seven gods come back into a world that chained them once and remembers why."

"And the Ministry wants to re-chain them."

"The Ministry wants many things. I want something simpler." For the first time, something moved behind his eyes — not warmth, never warmth, but appetite, banked and patient as a lime kiln. "A binding requires willing, god-touched hands. Seven of them. We have six candidates in custody. Devout, frightened, manageable. What we lack is a seventh who has done it before — because the working remembers its makers, and a made thing opens easier to a familiar hand. Bring me the names of any other surviving vessels you know. Surrender the sigil — intact, alive, uncut. And drive the seventh stake again, where you drove it before." He smiled, thin as a paper cut. "Do that, and the pilgrim caravan walks free. Every soul. The blind girl, the mason, the widow with the twins. I'll give them escort to the Door of Ash myself. Food, papers, passage. The Ministry keeps its word; ask anyone we haven't killed."

Orsen sat very still.

This was the trap, and she could see every wire of it, and that changed nothing, because the wires were real. Sefa, who heard gods the way other children heard music. Brannic, who'd taken the caravan job to feed a family in the border camps. The widow Anthe with her twin girls asleep against her ribs even now, small breaths in the dark. Forty-some souls who had followed a rumor of safety north and found only her.

All she had to do was damn the world.

"You'd kill them anyway," she said. "After. Once I'm underground with a stake in my hands, you'd burn the caravan for optics."

"Yes," Rell said, without a flicker. "Probably. But you can't prove that, and hope is a currency, and I'm offering you some. Take it or leave it, but decide quickly — the pass closes within the day, and after that, everyone in this room dies of the cold or the avalanche or whatever comes up out of the deep when the last seal goes. I am genuinely the kindest thing currently being offered to you." He rose, brushed coal-dust from his coat, and retrieved the folder from the table with two fingers, delicately, though he let the portrait sketch slide loose and stay behind, face-up, her younger self staring at the ceiling beams. "You have until the snow stops. It's been stopping at dawn lately. The mountain likes to let us think it's finished."

At the door he paused, back to her, snow-light silvering his shoulders.

"One more thing, since you'll wonder. Your god's last words — the translation. *I will keep the road open for you.* The Ministry spent two years debating what road he meant." He glanced back, and his gray eyes caught the ember-light. "We concluded it was a threat. Gods threaten. But I've stood where you're going to stand, Vessa, at the edge of that sunken dark, and I've read the bindings, and I'll tell you what I actually believe, gratis, between professionals." His voice dropped, and for one unguarded instant he sounded tired, human, almost kind. "He wasn't threatening you. He was promising. He kept a door open in himself — one crack, one seam, one place where the stake didn't seat quite true — because he knew his blade would need a way back to him someday. He forgave you before the hammer fell. That's what gods are. That's why they have to be chained." He straightened, and the kindness shuttered away. "Think on which of us is offering you mercy. You know where to find me. I'm the only man in the mountains standing perfectly still."

Then he was gone, and the door closed without sound, and the sleepers all sighed at once, together, like a tide going out, and began to stir and mutter and wake as if no time had passed at all.

* * *

Orsen sat by the dead fire until the sky went gray, then white, then the particular bruised gold that meant dawn behind cloud.

The portrait lay on the table where Rell had left it. Young Vessa looked out of it with eyes full of certainty, and Orsen tried to hate her and found she couldn't, and tried to forgive her and found she couldn't do that either. *You drove the stake smiling,* the file said. She remembered the smile. It hadn't been joy. It had been the face you make when you're holding something shut with your whole body and you dare not let it waver — the smile of a woman standing on a lid, pressing down, telling herself the screaming underneath was only wind.

*I will keep the road open for you.*

Five years of silence, and it had never been silence. It had been a god holding a door open with his ruined hands, waiting for her to walk through, and she had spent those five years selling luck-charms and calling herself dead and calling it peace.

Sefa appeared beside her without sound, the way the blind move, and stood looking — facing — the portrait with her empty eyes.

"That man was here," she said. Not a question. "The still one. He smells like cold stone and ink." Her head turned, finding Orsen's face by some sense older than sight. "He showed you the past."

"He showed me a file."

"Mm." Sefa sat down across from her, small hands folded, and for a long moment neither spoke. Then, gently, terribly: "He's lying about one thing. Not the file. The file is true — I can hear it humming in you now, all that old iron. But he lied about the choice." She leaned forward. "He says: surrender the sigil, name the vessels, save the caravan. As if those were three things. They're one thing. Give him the sigil and you give him the key to every vessel alive, because the sigil knows its siblings. Name them and you've surrendered it already. There was never an offer, Orsen. There was only a hook, dressed as a hand."

Orsen looked at the girl — fourteen years old, blind, wrapped in three coats that didn't fit, carrying a god's voice in her skull like a bird in a cage — and felt the full weight of what Rell had actually put on the table.

Not her soul. Her soul was already forfeit; she'd sold it five years ago with a smile on a sunken street. What Rell was offering to buy was everyone she'd failed to protect since.

Outside, the snow was stopping. Flake by flake, the way it had each dawn, the mountain holding its breath between blows. In the quiet she could hear the groan of the deep places, closer now, and beneath it — faint, rhythmic, patient as a heartbeat under floorboards — something that might have been knocking.

Or might have been a door, very far below, being held open.

Sefa rose. "They'll want to break camp soon," she said. "While the snow's stopped. Tallow's route goes through the high col, and the col won't wait." She paused at the edge of the firelight, and her blind eyes seemed, as always, to be looking at something slightly behind the world. "Whatever he asked you — you should know the god under the ice has started saying your name in his sleep. Both of them. The one he gave you, and the one you buried." A small, sad smile. "He says them the way a man says a prayer. Decide fast, blade of Ossuin. Roads close. But roads also open."

She walked away toward the others, and Orsen sat alone with the portrait, and the stopped snow, and the knocking far below, and understood that whatever she chose, she would be choosing it as a woman she had spent five years pretending she had never been.
