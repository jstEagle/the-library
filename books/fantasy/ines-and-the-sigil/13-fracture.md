## 13. Fracture

The leaflets came down with the snow.

Orsen found the first one plastered against the waystation door, half-frozen, the ink bleeding where meltwater had touched it. She peeled it free and read it twice before her hands stopped shaking enough to read it a third time.

*WANTED: THE VESSEL VESSA. Blade-sworn of Ossuin. Architect of the Binding. This woman walked among you as "Orsen Vane." She drove the stake herself. She is the reason your gods are screaming. She is the reason your children freeze. Reward: passage out. Pardon for all sins. The Ministry forgives those who help it silence its enemies.*

Below the text was an engraving of her face — younger, harder, hair cropped short in the style of the godsworn, eyes that had not yet learned to flinch. Someone had drawn the sigil on her forearm in careful detail, the seven-pointed star of Ossuin's oath, so accurate it could only have been copied from Ministry records.

She stood there until the cold worked through her boots and into the bone.

Behind her, inside the waystation, thirty-one pilgrims huddled around a fire that Yara's defectors had built from broken pews. Sefa was singing — one of the quiet hymns she made up as she went, melodies that sounded older than any hymn Orsen knew. The survivors of the massacre village had joined them at the pass, and then the stragglers from the avalanche, and now they were a congregation without a church, following a blind girl through a country that was dying from underneath.

Orsen folded the leaflet into quarters, and eighths, and sixteenths, until it was a small hard knot she could close her fist around.

Then she went back inside, and said nothing, and waited for the second leaflet to arrive.

It arrived by morning.

* * *

They came down from the high pasture road just after dawn — two riders, no colors, dropping paper as they went like crows seeding a field. By the time anyone thought to chase them they were gone over the ridge, and the wind had already done their work, scattering white rectangles across the camp like a blizzard of accusations.

Orsen watched from the edge of the fire circle as a woman picked one up, read it, and looked up slowly. Then another. Then a man thrust his copy toward his neighbor, jabbing at the engraving, and the neighbor turned, and found Orsen standing there, and the look on his face was the look of a man who has found a snake in his boot.

"She drove the stake," the woman whispered. Not to anyone. To the air. To the idea of it.

"I was told she healed a boy," someone else said. "In Corvane. My cousin's neighbor saw it—"

"Healed him so she could keep playing at being holy," the first woman snapped. "You think the Ministry lies about this? You think they'd print her face for nothing?"

Yes, Orsen wanted to say. Yes, they would. They printed whatever kept the silent silent. But even as she formed the words she understood they didn't matter. Truth had stopped being a currency here; fear had inflated past it.

Sefa rose from the fire. Her blind eyes found Orsen across the smoke with that unerring accuracy that had never stopped unnerving her. "Everyone sit down," Sefa said, mild as milk. "The snow is deep, the pass is closed behind us, and we are all each other has. Whatever this paper says, it doesn't warm anyone."

"It says she chained them," the woman said. "It says she's the reason my sister's village burned."

"It says a lot of things."

"You *heard* them, girl. You hear the gods right now. Are they screaming or aren't they?"

Sefa was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had gone very soft, which was how Orsen knew it was bad. "They're screaming."

The word moved through the crowd like a blade through wet cloth. Faces turned. And every one of them, when it landed on Orsen, carried the same question, and the question had teeth.

"Then ask them who did it," the woman said.

"I don't need to ask," Sefa said. "I've known since the river."

The world went very still and very loud at once. Orsen heard her own heartbeat, heard the fire pop, heard the wind worrying the loose shutter of the waystation like a patient animal.

"You've *known*," Orsen said.

"Since the river." Sefa's face was calm, and sorry, and absolutely immovable. "I heard your name in the water, Orsen. Both of your names. I hoped I was wrong. I prayed I was wrong." She reached out and took Orsen's wrist, gently, and pushed back the sleeve, and showed the crowd the sigil burning on her forearm — brighter now than it had ever been, seven points of ember-light that no sleeve could hide anymore. "But the god she bound won't stop calling to her. That's not the mark of a jailer. That's something else. That's—"

"That's exactly what a jailer's mark looks like when the prisoner starts rattling the bars," Yara said.

The defector came around the side of the waystation with her pack already shouldered and three of her people behind her, armed, dressed, ready. Orsen hadn't even heard them packing. That was what five years of Ministry service taught you: how to leave quietly.

"Yara," Orsen said.

"Don't." Yara held up a hand. "Don't make me say anything kind, because I don't have any saved up. I cut a deal to get these people through the pass. That deal assumed you were a smuggler with a cursed arm. It does not cover a Ministry asset who helped build the thing that's killing this country." She jerked her chin at the leaflets drifting against the stones. "Rell doesn't print lies about his own people. He prints truths he can use. Which means everything on that page is real, which means everything you told us about running *from* the Ministry was theater, which means I'm done."

"I pulled the boy back from death in Corvane. Does that sound like theater?"

"It sounds like a vessel doing what vessels do." Yara's eyes were flat and gray as slate. "You want to know what I know? I processed vessel files for six years. Do you know what the files say about the ones who turn? They always heal someone first. Always. It's how they bind a following. It's step one of the doctrine. *Miracle, devotion, weapon.*" She spat into the snow. "I've read this story from the other end, Vane. I'm not living through it."

"Where will you go?" Sefa asked. "The pass ahead is worse than the pass behind."

"Better to die lost than be led by the person who dug the grave we're all standing in." Yara adjusted her pack strap. Then, for just a moment, something flickered behind the slate — some old tired human thing. "For what it's worth, I believe you'd die for these people. I just don't believe you'd tell them why you owe them that."

She walked. Three defectors followed her, and then a fourth — a young man Orsen had shared watch with, who wouldn't meet her eyes — and then the snow swallowed them and the wind erased the sound of them and they were gone.

Thirty-one became twenty-six.

Orsen stood in the settling quiet and felt the shape of the group change around her, the way a body changes when an organ is removed. She had spent five years learning to belong to no one. She had forgotten how much it hurt to be expelled.

"Tallow," she said. "We need to move. Now. Rell's riders know exactly where we are."

Tallow was at the far end of the fire, and he was looking at her with an expression she had never seen on him before. Tallow's face was a professional instrument — greed, charm, grievance, all of it deployed, none of it real. What was on it now was grief, badly hidden, the way he hid everything, behind a shrug that didn't quite work.

"Well," he said. "You always were more trouble than your cut."

"Tallow—"

"Twenty years I ran charms for you. Twenty years, and never once asked what the sigil cost you, because asking questions is bad business." He rubbed his jaw. "Should've asked."

"You believed the leaflets too?"

"I believe Rell wants you alive, which means everyone near you dies slower, and I believe the pass ahead has one narrow place where six riders can be stopped by one man with a crossbow." He was already checking the bow as he said it, running his thumb along the string the way he used to check coin edges. "And I believe you're going to argue with me about it, and I haven't got the patience."

"There are other ways through."

"Not for twenty-six people and a cart. There's the throat of the pass and nothing else, and the throat needs holding." He slung the quiver. "So I'll hold it."

"No." The word came out of her harder than she meant. "No. We hold it together, or we find another—"

"Together?" He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Girl, they just read your name off a piece of paper and stepped away from the fire. There is no together. There's you, and there's everyone you get killed." He looked at her then, straight on, and let her see the grief he'd been hiding, because he was leaving and dead men could afford honesty. "I liked you better when you were nobody. But nobody was always a lie, wasn't it? You were always her. Vessa. The stake-driver. You just hadn't gotten around to admitting it yet."

He walked up the trail toward the throat of the pass, and this time Orsen did not follow, because if she followed she would stop him, and she could not stop him, because he was right.

That was the worst part. He was right.

* * *

They heard the fighting begin an hour later.

Sound carries strangely in mountains — it arrives late and wrong, flattened, as if the rocks themselves are reluctant witnesses. First the crack of the crossbow, once, twice, a pause, three times fast. Then shouting, thin with distance. Then a horn, Ministry-issue, three rising notes, and a long silence.

Then nothing, for a very long time.

Sefa stood beside Orsen at the mouth of the trail, her face tilted toward the pass, listening to the nothing. The twenty-four others waited behind them with the cart and the children and the wounded, packed like sheep in a storm, waiting for someone to tell them whether to run.

"We go now," Orsen said. "Through the throat while they're regrouping. Keep low, keep moving, don't stop for anything."

"And Tallow?" one of the men asked.

Orsen looked up the trail, at the white switchbacks climbing into the gray sky, at the place where a man with a crossbow and twenty years of small dishonesties had decided to become, for one hour, entirely honest.

"He's holding the door," she said. "Our job is to walk through it."

They went. Orsen took the rear, because rearguard was where cowards hid and where the guilty belonged, and she was no longer sure which she was. As they climbed through the throat of the pass — a slot in the rock barely wide enough for the cart, walls rising sheer on either side — she passed the place where Tallow had made his stand. A barricade of stone and broken cart-wheel. Spent bolts in the snow, a dozen of them, more than any one quiver should hold, which meant he'd stripped the dead. Blood, frozen black, a great deal of it. And bootprints, many bootprints, going up and over the barricade and not coming back.

She did not let herself look longer. Looking longer was a luxury for people with time to grieve, and grief was a debt collector that caught you if you stood still.

Near the top of the throat, a body lay in the snow wearing Ministry gray. Beside it, half-buried, a scrap of white paper, weighted with a stone.

Orsen should have walked past. She stopped.

She brushed the snow away. It was a leaflet, one of the earlier ones, torn and refolded — and on the back, in Tallow's cramped, ugly hand, in ink that had frozen and thawed and frozen again:

*If you're reading this I'm dead, so skip the crying. The throat's held. Take the east fork at the cairn — the map's sewn in your bedroll lining, has been since Corvane, you never check anything. Don't trust the girl's god. Don't trust yours either. Trust the twenty-six. They're the only thing in this country that's real.*

*You were never more trouble than your cut.*

*Pay my passage forward.*

Orsen knelt in the snow and read it four times, and then she folded it the way she'd folded the leaflet — smaller and smaller, a knot she could close her fist around — and put it next to her heart, where the other one lived. Two papers now. One accusation, one absolution. She suspected she would carry both for the rest of her life, and that they weighed exactly the same.

Ahead, Sefa waited at the fork, head tilted, listening to something far below the snow.

"The god is louder here," Sefa said. "Ossuin. He's saying your name. He's been saying it since the pass." A pause, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Orsen. He isn't begging you to free him anymore."

The wind came down the throat of the pass behind them, carrying the last of the smoke, the last of Tallow.

"What's he saying?"

Sefa's blind eyes were full of something Orsen had never seen in them before. Fear.

"He's saying *come and see what they made me into.*"
