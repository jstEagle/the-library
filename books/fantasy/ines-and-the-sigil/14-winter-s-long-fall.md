## 14. Winter's Long Fall

Sefa heard the cold coming before anyone else did.

It was not like other weather. Other weather had voices — wind worrying at the rocks, rain ticking on canvas, thunder rolling its great bone drums across the hills. This was different. This was a silence moving toward them, vast and patient, the way a predator is patient. She stood in the lee of the ruined waystation with her face turned north and listened to it come, and what she heard underneath the silence was singing.

Or screaming. At this distance, in this state, she could no longer tell the difference.

"Sefa." Orsen's voice, close. The woman had learned to announce herself — a habit Sefa both appreciated and mourned, because it meant Orsen had decided she was fragile. "You've been standing there an hour. We should move."

"It's coming," Sefa said. "The winter. It's not falling from the sky, Orsen. It's rising out of the ground."

Behind them, one of their two remaining companions — Bren, the broad-shouldered carter who had once driven pilgrims for coin and now drove nothing but his own terror — made a small sound in his throat. The other, Ivet, a thin former lay-sister of a congregation that no longer existed, said nothing at all. She had stopped saying much of anything since the village of ash. She prayed constantly, silently, her lips moving like a moth trapped against glass, and Safa did not have the heart to tell her that the gods were in no condition to listen.

"Then we move faster," Orsen said.

They moved.

* * *

The country was dying quietly, and that was the worst of it. Sefa had imagined apocalypse as fire, as armies, as the sky cracking open like an egg. She had not imagined frost creeping up fence posts in neat silver hierarchies while birds fell asleep mid-flight and dropped into the snow without a sound. She had not imagined a farmhouse they passed where the family sat frozen at their supper table, not dead exactly — she could hear the faintest thread of breath in them, three heartbeats slowed to almost nothing — preserved like flies in amber, waiting for a spring that might never come.

"They're not gone," she whispered, when Orsen pulled her away from the window. "They're *waiting*. Everything's waiting."

"The gods are dying," Orsen said. Her voice was flat in the way it went flat when flatness was all that held her together. "Dying things pull warmth toward themselves. You know this."

"I know the gods aren't supposed to be able to die." Sefa turned her blind eyes toward the woman's voice, toward the place where she could feel the sigil burning under Orsen's sleeve like a coal wrapped in linen. She felt it always now, a second heartbeat that wasn't hers. "That's what everyone says. That's what the Ministry says, when they're killing people for saying otherwise. Gods can't die. So what does it mean, Orsen, when everything holy in the world starts freezing?"

Orsen didn't answer. She never answered that question. But her hand found Sefa's shoulder and squeezed once, briefly, and Sefa heard the sigil flare hot — anger or fear or grief, she couldn't parse which — before Orsen walked away to break trail through the deepening snow.

Bren came up beside her, breathing hard, dragging the handcart that held everything they owned: rope, dried fish, a tent, Ivet's prayer book, the map Tallow had died to buy them time to keep. "The girl hears things," he muttered, not unkindly, to someone behind him. "Girl hears things, we should ask the girl things."

"I'm here," Sefa said. "And yes. Ask."

A pause. Then, grudgingly respectful: "How far to the drowned city?"

She listened. Not with her ears — with whatever she had become since the river showed her its memories, since the gods' voices had poured into her like water into a cracked cup. North, past the white hills. Down, past the terraces where orchards had been. Down further, below the waterline of a country that had drowned its own heart and called it peace.

"Three days," she said. "If the cold lets us."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we'll be waiting too. Like the family at the table." She smiled, because Bren needed her to smile. "But I don't think it wants us yet. The cold, I mean. It's looking for something specific."

"What?"

She didn't say: *her.* The sigil-bearer. The maker of the stake. She didn't say it because Orsen was twenty feet ahead and had ears like a fox and carried enough guilt already to sink a ship. Instead she said, "Warmth. It's looking for warmth. And we're the warmest thing left on this road."

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a chapel, and the winter fell on them like a curtain.

Sefa woke to a sound she had never heard before and hoped never to hear again: snow landing on snow, in quantities so enormous that each new layer compressed the last with a soft, wet *thump*, like the slow heartbeat of something burying the world. Inside the chapel the air had gone sharp enough to cut. Ivet's lips had gone blue around her silent prayers. Bren was shaking so hard his teeth chattered in a rhythm Sefa could set words to, and the words were *we die here, we die here.*

Then the fire lit itself.

Not quite itself — Orsen knelt over the kindling with her sleeve pushed back, and the sigil on her forearm was burning visible through the cloth, gold-white light leaking between the stitches of the world, and where her hand touched the wood, flame bloomed without smoke. Warmth rolled across the chapel in a wave. Sefa gasped. It wasn't heat like a fire's heat. It was heat like a memory of summer, like a mother's kitchen, like every safe place she had ever been folded together and handed back to her.

"How long can you do that?" she asked softly, when Bren and Ivet had collapsed into exhausted sleep near the flames.

Orsen was quiet a long moment. In the dark, by sound alone, Sefa built her image: sitting cross-legged, arms wrapped around herself, head bowed. A woman carrying something heavy and pretending it weighed nothing.

"Less every time," Orsen said finally. "It's like drawing from a well that's refilling slower than I drink. Whatever's waking beneath the country — it feeds me, and I feed it, and neither of us has much to spare." A dry, terrible laugh. "Five years I begged my god for one word. Now he pours power through me like a burst dam and I'd give anything for him to shut up again."

"He's not talking to you?"

"No." Flat again. "He's *screaming*. Can't you hear it? All of them?"

"Yes," Sefa said. "I hear it all the time. Seven voices under the ice." She hesitated. "Do you want to know what they're saying?"

"No," Orsen said immediately. Then, after a silence long enough for the fire to crackle twice: "Yes. Tell me."

Sefa listened. She had gotten good at listening — at sorting the seven voices the way a musician sorts instruments in an orchestra. There was the deep one, the mother-of-stone, whose grief came in tectonic pulses. There were the twins, who bickered even while drowning. There was the bright one, the singer, whose voice had gone hoarse weeks ago and now came out in whispers that broke Sefa's heart. And there was Ossuin — Orsen's god, the blade-lord, the silent one — whose voice was the strangest of all, because most of the time he wasn't screaming at all.

Most of the time, he was listening.

"He watches you," Sefa said. "Even bound. Even starving. When you burn the sigil, all the others cry out — pain, or envy, or hope, I can't always tell. But Ossuin goes quiet. He goes quiet and he *listens*, like a man pressing his ear to a door." She reached out and found Orsen's wrist, the one with the sigil, and felt the woman flinch and then go still. "He's not screaming, Orsen. He's praying. I think you're the only prayer he has left."

"Don't." Orsen's voice cracked down the middle. "Don't say that."

"Why not? It's true."

"Because if it's true, then what I'm about to do—" She stopped. Breathed. Started over, colder: "Sleep, Sefa. Tomorrow we walk into the drowned city, and whatever happens there, you'll want your strength."

Sefa slept. But she dreamed of a door pressed against an ear, and behind the door, a god whispering a name over and over like a rosary — not *Orsen*, not *Vessa*, but something older than both, something that made the sigil glow in her dream like dawn through a keyhole.

* * *

On the second day, the sun rose in the wrong place.

Sefa couldn't see it, but she could feel it — the faint warmth crawling along the wrong side of her face, east when it should have been south — and Bren confirmed it with a strangled oath. "Sun's coming up out of the north," he kept saying. "Sun's coming up wrong. Sun's coming up *wrong*." Ivet had begun reciting the liturgy aloud for the first time since the massacre, her voice thin and steady, and Sefa thought: good, let her, let someone speak to them properly, even if the gods are too far under to hear.

The land itself was failing. Roads they crossed had frost-heaved into ridges like the backs of buried serpents. Rivers ran sluggish and gray, crusting at the edges, and once — Sefa would remember this all her life — they passed a waterfall frozen mid-fall, a hundred feet of water hanging motionless in the air like a sheet of glass, fish suspended inside it, turning slowly, still alive. Bren threw a stone at it. The stone stuck.

"The binding is failing," Sefa said. "That's what this is. Five years ago they chained the seasons to the gods' sleep — I heard the river say it, I heard the stones say it. As long as the gods slept, the world above stayed ordinary. Now they're thrashing, dying, waking — and the chains are tearing loose, and the seasons are coming apart with them." She spread her hands. "Winter isn't falling anymore. Winter is *leaking*. Out of the prison. Into everything."

Orsen walked ahead and said nothing, but Sefa heard her sleeve rustling, heard her fingers pressing hard against the sigil, over and over, like a woman checking a wound.

Near dusk they found the second frozen village, and this one was worse than the first, because this one was singing.

Every inhabitant stood in the street, upright, frosted over, faces tilted toward the ground — toward the drowned capital, though none of them could have known the direction — and when Sefa passed among them she heard, faintly, from inside the ice, the hymn they had been singing when the cold took them. Still going. Slowed to a drone, stretched across centuries, but going. A congregation mid-verse, preserved at the moment of worship, and Sefa put her hands over her ears even though her ears had never worked and it changed nothing, because the song wasn't coming through her ears.

"*We have to help them,*" Ivet said suddenly — her first full sentence in days, wild-eyed, grabbing Sefa's arm. "Break the ice, wake them, we can't just—"

"We can't," Orsen said. Gently, horribly gently. "There's no breaking it. There's no waking them. They're not frozen, sister. They're *paused*. And the only thing that unpauses them is what's happening under the capital." She looked north, toward the drowned heart, and Sefa heard the sigil roar like a struck bell. "Which is why we keep walking."

* * *

The third day, the snow stopped falling upward.

That was how Sefa described it afterward, knowing it made no sense: flakes drifting sideways, then slanting up, then hanging in the air entirely, thousands of them, motionless, while the light went strange and sourceless and Bren wept openly and Ivet's liturgy trailed off into silence. The world had become a painting of itself. Sound flattened. Even the gods' screaming went quiet — not gone, Sefa realized, but *close*, so close and so vast that it stopped being a sound and became the medium everything else happened inside, the way fish don't hear the ocean.

"We're here," she said. "Above it. The drowned city."

She felt Orsen stop beside her. Felt the sigil's heat shift — banked coals becoming open flame. Felt, beneath her feet, through forty feet of frozen earth and deeper water older than any of them, the seven prisons turning in their sleep like animals dreaming of the door.

"There's a stair," Sefa said. "Under the chapel hill. The stones remember it. The stones have been *waiting* for someone to remember it." She turned her face toward Orsen, toward the burning she could feel like a campfire against her cheek. "They've been waiting for you specifically. The maker of the stake. The stone remembers your hands, Orsen. It remembers them very well."

For a long moment, nobody spoke. The suspended snow hung around them like a held breath.

Then Orsen Vane — blade-sworn, god-marked, the woman who had buried a name and dug up a legend — said, in a voice smaller than Sefa had ever heard it:

"Then let's go introduce myself."

And beneath their feet, so deep that it was less a sound than a shifting of the world's weight, something ancient rolled over and began, at last, to climb toward the surface.
