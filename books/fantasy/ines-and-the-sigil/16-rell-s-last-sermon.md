## 16. Rell's Last Sermon

Inquisitor Halvard Rell had preached his first sermon at nine years old, standing on a crate in a fish market, and he had learned the same lesson then that he knew now: a crowd does not want truth. A crowd wants shape. Give them a shape to fit their fear into and they will follow you anywhere, even down.

He stood now at the mouth of the sunken capital's great nave, where the flooded streets of old Corvane had become corridors of black water and the cathedral itself rose out of the deep like the ribcage of something that had died praying. Ministry lanterns — forty of them, cold white, flameless — floated in a slow orbit around him, each one held aloft by a Silence-bearer whose lips moved in the old counter-liturgy, the words that unmade prayer. The light did not warm anything. It only made the dark legible.

And the dark had so much to say.

The water below the walkways was not still. It shivered with light from beneath — gold, then blue, then a bruised violet that Rell had no name for — as if the drowned city sat atop seven buried lamps and someone below was turning up their wicks one by one. Every few minutes the whole cavern exhaled, a long subterranean groan that rattled silt from the vaulting overhead, and the pilgrims huddled on the far shore flinched like sheep hearing wolves.

Rell adjusted his grey coat. He had dressed for this the way other men dressed for coronations.

"Word of the day," he said quietly, to the woman at his shoulder. "Not purge. Not silence. *Consecration.*"

Adjunct Vess looked at him the way she always did when he used church words — as if they were loaded weapons he kept for occasions exactly like this one. "They'll hear it as surrender, lord Inquisitor. The doctrine is explicit. Divinity returns, divinity dies."

"The doctrine was written by frightened men who thought the gods were gone." Rell stepped forward onto the causeway, boots ringing on wet stone. "I have read the reports from every site where a vessel has burned. Do you know what I found in them? Not heresy. *Anatomy.* Each vessel carries a thread. Each thread runs down. And all the threads run here." He spread his hands toward the glowing water. "We are not standing over graves, Adjunct. We are standing over a treasury."

Below them, deeper than any sounding line could reach, something turned over in its sleep, and the violet light surged bright enough to throw shadows from the floating lanterns.

Vess said nothing. She had been with him eleven years and had learned that his silences were assignments.

"Bring me the girl," Rell said. "And bring me Vane. Gently. What comes next must look like mercy, or none of them will kneel."

* * *

Orsen heard the Ministry before she saw them — not boots, not voices, but the *absence*. The cavern's constant low murmur, the sound Sefa said was the gods breathing through stone, thinned and stuttered wherever the white lanterns passed, like a song with holes punched through it. She had spent five years living inside that absence. Her body knew it the way a scar knows rain.

"They're crossing the causeway," she said. "Forty, maybe fifty. Lantern-bearers in front."

Sefa turned her blind face toward the sound, and Orsen saw the girl do the thing she always did — tilt her head as though listening past the world to something underneath it. "They're carrying something else too," Sefa said. "Iron. A lot of iron. And chains that are... singing. Badly. Like a hymn played backwards."

"Binding-chains." Orsen's hand went to her sleeve, where the sigil slept under its wrapping of linen. It had been burning steadily since they'd descended into the drowned heart, a heat like a coal sewn beneath her skin. "Chains made to hold what's already chained."

Down the causeway came the Ministry column, and at its head, unmistakable even at distance by his unhurried gait, walked Halvard Rell.

Orsen had faced him once across an interrogation table and twice across a burning village, and each time she had come away with the same impression: that Rell was not a cruel man, which would have been simple, but a *devout* one, which was infinitely worse. Cruelty wants things. Devotion wants everything.

"He's stopping," murmured Doss, one of the two escorts who remained of Tallow's caravan, a broad former quarryman with a crossbow he held like a rosary. "Why's he stopping?"

Because, Orsen realized, her stomach going cold, Rell had not come to fight. He had come to *speak*. He raised both arms, and the lanterns rose with him, and his voice carried across the black water with the practiced ease of a man who had spent his life making large rooms small enough to hold only his words.

"Pilgrims! Wanderers! Children of a country that was erased with a signature!" The echo gave his voice a congregation it didn't deserve. "You have walked through winter that falls like a verdict. You have seen villages emptied. You have been told the gods are waking, and you have been told the gods must die, and you do not know which story will kill you first. So let me tell you the third story. The true one."

Orsen started forward. Sefa caught her wrist — how, blind, she'd known to move that fast, Orsen never asked.

"Wait," Sefa whispered. "Listen to what he's really saying. Underneath."

"I am Halvard Rell," the Inquisitor called, "last servant of the Ministry of Silence, and I am here to confess. We were wrong. Not in our fear — our fear was holy. But in our answer. We tried to bury the divine, and burial is not the same as death, as this land has learned. The gods are rising whether we will it or no. And so the question before us is not *whether* they return." His smile, visible even at this distance, was gentle as a knife laid flat on a table. "The question is *who holds the leash.*"

The word went through the gathered pilgrims like current through water. Orsen felt it in her own chest, the awful seduction of it: after years of terror, a man offering not safety but *purpose*.

"A god unbound is a storm," Rell continued. "A god bound to the state is a wall. Seven walls, children. Seven living weapons, sworn to the new covenant, wielded by the one office that has spent five years proving it can hold divinity in check." He gestured, and four bearers brought forward what they'd carried wrapped in oilcloth: coils of black chain, and among them, mounted on a pole like a banner, a stake of pale wood banded in iron.

Orsen's sigil screamed.

Not burned — *screamed*, a silent pressure behind her eyes, and she knew that stake the way you know your own handwriting. She had shaped it. She had driven it. Five years ago, in another body, another name.

"You see?" Rell's voice dropped, intimate, and the acoustics of the drowned cathedral conspired to carry it anyway. "Even the instruments agree. The old binding was crude — a prison. What I offer is a *marriage*. Kneel, and be the generation that tamed heaven. Refuse—" and here his gentleness finally cracked, just at the edge, showing the furnace under the porcelain, "—and be remembered the way we remember everyone who stood between the Ministry and its purpose. Briefly."

* * *

It was Sefa who answered him.

The blind girl stepped out onto the rocks at the water's edge, small and straight-backed, and her voice — that strange, resonant voice that seemed to come from slightly to the left of her body — rang across the cavern without effort.

"You're holding the wrong end of the leash," she said.

A ripple of startled laughter ran through the pilgrims. Rell's head tilted, a connoisseur encountering an unexpected vintage.

"Child. You cannot even see the room."

"I can see what's under it better than you can." Sefa's face was serene and terrible. "You think they're lamps to be carried. They're *drowning men*, and you're standing on their hands asking them to swear fealty for air. I hear them, Inquisitor. All seven. And do you know what the loudest one is saying right now?" She paused, and Orsen watched the girl's expression flicker with something close to pity. "He's saying your name. He says you prayed to him once. When you were young. Before the Ministry took you. He remembers your voice."

For one heartbeat — Orsen counted it, later, in memory, one full heartbeat — Halvard Rell's composure failed. Something moved behind his eyes like a door blowing open onto a room he'd bricked shut decades ago. Then it closed, and what came through the gap instead was rage refined into architecture.

"Take the girl," he said quietly. "Take them all. Consecrate the ones who kneel. Burn the rest."

The lanterns dropped.

What happened next happened in layers, and Orsen's mind recorded them in fragments she would spend the rest of her life reassembling. The Silence-bearers began the counter-liturgy and the world went *muted*, prayers dying mid-word, Sefa's voice cut off like a snipped string. The crossbows of the escort loosed — Doss's bolt took a bearer through the throat and the man's lantern fell spinning into the black water, and where it sank the water *boiled* with released divinity, a column of gold light erupting upward with a sound like a struck bell the size of a mountain. The groan beneath the city became a roar. Cracks raced across the vaulted ceiling, shedding stone the size of carts.

"DOWN!" Orsen roared, hauling Sefa behind a fallen pillar as masonry annihilated the causeway where Rell had stood — but Rell was already moving, impossibly calm, walking through falling rock as though the universe had agreed in advance not to hit him.

Then the water rose.

Not waves — *presence*. The flooded streets of the dead capital lifted as one mass, a black wall ten feet high, and inside it Orsen saw shapes: faces, hands, the suggestion of vast limbs turning slowly, the imprisoned gods reaching up through their own drowning with whatever strength the breaking prison allowed. The wall hung there, trembling, and Orsen understood with total clarity that it was waiting. Looking for permission. Looking for *her* — for the sigil, for the maker of the stake, for the blade they had sworn five years ago and never unsworn.

"Vane!" Rell's voice cut through the chaos, and he was suddenly near, closer than battle should allow, flanked by six bearers whose counter-prayers pressed against her like deep water. His coat was torn; a line of blood crossed his cheek; he had never looked more alive. "Look at them! Look at what they are without us — flood, quake, avalanche, a country freezing from underneath! You've seen it! You of all people know what freedom costs!" He flung his arm toward the glowing depths. "Help me bind them properly and I will give you back your name. Vessa. Blade of Ossuin. There is a throne in the new covenant with your shape on it!"

"You read my file," Orsen said. Her voice came out flat and cold and very steady, and beneath her sleeve the sigil blazed so hot the linen began to smoke. "All the way to the bottom."

"To the last page."

"Then you know the last page isn't a name." She pulled the wrapping away, and the sigil rose into the air off her skin like a live ember, seven-pointed, burning gold-violet, and every Silence-bearer within thirty feet staggered as their counter-liturgy frayed against it. "It's a choice. And I already made it."

The wall of water came down.

It did not fall on the pilgrims. It fell on the Ministry column — guided, precise, furious — and the white lanterns went out by dozens, and the counter-silence ripped apart like wet paper, and in the sudden restored roar of the gods' breathing, the whole drowned cathedral began, at last and irreversibly, to collapse.

Through the thunder Orsen heard Rell still shouting — not retreating, *preaching*, bellowing consecrations over the ruin as his bearers dragged him back toward the far stair, chains clattering, the pale stake borne up above the fray like a standard. "THE COVENANT ENDURES!" he screamed, to no one, to everyone, to the seven drowning lights below. "KNEEL OR BURN — THE CHOICE IS STILL YOURS—"

Stone sheared away above the pilgrims' refuge. The floor tilted. Far below, through widening fissures, Orsen glimpsed it at last: the prison proper, a vast ring of ancient chains descending into light, and at its center, impaled through the heart of the deepest dark, the twin of the stake on Rell's banner — her stake, her work, her sin, glowing like a wound in the world.

And around it, straining against five years of iron, seven shapes the size of cathedrals turned their attention upward.

Every one of them looked at her.

"Sefa!" Orsen grabbed the girl's arm as the stairway ahead crumbled into rubble and the only way down opened where no way had been — a fissure breathing heat and gold. "The prison. Can they hear me down there?"

Sefa's ruined eyes were wide, streaming tears that might have been terror or joy. "They've never stopped," she said. "They're waiting for you to finish what you started."

The ground gave way, and Orsen Vane — Vessa, blade-sworn, maker of the stake — fell toward the heart of the gods.
