## 19. Nameless

The Door of Ash was not a door.

That was the first thing Orsen understood as she stood before it in the collapsing dark of the drowned capital — that every story she'd ever heard had lied about the shape of leaving. It was not a gate with hinges. It was not a wall with a gap. It was a standing absence, a vertical seam in the world where the air simply stopped agreeing to be air, and through it she could see — impossibly, terribly — a gray morning sky over a borderland of scrub pine and frost-killed grass, ordinary as bread, ordinary as grief.

Behind her, the prison of the gods was coming down.

She could feel it in her teeth. Seven voices rising through the stone like water through cracks, and the stone, which had held them for five years, deciding at last to let go. Chunks of the ceiling fell into black water and did not splash so much as *vanish*, swallowed by depths that no longer obeyed depth. Rell's soldiers were screaming somewhere in the upper galleries — or praying; from this distance the two sounded the same. The binding-stake lay at her feet, pulled free, still warm, still shaped exactly like the grip of her own hand.

Sefa stood beside her, blind eyes turned toward the Door, her face lit by a light Orsen couldn't see but could feel pressing against her skin like a hand.

"It's open," Sefa said. "It's been waiting for you a long time."

"Then it can wait a moment more." Orsen's voice came out rougher than she intended. She looked down at her forearm, where the sleeve had burned away during the battle, where the sigil of Ossuin blazed against her skin like a brand freshly struck — silver-white, alive, *awake* for the first time in five years and burning brighter now than it ever had in the old days, because its god was close, so close, rising through the drowning dark toward the surface of the world.

*Vessa.*

The name arrived not in her ears but in her bones, the way it always had, the way it had when she was nineteen and kneeling in a temple of black marble with her forehead to the floor while something vast and patient looked at her and chose her.

*Vessa, my blade. Vessa, my hand.*

"No," Orsen said aloud, to the god, to herself, to the girl she had buried in a shallow grave five years ago behind a fish-market in Corvane. "My name is Orsen."

The sigil flared. Pain, or joy — there was no telling them apart anymore.

*You pulled the stake,* Ossuin said, and his voice was everywhere now, in the water and the falling stone and the marrow of her. *You freed me. Come back to me. Be what you were made to be.*

"I was never made," she said. "I was *used*. There's a difference, and I've finally learned to spell it."

The ground shuddered. Somewhere above, the last of the Ministry's forces were fleeing upward through the collapsing tiers of the sunken city, and Rell — she didn't know if he lived, and found, to her own surprise, that she didn't much care; whatever he had become, he had stopped being a man somewhere around the moment he'd tried to put seven gods on leashes. The dark halls groaned. Water rushed in through wounds in the walls, and where it touched the freed divinity it turned to steam, to mist, to something that might have been rain falling upward.

Sefa touched her arm. Gentle. Always gentle, even now.

"He's asking you again," the girl said softly. "I can hear him. He's asking very loudly."

"I know what he's offering." Orsen watched the sigil burn. Power beyond anything she'd carried even at her height — the strength of a god unbound, poured without limit into his chosen blade. She could feel it waiting, an ocean held back by the thin membrane of her refusal. One word of assent and she would be more than she had ever been. She would be *Vessa* entire, remade, restored, a weapon worthy of a risen god in a world about to learn what risen gods meant.

One word.

"No," she said again, quieter this time, and meant it all the way down.

The sigil screamed.

---

Crossing the Door was like walking through the memory of fire.

Not heat — the *memory* of it, the shape of burning without the substance, and inside that shape everything she had been taken from her in careful, surgical pieces. She felt the sigil go first. It didn't fade; it was *unmade*, unwritten from her skin letter by letter, and each letter left was a small death — she heard herself cry out and did not recognize the voice. Then the strength, the god-given endurance that had let her fight for three days without sleep, drain out of her muscles like warmth from cooling iron until her knees buckled on ordinary grass and she went down hard on hands that shook like any mortal woman's.

And then — last, longest, worst —

*Vessa.*

The name peeled away. Not erased. *Buried*, returned to the grave she'd dug for it, and this time the grave was deep enough, this time the earth stayed packed over it, and what remained when the digging was done was —

Nothing.

Orsen knelt on the far side of the Door of Ash in the gray morning light of a country that existed, and she was no one at all.

She could see the seam of the Door behind her when she turned — a shimmer in the air, thinning even as she watched, because the Door of Ash was exit for mortals only, and the things now rising through the drowned capital were done with doors. The land beyond the border trembled. Far off to the south, past the hills, she saw the sky change color — a great slow unfurling of gold and storm-light, like dawn arriving from the wrong direction, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and opening its eyes.

The gods were coming up.

All seven of them. She could feel the absence of their pressure on her soul the way a deaf woman feels the absence of thunder — by the strange new quietness of the world, by the way the frost on the grass began, even now, even in the gray cold, to loosen and melt. Five years of unnatural winter ending in an hour. Five years of silence breaking like ice on a spring river.

She should have felt triumphant. She searched for it — turned the feeling over in her hollowed chest like a woman searching pockets — and found instead only grief, vast and clean and strangely painless, the grief of a funeral that is also a birth.

"They're beautiful," Sefa said.

The girl had crossed after her — of course she had; the Door wanted mortals, and Sefa was the most mortal thing Orsen had ever known. She stood with her face tipped up toward the southern sky she could not see, tears running freely down her cheeks, smiling.

"You can't see them," Orsen said. Her voice sounded strange. Thin. Unarmored.

"I don't need to." Sefa laughed, wet and wondering. "They're so *loud*. All of them. Even him — especially him. He's looking for you, Orsen. He's calling—"

"Don't." It came out sharper than she meant. She softened it with a breath. "Don't tell me what he's saying. Not today. Maybe not ever. I gave up the right to hear him when I walked through."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then, gently: "He isn't angry."

"That's worse," Orsen said, and sat down in the melting frost, because her legs had decided the matter for her.

---

They waited there, the two of them, while the world ended and began.

Others came through the Door throughout the morning — pilgrims from the scattered caravan, refugees who had followed rumors north for months, a handful of Yara's defectors blinking in the daylight like men surfacing from deep water. Each one crossed and stumbled and wept or laughed or both, and none of them looked twice at the gray-faced woman sitting in the wet grass, because none of them knew her. That was the miracle of it, Orsen thought. That was the price and the gift in a single wrapping. To everyone pouring out of the dying country, she was nobody. A tired woman by the roadside. The legend of the Sigil-Bearer, the vessel, the blade-sworn who freed the gods — that story belonged to the other side of the Door, and the Door did not carry stories across.

Tallow would have appreciated the irony. She thought of him holding the pass, buying their lives with his own, and had to press her fist against her mouth for a while.

The trembling in the land grew. Then, near midday, it stopped — all at once, like a held breath released — and the horizon to the south filled with light.

She saw them rise.

Not clearly. Not in any shape her mind could hold and keep. But she saw — everyone on the hillside saw — seven vastnesses ascending out of the drowned heart of the country, unfolding upward through cloud and storm-light, and the land beneath them thawing in widening rings like frost retreating off a windowpane. Rivers that had been frozen for five years cracked and ran. Birds — actual birds, she hadn't seen birds in Corvane in two years — rose startled from the pines.

And across all of it, brief and immense, she felt a gaze pass over the borderland. Over the refugees. Over her.

It paused on her. She knew it did. Something in the light lingered the way a hand lingers on a doorway.

*Goodbye, Vessa,* it seemed to say. And then, softer, in a tone she had never heard from him in all her years of service — a tone she might almost have called *grateful*: *Thank you, whoever you are.*

Then it moved on, southward, toward a world that would have to learn seven new-old names.

Orsen bowed her head and let herself cry — really cry, for Tallow, for the boy she'd healed and the hundreds she hadn't, for the village of ashes, for the young woman with the stake in her hands five years ago who had truly believed she was saving the world, for the god she had loved and chained and freed and lost, all in one life, all in one body too small to hold it.

Sefa sat down beside her and said nothing, which was the kindest sermon anyone had ever given her.

When the crying was done — and it took a long time — Orsen wiped her face on her ruined sleeve and noticed, distantly, that her forearm was bare. Smooth. Unmarked. No sigil. No scar where the sigil had been. Just skin, ordinary skin, pale in the gray light, belonging to nobody's god.

"Hungry?" Sefa asked.

"Starving," Orsen said, and discovered she was laughing, a little, helplessly. "Gods. I'm starving. I haven't been hungry in five years — I ate because the body needed it, but hungry—" She pressed a hand to her stomach like a woman confirming a wound had closed. "I'm actually hungry."

"There's a camp," Sefa said. "Down the valley. Smoke from the chimneys. People." She tilted her head, listening to something beyond hearing. "They're afraid of us. Of what followed us out. Someone should tell them the gods aren't angry either."

"Is that a job for a prophet?"

"I'm not a prophet." Sefa smiled. "I'm just a girl with good ears. But someone has to talk to them, and you have a face people trust when they're frightened."

"I have a face people *burned in effigy* three weeks ago."

"That was Vessa's face," Sefa said simply. "This one's different. This one's just tired."

Orsen looked at her — this blind child who heard gods and forgave them, who had walked through hell singing — and felt the first faint stirring of something she hadn't dared feel since Corvane: not hope exactly. Something smaller and sturdier. Curiosity, maybe. The question of what a person could be, starting from nothing.

"All right," she said. She got up. Her legs held. Ordinary legs, ordinary strength, and they held. "But I'm not leading anyone. I'm not leading *you*. We walk together or not at all."

"Together," Sefa agreed, and reached out, and Orsen took her hand.

They started down the valley through the melting snow, two small figures in a landscape busy with miracles, and behind them the Door of Ash thinned to a shimmer, then a rumor, then nothing at all. The country on the other side of it no longer officially existed. The woman walking away from it, for the first time in her life, officially existed as no one — no god's blade, no Ministry file, no legend, no name anyone owed or feared.

Just a tired woman, mending nothing, selling nothing, owing nothing.

Walking toward smoke, and supper, and whatever came next.
