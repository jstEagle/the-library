## 21. Small Charms, Honestly Sold

The stall had been Tallow's idea once, back when he was alive and full of schemes: *You've got hands that know knots and a face nobody trusts twice, Vane. That's a merchant.* She had laughed then, in the way you laugh at a dead man's jokes, and for two years she had not thought of it at all.

Now the stall stood at the east edge of the border camp, where the refugee road spilled down out of the pass and flattened into mud and trampled grass, and every morning she unfolded its canvas awning with the same small ritual: three pegs, two ropes, one pole that still bore a scorch mark from the night Rell's riders burned the pilgrim caravan's wagons. She had kept the pole. She could not have said why, except that some things deserved to go on being useful after everything else about them was ash.

Her wares lay on a plank table worn smooth by other people's winters: knotted cords dyed with madder root, river-stones drilled and strung, little tin medallions she hammered flat from melted-down buckles and spoons. Luck-charms. Road-charms. A charm against nightmares that worked, as far as she could tell, because holding something cool and smooth in your hand when you woke gave your fear somewhere to go. Nothing more than that. No god behind any of it. No sigil burning under her sleeve, because there was no sigil anymore — only a patch of skin on her left forearm, pale and ordinary, where the mark had been flayed away by nothing at all, by choice itself, by walking through a door made of ash.

She sold them honestly. That was the whole religion of it now. When a woman bought a charm for safe childbirth, Orsen told her plainly: *I don't know if this works. I tied the knot well, and I thought of you while I tied it, and sometimes that is enough.* When a man asked for protection on the road south, she sold him a good knife instead, because a knife was honest about what it was. The camp had learned to trust her precisely because she promised so little. In a country that had drowned under the weight of answered prayers, a woman who admitted her charms were only cord and stone had become, absurdly, the most credible merchant on the road.

Spring came late to the borderlands and all at once when it came. One week the ditches held grey ice; the next they were loud with meltwater and the first black flies. The mountains behind the camp had begun to give up their snow, and with it their dead — carts came down the pass most mornings bearing what the thaw uncovered, and the camp buried them in long rows above the river, and the rows grew, and the river ran high and cold and clean.

Orsen watched all of it from behind her table with a merchant's patience and a soldier's habits. She still woke before dawn. She still sat facing the door. Some part of her would always be counting exits, and she had stopped apologizing to herself for it.

* * *

The first traveler who recognized her came on a Tuesday, though there were no Tuesdays anymore worth keeping count of, so perhaps it was not a Tuesday; the days had gone soft at the edges since the gods rose. He was a drover, broad and sunburnt, leading six oxen up from the lowlands toward whatever passed for work these days. He stopped at her stall, looked at the charms, looked at her face, and went very still in the way men did when memory arrived uninvited.

"You're her," he said. "The Vessel. The one who pulled the stake."

"I'm a woman selling charms," Orsen said. "This one's for luck on the road. It's cord and a river-stone. Costs a copper."

He bought three. He paid with silver and refused change, and stood there turning the charm over in his huge hand as if it might speak. "They say the gods walked out of the ground because of you. They say the winter broke the same hour."

"The winter broke because winters break," Orsen said. "It was a hard year. They're shorter here. You'll see."

"They say—" He stopped. He was looking at her forearm, at the pale bare skin below her rolled sleeve, and his voice dropped into something almost tender. "They say you gave it up. All of it. To let them out."

Orsen tied his three charms together with a length of twine and pushed them across the table. "They say a lot of things," she said. "Most of it was Sefa. She's the one worth listening to. She preaches by the big fire on rest-days, if you want the truth of it instead of the rumor."

But the drover only shook his head slowly, the way men did when they had decided what to believe and wanted no corrections, and he touched his forehead to her — actually bowed, this grown man with his oxen and his road-dust, bowing to a tired woman behind a plank table — and led his animals on north.

Orsen sat back down on her stool. Her hands were steady. That surprised her a little. She picked up the next unfinished charm, a half-knotted cord, and worked at it until the shaking she hadn't noticed started stopped.

That was how it went, that first week of true spring. Word traveled the camp roads faster than carts. Travelers came who had known her legend the way you knew a song — half-remembered, mostly wrong, beloved anyway. A widow brought her daughters to look at the woman who had freed the gods, and the girls stared at Orsen's patched coat and cracked knuckles with open disappointment. A former Ministry clerk, gaunt and guilt-ridden, bought a nightmare-charm and whispered, *I processed the vessel lists, I didn't know, I didn't—* and Orsen told him, not unkindly, that everyone in that country had been holding a rope someone else was pulling, and that the only thing to do with a rope now was tie it to something useful. An old soldier pressed a coin into her hand and said Ossuin's name like a toast, and Orsen took the coin and said nothing, because there was nothing left in her that rose at the sound of that name except an ache like an old fracture in cold weather.

She was tired. That was the truth the legend-wearers never saw. They came looking for a burning sigil and found a woman with flour on her sleeves from the bread she baked before opening the stall, a woman whose great act of faith had consisted, in the end, of letting go — which looked, from the outside, like nothing at all.

* * *

Some days the market was quiet enough that her mind wandered where she did not want it to go.

She thought about the Door of Ash, or rather about the moment before it, the stake in her fist, Ossuin's voice like warm oil: *Leave me bound, Vessa. Rule beside me. I will make you what you were always meant to be.* And her own answer, which had not been words at all but simply her hand closing and pulling, the way a hand pulls a splinter from its own flesh. She thought about how the prison had come down around them, how Rell had screamed — actually screamed, that immaculate man, his living-church ambition collapsing into the dark with him — and how the last thing she had seen of her god was not gratitude but something older and stranger, relief maybe, or grief, the face of a drowning man finally allowed to drown.

She did not regret it. She checked, some mornings, the way you check a wound for fever. Still no regret. Only the enormous blank space where five years of purpose used to be, and in that space, gradually, things were growing: the stall, the bread, the knots, the small honest economy of copper coins and modest promises.

Tallow would have hated it. Tallow would have called it smallness. But Tallow had died holding a mountain pass so that strangers could live, and in his last hour he had gripped her wrist and said, gasping, laughing bloodlessly, *Look at me, Vane — biggest thing I ever did and it don't pay a damn* — and she understood now that he had not been complaining. He had been amazed.

* * *

On a bright morning with the meltwater roaring in the distance, Sefa came to the stall.

The blind girl — not a girl anymore, not quite; she was seventeen now and carried herself like a woman who had been listened to by thousands, because she had been — came down the market row without a guide, her stick tapping a rhythm everyone in the camp had learned to step around respectfully. She wore white, still, though the white was camp-white now, mended and grey at the hems. She stopped at the table and turned her face toward Orsen with that unnerving accuracy she had, seeing without eyes straight through to whatever was underneath.

"Your knots are getting better," Sefa said.

"My knots were always good."

"Your knots were desperate before. Now they're just knots." Sefa smiled. She reached across the table, palm up, an old habit between them, and Orsen put a finished charm in it — a simple one, cord and stone, the kind she sold for a copper. Sefa closed her fingers around it and held it to her ear as if listening. "For me?"

"For whoever needs it. Take it to your fire tonight. Tell them honestly what it is."

"I always do." Sefa turned the charm over. Her expression did something complicated. "They ask me about you, you know. Every rest-day. Half my congregation thinks you're a saint and the other half thinks you're a coward who wouldn't take up the mantle, and both halves are wrong, and I tell them so, and they don't listen, because people would rather have a saint or a coward than a woman selling charms." She paused. "Ossuin asks too."

Orsen's hands went still on the table. "You hear him still."

"Every day. All seven of them, fainter now, further away — they're going wherever gods go when they're free, and it's far, and it's good, I think, whatever it is. But him especially. He lingers." Sefa's blind eyes were aimed slightly past Orsen's shoulder, at the mountains, at the drowned country beyond them. "He says your name. Not Vessa. Not the blade-name. He says—" she frowned, hunting for it, "—he says the name you'll choose. He says he can hear it coming, the way you hear weather."

"He should have thought of that before he went silent for five years," Orsen said, too quickly, and Sefa laughed — the bright, unbothered laugh that had survived massacres and avalanches and the building of a new faith, and that Orsen privately considered the single most miraculous thing the gods had ever done.

"He isn't sorry," Sefa said gently. "I want you to know that. Whatever he was, chained — he isn't sorry you pulled it. He says the starving years taught him what the fat centuries never could. He says—" and here she tilted her head, listening to something Orsen could not hear and did not entirely wish to, "—he says a god who has been hungry knows what bread is worth. He says thank you for the bread."

Orsen looked down at her table. At the cords and stones and hammered tin. At her own hands, scarred, competent, belonging to no god and no country and no name she hadn't chosen.

"Tell him," she said at last, "that the bread costs a copper. Like anyone's."

Sefa grinned and pocketed the charm and tapped away down the row, and the market noise closed over her like water.

* * *

The season turned properly. The camp grew gardens. Someone built a shrine by the river that was really just a flat stone and a wind-bell, and somehow that was enough, and somehow that was the point. Ministry survivors drifted through occasionally — the organization had dissolved into shame and paperwork — and none of them looked at Orsen twice, because the sigil was gone and the Vessel was gone and what remained was a woman of perhaps forty with a merchant's squint and a baker's forearms.

One evening near the end of the week she stayed late at the stall, re-inking her little price signs, and watched the light go gold and then violet over the pass she had crossed in winter with four companions and come out of with two. Somewhere up there Tallow lay under snow that was finally, after everything, melting. She raised her cup of tea to the north. *Biggest thing you ever did,* she thought. *Don't pay a damn. Worth everything.*

She was blowing out her lamp when she heard small footsteps on the board path — quick, light, hesitant. A child came around the awning post: a boy of six or seven, thin in the way camp children were thin, wearing a coat cut down from an adult's, clutching something in one fist. He stopped at the edge of the lamplight, gathering courage.

"We're closing," Orsen said, not unkindly.

The boy held out his fist anyway. In it, gleaming dully, was a copper coin — worn nearly smooth, older than he was, the kind of coin that had been loved and spent and saved and spent again through the whole dying of a country.

"I need a charm," he said. His voice wobbled and steadied. "My mama's having the baby tomorrow and Papa says it's going to be hard and I heard you're the one who mends luck."

Orsen looked at the coin. Then at the boy's face, solemn and terrified and hopeful, lit gold at the edges by her guttering lamp. Behind him the camp fires were coming alight one by one down the valley, hundreds of them, thousands, a country of the countryless kindling itself against the dark — and somewhere far off, faint as weather on the horizon, she thought she heard a voice that might have been a god saying a name she hadn't chosen yet.

She reached out and took the coin.

"Well," said the woman who had been Vessa, who had been the Vane, who had been no one at all for a whole winter, "let's see what we can do."
