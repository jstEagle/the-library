## 22. Ines

Spring came to the borderland the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once.

One week the refugee camp was a sea of gray canvas and grayer faces, mud frozen into ruts that broke ankles. The next week the ruts ran with meltwater, someone had planted onions in a barrel by the well, and children were everywhere, shrieking through the lanes like sparrows, as if they had been waiting all winter for permission.

Orsen watched them from behind her stall and did not know what to do about it.

The stall itself was nothing. A plank across two barrels, a strip of faded canvas for shade, a wooden box lined with wool where her wares sat in neat rows: charms, honestly made. Knots tied three times against bad luck. Beads of river-glass polished smooth for travelers who wanted safe roads. Small copper discs stamped with no sigil at all, because there was no sigil left in her hands to stamp, sold only as what they were — pretty things, warm things, things a person could hold onto when the night got long.

No god behind any of it. That was the whole point. She said so to every customer, plainly, the way Tallow used to say *no refunds* — and somehow, in this camp of people who had lost gods and countries and names, honesty turned out to be the best sales pitch she'd ever had.

"You're the one," people whispered anyway. "You're the one who opened the Door."

"I'm the one who sells knots," she would answer, and hold out her hand for the coin.

It had been four months since she walked out of the dying country nameless. Four months since the stake came free in her grip and the world beneath the world tore open like a seam, since Ossuin rose out of his prison roaring light, since Rell's empire of silence came down around him like a house of cards in a draft. Four months since she crossed the Door of Ash and felt everything he had ever given her peel away — the strength, the sigil, the burning certainty, the name Vessa that had never truly been hers — until what stepped out onto the far side of the border was just a tired woman with scarred hands and an empty sleeve where a god used to live.

She missed it. She would be lying if she told herself otherwise. Some mornings she woke reaching for the weight of the blade-sworn power the way an amputee reaches for a limb, and found only her own small human strength, barely enough to lift a crate of beads. And every morning she woke free, and the two facts lived together in her chest like strangers forced to share a bed, and neither one ever quite got comfortable.

But the thaw kept coming. The land behind the Door — the country that officially no longer existed, though its people kept walking out of it — was turning green at the edges. The rivers that had run black with the gods' death throes ran clear now. And once, from the high ground above the camp, Orsen had seen it: far to the south, past the broken passes, a figure of light standing astride a mountain ridge like a man standing up from a long sleep, stretching, shaking winter off his shoulders.

Ossuin. Her god. Free.

He had not looked for her. Or if he had looked, he had not found anything worth finding — because there was nothing left in her of Vessa, nothing left for him to claim. She had made sure of that at the Door. She had given up the sigil, the strength, the sworn name, everything he could have used to call her back into service.

What remained was hers. All of it. Even the tiredness. Especially the tiredness.

* * *

The child came on a Tuesday, though nobody in the camp kept track of Tuesdays anymore except the old priest from the eastern parishes, who rang a cracked bell out of habit and called it keeping time for God, whoever God was now that seven of them walked abroad.

Orsen noticed the girl before the girl reached the stall, because you learned to notice children in a place like this — which ones were orphaned, which ones were hungry, which ones were the dangerous kind of quiet. This one was none of those, or not only those. She was maybe eight, gap-toothed, hair braided by someone who loved her but not skillfully, and she approached the stall the way children approach anything that frightens and fascinates them: sideways, in a slow orbit, pretending great interest in a display of river-glass beads while working up her courage.

Orsen let her orbit. She'd sold enough charms to know when to speak and when to wait.

Finally the girl planted herself in front of the stall, chin up, the way someone much older must have coached her, and slapped a coin on the plank. It was a copper from the old country — Corvane mint, the silent years, the face worn nearly blank.

"For my mama," the girl said. "Something so she stops crying at night. Do you have something for that?"

The question went through Orsen like a knife through wet cloth. She thought of a fisherman five years ago, frightened, buying a luck-charm from a woman whose god had gone quiet, paying with coins just like this one. She thought of a boy who had lived because a dead sigil flared back to life at the worst possible moment. She thought of everyone she had failed to save between then and now, which was a longer list than the ones she'd saved, and always would be.

"No charm stops crying," Orsen said gently. "Anyone who tells you different is lying, and I don't sell lies."

The girl's face began to crumple.

"But." Orsen held up one finger. "I have this."

She reached into the box and took out a knot-charm — cord wound three times and bound, plain brown wool, no bead, no sigil, nothing but the work of her own two hands. "This is for holding onto. When the nights get bad, you hold this, and you squeeze it, and you think about one good thing that happened that day. One thing. Even a small one. Even if it's just that the bread didn't burn." She leaned forward. "It doesn't work magic. But your hands need somewhere to go when your heart is too heavy, and this gives them somewhere. And sometimes —" she tapped the plank, once — "sometimes that's enough to get to morning. And enough mornings in a row, and your mama finds out she's stronger than she thought. That's not a charm's doing. That's hers. But the knot helps her remember it."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate weighing testimony. Then she picked up the knot, squeezed it once, hard, and nodded as if a contract had been signed.

"That'll do," she said.

"Good. Because that's the honest price." Orsen pushed the copper back across the plank. "Keep your coin. First one's free to anyone who asks the right question."

"What's the right question?"

And here it was. Orsen had known it was coming — had felt it building in the girl's orbit around the stall, in the way the other children had been whispering near her market spot for weeks, in the way adults looked at her and never quite asked. Everyone in the camp knew the legend. The woman who pulled the stake. The blade who unmade the prison. The vessel who gave up her god. They called her the Nameless Blade, the Saint of the Door, the One Who Chose — half a dozen names invented by strangers, none of them hers, all of them wrong.

Everyone wanted to know her name. No one had dared ask.

A child asked, because children are the only honest people left after a war.

"What's the right question?" the girl repeated.

"The right question," Orsen said, "is the one you just asked without knowing it. *Who are you?* Not what are you, not what did you do. Who."

The girl frowned. "So who are you?"

Orsen looked at the coin on the plank. Old country mint. Worn face. A face rubbed almost smooth by years of passing hand to hand through a nation that had tried to erase itself, and failed, because its people kept carrying it across borders in their pockets.

Her mother had carried a coin like that. Her mother, in the years before the temples, before the swearing, before a god named Ossuin had looked down at a farm girl with steady hands and said *you will be my blade*, and everyone had started calling her Vessa as if Ines had never existed at all. Her mother had called her something else. Something small and warm, said over a kitchen table, said in the dark of a fever-night, said last of all on a platform with soldiers everywhere, pressed into her ear like a secret being smuggled out of a falling country:

*Whatever they make you, whatever they call you — you come home to this.*

"Ines," she said.

It came out rusty. It came out true.

"My mother's name for me. Before any god claimed me. Before the war, before the prisons, before any of it." She smiled, and felt her face do something unfamiliar with the shape of it — not the merchant's smile, not the blade's flat calm. Just a woman's smile, on a spring morning, giving a child her name. "My name is Ines."

The girl repeated it, tasting it, decided it was acceptable, tucked the knot-charm into her pocket next to the returned coin, and ran off toward the onion barrel shouting a name to her friends — *Ines, her name is Ines* — as if announcing the discovery of a new color.

Orsen stood behind her plank-and-barrels stall in a refugee camp at the edge of a dissolved nation, and discovered that a name could weigh nothing and everything at once.

* * *

Sefa found her at closing.

The blind girl — not a girl anymore, really; the winter had put ten years on her and taken the softness out of her voice — came down the market lane with her white staff clicking on the boards, and the crowd parted for her the way water parts for a keel. People still touched her sleeves as she passed. Some still wept. She bore it the way she bore everything now, patiently, like a woman carrying water uphill, and only Orsen saw how heavy the yoke had gotten.

"They're asking for you again," Sefa said, settling onto the barrel beside the stall without waiting for invitation. Old habit. "The congregation. They want the Saint of the Door to speak at the spring rite."

"There is no Saint of the Door. There's a woman who sells knots."

"Mm." Sefa tilted her face toward the sun, which was going down over the tent-lines in bands of gold and rust. "You know I can hear them. The gods. You've never asked me what they say about you."

"Because I don't want to know."

"Liar." But it was gentle. "They say very little, actually. Freed gods are quieter than bound ones — did you know that? Ossuin spends most of his time watching rivers thaw. He says —" Sefa paused, listening to some frequency Orsen had lost forever at the Door, and her expression softened into something complicated. "He says the blade who freed him owes him nothing, and he is prouder of her than he has words for, and he will never ask her for anything again. He says that last part every day. I think he says it to himself."

Orsen busied her hands with the charm-box, latching it, straightening rows that were already straight. "Tell him thank you. Tell him —" Her throat closed. She worked it loose. "Tell him the knot helped. Tell him I got to morning."

"I'll tell him." Sefa reached out and found Orsen's wrist with unerring accuracy, blind eyes aimed at the middle distance, and gripped it once, hard, the way you grip a rope. "Stay for the rite, Ines. Not to speak. Just to stand there. They need to see that a person can give up a god and keep living. That's the sermon nobody else can give. You don't even have to open your mouth."

"I'll think about it."

"You won't. But you'll come. I've heard you lie better than that." Sefa stood, clicked her staff once against the plank — a blessing, or a knock on a door — and moved off down the lane into the gold light, and people reached out to touch her sleeves, and she let them.

Orsen latched the box. Folded the canvas. Loaded the barrels onto the little cart she'd bought off a widow in March, and hauled it through the lanes toward the small room she rented above the cooper's shed, where the evening smelled of wood shavings and someone's supper and the far-off green smell of a world remembering itself.

At the corner by the well she stopped. The high ground was there, past the last row of tents, and beyond it the southern horizon, where the mountains of the drowned country stood black against the sunset. Somewhere out there, a god was watching rivers thaw. Somewhere out there, a nation that officially did not exist was quietly refusing to stop existing, one returning farmer at a time. Somewhere out there, the Door of Ash stood open in its ruin, letting people through in both directions now — refugees going out, and lately, in ones and twos, the stubborn going home.

She thought about going home herself. Not to serve. Not to rule beside anything. Just to walk the thawed roads of the country that had made her, with no sigil on her arm and no name in anyone's records, selling honest charms from a cart, sleeping in barns, answering to no one.

Ines. Her mother's name for her. Hers to carry now, chosen at a market stall by a gap-toothed child's question, belonging to no god, no ministry, no legend, no one —

Hers.

She turned toward the sunset, and the thawing world, and tomorrow.

Behind her, faintly, the cracked bell rang the hour, and a child's voice carried down the lane, telling her friends, delighted, breathless, certain to spread it to every tent by morning:

"—and her name is *Ines*."
