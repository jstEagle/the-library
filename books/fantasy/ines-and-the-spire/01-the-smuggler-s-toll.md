## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

The wind in Thornfell spoke first, as it always did, rattling down the pass like a herald with bad news. Ulyana Crane stood at the head of the caravan and listened to it the way other people read weather: the gusts came from the east tonight, which meant the crown's patrols had shifted their route, which meant she had perhaps two hours before the road below filled with lantern-light and questions.

"Keep them moving," she said to Marta, her second, a broad-shouldered woman who could carry a mule over her back if the mood took her. "No fires past the split rock. No singing. If anyone asks, we're wool merchants out of Drenn Hollow."

"We *are* wool merchants out of Drenn Hollow," Marta said.

"Then it should be easy to remember." Ulyana pulled her hood up and turned back to face the refugees strung along the mountain road behind her — forty-one souls, by her count, though the count changed nightly. A family of tanners with soot still ground into their knuckles. An old priest who had renounced nothing but would not say what he'd fled. Two sisters carrying everything they owned in a wheelbarrow that squeaked like a dying bird.

Ten years she had walked this road, and ten years she had kept the one rule that mattered: *never use the gift*. Not for coin. Not for kindness. Not even once, not even a whisper of an oath, because swordspeakers were burned now, and the crown paid silver for names, and Ulyana Crane's name was worth more than most.

She felt the bound soul before she understood why.

It came to her the way a wrong note comes to a musician — through the soles of her boots, up through her teeth. The caravan was a river of small truths around her: fear, exhaustion, hope worn thin as gauze. But one thread among them was *knotted*. Someone at the rear of the column was wrapped in magic so dense and so old that her gift flinched away from it like a hand from a hot stove.

She stopped walking. The wheelbarrow squeaked past her. She let it.

At the tail of the caravan, a soldier sat on a boulder, waiting for the stragglers to catch up. He wore no colors, carried a plain sword, and had the kind of face you forgot while looking at it — brown hair, brown eyes, a scar across the jaw that might have been from a fall or a knife. Ordinary. Deliberately ordinary, she realized, because she had watched him walk all day and he moved like a man pretending to move like a soldier, which was its own kind of tell.

And beneath the ordinary skin, something screamed.

Ulyana had cut bindings off cursed blades and oath-breakers' tongues. She knew the texture of compulsion, the sour taste of a spell that held a person the way a fist holds a bird. This was worse. This was layered — three spells deep, maybe four — and the outermost layer was silence. Whoever he was, he could not say what he was. Perhaps he could not even think it clearly. The binding had been woven by someone patient and very, very good.

*Walk away*, said the voice of ten careful years. *He is someone else's catastrophe.*

She walked toward him instead.

"You're favoring your left side," she said, sitting on the boulder beside him. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and road dust, and his hands were wrong for a common soldier — too fine-boned, nails kept short out of habit rather than labor.

"It's nothing."

"Nothing doesn't bleed through a boot." She nodded at the dark stain at his heel. "There's a stream two bends ahead. I'll look at it there."

"I can manage."

"I'm sure you can." She stood, and made the mistake of meeting his eyes. They were gray, actually, not brown — the glamour over him was thinning with fatigue — and behind the dullness the binding imposed, something in them looked at her with desperate, animal attention. Like a man drowning who has just noticed the shore.

Her gift pressed against the inside of her ribs, wanting out. She clamped down on it the way she had every day for a decade.

*Not my problem,* she told herself, and did not believe herself, and hated that.

* * *

The stream ran cold enough to hurt. She knelt in the shallows and peeled the boot away from a gash along his calf — a blade wound, recent, badly stitched. He hissed but didn't pull away.

"Who did this to you?" she asked, meaning the leg, and watched his face do something complicated.

"A man I used to trust," he said. And then, as if the words had been dragged out of him by hooks: "I don't— I can't—" His jaw locked. His eyes went glassy. The binding caught him mid-sentence like a door slamming, and she saw the exact moment the spell took hold of his tongue, saw the terror flicker under the numbness when he realized he'd tried to speak and failed.

Ulyana's hands went still in the water.

Every instinct she had honed for ten years told her to finish the bandage, point him toward Vess, and never learn his name. But she had spent the whole day listening to that knotted thread of a soul, and now she had heard it strain against its leash, and there are some sounds a person cannot unhear.

"If I ask you nothing," she said quietly, "can you nod?"

He nodded.

"Were you running *from* the crown?"

A pause. Then, slowly, deliberately: he shook his head *no* — and the relief that broke across his ruined, ordinary face nearly undid her. Not fleeing the crown. Fleeing *to* somewhere, or *from* something else entirely, and the crown's hounds were the least of what hunted him.

"Saints' teeth," she breathed. "What are you?"

His mouth opened. The binding slammed shut. But his eyes — his eyes answered anyway, and what she saw in them was grief so vast and so familiar that it looked almost like her own reflection.

Lantern-light bloomed around the bend of the pass.

* * *

They came up the road in a wedge of six, cloaks the color of wet slate, white cords knotted at their throats. Witch-finders. Ulyana had seen them twice in ten years and both times from very far away. They carried no torches; the lead finder held a lantern of green glass, and within it a moth of pale light beat against the walls — a truth-snare, a snarl of woven intent that would drag every hidden thing into the open. Lies would glow on the liar's skin. Concealed oaths would smoke. Bound souls would shine like beacons.

Forty-one refugees froze on the road. Somewhere behind Ulyana, the wheelbarrow began, horribly, to squeak.

"Papers," said the lead finder. Her voice was pleasant, which was the worst thing about it. "Names, origins, purposes. The snare will do most of the work. We're only here to write things down."

Ulyana stepped forward, heart hammering a smuggler's rhythm — *be dull, be dull, be dull* — and produced the caravan's forged manifest with steady hands. Around her, the green light swept the line of refugees. A tanner's lie about his brother glowed faintly on his cheek and was waved through. The old priest's half-truth shimmered and faded. One by one they passed, and Ulyana counted the finder's steps toward the rear of the column, toward the boulder, toward the soldier whose whole body had gone rigid as a drawn bow.

Three steps. Two.

The snare's light touched him.

He lit up like a struck bell — not the soft glow of a lie but a blaze, layer upon layer of shining script erupting across his skin, spells answering spells, and the lead finder turned with an expression of slow, delighted hunger.

"Well," she said. "Hello, little secret."

The finders moved. The refugees scattered like startled birds. And Ulyana Crane stood in the cold water of the stream with a bloody bandage in her hand and ten years of silence pressing down on her like a tomb lid — and heard, beneath the green glare, that bound soul scream again, wordless, *help me*.

She drew her sword.

The steel sang as it cleared the scabbard, and the old words rose in her throat like floodwater breaking a dam, and she did not stop them.

"*By edge and by oath, by the truth in this blade: I cut what binds you. Be free.*"

Light detonated down the length of the sword. The outermost spell — the silence — tore apart with a sound like ice cracking on a lake, and the soldier gasped, doubling over, sucking in air as if he'd been underwater for years. The finders shouted. The green lantern shattered in the lead finder's grip.

And Ulyana stood exposed in the ruin of her decade, sword blazing, name burning its way back into the world — while the man beside her lifted his head, freed of one chain if not the rest, and said in a raw, rusted voice:

"They call me Aldric."

The name hit the pass like thunder. *Prince Aldric*, dead these five years, mourned in taverns and hanged in effigy by the crown that swore he'd never existed.

Above them, on the ridge, horns began to sound.

"Up," Ulyana snarled, hauling him to his feet. "We're gone before the second horn. And Aldric — whatever you are — you'd better be worth dying for."

He looked at her, gray-eyed and shaking, and said, "I'm not. But I'm all there is."

They ran north, into the dark heart of Thornfell, with the horns singing behind them.
