## 2. What Wears the Crown

The horns faded by midnight, but Aldric's ears kept ringing with them long after. He had learned, in five years of wearing another man's face and another man's silence, that some sounds never leave you. The horn that had sounded the night the binding was forged still lived behind his eyes. These new ones would join it.

They climbed through Thornfell's black ribs while the moon hid itself like a coward. Ulyana moved ahead of him, always three paces, always listening — a woman shaped by a decade of running from her own gift. He watched the way she placed her feet, heel to toe, silent even on scree, and understood that she had been hunted longer than he had been hidden.

"Talk," she said, without turning.

"I don't—"

"You've been chewing on something since the pass. I can hear it grinding. Talk, or trip over it later when it matters."

He almost laughed. It came out as a cough. "You want to know what it's like." He flexed his hands in the dark, hands that had held a spear for a garrison he couldn't name, under officers whose faces slid off his memory like rain off slate. "It's like being a room where someone else lives. You see through the windows. You hear what's said. But the doors are painted on. You press your palms against them and nothing opens."

Ulyana stopped at a shelf of rock and looked back at him. In the starlight her gray eyes were nearly colorless. "Who forged it?"

"A sorcerer. I never saw a face. I saw hands — old hands, ink-stained. And my father's voice saying yes." His throat closed around the word. "That's the part I can't stop hearing. Not the spell. The *yes*."

She said nothing for a long moment. Below them, wind combed the pine forest like fingers through dirty hair.

"My mother sold bread," Ulyana said at last. "When the crown outlawed swordspeaking, they burned our house with her in it because she wouldn't give up my name. I was fourteen. I've hidden ever since. Ten years of being no one." She turned and climbed again. "So don't tell me about doors, prince. I know every kind there is."

He followed her into the dark, and for the first time since waking inside a soldier's body, felt less alone than afraid.

* * *

Vess announced itself two days later as a smell — woodsmoke, river mud, frying onions, and beneath it all the iron tang of a city that minted its own coins and answered to no crown. The free city. The last place in the nine provinces where a witch-finder needed a warrant and a warrant needed a bribe.

They came down off the hills at dusk, and Ulyana cut his hair with a knife against her knee in an alley off Tanner's Row, so that the soldier's face he wore would read as vagrant rather than deserter. She bought them cloaks with frayed hems and taught him to walk like a man with nowhere to be.

"Rule one," she murmured, threading his arm through hers like a lover's, though her grip said *prisoner*. "In Vess, everyone is selling something. Most of them are selling you."

"And rule two?"

"Don't make me need one."

They found a room above a dye-house, where the reek of mordant covered their scent from any hound the crown might send, and it was there, on the third morning, that Aldric saw the proclamation.

It hung outside the chancery on Copperlane, nailed to a board scarred by a hundred older notices, and the crowd around it was thick enough that he had to shoulder through to see why. A herald stood on the steps reading aloud in a bored voice: *By grace of the Radiant Throne, King Edran Crane-Rightful, Protector of the Nine Provinces, declares amnesty for all who lay down arms within the month—*

Aldric stopped hearing the words.

Because the face on the proclamation was his father's.

Not a likeness. Not a herald's idealized portrait, all jaw and laurel. It was Edran — *his* Edran, the man who had taught him to hold a sword with his thumb along the flat, who had wept openly at his mother's funeral, who had once carried Aldric four miles through a snowstorm on his shoulders singing bawdy songs badly. The artist had caught the crease between the brows, the scar through the left eyebrow from a boar hunt, the way the mouth turned down at the corner as if perpetually about to say something wry.

His father's face, rendered in loving detail, above the words KING EDRAN.

"That's impossible," he heard himself say. His voice sounded very far away. "My father is dead. They told me — after the binding, they let me believe—" His hand rose toward the paper and Ulyana caught his wrist, quick as a snare closing.

"Not here," she breathed. But her eyes had gone to the proclamation too, and he saw something move behind them — recognition, then horror, then a cold fury he had not seen in her before.

"You know this face," he said.

"I know it's wrong." Her grip tightened. "Look again, Aldric. Really look."

He looked. And slowly, the way a beautiful room reveals rot when the light shifts, he saw it. The crease between the brows was there but too deep, carved rather than lived. The scar sat a half-inch too high. The mouth's wry turn had become something else — a sneer held carefully in place, the expression of a thing that had studied a smile the way a crow studies a lock. His father's face worn like armor over something that did not fit inside it.

"It's wearing him," Aldric whispered. "It's not impersonating him. It's *wearing* him. He's alive. He's in there, behind his own face, and that thing walks around in him and signs his name and—" The grief he had carried for five years, the clean grief of mourning, curdled in his chest into something hotter and far less merciful. "I mourned a living man. I lit candles for a prisoner."

"Aldric." Ulyana turned him by the shoulder, away from the crowd, away from the paper bearing his father's stolen face. "Listen to me. If the usurper wears Edran, then the binding isn't just on you. It's a cage with two doors. What holds you holds him."

"Then we go to the Spire." The words came out of him steady and strange, as if someone else were speaking — no, as if, for the first time in five years, *he* were speaking, the prince the soldier's body had buried. "Where it was forged. You said oaths bind steel to truth. Then unbind it. Cut me loose, and whatever's holding my father comes apart with me."

"The Spire is the crown's fortress. It's guarded by—"

"By men obeying a king who is himself a prisoner." He smiled, and felt how little joy was in it. "Let them take orders from a hollow man. Let the whole rotten thing stand on a lie. We pull the lie out and watch it fall."

For a moment Ulyana searched his face, looking, he realized, for the trembling gray-eyed boy from the pass. Whatever she found instead made her exhale slowly through her nose.

"There's a smuggler I trust," she said. "Oldest friend I have. He knows the roads under the Spire better than the masons who built them. We'll need him."

"Then send for him."

"I will." She hesitated — the first hesitation he'd seen in her — and glanced once more at the proclamation, at the king who was not a king, at the crowd murmuring its easy relief at the promise of amnesty. When she spoke again her voice was low, meant only for him.

"But understand what you're asking, Aldric. To break a binding forged at the Spire, I'll have to speak an oath there — a true one, spoken whole. Swordspeakers get one great oath in a lifetime. Mine's been waiting ten years, and if I spend it on you, it's gone. No second forging. No gift. Just a woman with a sword and a bad temper." Her gray eyes held his. "So before I spend my life's one truth on yours — you have to be certain. Certain enough to bet both our fathers' ghosts on it."

Above them, the herald droned on in his bored voice, promising peace in a dead man's name. Aldric looked at his father's hollowed face one last time, and felt the grief in his chest harden into something that could be carried — heavy, but carryable, the way a blade is heavy only until you learn its balance.

"I spent five years pressing my hands against painted doors," he said. "I'm done knocking. Open them or burn them down — either way, we're going through."

Ulyana held his gaze a moment longer. Then she pulled her hood up against the morning cold.

"Then pray your friend Bren answers fast," she said. "Because the moment I send word, the clock starts. And witch-finders keep time better than anyone alive."
