## 3. The Traitor's Road

The Spire showed itself at dusk, the way it always did — all at once, as if the mountain had been holding its breath.

Ulyana reined in at the ridge and let the horse blow beneath her. Below, the valley of Vael's Hollow opened like a cupped hand, and at its center rose the Spire: a needle of white stone stitched with old gold, older than the kingdom, older than the oaths it had been built to hold. Snow smoked off its crown in the last light. Ten years since she'd seen it. Ten years of keeping her mouth shut and her gift buried like a knife wrapped in oilcloth, and now she was riding straight back to the one place where speaking could kill her.

Beside her, Aldric sat his borrowed body like a man wearing someone else's coat. He'd stopped flinching at his own reflection three days ago. Progress, she supposed. The soldier's face he wore was plain, forgettable — brown hair going gray at the temples, a scar through one brow that belonged to some dead man whose name they'd never learn. But when Aldric looked at the Spire, something moved behind those eyes that no spell could disguise. Recognition. Hunger. Grief.

"That's where I was made into this," he said quietly.

"That's where we unmake it." Ulyana nudged her horse down the switchback. "Stay behind me until we know the ground."

"You always say that."

"Because you never listen."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost-smile, but she'd learned to hoard them the way a miser hoards coin. Somewhere between Thornfell and here — somewhere in the dark of a waystation with his head on her shoulder while fever shook him, somewhere in the fight at the ford when she'd cut two witch-finders out of their truth-snare and felt her oath burn bright and terrible in her throat — she had stopped pretending to herself about what he was to her. She hadn't said it. There would be time after. There was always time after, until there wasn't.

The valley floor was wrong.

She felt it before she saw it — a hush in the air, the particular silence of ground that has been listened to too carefully. Ulyana raised a fist. Aldric halted. Ahead, the pilgrim road ran clean and empty toward the Spire's outer gate, and the snow lay unbroken on either side, and not one crow sat in the bare trees.

"Bren," she breathed.

Aldric went still. "You're certain?"

She was. She'd known since the waystation at Coldmere, since the innkeeper mentioned riders asking after a woman and a soldier traveling east. Since Bren's letter — *meet me at the threshold, I have news worth the detour* — arrived two days early, which Bren's letters never were. Bren was many things: drunk, sentimental, the only soul alive who remembered Ulyana before the outlawry, before she'd buried her voice and her name together. Bren was also afraid. And fear, given enough coin or enough threat, always found its price.

"He sold us," Ulyana said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew how badly it hurt. "Amnesty. It's what they offer people like us. Our heads for our freedom."

"Ulyana—"

"Don't." She turned her horse off the road, into the treeline. "Don't defend him. Don't tell me he had no choice. Let me hate him cleanly, just for tonight."

They came at the Spire from the north, through the pilgrims' wood, leading the horses by the bridle through snow that squeaked like new leather. The trees thinned. Moonlight fell through bare branches in long silver blades, and at the edge of the wood the ground opened onto the threshold — a crescent of ancient flagstones where the road met the Spire's outer wall, ringed by standing stones carved with words no living tongue spoke. Oath-stones. Her ancestors' work. The binding that held Aldric had been forged somewhere above, but everything began here, at the door.

Bren stood alone among the stones.

Ulyana's heart did something stupid and hopeful anyway — looked for an escape in the set of his shoulders, a warning in his stance. Then the moonlight shifted, and she saw the witch-finders' lanterns kindling in the dark beyond him, one by one, like eyes opening. A dozen men in gray. Crossbows. And behind them, at the tree line, something else: a figure in a hooded cloak that didn't move quite right, joints bending a half-beat late, like a puppet remembering its strings.

"Ulyana." Bren's voice carried across the snow, cracked and raw. He hadn't shaved. His sword hung loose in his grip, point-down, the way a man holds a blade he doesn't want to use. "I'm sorry. God help me, I'm sorry. They had my daughter's name on a list. You understand. You of all people understand what it is to have one person you can't lose."

"I understand," Ulyana called back, and drew her sword. The steel sang — a low, true note, the sound of a blade that had heard oaths all its life. "I understand exactly."

Movement at the edge of her vision: Aldric, stepping forward, and the hooded figure peeling away from the witch-finders to meet him. As it walked, the hood fell back, and Ulyana heard Aldric make a sound she would carry to her grave.

Queen Maren's face looked out from under the hood. Older than Aldric remembered her, surely — worn smooth and strange, like a river stone — but hers. Her smile was gentle. Her eyes were the color of nothing at all.

"Aldric," the thing wearing his mother said fondly. "You've come home. Come, let mother look at you."

"No," Aldric whispered. But his feet were moving, and Ulyana understood with cold clarity that the binding lived in that face, that the usurper's sorcerer had dressed itself in the one shape that could walk him anywhere. She couldn't follow. She couldn't help. Because Bren was already coming, sword rising, tears freezing on his cheeks, and the witch-finders were spreading wide to cut off retreat, and there was nothing left in the world but the space between her blade and her oldest friend's.

They met among the oath-stones.

Bren fought the way he always had — patient, economical, left foot forward, testing with the point before committing. Twenty years of shared roads lived in his movements; she knew every feint before he made it, and he knew hers, and that was the horror of it. They danced a dance they'd invented together, and every step of it was a betrayal.

"You should have run," he grunted, parrying her cut. "When I wrote. I gave you the out. I told you to come alone."

"You told me to bring the prince to the threshold." Steel rang against steel. "That's not mercy, Bren. That's bait."

"It was both!" His voice broke on it. He pressed her hard, three cuts in quick succession, driving her back against a standing stone. Up close she could see what the amnesty had cost him already — the gray in his face, the tremor in his sword hand. Guilt is a poison; it kills the guilty slowest. "What was I supposed to do? Let them take Sera? You'd have done the same. Don't tell me you wouldn't have done the same."

Ulyana caught his blade on hers, twisted, threw him off. "I'd have died first."

"Liar." He smiled through his tears. "I've known you thirty years, Uli. You lie beautifully. It's why your oaths are so strong — you spend your whole life not saying true things, so when you finally do, the world listens."

Her guard dropped. Half a heartbeat — grief doing what no swordsman could. Bren's point flicked toward her throat, and she saw him pull it, saw him turn the killing line into a graze, a hot stripe of pain along her jaw, and that small mercy told her everything his words couldn't.

So she said the true thing. Here, in the middle of it, with crossbows trained and the boy she loved walking into his mother's stolen arms — she spent her hoarded truth like a dying woman spending gold.

"I love him, Bren." Her voice rang off the stones, and her sword drank the words and burned with them, edge going white-hot with spoken truth. "I love him, and I will go through you to reach him, and I am so sorry — do you hear me? I am so sorry —"

Bren staggered. Not from the blow she landed — though she landed it, flat of the blade across his temple, dropping him to one knee in the snow — but from the words. Truth-snare, witch-craft, whatever the finders had wound around him snapped like rotted rope. He knelt there, blinking, suddenly himself, suddenly free, staring up at her with blood in his hair and thirty years in his face.

"Go," he rasped. "Go to him. I'll hold the road."

Behind him, the witch-finders surged forward. Behind her, Aldric stood frozen in Queen Maren's gentle grasp, her hand cradling his cheek, his sword arm hanging dead at his side, his eyes already glazing as the binding pulled him back under —

And high above, at the crown of the Spire, a light kindled. White. Patient. Hungry.

Something up there had noticed them at last, and it was beginning, very softly, to speak.
