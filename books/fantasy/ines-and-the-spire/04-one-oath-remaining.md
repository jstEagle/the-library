## 4. One Oath Remaining

**Ulyana**

The Spire had no door. It never had. You did not enter the place where oaths were forged; you were *admitted*, the way a wound admits a blade, and Ulyana felt the stone take her measure as she climbed the last stair — reading her gift, weighing it, finding it nearly spent.

Behind her, Aldric's breathing was ragged but steady. Ahead, in the heart-chamber where the light fell like water through a thousand broken panes, something wearing a dead queen's face waited beside a throne of black iron.

"You brought him," the sorcerer said, and its voice was Maren's voice, warm as summer honey, and it made Ulyana's skin crawl to hear it. "How touching. The swordspeaker and the soldier-king. I've worn so many faces to bring you here."

On the throne sat King Edran.

Or what was left of him. He was not dead — that was the horror of it. His eyes tracked them, wet and desperate, and his mouth moved without sound, because the thing on the throne had taken even his voice for its own. Ten years of stolen years looked out from behind his face, and Ulyana understood suddenly why Aldric had wept at the proclamation in Vess: grief is not only for the dead. Sometimes it is worse for the living.

"Father." Aldric stepped forward, and the sorcerer's smile widened with his mother's lips.

"Careful," it murmured. "He'll beg. They always beg. And begging is such an *effective* leash — isn't that right, swordspeaker? Your whole gift is a leash. Oaths. Bindings. Truth hammered into steel. You're no better than I am. You simply dress your chains in honor."

Ulyana drew her sword. The steel sang low, the way it always did near a binding, resonating like a struck bell against the sorcery soaked into this room. "I've never bound anyone who didn't ask to be bound," she said. "That's the difference. That's the *whole* difference."

"Then ask him," the sorcerer said softly, gesturing at Edran with a hand that flickered, just slightly, at the wrist — like heat-shimmer, like something barely holding its shape. "Ask the king what he wants. Go on. I'd love to hear it."

* * *

**Aldric**

Aldric had spent ten years inside a spell, so he knew the architecture of captivity the way a prisoner knows the grain of his cell wall. He could see it now, laid bare in the heart-chamber: threads of pale light running from the sorcerer's chest into his father's, dozens of them, fine as spider silk, humming with borrowed life. The usurper wasn't merely wearing Edran's face. It was *feeding* on him, slowly, the way ivy feeds on an oak, keeping the body alive because the body was the throne's legitimacy.

And threaded through all of it, fainter than the rest, he saw a second strand — one that ran not to the sorcerer but out through the broken windows, south toward the capital, thinning with every mile they had traveled from Vess.

His own binding. Still anchored. Still waiting.

"Aldric." His father's mouth shaped the name without sound. Then, fainter — so faint Aldric felt it more than heard it, a pressure against his mind like a dying man's grip — *the sword. Let her use the sword.*

"He wants you to kill him," the sorcerer said pleasantly, watching Aldric's face. "Isn't that right, Edran? Say it. Oh — you can't. Well. We'll speak for you, won't we, Ulyana Crane? That's your gift. Speaking for other people. Deciding their truth for them."

"No." Ulyana's voice was quiet and absolutely steady. "My gift is hearing it first."

She turned to Aldric, and in the fractured light her eyes were gray as Thornfell slate, and he saw in them everything she would never say while the enemy listened — the pass in the snow, the fire in the smuggler's cave, Bren's blade and Bren's tears, the road where she had taught him to sleep without flinching. Ten days. It had been ten days, and it had been a lifetime.

"Whatever happens," she said, "don't look away. Promise me that much. Not an oath. Just a promise between us."

"I promise," Aldric said.

The sorcerer sighed, and Maren's face began to change — sliding like wax, features blurring through a dozen shapes, soldiers and courtiers and children, everyone it had ever been. "Then let's stop pretending any of us has choices left."

It raised its hand, and the threads began to pull.

* * *

**Ulyana**

The binding snapped taut and Aldric went down — not fighting it, she realized with a lurch; he couldn't, not fully, the old spell waking in his blood like a sleeping dog answering a whistle. He dropped to one knee on the Spire's heart-stone, and the pale thread in his chest brightened, and the sorcerer laughed with a voice that was now nobody's voice at all.

"There," it breathed. "There you are, little prince. Come home."

Ulyana moved.

Not toward the sorcerer — toward the throne. Toward Edran. Because she had finally seen it, the thing every witch-finder and every oathbreaker missed about bindings: they are not walls. They are *knots*. And a knot can be cut from either end.

Her gift rose in her throat like fire swallowing a coal. She had spoken perhaps two hundred true oaths in her life, each one a coin spent from a purse that did not refill, and she had hoarded them for a decade, hiding, rationing, surviving. This was the last one. She felt it sitting in her chest, enormous, final, a sun about to spend itself.

She pressed the flat of her blade against Edran's chained heart, and the steel sang louder, and she spoke:

*"By the steel I hold and the truth I keep — I hear you, Edran Crane's king, and I will not decide for you. If you want freedom, say it in whatever voice remains to you, and my oath will carry it."*

Silence. The sorcerer stopped laughing.

And then Edran — hollowed, voiceless, ten years a prisoner inside his own crowned skull — *pushed*. Not sound. Will. A lifetime of it, compressed into a single silent scream that shook dust from the Spire's broken vaulting: *FREE US. FREE MY SON. TAKE IT ALL.*

"NO—" the sorcerer shrieked, and pulled every thread at once.

Ulyana spoke the last oath of her life.

*"I swear it: father and son, unbound. Unmade in bondage, remade in freedom. By steel and truth, SO IT IS."*

The world went white.

* * *

**Aldric**

He felt the knot cut — felt it the way a man feels a rope burn through, sudden absence, terrible and clean. Every thread in the chamber snapped back along its own length. The sorcerer screamed, and its scream came apart into a hundred voices, a hundred stolen faces crying out as the shape that held them lost its anchor, unravelling like a garment with the seam picked — Maren's face, then nothing, then a shimmer of every face at once, then wind and ash drifting down over the black iron throne.

Edran slumped forward. Aldric caught him.

For three breaths his father weighed nothing at all, emptied. Then — slowly, like dawn coming up under a door — color returned. Breath returned. Eyes, ordinary and human and exhausted beyond all telling, focused on his son's face.

"Aldric," King Edran said, in his own voice, cracked and rusted and real. "You grew up."

Aldric meant to answer. What came out instead was a sound he hadn't made since he was nine years old, and he knelt there on the heart-stone holding his father and grieving and rejoicing in the same breath, until hands — strong, scarred, familiar hands — took his shoulder.

Ulyana stood above them. Her sword lay on the stones, and it was only a sword now. He could see it in her face before she said a word: the silence where the gift had been, vast and absolute, a room in her emptied forever.

"It's gone," she said. Her voice worked; it simply carried no more magic than any other voice in the world. She sounded almost surprised by how little it hurt. "All of it. I can't— I'll never speak another oath. I'm just—"

"*Just*," Aldric said, standing, taking both her hands, "the woman who walked me out of ten years of darkness. Who cut a witch-finder's snare with her bare gift in front of forty witnesses. Who gave the last coin of herself to free a king she'd known ten days." He turned — to his father, to the rising crowd of freed captives and smugglers and soldiers filling the heart-chamber, drawn up the stairs by the light still fading from the broken panes — and raised his voice the way he had never been permitted to raise it in ten years.

"Hear me! I am Aldric, son of Edran, and I claim the crown of Thornfell — not by conquest, not by spell, but by *truth*, witnessed and sworn!" A roar went up. He waited for it to crest, then lifted Ulyana's hand high beside his own. "And hear this also: this woman is not my sword. She is not my shield or my servant. She is the reason there is a throne to claim — and she will stand beside it as my equal, or I will stand nowhere at all!"

The cheering shook the ancient stones. Edran, weeping openly, nodded once — a king's assent, older than any law.

But Ulyana was looking past Aldric, past the crowd, at the far wall of the heart-chamber — where, in the settling ash of the unravelled sorcerer, something remained. A single thread of pale light, unbroken, running out through the broken windows. South. Toward the capital.

Toward whoever had forged the binding in the first place.

Her voice might be empty of magic, but it was not empty of purpose, and when she spoke, every head in the chamber turned.

"The spell is dead," she said quietly. "Its maker isn't. And someone in Vess is about to realize their weapon just came back to life — and is coming for *them*."
