## 1. Salt and Silence

The wind came off the high passes the way it always did in Thornfell — like something with an opinion.

Marguerite Thornbury stood at the head of the caravan with her collar up and her hood down, because the wind deserved to see her face when it insulted her. Below, the switchback trail dropped away into grey mist, and above, the peaks of the Veyran Range went up into cloud the color of old iron. Somewhere between those two greys was the road she'd been hired to keep eleven wagons, forty-two mules, and sixty-one stubborn souls alive on for nine more days.

"Ridge is clear," called Sefa from the point, her voice flattened by the wind. "No slides. No smoke."

"No smoke" meant no travelers. No travelers meant no trouble. In Thornfell, that was as close to a blessing as anyone got these days.

Marguerite raised two fingers, and the caravan began to move — a long, groaning line of salt wagons and wool bales, each one drawn by mules who hated the altitude more than they hated their drivers, which was saying something. She let the first three wagons pass her, then fell in beside the fourth, where the caravan master rode. His name was Odo Pell, and he had the face of a man perpetually surprised by the price of things.

"You're certain about the low road?" he asked, for the fourth time since dawn.

"I'm certain the high road has a checkpoint on it," Marguerite said. "And I'm certain whoever's running that checkpoint this season isn't on your manifest."

Odo's mouth worked. "Salt's not contraband."

"Nothing's contraband until someone with a crossbow decides it is." She nodded at the mist below them. "Low road adds half a day. Half a day of mud instead of half a day of questions. Your salt arrives damp and your necks arrive attached. I call that a fair trade."

He grumbled, but he paid her, and paying her was the whole of his religion. Marguerite had learned long ago that most men's faith ran about that deep: coin in the hand, and trust that tomorrow would resemble today. It was a thin creed. It had also kept more people alive in Thornfell than any god had managed in years.

She rode ahead, checking the trail markers out of habit — cairns her own company had built, back when the Thornbury Blades had been twenty strong and feared rather than hired. The cairns were still standing. That was more than could be said for most of the men who'd stacked them.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a granite shoulder, a hollow the wind couldn't quite reach, and Marguerite walked the perimeter before she allowed herself anything else. Old habits. The Blades used to joke that she checked camp like a woman expecting her own ghost to sneak up on her. The ones who'd joked loudest were dead now, so she supposed the habit had won the argument.

Fires were small and shielded. Salt wagons drawn into a ring. Mules picketed inside. Sefa took first watch without being told; the girl had been with her two seasons and still hadn't learned to complain properly, which Marguerite counted as either loyalty or a failure of education.

Only then did Marguerite do the thing she did every night, the thing she would have denied under torture, the thing that shamed her more than any of the jobs she'd taken.

She prayed.

Not kneeling — she wasn't a peasant, and besides, her knees had opinions after thirty miles of scree. She just stood at the edge of the firelight, facing up-slope toward the peak the old maps named Veyra's Tooth, and she said the words her mother had taught her, the words every child in the Thornfell valleys had once known before the Order came through burning prayer-books and calling it reform.

*Voice in the stone, hear me.
Voice in the stone, keep my road.
What I break, let it be broken rightly.
What I keep, let me keep it clean.*

Four lines. A child's prayer, a drover's prayer, a soldier's prayer. Two hundred years ago there had been temples on every pass with priests who sang the responses, and pilgrims climbing barefoot in winter to prove their worth, and the god's voice — they said — moving through the mountain like heat through iron, so that the stones themselves hummed at dusk.

Marguerite had never heard the mountain hum. Nobody alive had. The last recorded miracle of Veyra was older than her grandmother's grandmother, and the Ashen Order had spent those generations making sure it stayed that way. They'd sealed the shrines. Melted the bells. Burned the singers — or worse, recruited them. And somewhere in all that ash and scripture, the voice in the stone had gone quiet, if it had ever been anything but a story at all.

That was the part Marguerite believed, most days. That it had always been a story. That the Order hadn't killed a god, only murdered a rumor, and the rumor had died easily because rumors die easy.

But she still said the words.

She stood there in the cold with her breath steaming and said four lines to a mountain that had never once answered, and then she felt the familiar shame rise in her throat like bile, and she swallowed it, and she went back to the fire to eat her salt-pork and beans like a sensible woman.

Sefa glanced up when she sat. "You do that every night."

"I check the perimeter every night too. You don't comment on that."

"The perimeter's real."

Marguerite looked at her over the fire. Sefa was nineteen, sharp-eyed, missing two fingers to frostbite from her first winter on the roads, and possessed of the particular courage of the young, which is to say she hadn't yet learned what fear cost. "Eat your beans," Marguerite said.

"It's just — my gran used to say the words. Before she died. Said the god was sleeping, not gone. Said the Order didn't kill it, they—"

"They what?"

Sefa shrugged, suddenly interested in her bowl. "I don't know. Gran said a lot of things. She also said the ferrywoman at Greywater could steal your face, so."

"She can't," Marguerite said. "But don't look her in the eye anyway. Some habits are cheap."

The fire cracked. Up-valley, a rockfall went somewhere in the dark, a deep grinding mutter like a giant turning over in its sleep, and both of them looked up at the sound, and neither of them said what they were both thinking, which was: *like a voice.*

Marguerite finished her meal, cleaned her bowl with snow, and turned in. She slept the way she always slept — lightly, with one ear on the camp, dreaming of nothing she remembered.

* * *

The third day brought them down into the low road, and the low road brought them trouble, because roads always do.

It came in the form of a shrine.

It stood in a fold of the hillside where the trail bent around a spur of black rock — a little alcove of stacked stones, roofed with a slab, no bigger than a coffin. There would have been hundreds of them once, dotting every mile of the smuggler roads, each one holding a chip of consecrated stone or a ribbon or a carved name. This one had been wrecked recently. Deliberately. The slab was cracked down the middle, the stacked stones scattered, and someone had chiseled out the old inscription and cut new letters in its place:

**THE MOUNTAIN IS STONE. STONE SAYS NOTHING. WORSHIP NOTHING THAT CANNOT ANSWER.**

Order work. Fresh enough that the chisel dust still clung to the cracks.

Odo Pell crossed himself with the salt-merchants' sign, thumb to brow, finger to lips. "Blasphemers," he muttered. "Cutting words into the god's own rock."

"That's not the god's rock," Marguerite said. "That's just rock. Everything up here is just rock." But she'd stopped her horse, and she was looking at the shattered shrine longer than a woman looking at just rock ought to.

Because she knew this shrine. Had known it. As a girl, riding the passes with her father's freight-train, she'd stopped here a hundred times. Her brother Tomas used to leave things in it — a copper bit, a feather, once a whole dried apple he claimed he wasn't hungry for, though he was always hungry. And the old inscription beneath the slab had been worn smooth by centuries of touching hands, and Tomas used to press his palm flat against it and say, *listen, Mag, listen, you can feel it humming,* and she'd press her palm against it too and feel nothing, absolutely nothing, and say so, and he'd say *you have to want to hear it,* and she'd say *that's how you know it's nothing, Tom, you have to buy it first.*

Tomas was fourteen years dead. The apple was probably still in there, under the rubble, petrified into the stone it had been left for.

"Thornbury?" Odo said. "We losing light?"

She swung down, and before she knew she'd decided to, she knelt and began restacking the fallen stones. Just the outer ring. Just enough that the little structure looked like a shrine again instead of a warning. She righted the cracked slab without trying to mend it — some breaks you don't pretend to fix — and she swept the chisel dust away with her glove.

Then she pressed her palm flat against the stone, the way Tomas used to.

Nothing. Cold rock, cold wind, the distant complaint of mules. Sixty-one souls behind her waiting on her word, and her kneeling in the dirt with her hand on a broken altar like a madwoman.

She took the dried apple from her saddlebag — she carried them for the scurvy, that was the story she told herself — and set it in the hollow beneath the slab. Then she stood, mounted, and gave the order to move, and dared anyone in the caravan to have seen anything worth mentioning.

Nobody mentioned it. But Sefa rode past the shrine last, and Marguerite saw her touch the stone as she passed, quick, like a thief.

* * *

On the fifth night, the weather turned.

It came down off the peaks an hour after sundown — not snow yet, but the promise of it, a hard dry cold that made the mules bellow and the fires shrink. Marguerite doubled the watch, moved the picket line into the rocks, and sat up later than she meant to, oiling her sword by firelight and listening to the mountain make its noises.

That was when the dog started barking.

They didn't have a dog. That was the first thought, arriving with cold clarity even as her hand found her sword hilt. The second thought was: *someone else's dog.* Which meant someone else. On the low road, in this weather, at this hour.

She was up and moving before the third thought arrived. Sefa met her at the picket line, crossbow already spanned. "North side," the girl whispered. "Something coming down the trail. Fast."

"Not riders. Footsteps."

"One man," Sefa said. "Maybe. He's — Thornbury, he's falling down a lot."

Marguerite put her eye to the dark and waited. The wind gusted, thinned, gusted again, and in the gaps she heard it: stumbling, labored breathing, the wet click of a throat trying to work around blood. Then a shape resolved out of the murk — a man, alone, reeling along the trail with one arm wrapped around his ribs and the other stretched out ahead of him like a man walking into a wall he couldn't see.

He was wearing courier's leathers, road-stained, and he was leaving a dark trail behind him on the frozen ground that even the moonless dark couldn't hide.

"Light," Marguerite said quietly, and Sefa unshuttered the lantern.

The man flinched from the light like a creature of the deep woods, threw up a hand, and croaked: "No — no lights — they'll see—"

"Who'll see?"

He didn't answer. He was looking past her, past the fires, past everything, with the fixed desperation of a man who has run past the end of his strength and is now running on something that isn't strength at all. His eyes found hers. Whatever he saw in her face made him lurch forward, off the trail, straight into the firelight, straight into the ring of wagons, and he grabbed the front of her coat with a bloody hand and said:

"You're armed. You're — mercenary. Good. Good." He was fumbling at his chest, at the lacings of his jerkin, fingers too cold and too clumsy to work. "Take it. Take it — I can't — they'll get it off my corpse otherwise, and it has to go south, it has to go *down*, do you understand? Down is the only direction that matters anymore."

"Slowly," Marguerite said. "Who's chasing you?"

"Ashen." The word came out of him like a cough. "Ashen Order. Six of them, maybe eight, they don't stop, they don't eat, they—" He got the lacing open at last and pulled out a leather-wrapped roll, oilskin over stiff hide, tied with waxed cord, and pressed it into her hands with both of his, folding her fingers shut over it the way you'd close the hand of the dead. "The map. It's the map. It's the only map. Everyone else who carried a copy is dead, I watched them — Greymill, the ferry, the—"

Behind him, up the trail, Sefa said sharply: "Lanterns. Three of them. Coming down fast."

The courier's eyes went wide and white. He sagged, and Marguerite caught him, and felt the warmth of his blood soaking through her sleeve, and understood in one cold flash that the warmth was leaving him faster than any bandage in her kit could argue with.

"Listen," he whispered. His mouth was at her ear now, and his whisper was the whisper of a man spending his last coins. "Below the stone. The voice below the stone. It's real — it was never a story — they didn't silence it because it was mad, they silenced it because it *spoke* — it spoke and they couldn't bear what it—"

"Lanterns are closing," Sefa said.

"—the map shows the way, the marks, the marks are the old drovers' signs, you know them, your kind cut them—" Blood filled his mouth and he coughed, wet and terrible. "Swear it. Swear you'll take it down. Swear it on whatever you still pray to."

And here was the thing Marguerite would think about for the rest of her life, in the vault and out of it, in the years after: she had meant to say no. She had meant to say *I'm a guide, not a grave-goods carrier, find yourself another fool.* The words were already in her mouth.

But he was dying in her arms on a frozen road in the mountains where her brother had died, and he had said *swear it on whatever you still pray to,* and whatever she still prayed to was nothing, was an empty altar, was four lines said to cold stone every night for twenty years with no answer — and some spiteful, starving corner of her soul heard that phrase and thought: *nothing? You want me to swear on nothing? Fine. Let nothing witness it.*

"I swear," she said.

The courier smiled. It was the worst smile she had ever seen, relieved and ruined at once. "Good," he breathed. "Good. Then it's yours now. Debt's yours now. Sorry. Sorry about that."

Then the light went out of him between one breath and the next, the way it does, without ceremony, and his weight became dead weight, and Marguerite Thornbury knelt on the low road holding a stranger's corpse and a stranger's debt while three lanterns came down out of the dark behind him.

She laid him flat. Closed his eyes. Took the oilskin roll and pushed it down inside her coat, against her skin, where it lay cold as a second knife.

"Sefa," she said. "Get Odo. Wake the camp. Quietly."

"What do I tell them?"

"Tell them a traveler died on our watch and we're about to have visitors." She stood, wiped her hands on her coat, and turned to face up the trail, toward the lantern-light swaying closer through the freezing dark. "And tell them nobody speaks but me."

The lanterns came on. Behind them, faint but unmistakable in the wind, came the sound of voices singing — low, disciplined, many-throated, a hymn in the old tongue that the Order had kept and everyone else had been made to forget.

Marguerite stood in the middle of the road, over a dead man, with a god's stolen secret against her ribs, and waited for the Ashen Order to arrive.
