## 2. The Courier's Last Mile

The mule died at sundown, and Marguerite took it as an omen of the honest kind — the kind that didn't ask for worship.

She'd known the animal was failing since Greymill, three days back. It had a rattle in its chest like a loose shutter in wind, and it had stopped complaining about the grade of the pass, which was how a mule told you it had given up on living. She'd done what needed doing behind a screen of larch with her knife and a prayer she didn't mean, because Veyra had no use for half-measures even if He had gone quiet as a grave these last six years. Then she'd butchered what she could carry and salted the rest against the cold, and now the meat hung in strips from a line strung between two pines above her fire, steaming faintly in the blue dusk.

The caravan she'd guided over the pass was long gone down the southern switchbacks, lanterns bobbing away toward the lowlands like a string of drowned stars. Her contract was fulfilled. Paid debt, closed ledger. In the morning she would walk back north to Thornfell proper, drink something bad in a tavern that smelled of tallow, and wait for the next fool with cargo and more faith than sense.

She turned the spit. Below her, the valley filled up with shadow the way a cup fills with wine.

*Veyra of the Stone, keeper of the deep roads, hear me.* The words moved through her mouth on their own, worn smooth as river stones by twenty years of use. *Guard this fire. Guard this flesh. Keep my feet on the path.*

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered anymore. Six years ago the god's presence had been a weight at the base of her skull, warm as a hand; then one winter it simply wasn't, and every sworn blade in Thornfell had felt the absence like a tooth pulled. The priests said He slept. The priests said a great many things, their voices pitched a little too high, their altars swept a little too clean. Marguerite kept praying anyway, out of habit, out of stubbornness, out of some stubborn animal loyalty to the thing that had once held her oath — the way you still set a place at the table for the dead.

Habit. That was all. She fed the fire another branch and told herself that was all.

The courier came out of the treeline an hour past full dark.

She heard him before she saw him — a crashing, arrhythmic, nothing-like-a-deer sound, the noise a man makes when his body has stopped taking orders. Marguerite was on her feet with her sword drawn before her mind caught up, backing to the boulder at the camp's edge where the firelight wouldn't reach her eyes. Twenty years of passes had built that reflex deeper than thought.

"Light," the man gasped. "Gods — light — I can see your fire—"

"Stop there." Her voice came out flat and cold, the mercenary's voice, the one that made green bandits reconsider. "One more step and I open you."

He stopped. He swayed. And the firelight found him, and Marguerite forgot, for a moment, how to breathe.

He was young — twenty-five at most — and he was dying. There was no other word for the grey of him. An arrow stood in his left shoulder, snapped off at the fletching, and beneath it his side was soaked black to the hip, blood loss written in the waxy pallor of his face and the way his eyes couldn't seem to settle on anything for longer than a heartbeat. His horse, she realized, must have fallen or been killed behind him; he'd run the last miles on foot through broken country, which meant he'd been running on something beyond muscle. Something with teeth.

"Please," he said. "I need — I'm looking for someone going north."

"You've found someone going nowhere until morning." She didn't lower the sword. "Who's after you?"

His face did something terrible then — not fear exactly, but recognition, the look of a man who has just understood the shape of the trap he's already inside. "You're Thornbury," he whispered. "Marguerite Thornbury. The Blades' guide. I was told — in Greymill they said if anyone could get through the high passes unseen—"

"Who told you? Who's *after* you?"

"Riders." He swallowed, and the swallow cost him visibly. "Ash-grey cloaks. They don't ride like men ride. They ride like — like weather coming." He laughed, a small cracked sound with no humor in it anywhere. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm losing the thread of it. My name is Aldric Vane. I carry dispatches for the Archive at Cael Harrow, and there are four of them, and they do not stop."

Marguerite had heard of the ash-grey cloaks. Everyone who worked the Thornfell roads had heard of them, the way sailors hear of a particular stretch of coast that eats ships. The Ashen Order. Zealots in grey who came down out of their fortress-monastery at Vespergate twice a year to collect tithes and ask questions, polite as tax collectors and twice as feared. They had never, in her experience, chased a man through the high country at night until his heart burst.

"They're a day behind you," she said. "Maybe less. Get off your feet before you fall down."

"I can't stay." But his knees were already folding, and she caught him under the good arm and lowered him against the pine trunk, and the moment his weight settled he made a sound like a hinge rusting shut. "I can't stay and I can't go on and I can't let them take it." His hand was digging at the satchel strap across his chest with fingers that barely worked. "Do you swear by anything? Anything at all?"

The question landed strangely, a fishhook under the ribs. "I swore by Veyra," she said. "Once. Long ago."

Something moved through his exhausted face — relief, or grief, or both wearing the same coat. "Then this is yours to keep or refuse," he said, "but hear me first, because I haven't got long and I won't waste it." He pulled the satchel around and pressed it flat against his thigh, and his eyes came up to hers and held there with the last of his strength gathered into them like light into a closing door. "Two hundred years ago the Order did not silence a mad god. Whatever they told the world, whatever they preach from Vespergate — it isn't true. They silenced Him because of what He *said*. Because truth is a weapon and they wanted to be the only ones armed." A cough took him, wet and deep. "What I carry is proof. Where it's hidden. How it can be woken. The voice below the stone, Thornbury. The voice below the stone."

"The voice—" She shook her head. "I don't know what that means."

"No," he said gently, terribly. "But you will."

He pushed the satchel into her hands.

It was lighter than she expected. Leather, scarred and travel-worn, buckled tight, with a map case sewn into its spine that clacked faintly when it moved — oilcloth-wrapped tubes, the sound of paper rolled around something stiffer. She looked down at it and then back up at him, and everything in her that had survived twenty years of mercenary work was screaming the same single syllable: *no.*

"I can't take this," she said. "Whatever this is, I'm not part of it. I guide caravans. I kill people who make me. That's the whole of my ambitions."

"I know." He smiled. It transformed his face, briefly, into the face of someone much younger, someone who might have been somebody's son in a village somewhere with his boots drying by a door. "That's why it has to be you. Anyone who *wants* it is already theirs." His breath hitched. "Swear it. Swear by your silent god. Not to deliver it — just to keep it out of their hands. Just that. Just long enough."

"Aldric Vane—"

"*Just that.*"

And here is the thing Marguerite would turn over in her mind for months afterward, lying awake in bad beds in worse towns: she could have refused. He was dying either way; the satchel would have sat beside his corpse until the crows or the Order found it, and none of it would have been hers. She could have walked into the dark and let the mountain sort it out.

Instead she heard herself say, in the old cadence, in the words her mother's mother had used before her: *"By stone and silence, by the deep road and the sleeping god — I will keep it from their hands."*

The oath left her mouth and the night seemed to lean in around it.

"There," Aldric Vane whispered. "Now neither of us owes Veyra nothing." He sagged back against the tree. "They'll come asking. You were never here. You saw a deer. You're a caravan guard heading south, you know nothing, you've never seen my face." His hand found her wrist, grip shockingly strong for a dying man. "Thornbury. They'll offer you kindness first. Kindness is how they start. Say yes to nothing."

Then the strength went out of him all at once, like a tide withdrawing, and he slid down the pine trunk and lay half across her firewood, and within the hour he was dead — quietly, without drama, between one rattling breath and the next, his eyes fixed on the strips of mule meat turning above the flames as though he found them very interesting indeed.

Marguerite sat with him while the fire burned down, and did not pray, and did not sleep.

* * *

She buried him shallow, because the ground was iron with frost and she had a knife and a shovel-blade and no time. She stacked stones over the place where the earth was disturbed, enough of them to tell any passing wolf that this meal had been paid for. She took his boots, because they were better than hers and he had no further use for them, and hated herself a little for how little the hating slowed her hands.

The satchel she strapped beneath her cloak, against her spine, where a sword usually rode.

She was kicking dirt over the fire when she heard them.

Hooves. Many, moving fast, and then not fast — slowing, spreading, the disciplined deployment of riders fanning out through trees. No voices. That was the worst of it, the detail that crawled up her arms and made the hair stand: no horns, no calls, no challenge. Just the soft multiple percussion of horses in the dark and the occasional creak of leather, closing in from the north and west in a wide, patient arc.

They had followed the blood, probably. Or the ravens. It didn't matter. What mattered was that she had perhaps ninety seconds and one road out, southeast along the ridge, and that a woman walking alone at night with fresh-buried earth behind her camp was a story that would not survive even a stupid question.

Marguerite did the only thing her twenty years had trained her to do. She became unremarkable.

She dragged the saddle onto her remaining horse — the bay mare, sharp-eared and already rolling her eyes at the smell on the wind — and she pulled the courier's bloodied bedroll from where it lay and tossed it casually near the fire ring, visible, explainable. A traveler. A stranger who'd shared her fire and moved on, or tried to. She banked the fire low, mounted, and walked the mare southeast along the ridge trail at the pace of a woman with nowhere urgent to be, and she had covered perhaps four hundred yards when the grey cloaks found her fire.

They let her get another two hundred yards before the horn sounded — a single low note, more felt than heard, a sound like a tomb door remembering how to close — and then hooves again, converging, and torchlight bloomed through the pines ahead of her, cutting off the trail.

Five of them. Ash-grey riding cloaks, hooded, faces shadowed, and the horses beneath them were tall pale beasts she didn't recognize the breed of, moving with a smoothness that was almost wrong. The lead rider raised a gauntleted hand and the others halted in a practiced fan, blocking the trail, torches held high and steady.

"Lady traveler." The voice from the lead rider was a woman's, level and pleasant, the voice of someone asking directions at a shrine. "A moment of your time, if the road allows."

Marguerite reined in. Her heart was going like a drum in a parade, and she made her face into the bored, tired mask of a working guard, and she was distantly amazed at how steady her own voice came out.

"Road allows what it allows," she said. "Make it quick. I'm due in Lowmark by week's end."

"We're searching for a fugitive." The lead rider nudged her horse forward, and the torchlight climbed her features — forties, hard-featured, calm-eyed, with the kind of composure that comes from never once having doubted herself. "A courier. Young man, dark hair, wounded. He passed this way tonight. Did you see him?"

"I buried a man tonight, if that's what you're asking."

Silence. One of the flanking riders shifted in the saddle.

"Buried him," the lead woman repeated.

"He came into my fire bleeding out of a dozen holes. Arrow in the shoulder. Asked for water, asked for a priest, got neither in time." Marguerite shrugged, and the shrug was perfect, weary, the shrug of a woman who had seen too many men die to spend feeling on one more. "I put him in the ground back there with stones on top. You're welcome to dig him up and check his shoulder. You'll find your arrow in it."

It was a risk and she knew it — offering the grave invited inspection of the grave — but a refusal to let them look would hang her faster than the look itself. The lead woman studied her for a long moment, torchlight moving across her face like water.

"And the man's effects?" she said. "His satchel? His papers?"

"Robbed before I found him, or he'd hidden them, or whoever cut him up took them. I found a man, a bedroll, and a corpse. I took the bedroll for burning and the boots because mine are worn through. If that offends the Order's sensibilities, the Order's welcome to bill me." She let a beat pass, then added, with the precise degree of insolence a free company guard could afford: "Am I accused of something, ser? Only I've a contract waiting, and contracts don't keep."

Another silence, longer. Somewhere upslope, a stick cracked — the other riders, spreading wider than she'd thought, and her stomach turned slowly over. They weren't five. They'd never been five. The fan had been closing behind her the whole time.

"You gave him burial," the lead woman said at last, and something in her tone shifted — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but interest, the way a jeweler looks twice at a stone. "That was pious of you. Most travelers strip a corpse and move on. Do you hold faith, lady traveler?"

"I was raised to Veyra," Marguerite said. "Like everyone in these mountains."

"Mm." The woman's hood tilted. "And does Veyra answer you?"

The question slid in under her guard like a thin knife. *Say yes to nothing,* the dead man had said. "The god sleeps," Marguerite said. "Everyone knows it. I say my words anyway. Habit. Goodnight, ser."

She clicked her tongue and walked the mare forward, straight through the gap between two torches, every muscle in her body braced for the bolt in the back, the rope, the voice saying *stop her.* The grey riders parted. Hooves crunched frost behind her, unhurried, letting her go.

She held the pace. Two hundred yards. Five hundred. Until the torchlight was swallowed by the trees and the horn did not sound and the night was only night again, vast and indifferent and full of listening.

Then she put her heels to the mare and ran.

* * *

She didn't stop until the sky went grey, ten miles south and a thousand feet down, in a hollow of rock above the frozen shallows of the Greymill river. She saw to the horse first — always the horse; a dead guide walks — rubbing her down, watering her, feeding her from the nosebag. Then she sat on a stone with the satchel in her lap and the sunrise coming up red over the eastern peaks like a wound reopening, and she unbuckled it.

Inside: a wax-sealed letter bearing the broken seal of the Archive at Cael Harrow. A locket with a miniature portrait of a woman she didn't know, painted in the style of thirty years past. And the map case, and inside the map case, wrapped in oilcloth around a slat of stiff vellum, a chart of the Thornfell high country so detailed it made her breath catch — passes marked that she knew, passes marked that she'd only ever heard smugglers whisper about, and passes marked that she had never seen on any map made by living hands, deep routes under the mountains themselves, drawn in faded ink in a hand two centuries dead. At the center of it all, circled twice and annotated in a cramped antique script, a symbol she recognized from a hundred roadside shrines: the closed eye above the mountain.

Beneath it, in fresher ink, someone had written a single line:

*The voice below the stone. If I die, give it to no one who wants it.*

Marguerite sat for a long time with the map across her knees.

Below her, smoke was rising from Greymill's chimneys — woodsmoke, cooksmoke, the ordinary smoke of ordinary people buying bread and arguing about prices. A market town. A town with brokers, with fences, with men who bought strange things and asked no questions. A town where a map like this might fetch enough coin to retire on, if she could find a buyer before the buyers found her.

She thought about Aldric Vane's face going slack above her firewood. She thought about the lead rider's calm voice: *does Veyra answer you?* She thought about the way the night had leaned in when she'd sworn, and about the fact that she had sworn at all — that after six years of praying into a silence, the first thing her silent god had gotten from her was a promise made over a dead man's satchel.

There was no version of this where she sold it and walked away. She understood that now, with the clean cold clarity of a blade going in. Whoever killed the courier would come for the map, and whoever came for the map would come for whoever held it, and the only question left in the whole wide world was whether she met them empty-handed or not.

"Damn you," she said aloud, to the mountain, to the god, to the young man under the stones. "Damn every one of you."

The mountain said nothing. The god said nothing. But the wind came down off the high passes and moved through the frozen shallows below her, and for one absurd moment the ice sang — a low harmonic hum, resonant, almost like a throat clearing after a long silence — and Marguerite Thornbury sat very still and told herself it was only the cold.

Then she folded the map away, strapped the satchel against her spine, and rode down toward Greymill, where by nightfall the first buyer would already be dead.
