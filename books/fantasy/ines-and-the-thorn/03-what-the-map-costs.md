## 3. What the Map Costs

Greymill smelled of tallow smoke and river rot, the way border towns always did — a place that had given up pretending to be anything but what it was. Marguerite came down off the pass road at midmorning with two days of dust on her cloak and the courier's map pressed flat against her ribs, sewn into the lining where her knife used to ride before she'd moved the knife to her boot.

The town sat in the crook of the Thorn like a bruise: timber houses leaning against each other for support, a market square churned to mud, and above it all the grey bulk of the tollhouse where everything in Thornfell that couldn't be taxed was instead bought. She'd sold salvage here a dozen times over the years. Salt from the eastern pans. Blades pulled off dead men who no longer needed them. Once, memorably, a crate of Order vestments stolen off a mule train, which had paid for an entire winter and half of the following spring.

She knew how this worked. That was the problem. She kept reaching for the shape of it — walk in, name a price, walk out richer — and the shape kept refusing to fit around what she was carrying.

*The voice below the stone.* The courier's last words had followed her down two passes and across the high moor, patient as a debt collector. She'd tried to leave them behind on the road. They'd declined to stay.

She took a room at the Drowned Bell because it was the kind of inn where nobody remembered your face unless they were paid to, and because its keeper, a one-eyed woman named Hesk, owed Marguerite for a job three winters back that had never been spoken of since. Hesk took her coin without counting it, which was its own kind of greeting.

"Staying long?" Hesk asked. Her remaining eye did the work of two.

"One night. Maybe two." Marguerite set her pack down and did not look at the wall where the map lay hidden inside her coat, hanging on its peg like any other coat in any other town. "I'm looking for a buyer."

"Of course you are." Hesk scratched under her eyepatch. "What kind?"

"The kind who doesn't ask questions."

"That's most of Greymill, love. Questions cost extra here."

Marguerite almost smiled. It didn't take. "Old things. Papers. Something with a provenance I can't discuss."

Hesk was quiet a moment, and Marguerite watched the arithmetic move behind her eye. Three winters ago there had been blood, and Hesk had walked away from it clean, and some debts made people helpful forever and others made them careful. Marguerite wasn't sure yet which kind this was.

"Ferrant Voss," Hesk said finally. "Keeps the antiquary on Tanner's Row. Buys anything old enough to be dangerous and pays enough to make it worth the risk. He'll want to know where you got it."

"He won't get an answer."

"Then he'll pay less." Hesk shrugged. "But he'll pay. And he won't sell you to the ash-cloaks, if that's what's chewing at you. Voss hates the Order more than he loves coin, and he loves coin the way other men love breathing."

*If that's what's chewing at you.* Marguerite held Hesk's gaze until the woman looked away first, then went upstairs to sit with her back to the wall and watch the window until dark.

* * *

Tanner's Row after sundown was a gutter of yellow light and rendered fat, the tanneries shut down but their stink baked into the stones so deep that rain would never wash it out. Voss's shop stood between a chandler and a shuttered dyehouse, its windows fogged with age, its door unlocked — which told her something about either the man or the town, and possibly both.

Ferrant Voss turned out to be a narrow, quick-eyed man of sixty-odd years with ink stains to his elbows and the particular stillness of someone who had survived a long time by knowing when not to move. He looked up from his ledger when she entered, took her measure in a single pass — boots, sword, the set of her shoulders — and said, "Closed."

"You're not. You just wish you were."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. "Hesk sent you."

"Hesk mentioned you. There's a difference, and if you're smart you'll remember it."

He set down his pen. "And what is it you're selling?"

Marguerite had rehearsed this on the road. Not the words — the *posture*. The bored mercenary with salvage she didn't care about, the woman for whom this was Tuesday. She let him see the sword before she let him see anything else, and only then drew the map from her coat and laid it on the counter between them, unrolled halfway, enough to show the vellum's age and the fine faded linework of the passes.

Voss went very still.

It lasted perhaps two seconds. A man like Voss should have been able to hide it better, and the fact that he couldn't told her more than any appraisal could have: whatever this map was, he recognized it, or he recognized what it might be, and recognition frightened him.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you get this?"

"I thought we established you wouldn't ask."

"And I thought," he said, "we'd established nothing at all." His eyes hadn't left the vellum. His hands stayed flat on the ledger, deliberately, the way a man keeps his hands visible when he wants to seem harmless — or when he's stopping himself from reaching. "Do you know what this is?"

"A map."

"It's a survey of the high Thorn. Interior routes. The sealed routes." He looked up at her at last, and behind the fear in his face there was something else, something hungry, and that hunger scared her worse than the fear. "There are perhaps four documents in existence that chart the Underroad approaches, and the Order burned three of them publicly in Greymill square twenty years ago. I attended. I was younger then, and stupid enough to attend." He touched the edge of the map with one fingertip, light as a priest touching a relic. "Who died to give you this?"

"No one I owe."

"Everyone who carries these owes someone," Voss said. "That's how they work. That's what they *are*." He straightened, and the merchant came back into his face like a mask sliding home, and when he named a figure it was triple what she'd expected and she knew instantly that it was half what the thing was worth and that both of them knew it. "For the map alone. Unopened. I don't read it, you don't tell me where it came from, and you leave Greymill tonight."

"Tonight?"

"Before morning." He was already counting out coin from an iron box, and his hands weren't quite steady. "I mean it, mercenary. Take the money and keep walking. There are men in ash-grey cloaks asking after a courier on every road within fifty miles, and they pay informants in ways I don't want described in my own shop. Whatever you think you've found—"

The knock at the door stopped him mid-sentence.

Three soft raps. Then, after a pause measured too precisely to be accidental, three more.

Voss went white. "Back room," he breathed. "Now."

Marguerite didn't move. Her hand had already found her knife. "Who is it?"

"If I knew, I wouldn't be afraid," Voss whispered, and that, at least, she believed.

The knock came again. Then a voice, mild and reasonable, the voice of a neighbor borrowing flour: "Master Voss. We saw your lamp. It's late for the lamp, Master Voss."

Through the fogged glass of the front window, Marguerite saw shapes. Two of them. Ash-grey cloaks, hoods up, faces lost in shadow — and standing in the street with the patience of men being paid by the hour.

*They followed me.* The thought arrived cold and complete. *Not Voss. Me. From the pass, or from the moor, or from the moment the courier died. They let me lead them to whoever I'd run to.*

"Back room," Voss said again, barely a sound. "There's a cellar. Cellars connect, in Greymill. Everything connects, that's the whole point of the town—"

The door handle turned. Locked. A pause, almost courteous. Then a shoulder hit the wood hard enough to crack the frame.

Marguerite went through the back room doorway as the front door gave way, map snatched off the counter, and heard Voss say — loudly, in a voice pitched to carry, a merchant's voice doing what merchants' voices do best — "Gentlemen! Gentlemen, please, the hour, the *hour*, whatever you're selling I'm not buying—"

She didn't wait to hear the rest. The cellar stairs were black and slick, and at the bottom, exactly as promised, a low brick tunnel ran off into the dark toward somebody else's cellar, somebody else's life, the whole rotten interconnected town holding its breath around her.

Behind her, muffled through floors, she heard Voss laugh — a real laugh, easy, unafraid — and then the sound of chairs, of pouring, of commerce resuming. Whatever Voss was, he was very good at it.

Marguerite put the tunnel between herself and the lamplight and did not stop moving for a long time.

* * *

She didn't go back to the Drowned Bell. That was the first rule of being hunted, learned young and confirmed often: never sleep where you're expected. Instead she paid a fishwife three coppers for the use of her shed, wedged herself between salt barrels and nets that stank of a decade's catch, and slept in fits with the map under her coat and her knife loose in her hand.

Dawn came grey through the shed's slats. She gave it an hour past full light — long enough for the town to wake, short enough to beat the worst of the crowds — and worked her way toward Tanner's Row through back lanes, hood up, pace unhurried. Unhurried was important. Hurried men got remembered.

She wanted her coin. That was the honest truth of it, the small greedy human truth underneath all the larger fears: Voss had counted out a fortune onto his counter and she'd left it sitting there, and somewhere in the night her mind had begun constructing reasons why she might reasonably go back for it. She'd shot those reasons down one by one, and here she was anyway, three streets out, telling herself she only wanted to *look*. To see whether the shop stood open. Whether Voss stood behind his counter. Whether the ash-cloaks had been a warning or a promise.

Tanner's Row answered her before she reached it.

She heard the crowd first — that particular murmur, low and dense, that gathers around the dead the way flies gather around everything else. Then she saw them, a knot of thirty bodies outside the chandler's, and beyond them, framed in the open door of the antiquary, two town watchmen and a spreading darkness on the floorboards that even from forty feet she recognized.

Marguerite stopped at the mouth of the row and let the current of early traffic carry her gaze along naturally, a woman idling, no more. Through the gap in the crowd: Ferrant Voss, on his back among his ruined shelves, eyes open, throat opened wider. His ledger lay near his hand, pages torn out. Every scroll, every case, every shelf of old paper in the shop had been pulled down and slashed — not searched. *Slashed.* Searched-for leaves evidence intact; this was erasure. Someone had gone through Voss's inventory destroying anything old enough to matter, and then, having done it, had cut his throat almost as an afterthought.

Almost.

Because the watchmen were saying things, and the crowd was repeating them, and the words came down the row to her on the morning air in fragments she couldn't stop hearing:

"—ash-grey cloaks, seen leaving before first bell—"

"—asking about a woman, they said, a mercenary woman—"

"—the courier, they're saying he came through here too, they're saying anyone who touched it—"

*Anyone who touched it.*

Marguerite turned and walked away, one step at a time, matching the town's rhythm, while something cold assembled itself in her chest piece by piece like a lock taking a key.

She had told herself, on the road, that the map was salvage. That everything was salvage, if you got it cheap enough and sold it fast enough. Find a buyer, take the coin, vanish south past the tollhouses where the Order's reach thinned to nothing. There was always a version where you simply walked away. There was always a version. That was the whole architecture of her life — every job, every debt, every grave she'd dug for other people's mistakes — built on the certainty that distance could be bought and bought cheaply.

But Voss had never carried the map. Voss had only *looked* at it, for the length of a conversation, and this morning his blood was soaking into boards he'd owned for thirty years. The ash-cloaks hadn't come for the map — they'd come because a map-carrier had spoken to him, and that was enough, and they'd known it was enough, because they had done this before, many times, for two hundred years, and they had gotten very good at knowing exactly how much death a secret required.

Which meant the ledger in her head had just changed currencies. It wasn't *how much is the map worth?* anymore. It was *how much am I worth alive, and to whom, and for how long?*

She cut down an alley between the chandler's and the dyehouse, and stopped.

Two men stood at the far end. Ash-grey cloaks, hoods back now, faces ordinary as bread — one heavy, one thin, the thin one holding a slate and writing on it with a stub of charcoal. Not watching the alley. Watching the *crowd* on Tanner's Row, cataloguing it, the way she herself would catalogue a battlefield for survivors.

The thin one glanced up. His eyes passed over her — hooded woman, unremarkable, one more body in a town full of them — and moved on.

Marguerite breathed again, slowly, and eased backward out of the alley without turning her back on them, because turning your back was a habit that got you buried.

*They don't know my face,* she thought. *Not yet. The courier died before he could describe me. Voss died before he could describe me. Right now I'm a rumor with a hood on.*

Right now.

She made her way to the edge of town by streets she chose at random, doubling back twice, and fetched her gear from the Drowned Bell's stable loft where she'd stashed it rather than her room — Hesk would understand; Hesk would probably have charged her extra for the understanding — and stood a last moment in the shadow of the tollhouse looking north, at the high grey shoulders of the Thorn rising into cloud.

South was safety. South was the lowlands, the toll roads, the whole soft world where a woman could lose herself in ten thousand strangers. Every instinct she owned pointed south.

But the map was sewn against her ribs, and the courier's whisper was stitched somewhere beneath that, and the ash-cloaks were already combing Greymill for a woman they couldn't describe, which meant that sooner or later they'd stop describing and start *guessing*, and the Order had never once in its history been accused of guessing small.

And there was this, too, which she admitted only in the privacy of her own skull: Voss had known what the map was. Had feared it and wanted it in the same breath. *A survey of the sealed routes.* Which meant someone else in this world knew what it was — someone who might know more than a dying courier's last four words — and that someone was worth finding before the ash-cloaks found her.

She turned north, toward the mountains, toward the voice below the stone, and told herself it was strategy.

It sounded almost true.

Behind her, at the mouth of Tanner's Row, the thin man with the slate wrote one final line, tore the page free, and handed it to a rider whose horse was already saddled.
