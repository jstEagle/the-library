## 5. An Unwilling Partnership

Greymill smelled of wet wool, tallow smoke, and the particular rot that gathered where a river met too many secrets. Marguerite had been in town two days, and she had spent both of them learning the shape of her cage.

It was not a large cage. Greymill clung to the Thornfell border like a barnacle on a hull — three streets deep, one market square, a customs house that took bribes with the bored efficiency of a man counting sheep. But every road out of it now had eyes. She'd watched an ash-grey cloak at the north gate yesterday, standing in the drizzle without moving for an hour, watching faces the way a fisherman watches water. This morning there'd been another at the ferry landing, and one more lounging against the wall of the Saltcellar tavern, pretending to drink a beer he never touched.

They weren't searching for a woman with a map. They were searching for anyone who *moved wrong*. And Marguerite had spent thirty years learning to move right, which meant she knew exactly how badly she was failing at it now. Her shoulders sat too high. She checked sightlines twice instead of once. She slept with the map sewn into her coat and woke four times a night to touch it through the fabric like a woman checking a wound.

The dead buyer hadn't helped. Hask Fennel had been found Tuesday morning floating in his own cistern, his ledger burned in his hearth, his front door locked from the inside. The Greymill watch called it misadventure. Marguerite had seen enough drowned men to know when one had been held under, and she'd also noticed that no one in the Order's grey cloaks seemed particularly interested in asking questions about it. They already knew what killed him. They were just waiting to see who came next.

Which meant whoever came next was her.

She told herself, for the second day running, that she could still walk away. Burn the map. Melt the wax seal off it, feed it to the river, forget the courier's blood-warm hand pressing it into hers, forget the words he'd spent his last breath on: *the voice below the stone.* People died in Greymill every week over debts far smaller than this. The world would not notice one more secret fed to the current.

But she kept seeing the courier's face. Not the wound — she'd seen plenty of wounds — but the *certainty*. He had looked at her the way dying men look at priests. He had chosen her. Out of every traveler on that road, bleeding out in the dark, he had pressed two hundred years of somebody's terrible secret into the hands of a mercenary who prayed to a god that never answered, because apparently the world had a sense of humor after all.

And underneath all of it, quieter than she wanted to admit: the map was beautiful. She'd studied it by candlelight until she could draw half of it from memory. It wasn't drawn so much as *composed* — smuggler roads she knew marked alongside roads she didn't, and threaded through them all, a route that descended rather than crossed, down beneath Thornfell's roots to a chamber marked not with an X but with a single symbol: a circle bisected by a line, like a mouth closed by a stitch.

She'd been staring at that symbol too long when the trap finally sprang.

* * *

The underbelly of Greymill was called the Warrens, though nobody official admitted the name. It lived beneath the fish-market, a warren of cellars and connecting tunnels left over from some older town's foundations, where smugglers stored goods and information changed hands over dice games and worse. Marguerite went down into it on the third evening because staying above ground had become its own kind of death sentence, and because a fence named Corla Vetch owed her a favor and owed the truth even more.

Corla kept a back room behind a curtain of hanging eels, dried and glistening, and it was there, in the smell of brine and old rope, that Marguerite saw him.

He was sitting at Corla's table with a cup of tea he clearly wasn't drinking, and he was the most conspicuous thing Marguerite had ever seen in the Warrens. Young — mid-twenties, maybe — with ink-stained fingers, a scholar's stoop, and the kind of pale complexion that came from years spent indoors among books. His clothes were good quality and wrong for the setting: a wool coat cut for a city street, boots soft as gloves. He looked like a library that had learned to walk and immediately regretted it.

And he was watching the door. Watching it the way she'd been watching doors for three days.

Marguerite's hand went to her knife before her mind finished the thought. Because here was the arithmetic, cold and simple: the Order couldn't find her, so they'd set something they *could* find. A scholar, out of place, nervous, planted in the one cellar in Greymill where a person holding a stolen map might come looking for help. Bait dressed as prey.

The young man looked up. Their eyes met. And she watched him arrive at exactly the same conclusion about her — she saw it happen, saw his face go through recognition, alarm, and terror in the space of a breath, saw his hand slide inside his coat toward whatever a bookish man carried instead of a sword.

Neither of them moved for one full second.

Then everything happened at once.

He came up out of his chair fast, faster than she'd guessed, and she was already moving, knocking the table between them, her knife out and level. He had a short blade of his own — a scholar's knife, thin, meant for cutting pages — and he held it wrong, grip too high, elbow tucked, but he held it *between* them, which was the only part that mattered.

"Put it down," Marguerite said quietly.

"You first." His voice cracked on the second word, which undermined the demand considerably, but his eyes didn't waver. That was the strange thing. His hands were shaking and his voice was failing and his stance was a disaster, and none of it mattered, because behind all of it was something harder than fear. "You're one of theirs."

"I'm nothing of theirs."

"The grey cloaks. The Ashen Order." He said the name like it tasted of ash too. "You've been circling Greymill for three days. Everyone in the Warrens has been paid to mention you if you show your face. I bought the same information you did, so don't pretend—"

"Everyone's been *paid*?" Marguerite laughed, low and bitter. "Then you know how this works. You know what I'm worth, and you know what happens to people who collect. So either you're the stupidest bait the Order ever dangled, or—" she shifted her weight, testing him, and his blade tracked her faithfully, which told her he'd had exactly enough training to be dangerous to himself, "—you're not bait at all. You're just another fool carrying something you shouldn't."

Something flickered across his face. Recognition. The specific flinch of a man whose secret has been stepped on.

"That's it, isn't it," she said softly. "You're carrying something."

"I could say the same of you."

"Say it, then."

For a long moment neither of them breathed. The eels swayed gently on their strings between them and the door. Somewhere above, the fish-market was closing; the floorboards groaned with the day's last commerce. Marguerite measured the distance, the angles, the odds. She could take him. Two moves, maybe three. But a fight made noise, and noise in the Warrens brought watchers, and watchers brought grey cloaks, and she was suddenly, exhaustingly tired of doing arithmetic that always ended the same way.

"Corla," she said, without taking her eyes off him. "How long has he been here?"

From behind the eel-curtain, dry as old rope: "Since noon. Paid for the room till midnight. Asked after routes south." A pause. "Also asked after you, love. By description. Didn't give a name."

"See?" The scholar's voice lifted, desperate and fierce at once. "I asked after *you*. Do you understand? If I were working for them, I wouldn't need to ask. They'd have told me where you sleep." He swallowed. "Would you like to know how I know they haven't found where you sleep? Because I've spent two days and most of my money finding out myself, and it nearly broke me, and if the Order could do it that easily they'd have done it already."

Marguerite didn't lower her knife. But she stopped calculating the kill.

"Who are you?"

"Tam." He said it carefully, like setting down something fragile. "Tam Veyranne."

The name landed strangely. Not the surname — surnames were cheap — but the way he said it, the way *Veyranne* seemed to mean something to him beyond itself, some inheritance worn smooth by generations of handling.

"And what are you carrying, Tam Veyranne?"

"What everyone who comes to Greymill is carrying, these days." His gaze dropped, briefly, to her coat — to the place where the map lay sewn against her ribs — and back up. "Or hiding."

The silence stretched. Outside, rain began again, drumming faintly through the ceiling boards.

Here was the truth Marguerite would examine later, in the small hours, turning it over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit: she believed him. Not because of anything he'd proven — he'd proven nothing — but because she recognized the look. It was the courier's look. The look of a person carrying something too heavy, who has run out of places to put it down.

"Knife away," she said. "Slowly."

He hesitated. Then he lowered the page-knife, turned it, and laid it on the table between them, handle toward her. A gesture of surrender, or of trust, or of a gambler pushing in his last stake. She couldn't tell which, and it bothered her that she couldn't.

"My turn," Tam said. "Show me yours."

* * *

They ended up in the back of Corla's room with the eel-curtain drawn and a single candle lit, because Corla charged extra for candles and extra again for discretion, and because Marguerite had decided — provisionally, angrily, with a knife still within easy reach — that the fastest way out of a trap was to walk into it with your eyes open.

Tam unbuttoned his coat with careful fingers and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Inside was a book. Or what remained of one: a quarter-bound volume, its cover scorched, its pages charred at the edges, perhaps a third of it surviving. He opened it with the tenderness of a man touching a relic.

"I burned it," he said. "Three days ago. In my own study, in my own city, with my own hands." He turned a page; the charred paper crackled like frost. "What survived, I brought with me. My life's work. Everything I knew about the silencing of Veyra."

Marguerite went very still.

"Say that again."

"Veyra. The silent god. The god of Thornfell, of the passes, of every oath sworn on stone." He looked up at her, and there was no fear in his face anymore — only the terrible calm of a man who had passed through fear and come out the other side into certainty. "You pray to her. Don't bother denying it. I've watched you do it — at the shrine by the well, yesterday morning. Your lips moved. Old habit. The way soldiers cross themselves before a battle they expect to lose."

"I don't pray," Marguerite said. "Not anymore. There's no one listening."

"No," Tam agreed quietly. "There isn't. That's rather the point."

He turned the burnt book around so she could see the surviving pages. Diagrams. Passages in three scripts, two of which she didn't recognize. And there, repeated across a dozen pages in different hands and different centuries, the symbol from her map: a circle bisected by a line.

"This is the mark of the sealing," Tam said. "Every account agrees on it. Two hundred and twelve years ago, the Ashen Order performed a rite in the deep places of Thornfell. The histories — the ones they permit — say they silenced a mad god. That Veyra's voice had turned destructive, that her truths drove whole valleys to slaughter, that the Order saved the world by taking her voice away." He tapped the symbol. "This is the sigil they carved over the vault mouth. Every sealed site bears it. And your map—" his finger rose, pointing at her coat, steady despite everything, "—your map leads to the largest sealed site in Thornfell. The original one. Where they did it."

"You're guessing."

"I'm reading." He pulled a folded scrap of paper from the book — a rubbing, charcoal on vellum, taken from stone somewhere. The same circle-and-line, surrounded by script. "I've spent nine years studying the silencing. Nine years, and every archive that would have me, and every archive that wouldn't. And three days ago I found a reference in a text I wasn't supposed to be able to read, and I understood what the Order actually buried under Thornfell." He leaned forward, candlelight carving shadows under his eyes. "They didn't bury a mad god, Marguerite Thornbury. They buried her *voice*. Cut free of her, caged in stone, still alive. Still speaking. Into the dark, for two hundred years, with no one to hear it."

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, a cart rattled past, and the eels swayed on their strings.

"That's impossible," Marguerite said.

"It's heresy. Those aren't the same thing."

"They silenced a god." Her voice came out flat, hard, the voice she used for haggling and for killing. "That's what happened. Everyone knows it. The Order muzzled something that would have burned the world, and we've had two hundred years of quiet because of it. I grew up under that quiet. I swore oaths under it. My brother—" She stopped. Swallowed the rest. "The Order isn't kind, but it isn't a lie."

"Everything kind is a lie somewhere underneath." Tam's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "Ask yourself one question. One. If the Order truly saved the world, why do they kill everyone who learns the truth? Why did they drown Hask Fennel in his own cistern for buying a map? Why is there a grey cloak on every road out of this town hunting a leather tube?" He shook his head slowly. "Truth doesn't need that many guards, Marguerite. Only lies do."

She wanted to hit him. She wanted it with a clarity that surprised her — a clean, physical wanting, fist to face, the oldest argument there was. Because he was wrong. He had to be wrong. Because if he was right, then every prayer she'd ever whispered into the empty air had been heard and unanswered not because no one listened, but because someone had *stitched the mouth shut*. And every oath she'd ever sworn to Veyra — including the one sworn over her brother's body, the one that had made her who she was — had been received by a god rendered mute, unable to answer, unable even to say *I hear you.*

Two hundred years of prayers falling on stitched lips.

"You can't know that," she said. "You have charred paper and a symbol and a story. Stories are cheap. I've killed men for less than the stories they told."

"Yes," Tam said simply. "I imagine you have. And yet here you are, three days later, still carrying the map. Still wearing it against your heart." He nodded at her coat. "If you believed the Order's version — if you believed the map led to nothing, or to madness, or to a lie — you'd have burned it by now. You're a practical woman. Practical women don't die for curiosities." He paused. "So the question isn't whether you believe me. The question is why you haven't destroyed it."

Marguerite stood up. Walked to the eel-curtain. Stood there with her back to him, listening to the rain, feeling the map's small stiff rectangle against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Because he was right, and she hated him for it. She hadn't burned it. In three days of dead buyers and watching gates and sleeping with a knife in her hand, she had not burned it, and there was only one honest reason: the courier's face. The certainty in it. *The voice below the stone.* Dying men lied, but rarely with their last breath, and almost never about things that cost them their lives to carry.

"If I do this," she said finally, without turning around, "we do it my way. My roads, my rules. You follow orders. You don't speak to strangers. You don't tell me what to believe." She turned. "And if it turns out you're the Order's creature after all — if this is the longest con ever run — I will kill you somewhere quiet and leave you for the crows, and I will not feel bad about it."

Tam considered her for a moment. Then, absurdly, he smiled — a tired, crooked thing, the first honest expression she'd seen on him.

"That's the most reassuring offer I've had all week," he said. "Yes. Agreed. Your roads, your rules." He began wrapping the burnt book with careful hands. "One condition of mine."

"Of course there is."

"When we reach the vault — if we reach the vault — before you decide anything, you listen to it. To her. Whatever's left of her voice down there in the dark." He looked up, and the candlelight caught something in his eyes that might have been faith or might have been grief; with scholars, Marguerite suspected, the two wore the same face. "Two hundred years is a long time to scream without an answer. Whatever we find, she deserves one hearing. Promise me that."

Marguerite thought of the empty altars. The silent shrines. Thirty years of speaking into a well with no bottom.

"I promise nothing," she said. "But I'll listen."

Outside, the rain thickened, and beneath the sound of it — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable once she heard it — came the tolling of the market bell. Once. Twice. A third time.

Not the hour. The hour had passed already.

Corla's voice came through the eel-curtain, flat and urgent: "Grey cloaks at the fish-market gate, love. Three of them. Asking after a scholar."

Tam went white. "They found me. How did they—"

"Later." Marguerite was already moving, snuffing the candle, pulling her coat tight over the map. "Back way. Can you run?"

"I'm a scholar."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

She grabbed his collar and hauled him toward the cellar's rear passage, into the dark and the dripping stone, as the bell tolled a fourth time and boots began to thunder on the floorboards above.
