## 6. The Smuggler's Road

They left Greymill before the bells rang prime, when the fog still owned the streets and the only witnesses were cats and drunkards too far gone to remember their own names, let alone hers.

Marguerite led them out through the tannery yards, past the lime pits steaming in the cold, and up a goat track so steep that Tam had to use his hands as often as his feet. He complained about it. Not aloud — he was learning that much — but she could hear the complaints building behind his teeth like water behind a dam.

"Say it," she said, not turning around.

"Say what?"

"Whatever it is you're chewing on. You've been chewing since the bridge."

A pause. Gravel skittered under his boots. "I was going to ask how long we'll be walking."

"As long as it takes."

"And where are we walking *to*?"

She stopped on the trail and looked back at him. The fog had thinned enough to show the valley below, Greymill's chimneys bleeding smoke into grey air, and beyond it the first true shoulders of Thornfell rising like the backs of sleeping animals. Somewhere down there, men in ash-grey cloaks were asking questions. By noon they would have answers. By nightfall they would know which road Marguerite had taken — unless she took no road at all.

"We're walking to a place that isn't on any map," she said. "Including yours. Especially yours."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not meant to be." She turned and kept climbing. "Reassuring is for people with nothing worth stealing."

* * *

The smuggler roads of Thornfell were not roads in any way a cartographer would honor. They were habits of the land — deer trails widened by generations of men who had something to move and someone to hide it from. Salt, tin, untaxed brandy, fugitives, corpses. Marguerite had learned them at seventeen from a one-eyed woman named Bracka who had charged her nothing for the knowledge because, as Bracka put it, "the paths keep themselves alive by being walked, girl, and I'm too old to walk them all."

For six years Marguerite had walked them for pay. Now she walked them to stay alive, and found they fit her feet the same either way.

By midmorning they had crossed the spine of the first ridge and dropped into a hanging valley the Order's maps did not contain. She knew this because she had once stolen an Order map, specifically to check. The valley held a tarn the color of old pewter and a shepherd's hut with its roof caved in, and nothing else. It was beautiful in the way that empty places are beautiful, which is to say it made her nervous.

Tam stood at the edge of the tarn catching his breath and staring.

"You've never seen mountains," she said.

"I've seen mountains. From windows. In books." He crouched and touched the water and pulled his hand back fast. "It's colder than it looks."

"Everything here is colder than it looks. Drink upstream of where you wash. Eat before you're hungry. Sleep dry or don't sleep." She was digging in her pack as she talked, laying out strips of dried mutton and a twist of hard bread. "And when I tell you to move, you move. When I tell you to stop, you stop so fast your teeth click. Understood?"

"I'm not a soldier."

"No. You're luggage that talks. But useful luggage, if it does what it's told."

He flushed. She watched him decide not to argue, and felt something that was almost respect. A scholar who could swallow his pride was rarer than a scholar who could read. She'd known plenty of the second kind. Most of them had died arguing about whose fault it was.

They ate. The tarn lay flat and silent around them, and above it the peaks went up and up into a sky scrubbed clean by wind.

"The Order will follow us," Tam said eventually. "Serane doesn't strike me as someone who gives up."

"She won't give up. She'll get better." Marguerite chewed and swallowed. "That's the thing about zealots, Tam. Fanatics burn out. True believers learn. Serane is the kind that learns."

"How do you know her?"

"I don't. I know her type." She brushed crumbs from her knees. "There was an inquisitor in Vell's Crossing, when I was younger. Quiet man. Never shouted, never threatened. He just asked questions until people started answering questions nobody had asked. Whole town turned itself inside out for him in a month. Two hundred souls, and he never raised his voice once."

"What happened to him?"

"He left. That was the frightening part. There was nothing left there worth staying for. He'd already taken everything." She stood, shouldered her pack. "Come on. We've got light enough to make the Narrows before dark, and I'd rather sleep above the treeline than below it."

"Why?"

"Because things that hunt at night live below the treeline." She smiled without warmth. "Mostly."

* * *

The Narrows nearly killed them both, and it was entirely Tam's fault, and he knew it, and to his credit he didn't pretend otherwise.

The passage was a seam in the mountain face where a river had once run and then thought better of it — a slot of stone barely wide enough for a loaded pack, running forty feet between walls slick with seep-water. Marguerite had come through it a dozen times. The trick was to go sideways, pack-first, and never look down at the black gap where the floor gave out over the drop.

She went through first, turned sideways, exhaled, and shuffled across in the dark with her fingertips reading the stone. On the far side she called back: "Sideways. Left shoulder against the wall. Don't stop in the middle."

"I heard you."

"Don't *stop* in the middle."

He got halfway. Then his boot found the loose slab she had warned herself about and hadn't warned him about, because she had forgotten, because she had assumed anyone with functioning eyes would see it — and the slab tipped, and Tam made a sound like a man being unmade, and his arms windmilled, and his satchel swung out over the void with all his notes and his grandmother's secrets swinging inside it.

Marguerite shot her arm back through the gap and caught his wrist.

For a moment they hung there, his weight dragging her half off her own footing, the drop breathing cold air up at them both. Below, somewhere far down, water ran over stones it had been running over since before either of their families existed.

"Drop the satchel," she said.

"No—"

"*Drop it.*"

"I can't, it's—" His voice cracked. "It's everything. It's the research, it's the translations, it's—"

"It weighs more than you do and it's pulling us both over. Drop it or I let go of you and we both find out what you're worth."

A horrible pause. Then his fingers opened, and the satchel fell, and they both listened to it go — a soft leather sound, then a harder sound, then nothing at all. Marguerite hauled him through the gap and they collapsed together on the far side, gasping, two arm-lengths of stone between them and a very long lesson in consequences.

Tam stared at the gap. His face was the color of the tarn.

"My grandmother's letters," he said. "Her account of the sealing. Everything I spent nine years finding." He laughed, a small broken sound. "Nine years. And it's gone in four seconds."

"It's gone because you carried too much and stepped where you weren't looking." She said it flatly, because it was true, and because kindness right now would have been a lie. "You want to survive these mountains? Own nothing you can't throw away in one breath. Including your life. Especially your life. The moment you cling to anything out here, the mountain takes it and takes interest."

"You talk about your own life like it's a coin."

"It is a coin. I spend it carefully." She got up, checked the sun, checked the trail. "What do you remember? Of the letters. Without the paper."

He blinked. Wiped his face with both hands. "More than I expected to," he admitted slowly. "I read them so many times I could recite most of it. The vault's location was always encoded — that was the point of the map — but the *ritual*, the words that wake the voice or bind it, those were in plain text. My grandmother wrote them out deliberately. She said someone should know, even if no one ever acted."

"Then the mountain took paper and kept the gold." She offered him a hand up. He took it. His palm was sweating and shaking and he gripped anyway. "You're heavier than you look, scholar."

"You're stronger than you look, mercenary."

"Everyone says that." She released his hand. "It's the shoulders."

Something happened to his face then — the ghost of a laugh, strangled at birth, but present. First one she'd seen from him. She turned away before it could become anything contagious.

* * *

They made the cave shrine at dusk.

She hadn't planned on it. The shrine sat a half-mile off the route she meant to take, tucked into a fold of the hillside where a spring came out of the rock and ran bright and freezing over red stone. But the light was dying faster than she liked, and the shrine had a deep mouth and a dry floor, and there was a certain argument her body made about prayer that her mind had never managed to overrule.

Veyra's shrines were all the same, wherever you found them in Thornfell. No idol — the god of the high passes had never tolerated likenesses, or so the old stories said. Just a hollowed niche in the living rock, a basin for the spring water, and a ledge for offerings. Once, the ledges would have been crowded: salt, iron nails, braided hair, the first teeth of children, the last coins of the dead. Now every shrine she had entered in six years held nothing but dust and the occasional mouse.

This one held a single item. Someone had left a strip of linen, folded with care, marked with ash in the shape of a closed eye.

Fresh. Days old, maybe less.

Tam saw it too. "Someone still comes here."

"Some people never stopped." Marguerite set down her pack and did not look at the niche. "Praying to a god that doesn't answer. That's not faith. That's habit wearing faith's clothes."

"Is that what you're doing? Habit?"

"I'm not doing anything. I'm making camp."

But her hands, unpacking, slowed. And stopped. And she stood in the mouth of the cave with the spring talking to itself behind her and the ash-eye watching from its ledge, and she heard herself say, in the voice of a much younger woman, kneeling over her brother's body in the snow:

*Veyra, who walks the high passes. Veyra, whose silence is patience. Take my oath and hold it. Take my years and spend them. Only speak.*

Six years of that. Every morning, every night, at every shrine she passed, in every weather, through every job and every wound and every grave. Six years of speaking into a well and hearing nothing come back but her own voice, thinner each time.

She knelt at the basin. Cupped water in her hands. It was so cold it burned.

"Look," Tam began, gently, from behind her, "if this is a ritual you need privacy for—"

"Quiet."

He quieted.

She drank. Set her hands on the wet stone of the basin's rim. Bowed her head, and spoke the old words, and hated herself a little for it, and spoke them anyway.

*Take my oath and hold it.*

The spring stopped.

Not froze — stopped. The water in her cupped hands went still as glass, and the sound of the stream cut off as if a door had closed somewhere underground, and in the sudden silence Marguerite felt the mountain lean toward her. There was no other way to describe it. The whole mass of Thornfell, ten thousand feet of granite and grief, shifted its attention onto the kneeling woman in the dark like a wolf turning its head.

Then, faint as a memory of a memory, something moved beneath the stone. Not a voice. Not yet a voice. A pressure, a rhythm, the way you feel thunder through the soles of your boots before you hear it. Three beats. Pause. Three beats. Like a heart remembering what hearts are for. Like someone on the far side of a very thick wall, knocking.

*Knocking.*

The spring resumed. The water ran. The mountain settled back into being merely a mountain, vast and indifferent and full of ore and bones.

Marguerite stayed kneeling a long time. Her hands were shaking. She told herself it was the cold.

"Well?" Tam's voice, careful, from the shadows. "What happened?"

"Nothing." She stood. Wiped her hands on her thighs. "The spring hiccupped. Happens in karst country. Water finds a new channel, the flow stutters."

"That's not what happens in karst country."

"You're a scholar of sealed libraries, not of springs."

"I'm a scholar of *this*," he said, and now his voice had an edge she hadn't heard before, sharp and bright. "Marguerite. The seal on the vault is failing. That's what my grandmother's letters implied — the binding weakens as the voice grows more desperate. If it's strong enough to reach up through sixty miles of rock and touch a shrine basin, then it's not a hiccup. It's a *knock*. Something down there knows we're coming."

"Or something down there wants us to think it knows we're coming." She busied herself with the fire, striking flint harder than she needed to. "Gods play games, Tam. Dead gods play longer ones. I'm not going to flatter myself that a being two centuries buried has been waiting specifically for me to kneel in the mud."

"Two centuries ago it was screaming loud enough that an entire order of zealots decided the world couldn't afford to hear it. Maybe it hasn't been waiting for *you*. Maybe it's been waiting for *anyone*." He crouched across the fire from her, and the flames lit the underside of his face and made him look older, and more stubborn. "You felt it. Don't tell me you didn't, because your hands haven't stopped shaking since."

She looked at her hands. Stopped them. Looked at him.

"Here's what I know," she said. "I know that for six years I prayed into a hole and got nothing. Tonight I got three knocks. And I know that whatever knocked, it waited until I had a map to its prison and a scholar who can open it. That's not a miracle. That's a fisherman choosing his bait." She fed a branch to the fire. "Hope is a currency, Tam. Everyone spends it. The trick is noticing who's printing it."

"And if it's genuine?" Quietly now. "If the god is real, and wronged, and reaching for the only help it can reach — what then?"

"Then it can knock louder."

She rolled into her bedroll with her back to him and the fire between them, and did not sleep, and did not pray again, and listened to the mountain breathe.

* * *

Dawn came up white and hard. They broke camp in silence, and were halfway down the trail when Marguerite stopped so abruptly that Tam walked into her back.

Below them, maybe two miles distant where the valley narrowed to a defile, a thread of smoke rose straight up into the still morning air. Cookfire smoke. Deliberate, unhurried — the smoke of men who had arrived in the night and were not hiding.

And on the ridgeline opposite, catching the early sun, something flashed. Glass. A spyglass, or a heliograph mirror. Ash-grey figures, small with distance, moving along the crest with the disciplined economy of soldiers who knew exactly where they were going.

"They're ahead of us," Tam breathed. "How are they *ahead* of us?"

"Because Serane doesn't chase." Marguerite's mind was already moving, laying the terrain over the threat like a hand over a wound. "She cuts angles. She guessed our line and she's sitting on it." She turned, scanning the slopes, and her eyes caught on the high shoulder to the west — a route she had sworn, six years ago, over a grave, never to take again.

The old pass. Her brother's pass.

Tam followed her gaze. "That way?"

"No."

"Then which way?"

She stood a moment longer, jaw tight, the cold wind pulling at her cloak. Somewhere below, the Order was lighting cookfires with her name on them. Somewhere above, the mountain had knocked three times and gone quiet.

"There's another way," she said at last. "It's worse."

"Worse than the Narrows?"

"Worse than the Narrows." She shouldered her pack and started climbing west. "Much worse. Keep up, scholar. And whatever you see up there — however many cairns you count — don't ask me who they're for."

Tam opened his mouth. Closed it. Followed.

Behind them, faint on the wind, the smoke rose straight as a plumb line, marking exactly where they were not.
