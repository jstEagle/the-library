## 8. The Ferryman's Debt

The river found them before they found it.

They heard it first as a low grinding beneath the birdsong, a sound like millstones turning somewhere under the world, and then the trees opened and there it was: the Vessa, wide and grey-green and moving with the patient violence of water that had been cutting through these mountains since before the mountains had names. Ice rode the current in flat white plates, clicking against each other like crockery. The far bank was a smudge of pine and shadow.

Marguerite stood at the treeline and felt her stomach drop.

Not from the river. From the crossing.

She'd known this was coming — Tam's map put the Vessa between them and the high passes, and there were only three ways over it in this stretch: the bridge at Kell's Ford, two days' march north and crawling with Order men; the gorge, which killed smugglers every winter and would certainly kill a scholar; or the ferry.

The ferry.

"I've heard about this place," Tam said behind her. He said it the way men say things when they're hoping someone will contradict them. "The ferrywoman. Smugglers talk about her like she's a story you tell children so they don't wander off."

"Smugglers talk about everything like that," Marguerite said. "Half of it's true and the other half is worse." She adjusted the map tube slung across her back and started down toward the water. "Stay close. And whatever she asks you, don't answer before I do."

"What will she ask?"

"That's the problem," Marguerite said. "Nobody knows until they're standing in front of her."

* * *

The ferry house sat on a shelf of rock above a shingle beach, a low building of black timber with a roof furred green by moss, and beside it, moored to an iron ring driven into the stone, the barge itself — broad-beamed, flat-bottomed, old enough that its planks had gone silver-grey and soft-edged, like driftwood. A lantern hung from the eaves though the light was still good, and it burned a color Marguerite didn't love: not oil-yellow but something faintly violet, the color of the sky just before a storm decides.

The ferrywoman was mending a net on the porch.

That was the first strange thing, because no one fished the Vessa this far up. The current ate nets. But she sat there with a wooden needle and a half-made web of cord in her lap, working without looking at her hands, and she did not look up when their boots crunched onto the shingle either. She was older than any woman Marguerite had ever seen — not frail-old, not bent, but worn smooth the way river stones are worn, as if time had given up trying to change her shape and settled for polishing it. Her hair was white as the ice on the current and bound in a single rope down her back. Her hands, Marguerite noticed, were clean. Nobody's hands stayed clean on the Vessa.

"You'll be wanting the crossing," the woman said. Her voice was low and even, a voice for counting money in.

"We are," Marguerite said.

"It's four coppers a head to the near bank. Six if the ice is running heavy, and it is." She pulled the needle through the net. "Payment first. Then the boat."

Marguerite had counted her coin twice that morning, lying awake while Tam snored against his pack. She knew what was in her purse to the copper, and she knew what four coppers each came to, and she knew — with the particular cold clarity that had kept her alive through eleven years of mercenary work — that the arithmetic didn't work. She had eight coppers. The crossing cost eight. Which left nothing for food, nothing for the tolls on the Underroad ahead, nothing for the hundred small bribes that kept people like her breathing in Thornfell.

But that wasn't the real problem, and the ferrywoman knew it, because when Marguerite reached for her purse the old woman finally looked up.

Her eyes were the color of the lantern.

"No," the ferrywoman said, and set down the net. "Not coin. Not from you two."

Marguerite's hand stopped halfway to her belt. "You just named your price."

"I name a price for everyone. It doesn't mean I take it." The woman rose, unhurried, joints moving like well-oiled hinges, and came to the edge of the porch. Up close she smelled of nothing at all, which was somehow worse than the violet lantern. "Coin is for travelers. You're not travelers. You're carrying something." Her gaze slid past Marguerite's shoulder to Tam, who had gone very still in the way of a man who has spent years in archives and has learned that stillness is sometimes the only armor available. "Something the ash-cloaks want very badly. Something that makes the water hum when you walk the bank. I felt you coming a mile downstream."

Tam made a small sound. Marguerite didn't turn to look at him, but she shifted her weight, putting herself fractionally more between the ferrywoman and the map.

"The price stands," she said. "Eight coppers."

"And I told you I'm not taking them." The ferrywoman smiled, and it was a terrible thing, kind and hungry at once. "Sit. Both of you. Let me tell you how the crossing works, since your friend here only knows the stories, and the stories are wrong in all the important places."

* * *

They did not sit. They stood, because sitting felt like surrender, and the ferrywoman talked anyway, her needle moving again through the net as if the conversation were merely weather.

"This river runs on debt," she said. "Everything does, if you look closely enough — coin is just debt you can hold in your hand. But the Vessa is honest about it. Nothing crosses me without paying, and I don't set the price. The crossing sets the price. I only read it." She nodded at Marguerite's purse. "For most folk, it reads coin. For some it reads labor — I've had soldiers row for me, had a poet who owed me a song, had a woman once who paid with a year of her dreams, poor creature slept like the dead after and woke poorer than she knew. The crossing knows what you can spare better than you do."

"And for us?" Tam asked. His voice was steady. His knuckles, wrapped around the strap of his satchel, were not.

"For you?" The ferrywoman looked at him for a long moment, and Marguerite watched something move behind those violet eyes — recognition, maybe, or appetite. "You I could take cheap. You're full to the brim, scholar. Memories stacked like cordwood, and half of them you'd thank me for taking. Your grandmother's kitchen. Your first library. The smell of the sealed stacks, dust and lamp-oil and the particular silence of forbidden shelves." Tam flinched as if she'd struck him. "Oh yes. I know what you carry. The river tells me things. That's the other part the stories get wrong — I don't fish this water. This water fishes for me."

"His memories aren't for sale," Marguerite said.

"Everything's for sale. That's the whole lesson of the world; I'd have thought a sellsword would have learned it by now." The needle paused. "But he's not what interests me. He's freight. You're the cargo, Thornbury."

Marguerite went cold from the boots up. "I never gave you my name."

"You didn't have to." The ferrywoman set down the net at last, folded her hands, and regarded her with an expression that was almost gentle. "You swore an oath to a silent god over your brother's body. Oaths leave marks. Yours is written on you like a brand — I can read it from the far bank. Veyra's mark, half-faded, still bleeding at the edges. Do you know what that makes you, girl? It makes you the last living contract the god has. Everything else burned or ran. You stayed."

The wind came off the water and Marguerite let it, because her face was doing something she didn't want witnessed.

"So here's my price," the ferrywoman said. "Not coin. Not his memories, though the offer stands, scholar, and I make it honestly — I'd give you a lighter heart for it, and the river would carry us fat and happy all season." She leaned forward. "Yours. One memory. The crossing chooses which. That's the toll. That's the trade. One memory out of your head, into the water, and you ride dry-shod to the far bank with your map and your scholar and your secrets intact."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk upstream," the ferrywoman said pleasantly, "and you find out why nobody walks upstream."

* * *

They walked upstream.

It took Marguerite a quarter mile of shingle to trust her voice again, and even then what came out was mostly breath. Tam matched her stride in silence, which she was grateful for, because anything he said right now — *we should go back, we should pay, we should have paid* — was going to make her do something regrettable.

It wasn't the price itself. She'd paid worse. She'd watched friends pay worse. Eleven years of the trade taught you that survival was always itemized; you just didn't always get to see the bill until afterward. No — what had turned her blood to river-water was the certainty in the old woman's voice. *The crossing chooses which.* Not *you choose*. The thought of some current-eyed ancient reaching into her head and lifting out whatever it liked — her brother's laugh, the weight of him on her shoulders the summer he broke his ankle, the exact sound of Doss swearing over a bad card hand — and keeping it, feeding it to the water —

"There's a path," Tam said finally. "Above the gorge. I read about it. The drovers used it before the Order closed the high roads — switchbacks cut into the north face, they called it the Ladder."

"The Ladder's a graveyard with steps."

"Then we'll be among company." He almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "You could have paid, you know. One memory. Against everything else we're carrying, it seemed—"

"Don't." Marguerite stopped and faced him, and the wind pushed her braid across her mouth and she pushed it back. "You don't get to decide it's cheap. Neither does she. That's the trick of people like her — and people like the Order, if you haven't noticed — they make the price sound reasonable. One memory. One name. One village. One god's voice. There's always a reason, and the reason always sounds fair right up until you're standing on the far bank wondering what's missing." She started walking again, faster. "I've seen men sell pieces of themselves one toll at a time until there wasn't enough left to stand a watch. I'm not doing it. Not for a river. Not for a god. Not for anyone."

Tam was quiet for ten paces. Twenty. Then: "My grandmother crossed this river once. Before the sealing. She wrote about it — a line in her journal, just one line, I thought it was nothing. *Paid the ferrywoman and forgot why I wept.*" He hugged the satchel tighter. "I thought it was poetry."

Marguerite said nothing. The river ground its ice below them, patient as a creditor.

* * *

The Ladder was exactly as bad as remembered, which meant it was worse than described.

The path clung to the gorge's north wall like a scar, a shelf of hacked stone barely wide enough for one boot in front of the other, dropping away on the left into a hundred feet of churning grey. Spray off the rapids had frozen in curtains along the rock face, and where the switchbacks turned, the ice had built up in ridges that had to be broken with a knife butt before each step. Tam moved like a man defusing something — slow, deliberate, sweating despite the cold, his city boots finding purchase where none existed and his breath coming in little prayers to saints Marguerite was fairly sure were fictional.

"Left foot on the ledge," she called back, for the fourth time. "The ledge, not the ice. Trust the stone, not the shine."

"I am trusting the stone," Tam said through his teeth. "The stone and I have an understanding."

Below them the Vessa roared through the narrows, and the sound filled the gorge so completely that Marguerite heard the riders before she saw them — or rather, she heard the absence of the river, a dead patch in the roar upstream, the way sound goes flat around a large body.

She flattened against the rock and put out one arm, and Tam, bless his terrified archive-trained instincts, dropped without a word.

They came around the bend above the ferry landing: six riders, ash-grey cloaks stiff with frost, moving at a walk along the bank. At their head rode a figure on a pale horse whose bearing Marguerite recognized even at distance and through spray — the particular upright rigidity of someone who has never once doubted themselves. An officer. Behind the riders, at the landing, the black ferry house sat with its violet lantern lit, and the ferrywoman stood on her porch watching the Order arrive with her net folded over her arm, and Marguerite would have given a month's pay to hear that conversation.

One of the riders dismounted at the shingle. Knelt. Rose. Pointed — not at the ferry house, not at the water.

Up. At the cliff face. At the Ladder.

"They've got a tracker," Tam breathed. "Or a—"

"Move." Marguerite hauled him up by the collar. "Don't run, run smart. Steps first, then pace. If you fall here you die loud and I can't stop to grieve, so don't."

They climbed. The gorge narrowed, the path steepened, the ice thickened, and below them the riders reached the base of the first switchback and began, unhurried, to follow. Unhurried, Marguerite noted, and hated them for it. Men who hurry make mistakes. Men who know the prey is trapped on a ledge with a drop on one side and a wall on the other can afford patience.

At the third switchback the path passed under an overhang where an ancient rockfall had left a wedge of stone balanced above the trail, propped on rubble, furred with ice. Marguerite had marked it on the way up without knowing why. Now she knew.

"Tam. See the wedge?"

He looked. She watched him understand — watched the horror arrive, then the arithmetic, then the question he was too decent to ask quickly.

"It might not hold a man's weight," he said carefully. "If someone were to... brace against it..."

"It held my weight coming up. It won't hold six riders' worth of vibration, not with the freeze-thaw working the crack." She drew her knife and hammered the butt into the seam above the prop, once, twice, feeling for the hollow note of loose stone. There it was — a deep drum-sound, a tooth already aching. "I'm not killing them. I'm closing the road. There's a difference, and I need you to believe there's a difference, because I need one person on this mountain who thinks I'm not a monster."

"I think," Tam said, gripping the wall as the first rider's horse came into view two switchbacks below, "that you're a person who keeps choosing the least terrible thing available, and that someday the list runs out." He met her eyes. "Do it."

She hit the seam three more times, hard, angling the blows, and the wedge groaned, shed its ice in a white sheet, and came down.

The sound was enormous. The path simply ceased — forty feet of ledge became rubble, became dust, became a plume of snow-smoke boiling up out of the gorge, and the first rider wheeled his horse back from the edge with a scream that the river swallowed whole. When the dust cleared there was a gap in the Ladder wide as a chapel door and twice as holy, and on the far side of it, Marguerite and Tam stood gasping, and on the near side, six ash-grey figures stood looking up at them across thirty feet of empty air.

The officer removed her hood. Even at distance, even through spray, Marguerite saw that it was a woman, young, composed, with a face like a drawn blade — and that she was not angry. That was the worst part. She looked up at the gap, then at Marguerite, with the mild interest of a clerk finding an error in a ledger, and made a small note in a small book, and turned away.

"She's marking us," Tam whispered. "Why is she marking us? Why isn't she—"

"Because she doesn't need to chase us." Marguerite's mouth had gone dry. "Because she already knows where we're going. The map only leads one place, Tam. We're not running from her anymore." She shouldered her pack and turned up the trail, away from the river, away from the violet lantern burning patiently in the dusk below. "We're running ahead of her. There's a difference, and I need you to believe there's a difference."

Behind them, faint under the river's grind, she could have sworn she heard the ferrywoman laughing.

Ahead, the pass climbed into dark, and somewhere in that dark, she knew, the road home was already waiting — the one road she had sworn never to walk again, and the only one left.
