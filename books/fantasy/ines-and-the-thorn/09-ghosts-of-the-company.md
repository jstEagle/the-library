## 9. Ghosts of the Company

They came out of the treeline at dusk, and Marguerite knew them before she saw a single face — by the way the birds went quiet in a widening ring, by the discipline of it. Bandits made noise. Wolves made noise. Only the Thornbury Blades moved through country like they owned the deed to it.

She was banking the fire when the first whistle came, three notes rising, the old signal for *parley, weapons sheathed*. Her hands kept working. Her heart did not.

"Company," Tam said from his bedroll, and there was no question in it. He'd heard her stories for two weeks on the road — told around fires, mostly, because that was where her stories lived: Doss breaking a siege line with a stolen goat and a barrel of pitch; Wren singing the wounded to sleep with songs in no language anyone knew; the winter the Blades ate their boots and called it a feast. "How many?"

"Twelve, if they're all here." She stood, brushed ash from her knees. "Doss doesn't travel light when he means it."

"Does he mean it?"

She looked north, where the pines stood black against a bruised sky, and thought about the coin the Order must have weighed out to buy the Thornbury Blades whole. The Blades had never been cheap. They had never been for sale at all, as far as she'd known.

"He means it," she said.

* * *

They came down through the trees in loose file, torches unlit, the last grey light doing their work for them. Marguerite counted eleven and then stopped counting, because the twelfth figure walking at the front wore a captain's half-cloak pinned with a boar's tusk, and twenty years fell away like snow off a branch.

Doss had gone grey at the temples. Everything else was the same — the wide hands, the scar splitting his left eyebrow, the way he walked like the ground owed him money. He stopped ten paces off, close enough to talk, far enough to be respectful of what either of them might do next.

"Margo." Her old name, the one only the company had ever used. It landed in her chest like a thrown stone.

"Doss." She kept her voice level. "You're a long way from the Salt Road."

"I'm wherever the work is." His eyes went past her to Tam, cataloguing him the way he'd catalogue any asset: threat, value, liability. "You look thin."

"You look employed."

A few of the others shifted. She knew them all. Brannic at the left flank, still favoring the knee she'd watched an ogre's maul ruin at Karst Ford. Little Wren, who wasn't little anymore, who'd been fifteen when Marguerite left and was now broad-shouldered and hard-eyed and holding a crossbow like she'd been born to it. And Ferro, at Doss's right hand, grinning the way he always grinned before something went wrong.

"We heard you died," Wren said. Her voice hadn't changed at all. That was somehow worse than everything else.

"Rumors of my death were slightly exaggerated," Marguerite said. "Mostly by me."

Doss took a step forward. "Here's how this goes, Margo, because I'd rather say it plain than dance it. Three days ago, riders in grey found us at Hollow Bridge. Paid us more than we've seen in five years of contracts. One job. Retrieve a map and the man carrying knowledge of it, deliver both to their inquisitor, walk away rich." He spread his hands. "Then we heard whose trail they wanted followed. Yours."

"And you took the coin anyway."

"I took the coin," Doss said, "because if I didn't, they'd have hired someone worse. At least this way you get a choice." He let that sit. "Give me the map, Margo. Give me the scholar. You walk south tonight with your life and my word nobody follows. That's not a threat. That's the best deal anyone in these mountains is going to offer you, and I'm offering it because I buried friends beside you and I don't want to bury you too."

Behind her, she felt Tam go very still. She didn't blame him. It was a good speech. It was even, she suspected, sincere.

The problem was that she'd spent two weeks learning what the map was. What the Order had done. What Veyra's silence had cost — the empty altars, the prayers that fell like stones into wells with no bottom. If she handed over the map, the silence stayed. Forever. Sealed under the Order's doctrine and Doss's coin.

And if she refused —

"Ferro," she said quietly. "Still carrying that grin?"

"Always, Captain Thornbury." He bowed, mocking, sweet.

"Then I'll ask once." She let her hand fall to her sword hilt, slow, deliberate, so every one of them could see it. "Stand aside."

* * *

It happened the way these things always happened — fast, ugly, and nothing like the drills.

Ferro moved first, because Ferro always moved first, and that was the whole tragedy of him. He came in low with his short blade, the same opening he'd used in a hundred sparring yards, and Marguerite read it the way she read her own heartbeat. She turned inside his guard, caught his wrist, and drove her knee up into the meat of his thigh — a disabling blow, a *sparring* blow, the kind you gave a friend.

But Ferro had spent six years getting better while she'd spent six years getting harder, and he twisted with the strike instead of against it, and his blade opened her forearm to the bone of her sleeve before she could disengage. Blood sheeted hot down her wrist. She heard Tam shout. She heard Wren's crossbow snap and felt the bolt tear through her cloak instead of her throat because Tam had lunged against her at exactly the wrong right moment.

Then it was all noise and torchlight — someone had lit a torch, which meant someone meant killing — and Marguerite stopped being a person saying goodbye to her family and became the thing the mountains had made her.

She broke Brannic's guard with a pommel strike and put him down on his bad knee, gentle as she could, which was not very. She caught Wren reloading and closed the distance before the girl could crank the windlass, slammed her into a pine, and held her there with a forearm across the collarbone while Wren's eyes went wide and furious and, underneath it, hurt.

"You trained me," Wren snarled.

"I trained you to duck." Marguerite wrenched the crossbow free and hurled it into the dark. "Duck better."

Then Doss hit her.

He'd always fought like a landslide — patient, total, no wasted motion — and he came in now with his longsword flat, trying to break her arms rather than open them, which was its own kind of mercy and she hated him for it. Steel rang in the dark. Around them the Blades pulled back into a ring, because this was company law older than any of them: captains settled it, everyone else watched.

"You're going to die for a piece of leather," Doss grunted, driving her back a step.

"I'm going to live for one." She slipped his next cut and answered with a slash across his sword arm — shallow, warning, the same mercy he was showing her. "That's the difference between us tonight."

"The Order will send worse than us."

"Let them send worse."

"They'll send *me*, Margo." For a moment, in the torchlight, the captain fell away and it was just Doss, tired, grey, holding a sword he didn't want to use. "Next time there won't be a parley. Next time I'll have orders and no room under them. Walk away. Please."

Her arm was bleeding freely. Her breath came ragged. And for one long second she stood in the ring of her oldest friends and wanted, with a nakedness that shamed her, to put the sword down. To take the deal. To have a table to eat at again, and people who knew her name, and a fire she didn't have to bank alone.

Then she thought of the courier dying in the dirt with the map pressed into her hands. *The voice below the stone.* A debt she'd never asked to owe, and the only currency she had left to pay it with was everything she was standing here refusing to give up.

"No," she said.

She feinted high, dropped low, and swept Doss's legs — the oldest trick in the company book, the one he'd taught her, the one he should have seen coming and would hate himself forever for missing. He went down hard. She put her boot on his sword wrist and her point at the join of his neck and shoulder, and held it there, trembling, over the pulse of the man who'd taught her to hold a blade.

Nobody breathed.

"Go home, Doss," she said. "Tell the Order you lost me in the passes. Take their coin and drink to my ghost. Because if you follow me again, I swear on every grave we ever dug together, I'll stop pulling my cuts."

His eyes searched hers, looking for the bluff, finding none of it. Whatever he saw there made him close his eyes briefly, like a man accepting a wound.

"Company," he said hoarsely. "Weapons down. We're done here."

Marguerite stepped back. She sheathed her sword with blood-slick fingers, picked up her pack, and took Tam's shoulder to steer him toward the dark of the treeline. Behind her, the Blades parted to let them pass, and not one of them met her eyes except Wren, who stared at her with something that wasn't hatred yet but was learning the shape of it.

At the edge of the firelight, Doss spoke once more, quiet enough that only she could hear.

"That wound needs stitching."

"I know how to stitch."

"I know." He almost smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen all night. "Walk fast, Margo."

* * *

They climbed for an hour before she let them stop, putting ridges and river-noise between themselves and the camp. In the black socket of an old bear den, by feel rather than light, she stripped her sleeve and stitched her own forearm with shaking hands while Tam held a shielded candle stub and pretended his hands weren't shaking worse.

"She aimed wide," Tam said finally. "The crossbow girl. First bolt went a foot high. Deliberate."

"Wren." Marguerite tied off the knot with her teeth. "Yes."

"And the big one — Brannic? He fell without fighting after the first exchange. I've seen men surrender. That wasn't surrender. That was *permission*."

"Brannic's knee's been bad since Karst Ford. Maybe he couldn't have chased us anyway." She wrapped the arm, tight, and leaned her head back against cold stone. "Or maybe half my old company just decided I wasn't worth the coin. Don't build a cathedral out of it, Tam. Ferro fought true. Ferro cut me to the bone."

"But Doss—"

"Doss did his job." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "He offered me the door first. Then he tried to put me through it gently. Then he lost. That's a professional soldier having a bad contract, not a rescue."

Tam was quiet a while. The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound like distant voices.

"You're bleeding grief," he said at last, carefully, the way he handled anything fragile. "I can see it. You're allowed to call it what it is."

"My grief is my own business."

"It stopped being only your business the day you didn't sell that map in Greymill." He said it without accusation, which was the most infuriating thing about him. "Every mile we walk, your business becomes mine. So I'll say it plainly: those people love you. Some of them disobeyed orders tonight because of it. And the Order will notice which ones. When Serane's agents go through that camp — and they will — she'll learn that the Thornbury Blades' hearts weren't in it. Do you understand what happens to soldiers whose hearts aren't in it?"

Marguerite looked at her bandaged arm, at the blood soaking through, hers and Ferro's mixed past telling apart.

"They get replaced," she said. "Or purged."

"Purged," Tam agreed softly. "So every friend you have left is now standing in front of the same wall we're running from. That's what the map costs. That's what it has *always* cost. People who touch it stop belonging anywhere safe."

She wanted to argue. She opened her mouth to, and found nothing there but the memory of Wren's face — hurt under the fury, the face of a girl she'd taught to hold a knife — and shut her mouth again.

"We move at first light," she said instead. "There's a pass east of here the Blades don't know. Old smuggler road. It drops into the valley below the Hanged Men."

Tam frowned. "Why is it called that?"

"Because of what the Order hung there, two hundred years ago." She pulled her cloak over the bandage and lay back against the stone, staring at the black ceiling of the den. "Everyone the silence swallowed. Priests, singers, anyone who claimed to hear Veyra speak. The Order strung them along the pass as a lesson."

"And you want to walk into it."

"I want to walk *through* it," Marguerite said. "And tomorrow night, when we camp in the middle of all those bones, you're going to tell me everything your sealed library knew about why. All of it. No scholar's hedging. Because Doss offered me a clean death today, Tam, and I turned it down, and I intend to know exactly what I bought with the refusal."

In the dark, she heard him swallow.

"All of it," he said.

Outside, far behind them and below, a single bird cried out in the night and went silent — and Marguerite, drifting toward a sleep she knew would be full of torchlight and old names, could not shake the feeling that it sounded almost like a bell. Like something far beneath the stone, turning over in its long sleep, beginning to listen.
