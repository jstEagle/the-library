## 10. What Tam Remembered

The fire was small — Marguerite's rule, not his — and Tam sat across it watching her clean her knives the way other people prayed: slowly, precisely, with total attention.

They had made nine miles since dawn, following a deer trail that was barely a rumor of a path, and she had spoken perhaps forty words the entire day. He had counted. Counting words was easier than counting the things he wasn't saying.

The map lay between them, wrapped in oilcloth, and every hour he didn't speak made the lie heavier. Not a lie, exactly. An omission. His grandmother would have called it an omission and then boxed his ears for it anyway.

"Your hands are shaking," Marguerite said without looking up.

"It's cold."

"It's not cold." She tested an edge against her thumb, found it acceptable, sheathed the knife. "You've been shaking since Greymill. You shake when you're deciding something. So decide."

Tam looked at the fire. Sparks climbed toward a sky needled with stars, and somewhere above them the peaks of Thornfell stood black against blacker, holding whatever they held beneath their roots. Two hundred years of stone. Two hundred years of his family's name carved into the underside of it, where no one was meant to look.

"If I tell you something," he said, "you're going to want to kill me."

"That's true of most people I meet." She set down the whetstone. "Tell me anyway."

* * *

He had rehearsed this confession for three days, in the privacy of his own skull, and every version collapsed under its own weight. So he did what scholars do when the argument fails: he started at the beginning and hoped the structure would save him.

"The Sealed Library keeps records the Order doesn't know it keeps," he said. "Copies of copies. Things smuggled out before the purges. I told you I was a cataloguer. That's true. What I didn't tell you is what I catalogued."

Marguerite said nothing. The fire popped. He took it as permission.

"When Veyra was silenced — two hundred and six years ago, by the reckoning we use — the histories say the god went mad. That its voice turned on its own faithful, that the Order did what had to be done to protect the mountains. Every text agrees. Every text was written after." He swallowed. "I found a document from before. A work ledger. Quarry accounts, wages paid, timber requisitions. Boring beyond belief, which is why it survived — no one burns an invoice. Except this invoice was for the building of a vault. And the mason's mark at the bottom—" His voice caught. He steadied it. "The mason's mark was my family's. The Vessics. We built the cage."

Marguerite's face did nothing at all. That was worse than anger.

"They weren't forced," Tam went on, because stopping now would be mercy and he hadn't earned it. "That's the part the ledger makes clear. Paid well, treated as honored craftsmen, given a blessing by the Grand Inquisitor himself. My ancestors built the vault that holds Veyra's voice, and they knew what they were building, and they were proud of it. There's a letter. One of the masons wrote to his brother about it. *We have done a great thing for the faith. The mountain will keep its silence forever.*" He looked at his hands. "That man was my great-great-great-grandfather's brother. Give or take."

The wind moved through the pines above them. Marguerite reached over and fed a branch to the fire, unhurried, and Tam understood she was letting him hang himself completely before she cut him down.

"And the map?" she said.

"My grandmother's." The word came out of him like a splinter leaving flesh. "Sera Vessic. She was an archivist too, before me. Before the Library sealed the deep stacks. She spent forty years assembling what the Order destroyed — fragments, mostly. And near the end, the route. The actual route into the vault, reconstructed from quarry ledgers and a pilgrim's diary and half a burned survey chart. She hid it inside a devotional — a psalter, innocuous, blessed twice. When she died it passed to my mother, and my mother couldn't read the cipher, and neither could I, at first. It took me six years." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "Six years of penance disguised as scholarship. Because I knew what my family had done, and I thought if I could find the voice — if I could give it back — the debt might balance."

He finally looked up at her.

"I didn't lie to you, Marguerite. Everything I told you about the vault, the seal, the restoration rite — all true. But I let you believe I was a stranger who stumbled into this. I'm not. I'm the heir to the people who built your god's prison. The courier died carrying my grandmother's map. Whatever the Order is doing to the mountains right now, they learned the trail existed because someone like me had been asking questions someone like me should never have asked."

Silence. Long enough that he heard his own pulse.

Then Marguerite stood up.

* * *

She didn't draw a blade. Somehow that was more frightening.

"You knew." Her voice was level in the way ice is level — thin, and hiding depth. "In Greymill. When you explained the map. When I asked how a library scholar reads Order cipher, you said you'd studied it. You stood there and watched me decide to trust you with my life, knowing your family built the thing we're walking toward."

"Yes."

"Yes," she repeated. She began to pace, three steps, turn, three steps, a soldier's economy even in fury. "Do you know what I've lost since I took that map off a dying man? Do you know what I've spent? There are Blades behind us — my Blades, my company, hunting me because of this. There's an Inquisitor ahead of us who pulls threads like a spider with a budget. And the whole time, the whole *time*, I've been marching beside the one man alive who could have told me what we're really walking into. Not a vault. A *family business*."

"I was going to tell you."

"When?"

Tam had no answer. She stopped pacing and faced him fully, and in the firelight her eyes were the color of wet slate.

"When we reached the door? When it was too late to turn back? Or never — when it stopped mattering whether I consented because I'd already bled for it?" She shook her head slowly. "I've been sold before, Tam. By captains, by employers, by men who swore oaths prettier than yours. I know the shape of it. The only difference is you feel bad while you do it."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Fairness is for people who don't get lied to."

He stood up too, and the motion surprised them both — soft-handed Tam of the archives, on his feet, voice cracking but holding. "And what would you have done? If I'd told you in Greymill, in that cellar, with the buyer's blood still drying upstairs? You'd have left me there. Or worse. Don't tell me otherwise — I watched you interrogate a fence with a knife through his sleeve. I told you the truth as fast as I could survive telling it. That's not nothing. That's the best I could manage, and I managed it, and here we are, nine days from the vault, and I'm the only person in Thornfell who knows how to open it."

"Is that what this is? Leverage?"

"This is *penance*!" It came out louder than he intended, echoing off the rocks, and he dropped his voice to a furious whisper. "You think I wanted this inheritance? You think I sleep well? I have spent my entire adult life trying to unmake what my family made, and every step of it has cost — my post, my name, my safety, and now—" he gestured at the dark, at the road, at everything, "—now it's costing *you*, and I hate that more than you can possibly—"

"Don't." Her hand was on her knife now, though it hadn't been a moment ago. "Don't tell me what you hate. You want to know what I hate? I prayed to Veyra for eleven years. Eleven years of silence. I thought the god had abandoned us, or died, or never existed, and I kept praying anyway because the habit was the only faith I had left. And the whole time the god wasn't gone. It was *muzzled*. Buried. Screaming into stone while my family—" she caught herself, jaw tightening, "—while *someone's* family got rich on quarry contracts."

"My family," Tam said quietly. "Say it. It's mine to carry."

For a moment he thought she would strike him. He braced for it, almost welcomed it — pain as arithmetic, a payment rendered.

Instead she sat back down across the fire, abruptly, like a puppet with cut strings, and put her face in her hands.

* * *

They stayed like that a long time. Tam didn't dare move. Above them the stars wheeled their slow indifferent courses, and the fire burned down to coals, and Marguerite Thornbury — who had killed men for lesser betrayals, who trusted no one and slept with a blade under her cheek — sat with her head bowed as though the weight of two centuries had finally found her shoulders.

When she spoke again, her voice was different. Smaller. Raw at the edges.

"My brother swore an oath to Veyra the day he turned sixteen. Same as me. Kellen. He believed — really believed, the way you only can before the world gets its hands on you. He used to say the god listened best in the high passes, so he'd go up alone sometimes, just to talk." She lifted her head. Her eyes were dry, which somehow made it worse. "He died in the Underroad. Cave-in, seven years ago. I was twenty feet away. And the last thing he said — the last thing — was that Veyra would keep him. That the god had *told* him, once, in the high passes. That he'd hear the voice again on the other side."

Tam's throat tightened. "Marguerite—"

"There was no voice. There was never any voice. He died believing in a god that had already been stolen out from under him, and I buried that oath with him, and I have hated Veyra ever since for being silent." She laughed, short and terrible. "Except it wasn't silence, was it? It was a theft. Someone robbed my brother of the thing he died holding onto, and I've spent seven years blaming the victim."

"You didn't know."

"Neither did you." She looked at him across the coals, and something in her expression shifted — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but recognition. The grim kinship of people carrying loads they never chose. "Two hundred years ago your family built a cage. Seven years ago I swore hatred at a god for a crime committed against it. We're both paying interest on debts we didn't sign for." She picked up a stick and stirred the embers, hard, as if the fire owed her money. "Doesn't make me forgive you. You should have told me."

"I know."

"But you're telling me now. In the open, with no leverage, when I could still walk away." She exhaled through her nose. "Doss would say that's the first honest act of a coward. Doss is wrong about most things. It's the first honest act of a man who ran out of road."

"Is that what this is? The end of the road?"

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "Ask me tomorrow. Tonight I'm too tired to decide whether to hate you properly." She tossed the stick down and lay back against her pack, blade drawn and laid along her thigh, an old habit. "Sleep. We cross the scree at first light, and after that the Order's patrols thicken. If you have any other confessions, Tam of the Sealed Library, I'd have them tonight."

He hesitated. There was one more thing. There was always one more thing.

"Sera — my grandmother — she didn't just reconstruct the route," he said. "Her notes say the seal was designed to fail. Slowly. Deliberately. The masons built a flaw into the vault — a fault line in the working, something the Order never detected. She believed they did it on purpose. That some part of them couldn't live with what they'd done, so they left a door." He met Marguerite's eyes across the dead fire. "If she was right, the vault isn't just a prison. It's a countdown. And we may already be too late to choose anything at all."

Above them, far up the black shoulder of the mountain, something shifted — a sound too deep for avalanche, too rhythmic for rockfall, like a vast throat clearing after two hundred years of silence.

Marguerite's hand closed around her knife.

"First light," she said. "We run."
