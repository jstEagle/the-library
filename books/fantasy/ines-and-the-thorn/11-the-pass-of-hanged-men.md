## 11. The Pass of Hanged Men

The storm came down on them like a debt collector.

Marguerite had watched it build all afternoon — a bruise-colored wall rolling off the high peaks, swallowing the ridgelines one by one, and she had said nothing to Tam because there was nothing to say. There was no shelter between them and the pass, and the pass was the only way through. So they walked into it, as her father had walked into it, as every smuggler who ever worked Thornfell had walked into it, and you did not speak about it because speaking made it real.

By dusk the wind was a living thing. It came screaming up the gorge with knives of ice in its teeth, and the snow didn't fall so much as travel sideways, in white sheets that erased the world ten feet at a time. Marguerite went first, rope around her waist, the other end knotted under Tam's arms like he was cargo. He had stopped complaining an hour ago. She could hear him behind her only when the wind paused for breath — the crunch of his boots, the wet rattle in his chest that she didn't like at all.

The gallows began where the gorge narrowed.

She knew they would be there. Everyone who ran the Underroad's upper branches knew. The Order had built them two hundred years ago, during the purges, when Veyra's faithful were strung up along the trade passes as a message written in bodies: *this road is closed to god-speakers.* The Order had never taken them down. Why would they? A warning kept working long after the men who hung it were dust.

Even blind in the whiteout, Marguerite felt them coming before she saw them — a wrongness in the echo, the way sound came back from the rock walls too quickly, too flatly. Then the snow thinned for a heartbeat and there they were: seven frames of black timber marching up both sides of the gorge, maybe forty of them in all, their crossbeams furred with frost. Most hung empty now, chains swaying and clanking in the gale like slow bells.

Most.

Three still held shapes. Old ones, mummified by the cold, bound in rusted wire rather than rope because rope rotted and the Order wanted these to last. They turned slowly on their chains above the road, faces gone to leather, arms out stiff, and the wind moved through their ribs and made a low moaning note that Marguerite had heard once before, when she was nine years old, and had spent thirty years trying to forget.

"Veyra's mercy," Tam breathed against her shoulder. He'd come up close enough to shout over the wind without shouting. "Are those—"

"Priests." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Veyran priests. The Order hanged them here so every caravan would see what happens to people who pray out loud." She tugged the rope. "Keep moving. Don't look up."

"I'm looking up."

"I know you are. Stop."

He didn't, quite. She heard his footsteps falter beneath the third gibbet, and then he said something she almost lost to the wind — "They left them here. Two hundred years, and no one cut them down?" — and there was such raw horror in it that Marguerite stopped too, despite herself, despite the cold eating through her seams.

"The Order calls them the Vigil Posts," she said. "Faithful families used to sneak up here at night to bury whoever fell. The Order started hanging them higher." She looked up at the turning dead, at the frost in their empty eye sockets, and found that the old anger was still there, banked but alive, hotter than the storm. "Come on. There's a cave past the last frame. We make it there or we freeze standing up, and I refuse to die somewhere the Order uses as furniture."

* * *

The cave was exactly where her memory said it would be, which surprised her more than it should have. Memory was a liar; she'd learned that young. But the crack in the north wall was there, half-hidden behind an icefall, just wide enough to squeeze through sideways, and beyond it the passage opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, dry-floored with old gravel, its ceiling lost in black.

Marguerite got a fire going small and mean, screened behind her pack, and they thawed in shifts while the storm screamed outside like something denied entry. Tam sat with his back against the wall, hands shaking so hard he could barely hold the tea, and she pretended not to notice, because noticing would have required her to do something about it, and there was nothing to do but keep walking tomorrow.

"They knew," he said suddenly. "The priests. That's the part I can't stop thinking about. They knew what would happen if they preached here, and they came anyway." He stared into the fire. "My whole life I've studied the Silence from archives. Ledgers. Trial records. It's easy to think of it as policy. Paper. And then you see three men who died of exposure in chains because they wouldn't stop saying a god's name, and paper doesn't—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Paper doesn't hang."

"No," Marguerite said. "It doesn't."

She should have slept. Instead she listened — to the storm, to the mountain, to the particular quality of silence that meant someone else was listening too. Thirty years of mercenary work had taught her that ambushes lived in quiet places, and this pass was the quietest killing ground in Thornfell. If Serane had reconstructed the courier's route — and Marguerite had to assume she had; the woman struck her as thorough the way avalanches were thorough — then the Pass of Hanged Men was the choke point. One road in, one road out, walls of stone on either side.

"We move before first light," she told Tam. "Storm breaks by dawn. If anyone's waiting for us, better we hit them tired and half-blind than sit here until they find our smoke."

Tam nodded. Then, quietly: "You think they're coming."

"I think the Order doesn't leave debts unpaid. And I think my old captain doesn't either." She fed a stick to the fire. "Sleep, scholar. Tomorrow's going to cost."

* * *

Dawn came grey and windless, the storm spent, the pass buried knee-deep in fresh powder that glittered like crushed glass. Marguerite stood at the cave mouth and read the gorge the way other people read letters.

The snow told her everything. It told her that nothing had crossed from the south since midnight. It told her that a family of snow-foxes had hunted along the east wall. And it told her — in a smudge of disturbed drift near the fourth gibbet, already softening at the edges, hours old — that something had come down from the north in the dark and settled in to wait.

She counted six sets of prints, mostly hidden under fresh fall, leading to cover among the gallows' footings. Light tread. Spaced like soldiers. And further up, half-erased by wind, broader tracks that had come and gone twice — scouts reporting back.

"Blades," she murmured. "Six of them, dug in at the narrows. And someone's feeding them information from above." She turned. "Order spotters on the rim, most likely. Which means the moment Doss's people engage us, Serane's people come down the cliffs, and we die in the middle."

Tam had gone pale, and not from the cold. "Doss. Your captain."

"My former captain." She checked her blades — the long knife at her hip, the short one at her back, the hatchet on her pack strap. "He'll have brought people who loved me once. That's how the Order works. They don't send strangers to kill you; they send your friends, because your friends hesitate and strangers don't." She met his eyes. "So listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once. When it starts, you don't fight. You run for the icefall on the west wall — the blue ice, behind the second gibbet. There's a chimney behind it, a crack in the rock. Climb. Don't stop climbing until you're on the shelf above, and then follow the ledge north until you see cairns. You can get down to the river valley without ever touching the pass floor."

"And you?"

"I'll be along."

"That's not an answer, Marguerite."

"It's the only one you get." She pulled her hood up. "You're carrying the map's meaning. I'm carrying the map. If one of us has to reach the vault, it's cheaper if it's me. Now go stand by the fire and look cold and stupid until I give the word."

* * *

They came down the pass at mid-morning, two travelers struggling through the drifts, and Marguerite made sure they looked like exactly that — Tam stumbling, her hauling him by the arm, both of them bent into the wind that wasn't blowing anymore. The silence after the storm was enormous. Their footsteps cracked like gunshots.

The narrows came up fast: a throat of stone barely eight feet across, walled by the fifth and sixth gibbets, their empty chains hanging motionless in the still air. Marguerite counted her heartbeats as they entered. At twelve, she said, "Now," and shoved Tam toward the icefall, and the pass exploded.

Crossbows first — two bolts from the jumble of rocks at the narrows' mouth, one splitting the air where her head had been a half-second earlier, the other punching through her cloak sleeve and pinning the fabric to the gibbet post behind her. She dropped, ripped free, and rolled, and then they were coming out of the snow like wraiths: six figures in the grey-and-white of the Thornbury Blades, winter leathers, faces wrapped, steel drawn.

And at their center, tall and grey-bearded and moving like the mountain itself, Doss.

"Marguerite!" His voice filled the gorge. "Last offer, girl! Map and the scholar, and you walk! On my oath!"

"On your oath!" She laughed, and hated how it sounded. "Your oath bought me a crossbow bolt, Doss. Give my love to whoever's paying you."

Then it was blood and snow and the ringing chaos of close steel, and Marguerite stopped thinking in words and started thinking in ground. That was her gift, the thing that had made her name before the Blades ever had it: she fought terrain the way other fighters fought men. And this pass — God help her, this pass she had known since childhood, since her father pointed at the gibbets and told her what the Order did to people who sang.

She gave ground instead of taking it, backing between the fifth gibbet's footings, drawing them in. Brannick came first, big and careful, the man who'd taught her to throw a hatchet — she took his ankles out with a sweep of frozen scree and put the pommel of her knife into his temple, gentle as she could make it, and felt him go down breathing. Sera came next, quick, angry, and Marguerite caught her wrist and used her own momentum to fold her into the gibbet post, once, twice, until the knife dropped. "Stay down, Sera. Please stay down."

Then the cliff answered.

A horn note from the rim, thin and clear, and ash-grey figures rose along the ledges above — four, five, six of them, ropes hissing over the rock, crossbows leveling down into the narrows. The Order, done waiting. Bolts came down like iron rain, and one of them took a Blade through the shoulder — a boy named Pell, sixteen, who screamed — and the whole fight curdled at once, mercenaries and zealots tangled together in a kill-box neither side had planned.

"Doss!" Marguerite roared. "They're shooting your people! Look up, you stubborn ox, look UP—"

He looked. She saw him understand, saw the terrible arithmetic cross his face — the Order hadn't hired witnesses, they'd hired expendables — and in that heartbeat of his hesitation, Marguerite did the thing he would never have expected, because she had never done it in eleven years of soldiering.

She charged him.

Not to kill. To break. She hit him low and hard, drove him back off his feet into the deep drift beneath the sixth gibbet, and as they went down together a crossbow bolt meant for her buried itself in the snow where his chest had been. For a moment they lay tangled, face to face, his breath hot and furious against her cheek.

"You're saving me," he snarled. "You treacherous little—"

"I'm settling accounts." She drove her elbow into his jaw, hard enough to ring his bell, and rolled off him. "Go home, Doss. Take them and go home. The Order will hang you all when this is done — you know it, I know it, and I'd rather it never gets the chance."

Above her, on the rim, an Order marksman reloaded with calm, professional patience, and swung his crossbow down toward the icefall — toward Tam, halfway up the blue ice, exposed as a lichen-spotted fly on a wall.

Marguerite didn't decide anything. Her body decided for her, the body that had carried a brother down a mountain and sworn an oath over what was left. She snatched Pell's fallen crossbow off the snow, dropped to one knee, and fired the loaded bolt she found in it without aiming, the way her father had taught her, the way you shoot when there's no time to shoot.

The marksman's shot went wide. Her bolt took him in the thigh, and he sat down hard on the rim, and the angle of the fight broke.

* * *

Afterward there was the chimney, and the climb, and the ledge, and the long staggering walk north between the cairns with the wind rising again at their backs like something herding them. Tam climbed well, better than she'd guessed — city hands, but stubborn ones — and when she finally hauled herself onto the shelf and collapsed beside him in the shadow of a boulder, they were both bleeding, both shaking, both alive.

He was looking at her arm, where a blade had opened her sleeve and the flesh beneath from elbow to wrist. Without asking, he tore a strip from his own shirt, poured the last of the spirit-flask over it, and began binding the wound with the clumsy, deliberate care of a man handling a relic.

"You came back for me," he said. "On the ice. You shot a man off a cliff-edge for me, and then you climbed back down into that pit to pull Pell out of the line of fire before you'd take one step toward the exit. I counted. I was watching the whole time." He tied off the bandage. His hands weren't shaking anymore. "I had you figured for ruthlessness, Marguerite. Cold ledger, warm blade. That's what the archives say about the Thornbury company."

"The archives say a lot of things."

"So do I, professionally." He sat back against the boulder. Below them, tiny with distance, the pass was emptying — Blades carrying their wounded south, ash-grey cloaks dragging their marksman down off the rim. Somewhere down there, Doss was waking up with a broken jaw and a choice. "But ruthlessness doesn't bleed for strangers. So either I've misread you completely, or you've decided I'm not a stranger anymore."

Marguerite looked out at the mountains — at Thornfell rolling away white and endless to the north, toward the vault, toward the voice below the stone, toward whatever waited for them in the dark under the world. The ledger. He'd called it that once, early on, back in Greymill: the running count of debts between them, each certain the other would default.

"Ledger's not balanced yet, scholar," she said. "But it's not in the red anymore, either." She flexed her bandaged hand, testing it, and found it would hold a blade. "Get some water. We've got light for another two miles, and I want them behind us before dark — because that markman knew our route, Tam. Not guessed it. Knew it. Someone told the Order about the icefall chimney, and there are exactly two people alive who knew that climb."

Tam went very still. "Who?"

Marguerite stood, and looked north, and said the name she'd been chewing on since the rim:

"Ferrywoman."
