## 12. The God That Answers

The temple found them the way mercy finds the damned — late, and only because they had stopped looking for it.

Marguerite saw it first as a shape against the snow, a hunched black shoulder of stone rising out of the white where the pass bent north. For a long moment she thought it was a rockfall, another corpse of the mountain to be climbed or skirted, and then she made out the broken line of a colonnade, pillars snapped at different heights like teeth in an old man's jaw.

"Veyran," Tam said behind her, and there was something in his voice she didn't like. Something almost tender.

"Veyran what?"

"Temple. There were hundreds once, before the Order. Way-shrines, mostly. This one would have sat on the pilgrim road." He came up beside her, breath steaming, his scholar's coat crusted with ice at the hem. "They left some standing. I never understood why."

"They left them because knocking down every stone takes time," Marguerite said, "and zealots are lazy like everyone else." But she was already moving toward it, because the wind had teeth and the light was going, and a roof that was mostly holes was still better than no roof at all.

Inside, the temple had been gutted the way the Order gutted everything — altar smashed, niches empty, the face chiseled off every carving of the god. What remained was architecture: walls thick enough to hold out the storm, a floor swept clean by two centuries of wind, and in the far wall a semicircular alcove that might once have held a statue and now held nothing but shadow.

Tam stood in the middle of the nave, turning slowly, and Marguerite watched him do the thing he did when he forgot to be afraid — reading the room like a page.

"There would have been an acoustic chamber," he murmured. "Under the alcove. The Veyrans built their temples like instruments. The whole building shaped to carry the voice from the holy of holies out to the congregation. You could whisper at the altar in one of these places and be heard in the back row as if you stood beside it." He looked at her. "Do you know why?"

"Because priests like to be heard."

"Because Veyra's voice *carried*," Tam said. "That was the god's whole nature. Not thunder, not command. Carrying. A voice that reached into you and brought out what was already there." He smiled, thin and cold. "The Order couldn't kill that. So they buried it."

"Save the lecture." Marguerite dropped her pack by the wall and began unpacking the small camp stove, the resin cakes, the tin of tea that had gone bitter weeks ago and stayed bitter. "We sleep in shifts. You take second watch. And if your god starts talking to you in the night, wake me before you answer."

Tam laughed — a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody. Then he saw her face and stopped.

"You're joking," he said.

"I don't joke about gods."

* * *

She took first watch, sitting with her back to the ruined alcove because she did not want to look at it, and the storm came down on Thornfell like a judgment nobody had bothered to write up.

She thought about her brother. She had been thinking about him more since the Underroad — no. Since the pass. Since the gallows, maybe. Since the courier died with his blood freezing on the map and said *the voice below the stone*, and something old and starving in her had leaned toward those words like a plant toward light. She hated that. She hated how much of her life had been spent leaning toward things that would cut her.

Sleep took her during the fourth hour of the watch, which had not happened on watch in twenty years, and that should have frightened her more than it did.

* * *

She dreamed of salt.

Not the salt of the caravan roads, packed in sacks and priced by weight. Salt as vastness. Salt as a plain that ran to every horizon under a sky the color of pewter, and she was walking it barefoot, and the ground gave slightly under each step, flesh-warm, breathing.

*You walked away from me for six years,* said the plain, in no direction at all. *And then you carried my map through the winter passes and cursed my name the whole way.*

"You weren't using it," Marguerite said aloud, to the sky, to the salt. Her voice sounded small and flat in all that openness. "A god that doesn't speak doesn't get prayed to. That's not faith, that's talking to furniture."

*I know.* Something moved across the horizon — not clouds; pressure, the sense of an attention turning. *I know what six years of silence does. I have had two hundred years of it. Do you know what silence is, mercenary?*

"A weapon. I've seen it used."

*It is a room,* said the voice, and now it was closer, and it was not coming from anywhere, it was coming from inside her own chest, resonant, patient, terrible in its gentleness. *Silence is a room with no doors. I have been in the room. Would you like to see what is in the room with me?*

"No," Marguerite said.

The salt plain tilted, and showed her anyway.

* * *

She saw a valley she did not know, green as a wound, terraced fields climbing both walls, and people in it — thousands, tens of thousands, a whole nation of them in the space between two ridgelines. She saw the banners of the Ashen Order come over the northern pass, ash-grey on grey stone, and she saw what happened after, and her dreaming body tried to look away and could not, because this was Veyra showing her, and Veyra did not permit looking away. That was the nature of the voice. It carried. It reached into you and brought out what was already there, and what was already there in Marguerite Thornbury was a soldier who had seen massacres and always, always looked.

She saw the Order burn the granaries first, because hunger breaks a people faster than steel. She saw inquisitors go door to door with lists. She saw a woman in a temple courtyard — this same temple, whole, its colonnade unbroken, its god-faced carvings smiling — refuse three times to renounce the hearing, and she saw what the inquisitor did on the third refusal, and she heard the crowd make the sound crowds make when they have decided that staying silent is the same as being safe.

*This is the war,* said the voice, and it was weeping, or the salt was rain now, or she was. *This is what they tell themselves they prevented. Two hundred years of peace, they say. Look at my peace, Marguerite Thornbury. Look at how it was bought.*

"I've seen purges," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "I've *done* purges, for pay. Why show me this? What do you want?"

*What I have always wanted.* The valley dissolved. The salt came back, and with it a warmth at her back like a hand laid between her shoulder blades, and she could not move, could not turn, could only stand and be touched by a god for the first time in her life. *To be heard. Nothing more. I was never a god of commands. I am a god of carrying. Every truth spoken in my temples was a truth the listener already knew and could not say alone.*

"And if I open the vault? If I give you back your voice?" She made herself ask it. Made herself stand in the warmth and ask the question she had been circling for a thousand miles. "Two centuries in the dark. Half-mad, Tam says. Screaming truths with no one to hear them. What comes out of that room with you, Veyra? What do you *carry* then?"

The hand at her back went still.

For a long moment there was nothing but the salt and the pewter sky and the sound of her own heart, and then, very quietly, with a gentleness that was worse than any threat:

*I don't know.*

* * *

She woke with wet tracks frozen on her cheeks and her hands fisted so hard her nails had broken skin.

Grey pre-dawn light. The storm had spent itself; wind still worried the broken colonnade with a low moan, but the screaming gusts were done. The fire had burned to a red eye of coals. Tam sat propped against the far wall exactly where she'd left him, awake, watching her with an expression she couldn't read in the half-dark.

"How long," she said. Her voice came out scraped raw.

"An hour. Maybe more." He hesitated. "You were talking."

"What did I say?"

"Nothing. That's the thing." Tam leaned forward, and the coal-light caught the fear in his face and made it honest. "Your mouth was moving and no sound came out. For an hour. Like someone had taken your voice and was — trying it on. Testing the fit." He swallowed. "There's an acoustic chamber under this floor, Marguerite. I told you. The whole building is shaped to carry. And something down in the dark has been listening to us breathe for two days."

Marguerite got up. Her legs shook and she let them, because Tam was watching and lying with her body would cost more strength than it was worth. She crossed the nave to the ruined alcove and stood before the shadowed hollow where the god's statue had been torn out, and she put her palm flat against the stone.

It was warm.

Stone in a mountain pass in deep winter, in a roofless ruin, after a night of storm. It should have been cold enough to burn. It was warm as a sleeping animal, and beneath her palm, faint as a pulse under bandages, she felt a rhythm that was almost — *almost* — a voice.

"It spoke to me," she said. "Or I dreamed it. Or it put the dream in me like a blade through a letter slot." She turned to look at Tam. "It showed me the Order's founding massacre. Real, or close enough. It showed me what the silence cost. And when I asked it what happens if we free it — what comes out of that vault after two hundred years — do you know what the god said?"

Tam had gone very still. "What?"

"*I don't know.*" Marguerite laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, half a sob. "Two hundred years they've lied to keep it caged, and the god itself can't tell me whether opening the door ends the lie or just changes whose it is. Serane thinks she's guarding the world. We think we're carrying justice. And the truth is—" She pressed her palm harder against the warm stone, as if she could hold it still, pin it, make it answer plainly the way men answered plainly. "The truth is nobody knows. Not the Order. Not the god. Not us."

Tam rose slowly. He looked older than he had in Greymill, younger than he had in the pass; the road was sanding him down to whatever was actually underneath. "My grandmother's notes said the seal wasn't built to hold the voice forever," he said quietly. "It was built to hold it until it could be heard safely. Until the world was ready. My family believed that day would come. I think the Order decided it never would." He paused. "But Marguerite — if the voice is leaking through already, strong enough to reach you in your sleep—"

"Then the seal is failing," she finished.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. Outside, the wind dropped altogether, and in the sudden quiet they both heard it: a sound rising from beneath the temple floor, from the instrument-shaped dark below the stones. Not words. Not yet. A hum, low and vast and patient, the note a struck bell holds after the ringing stops.

Warmth spread under Marguerite's palm, and the hum shifted, and for one vertiginous instant she could have sworn it was shaping itself around the rhythm of her own heartbeat — learning her, tuning to her, the way a voice carries best to the one ear that is already listening.

"We're not bringing it to the world," she said softly. "It's been calling to us the whole way down."

She pulled her hand from the stone, shouldered her pack, and stepped out into the blue morning snow — toward the vault, toward the voice, toward the only door left that had never once opened honestly.
