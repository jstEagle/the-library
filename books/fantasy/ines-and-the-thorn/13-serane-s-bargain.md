## 13. Serane's Bargain

The rain came down on Greymill like a penance nobody had asked for.

Inquisitor Serane Voss stood at the window of the Wayhouse of the Ninefold Flame and watched it sheet off the eaves, turning the mud of the market square into a grey broth. Below, a fishmonger argued with a cart driver; a child chased a dog through puddles; a woman hauled water up from the town well in yokes that creaked like old men. Ordinary life. Ordinary people, going about their ordinary hours without the faintest notion that the truth they lived inside had been curated for them the way a gardener curates a hedge — pruned here, cut back there, shaped into something that would not grow wild and strangle them all.

Two hundred years of pruning. Two hundred years of the Ashen Order holding shears that no one else was permitted to touch.

Serane did not resent the work. That was what her superiors had never understood about her, what made some of them uneasy when she entered a room — she did not do this out of ambition, or fear, or the small cruelties of petty power. She did it because someone had to, and because she had read the founding records, and because she knew — with a certainty that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal — exactly what the world looked like when the hedge went untended.

She turned from the window.

Tam of the Sealed Library sat in the chair across the room, wrists bound to the arms of it with ash-cord, ankles likewise, and he was watching her with the particular stillness of a man who has decided that panic is a luxury he cannot currently afford. She had to give him credit for it. Most scholars, by this hour, had wept at least once. He had not wept. His hands trembled — she could see the fine vibration of his fingers where they lay against the chair — but his face held.

"You've been comfortable?" she asked. It was not really a question. The room was warm, the fire fed, a supper of bread and salted mutton untouched on the table beside him. She had ordered it brought. Kindness, before pressure. Always kindness first. A man who believes you see him as a person will tell you things a broken man will lie about.

"I've been a prisoner," Tam said. His voice was dry, careful. "Comfortable is a strange word for it."

"A guest," Serane corrected mildly, "who has not yet decided to be useful." She crossed the room and took the chair opposite him, unhurried, arranging her grey cloak so that the flame-and-mountain sigil of the Order hung visible at her breast. Symbols mattered. Everything mattered. That was the whole of the craft, if you wanted to call it that: the understanding that every word, every silence, every cup of untouched mutton was a sentence in an argument you were conducting whether you meant to or not.

"Your research," she said. "The papers you burned in your rooms at the archive. We recovered fragments from the grate."

Something moved behind his eyes. Small. Controlled. But there.

"Char and conjecture," he said.

"Char and conjecture." She smiled faintly. "Yes. Except that conjecture does not send a scholar running into the night with a satchel full of notes and a grandmother's map, does it? Conjecture keeps a man at his desk. Only certainty makes a man run."

* * *

She let the silence work while the fire popped and spat.

Serane had been reconstructing the courier's route for eleven days now, and she thought of it the way she thought of all her work: as reading a text written in bodies instead of ink. The courier — one Aldric Vey, a name that had amused her grimly when she'd learned it, a man named for the god whose voice he died carrying — had come down out of the high passes along a line that no honest traveler used. Smuggler roads. Old roads. Roads that existed, she suspected, precisely because two centuries ago certain families had needed ways to move through Thornfell unseen, and had been paid very well by the Order itself to keep those ways open and unmarked.

That was the part the Grand Inquisitor's council still refused to look at squarely, and Serane had stopped raising it in council years ago. You could not prune a hedge with a blade that flinched.

Vey had carried the map south toward Greymill, which meant the vault it charted lay north and east, deep in the massif — which fit. It fit the old records, the ones sealed under triple oath in the Order's own archives, the ones Serane had read at twenty-three years old and had never once been able to unread.

*They did not silence a mad god,* the record said, in the hand of the First Inquisitor himself. *We silenced a truthful one. May whatever remains of mercy judge us gently.*

She had been young enough, then, to believe the guilt in that sentence was the guilt of necessity. She was forty-one now, and she still believed it, most days. On the other days she simply got up earlier and worked harder, because work was the only argument she'd ever found against doubt.

And now a mercenary woman with a dead god's name half-woven into her oaths was walking those same smuggler roads with a runaway archivist, heading for the one door in the world that must never open.

*Marguerite Thornbury.* Serane turned the name over in her mind like a coin of uncertain minting. The Blades' former quartermaster of routes — no, more than that, their pathfinder, the one they sent through country that ate other men alive. Expelled or departed three years past under circumstances the company's records declined to specify. Sworn to Veyra, which should have made her harmless — the god had not spoken since before the woman took her vows, and a prayer unanswered for years rots into habit, and habit into nothing.

But Serane had read the interrogation transcripts from the caravan guards. The Thornbury woman had lied to a Vigil interrogator's face with a flatness that even trained deceivers rarely managed, and she had done it while standing over a fresh corpse, with ash-grey cloaks riding toward her camp, and she had not run afterward. She had continued the job. Finished the contract. Collected her pay.

That was either a woman with nothing left to lose, or a woman with something so large to protect that everything else had become furniture.

Both kinds were dangerous. Only one kind could be bought.

* * *

"You know why I haven't questioned you yet," Serane said.

Tam's jaw tightened. "Because you're enjoying the view."

"Because," she said, ignoring him with the ease of long practice, "everything I need to know, you'll tell me better if you tell me yourself. Coerced testimony is a forgery, Master Tam. It looks right from a distance and falls apart in the hand. What I want is the original document." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and let her voice drop into something almost conversational. "So. Tell me about your grandmother."

He flinched. Actually flinched — shoulders drawing in, gaze dropping to the floorboards. There it was. Eleven days of patience, and the door had opened on the first knock.

"She was a seamstress," he said.

"She was a Warden of the Seal," Serane said. "Third generation. Your family helped build the vault, and then helped hide it, and then spent two hundred years pretending they hadn't. And somewhere in the last decade, a boy in the sealed archives started pulling threads, and found his own name woven into the pattern." She watched him breathe. Watched the firelight move on his face. "Am I wrong?"

The silence stretched. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, the fishmonger was still shouting about prices, absurdly, wonderfully ordinary.

"No," Tam said finally. Very quietly. "You're not wrong."

"Thank you." And she meant it; she always meant it. Respect for honesty was not a performance. It was the load-bearing wall of the whole profession. "Now. Here is what happens next, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide anything, because I suspect you are a man who decides slowly and well, and I would rather have your slow good judgment than your fast bad fear."

She rose, walked to the table, and poured two cups of wine from the jug the wayhouse keeper had left. She set one within reach of his bound hand — a small cruelty of courtesy, offering what he could not take — and drank from her own.

"The Order knows the map survives. We know the Thornbury woman has it. We believe — strongly — that she is making for the vault, though whether she understands what she carries is less certain. Our standing instruction regarding both of them is retrieval and quiet disposition." She said *quiet disposition* the way one says *the weather is mild*, and watched him understand it anyway. "I am authorized to carry out that instruction. I am also authorized—" and here she paused, deliberately, letting the pause do its work, "—to exercise judgment."

Tam looked up at her. His eyes were red-rimmed with exhaustion but steady. "What kind of judgment?"

"The kind that remembers you burned your own research rather than let it fall into our hands. The kind that notices you ran *toward* danger to warn a stranger rather than save yourself. The kind that concludes—" she set down her cup, "—that you are not a heretic, Master Tam. You are a penitent. And the Order has always known what to do with penitents."

* * *

She knelt then, which surprised him — she saw it land, the surprise, the recalibration behind his eyes. An Inquisitor of the Ashen Vigil did not kneel to prisoners. She did it anyway, slowly, so the meaning of it could not be mistaken, and she spoke low, the way one speaks in a confessional.

"Your family's name can be restored."

His breath caught audibly.

"Wardens of the Seal. Honored servants of the Silence. The record of their service — the true record, the one that shows they built the cage and kept it locked — unsealed, published, inscribed in the rolls at the Flamehold. Your children's children would carry a name that opens doors instead of one that burns paper in the night." She held his gaze. "All of it is within my authority to grant. All of it. In exchange for one thing."

"The map," Tam whispered.

"The map, and the woman carrying it, delivered intact into Order custody at a place and time of my choosing. Nothing more. No violence required of you. No oaths beyond the ones you already keep." She straightened, rising back to her feet, and let the distance return between them — grace withdrawn, arithmetic resumed. "You would be doing her a kindness, if it helps to think of it that way. What waits beneath that stone is not a rescue, Master Tam. It is a war with the lid taken off. Whatever she thinks she's walking toward — justice, restoration, a god's gratitude — she is wrong. And you know she is wrong, because you have read the same fragments I have, and more. You know what the voice *is*. You know what it does to a world that hears it."

"Do I?" His voice cracked on it, and he hated that she heard it crack; she could see him hating it. "Do I actually know? Or do I know what your Order taught my family to write down?"

For a moment — a single, unwelcome moment — Serane felt the coal in her chest shift.

She thought of the First Inquisitor's hand again. *May whatever remains of mercy judge us gently.* She thought of the founding massacre at Hollowmere, the village that had heard the god speak and had not survived the hearing — except that the official account of Hollowmere had gaps in it, gaps she had spent nineteen years telling herself were simple decay of the record, and there was a version of herself, a version she kept in a locked room behind her sternum, that suspected the gaps were not decay at all.

She put the thought back in its room. Locked it. This was not the day.

"What you know," she said evenly, "is that two hundred years ago, the wisest and most frightened people of their generation chose silence over speech, at terrible cost, and that every generation since has chosen to keep paying that cost rather than find out what happens when the payments stop. That is not ignorance, Master Tam. That is inheritance. And inheritance," she added, moving toward the door, "is also a debt. Yours comes due soon. Hers already has."

At the threshold she stopped, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"You'll be released within the hour. Your effects returned. The wayhouse keeper will tell you the back road out of Greymill is unwatched tonight — it will be true when he tells you, and it will stop being true at dawn." She opened the door onto the sound of rain. "You are cleverer than she is, and kinder than I am, and you love your family's ghost more than you fear mine. One of those three things will break. I am content to wait and see which."

"Serane." His voice stopped her. Not *Inquisitor*. Just her name, raw and human, and she allowed it. "If I don't come. If I choose her side. Will you kill me?"

She considered the question honestly, because he had earned honesty.

"No," she said. "I'll grieve you. There's a difference, and it matters to me." And then, because grief was a luxury for after: "But understand what you'd be grieving toward. You think she's carrying a map to a god's freedom. Ask yourself why a god who loved the world stayed silent for two hundred years even while its faithful prayed themselves hollow. Either it couldn't speak—" she pulled the door wide, letting the cold wet air roll in, "—or it learned what speaking costs. Neither answer ends the way she hopes."

She stepped out into the rain and closed the door softly behind her, leaving him the wine, the fire, and the choice.

* * *

From the shadowed gallery above the stable yard, forty minutes later, Serane watched Tam come out the wayhouse's rear door with his satchel over his shoulder, hood up, moving quick and guilty through the dark toward the unwatched road.

Her second, Brother Callen, materialized at her shoulder, rain streaming off the brim of his hat. "Shall we take him now? He'll lead us straight to her."

"No."

"He's a liability. The Grand Inquisitor expects—"

"The Grand Inquisitor expects results, and results are what he'll get." Serane did not take her eyes off the small dark figure dwindling up the muddy lane. "A prisoner tells you what he thinks you want to hear. A traitor tells you the truth. Let him run to her. Let him sit across her fire and weigh his family's name against her life, every hour of every day, and let him discover — as he will — that there is no weight on earth that makes that balance tip cleanly. Men break under that kind of arithmetic, Callen. They break slowly, and they break inward, and when they go, they go *toward* whoever offered them absolution first."

"And if he doesn't break?"

She was quiet a moment. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the tiles, running silver down the drainpipes, washing Greymill's filth into its gutters.

"Then he'll stand beside her when we breach the vault," Serane said, "and I will grieve him. As promised." She turned up her collar and descended toward the yard, where horses waited and the northern passes waited beyond them, white and silent and keeping, as they had kept for two hundred years, the one secret the world could not afford to hear. "Saddle the horses, Brother. Dawn comes early, and we have a mountain to outrun."

Behind them, up the dark unwatched road, a scholar walked alone into the rain, carrying a choice that was already beginning to carry him.
