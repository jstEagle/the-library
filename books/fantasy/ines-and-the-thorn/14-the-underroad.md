## 14. The Underroad

The door was a lie of stone.

That was how Marguerite had always thought of it, standing in the shadow of the collapsed watchtower where the smugglers' road met the mountain's root. To anyone walking the high pass above, it was nothing — a shelf of granite streaked with old soot, half-buried in scree. But she had pressed her palm against the cold of it at nineteen years old and felt the hollow behind it like a held breath, and Brenn had laughed and said, *the mountain swallows liars, Ellie, that's why they built the road inside one.*

She pressed her palm against it now, twenty years older, twenty years harder, and the stone was exactly as cold as she remembered.

"You're certain this is the way," Tam said. Not quite a question. He stood with his arms wrapped around himself against the wind, his scholar's cloak crusted with three days of snow, looking like what he was — a man who belonged in libraries, not on mountains.

"No." She let him hear the honesty of it. "But it's the only way left."

Above them, the Pass of Hanged Men was still smoking. The Blades would have carried word to Serane by now — if Doss lived to carry it, or if any of them did — and every road over Thornfell's spine would be watched by morning. The Underroad ran beneath all of it: forty miles of smuggler tunnel, old mine shafts, and forgotten pilgrim ways stitched together by generations of people who had things to move and reasons not to be seen moving them.

Marguerite knew it because she had grown up in it. And she had sworn, over a grave she had dug with her own hands, never to walk it again.

She pushed the stone.

It moved easier than it should have — counterweighted, oiled by someone recently, which meant someone was still using the road, which meant trouble later. For now there was only darkness breathing out at her, smelling of wet rock and lamp oil and something older underneath, something mineral and patient, like the inside of a closed mouth.

"Lamp," she said, and Tam handed it to her with hands that were not quite steady.

"Marguerite." He said her name the way he'd begun to lately — carefully, like setting down something breakable. "What's down there?"

"Forty miles of dark," she said. "And the place where my brother died."

She stepped through before he could see her face.

* * *

The first mile was easy, and that was the trap of it.

The Underroad began as a proper gallery — vaulted ceiling, drainage channel cut along the eastern wall, iron brackets for torches rusted to orange lace. The smuggler clans had maintained it once, back when Thornfell's border wars made contraband worth more than salt. Now the brackets were empty and the drainage channel was a stream of black meltwater whispering in the dark, and their single lamp threw a circle of light no wider than a grave.

Tam walked close behind her. She could hear him breathing, hear the small sounds of a man trying to make himself quiet and failing — the catch of his pack strap, the scuff of boots too fine for this ground. She found, to her surprise, that she didn't mind. In the pass, in the fight, noise meant death. Down here it just meant company.

"How far to the next junction?" he asked.

"Six hours, if the galleries haven't fallen."

"And after that?"

"After that we stop talking about after that."

He took the rebuke without flinching. He'd learned that much on the road — when to press and when to let silence do its work. They walked. The black water kept its whisper. Somewhere far ahead, deep in the mountain's gut, something dripped in a slow rhythm like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

It was at the third hour, when the gallery narrowed and the ceiling came down low enough that they had to stoop, that Tam finally asked the question she'd been waiting for since Greymill.

"The oath you swore to Veyra." His voice was careful in the way of a man stepping onto ice. "You've never told me what it was. What it cost."

The lamp flame bent as she ducked under a rib of stone. Beyond it the passage opened again, and she stopped, because she knew this place. Of course she knew this place. The mountain had been leading her here the whole time, mile by mile, and she'd been letting it.

"This is where I tell you," she said.

* * *

They made camp in a side chamber off the main gallery — a smuggler's rest, walled, dry, with an old fire ring of flat stones and a vent crack that drew smoke up into the mountain's bones. Marguerite built the fire small, out of habit, out of discipline, out of the simple animal need to see her own hands while she talked.

Tam sat across from her with his knees drawn up, waiting. He was good at waiting. It was, she suspected, the scholar's whole art.

"My brother's name was Brenn," she said.

The name came out of her like a splinter coming out of flesh — a relief and a wound in the same motion. She hadn't said it aloud in years. Not to anyone. The Blades had known better than to ask; Doss had buried the question somewhere deep the day he'd watched her come down out of the mountain alone, seventeen years old, carrying a shovel and nothing else.

"Brenn and I grew up on the Underroad. Our mother ran cargo — salt, mostly, and things hidden in salt. We were born in these tunnels, more or less. I learned to read by the marks on the walls. Route signs, toll tallies, clan glyphs." She turned her hands over, palms up, showing him the old calluses. "Brenn wanted more than salt. He wanted to matter. He used to say the mountain owed us, because it had taken our father, and one day he was going to collect."

The fire popped. Tam didn't move.

"When Veyra's voice went silent — when the Order did whatever the Order did, two hundred years ago — the god's cults didn't die. They went underground. Literally. Pilgrim roads became smuggler roads, shrines became waystations, and the faithful kept paying tolls to a god who couldn't answer anymore." She smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. "My mother thought it was romantic. My brother thought it was opportunity. A silent god, he figured, was a god with unclaimed treasure. Every shrine on this road had offerings that nobody was collecting."

"He robbed a shrine."

"He robbed *the* shrine. The Deep Altar, four miles past where we're sitting. Old even then. The smugglers left it alone out of superstition, but Brenn wasn't a smuggler, he was a believer — just a believer who'd decided faith was a market with bad pricing." Her jaw tightened. "I went with him because I always went with him. I was fifteen. I carried the pry bar."

The lamp oil smell seemed thicker suddenly, or maybe that was memory. She made herself keep going.

"There was a slab over the offering vault. Carved with Veyra's mark — the thorn circle, you've seen it on the map. Brenn got the bar under it and it wouldn't move, and he laughed, and he said, *Ellie, help me, the god's holding on.* And I put my hands on the bar and I pulled, and the slab came up like it weighed nothing. Like the mountain itself had decided to let go."

"What was in the vault?"

"Nothing." She watched the fire. "Empty. Swept clean. Centuries of offerings gone — or never there, or taken, I don't know. And Brenn stood there in the hole with the lamp and looked at all that nothing, and I saw something go out of his face. Something important. And then the gallery above us—" she gestured upward, at the tons of stone overhead, patient as everything else in this place— "came down."

Silence. The drip in the distance kept its slow count.

"It wasn't a collapse," Tam said quietly. "You don't believe it was a collapse."

"I believe the god was silent, and I believe my brother died in a hole dug into its body, and I believe those two facts are married whether or not anyone performed the ceremony." She wrapped her arms around her knees, mirroring him without meaning to. "I dug him out. It took me two days. I was fifteen and I dug for two days with a pry bar and my hands, and when I finally got to him he was—" Her breath caught, and she rode through it the way she rode through everything, by refusing to stop. "He was smiling. That's the worst of it. Whatever he saw in that empty vault, at the end, he was smiling. Like he'd heard something."

Tam was very still. "And the oath?"

"I dragged him up the Underroad to the nearest light. There was a chapel station — Order-built, one of the vigil houses they put on the old pilgrim ways after the silencing. There was a priest there. Ashen robes. And I knelt in front of him with my brother's blood all over me and I swore." She looked up, and let him see it, all of it, the thing she had never shown another living soul. "I swore that if Veyra ever spoke again — if the silent god ever had a voice to answer prayers — I would carry that voice wherever it needed to go. Even if it killed me. Even if it damned me. Because I needed to know what my brother heard. I needed to know if the nothing in that vault was really nothing, or if it was a god holding its breath."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark of the chamber, water found its way down stone.

"That's why the map pulled you," Tam said. "Not greed. Not curiosity."

"The courier said *the voice below the stone,* and every oath I ever swore stood up inside me at once." She laughed, short and bitter. "Seventeen years I prayed to a god that couldn't answer, Tam. Out of habit, out of stubbornness, out of some stupid loyalty to a dead boy's smile. And now the voice might actually exist, and I'm walking toward it through the exact tunnel where my brother died, and I can't decide if that's fate or a joke."

"It's both," Tam said. "Most true things are."

She looked at him across the small fire — this soft-handed, hard-surviving man with his burned research and his grandmother's map and his family's two-hundred-year-old sin, and she felt the strange, unwelcome thing she'd been feeling since the pass: trust. Not given. Accumulated. Deposited drop by drop in blood and blizzards until it had weight.

"Why did your family help seal it?" she asked. "Really. Not the version you gave me on the ferry road."

Tam fed a stick to the fire. When he spoke, his voice was low, pitched under the sound of the water, as if the mountain might be listening.

"They were cartographers. Vault-wardens, the Order called them — the families trusted to build the seals and forget the way. My grandmother broke the rule. She drew the map, hid it, and spent the rest of her life teaching me numbers that weren't numbers, symbols that weren't symbols, so that someday I'd look at a dying courier's leather chart and *understand it.*" He shook his head slowly. "I thought I was doing penance, Marguerite. Burning my research, running, hiding. But I think maybe I was doing what she designed me to do. Maybe the whole Oyelaran line was a fuse, laid two hundred years ago, waiting for someone to strike the match."

"Then we're both inherited sins," she said.

"We're both inherited *debts.*" He looked up, and in the firelight his eyes were steady. "The difference matters. Sins you carry alone. Debts you pay together."

Marguerite held his gaze for a long moment. Then she reached into her shirt and pulled out the map — Brenn's thorn circle, the courier's bloodstain, her grandmother-of-no-relation's secret geometry — and laid it open between them on the stone.

"Four miles ahead," she said, "there's a junction where three galleries meet. The Deep Altar sits in the middle of it. We'll have to pass it. I'll have to stand in the room where my brother died." Her voice didn't shake. She'd spent seventeen years making sure it couldn't. "After that, the stairs down to the vault gate. After that, no more maps. No more oaths. Just whatever's down there, and whatever we choose to do about it."

"Are you ready for that?"

"No," she said honestly. "But I've never once been ready for anything that mattered. Ready is a luxury for people with nothing to pay."

Tam almost smiled. Then the smile faded, and he cocked his head, listening.

Marguerite listened too.

Under the drip, under the whisper of the black water, under the small settling sounds of the mountain — there was something else. Faint. Rhythmic. Rising up through the stone beneath them like a pulse through a wrist.

A hum.

Tam's face went pale in the firelight. "Marguerite. Tell me you hear that."

She could feel it now, faintly, in the soles of her boots, in the flat stones of the old fire ring, in her teeth. The mountain was not silent. The mountain had never been silent. Something below the stone was singing to itself in the dark, half-mad, two centuries caged — and it was getting louder with every step they took toward it.

She banked the fire and picked up the lamp.

"Four miles," she said. "Let's go meet my brother's god."

* * *

The junction opened around them like a cathedral carved by accident.

Three galleries converged on a round chamber tall enough that their lamplight couldn't find its ceiling, and in the center of it, exactly where memory had kept it all these years, stood the Deep Altar — a block of black stone the height of a man, its surface worn smooth by centuries of hands, its sides ringed with the thorn-circle mark repeated so many times the carvings blurred into texture.

The hum was everywhere here. In the walls. In the floor. In the hollow where the offering vault had been, long since resealed, the slab sitting flush and innocent as if it had never been moved, never been emptied, never dropped a mountain on a laughing boy.

Marguerite stood at the edge of the lamplight and did not go closer.

Tam touched her elbow — gently, the way you touch something you're afraid will shatter. "We can go around," he said.

"No." She stepped forward, one bootfall, then another, the hum rising through her bones like water rising in a well. At the altar's base she knelt, the way she had knelt at nineteen in front of an Ashen priest, and pressed her palm flat against the black stone.

It was warm.

Everything in this mountain was cold except this, and the warmth pulsed faintly under her hand, in time with the hum, in time — she realized with a vertigo that dropped through her like a stone through ice — in time with her own heartbeat.

"I brought him home," she whispered, to the stone, to the dark, to whatever was listening below two hundred years of silence. "I'm sorry it took me so long."

The hum changed.

It was subtle — a shift in pitch, a held note bending — but Marguerite had spent her life reading rooms and reading men and reading mountains, and she knew attention when she felt it. Something vast and broken and buried had turned, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice.

And far down the southern gallery, deeper than the vault stairs, deeper than anything on Tam's grandmother's map —

a light kindled.

Not lamplight. Not firelight. A pale, sourceless glow rising in the dark like dawn coming up from underground, and with it, faint and clear and impossibly sweet, threaded through the hum:

singing.

Tam grabbed her arm. "That's not on the map," he breathed. "There's nothing south of the vault. Nothing. The passages end—"

"They end on paper," Marguerite said. She rose, and slung the lamp from its hook, and looked down the southern gallery where the light was growing, and felt every oath she had ever sworn standing up inside her at once. "Down here, the mountain keeps its own."

She started walking toward the singing, and the dark of the Underroad closed behind her like a throat.
