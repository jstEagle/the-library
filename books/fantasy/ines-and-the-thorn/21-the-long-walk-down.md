## 21. The Long Walk Down

The mountain let her go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long: slowly, finger by finger, leaving marks.

Marguerite walked out of the smoke and falling stone with Tam over her shoulders, his weight no more than a sack of grain and twice as stubborn, because he had fought her every step of the last hundred yards. He had wanted to stay in the vault with his grandmother's map and his family's sin and the dust that had been a god's voice an hour before. She had put him down once, gently, to tell him she would break both his arms if it made him easier to carry, and after that he had stopped arguing.

Behind them, Thornfell closed its throat. The Underroad collapsed in stages, a long rolling thunder like the mountain clearing its lungs, and then there was only snowmelt sound and wind and the small wet noise of Tam breathing against her neck.

She did not look back. Looking back was a toll too, and she could not afford to pay everything at once.

* * *

They made the treeline by dark, or what passed for dark under a sky still bruised orange from the fires below. Marguerite laid Tam against a granite shelf, checked the wound in his side — the crossbow bolt had gone through meat, not gut, and the field stitching she'd done by torchlight in the vault's antechamber was holding — and built a fire small enough to hide from anything that might still be hunting.

There was nothing left to hunt them. That was the strange part. The Ashen Order was ash now, its Grand Inquisitor dead by Serane's hand, its zealots scattered or kneeling in the ruins of their own certainty. The Blades were leaderless; Doss was buried under half a mountain, and Marguerite had not been able to dig for him, and that grief sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. No one was coming. For the first time since a dying courier had pressed a leather map into her hands on a cold dusk in the high passes, no one was coming.

Tam woke near midnight, feverish, and looked at her across the fire with eyes that seemed too old for his face.

"You should have left me," he said.

"I know." She fed the fire a stick. "I'm selfish."

"The record—"

"Is in your head, scholar. You wrote it three times before we ever went down. I heard you recite it in your sleep for two weeks. If you die, I'll find someone who can read your handwriting, which is a punishment you've earned." She looked at him until he looked away. "You don't get to choose penance anymore. You chose it when you burned your research and ran. Everything after that is just living."

He was quiet a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, deep in the broken rock, something hummed — faint, tuneless, neither voice nor wind.

"Do you hear that?" Tam whispered.

"Yes."

"What is it?"

Marguerite listened. It was not the voice. The voice was gone; she had unmade it herself, felt it come apart in her hands like a rope of light fraying strand by strand, and what it had poured into her in its last moments — *carry me, carry me, let every mortal hear* — had dissolved with it, thank all the empty heavens. This was only resonance. Stone remembering being sung to. A mountain with a song caught in its teeth.

"Nothing," she said. "It's nothing. Sleep."

But she lay awake most of the night listening to it, and if her eyes were wet when dawn came grey through the pines, there was no one awake to see, and she told herself that counted.

* * *

They went down together as far as the river, and there they parted, because Tam was a scholar and scholars belong to libraries, even ruined ones, and because Marguerite had learned on this road that carrying people further than they want to go is just another way of silencing them.

He stood on the south bank, pale and upright, leaning on a staff he'd cut himself and refused help cutting.

"Greymill," he said. "I'll write it all down properly. Names, dates, the Order's founding massacre, my family's part in it. Every archive that will take it. Some won't. Most won't." A ghost of his old pedantic smile. "I'll be insufferable about it."

"You already are."

"Marguerite." He hesitated, and she watched him decide whether to say the thing, and said it anyway, because he was Tam. "What you did down there. Everyone will call it destruction. They'll say you killed a god twice."

"They'd be right the first time and wrong the second."

"And you? What do you call it?"

She looked up the river, toward the pass, toward the white shoulder of Thornfell rising into cloud. The mountain hummed, very faintly, like a house settling after everyone has gone to bed.

"I call it paid," she said.

He bowed — actually bowed, the sealed-library fool — and turned south, limping toward Greymill and the long work of truth without a weapon in it. She watched him until the trees took him. Then she turned north and west, alone, and began the long walk down.

* * *

Oathless.

She kept turning the word over as she descended, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. Since she was nine years old, kneeling in the snow over her brother's body with the blood freezing black around him, she had belonged to Veyra. Not the way priests belong — she had never trusted priests — but the way a blade belongs to the hand that drew it. Her oath had been simple and terrible: *you keep the mountains, I keep the roads, and neither of us lets go.* Two hundred years of silence had hollowed that vow into habit, and habit into armor, and armor into identity, and now she had shattered the god's voice with her own hands and the oath had shattered with it, and underneath she found — nothing. No Veyra. No debt. No hand holding hers in the dark.

Just a woman on a road, owing nothing to anyone.

It should have felt like freedom. On the third day, crossing a high saddle where the pines gave way to bare wind-scoured stone, she stopped and tried to pray — out of habit, the same hollow ritual she'd performed over caravan altars for years, words shaped to a god who never answered.

Nothing came. Not silence-with-something-behind-it, the way the old silences had always felt, charged and watchful. Just silence, plain as an empty room.

She stood there a long time, waiting to feel grief, or loss, or the vertigo the ballads promised would follow deicide.

What she felt was lighter. Lighter and colder, like a pack with everything removed but the things she actually needed.

"All right," she said aloud, to the wind, to the mountain, to nobody. "All right."

And she walked on.

* * *

The river crossing came on the sixth day, and she knew it before she saw it, because the water sounded different here — slower, deeper, patient. The ferrywoman's stretch.

Maren was mending netting on the south bank as if the intervening months had never happened, as if she hadn't once offered to strip a fugitive scholar's memories as toll, as if the world hadn't ended twice upstream. She was a broad, grey-braided woman with hands like tree roots and eyes that had seen every kind of payment a desperate traveler could offer, and she looked up when Marguerite's boots crunched the shingle, and she did not seem surprised.

"Heard the mountain singing," Maren said. "Then heard it stop. Thought maybe someone had finally gone up and settled accounts."

"Someone did."

"Hm." She bit a thread, snapped it. "Crossing costs."

"I know the price."

"Prices changed," Maren said. She set down the netting and studied Marguerite with those root-old eyes. "Used to be I took what people wouldn't miss. A face. A year. A name they didn't like anyway. Order magic, old stock — my grandmother's trade, may she rot. But I learned something these past months, watching folk come off that mountain looking like they'd been flayed and sewn back wrong." She jerked her chin northward. "Taking memories people want rid of — that's easy. That's charity dressed as toll. The real price, the honest one, is taking something they want to keep."

Marguerite's hand rose halfway to her chest before she caught it. Because there was one thing. One thing the whole descent she had been guarding, turning over in the dark hours, telling herself she would keep it, that it was hers, that she had earned it —

The song.

In the vault, before the breaking, the voice had sung to her. Not the screaming truths, not the weaponized flood that had unmade Serane and the Grand Inquisitor both. Before that. In the antechambers, in the dream in the ruined temple, in the moment when the seal first cracked and the caged thing inside had thought, briefly, hopelessly, that rescue had come. It had sung. Two centuries of silence compressed into thirty seconds of sound, and it had been the most beautiful thing Marguerite had ever heard — a melody like water finding stone, like the first thaw, like her brother laughing, like every prayer she had ever thrown at an empty altar suddenly answered all at once and forgiven.

She had sworn, walking down, that she would keep it. That it was the one wage the whole catastrophe had paid her.

"That's not yours to take," she said quietly.

"No," Maren agreed. "It's mine to ask for. Taking's different." She folded her hands. "Here's the crossing, mercenary. Free, if you want it free. I'm owed nothing by you; you never signed with me. But you'll walk into Greymill and the lowlands and the rest of your life carrying that song, and you know what you'll do with it."

Marguerite said nothing, because she did know.

"You'll hum it," Maren said. "In taverns. To children. You'll write it down someday, or some bard will worm it out of you, and it'll spread, and a melody like that — a god's own voice, even a scrap of it — it'll do exactly what the voice wanted to do. Make people listen. Make them *need*. And then some clever bastard will build a church on it, and we'll be back where we started, except next time the muzzle will be made of music instead of stone." She shrugged, vast and unhurried. "Or you pay me the song, and you walk down owning nothing but the memory of having owned it. And every time you reach for it, you'll remember why you can't have it. Which is its own kind of keeping, if you're the sort who learns from scars."

The river ran. High above, faint as breath, the mountain hummed.

"How much of it?" Marguerite asked at last. Her voice came out rough.

"All of it. That's how tolls work. Half a memory is just a wound that sings."

Marguerite stood on the shingle a long time. She thought about arguing. She thought about swimming the ford in spate, which would likely kill her, and about walking fifty miles downstream to the bridge at Corven's Mill, which would certainly cost more than a memory by the time she got there.

Mostly she thought about Ines — no, not yet, she didn't know the girl yet, that was later, that was a door not yet opened — she thought about children, about orphans on Greymill's docks, about all the small ears a beautiful terrible melody could find. About Doss, who had followed orders to the end and died for a lie he'd never been allowed to see. About Tam, writing truth in ink instead of song, because ink couldn't be hummed in taverns.

About her brother, who had loved songs, and died on a road because a god's war had rolled over him like weather.

"Take it," Marguerite said.

Maren rose. She crossed the shingle, and her hands — those root hands — framed Marguerite's face, thumbs resting at her temples, gentle as a mother's. Up close she smelled of river water and lamp oil and something older, mineral, like the inside of caves.

"It will feel like losing," Maren said. "That's how you'll know it worked. Things worth keeping always feel like losing."

"I know," Marguerite said. "I've had practice."

Maren smiled, sad and brief. "Hold it one last time. All the way through. That's the rite — you have to give it willingly, and giving means hearing it whole."

So Marguerite closed her eyes and let it play.

The song rose in her one final time, and she did not flinch from it. Water finding stone. Thaw. Laughter. Forgiveness. Thirty seconds that had taken two hundred years to be spoken, and she held every note of it, and she wept while she held it, openly, the way she had not wept over her brother or Doss or the god itself, and when the last of it faded Maren's thumbs pressed her temples and the song went out of her like a candle going out — not violently, not torn, just *gone*, and the space where it had been was smooth and clean and aching.

She gasped. Steadied. Opened her eyes.

The river ran. The mountain hummed, far away, meaning nothing, at peace.

"Well?" Maren asked.

"I remember that I knew it," Marguerite said slowly, testing the shape of the absence. "I remember what it was like. I just can't—" She made a sound that was almost a laugh. "It's like a room I've been locked out of. I remember the door."

"That's the toll," Maren said. "That's the whole trick of it. Now get in the boat. River doesn't wait for the philosophical."

* * *

The crossing took ten minutes. Maren poled them across the slow brown water in silence, and when they grounded on the north bank she handed Marguerite down with a grip that was almost kind.

"Where will you go?" the ferrywoman asked.

"Down," Marguerite said. "Greymill, eventually. There's a company needs burying properly, and a boy's writings need reading, and—" She stopped. She had been about to say *and roads need warding*, and she didn't know where that had come from, except that she did. "There's work."

"There always is." Maren pushed off from the bank, then paused, pole planted. "Mercenary. The song — you'll grieve it. Weeks, maybe months. You'll wake up humming and there'll be nothing there, and it'll feel like the worst day of your life, over and over."

"I know."

"But you'll also wake up free," Maren said. "First time in your life, I'd guess. Oathless. Some people can't bear it. Most people, honestly." Her eyes crinkled. "But you struck me, coming through here the first time, as someone who'd survive getting what she wanted."

She poled away into the brown water, and Marguerite stood on the north bank of the river with an empty pack, an empty chest, and a full purse of ordinary silver, and began, at last, the long walk down.

* * *

Greymill appeared out of the drizzle on the ninth day, smoke-stained and indifferent, its docks busy, its taverns loud, its orphan children quick-fingered and sharp-eyed along the waterfront. Nobody looked twice at a tired woman with a sword and a soldier's walk. The world had already begun forgetting Thornfell. That was the way of the lowlands; they forgot fast, and called it peace.

Marguerite found a room. Found a bath. Found, at the bottom of her pack, wrapped in oilcloth, the courier's map — scorched, bloodstained, worthless now that the vault was rubble — and stood a long time deciding what to do with it.

She burned it in the hearth, and watched the lines of the smuggler roads curl and vanish, and felt nothing but the ordinary satisfaction of a debt destroyed.

On the tenth day she walked to the town charter-house, where they hired men to watch the roads, and spoke to a harried clerk with ink on his fingers, and answered his questions in short flat sentences, and signed her name — *M. Thornbury* — beneath the words *road-warden, southern passes*, and walked out with a badge of tin and a list of bridges needing inspection.

Her brother would have laughed. Doss would have laughed harder. She found, examining the feeling, that she didn't mind. Laughing at her had never hurt either of them.

That evening she took a bowl of stew at a dockside tavern, cheap and crowded and smelling of tar, and somewhere in the noise of it she caught herself reaching for a melody that wasn't there — the way a tongue goes to the gap, the way a hand goes to a sword no longer worn — and the ache came, exactly as Maren had promised, clean and bright and unbearable.

And under the ache, steady as bedrock, the other thing. The reason.

*I gave it up so it could never be used,* she reminded herself. *Not silenced. Ended. Chosen.*

Outside, the wind came down off the northern passes, off the white shoulder of the broken mountain, and as it moved through Greymill's streets it carried a sound — faint, tuneless, neither voice nor wind. A hum in the stones. Several heads turned toward the windows. Someone said the mountain was haunted. Someone else said it was settling. A child by the door tugged her mother's sleeve and asked what it was saying.

Marguerite finished her stew, pulled her cloak around her shoulders, and stepped out into the drizzle toward the sound — toward the small dark-haired girl asking her question, toward whatever answer the road-warden of the southern passes would give, toward the long ordinary work of a life that owed nothing to any god and remembered everything the silence had cost.

The mountain hummed. The girl waited.

Marguerite opened her mouth to answer.
