## 22. Ines and the Thorn

Three years is long enough for a town to forget, but not long enough for a mountain to.

Greymill had grown in the way of border towns that survive a scare — sideways, cautiously, with new shutters on old windows. The ash-grey cloaks were gone from the streets. The Order's waystation by the millrace had been pulled down beam by beam and the timber sold off to build a tannery, which everyone agreed was a fitting use for it. There was even talk of a festival come spring, the first since the purges, though the elders still argued about what to call it. *The Quiet Day*, some wanted. Others said that was too much like thanking the silence, and nobody could agree on what else to thank.

Marguerite Thornbury did not attend council arguments. She walked her road.

The wardenship had come to her the way most honest things came — grudgingly, and after she'd refused twice. A road-warden's badge was a stamped tin disc on a leather cord, worth less than the sword at her hip, and its authority extended exactly as far as people were willing to listen to her. Which, as it turned out, was quite far. Three winters of clearing passes, breaking smuggler rings that preyed on refugees, and dragging frozen couriers out of snowdrifts had done what no oath ever had: it had made her trusted without binding her.

She carried no god now. She carried a ledger instead — a small oilskin book in her inner pocket where she wrote down names. Not debts. Names. The courier who had died at her fire with a map pressed into his hands. Doss, who had died reaching for a blade meant for her. Tam, who had stayed behind in the dark so that someone would remember what happened there, and whom she had carried out over his furious, weeping objections because he had already given enough to stone. Serane, who had turned her own blade on her Grand Inquisitor and vanished into the high country, alive or dead, no one knew. Her brother, whose name she spoke aloud every morning on the pass road, wind or no wind, because a name spoken into empty air goes somewhere, even if nowhere answers.

The book was nearly full. She had never once written in it what any of them deserved. Only that they had been, and that they had cost something, and that she remembered.

On the last day of thaw, she came down into Greymill with mud to her knees and a week of high-pass frost still in her bones, leading a string of three rescued mules and one very indignant goat. The town smelled of woodsmoke and river water and the first loaves of the morning — the ordinary smell of a place that expected to see tomorrow, which was, Marguerite had learned, the rarest perfume in the world.

The children found her before the stable did. They always did. Word ran through Greymill's gutters faster than any courier the Order had ever fielded: *the warden's back, the warden's back*, and then small feet slapping cobbles, and then the crowd.

They knew the ritual. Mules to Harl the farrier, who paid her in coin and complaints in equal measure. Goat to Widow Senna, who claimed she hadn't asked for it and fed it better than she fed herself. And then questions, held back with visible effort until the business was done, because Marguerite had rules and Greymill's orphans had learned them the hard way.

Ines arrived last, as she always did, hanging back at the edge of the others with her arms crossed and her chin up, pretending she hadn't run.

She was nine now, maybe ten — orphan-age being an estimate in a town that didn't keep records of the unclaimed. Small and sharp-boned, with a scowl she'd borrowed from Marguerite and worn so long it had become her own. She had a scar on her forearm from a kettle of boiling water in the dye-house where she'd worked two winters, before Marguerite had made the dye-master pay out her wages and find her lighter work, and before Ines had informed Marguerite, with terrifying gravity, that she intended to be a road-warden too, when she was big enough, and that Marguerite would have to teach her everything.

Marguerite had said no one has to teach you anything you can learn walking. Ines had taken this as a yes.

* * *

The hum started that evening.

Marguerite heard it first from the steps of the Waystone inn, a mug of Harl's terrible beer going warm in her hand. It came down off the mountain on the wind the way it always did now — not a voice, not words, nothing with intent in it. Just resonance. Stone remembering itself. The vault had gone into the mountain three years ago, and the mountain had never quite stopped ringing, the way a bell holds its note if you lay your palm against it gently enough.

The first winter, the whole town had been afraid of it. People had slept with charms under their pillows and nailed iron over their doors, waiting for the god to come back angry, or the Order to come back at all. But the hum asked nothing. It promised nothing. It was the sound the world made when no one was commanding it, and slowly, season by season, Greymill had stopped flinching and started calling it what the children called it: *the mountain singing*. Mothers used it to soothe babies. Old men claimed they could tell the weather by its pitch. Nobody worshipped it. That was the difference, and Marguerite felt the difference in her chest every time, like a bone that had finally set straight.

Tonight the hum was low and steady, a sound more felt than heard, rising off the peaks with the evening wind.

Ines heard it too. She came out of the inn's shadow where she'd been waiting — not running this time, walking slow, the way she did when she'd rehearsed something — and stood beside Marguerite at the rail with her elbows on the wood, mimicking her posture exactly.

They listened together. Down in the lane, lamplighters were making their rounds. Somewhere a dog barked at the mules and thought better of it.

"What does it say?" Ines asked.

It was not the first time she'd asked. She asked every few months, with the patience of a child who has noticed that adults give different answers to the same question and is testing which ones hold. Once Marguerite had said *wind through the passes*. Once she'd said *old stone settling*. Once, on a bad night, near the anniversary, she'd said *nothing you need to carry yet* — and Ines, sharp as a fishhook even at eight, had understood that *yet* meant someday, and had waited out the year with the grim diligence of a creditor.

Marguerite looked at the mountain. Snowfields going blue in the dusk. The pass road she'd walked a hundred times, and the dark seam beneath it where a door had opened once and would never open again. Somewhere under all that stone was a silence that had been chosen — hers, freely given, the only gift she'd ever delivered that no one had paid her to carry. The voice had begged her, at the end. It had offered her her brother's heartbeat, her god's forgiveness, every truth in the world laid out like blades on a table. And she had held it while it broke apart, and she had felt it go into the rock rather than into the world, and she had walked out of Thornfell owing nothing to anyone, carrying only the memory of what the silence had cost and the song it had almost been.

"Nothing," Marguerite said.

Ines frowned. "I can hear it."

"I know."

"Then it says *something*."

"It says nothing," Marguerite said again, and crouched down so they were eye to eye, because this answer mattered and Ines deserved to receive it standing straight. "That's how you know it's at peace."

Ines considered this with the total seriousness she brought to everything — ledgers, scars, the education of future wardens. "The Order people said the god talked. My mother—" she stopped, adjusted. *My mother* was a sentence Ines was still learning to finish differently than she'd learned it. "The people before said gods talk, and you have to listen, and if you don't listen right you get punished."

"They did say that." Marguerite kept her voice level. "And look what listening got them."

"So it's better when it doesn't talk?"

"It's better," Marguerite said, "when nothing is telling you what you are. When the voice in your head is your own." She tapped Ines once, lightly, on the sternum. "That one's the only voice you owe anything to. Everything else — gods, captains, grand inquisitors, me — you listen, you weigh it, and you decide. That's the whole job. Wardening. Living. All of it."

Ines absorbed this. Then, because she was Ines: "Did you decide? About the mountain?"

The question went in deeper than a nine-year-old could know. Or maybe she knew exactly; orphans always knew more than their size suggested. Marguerite looked past her at the darkening slopes, at the place where the snow held the shape of everything she'd buried.

"Yes," she said. "I decided."

"And it doesn't hurt? Deciding?"

"It hurts like a set bone hurts. Bad, and then better, and then you can put weight on it."

Ines nodded slowly, filing this away next to everything else Marguerite had ever told her, which Marguerite suspected the girl kept in some inner ledger of her own — a mirror of the oilskin book, though Ines's probably recorded promises instead of the dead.

"The hum's louder tonight," Ines said, changing the subject with a child's abruptness. "Harl says it means a hard melt. Widow Senna says it means the mountain's happy. What do you say it means?"

"I say it means the mountain's awake," Marguerite said, "and nobody's making it do anything. Best sound there is."

They stood at the rail until full dark, shoulder to shoulder, the warden and the orphan who intended to be a warden, while Greymill lit its lamps below and Thornfell rang its long, empty, peaceful note above. Eventually Ines yawned enormously, ruined her own dignity, and stomped off toward Widow Senna's with a muttered *goodnight, warden* that was almost too casual to be affectionate and wasn't fooling either of them.

* * *

Marguerite climbed to her room above the stable, took out the oilskin ledger, and read it by candlelight.

Names in her own blunt hand. Some pages water-stained from a winter she preferred not to think about. On the last written page, Tam's name, and beneath it, added recently in smaller script: *writes to me now. From the coast. Says he's teaching. Says the archives will stay sealed another generation and that maybe sealed is right.* He signed his letters with the old scholarly formula — *yours in memory* — which had once been a religious phrase and was now just a true one.

She turned to the first page. Her brother's name, first line, first entry, ink gone brown with age. Below it, the courier. Below him, Doss. She read them the way she did every night — not as a prayer, because prayers asked things, and she asked nothing anymore of anything. As a roll call. As proof.

Then she closed the book, blew out the candle, and lay down to sleep the dreamless sleep of a woman whose debts were paid.

Except that night, for the first time in three years, she dreamed.

Not of the vault. Not of the voice begging in the dark, not of Serane's face as the truth unmade her, not of Doss falling. Those dreams had come for two winters and then, like a fever, broken. This was something else.

She dreamed of the pass road in summer, and it was ordinary — gravel, marmots, the white hump of the Shoulder against the sky. She was walking it with a string of mules, and the mules were calm, and the air smelled of pine and thaw. And the mountain hummed, the way it did, and in the dream the hum had a shape she could almost hear, the way you can almost hear your name in running water. Not words. Not commands. Just — acknowledgment. The sound stone makes when it notices you passing and bears you no ill will.

A road knows its walkers. That was all. That was the whole message, if it was a message: *the road remembers you, and asks nothing.*

She woke before dawn with wet eyes and no grief attached to them, which was new, and sat on the edge of the bed letting it be strange. Outside, the hum came thin and clear through the shutter cracks, and the first birds were arguing about the morning, and somewhere below a cart rattled toward the gate because Greymill was waking up whether the mountain sang or not.

Marguerite dressed, buckled on her sword, took up her staff, and touched the tin warden's badge at her throat — worthless, weightless, hers.

Downstairs, the inn yard was grey-gold with early light. And there, sitting on the mounting block with a travel bundle between her feet and her scowl firmly in place, was Ines.

"You're going up the pass today," Ines said. Not a question. She'd watched the farrier shoe the mules the night before; she counted everything.

"I am."

"Take me."

"No."

"I'm big enough for the low trail. To the shrine and back. You said yourself the low trail's safe as a kitchen."

"I said no such thing."

"You said *safer than most things*." Ines stood, chin up, bundle gripped in both fists, prepared for a siege. "You keep saying I'll learn it walking. So let me walk."

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. At the borrowed scowl. At the scarred arm and the stubborn spine and the fierce, unbought, unbowed heart of her — a child with no god over her and no master behind her, wanting only a road and someone to show her how to walk it without being owned by it.

The hum came down faintly off the mountain, asking nothing, promising nothing, keeping its peace.

"One trip," Marguerite said. "Low trail only. You do what I say the first time I say it, and you carry your own bundle."

Ines's face did something complicated and triumphant that she immediately tried to hide by scowling harder.

"And warden," Marguerite added, swinging up into the saddle as the girl scrambled for the lead mule's rope, "first lesson starts now. What do you hear?"

Ines stopped. Listened — really listened, head cocked, the whole mountain in her ears. Wind. Water. Birds. The deep, patient ring of stone at peace.

"Nothing," she said.

Marguerite smiled, turned the mule toward the pass, and rode up into the morning with the road unfolding ahead of them — long, hard, free, and worth every step.
