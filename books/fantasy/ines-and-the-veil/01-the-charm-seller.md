## 1. The Charm Seller

There are three rules to selling charms in Veymar, and I invented all of them myself, which is the only kind of lawmaking I've done in seven years.

Rule one: never sell anything that works.

Rule two: never let them see your hands shake when it almost does.

Rule three — and this is the important one, the one I carved into the back of my own skull somewhere around year two — *never be interesting.*

I was arranging my stall in the night market under a sky the color of wet slate, doing the small ritual of unremarkability that had kept me alive longer than any spell ever had. A jar of river-stones "blessed" by no one. Bundles of dried lavender that were just lavender, though I charged extra for the suggestion. Knots of red thread sold as luck-charms, tied in patterns I chose specifically because they meant nothing at all. That last part took discipline. My fingers wanted to tie them properly. My fingers had opinions about knots the way priests have opinions about God, and every night I overruled them, and every night they forgave me, and every night we started again.

"Evening, Master Corvin."

The fishwife from the end of the row, Marta, broad as a door and twice as hard to move. She bought a lavender bundle every third night whether she needed it or not, which she never did, because her husband grew better lavender on their own windowsill. It was charity, dressed up as commerce so we could both keep our dignity. In Veymar even kindness has to wear a disguise.

"Marta." I handed her the bundle before she could ask. "You look like you've been arguing with the tide."

"The tide cheats," she said, and paid me with a coin that had been in her mouth, and left, and that was the whole of our friendship: four sentences, once a week, for six years. I want to be clear that I treasured it. When you have erased yourself down to the bone, you learn the weight of very small things.

My name was Corvin. Corvin the charm-seller, Corvin with the bad knee and the dead wife he'd invented for sympathy purposes and the stall between the candle-dipper and the man who sold eels. Corvin had existed for seven years and had never once said anything clever, because clever is a scent and inquisitors hunt by scent. Corvin was boring the way a stone is boring, and I loved him for it, the way you love a coat that keeps the rain off. I put him on every dusk. I hung him up every dawn.

Underneath, there was just me, and I tried not to look at him much.

* * *

The market at night is a good place to be nobody. Three hundred stalls under oilcloth and lantern-glass, the harbor breathing its cold salt breath through every alley, and everyone here selling something they'd rather not discuss with the crown. Not magic — real magic hadn't been seen openly in Veymar since before my time, since the edicts, since the drowning pools got their name — but the *shape* of it. Hedge-remedies. Whispered fortunes. Love philtres that were wine and optimism. The city had outlawed wonder and gotten superstition instead, which is what always happens; you cannot kill a thing like that, you can only drive it into ditches, where it grows crooked.

I knew exactly how crooked it grew. I was its most successful product.

Seven years ago I had been something else. Seven years ago I had a name that wasn't Corvin and hands that could do things to thread and fire and blood that would have gotten me drowned in the palace square with the crowd singing the hymn they sing at drownings — the cheerful one, the one with the clapping. Seven years ago my order —

No. Let's not start there. Starting there is how people get caught. You think about who you were, and your face changes, and faces are loud. Rule three.

A boy stopped at my stall, maybe ten years old, holding a coin in his fist like it was hot. "Me mum wants a luck-knot. For the nets."

"For the nets," I repeated gravely, as though this were a matter requiring professional judgment. I selected a red thread knot — one of the meaningless ones, a pattern I'd learned from a fisherman's wife in a town whose name I don't say anymore — and held it out. "Tell her to hang it high. Luck rises."

That's the sort of thing charm-sellers say. It means nothing. It's supposed to mean nothing.

But my fingers had tied it while I wasn't watching, and when the boy took it, the knot sat in his palm with a faint warmth that had nothing to do with thread, and for half a heartbeat — half a heartbeat, no more — the market went quiet around us. Not silent. Quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone says a true thing. The lanterns leaned. Somewhere in my chest, behind seven years of locked doors, something lifted its head.

I snatched the knot back. "Wrong one," I said loudly, cheerfully, the way a man says *nothing to see here*. "This one's for luck in love. You don't want that, you're ten." I tied another, clumsily, deliberately wrong, my knuckles white with the effort of not letting the thread remember what it was for. "Here. Nets. High up. Goodnight."

He went off puzzled but satisfied, and I stood there with my heart slamming against my ribs like a prisoner who'd heard the cell door rattle.

That's the job, really. That's the whole job. Not hiding the magic — anyone can hide a sword. Hiding the *wanting*. Because the magic doesn't live in your hands, whatever the old texts claim. It lives in the wanting, and wanting leaks. Wanting comes out your skin at night. Wanting ties knots when you're not looking.

I closed early that night.

* * *

You should understand what happens to people like me in Veymar, so you'll understand why I did what I did next, which was nothing.

The inquisitors wear grey, because grey is the color of ash and ash is what they promise. They walk the markets in pairs with white gloves and long poles tipped with iron, and the poles aren't weapons. They're *listeners*. Iron remembers what touched it, and if enough of a certain kind of touch has passed near you, the pole hums. I've watched it happen twice. Once to an old woman who'd been healing fevers in the tannery district for forty years — she didn't run, she just stood there with her hands open, and I will hear the sound the crowd made until I die. Once to a boy of sixteen who'd lit a candle without flint because his sweetheart dared him. Sixteen. They fished him out of the pool at dawn and the hymn was still going.

So: rule three. Never be interesting. And above all, never, ever let iron remember you.

I was packing the stall — stones, lavender, thread, the little tin box of coins that represented Corvin's entire honest livelihood — when the pair came down my row.

They weren't hunting. That was the worst part. They were strolling. One of them was eating a candied plum off a stick, which struck me then, and strikes me now, as the single most obscene thing I have ever witnessed. The other dragged his listener-pole lazily along the cobbles behind him like a man walking a dog he didn't like, and the pole hummed its low idle note, the note it made when the world was clean.

And then the boy's mother reached my stall.

She was a net-mender, big forearms, tired eyes, and she had the luck-knot in her hand — the second one, the deliberately-wrong one, the safe one — except that thread is thread, and thread that has passed through my hands twice in one evening carries a little of the first time along with it, the way a bell keeps ringing after you've grabbed it. Faintly. Immeasurably faintly. To you and me, nothing.

To iron, perhaps a whisper.

The pole hummed. Changed pitch. Just slightly — the way a room changes when someone enters it.

The inquisitor with the plum stopped chewing.

I want to tell you I did something brave. I want to tell you that the old Ulyana — the one with the name and the order and the voice that could make steel sing — stepped out from behind Corvin's dull grey eyes and handled it, spun some elegant working that unraveled the memory out of the thread and left the inquisitor blinking at a plum.

What actually happened was this: I became the most boring man in Veymar.

"Evening," I said, in Corvin's flat voice, looking up from my coins with the mild irritation of a tradesman interrupted. "Stall's closing. Come back tomorrow if you want stones."

The inquisitor looked at me. Grey eyes, grey gloves, the particular patience of a man who has never once been in a hurry because the whole world eventually drowns. He raised the pole. It swung toward my stall — toward the thread, the lavender, the jar of blessed-by-no-one stones — and hummed.

I counted my heartbeats. I got to nine.

It was the boy's mother who saved me, though she'll never know it. "Three coppers for the knot," she said loudly, slapping coins onto my board, "and you shortchanged me on the lavender last week, Master Corvin, don't think I forgot," and she launched into a complaint of such magnificent, detailed pettiness — the bundle was thin, the stems were dry, her sister-in-law got a better one from the candle-dipper — that the inquisitor's attention slid off me onto her, and then off her entirely, because a haggling fishwife is the least interesting object in creation, and rule three applies to everyone.

The pole drifted past my stall. The hum faded back to its idle note. The inquisitor with the plum finished the plum.

They moved on down the row, and I stood there with three coppers and a complaint ringing in my ears, and I did not exhale until they'd turned the corner, and when I finally did exhale it came out sounding like something breaking.

"That," said the net-mender, quietly, not looking at me, folding her new knot into her apron, "was closer than usual."

"You saw that."

"I see everything on this row." She picked up her basket. "Whatever it is you're not, Master Corvin — whoever it is — you might want to stop being it soon. The greys have walked this market four times this month. Four. Used to be once, maybe." She shrugged, huge shoulders rising and falling like weather. "Something's got them stirred up. Something under the palace, my gran would've said, and she believed worse nonsense than I sell." She paused. "Than you sell."

Then she was gone, and I was alone with my stall and my shaking hands, and the market noise came back in around me like water closing over a dropped stone.

Four times this month. I turned that over while I packed, and it wouldn't sit still. Inquisitors don't stroll. Inquisitors don't buy candied plums. Pairs walk a market row when they're mapping it, learning its rhythms, deciding which stalls to visit later with more than a pole. Someone had given them a reason. Someone under the palace, maybe, in whatever grey stone rooms the orders come from.

Or something.

I laughed at myself for that — softly, alone, the way you laugh when the alternative is thinking. *Something under the palace.* Seven years of careful nothing, and one fishwife's grandmother-talk had me spooked. There was no something. There was a king I'd never seen, behind walls I'd never approach, and there was me, Corvin, bad knee, dead wife, jar of stones, and there was the long grey future of being nobody until nobody was all that remained.

I banked my lantern. I shouldered my pack. And that was when I noticed the smell.

Blood. Faint, from the direction of the harbor lane, threading through the salt and smoke — the specific copper-and-fear smell of a wound that's been bleeding a while. The market was emptying; the eel-seller was shuttering his cart two stalls down, whistling. Nobody else had caught it yet, or cared.

I should have gone home. Rule three, rule one, rule two, all the rules, the whole legal code of Corvin the charm-seller, written in seven years of staying alive: go home, bar the door, count your coppers, be no one.

The smell got stronger.

And underneath it — I told myself I imagined this, and I have been telling myself that ever since, and neither of us believes it — underneath the blood, the night carried a sound too low for ears. A hum, like thread remembering. Like iron listening. Like something old, far away beneath the city, turning over in its sleep and beginning, slowly, to pay attention.

I followed the smell instead of going home.

It led toward my shop.
