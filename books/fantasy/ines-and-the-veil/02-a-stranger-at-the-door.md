## 2. A Stranger at the Door

There are three rules to surviving in Veymar as a man who shouldn't exist, and I had followed all of them for seven years without exception.

Rule one: never work the same market twice in a month. Rule two: never sell anything that actually works. Rule three — and this is the one that keeps you breathing — never, under any circumstances, let anyone die on your floor.

I broke rule three on a Tuesday.

* * *

Tuesdays were closing days. I'd chosen them deliberately: a shop that shut early once a week looked like a shop with habits, and habits look like honesty. So when the bell above my door gave its cracked little cough at half past nine, I was already blowing out the lamps, already counting myself lucky that whoever it was would be turned away with an apology and a promise of morning hours.

The stranger came through the door the way water comes through a failing roof — all at once, and everywhere he wasn't wanted.

He was tall, or had been before whatever was happening to him finished its argument. His coat was courier's grey, the kind with the royal post's badge torn off at the shoulder, threads still hanging where it had been ripped rather than unpinned. He left a smear of himself down the doorframe as he came, dark and glistening, and his hand found the counter the way a drowning man finds driftwood.

"Closed," I said, because rule two has a cousin: never sound interesting. "Come back tomorrow."

He looked up at me. I have seen a lot of looks in seven years of selling nothing to no one — suspicion, contempt, the particular blankness of people who think charm-sellers are a step above beggars and a step below thieves. This was none of those. This was recognition. He looked at me the way you look at a door you've been searching a burning city for.

"You're him," he said. "You're the charm-seller who isn't."

"I'm afraid I'm exactly what I appear—"

"Ulyana." My name. My actual name, spoken aloud in my own shop for the first time in seven years, and it went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. "Delacroix." He said it like a password. Like a key turning.

Every instinct I owned stood up at once and started screaming. The sensible ones told me to deny it, to laugh, to reach for the little brass bell I kept for chasing off drunks. But underneath the sensible ones there was an older instinct, one I'd buried so deep I'd forgotten it had a pulse, and that instinct said: *he knows, and he came here anyway, which means he had nowhere else.*

That is a terrible reason to do anything. It is also, apparently, the only reason I respond to anymore.

"Lock it," he said, jerking his chin toward the door. Blood was coming from somewhere under his coat in quantities that made the word *somewhere* increasingly academic. "Please. Lock it."

I locked it. I want that on the record. Whatever else happened that night, whatever I became afterward, I locked the door first. Some small obedient clerk in my chest still believed that procedure could save us both.

Then he took his hand off the counter, reached inside his coat, and pressed something into my hands.

It was folded paper, soft with handling, stained along one edge in a way I didn't need lamplight to identify. He folded my fingers over it with both of his — his hands were cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season — and then he gripped mine around it like a man entrusting a child to a stranger on a sinking ship.

"The king," he said, and his voice was going somewhere I couldn't follow, thinning out like a thread pulled through cloth, "is not the king."

"What?"

But he wasn't listening anymore. He was looking past me, at the shelves of my honest dishonesty — the luck-knots and the love-charms made of string and optimism, the bottled fog I sold as bottled courage. And he smiled. That's the part I keep returning to, the part that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone. He smiled at my fake charms like they were the truest thing he'd seen in years.

"They'll come asking," he said. "Don't be home. Don't be *you*."

And then Ines's brother — though I didn't know yet that he was anyone's brother, that he had a sister counting his footsteps home on the docks — slid down my counter and died on my floorboards between the third plank and the fourth, the loose one, the one I'd been meaning to fix since spring.

* * *

For a long time — I couldn't tell you how long; clocks are cowards in moments like that — I stood there holding a dead man's map and listening to my own heart conduct its panic.

Here is what terror does to me, and I confess it freely: it makes me practical. Other men freeze or flee or find religion. I start making lists. It's a flaw. It may be the only reason I'm alive.

The list went something like this:

One. Body. Two. Map. Three. The blood on the doorframe, the blood on the counter, the blood on the third-and-fourth planks. Four. The fact that I had spoken my own name aloud in this room, which meant the room itself was now a kind of evidence. Five. Dawn, which was coming whether I liked it or not, dragging the harbor bells and the fish carts and the neighbors behind it.

I put the map in my shirt first. Against the skin. I don't know why — it felt less like hiding and more like keeping, and there is a difference, and the difference mattered to some part of me that was already changing and didn't know it yet.

Then I looked at the body for a long moment, and I said, out loud, to no one, "I did not sign up for this."

The body did not disagree.

Moving him was the worst hour of my life up to that point — a record that, I note grimly, did not hold. He was heavier than dying men usually get the chance to be, and he bled in ways that required decisions, and every decision required me to touch him, and touching him made it real in a way that the seeing hadn't. Somewhere in the middle of wrestling him toward the cellar hatch, I stopped and sat down on the stairs and put my head in my hands, because I am not made of stone, whatever the last seven years had tried to carve me into.

There's a cellar under the shop. Charm-sellers keep cellars; it's where you store the stock that would hang you if the inquisitors ever got curious about what *actually* cures headaches in my bottles. Seven years I'd kept that cellar empty of everything but dust and one crate of contraband luck-knots, and now it held a dead courier instead, laid out on the flagstones with his ruined coat folded under his head like a pillow, because even in panic I couldn't bring myself to leave his head on bare stone.

I covered him with sacking. Then, feeling ridiculous and doing it anyway, I tucked the sacking under his chin.

"There," I said. "That's — that's all I've got. I'm sorry. I don't even know your name."

Silence answered, the specific heavy silence of a cellar holding a secret.

I climbed the stairs, barred the hatch, and set about the third item on the list with a bucket, lye, and the steady hands of a man who had once closed wounds on a battlefield and had spent seven years pretending those hands belonged to someone gentler and stupider.

* * *

Dawn found me sitting behind my counter with the shutters open and the shop looking, as far as I could manage it, like a shop that had never hosted anything but commerce.

I had slept perhaps none. Every creak of the building became boots. Every gull's cry outside became a whistle signal. Twice I caught myself reaching for magic to calm my nerves — just a thread of it, a whisper of swordspeech to settle the heart — and twice I strangled the impulse before it could become a habit again, because that's how they catch you, in the end. Not with chains. With comfort.

The blood was gone. The body was below. The map was against my ribs, and I had made the mistake, somewhere around the fourth hour of sleeplessness, of unfolding it.

Just once. Just to know what I was carrying.

It was drawn on good paper, harbor-chart paper, the kind that survives salt. And across its face ran lines that I recognized the way you recognize your mother's handwriting — instantly, painfully, from a great distance. Not roads. Not boundaries. *Words.* Old ones. Swordspeech, the high dialect, the kind my order used for oaths and unbindings, inked in a hand that had known what it was writing even if it hadn't known how to read it back.

A dead courier had carried a map written in the language of a purged order, straight to the one surviving member of that order, and died saying the king was not the king.

I refolded it. I put it back against my skin. I opened my shutters and sold a love-charm to a fishwife and lied to her about its power and hated myself with a fresh, specific intensity.

Around noon, the bell coughed again, and I nearly came out of my skin.

It was only a boy. Street-thin, maybe ten years old, all elbows and enormous eyes, holding out a coin he'd clearly earned the hard way. "Mister. You got anything for bad dreams?"

I looked at this child — this ordinary, hungry, uninvolved child — and thought about the words *they'll come asking,* and about how many children in this city had already asked their own questions and vanished for it.

"For bad dreams," I repeated, and reached under the counter for the bottle of sugar-water and valerian that I sold to everyone and that worked on no one, and found that my hands were shaking too badly to pour it.

The boy noticed. Children always notice. He cocked his head at me, sharp as a gull, and said:

"You look like a man what heard something singing under the floor."

I gave him the bottle free. I closed early.

* * *

That night I sat in the dark with the map in my lap and the hatch barred and made the argument I'd been avoiding all day.

The safe move was obvious. The river was close. Sacking sinks. By morning the problem would be someone else's current, and by week's end I could be in Gellert or Corvane under a new false name, selling new false charms, having successfully survived my eighth year by erasing my ninth before it started. Seven years of discipline pointed that direction like a compass needle.

Against it stood a dead man's smile, aimed at my worthless charms like they were holy.

And underneath that — and this is the truth I've been circling all this time, the one I'd have denied under torture — underneath that was the simple, unbearable fact that someone had finally needed me. Not Ulyana-the-charm-seller, not the careful gray man with the forgettable face. Someone had run bleeding through a city that drowns witches, past a hundred doors, to *my* door, and pressed the last thing he had into my hands, and trusted me with it.

Seven years of self-erasure, and it turns out you can't erase the part of a man that wants to matter.

"I should burn it," I told the empty shop.

The map lay warm against my knee, humming faintly — or maybe that was my blood, or the harbor, or guilt. Hard to say. Guilt hums at a lot of frequencies.

"I should burn it and go and never think about any of this again."

I didn't burn it. Of course I didn't. I sat there until the small hours, holding it, and the longer I held it the more certain I became of two things I didn't want to be certain of: that the words on it were real swordspeech, older and stranger than anything I'd been taught; and that whoever wrote them had known exactly who I was, which meant somebody, somewhere, had kept a list of survivors.

Which meant somebody might be working down that list.

I was still turning that thought over when the knock came.

Three raps. Measured. Patient. The knock of a man who gets doors opened for him professionally.

I went very still. Through the gap in the shutters I could see the street, lamplit and empty except for a figure standing opposite my shop — grey-robed, unhurried, the hood pushed back just enough to show a face that was all patience and no warmth.

An inquisitor. On my street. At my door. Twelve hours after a man died whispering treason into my hands.

The knock came again. Three raps. Same rhythm.

And beneath the floorboards, beneath the sacking and the silence, my shop felt suddenly very small, and the map against my chest began — unmistakably this time, undeniably — to sing.
