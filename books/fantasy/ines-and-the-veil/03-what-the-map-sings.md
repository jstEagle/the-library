## 3. What the Map Sings

I did not sleep.

This is not a confession of grief or conscience. I simply want to be precise, because precision is the last discipline I have left. I sat in the chair by my dead hearth with a dead man's map folded in my dead master's oilcloth, and I listened to it hum.

Yes. Hum.

You would have heard it too, if you'd held it. A low note, felt more than heard, like a tuning fork pressed to your teeth. Like the moment before a bell rings. I have spent seven years teaching myself not to flinch at sounds like that, and there I was, holding one against my palm like a lover's letter.

The humming had words in it. That was the worst part.

Not common speech. Not the trade-tongue of the harbor or the high Veymari of the courts. It was *swordspeech*—the old cadence, the one we spoke with our whole bodies, where a vowel could be a stance and a consonant a cut. My order's dialect. The one Master Serafina used to drill into us until we dreamed in it. *The blade is a sentence,* she said. *Magic is only grammar.*

And here was a dead courier's map, singing her grammar back at me through the floorboards of my shop.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about maps that sing, because apparently I'm the last person alive who knows anything.

A swordspeaker's chart isn't drawn. It's *spoken*—inked while the cartographer holds the true name of a place in his mouth, so that the place stays in the ink, faintly saying itself forever. It's a forbidden art twice over: once for the magic, once for the memory. There were perhaps forty people in the world who could make one when I was a boy. I can name you eleven graves and thirty ash-heaps.

So either the dead man on my floor had been one of the forty—

—or he had known someone who was.

Or—and this was the thought I kept setting down and picking up again, like a coin I couldn't stop flipping—he had known *me*. Because the dialect in the hum wasn't just old swordspeech. It was *our* swordspeech. The coastal inflection. Serafina's cadence, passed down her line of teaching, the little downward turn she put on the third verse of every binding-formula because she said it made the magic sit down and behave. No other school spoke it that way. I would know. I spent seven years making sure no one else got the chance.

Which meant the man who bled out on my floorboards had gone looking for me. Not for a charm-seller named Maro Venn, whose papers were flawless and whose hands were beautifully ordinary. For whatever was underneath him.

He had known what I was.

I set the map on the table and looked at it the way you look at a snake you've decided not to kill yet. In the lamplight I could see the lines now—not roads, exactly. Channels. The harbor rendered as a system of throats, all of them feeding toward the palace rock. And beneath the palace, in ink so old it had gone brown, a shape I recognized from nightmares: a cathedral, drawn upside down, its spire pointing down into the deep water like a root.

Someone had written along its base in swordspeech. I read it without meaning to—the way you read your own name across a room. Seven years of silence, and the words came up my throat unbidden, tasting of chalk dust and winter mornings and a woman's voice saying *again, Ulyana, you're mumbling*:

*What was drowned was not destroyed. What was bound may be spoken free.*

I closed my mouth. My heart was going like a smuggler's oars.

"No," I told the map, conversationally, because talking to objects is the first sign of a lonely life and I had long since stopped fighting it. "No, thank you. I've read this story. The swordspeaker opens the seal, the swordspeaker dies, the city gets a statue. I've seen the statue. It's very dignified."

The map hummed.

It sounded, I thought, unconvinced.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the shutters, the way it always does in Veymar, like the sky apologizing. I went through the motions of opening the shop because motions are how I survive: sweep the step, unlock the door, arrange the luck-knots and the salt-charms and the little braided cords that are, I want to stress, entirely decorative. Seven years of being aggressively harmless. I could teach a seminar.

I was arranging a tray of river-stones—painted, not enchanted; I paint them myself, badly—when the bells started.

Not the harbor bells. Not the hour bells. The *third* bell, the one from the Citadel tower that nobody hears except during sweeps, and every person in the night market has memorized anyway, the way you memorize the sound of a storm coming. Three notes, then silence, then three notes.

Inquisitors. District sweep. Now.

Here is what a sweep looks like from the inside: the market doesn't panic. Panic is loud, and loud gets noticed. The market simply *closes*. Shutters bang down in a rolling wave like a wind moving through wheat. The fishwife vanishes mid-sentence. The boy who runs messages evaporates off the corner he's occupied since before dawn. Within ninety seconds the street is empty, swept clean as a temple floor, and I am standing in my doorway holding a tray of painted rocks like an idiot.

Maro Venn, charm-seller, licensed and dull, does not close his shop during sweeps. Maro Venn stands in his doorway and looks bored, because bored men are invisible, and invisibility is the only spell I've cast in seven years.

So I stood there. Bored. While the black-and-silver came up the street.

There were six of them, walking in the loose formation they use when they're not chasing anyone yet. Gray cloaks, silver mask-plates over their faces, each plate stamped with the Veil's mark—a circle with a vertical line through it, which the priests say represents the king's shadow cast by divine light, and which everyone else privately agrees looks like an eye that's been shut. At their center walked a reader, carrying the censer on its chain, swinging it slow.

The censer didn't burn incense. It burned *residue*. Old magic leaves traces the way a fire leaves smoke in cloth—a luck-knot whispered over, a healing done in a cellar, a word of power spoken even once. The censer tasted the air for it. And I stood in my doorway watching it swing closer, thinking about the dead man's blood on my floorboards, which I had scrubbed at midnight with lye and salt, and about the map upstairs under a floorboard of its own, humming its traitor's hum, and about whether residue could soak into wood, and whether wood could lie.

The reader stopped.

Six paces away. The censer swung between us, slow as a pendulum, and I watched the smoke curl off it—thin, gray, undecided. My face was doing something marvelous, I'm sure. Maro Venn's face has a whole repertoire: mild interest, mild boredom, the small apologetic smile of a man whose wares are useless and knows it. Behind that face, the rest of me was screaming in a dead language.

"You're the charm-seller," the reader said. Her voice was muffled by the mask, flat as slate.

"Maro Venn." I gestured at the tray. "Stones. Cords. Knots. All perfectly inert, I assure you. The knots are decorative."

"We know what the knots are."

"They're *decorative*," I said again, with the gentle firmness of a man explaining something to a child, and something in me marveled even as it shook: seven years, and the lying still fit like my own coat.

She looked at me a moment longer. The mask gave away nothing, but the censer's smoke drifted, thinned, and I let one breath out slowly through my nose.

Then the man beside her—the tall one, the one with the ledger chained to his belt—said something quiet to her, and she turned away, and the sweep moved on down the street, checking doorways, rapping shutters, leaving boredom in its wake.

I went inside and was sick in the washbasin. Then I washed the basin. Then I laughed, briefly and horribly, at the sink, at the stones, at the whole theater of it.

Because here's what the tall one with the ledger had said. I read lips. It's another skill from the old curriculum, though Serafina taught it for negotiation rather than survival. He'd said:

*"Blood trace in the drains. Two streets back. Courier-sized."*

They hadn't found the body. But they'd found where the body had been, and the trail led—was leading, even now, drop by drop through Veymar's ancient plumbing—toward my street. Toward my door. Toward the shop of Maro Venn, decorative knot enthusiast, who had spent one entire night with a corpse and a singing map and had scrubbed the wrong things.

* * *

I gave myself until noon to decide. This is a rule I keep: never run on fear, because fear runs stupid. Fear takes the map and forgets the boots. Fear burns the evidence and keeps the receipts.

I spent the morning doing inventory of my life, which fits in one satchel. That's the whole summary of seven years of careful living: one bag. Papers—Maro Venn's papers, flawless, worthless now if the blood-trace reached the inquisitors' readers. Coin, most of it sewn into the coat lining years ago against exactly this morning. The good knife. The whetstone. And then I knelt, pried up the floorboard, and took out the map.

It hummed against my palms like a trapped bird.

"I should burn you," I told it. "That's the sensible move. Burn the map, walk south, be nobody in some other city with worse weather."

The map hummed.

"It's not an argument," I said. "It's arithmetic. One dead courier means they'll sweep again. Two visits means questions. Questions mean the censer comes *inside*, and the censer finds the residue of a night's worth of scrubbing a floor that will never come clean, and then Maro Venn drowns in the harbor square to general public satisfaction, and the map goes in an evidence box, and everyone lives happily ever after except me."

The map hummed. Louder. And this time—I swear this on whatever's left of my soul—it changed pitch. Dropped low. Took on that downward turn on the third phrase, Serafina's turn, the one that makes the magic sit down and behave.

It sounded like her. It sounded like scolding.

I sat down on the floorboards with a dead man's map growling at me in my dead master's voice, and I understood, finally, the thing I'd been dodging since the stranger fell through my door: the past doesn't stay buried. It waits. It learns addresses. It sends couriers.

"The king is not the king," I quoted softly. "Is that it? Is that what he found? What did you show him?"

The map, naturally, said nothing useful. Maps are like masters that way.

But I had read the line beneath the drowned cathedral, and I knew the hand it was written in. I had copied that hand for ten years—slanting, economical, the descenders cut short because she said wasted ink was wasted breath. Master Serafina's hand, on a map that predated her death by decades, pointing down into the water beneath the palace.

My master had known about the cathedral. Had maybe *made* the map. Had died kneeling somewhere, keeping some secret, while I ran south and learned to paint rocks.

I folded the map along its old creases, wrapped it in the oilcloth, and put it inside my coat, against my chest, where I could feel it humming like a second heartbeat I never asked for.

Then I took Maro Venn's papers—flawless, beautiful, dead—and fed them into the cold hearth, and struck the flint, and watched seven years of careful nothing go up in a very tidy flame. It should have felt like dying. Mostly it felt like taking off tight boots.

I left the painted stones on the counter, arranged neatly, price tags attached. Let them be evidence of harmlessness. I left the door unlocked, which in Veymar is practically a suicide note, but I wanted the room to look abandoned, not fled. Abandoned rooms get looted. Fled rooms get searched.

I went out the back, through the yard where I grew mint in a cracked pot for tea and for the look of domestic innocence, over the wall, into the alley where the city keeps its shadows and I have kept mine.

Behind me, distantly, the third bell rang again. Three notes, silence, three notes. A second sweep, already. They'd found more than blood.

Ahead of me, the harbor smelled of tar and low tide, and somewhere under all that gray water lay an upside-down cathedral, singing in a language only two people alive could speak—one of them dead, and one of them, as of this morning, officially no one at all.

I pulled my collar up, became nobody, and walked toward the water.

I'd made it four streets when I realized I was being followed.
