## 4. Ines

My brother was dead three days before anyone told me, and they told me wrong.

"The courier fell," said the harbormaster's clerk, not looking up from his ledger. "Slipped on the wet stones by Tanner's Row. Terrible thing."

I'd grown up with Tam. Tam didn't slip. Tam could walk a greased spar in a gale with a crate of contraband brandy balanced on one shoulder and a lie ready for any harbor official who asked. The wet stones of Tanner's Row had never once gotten the better of him in twenty-six years, and I refused to believe they'd managed it the week he finally saved enough coin to stop running other people's messages and start running his own.

So I did what you do when the city lies to you politely: I went to find out what it was lying about.

* * *

The dockworkers' network isn't a smuggler's ring, whatever the inquisitors' reports say. It's a family that happens to move cargo. We know which ships carry what, which inspectors can be paid in silver and which need to be paid in silence, and—most importantly—who goes where, when, and why. When someone dies in Veymar, the docks hear about it before the bells do. Usually.

Nobody had heard about Tam.

That was the first wrong thing. A courier dies on Tanner's Row, in broad evening, and no dockhand saw it? No fishwife selling the last of her catch? No child running errands who'd stopped to watch the body get carried off? Veymar's poor are vultures out of necessity; we descend on every misfortune because misfortune is often the only wealth that trickles down. But Tam had died alone, unseen, unremarked, like a man erased rather than a man killed.

The second wrong thing was the inquisitors. They'd been to Tanner's Row already—I found their chalk mark on the doorpost of the chandler's shop, the little spiral they leave when they've swept a place and found nothing worth burning. Except they don't sweep for fallen couriers. They sweep for magic. Which meant either Tam had fallen somewhere interesting, or someone wanted the district to think so.

I pulled in favors. Maren, who runs night cargo out of Pier Nine, remembered an inquisitor boat anchored off the Row two nights back, riding low and silent, lamps doused. Old Piet had seen a body wrapped in canvas going onto it—or thought he had, or maybe it was sailcloth, or maybe he'd had too much of the cheap gin he swears is medicinal. Everyone remembered something and nothing, the way people do when remembering might cost them.

But one thread held. A woman at the fish market—a brusque old eel-seller named Greta who has known me since I was small enough to steal from her buckets—mentioned she'd seen Tam, three nights ago, heading away from the harbor district. Heading uphill. Toward the Night Market.

Toward the charm sellers.

I stood there among the fish guts and the shouting and felt something go cold in my chest, colder than harbor water in winter. Because Tam hadn't been running messages for clients lately. For the past month he'd been running something else. He'd been careful about it—more careful than he'd ever been, and Tam was never careful, that was our whole argument, that was the shape of every fight we ever had—but I'd seen the change. He'd come home late with salt-stained boots when he hadn't been near the water. He'd started locking his room. He'd looked at me once across the supper table with an expression I couldn't read then and could read now, with terrible clarity: it was the look of a man deciding how much to tell the person he loved most in order to keep her safe, and choosing almost nothing.

He'd been investigating something. And whatever it was, it had taken him uphill to a charm seller, and then it had killed him, and then it had unwritten him from the city's memory as neatly as a clerk blotting a line.

I closed my stall early. First time in six years.

* * *

The Night Market doesn't open until the sun's fully gone, because its merchants sell things that prefer darkness: charms against nightmares, love-knots of dubious honesty, tinctures for complaints polite doctors won't name. Half of it is fraud. The other half is fraud with real teeth. In Veymar, where spells are punishable by drowning, the whole market exists in a state of permanent plausible deniability, and everyone from the buyers to the watchmen who take their cut pretends very hard that nothing here is anything but knickknacks.

I went in wearing my working clothes—salt-stiff jacket, boots with the toes kicked flat from ladder rungs—and carrying a knife I didn't plan to use and absolutely planned to be seen holding. Grief does that. It wants a weapon even when the enemy is a rumor.

I worked the stalls first. Charm sellers are gossips; fear makes them observant, and observation makes them talkative if you lean on the right rib. I described my brother. Younger than me by four years, quick grin, ink-stained fingers from copying routes, a habit of tapping his chest twice before he lied—which meant he tapped it constantly. Most of them shook their heads with the smooth practiced blankness of people whose survival depends on knowing nothing.

Then I reached a stall at the market's quiet edge, set back from the lantern-string, selling luck-knots and warding-threads and other small comforts. The man behind it was younger than I expected, with careful hands and a face built for being forgotten—until you looked at his eyes, which were doing all the work his face refused to do. Something in him went still when I showed him Tam's sketch. Not guilty-still. Recognizing-still.

"You've seen him." I put the sketch down on his table and kept my fingers on it. "Three nights ago. He came here."

"I see a lot of faces." His voice was level, but his hands had stopped moving. Charm sellers' hands never stop moving; stillness is a confession.

"Then see this one again. He was my brother. His name was Tam. He died on Tanner's Row, except he didn't die anywhere, because nobody saw it happen, and the inquisitors chalked the doorposts like he was a spell instead of a corpse. So somebody knows something, friend, and your hands just told me it's you."

Around us the market murmured on, oblivious or determinedly so. He glanced past my shoulder—the way people do when they're checking whether the dark itself is listening—and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"Not here."

"Then where?"

"Nowhere. That's safer." He started gathering his wares with those careful hands, closing up early, and I understood he was about to vanish into the city the way Tam had vanished, and I would lose the only thread I had. So I did the thing I'm best at, the thing the docks taught me and grief finished teaching me: I made myself impossible to ignore.

"My brother carried things for a living," I said, loud enough for the nearest stalls to hear, soft enough to pass as intimacy. "Secrets, mostly. He was good at it because he never opened them. Whatever he brought you, whatever he told you, I'm not asking you to confess anything. I'm asking you to help me bury him properly. Because right now he's a lie in a harbormaster's ledger, and our mother—" Our mother was dead too, years back, but the sentence worked anyway, and I hated myself a little for using her. "—would climb out of her grave if she knew he was lying in some unmarked hole."

His hands went still again. And in his eyes I saw it—not guilt exactly, but the specific exhaustion of a man who has been carrying something heavy alone for a long time and has just realized the weight is about to double.

"Tomorrow," he said. "After dusk. There's a tea house on Coil Lane, the one with the crooked sign. Come alone."

"I don't do alone."

"Then bring your knife and leave your friends." A ghost of something crossed his face—not quite humor, but adjacent to it. "You'll want the knife less than you think and the friends more than you'll admit."

* * *

I brought my knife. I also brought three of my people stationed within a whistle's reach, because I don't survive on trust, I survive on redundancy. If he noticed—and later I learned he had, because of course he had—he never mentioned it, which told me something important about him: he wasn't offended by suspicion. He expected it. He wore it like an old coat.

The tea house was empty at that hour except for a proprietor who took one look at us and developed a sudden fascination with the far wall. We sat in the back corner, under a lamp turned low, and he set something on the table between us, wrapped in oilcloth.

I knew before I unwrapped it. Some part of me had known since the fish market, since the cold feeling in my chest. You spend your life around cargo; you learn to feel the weight of a thing before you see it.

It was a map. Folded small, stained along one edge—dark stains, old now, browned the way blood browns—and sealed with wax that had been pressed with a mark I recognized the way you recognize your own front door in a dream.

A circle broken by a single vertical line, with three dots beneath like rain falling from it.

Our grandmother's mark. The seal she pressed into the wax of every letter she ever sent us, every parcel, every scrap of embroidery she hid in the lining of Tam's coat when we were children and she was telling us, in her crooked half-whisper, stories about the old days—about voices that could shape the world, about an order of sword-sworn speakers, about the time before the drowning law. We'd laughed. Children laugh. She'd pressed her thumb to the mark and said, *Remember this shape. Shapes are how the truth finds its way home.*

Tam had known. Tam had found this map—with her seal on it, with her mark, meaning it came from her side of the family, from whatever she'd been before she was just an old woman with stories—and he'd carried it through the city to a stranger, and he'd died reaching him, and the stranger had kept it safe.

"Why?" My voice came out rough. "Why you? Why not bring it to me?"

"He didn't have a choice." The charm seller—Ulyana, he'd finally given me a name, though I suspected it was one of several he owned—turned the map gently toward himself, not taking it back, just touching its edge like a man touching a sleeping animal. "He was bleeding out. He had seconds, not minutes. He needed someone close, someone who wouldn't ask questions until morning, someone who—" He stopped. Started again. "Someone who knew how to hide things. I think he chose me because I looked like a man who hides things well."

"And did you?"

"Yes."

"At least you're honest."

"It's cheaper than lying, in the long run." He met my eyes then, and I gave him credit for not flinching. "Your brother said something before he died. I've turned it over for three days and I can't make it mean anything except what it sounds like. He said: *the king is not the king.*"

There it was. The cold thing in my chest cracked another degree. Because Tam had written to me once, months ago, in one of his coded letters—we'd used grandmother's cipher since we were children, dots and dashes hidden in shipping manifests—and the letter had been mostly ordinary, routes and payments and gossip, except for one line near the end that I'd assumed was a joke:

*Found something big, sis. Bigger than cargo. Might need your boats soon. Also—do you ever wonder why the palace windows are always lit but nobody ever sees the king?*

I'd laughed when I read it. I'd written back, *Because kings are like lighthouse keepers, you great fool—they work at night.* He'd never answered.

Six weeks later he was dead.

"I'm coming with you," I said.

Ulyana blinked. "With me where?"

"Wherever that map goes. Whatever Tam was carrying it toward. You're going after it—I can see it in you, you've been circling it for three days like a man pacing a cell. Well, I'm telling you now: that map has my grandmother's seal on it, which means it's mine by blood, and my brother died delivering it, which means it's mine by debt. You don't get to carry both of those alone."

"It's not safe." He said it tiredly, the way a man says something he knows won't work. "Inquisitors swept my street yesterday. They're looking for something. If they connect me to your brother's death—"

"They already connected him to something worse than death. They erased him." I leaned forward. "Listen to me, charm seller. I run cargo the palace doesn't know exists, through channels the inquisitors can't chart, using people who'd walk into fire before they'd talk. You have a map and a dead man's words and, I'm guessing, no idea who to trust. I have a network, a boat, and nothing left to lose that I care about keeping." I tapped the seal, the broken circle, the falling rain. "And this says we're family, whether you like it or not. Grandmother didn't mark strangers."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lamp between us guttered, steadied. Somewhere outside, the evening bells rang the hour, and beneath them, faint as a heartbeat, I could have sworn the map hummed—just once, like a struck string remembering a note.

Ulyana heard it too. I watched his face change, watched three days of careful loneliness crack down the middle, and what was underneath looked a lot like relief, which surprised him more than it surprised me.

"There are things I should tell you," he said quietly. "About what I am. About what this is. And when I tell you, you may wish you'd stayed on your docks."

"I've drowned worse things than truths." I stood, tucked the map into my jacket, over my heart, where cargo that matters rides. "Coil Lane's got a back exit. Let's use it. I find I've stopped trusting front doors."

As we slipped out into the dark, he asked, almost shyly, "Why back doors?"

"Because my brother went out a front one," I said, "on a dry street, in daylight, and the city swallowed him whole. From now on, we go where the city isn't looking."

Behind us, in the empty tea house, the lamp burned on. And somewhere beneath the harbor, in the drowned dark where the old cathedral slept, something turned over in its sleep—and began, very softly, to listen.
