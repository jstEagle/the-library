## 5. The Drowned Cathedral

*Ulyana*

The map's first clue was a tide table.

That was the joke of it, I suppose. Seven years of hiding, seven years of pretending to be a man who sold knotted string for luck, and the great secret of my order turned out to be written in something as mundane as when the water went out. The map showed the harbor at low tide, a specific low tide—the kind that came twice a year, when the moon pulled hard enough to bare the old pilings beneath Fisherman's Walk. In the corner, in that same flowing swordspeech I'd spent two sleepless nights translating, were three words: *where we began.*

"Where we began," I muttered, crouched on the rotting planks with my sleeves rolled up and my boots off, because there is no dignity possible in wading through harbor muck and I'd decided not to pretend otherwise. "My order began in a lecture hall in Serevan. With a very dry reading of the Oath Codex. If there's a chapel down there, someone has a romantic memory."

"You talk to yourself a lot," Ines said behind me.

"I talk to myself constantly. It's the only conversation in Veymar where I'm certain the other party isn't an inquisitor."

She didn't laugh. She hadn't laughed once in the three days since she'd found me, which I understood—grief does that, folds you up like a letter no one will read—but I kept making the attempts anyway, the way you keep tapping a wall to find where it's hollow. Somewhere under all that dockworker flint was a woman whose brother had died on my floorboards, and I owed her more than silence. I owed her whatever was at the bottom of this harbor.

The tide was going out. The moon had been right. And there, exposed between two barnacled pilings like a wound in the world, was a door.

Not a hatch. A *door*. Oak, iron-banded, carved with the seven-pointed star of the Swordspeakers' Concord, and so thoroughly crusted with salt and weed that it looked less hidden than *forgotten*. Which, I realized with a slow cold feeling in my stomach, was exactly what it was supposed to be. Not secret. Forgotten. There's a difference. Secrets are kept by the living. This had been left to the dead.

"That's your star," Ines said. "The one on the map's seal."

"It's everyone's star, if they're unlucky enough to be born into the Concord." I touched the carving. My fingers remembered the gesture before my mind did—two taps, then a drag, the greeting-sign every student learned before they learned to hold a blade. Old habit. Useless habit. The door didn't care about manners; it cared about blood, or voice, or some other thing I'd spent seven years starving.

I spoke the word anyway. Quietly. Just a breath of it, the opening syllable of swordspeech, the first time I'd let the language past my teeth since the night I ran.

The door groaned open like something waking from a long illness.

"Well," Ines said. "That's either good or very bad."

"In my experience those are usually the same thing."

* * *

*Ines*

The stairs went down farther than any building in Veymar should have allowed. I counted them because counting kept me from thinking about Tammy—about Tomas, my brother, who had walked these same steps maybe, or maybe hadn't, who had pressed a folded paper into a stranger's hands and died for it. Counting also kept me from thinking about how the air changed as we descended. Salt gave way to stillness. Stillness gave way to something older, like walking into a room where someone had been crying for a hundred years and only just stopped.

The charm-seller—Ulyana, though he'd given me four other names before settling on that one, and I trusted none of them—held up a small glass globe he'd made glow with a word I pretended not to hear. Light spilled down the stairwell and showed me water.

Black, flat, motionless water, filling the space below us like a floor made of night.

"It's flooded," I said. Brilliant observation, Ines. Years of dock work, wasted.

"Flooded, yes." He crouched at the last step, holding the light low. "But look at the line on the walls. See it? The waterline's marked with old salt. That line hasn't moved in decades. The water isn't rising. It's being held."

"Held by what?"

"By what's down here, I'd guess. Or by what was built to keep it polite." He rolled his trousers higher. "It'll be waist-deep at most, if the map's proportions are honest. Are you a swimmer?"

"I'm a dockworker. I can swim, fight, and swear in four languages. Two of them might be useful down here."

Something crossed his face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile would go if it could remember the way. "Then after you, mistress Delacroix."

"Don't call me that. You don't get my family name just because my brother bled on your floor."

He took the rebuke without flinching, which annoyed me more than an argument would have. We waded in.

The cold hit like a slap. Underfoot, the stone was smooth and worn in channels, the way stone gets when thousands of feet have walked the same path for generations. My light—I'd brought a lantern, real fire, because I trusted flame more than I trusted him—caught on things floating near the surface: a wooden practice sword, its edge blunted to velvet. A belt buckle with the seven-pointed star. A child's shoe.

I stopped looking at the floating things after the shoe.

The chamber opened around us, and even half-drowned it was beautiful, which made it worse. Pillars rose out of the black water in two long rows, each carved with figures mid-motion—sword forms, I realized, frozen in stone. The ceiling arched high overhead, painted with stars that had survived the damp better than anything else in Veymar survived anything. At the far end stood an altar, and above the altar, mounted on the wall where a god should have been, hung a single sword.

It was rusted. It was ordinary. It was the most frightening thing I'd ever seen, because I knew—I don't know how I knew, but I knew—that my grandmother had held it.

"Tomas came here," I said. My voice bounced strange off the water. "Didn't he. This is why he had the map. This is what he found."

"I think," Ulyana said slowly, "that we're about to find out exactly what he found."

Because along the near wall, above the waterline, someone had nailed papers. Dozens of them. They were yellowed and warped but legible, and the nearest one bore a seal I recognized from every tax document and market license in the city: the crown of Veymar.

I swam-walked closer. Ulyana raised his little globe of light, and we read together.

*By decree of His Majesty, King Aldric of Veymar, in the seventh year of his reign: the order known as the Swordspeakers' Concord is dissolved. Its members are declared enemies of the crown. Its teachings are declared heresy against the peace. Let none shelter them, let none mourn them, and let their names be struck from every record, that their memory may drown with their works.*

Dated seven years ago. Signed with the king's own hand.

Seven years. Seven years ago, almost to the season, my grandmother had stopped writing letters. Seven years ago, the stories said, the last swordspeaker had died quietly somewhere far away, and good riddance, because magic was dangerous and the king protected us from it. Seven years ago, Ulyana Delacroix—or whoever he'd been before he became a charm-seller with four false names—had run.

I turned to look at him. He was standing very still in the waist-deep water, light trembling in his hand, reading the decree over and over as if the words might rearrange themselves into something kinder.

"They didn't die out," I said. "Everyone says the order just... faded. Died natural. But this says—"

"This says the king murdered them." His voice was flat. Too flat, the way water goes flat right before something comes up through it. "Declared heresy. Struck from record. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought we lost. I thought we failed and scattered and the world simply moved on. I didn't know there was a decree. I didn't know it was *law*. If it's law, then it wasn't panic, it wasn't fear—it was policy. Someone sat at a desk and wrote out the death of everyone I loved in formal language, and then had it posted, and then had it *filed*."

"Your master," I said. "The one you won't tell me about."

"She stayed behind." He said it like pulling a tooth. "She told us to run. She stayed to hold something closed. I always told myself she chose it. Reading this—" His hand shook, just slightly, and the light shivered across the drowned pillars. "Reading this, I think she wasn't given a choice at all. None of us were. The decree went out and the inquisitors went out and the children—" He cut himself off.

"The children what?"

He didn't answer. Instead he waded toward the altar, toward the rusted sword, and I followed because following was the only thing I had left of my brother now.

Behind the altar, half-hidden by shadow and salt-stain, was a list. Names, hundreds of them, scratched into the stone in a hurried hand—different hands, I saw as I got closer, dozens of different hands, added over years. Some names had lines drawn through them. Most didn't.

At the bottom, in handwriting I'd know anywhere because it used to appear on the corners of my letters, was *Tomas Delacroix*. No line through it. Fresh enough that the scratches still held dust.

"He found this place," I whispered. "He found the list, and he added himself to it, and then he went to find the last name on it that didn't have a line through it yet."

Ulyana followed my finger. Up the column, near the top, worn nearly smooth by water and time, was a name I watched him read with his whole body. His lips shaped it silently. Then he said it aloud, barely: "Sera Vane."

"Who's Sera Vane?"

"My master." He stared at the name a long moment. "Ines. Look at the date beside it."

Beside the name, scratched small, was a date—and a note, in the same hurried hand as the rest: *still holds.*

"Still holds what?" I asked.

And from somewhere beneath the black water, beneath the stone floor, deep under the harbor where nothing should have been able to breathe—something answered. Not in words. In a single, low, patient pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a wall, like something that had been waiting seven years for exactly this sound: footsteps in its chapel, and voices reading its decree, and a swordspeaker's voice saying names aloud.

The water around our waists trembled. Every pillar figure seemed, for one horrible moment, to turn its carved face toward us.

Ulyana snuffed his light instantly. In the dark, his hand found my arm, gripping hard.

"We need to leave," he breathed. "*Now.* Whatever she sealed—it knows we're here. And I think it just sent word to someone."

Above us, faint through fathoms of stone and harbor water, came a sound I'd grown up knowing and dreading: the bells of the palace district, ringing not the hour, but the alarm.

The inquisitors were coming.
