## 6. Brother Cassian

I have heard the confession of the King of Veymar every Thursday for eleven years, and I have never once understood a word of it.

Let me be precise, because precision is the last honest thing I own. I am not deaf. I am not stupid, whatever the canon-archivists whispered when I was passed over for the Cathedral seat a third time. I speak six languages, three of them dead, one of them so obscure that when I recited its only surviving prayer at my ordination, old Bishop Halloway wept and said I had the ear of a scholar and the soul of a nuisance. I know what human language sounds like. I know what tongues sound like when a man is merely babbling, or drunk, or mad, or speaking in the ecstatic gibberish that certain fervent parishioners mistake for the divine.

The king's confessions are none of these.

They sound like water being spoken. Like a voice dragged through a long wet corridor. There are words in it, or something shaped like words—syllables that repeat, that build, that pause as though waiting for an answer—but they do not belong to any grammar God saw fit to give mankind. And the king speaks them fluently. Fluently, and with a cadence I have come to recognize across eleven years of Thursdays: the cadence of a man reporting to a superior.

That is the part I have not told anyone. Not the language. I could have explained the language away, if I were a fool, and I have spent eleven years cultivating just enough foolishness to survive in this palace. No. The part I have not told anyone is that the king does not confess *to me*. He kneels at the prie-dieu with his back to the screen, and he speaks that drowned, gurgling speech into the dark, and I sit on my side of the screen and give absolution in Veymari, and I have slowly, over more than a decade, come to understand that I am not the priest in that room.

I am the furniture.

* * *

Tonight the confession ran long.

It usually does, the week before the Binding Festival. The palace fills then with the low hum of preparation—carpenters building the festival stages, cooks arguing over the ceremonial fast, inquisitors polishing their white masks until the corridors smell of vinegar and something else, something underneath, like a cellar that has never once been dry. The whole city tightens like a fist around a coin it is afraid to look at.

The king spoke for two hours. I timed it against the office bells. At one point he said a phrase I have heard every single year on the eve of the festival—three syllables, rising, then a fourth that falls like something dropped down a well—and I realized, sitting there in the dark of the confessional with my hands folded like a good priest, that I had begun to anticipate it. To *wait* for it. The way you wait for a familiar footstep in a house you thought was empty.

*It is nearly time. It is nearly time. It is nearly time, and I am hungry.*

I don't know how I know that's what it means. That is the worst part. Eleven years of listening and the meaning has soaked into me like water into stone, without my consent, without any door I could point to. I simply know, the way you know the mood of a room when you enter it.

When he finished, I gave the absolution. *Ego te absolvo*, in the old tongue, because the old tongue at least has the decency to mean what it says. And then the king did something he has never done in eleven years.

He paused. And he laughed.

It was not the king's laugh. I want to be clear about that, because I have heard the king laugh—at festival feasts, at the small dry jokes of his chancellors, at the antics of the acolytes' choir. The king laughs the way a tired man laughs, politely, from somewhere behind his eyes. This laugh came from somewhere else. It came from lower down, from the region of the ribs, and it was wet, and it was pleased, and it was directed—not at me, I think, but at the screen between us. At the shape of a man sitting behind it.

Then he said, in perfect, unaccented, beautiful Veymari:

"You count the bells, Brother Cassian."

I did not move. I am not certain I breathed.

"Eleven years," the voice said, and it was the king's mouth, the king's voice, and it was not the king at all. "You count them every week. Two hours, forty minutes, and then the little bell, and then you give your little absolution, and then you go and you eat your little supper and you write in your little journal, and you tell yourself that doubt is a form of prayer."

The candle behind the screen guttered, though there was no draft.

"Doubt *is* a form of prayer," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "The first form. Before praise. Before petition. Before anything, a man must ask whether anyone is listening."

Something shifted behind the screen. I had the impression—no more than an impression, a pressure at the edge of sight, the way you can feel a large animal standing very still in a dark field—of something turning to look at me. Truly look.

"You are the only one who listens," it said. "Do you know how rare that is? They kneel and they hear what they expect. You hear what is there." A pause, long enough for me to hear my own heart misbehaving. "I could use a listener, Brother Cassian. The king's confessor sees so much. Hears so much. And no one ever thinks to wonder about the furniture."

Then the door opened and the king walked out—walked *past* me, and it was the king again, tired and gray and human, and he nodded to me with the small courtesy he always showed and said, "Thank you, Brother," and left.

I sat in the confessional until the candle burned down to a puddle, and then I sat a while longer in the dark, because I had discovered that my legs were not currently accepting instructions.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I ask you to remember it, because there may come a day—there has already come one day—when it is weighed against everything else.

I went back to my cell, and I took out my journal, and I did not write in it. I sat with the pen uncapped for the better part of an hour. Eleven years of entries in that book. Careful, coded, deniable entries: *The king's confession was lengthy. His Excellency seemed fatigued. The festival preparations proceed.* A decade of a man describing a locked room without ever once mentioning the door.

Because here is the arithmetic of the palace, and I learned it early. Inquisitors do not investigate sin. Sin is their *harvest*. They investigate questions. A priest who notices nothing is safe. A priest who notices and says nothing is safer still. A priest who notices and *writes it down* is a man with a document, and documents outlive their courage, and everyone in this building knows it, and so the man with the document has a very short life expectancy and a very long funeral.

I had spent eleven years being safe. And tonight something had looked at me through a screen and told me, in the king's own mouth, that it knew I was not safe—that I was *useful*—and the horror of it was not the threat. The horror was the courtesy. It had not needed to threaten me. It had offered me a promotion.

The furniture, invited to sit at the table.

I capped the pen. I took my journal to the brazier in the cloister walk, and I fed it to the fire page by page, and I stood there in the cold watching eleven years of cowardice curl and go up as ash, and I will not pretend it felt like liberation. It felt like amputation. It felt like the moment on the surgeon's table when you realize the limb was already lost and the cutting is only the acknowledgment.

But here is what the fire left me, and what no inquisitor can confiscate: a question with a shape to it.

The thing in the king speaks a language. Languages have histories. Languages have *speakers*—plural, or they had them once. No tongue is spoken by one mouth alone; even the loneliest hermit learned his words from someone. Which meant that somewhere in the past of this city, that drowned wet speech had lived in other throats. And a language does not simply die. It is *killed*. And Veymar kills things and drowns them and then passes laws against mentioning the drowning.

I am, as established, a nuisance with a scholar's ear. So I did what scholars do.

I went to the archives.

* * *

The palace archive is a cathedral's forgotten attic, and I say that with love, because I have spent thirty years of evenings in forgotten attics. The canon-archivist, Brother Odo, is ninety-one years old, half-blind, and devoted to me in the way of old men who have decided you are the last person who will listen to their stories. I brought him sweet wine and flattery, which between friends we may call the two sacraments of the archive.

"Odo," I said, "the rite of the Binding. The original. Not the festival form—the *source*. Where did the words come from?"

He sipped his wine and looked at me with his clouded eyes, and I had the sudden, uncomfortable certainty that behind the cataracts was a man who had been waiting eleven years for me to ask exactly this.

"The words came from the swordspeakers," he said.

The word landed in my chest like a stone dropped down the same well as that fourth syllable.

"Tell me."

"You know the story they teach. That the swordspeakers were sorcerers, heretics, that the crown purged them for the city's safety seven years back." He waved a spotted hand. "The story is newer than my boots, boy. I was in this archive before your mother was christened. The swordspeakers were not sorcerers. They were *priests*—or the next thing to it. An order of speakers whose words did what they said. You said *cut* and the air cut. You said *bind* and—" he paused, and his wine trembled very slightly in the cup— "and it stayed bound."

"And the rite of the Binding Festival?"

"The old rite is in swordspeech. Every word of it. The Church has been singing a translation for a hundred years, a pretty little liturgy about devotion and continuity, and underneath it, every festival eve, the true words are spoken in the king's chamber. They have to be. A translation won't hold." He set down the cup. "The order swore the binding. Which means, boy, that only the order can unmake it. That is how oaths of that age work. The lock knows its own key."

"Then the purge—"

"Was not a purge of sorcerers." Odo's voice had gone very quiet, the way men go quiet in sickrooms. "It was a *key control*. You do not leave keys scattered about when there is a lock that must never open. They hunted the swordspeakers down to the last one. Drowned them, the way they drown everything now." He looked at me, and the clouded eyes were not clouded at all. "Everyone believes they got them all."

"But you don't."

"I believe," Odo said, "that in seven years of hunting, in a city of tunnels and harbors and night markets and people who owe each other favors, one man can hide. One man with a reason to hide, and a craft for small quiet magics—because that is what the swordspeakers were *for*, boy, before the crown bent them to the great oath. Small magics. Luck-knots. Charms. The little words that keep ordinary people alive." He refilled my cup with a hand that had stopped trembling. "If I were such a man, and I wished never to be found, I would be exactly where no one looks. Somewhere poor. Somewhere crowded. Somewhere a man can be one face among ten thousand."

The night market, I thought. The low quarter, where the inquisitors patrol in threes because even they do not like the dark down there.

"Odo," I said. "Why are you telling me this? Eleven years I have come to you with careful questions and you have given me careful answers. Tonight you hand me a heresy with both hands."

The old man was silent a long moment. When he spoke, he did not look at me.

"Because three nights ago, a courier came to the palace gate with a delivery for the chancellery. A boy, dock-born, quick, with a courier's badge and a grandmother's seal on his papers. The gate log says he was turned away for a forged seal." His voice dropped further. "The gate log is wrong. I read the original entry before it was amended. The seal was *real*, boy. Old. Older than the law against magic. And the boy was not turned away—he was taken. Through the postern, down toward the water, by four inquisitors. And the amended log says he was never there."

The candle flame between us stood perfectly still, the way it had stood in the confessional, and I understood that we were both, in our different ways, standing at the same door.

"The king's confessor," Odo said, "hears confessions. That is his office. But a confessor who has heard the thing in the king's chamber speak the king's own mouth—that man is no longer a priest of this palace. He is a priest of whatever comes next. And whatever comes next, boy, will need someone who knows where the bodies are written down." He finally looked at me, and there was no wine-warmth in it at all, only an old man's terrible clarity. "Find the swordspeaker. Before the festival. Because if the binding is renewed for another century, there will be no *next*. Only more Thursdays. More two hours and forty minutes. And you, counting bells, until they count you."

I left the archive with the sweet wine untouched in my cup and one name in my memory, given to me at the door in Odo's cracked whisper—the name of a fence in the low quarter who owed the old man a debt and could, for a price, put a curious priest in the path of a certain charm-seller. A man with no recorded name. A man the market called, when it called him anything at all, *the quiet one*.

I walked back across the palace grounds under a sky the color of wet slate, past the festival scaffolds, past the inquisitors' barracks where the vinegar smell hung thickest, and I said my evening office aloud as I walked, every word, in the old tongue, because the old tongue at least means what it says.

Tomorrow I go down into the low quarter in a borrowed coat, with no collar and no title, to find a dead order's last survivor and tell him what I have spent eleven years being too afraid to say even to my journal:

The king is not the king. Something else is. And I believe—I *need* to believe—that the words to end it are still alive in someone's throat.

I only pray, if prayer still means anything, that I am not the one who leads the inquisitors to him by asking my questions too loudly.

The bells rang the hour. I counted them, out of habit.

Then, for the first time in eleven years, I stopped.
