## 7. The Child Who Hears It

The thing about grief, I've learned, is that it doesn't make you careful. It makes you *hungry*. Every dockhand in Veymar knows you don't work a cargo with an empty belly, and every smuggler knows you don't run a job with an empty heart. You do stupid things. You take shortcuts through alleys where the lamps are broken and the shadows have opinions.

So maybe that's why I was in Saltrow at dusk, three days after my brother died on a stranger's floorboards, walking a route I had no business walking, when I heard the singing.

Not real singing. Not the kind that comes out of throats. This was thinner than that, like a wire pulled taut inside my skull, humming a tune I almost knew. I stopped under the leaning eave of a chandler's shop and pressed two fingers to my temple, because for one ugly moment I thought I was finally losing my mind—grief does that too, they say—and then I saw the child.

She was sitting cross-legged on an overturned crate outside the chandler's, small even by street-child standards, all elbows and knees wrapped in a coat three sizes too big with salt-stiffened cuffs. Her eyes were closed. Her lips moved.

And hanging from her grubby fist by its cord was a scrap of parchment folded into a diamond—the same fold, the exact same fold, as the map that had come off my brother's body. The map currently hidden in a false bottom of Ulyana's charm chest, wrapped in oilcloth like something that might bite.

I crossed the alley before I'd decided to move. "Where did you get that?"

The child's eyes snapped open. Grey eyes, sharp as fishhooks. She looked me up and down with the frank appraisal of someone who'd been sizing up adults since before she could walk, and then she did the smartest thing a street kid can do: she stood up slow, kept the map-copy against her chest, and said nothing at all.

"I said—" I caught myself, lowered my voice. The last thing either of us needed was attention. "That's a copy. Of a document that belongs to me."

"It's not a copy." Her voice was hoarse, a whisper's voice, the voice of a kid who'd learned that talking gets you noticed. "It's a *listening*. It hears what the big one says and writes it down smaller. Mostly wrong. But it tries."

I stared at her. "It what?"

"The big one sings." She tapped her temple with one finger, the way you'd tap a barrel to hear if it was full. "In here. All night, every night. It doesn't stop. So I wrote some of it down so it would be quieter." A pause, and then, with the defensive edge of a child caught doing something shameful: "It helps. A little. Sometimes."

My mouth had gone dry. "You can hear it? The actual map? In your head?"

"You can't?" And there it was—not mockery, but genuine surprise, the way you'd look at someone who admitted they couldn't smell the harbor at low tide.

I sat down on the crate she'd vacated, mostly because my legs had opinions of their own. Three days ago a dying man had pressed a singing map into a charm-seller's hands and told him the king wasn't the king. Two days ago I'd watched that charm-seller hold the map up to lamplight and go grey as cod, listening to words in a language that made his hands shake. And now here was a child who heard it too, sitting in plain sight in Saltrow like bait on a hook.

"Listen to me," I said, and crouched so we were eye to eye. "What's your name?"

"Pip."

"Real name."

"That is my real name. I named myself." A shrug that swallowed half the oversized coat. "Names are like coats. You pick ones that fit."

Gods help me, I liked her immediately, which should have been my first warning.

"Pip. Do you know what happens to children in this city who can do things other people can't?"

She gave me a look of magnificent, weary contempt. "They get took. Everyone knows that. The gray-cloaks take 'em." She said it the way you'd say *it rains in spring*—a fact of weather, not a tragedy. Then her chin lifted, defiant. "But I'm careful. I sleep different places. I never sing along."

"Sing along," I repeated faintly.

"When it gets loud. Some nights it gets loud, like it wants me to answer back." Her fingers tightened on the paper. "I never do. Mostly."

"*Mostly?*"

Her eyes slid away from mine, toward the harbor, toward the black water slapping at the pilings below Saltrow's edge. "There's something down there," she said quietly. "Under the water. It sings too. Lower. Like a whale, if a whale was sad and angry at the same time. And sometimes when the map sings loud, the whale-song answers, and it's hard not to answer *back*, because—" her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in all that street-hardened armor, "—because it sounds lonely."

I think that was the moment I stopped being able to pretend this was somebody else's problem.

Because I knew about lonely things under water. My brother had spent six months writing letters about disappearances—dock kids, beggar kids, children nobody would miss—and getting paler and more frantic with each page until he started carrying that map and stopped coming home. He'd died reaching for a stranger because he believed, with everything left in him, that the stranger could do something.

And here was exactly the kind of child Tam had written about, sitting on a crate, holding a piece of the song in her fist, answering—or nearly answering—a voice from under the harbor.

"Right," I said, standing. Decision made somewhere below conscious thought, in the place where grief lives. "You're coming with me."

Pip went rigid. Street kids know what "coming with me" usually means, and none of it is good. "I don't go nowhere with strangers."

"Good instinct. Keep it." I held out my hand anyway. "But the gray-cloaks sweep Saltrow on festival eves, and the Binding Festival is soon, and you're sitting out here holding contraband in your fist. So: option one, you come with me tonight. Option two, you stay here and find out how careful you really are."

She weighed it with the gravity of a harbormaster assessing a storm. Then she spat in her palm—an old dock gesture, sealing terms—and slapped it into mine.

"Fair warning," she said. "I snore."

---

Ulyana objected. Of course he objected. Objecting is that man's native language; he probably dreamed in footnotes and caveats.

We were back at the shop—my brother's blood still ghost-staining the floorboards no matter how many times he scrubbed them, though neither of us mentioned it—and I'd marched Pip in past him like contraband myself. He'd taken one look at the child, one look at me, and started in with the objections before the door was even shut.

"No," he said. "Absolutely not. Do you understand what you've done? You've walked through half the city with a child who can hear that—that *thing*—and brought her straight to the one building in Veymar where an inquisitor would only need to look twice—"

"She already knows about the map. She hears it. Constantly. Has done for months, apparently, without anyone noticing except whatever's under the harbor."

"Which is *worse*—"

"And the gray-cloaks are taking kids like her, Ulyana. Kids nobody will miss. Well, guess what, she's got nobody to miss her now except me, and I'm telling you, I am *not* easy to miss."

He pinched the bridge of his nose. Seven years of hiding himself away, and I'd known the man barely a week, but I was starting to build a catalog of his tells. The nose-pinch meant the objection was running out of road. "Ines. Think. If the Veil can sense her—if it's been calling to her—then bringing her here isn't shelter. It's stacking kindling next to the hearth."

"Then we teach her not to catch." I turned to Pip, who was watching our argument with the detached interest of someone watching gulls fight over a fish head. "Show him."

"Show him what?"

"What you showed me. What you wrote down."

Pip hesitated. Looked at Ulyana—really looked, the way she'd looked at me in Saltrow, sizing up whether he was safe or merely present. Whatever she saw, it made her unfold the little diamond of parchment and smooth it flat on the counter between us.

It was covered in cramped, spidery marks. Not letters. Not any alphabet I knew. Lines and hooks and slashes, arranged in patterns that hurt to follow, like reading caused a mild headache.

Ulyana went very still. That particular stillness—I'd seen it once before, the night I'd found him holding my brother's map up to the light. The stillness of a man looking at his own grave.

"Where did you learn these characters?" His voice had gone quiet in a way that was worse than shouting.

"Nowhere." Pip shrugged. "They came out of my hand. When I listen hard, my hand wants to write what it hears. Is that wrong?"

"It's—" He stopped. Started again. "No. It's not wrong. It's old." He touched one of the marks with one fingertip, feather-light, the way you'd touch a scar. "This word here. It means *listen-beneath*. And this one—" his finger moved, trembling slightly, "—this is a warning. *Do not answer the low voice.*"

Something in my chest went cold. "Tam must have known," I said. "About the children hearing it. That's why he was investigating the disappearances. That's why he—"

"Yes." Ulyana wasn't listening to me anymore. He was staring at Pip's transcription like it was a letter from the dead. "This dialect—it's swordspeech. The old form. The ceremonial register. Even among my order, only masters used this form, and only for—" He cut himself off, jaw tightening.

"For what?"

"For oaths." He looked up at Pip, and I watched something complicated happen behind his eyes—fear and wonder and a grief so old it had fossilized. "Child. Can you speak it? Not write it. Speak."

Pip glanced at me. I nodded, not knowing why, just knowing she needed permission from somebody.

She took a breath. And then she opened her mouth and spoke four syllables in a language I'd never heard, a language with edges on it, a language that sounded less like sound and more like a blade being drawn slowly from a sheath—

—and every lamp flame in the shop leaned toward her at once.

Not flickered. *Leaned.* Bent toward the child like iron filings to a lodestone, and the air pressure changed, and the map in the chest in the corner began to hum audibly through the wood, and Ulyana made a sound I'd never heard a grown man make—a small, wounded noise, like a man hearing his mother's voice after twenty years.

"Stop," he whispered. "Stop, please—"

Pip shut her mouth. The flames righted themselves. The humming faded. Silence rushed back in like water filling a footprint.

Ulyana braced both hands on the counter and hung his head. For a long moment he didn't say anything at all. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough.

"Do you know what you just did?"

"No," Pip said honestly.

"That was a summons-phrase. First-year students learned it before anything else. It's how a swordspeaker calls a blade to hand." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "Seven years. Seven years I've kept my mouth shut and my head down, and a child walks in off the street and speaks it *perfectly*. Better than perfect. Untrained. Natural." He looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once. "Do you understand now? She's not just hearing the map. The map is *teaching* her. Or something through the map. And whatever's under the harbor has been listening to every lesson."

"Then we keep her close and teach her faster," I said. "Or we teach her to be quiet. Either way, we don't throw her back out onto the street to be taken by the first gray-cloak who notices her humming."

"Ines—"

"She sleeps in the loft above the shop. Tonight. We argue about it tomorrow." I held his gaze. "You hid for seven years, Ulyana. How did that work out for the order? For your master? Hiding didn't save anybody. Maybe it's time somebody in this city tried the opposite."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Pip, who was watching him with those fishhook-grey eyes, unblinking, waiting—no, *hoping*, in the guarded way of children who've learned hope is expensive.

"Fine," he said at last, and the word cost him visibly. "The loft. One night. We'll see."

Pip's face did something extraordinary—it stayed completely neutral while somehow radiating triumph. "I snore," she reminded him.

"So I've heard."

---

That night I couldn't sleep. I climbed to the loft around midnight, ostensibly to check on her, actually because the silence of my own room had gotten unbearable since Tam died—silence used to mean safety, and now it just meant absence.

Pip was awake. Of course she was. Sitting up in the nest of blankets, knees drawn up, the stolen scrap of parchment on her lap.

"It's louder tonight," she whispered when she saw me.

"The map?"

"The whale." She hugged her knees tighter. "It knows I'm here. Near the map. It's... asking questions. In the singing. It wants to know who taught me the words." Her chin trembled, just once, and she mastered it. "I didn't tell it anything. I sang the quiet-song instead. The one that goes *do not answer, do not answer.*"

"You did well." I sat down beside her. Up close, in the lamplight, she looked younger. Smaller. The coat swallowed her whole. "Pip. Tomorrow, we start figuring out what that map actually is. Properly. With Ulyana. And we figure out what it wants, and what the thing under the harbor wants, and then we decide what we're going to do about it. Together. That's the deal. Nobody carries this alone."

She considered that with the seriousness of a magistrate. Then: "Is the charm-man going to teach me? Real teaching? Not just quiet-songs?"

"I suspect he'll complain about it extensively and then teach you better than anyone alive."

A ghost of a grin. "Like a gull. All noise, then steals your bread."

Despite everything—despite Tam, despite the gray-cloaks, despite the sad angry whale under the water—I laughed. First time in days. It came out rusty and surprised us both.

"Go to sleep, Pip."

She lay down. Closed her eyes. Then, in the dark, so quietly I almost missed it:

"Ines. Thank you for finding me."

"I didn't find you," I said. "You were singing. I just followed it home."

And from beneath the harbor, faint and far and patient as tide, something sang back.
