## 8. Seven Years Ago

There is a particular quality to the light on the morning your world ends. I've thought about this more than any man should think about anything, and my conclusion—arrived at after seven years of insomnia and one very expensive habit of counting ceiling cracks—is that it's always ordinary. The sun does not go red. The birds do not fall silent in mourning. On the morning Master Serafine woke us with a bell instead of a knock, the light through the dormitory shutters was gold and gentle and completely indifferent, the kind of light that makes you want to stay in bed.

I stayed in bed for approximately four seconds. Then the bell rang again, and it was not the morning bell. It was the *war* bell—the one cast from a sword that had killed someone important, which our order considered either deeply reverent or deeply funny depending on who you asked. Serafine only rang it when she wanted us awake in our bones.

I was twenty-three. I had been a swordspeaker for eleven years, which meant I could put words into steel and have them *stay there*, humming, until they were needed. My specialty was wards. Not glamorous. Not the dueling cantos or the cutting verses that made the older students insufferable at festivals. Wards. Sentences you spoke into a doorway so that nothing with ill intent could pass. I was good at them because I was, even then, a coward with excellent diction.

The hall smelled of tallow and wet stone. Twenty-two students stood in various states of undress and alarm, and Serafine stood at the head of the room holding her sword—*Vigil*, plain as a farm tool, older than the kingdom—with the point resting on the flagstones like she was too tired to lift it.

I had never seen my master tired before. It frightened me more than the bell.

"They're coming," she said. No preamble. Serafine believed preambles were for people who hadn't finished thinking. "The king's men. Inquisitors, soldiers, and worse. They'll be here by midday."

"Worse than soldiers?" That was Brecht, who was nineteen and had never once considered that a question could be unwelcome. "What's worse than—"

"The thing that sent them." She looked at him until he sat down. "Sit. All of you. What I say next, you will carry for the rest of your lives, however long I can make those."

She told us about the oath.

Not all of it. Even now, with everything I know, I couldn't tell you all of it—I suspect she didn't know all of it herself. But she told us enough: that two hundred years ago, the swordspeakers had ended a war no army could end, and the price had been a king. Our king's grandfather's grandfather, bound to something old and hungry that lived beneath the palace, something we had named the Veil because naming things is what we do and because none of us wanted to look at it long enough to name it properly. Bound so that it would sleep. Bound with a sentence spoken in the oldest dialect of swordspeech, the war-tongue, the one only masters learned.

And bound *renewable*. Every hundred years, on the Binding Festival, a swordspeaker would kneel in the drowned cathedral beneath the palace and speak the renewal, feeding the Veil a century's worth of gathered magic to keep it dreaming. That was the original sin of my order. We didn't just bind the monster. We built it a feeding schedule.

"It has been waking," Serafine said. "Slowly. For years. The king stopped coming to his own council meetings. The confessions his priest hears are in no tongue of men. And three months ago, the crown issued a decree I have read four times hoping I misread it." Her voice did something I had never heard it do. It shook. "The swordspeakers are to be purged. All of us. Because we are the only ones who can unmake what they've decided to keep."

"Why keep it?" I asked. I remember asking. I remember my voice sounding very small in that stone room. "If it's waking anyway, why not let us unbind it?"

"Because unbinding requires a swordspeaker," she said, "and the Veil knows it. It has been whispering to the throne for years, love. Telling them we're the disease. Telling them that if every voice that could speak the unbinding is dead, the binding holds forever." She smiled, and it was the worst thing I saw that day, and I saw a great many terrible things that day. "It is going to murder an entire order of knights to protect its own leash. And the kingdom will thank it for it."

* * *

What happened next took less time than it takes to tell, which is the cruelty of catastrophes. They are always over before the telling begins.

Serafine split us. That was the plan—not a battle plan, never a battle plan; she looked at twenty-two students and one aging master against the might of a corrupted crown and did not insult us by pretending. She gave us routes. She gave us false names. She gave each of us a single instruction, spoken in the war-tongue so that no informer among us—if there was one, and gods forgive me, I still wonder—could repeat it to save their skin:

"*Scatter. Survive. Speak nothing.*"

The great oath, the binding itself, was sealed under the cathedral. She would go down and hold it, reinforce it, buy the world another hundred years if she could. That was her duty. Ours was the opposite: to become nothing. To bury our blades, silence our voices, and live small lives in small places until the world forgot there had ever been such a thing as a swordspeaker.

"Silence is the last duty," she told us. She said it to all of us, but she said it looking at me, because I was her best student at wards and her worst student at courage, and she knew exactly which of those facts mattered now. "You will want to fight. You will want to be seen. You will want, above all things, to matter. Don't. A dead hero saves no one. A living coward might. Be cowards, my loves. Be magnificent cowards. Live."

Then she kissed Brecht's forehead, and Tam's, and mine, and walked out the north door toward the harbor and the water beneath the palace, and that was the last time I ever saw her alive.

I know how that sounds. I know what you're waiting for me to say next, the part where I turned back, where I drew my blade and spoke a ward over her retreat, where I stood on the wall and sang the cutting verses while the inquisitors broke like waves. That's the story I told myself for years, in the dark, the version where I'm brave for at least a paragraph.

Here is what actually happened.

I ran.

* * *

Not immediately. Let me be fair to my younger self, since no one else has ever been. There was a moment—a real one—where I stood in the courtyard with Vigil's sister-blade in my hand, a sword called *Patience* that I had spoken eleven years of words into, and I watched the smoke rise from the direction of the market road, and I felt the oldest verse in my chest, the one they teach you first: *I am the word and the edge and the wall between.*

I felt it. And then I heard hooves.

Four riders came around the granary. Not inquisitors yet—outriders, scouts, boys younger than me with royal colors and nervous hands. They saw me standing in the courtyard of a burned-out chapter house with a naked sword, and one of them shouted, and I want to tell you I answered with steel and song.

I answered with a ward. A good one—the best I've ever cast, before or since, terror being a wonderful teacher. I spoke the sentence into the air between us and the courtyard gate sealed itself with a sound like a struck bell, and the horses screamed and shied, and I had perhaps thirty seconds.

Thirty seconds in which I could have gone back inside. Could have taken the tunnels Serafine had mapped for us, down toward the harbor, toward her, toward the cathedral where my master intended to spend her life buying the world time. Thirty seconds in which I stood there with my heart hammering and did the arithmetic that cowards do, quickly and without mercy: one half-trained warder against an army, odds of changing anything, roughly zero.

I ran out the postern gate. I ran through the bean fields. I ran until my lungs tore, and then I kept running, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant hearing her voice saying *be a coward, my love*, and I could not bear for her to be right.

I buried Patience in a farmer's field outside Veymar, wrapped in oilcloth, three feet down beneath a fence post. I remember patting the dirt flat and apologizing to it. Out loud. To a sword. That's the kind of dignity I brought to the last duty of my order.

Then I walked into the city that would kill me if it knew my name, and I began to erase myself.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding: it works. That's the horror of it. It works *beautifully*.

Within a year I was Ullo, a charm-seller of no particular talent, a thin man with a limp he exaggerated for sympathy and a stall in the night market that sold luck-knots and fever-charms that were, mostly, just knots and fevers' worth of wishful thinking. Within three years I had a shop. Within five I had regulars who trusted me, neighbors who waved, a routine so thick with small repetitions that it functioned exactly like the wards I used to cast: nothing with ill intent could pass. Nothing passed at all.

I told myself I was keeping the faith. Silence is the last duty. I wasn't fighting, but I wasn't speaking, and speaking was the crime, wasn't it? Wasn't that what she'd asked?

But here is the ledger I kept in the hours when the ceiling cracks wouldn't count fast enough. Year two: the inquisitors burned a woman in Lantern Square for "unlicensed healing," and I stood in the crowd with my head bowed and my magic folded up small behind my ribs like a knife in a boot, and I did nothing, because doing something would have cost me my cover, and my cover was the last duty. Year four: they started taking children who heard things they shouldn't, and I told myself I didn't know for certain, and I was lying, and I did nothing. Year six: a drunk in my shop bragged that his cousin worked the palace kitchens and the king hadn't been seen in daylight in a month, and I poured him another cup and changed the subject, because curiosity was dangerous, and safety was the last duty.

Somewhere along the way, *silence is the last duty* became *silence is the only duty*, and then simply *silence*. The order had been twenty-two students and a master. By year seven, as far as I knew—and I made sure not to know—it was one man selling luck-knots.

I want to be clear about what shame feels like, since I've had seven years of field research. It doesn't feel like fire. It doesn't feel like guilt's dramatic cousins, grief or rage. It feels like dust. It gets into everything. You dust the shelves of your little shop and it's there. You knot a luck-charm for a girl with a sick mother and it's in the knot. You wake at three in the morning in the narrow bed above your narrow life and it's in the dark, patient as a creditor, and it says the same thing every night, in Serafine's voice, in your own voice, in the voice of every student who scattered and maybe fought and maybe died with a blade singing in their hands:

*You ran. She stayed. You ran.*

And every night I answered it the way I'd answered everything for seven years. Quietly. With practice. With the flawless technique of a man whose one true gift was building walls out of words.

* * *

On the last night of my seventh year of hiding—the night before a dying courier would bleed out on my floorboards and ruin my careful life forever—I dreamed of the chapter house.

I dreamed the bell was ringing, the war-bell, and I was in the dormitory again with the gold light coming through the shutters, and Serafine stood at the head of the hall with Vigil's point on the stones. But in the dream, when she said "*Scatter. Survive. Speak nothing,*" the hall was empty except for me. Twenty-one empty chairs. And she looked at me the way she'd looked at me that morning, tired in a way I'd never seen before, and she said:

"Silence is the last duty, Ulyana. It is not the first."

I woke up with the phrase sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal, and I lay in the dark above my shop and counted cracks until dawn, and I told myself—as I had told myself ten thousand times—that there was nothing to be done, that I was one man, that the past was buried deeper than Patience under the fence post, that tomorrow would be the same as yesterday: charms, knots, small lies, safe silence.

Tomorrow, a stranger would press a bloodstained map into my hands and die on my floorboards, and the map would hum in a dialect only dead men should know, and the past would climb out of its grave and stand in my shop dripping harbor water.

But that night, I didn't know that. That night I only knew the dust was thicker than usual, and somewhere across the city, under the palace, under the harbor, something old was dreaming—and my master's skeleton, though I did not know it yet, was kneeling at an altar, holding a door I had run away from.

Seven years of silence. And underneath it, all along, the first duty, waiting.
