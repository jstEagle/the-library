## 9. Audience with the Crown

There is a particular kind of sin that arrives wearing a cassock and carrying a wax seal.

I had been a priest of the Palace Chapel for nineteen years, and in all that time I had never once committed forgery. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept — the ones the Veil's inquisitors don't burn, if such a place exists. Nineteen years of honest ledgers, honest blessings, honest prayers spoken over honest bread. And then one gray morning in the week before the Binding Festival, I sat down at my own desk with the Chancellor's invitation template open beside me and a pot of good ink, and I became a forger.

The strange thing was how easy it felt. Like the sin had been waiting in me all along, folded up small, patient as a moth in winter.

"You're hesitating," said Ulyana Delacroix from the corner of my cell — my *cell*, though it was really just a room with a cot and too many books; priests of my rank don't get cells, we get closets with better views.

"I'm praying," I said.

"You're staring at the wax."

"Praying," I repeated, "with my eyes open. It's an old tradition. Very obscure. You wouldn't know it."

He almost smiled. In the three weeks since he'd come to me — or rather, since I'd come to him, following rumors through dockside taverns like a man chasing smoke — I had catalogued perhaps four of those almost-smiles. They were rationed out like communion wine during a plague year. The rest of the time his face did what faces do in Veymar: nothing at all. Seven years of hiding teaches a person to make their face into a locked door.

I pressed the seal into the cooling wax. The Chancellor's crest — a stag rampant under a crown — came out slightly lopsided. I stared at it with the particular horror of a perfectionist committing his first crime.

"No one will look that closely," Ulyana said.

"The Chancellor's secretary looks at everything closely. It's why she has that squint. She once caught a baron forging his own lineage and had him exiled by lunchtime."

"Then we're fortunate," he said, "that charm-sellers are beneath her squint."

That was the whole architecture of the plan, such as it was: every year before the Binding Festival, the palace opened its gates to licensed tradesmen to prepare the festival grounds and bless the goods — and among the licensed trades, tucked between the candle-dippers and the incense merchants, were the charm-sellers. Not *magicians*, mind you. Magic was punishable by drowning, and everyone knew it, and everyone also knew that half the charms sold in Veymar's night market worked and the other half were knotted string sold to the desperate at criminal markup. The palace tolerated charm-sellers the way a body tolerates bacteria: necessary, distasteful, best not examined.

So I forged an invitation naming one "Master Ulwin Ferro, charm-seller, licensed district of Lowmarket," and I prayed — eyes closed this time, properly — that no one would notice that Master Ulwin Ferro had been invented eleven days ago in a priest's closet, or that his license was a scrap of paper I'd aged with tea, or that his hands, when I'd shaken one, had felt like they could unmake the world if they ever decided to stop being careful.

* * *

Here is what I had not told Ulyana, because telling it aloud would have made it realer than I could bear:

For six months, I had been hearing confessions from the king's chamber.

This was not unusual in itself. King Aldric was old and frightened of death — most kings are, death being the one peasant that storms the castle without an army — and twice a month Father Bertrand and I took turns kneeling outside his bedchamber to hear confession through the door, per the old rite for sovereigns who could not easily be moved. The king spoke softly. The king always spoke softly. And the king spoke in no language I had ever learned.

I told myself, for months, that it was a dialect. There are forty tongues in the empire and I am fluent in nine and literate in twelve more, but there are always more languages, that's the trap of scholarship — you spend your life learning them and die knowing you've missed some. I told myself it was age, that the king's mind had slipped its moorings and drifted back to some childhood tongue from some province no map remembered. I told myself the words *sounded wrong* only because I was tired, because the chapel was cold, because the candles guttered in drafts that didn't exist.

But here is the thing about a lie you tell yourself nightly: it wears thin in the places where you grip it hardest.

And there was the other thing. The sound underneath the words. When the king finished speaking — when whatever wore his voice finished speaking — there came a pause, and in the pause a sound like water moving in a very deep place. Not dripping. Moving. Purposeful. The way water moves when something in it decides which way to go.

Six months of that. Six months of kneeling on stone, holding my crucifix like a talisman against a darkness the Church officially declared did not exist, whispering the rites of absolution over a voice that answered in a tongue older than absolution, and going back to my closet each night to write in my private journal in cipher, because I had begun to suspect that even my own thoughts might not be entirely private anymore.

When I heard the rumor — a swordspeaker alive in Lowmarket, a charm-seller whose luck-knots actually worked — I went looking for him the way a drowning man goes looking for driftwood. Without dignity. With both hands.

* * *

The morning of the audience, I met Ulyana at the tradesmen's gate with the forged invitation sweating in my palm.

"You look terrible," I told him. It was true. He had shaved off the two days of beard he'd grown for the disguise, then grown it back overnight somehow, and he wore a merchant's coat that fit him like an apology.

"You look like a man committing treason," he said.

"I *am* a man committing treason. That's different from looking like one. Treason should be comfortable, or what's the point." I handed him the invitation. "Master Ulwin Ferro. Remember the name. If anyone asks, you've held a Lowmarket license for four years, your specialty is household charms — protection against damp, mice, and unfaithful spouses — and you learned the trade from your mother, who is dead, God rest her, because dead mothers can't be questioned."

"And if they ask why a priest is vouching for me?"

"Because I'm the chapel's procurator of festival goods, which is true, and because clergy are assumed to be honest, which is the finest joke the empire ever played on itself." I straightened his collar. He flinched — a small flinch, quickly mastered, but I saw it. Seven years of hiding makes another man's hands near your throat feel like a verdict. "Forgive me," I said.

"Don't apologize. Just tell me where I'm going."

"The Great Hall, east screen. The charm-sellers set their stalls along the colonnade, but the Chancellor likes a few of us presented formally — it flatters the guilds. You'll stand behind the carved screen while the court files past. You won't speak unless spoken to. You'll bow to the king if he passes, and—" I stopped.

"And?"

"And if anything happens, you walk toward me. I'll be at the west pillar in deacon's gray. Don't run. Running is how people get noticed."

He nodded once, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition, maybe. A man returning to a house he'd burned down himself.

"Father Cassian," he said quietly. "Why are you doing this? Truly. You have a position. A life. You could report me tomorrow and be rewarded."

I considered lying to him. Priests are supposed to be good at comfort, and lies are the cheapest comfort there is. But he had spent seven years marinating in other people's lies — his own included — and I suspected the truth might be the only coin he'd still accept.

"Because I hear things," I said. "In the king's chamber. Things I shouldn't hear. And I have spent six months telling myself they mean nothing, and I am very tired, Master Ferro, of telling myself things." I touched his shoulder, lightly, the way you touch a door you're afraid is load-bearing. "Go. Bow well. And whatever you see in there — see it for both of us."

* * *

I did not see the audience. I want to be precise about that, because what follows I know partly from what Ulyana told me afterward and partly from what I witnessed in the hall itself, standing at my west pillar with my hands folded and my heart conducting a percussion recital against my ribs.

The court filed in at the ninth bell. I knew the choreography by heart: the chamberlain, the banner-bearers, the Chancellor with her squint, the honor guard in white and gold, and last — always last, so that the room had time to arrange its awe — the king.

King Aldric came down the hall slowly, leaning on a cane he hadn't needed five years ago, and the court bowed in waves like wheat before wind. I bowed too. I have bowed to this man at every festival for nineteen years. My knees know the angle better than my prayers.

And as he passed — ten feet from me, close enough that I could smell the myrrh they burn in his chambers to cover some other scent, something mineral and cold, like wet stone in a cave — I looked at his hands.

Old men's hands shake. His did not. His hands hung at his sides with a stillness that was not calm. I have watched dying men; I have watched statues; I have watched the harbor at slack tide. His hands were like the harbor. Something beneath the surface, waiting for the tide to turn.

And then — I swear this on every sacrament I still believe in, and I'll confess now that the list grows shorter by the week — the skin of his wrist *moved*. Not trembled. Moved. A slow ripple, traveling from wrist to elbow, the way light travels under water, the way a shape shows through a bedsheet when someone walks a hand beneath it.

No one else saw. Or no one else let themselves see. The court of Veymar has had nineteen years of practice at not seeing, and practice makes perfect.

Ulyana stood behind the east screen. I could not see him, only the carved lattice of saints and stags, and beyond it the blur of his shoulder. But I heard — the whole hall heard — the moment the king paused.

It was a small thing. The royal procession simply stopped, three paces short of the dais, and the king turned his head — slowly, slowly, like a weathervane finding a wind — toward the east screen. Toward the charm-sellers' stalls. Toward the place where a man named Ulwin Ferro stood holding his breath among the knotted strings and dried herbs.

The hall went quiet in that special way palaces go quiet, which is to say loudly. Two hundred courtiers stopped breathing in unison. The Chancellor's squint narrowed. Somewhere a candle spat.

And the king smiled.

I have seen the king smile a thousand times. It is a public smile, polished by decades of use, warm as a coin left out in the sun. This was not that smile. This smile arrived late, as if summoned from somewhere far away, and it sat on his face the way a mask sits on a wall — correctly, and yet somehow facing the wrong direction.

"Charm-sellers," the king said, and his voice was his voice, and yet underneath it, faint as a fish passing under ice, something *else* said it too, a half-beat behind, in that tongue I had heard through his chamber door. "How the city loves its little magics."

The Chancellor laughed, bright and rehearsed. The court laughed. I did not laugh. Beside me, a young guard did not laugh either; he was frowning at the king's shadow, which — I checked, I made myself check — lay on the marble floor pointing toward the east screen, though every other shadow in the hall fell west with the windows.

The procession resumed. The king climbed the dais. The audience proceeded through its liturgy of petitions and presentations, and I stood at my pillar performing the deepest prayer of my life, which consisted mostly of the word *please* repeated like a heartbeat.

An hour later, Ulyana found me at the west pillar. He walked toward me the way I'd told him — unhurried, a tradesman tired on his feet — but his face had gone the color of candle wax, and when he reached me he gripped my arm harder than any man had gripped it in nineteen years of polite handshakes.

"It's in him," he whispered. His lips barely moved. "Under the skin. I saw it move. Cassian — it *looked* at me. Through him. It knows what I am."

"Did it speak?"

"Not in words." He swallowed. "In swordspeech. One phrase. Old dialect. It said—" and here his voice failed him, and he had to try twice, "*—the last oath-breaker comes home.*"

The bells began ringing the noon hour, and around us the court dissolved into its usual noise, and no one marked the priest and the charm-seller standing together in the colonnade's long shadow. But far away, beneath our feet — beneath the palace, beneath the harbor, beneath the drowned places the maps pretend not to show — I felt, or imagined I felt, or perhaps did not imagine at all, a great slow attention turning toward us like the head of something waking from a century's sleep.

"We leave now," Ulyana said. "Separately. Now, Cassian."

"Yes," I said. "God help us. Yes."

Behind us, in the Great Hall, the Binding Festival's preparations continued merrily onward — six days early, and counting.
