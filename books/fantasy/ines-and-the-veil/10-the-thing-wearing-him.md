## 10. The Thing Wearing Him

*Ulyana*

I had been in the palace for less than an hour, standing behind a screen in the king's receiving chamber selling nothing, when I felt the Veil notice me.

There's no polite way to describe it. It was like being touched by something cold and wet on the back of the neck—except the touch went through the neck, through the skull, and settled somewhere behind my eyes like a fishhook finding purchase. Every swordspeaker learns to feel the moment a larger power takes an interest. My master called it *the turning of the head*. She said you'd know it because the world would go quiet around you, the way a forest goes quiet when a predator passes.

The chamber went quiet. The courtiers murmuring over their petitions, the clerks scratching at their ledgers, the fussy chamberlain complaining about candle smoke—all of it dropped away as if someone had put a hand over the mouth of the world.

And behind the screen, past the brazier smoke and the gilded partition, King Aldric of Veymar turned his head and looked directly at where I stood.

He wasn't looking at me. That was the horror of it. He was looking *through* me, with eyes that had gone the color of deep water, and beneath the skin of his face something moved—not a muscle, not a tremor, but a slow rolling shift, like current under ice. Like something swimming just below the surface of a man and pressing up against him from inside.

*There you are,* said a voice that did not use air. It arrived fully formed behind my teeth, tasting of harbor water and old iron. *A sword that thinks it's hidden.*

I did what any sensible man does when a god notices him. I picked up my tray of luck-knots and false charms, bowed to the chamberlain, and walked out of the receiving chamber at the pace of a man who has absolutely nowhere urgent to be.

Behind me, very softly, the king began to laugh. It was Aldric's throat making the sound, but it was not Aldric's laugh. It was the laugh of something that had been holding its breath for seven years and had finally, finally exhaled.

* * *

*Ines*

I was mending nets on the Harborside steps when the bells started wrong.

Veymar's bells have a language. Dockworkers learn it before they learn letters: the hour chimes, the tide bell, the fire pattern, the festival peals. What came off the palace towers that afternoon was none of these. It was a single low note, struck over and over, no rhythm to it, like a heart beating inside a bronze chest. The gulls went up off the water all at once, thousands of them, a white rag torn across the sky.

Old Tamor dropped his net and crossed himself, which was funny, because Tamor hadn't crossed himself since the war. "Inquisition call," he said. "Haven't heard that note since I was a boy. They're hunting something."

They were hunting Ulyana. I knew it the way you know weather coming—in your knees, in the ache of an old scar. The charm-seller with the careful hands and the careful silences, who looked at my brother's bloodstained map like it was a letter from a dead friend. The man who'd walked into the palace that morning wearing a forged invitation and Cassian's borrowed confidence, carrying nothing but a tray of knots that were only knots.

"Stupid," I said out loud, to nobody. "Stupid, brave, useless man."

Then I was running, because that's what Delacroixs apparently did instead of thinking. I cut through the fish market, past the brine barrels and the shouting, down into the merchant quarter where Ulyana's shop sat tucked between a dyer's and a coffin-maker like a secret nobody wanted to keep.

The street was already filling with gray coats. Inquisitors moved through Veymar the way oil moves through water—silent, spreading, getting into everything. There were six of them at the corner by the coffin-maker's, and they weren't knocking on doors. They were simply standing there, faces tilted upward, like hounds tasting wind.

I took the alley.

* * *

*Ulyana*

Cassian was waiting where he'd promised, in the shadow of the tannery arch, still in his vestments, looking like a sheep that had wandered into a wolf convention and decided to stay for the conversation.

"You felt it," he said. Not a question.

"It felt *me*." I kept walking. "We need Ines. We need the map. We need to be anywhere but here."

"The cathedral—"

"Is six days away and worthless if we're drowned in a cistern tonight. Move."

We made it three streets. Then the gray coats came out of the walls.

That's what it had felt like, afterward—the only way I could ever describe it. We turned onto Cooper's Row and the inquisitors were simply *there*, stepping out of doorways and mouths of alleys, six of them, then ten, moving without hurry, without signals, as if they all heard the same music. Which, I suppose, they did. The Veil doesn't command its hounds with words. It pours itself into them a little, enough to steer, and steers them at whatever it wants caught.

"Cassian," I said quietly. "How attached are you to that collar?"

"Not especially."

"Good." I pulled him left, down a stair-cut between buildings that smelled of tallow and dead rats, into the labyrinth of the merchant quarter where I had spent seven years learning every route a body could take without being seen. Seven years of smallness. Seven years of memorizing shadows. It turned out all that cowardice had been training.

We ran. Over a drying yard hung with dyed cloth, reds and blues snapping in the wind like the flags of a country that didn't exist. Across a rooftop where pigeons exploded around us in gray thunder. Down through a cooperage, over barrels, while somewhere behind us the low bell-note followed, patient as tide.

"They're not chasing," Cassian panted. "Why aren't they chasing?"

"Because they don't need to catch us." I pulled him into the mouth of a drain culvert, black water shin-deep, and stopped to listen. "They need to herd us. A thing that size doesn't run after mice. It closes its hand."

"And then?"

"Then it squeezes."

* * *

*Ines*

I found them by accident, which is a lie; I found them because I'd spent twenty years reading this city the way sailors read water, and I knew exactly which drain culvert a clever man would pick and exactly how stupid it was, because the culvert emptied into the canal basin, and the canal basin was where the inquisitors would be waiting, because I would have been waiting there too.

So instead of following them into the trap, I got ahead of it.

The basin was a wide stone bowl where five canal mouths met, spanned by the old customs bridge. Two gray coats stood at each end of the bridge already, and more coming along the quays, unhurried, closing like a purse string. Above them, on the warehouse roofs, figures stood at the edges, looking down into the water. Looking down at the water like it might answer them.

And the water was answering. I could see it from the top of the salt stairs—a slow spiral turning in the middle of the basin, against every current, wide as a wagon wheel, and underneath it a light. Greenish. Patient. Getting brighter.

Whatever lived under the palace had reached into the harbor, and it was fishing.

Ulyana and the priest came out of the culvert mouth right on schedule, wading, soaked to the thighs, and stopped dead when they saw the bridge. I saw Ulyana's face do the arithmetic and arrive at the correct, terrible answer: nowhere left.

So I did the thing my brother would have done, and hated myself for doing it, and did it anyway.

I knocked over the tar vat.

The ropewalk above the basin kept a half-barrel of pitch tar on a hoist for waterproofing cable. One good shove and two hundred pounds of hot tar came off its hook and went over the edge of the walkway, down across the customs bridge, and the world turned black and screaming. The inquisitors scattered—I'll say this for them, they scattered fast—and the tar caught the lantern someone dropped and burned across the stones in a sheet of orange, and the smoke went up thick and sweet and foul, and through it I shouted the only thing worth shouting:

"ROOFTOPS! MOVE!"

* * *

*Ulyana*

She came out of the smoke like something from a ballad, all elbows and fury, grabbed my sleeve, and hauled.

"Ines—"

"Don't talk. Climb."

We climbed. Her route was better than mine—that stung, and I filed the sting away to examine never—up a stack of salt crates, along a warehouse ridge, tiles slick under our hands, the burning basin falling away behind us and the bell-note pursuing, always pursuing, that patient bronze heartbeat. Cassian labored behind us praying under his breath in liturgical Latin, which I suspect was less devotion than momentum.

We were four roofs from the tanner's quarter and freedom when the roof ended.

Not collapsed—*ended*, sheared away in some old fire, a gap of twelve feet down to a yard full of broken glass and rusted hooks. Beyond it, the next roof went on toward safety like a promise. Behind us, gray coats poured over the ridge we'd just crossed, and among them, walking last, unhurried, was one who moved differently. Taller. Its head tilted at an angle no neck should allow, and where its eyes should have been there was only the green light, the same light as the water, the same light as the king's skin.

It looked at me. The whole street went quiet again. The fishhook settled behind my eyes.

*Swordspeaker,* it said, in the voice that didn't use air. *The king remembers your order fondly. He asks: will you come home?*

"He asks nothing," I said aloud. "You do."

The thing smiled, if a hole can smile.

It was Ines who solved the gap. She was already ripping the coil of net-mending line off her belt—of course she carried line, she was a dockworker, they're born with rope in their fists—looping it around a chimney brick, testing it with one hard pull, and throwing the other end to me.

"Tie off. Swing them across. I'll hold."

"You'll hold *me*?" I said. "I'm heavier than I look."

"Everyone says that. Nobody is." She braced the line around her hips, boots against the chimney base, and jerked her chin at the gap. "Go. Priest first."

Cassian swung across shrieking a psalm. I went second, less shrieking. And then Ines unwound the line from herself, tossed it across, and stepped back from the edge to face the gray coats alone—because the line had to come back across somehow, and someone had to throw it, and she had already decided, with that terrifying dockworker arithmetic, that the someone was her and the cost was hers to pay.

"Ines—" 

"Throw it to the priest. Go." She drew the long cargo knife off her belt and set her feet. Through the smoke, the tall one with the green eyes began to walk toward her, and the others spread out along the roof edges like ink finding the cracks in paper.

I have faced down worse odds than six-to-one. I once fought three Veil-bound knights on a burning bridge with a broken arm. But I had magic then. Now I had a knife-less hand, a rope, and a woman on the far side of a gap telling me to leave her, and I want it recorded that I did not hesitate because I was wise. I hesitated because I was a coward who had spent seven years practicing leaving people.

Then I thought about her brother dying on my floorboards, reaching me, and what it means that no one was there.

I threw the line to Cassian. "Hold this. Don't let go even if I die. *Especially* if I die, you'll need to tell everyone how stupid I was." And I swung back across the gap, into the smoke, toward the green-eyed thing and the woman with the knife.

What happened next happened fast and badly, which is the honest way most courage happens. I got between Ines and the tall one for exactly long enough to learn that a body animated by the Veil does not tire, does not feint, and hits like a collapsing wall. I took a backhand across the ribs that lifted my feet off the tiles and showed me the sky sideways. Ines got her knife into the thing's shoulder to the hilt, which would have dropped any living man and did nothing at all except make it turn toward her with that awful unhurried tilt of the head.

Its hand closed on her arm.

I heard the crack from across the roof. I heard her scream—short, furious, more anger than pain, a scream that swore at the thing even as it broke her—and something in me that had been sitting quietly for seven years stood up and took the leash out of my frightened hands.

I didn't speak swordspeech. I didn't have the words anymore, or the right, or the power. What I had was a roof tile, and rage, and the element of surprise enjoyed by men everyone believes are harmless. I brought the tile down on the thing's head with everything my shoulders had, twice, three times, until the skull cracked and the green light guttered like a lamp out of oil, and the body folded, and the other gray coats stopped as one, heads tilting, listening to some voice I couldn't hear.

Then they withdrew. All of them. At once. Unhurried. The message received; the demonstration complete.

* * *

*Ines*

He carried me down off the roofs with my arm cradled against his chest like I was made of glass, which insulted me so much I nearly forgot it hurt. Broken, the arm. Maybe the shoulder too. The pain came in waves now, white and total, and through it I kept saying things like "put me down, you great soft-handed charm peddler," and he kept saying things like "no," in a voice I'd never heard him use. Flat. Certain. The voice of a man who had stopped hiding from something.

Cassian led us through the tannery lanes to a smuggler's bolt-hole I've used since I was fifteen—a cellar under a chandler's, dry, dark, smelling of wax and tallow. He splinted my arm with hands that shook only a little, which for a priest was practically battlefield surgery, and Ulyana knelt there in the dark with his palms hovering over the break, and I watched him *not do it*. Watched him want to. Watched the wanting cost him.

"Do it," I said, through my teeth. "Whatever it is. Whatever it costs. Do it."

"I can't heal this. Not anymore. Not without—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Seven years ago I could have mended this in a breath. Now all I am is a man with steady hands and a splint."

"Well." I leaned my head back against the wall and let the wave pass. "Your steady hands are better than most men's miracles. So stop looking at them like they're a grave."

He looked at me then—really looked, the way you look at someone when you've seen them break and hold anyway—and something passed between us that neither of us had time to name. Outside, far off, the bell-note tolled on and on, patient as tide, and beneath it, fainter, from under the harbor, from under the whole city, something else had begun.

Singing.

Pip's phrase. The map's melody. Answered, note for note, by a voice with no throat at all.

Ulyana went very still. "It knows the song," he whispered. "Gods help us—it's learned the song."

And somewhere out in the smoking dark of Veymar, a child who heard maps sing was alone, and the things with green eyes had stopped hunting us, because they had found something easier to carry home.
