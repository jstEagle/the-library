## 11. Triage and Truths

The safehouse was a smuggler's loft above a rope-walk, all salt-stained beams and the smell of tar, and it had exactly one virtue: no windows anyone could see through. I put Ines on a cot that smelled of hemp and old fish, and she laughed at me for it, which was how I knew the wound was bad. People laugh when they're trying not to scream.

"Your healing is terrible," she said. "You press like a washerwoman."

"I've never once been accused of being gentle."

"You're pressing on my *liver*, Ulyana."

I was. I moved my hands. The inquisitor's blade had gone in under her ribs, shallow but long, a slicing cut meant less to kill than to mark — the Veil's hunters liked their quarry bleeding, made the tracking easier. We'd washed the blood off her in the harbor shallows like animals, which was why she was shivering now, teeth chattering against the fever that was already climbing.

"I need light," I said.

"You need to stop talking about light and do something."

"I'm going to do something you won't like."

Her eyes found mine in the dark. Even half-delirious with pain and loss of blood, Ines Delacroix — no relation to me, though the city would have hanged us both for sharing a name with the dead courier — had a gaze like a fishhook. It went in easy and came out hard.

"Do it anyway," she said.

So I did what I had sworn seven years ago never to do again. I took off my left glove, laid my bare palm over the wound, and spoke.

Not aloud. Swordspeech has no sound unless you want it to; it lives in the wrist, in the breath behind the sternum, in the small muscles of the hand. My order had used it to bind oaths into steel and unbind souls from bodies. But every discipline has its humble cousins, and healing was the humblest — needlework magic, my master called it, the magic of mending what fools have torn.

The words moved through me like cold water through a cracked pipe. I felt them leave. That's the thing no one tells you about magic: it isn't power flowing out of you, it's *self* flowing out of you. Every charm costs a coin of yourself, and I had spent seven years hoarding myself like a miser, and now I was spending whole years' savings in a single breath.

The wound began to close. Ines hissed, arched, grabbed my wrist with a grip that would have bruised a lesser man's bones.

"That's — " she gasped. "That's not charmseller work."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

"It's the thing they drown people for."

"Yes."

She held my eyes while the magic knitted her flesh, and she didn't look away, and I will confess something here that I confessed to no one else: that was the moment I stopped being afraid of her. Not because she trusted me. Because she watched me commit treason on her behalf and her only reaction was to note it, file it, and keep breathing.

When it was done I sat back on my heels, shaking. The candle I'd lit had burned down two fingers' worth. Time goes strange when you spend yourself.

"You're gray," she said.

"I'm always gray."

"You're grayer." She tried to sit up, failed, settled for propping herself on one elbow. Her fever-bright eyes were doing arithmetic. "Seven years," she said. "You've been hiding seven years. Selling luck-knots and love-charms that are just knots. And you can do *that*."

"The love-charms are also just knots," I said weakly.

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a whip. "Don't do the wry thing right now, Ulyana. My brother is dead. There's a shadow wearing the king. Children are disappearing. And the man who could have — who might have — " She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter, which from Ines was far more frightening. "Tell me what you are. All of it. Or I walk out of this loft and find someone else, and you can go back to your knots."

There was nowhere to sit except an upturned crate, so I sat on it, and I told her.

* * *

I told her about the Order of the Sundered Oath, which sounds grander than it was. Forty swordspeakers at its height, living in a chapter-house above the Salt Gate, teaching children to speak with their hands and fight with their voices. Keepers of the old accords. Sworn to the crown since before anyone remembered why.

I told her about my master, Serah Vael, who had hands like driftwood and a laugh that could start arguments in empty rooms. Who taught me that swordspeech wasn't a weapon, whatever the ballads said. "A sword ends things," she used to say. "A word begins them. Be very careful which you reach for first."

I told her about the war. The one in the histories they don't teach dockworkers, the one that ended so suddenly, seven years ago, when everyone expected another decade of it. The one that ended because forty swordspeakers stood in a drowned cathedral beneath the palace and bound their king to something ancient and hungry, trading one horror for another, because the other horror had been eating the countryside alive.

"We didn't know what the Veil would become," I said. "We knew it fed on magic. We thought — we thought we could starve it slowly. Ban magic in the city, quietly, give it almost nothing. A controlled diet. Our order would be the wardens. We'd watch the king, watch the Veil, and in a generation we'd find a way to end it properly."

"And instead?"

"Instead the ban became a purge. Someone at court realized that outlawed magic doesn't disappear — it hides, and hiding magic still leaks. Every hedge-witch, every charm-seller, every child with a gift the neighbors noticed. The Veil grew fat on them. And then someone realized the wardens themselves were the largest meal at the table." I looked at my hands. "They came for us at night. Serah scattered us first — sent us out the back ways with orders to run, to hide, to stay silent. Silence was the last duty, she said. I ran. I ran and I hid and I sold knots for seven years while the people who raised me were dragged to the harbor."

Ines was quiet a long moment. The fever had flushed her brown skin dark, and sweat stood on her upper lip, but her voice, when it came, was steady as a keel.

"You could have fought."

"Forty of us couldn't beat the palace. Two hundred of us couldn't have. And fighting would have fed the Veil a feast of swordspeech in a single afternoon." I heard myself making the argument I'd been making in my own skull for seven years, and hated the sound of it, the way you hate a floorboard that creaks in the same spot every time. "Staying silent was supposed to be the honorable choice. Starve it by starving ourselves."

"Did it work?"

"You're lying in a safehouse with a closed knife-wound, hunted by inquisitors, while children vanish off the streets," I said. "What do you think?"

She didn't gloat. I'd braced for gloating. Instead she reached over and took my bare left hand — the one I'd healed her with — and turned it over, studying the palm like a buyer checking a blade.

"So the courier," she said. "My brother. He found you on purpose."

"I think so. 'The king is not the king.' He knew enough to say that. He knew where to find a hidden swordspeaker, or he guessed, or someone told him." I hesitated. "Ines, I've been asking myself for days whether he died because he found me. Whether I'm the reason he was followed."

"He was followed because he was asking questions," she said flatly. "He was always asking questions. Drove our grandmother mad. Drove the harbormaster mad. He'd pull a thread until the whole sweater came apart." Her thumb traced something on my palm — absently, I think, the way grieving people touch things. "He wrote me letters. Did I tell you that? Every route he ran, he wrote. Half of them never arrived, but the ones that did — "

She stopped. Her face changed. Something moved behind her eyes, some door opening onto a room she hadn't meant to enter.

"Ines?"

"He told me," she whispered. "He told me and I thought he was being dramatic. He did that. Loved a good story about himself." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "Six months ago he wrote that the harbor children were going missing, and the watch wasn't looking, and the families that complained got visited by men in gray. Then three months ago — I kept the letter, it's in my chest at my rooms — he wrote that he'd counted them. Eleven children in eight months, all from the low quarters, all between six and ten years old. And he wrote — " Her voice caught. She pushed through it. "He wrote, *they aren't being taken, they're being harvested, there's a difference, harvests have seasons.*"

The rope-walk creaked below us. Somewhere a gull screamed its idiot heart out.

"Harvests have seasons," I repeated. My mouth had gone dry. "Ines. When is the Binding Festival?"

"Nineteen days." She was already pushing herself upright despite everything I'd done to her ribs. "Nineteen days, and every year it's bigger, and every year more gray coats come to the city for it, and every year — " She went pale under the flush. "Pip. Ulyana. Pip hears the map sing. Pip is nine."

"I know."

"We have to get the child off the streets tonight."

"We will. But first — " I took her shoulders, gently, feeling the new-knit flesh flex under my palms, still tender as scar. "First you lie back down, because if you tear that wound open I'll have to spend more of myself, and I'm down to lint and buttons."

For once, she obeyed. But her hand shot out and caught my sleeve as I rose.

"The letters," she said. "Ulyana, there are more letters. A year of them. Routes, names, dates, counts. He mapped the disappearances the way he mapped smuggling lanes — exact, obsessive. If the Veil has a pattern, a season, a *schedule* — " Her fever-bright eyes fixed on mine, and grief and purpose in them had become, by now, indistinguishable. "It's all in my chest in my rooms on Cordwainer Lane. Which the inquisitors may already be searching. Which means we need to move tomorrow. At dawn."

"At dawn," I agreed.

She lay back. Within minutes the fever pulled her under, into a sleep full of twitching and half-spoken names — her brother's name, mostly, and once, softly, *grandmother*.

I sat on my crate and watched her breathe and thought about letters in a chest on Cordwainer Lane. Eleven children in eight months. Harvests have seasons. Nineteen days.

And beneath all of it, quieter, the thought I couldn't shake: her brother had spent months pulling threads, alone, with no one to help him, because the one man who might have listened was selling knots and calling it survival.

At dawn, I decided, we would get the letters.

And if the inquisitors had gotten there first —

Well. I had spent seven years learning exactly how much noise a quiet man could make.
