## 12. The Defector's Bargain

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a sacristy at three in the morning, and I have spent thirty years learning to read it the way sailors read weather. Most nights it is a comfortable silence—the silence of vestments breathing on their hooks, of candlewax settling, of God being patient with me. Tonight it was the other kind. The kind that means something has already gone wrong and is waiting for you to notice.

I noticed.

I had spent the evening doing what I had been doing for eleven nights now, which was committing crimes against my own church with a steady hand and a clear conscience, two things I had previously believed were mutually exclusive. The liturgical archive beneath the Basilica of Saint Verane is kept under triple lock, and the third lock is not a lock at all but a vow—a spoken oath every archivist swears on ordination that he will not remove documents from the vault. I had sworn that oath at twenty-four, kneeling on cold stone, feeling very serious about it.

At forty-nine, I folded four folios into the lining of my coat and told God, *You can bill me later.*

The records themselves were older than the basilica, older than the city above them, written in that cramped pre-Reformation hand where every letter looks like it's apologizing for existing. I had found what I needed on the fourth night of searching, buried in a rite so thoroughly expunged from the living liturgy that its pages had been sewn shut—actually sewn, with black thread, by someone who wanted it forgotten badly enough to do needlework about it.

*The Rite of Binding and Unbinding.* One ceremony, two uses. That was the detail that had kept me awake: they were never meant to be separate. Whoever wrote it understood that anything bound can be loosed, and built the loosening into the binding itself, like a knot that remembers how to be rope.

And the loosening required a voice. Not any voice. A swordspeaker's.

I had not known what a swordspeaker was until nine days ago. Thirty years a priest of Veymar, and I had learned the word from a dying courier's sister, shouted across a dockside tavern while she held a knife to my throat because I'd asked one question too many about her brother. Ines Delacroix does not believe in gentle introductions. She believes in knives. We have reached an understanding: I bring information, she doesn't stab me, and we both pretend this is a friendship.

Now I stood in the doorway of Ulyana Delacroix's shop—it turned out Ines's brother had died reaching a charm-seller, and charm-sellers apparently ran in families, or at least in conspiracies—and knocked the way you knock when you're carrying stolen scripture and don't want the neighbors to hear.

No answer. But lamplight moved behind the shuttered window, and I heard the low murmur of voices inside, so I let myself in the way Ines had taught me, which is to say I tried the handle first because she never locks anything, an oversight I have criticized and she has ignored.

They were all there. The shop smelled of dried lavender and candle smoke and, faintly, of blood that had been scrubbed and scrubbed and never quite left—I had noticed it my first visit and said nothing, because some floors deserve your silence. Ulyana sat behind his counter looking like a man who had been arguing with a map and losing. Ines occupied the one chair, boots up, whittling something that was either a peg leg or a threat. And on the floor by the stove, cross-legged, surrounded by wood shavings, sat the child.

Pip looked up at me with those enormous eyes and said, "He's got paper in his coat. Lots of it. It smells old."

"Good evening to you too," I said.

"He always knows," Ines said, without looking up from her knife-work. "Told you."

"You did." I set my satchel on the counter. My hands were steadier than I expected them to be. "I've brought something. You'll want to sit down, though most of you already are, so really this is just for Ulyana."

Ulyana didn't move. He had that quality I was beginning to recognize—a stillness that wasn't calm but compression, like a man holding a door shut from the inside. "What is it, Cassian?"

"The rite," I said. "The binding rite. The actual text, not the sanitized version they teach seminarians, which describes the Veil as a *metaphor for the king's burden*, which is the single most embarrassing sentence ever committed to vellum." I drew out the folios and laid them on the counter, and the black-sewn one last, and watched Ulyana go pale in the particular way of a man seeing a ghost made of paperwork.

"Where did you get these?"

"The archive under Saint Verane. Where they've been since before the purge." I tapped the sewn folio. "This was closed up seventy years ago and hasn't been opened since. Until eleven days ago. By me. With a knife, if you're wondering; the thread was stubborn."

Ines put down her whittling. That, more than anything, told me how much this mattered—she only stopped working when her hands were needed for something else.

"Read it," Ulyana said quietly. "Tell me what it says. All of it."

So I did. I am, whatever else has become of me, still a priest, and reading aloud is the one part of the job I have never grown tired of. My voice filled the little shop, and the others went still around me, even Pip, who listened with his head tipped as though hearing a second layer underneath my words—which, given what I now knew about that child, he probably was.

"The binding," I read, "is wrought by the crown's consent and the order's voice together, and shall be renewed upon each century's turning at the Festival of the Binding, lest the Veil grow restless and seek its sustenance abroad." I looked up. "That last phrase. *Seek its sustenance abroad.* Do you understand what that means? It means everything we've seen—the drownings, the purges, the outlawing of magic—it was never doctrine. It was husbandry. They weren't suppressing magic. They were *farming* it. Every spell drowned in the harbor, every witch burned, every child with a gift taken away in the night—all of it funneled straight into the thing wearing the king."

"We knew that," Ines said flatly. "My brother figured out half of it before they killed him for it. Get to the part that matters, priest."

"The part that matters," I said, "is here." I opened the sewn folio to the page I had marked with a ribbon stolen, in a final flourish of hypocrisy, from the basilica's own vestry. "*The unbinding shall be spoken by a voice of the old order, within the walls where the binding was first wrought, upon the eve of renewal, and not otherwise; for the words are keyed to the place and hour as a lock to its key, and spoken elsewhere or elsewhen they are only words.*"

Silence. The bad kind again, but shared this time, which made it almost bearable.

"Where was the binding first wrought?" Ulyana asked. His voice had gone very quiet, and I thought—not for the first time—that this man treated questions like wounds, probing them gently, bracing for what came out.

"That took me another three nights," I admitted. "The location isn't written anywhere, because everyone alive when the rite was sealed knew it, and knowledge that everyone shares doesn't get recorded. But there's a marginal note in this one—" I turned to the flyleaf, where a long-dead archivist had scrawled in ink gone brown as old blood: "*the cathedral below, may it rest, may we be forgiven.*"

"The drowned cathedral," Ulyana said. "Under the palace."

"Under the harbor, technically. The palace was built over the harbor after they flooded it. Which brings us to the timing." I felt my stomach tighten around the words, the way it had tightened in the archive when I'd first worked the arithmetic. "The rite renews each century. The last renewal was ninety-four years ago. The Festival of the Binding falls on the solstice, and the eve of renewal—the night the unbinding must be spoken—is six days from tonight."

"Six days," Ines repeated.

"Six days. And then another hundred years of this. Another century of children vanishing and witches drowning and a god-shaped leech fattening itself under the palace while the whole city calls it piety." I closed the folio. My hands, I noticed belatedly, were no longer steady at all. "There's more. There's a cost. There's always a cost; nothing in the old rites is free, that's how you know they're real and not theology. Listen." I found the passage and read it slowly, because I had read it a dozen times alone in my cell and it had not gotten easier. "*The voice that speaks the unbinding speaks it away. The gift and the word depart together, and return to no man twice. Let him who would loose the king count well what he carries, for he shall set it down and walk from that place lighter than any man wishes to be.*"

I looked at Ulyana. He was staring at the folio like it had addressed him personally, which, in fairness, it had.

"It takes your voice," Ines said. Not a question. "All of it? Or just the—the magic words?"

"The text isn't specific, which frightens me more than specificity would. *Lighter than any man wishes to be.*" I shook my head. "It might mean only the swordspeech. It might mean everything. The people who wrote this weren't interested in fine print; they were interested in making sure whoever did it understood it was a pyre, not a bargain."

*

We argued for two hours. I want that on the record somewhere, in whatever ledger God keeps on defrocked priests: we argued, properly, with raised voices and slammed counters and one memorable moment when Ines threw a luck-knot at Ulyana's head and missed, which she later claimed was intentional.

The argument was not about whether to act. Nobody argued for doing nothing; I think we had all passed through that country at different speeds and burned it behind us. The argument was about Ulyana—about whether he was allowed to spend himself, whether six days was enough, whether the plan was madness or merely suicide dressed in better clothes.

"You heard what it says," Ulyana said, for perhaps the fifth time. "A voice of the old order. I'm the last one. If I fail—if I'm caught, if the words come out wrong—there is no second attempt. Ever. For a hundred years."

"So don't fail," Ines said.

"It's not that simple."

"It's exactly that simple. Complicated is what cowards call simple when they're scared." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and fixed him with the look I imagine she used on dockhands who shorted her crates. "My brother bled out on this floor reaching you. He didn't reach you so you could stand in a shop having feelings about it. He reached you because you were the only person in Veymar who could finish what he started. So finish it."

"And if the cost is everything?" Ulyana's voice cracked on it, just slightly. "If I speak the unbinding and lose not just the magic but the speech itself, the words, all of it—and the Veil survives anyway? Then I'll have paid the whole price for nothing, and there won't even be enough of me left to regret it properly."

I should say here that I had watched Ulyana closely over our few meetings, the way I used to watch penitents, looking for the gap between what a man says and what he believes. And what I saw in that moment was not fear of death. It was fear of emptiness. This man had spent seven years being nobody, and I think some part of him had concluded that nobody was all he deserved to be, and the rite threatened to make it permanent.

It fell to me, then. God has a sense of humor, and it is not a kind one; He has always preferred to have the coward deliver the courage.

"Cassian," Ulyana said, turning to me, "you're a priest. Your whole life is the Word. Tell me honestly—what's left of a man who gives up his voice?"

I thought about my vows. The ones I'd kept and the ones I hadn't. The sermons I'd preached to congregations that came to be seen rather than to hear. The confessions I'd absorbed for thirty years, including, lately, the ones that leaked from the king's chamber in no human tongue, the syllables that had crawled into my ears and nested there and taught me that something in the world was hungry in a way hunger was never meant to work.

"What's left," I said, "is what was there before the voice. I've thought about this more than you'd credit. I've spent thirty years talking to God, and I'll tell you a professional secret: He's never once answered in words. Not one. Everything true I ever learned about Him came in the silences between my own noise. A man without a voice isn't empty, Ulyana. He's finally quiet enough to hear." I smiled, and it hurt. "Besides, I'm about to lose my vows, my church, and quite possibly my neck. You can lose a voice. We'll be ruined together. It'll be good company."

"That's a terrible comfort."

"I'm a priest. Terrible comfort is the entire trade."

Pip tugged my sleeve. I looked down into that uncanny small face. "When the words go," Pip said, "they don't go nowhere. I hear where they go. Things that are given up don't die. They just go somewhere loud." Pip frowned, working at it. "The map knows the way. The map goes there too."

None of us had an answer to that. I'm not sure it was meant as comfort. Children like Pip speak prophecy the way other children spill things—accidentally, and then look surprised at the mess.

*

It was past five when the argument ended, not with resolution but with exhaustion, which is how most true resolutions arrive. Ines had fallen asleep in the chair mid-threat, knife still in hand. Pip slept against her side like a barnacle. Ulyana stood at the window, watching the sky go grey over the rooftops, and I stood beside him with my stolen folios under my arm, feeling the weight of them like a second spine.

"I need to say something," I said, "and then I need to never say it again, because I intend to live as a hypocrite of action rather than a coward of reflection."

Ulyana waited.

"When I take these records back—or rather, when I don't take them back, when I simply let them be discovered missing, which will happen within the day, because I've arranged to be conspicuously absent from morning office—they will know it was me. Chancellor Maldive's inquisitors are many things, but sloppy with ledgers isn't one of them. I'll be questioned. Then I'll be tried by canon court, which will unfrock me. Then, depending on mood, they'll hand me to the secular courts, who will drown me in the harbor with everyone else who ever loved the wrong thing." I said it evenly, and discovered, saying it, that I meant it evenly. "I've known this since the third night in the archive. I did it anyway. I want you to understand that I did it anyway."

"Why?" Ulyana asked. And it was an honest question, not a rhetorical one—he genuinely could not see it, this man who had hidden so long he'd lost track of his own worth.

"Because eleven days ago I heard something come out of the king's mouth that recited my prayers back to me wrong. Every word correct. Every word *wrong*. Like hearing your mother's voice from a stranger's face." I looked out at the grey morning, at the bells of Saint Verane just beginning, far off, to mark the hour I was deliberately missing. "I gave my life to the Church because I believed it stood between the flock and the wolves. Somewhere along the way the Church became the wolf, and I stayed anyway, because staying is easy and priests are trained for it. Well. I'm done training." I turned to him. "I'm choosing a side, Ulyana Delacroix. It's yours. It's a small side, poorly armed, containing a smuggler, a mute-to-be, and a child who hears maps sing. But it's the right one, and I've waited thirty years to be sure of anything, and I'm sure of this."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he extended his hand, and I took it, and his grip was careful and warm and trembling slightly, and I thought: *there it is. That's the man worth a voice.*

"There's one more thing," I said, as I gathered my coat. "The rite requires preparation—the speaker must learn the full unbinding, and the text is long, and the dialect is dead. Six days is cruelly short. But there's someone in this room who's been absorbing the map's language in his sleep, and if half of what I suspect about that child is true—"

The door banged open.

Pip sat bolt upright, wide awake, eyes huge and fixed on nothing—or on something very far away, down deep, under the harbor. And in a voice that was not entirely Pip's own, thin and clear and rising, the child began to sing.

The hair on my arms stood up. Because I knew the melody. I had read its notation three nights ago in a sewn-shut folio, and it was the opening verse of the unbinding—and Pip was singing it perfectly, note for note, in a dialect dead for seventy years.

And from somewhere beneath us—from beneath the shop, beneath the street, beneath the whole sleeping city—something sang back.
