## 13. Pip's Song

The map sings in a language nobody taught me, and I would like it noted, for the record, that this is not my fault.

I'm telling you this first because when Ines finds out—and she will find out, because Ines finds out everything eventually, usually by hitting it with a boat hook—I want history to remember that I tried to be honest. Or at least that I planned to be honest. There's a difference, and the difference is about how long you get to keep your supper.

Here's how it started, or here's how it started as far as I can tell, because dreams don't come with beginnings, they just come with middles that feel like they've been going on since before you were born.

Three nights ago—no. Four. The night after the priest with the soft voice brought the stolen books and Ulyana went gray as old fish reading them. That night I dreamed of water.

Not scary water. Harbor water, green-brown, the kind with eelgrass waving in it like it's trying to tell you something important but keeps forgetting the words. And under the water there was a door. Not a door like a door. A door like a *hole shaped on purpose*, which is different, the way a smile is different from a wound even if they're in the same place.

And the door was humming.

I don't mean buzzing. I mean humming the way Gran used to hum when she was mending nets, low and steady, except the tune was one I'd never heard and somehow already knew every note of. It went through my chest like a second heartbeat. And a voice—not the humming, a voice, dry and patient, like paper turning pages—said:

*You hear it. Good. Few do, now.*

I woke up with salt on my lips even though I sleep three floors from the harbor and there's no salt anywhere near my bed unless Ines has been cooking, which is its own kind of nightmare.

That was four nights ago. Since then, every night, the dream comes back, and every night I learn another verse.

* * *

You should understand something about me: I've been listening to things other people can't hear since before I could talk proper. The bells of Veymar don't just ring, they *argue*. The tide has opinions. Old houses mutter in their sleep about the people who used to live in them. When I was small I told everyone about it, because I was small and stupid, and the grown-ups did what grown-ups in Veymar do when a child says something magical—they got very quiet and looked at each other over my head.

Mama fixed that fast. "Pip," she said, crouching down so her eyes were level with mine, and her voice was gentle but her hands were shaking, "you hear things. Fine. You hear them for *yourself*. You don't hear them out loud. Ever."

So I learned to keep my mouth shut about the world's secret noise, same as I learned to keep my sleeves rolled so pickpockets can't grab them, same as I learned which drains you can sleep in during rain and which ones flood. Veymar teaches children a lot of survival lessons. Most of them are versions of the same lesson: *be small, be quiet, be nothing.*

Then Mama died, and being nothing stopped feeling like a trick and started feeling like the whole job.

Anyway. My point—the point I'm circling like a gull over fish guts—is that hearing things is old news to me. What's new is *answering*.

Because last night, in the dream, after the third verse settled into me like a swallowed stone, I hummed it back.

I know. I know. Mama's voice in my head, *you don't hear them out loud.* But this wasn't out loud. This was underwater, in a dream, behind a door that was already humming. It felt safe the way a locked room feels safe right up until you notice the lock is on the outside.

I hummed the verse back.

And the thing under the harbor went quiet.

Not peaceful-quiet. The quiet of a cat that heard the can opener and stopped washing itself. Every bit of the dream held still—the eelgrass, the brown-green light, the slow drift of silt—and then, from somewhere much deeper than the door, something breathed in.

It sounded pleased.

I woke up sitting straight up in the cot with my heart going like a drum in a festival parade, and downstairs Ulyana's shop-floor creaked the way it does when he can't sleep and paces between the charm shelves, touching each one like he's counting soldiers. I lay there listening to him count his little magics and I thought: *he'd know. He knows songs. He's a swordspeaker, whatever that is, and the map sings swordspeech, and he could tell me if what answered me last night was bad-bad or just regular bad.*

But telling him means telling Ines, and telling Ines means admitting I've been singing to the map for two nights without saying anything, and Ines has a way of looking at you when you've kept a secret that makes you feel like a hole in a boat. So instead I did what any sensible orphan does with a problem too big to hold.

I sat on it until it grew teeth.

* * *

Which brings us to this morning, and the kitchen above the charm shop, and the exact moment everything went wrong in front of witnesses.

It's a good kitchen, Ines's kitchen. Small, crooked, always smells faintly of tar and tea. This morning it had all five of us crammed in it: Ines arms-crossed against the wall like the wall owed her money; Ulyana hunched over Cassian's stolen liturgical books with his finger tracing lines of dead script; Cassian himself stirring tea he wasn't drinking, wearing his plain gray clothes like a man who'd taken off a uniform and couldn't get comfortable in anything else; and me, eating bread, keeping my mouth shut about dreams, per policy.

Ulyana read aloud sometimes, translating the old words into trader-talk. "...the binding rite requires a voice of the first order, spoken within the drowned nave, upon the eve—" He stopped. Frowned at the page. Tapped it like the page was lying to him.

"What?" Ines said.

"It says the key must be *sung*." His frown deepened. "But the map isn't a song. Maps don't sing. It's a diagram, a route, a—" He trailed off, because I had stopped chewing.

I want to be clear: I hadn't decided to say anything. My mouth just went ahead of me, the way mouths do when they're attached to someone who spent four years learning that silence gets people disappeared. Because Ulyana stood there looking lost, and Cassian looked frightened, and Ines looked like grief wearing a person, and I thought, *they need the song and I have the song,* and out loud, before my brain could file an objection, I said:

"The third verse goes up at the end. Like a gull. You've all been reading it flat."

Five heartbeats of silence. I counted them. It's a habit; silences in Veymar are worth counting, because their length tells you whether you're about to be fed or arrested.

Ines moved first. She crossed the kitchen in three steps and crouched in front of me, and her eyes were doing the thing where they're hard as hulls but underneath there's water moving. "Pip," she said, very carefully, "what did you say?"

"The map." My mouth had clearly mutinied entirely; no point abandoning ship now. "It's not directions. Well—it's directions, but only if you sing it. It's a key. Keys aren't read. They turn." I took a breath. "I've been learning it. In dreams."

* * *

They didn't yell. I almost wish they had. Yelling is simple. What happened instead was worse: they all went careful, the way people go careful around a cracked mast, and Ulyana pulled out the map—that bloodstained fold of hide that killed Ines's brother getting it here—and unrolled it on the table between the teapot and the bread.

"Sing it," he said quietly. "Just the first verse. Quietly."

And here's the thing I should probably confess, since we're confessing: I sang more than the first verse. I sang the first, and when nothing exploded, I sang the second, and by the third I'd forgotten to be scared because the song is *beautiful*, that's the trap of it, it's beautiful the way the harbor is beautiful at dawn right before the work boats go out and men die hauling nets. The notes came up out of me like they'd been waiting years for permission, and the map—

The map woke up.

The ink-lines lifted off the hide. Not floating—*swimming*, silver threads rising a finger's width off the surface, braiding and unbraiding, drawing shapes in the air: a stair, a gate, a long descending passage, and at the bottom of it all a shape like a cathedral seen through water, blurred and glowing. The bloodstain at the map's edge flared red-gold and became, briefly, a word in the old script. Then everything sank back into the leather and lay still, and the kitchen smelled like low tide.

Nobody spoke. Cassian had both hands pressed flat on the table like the floor might pitch. Ulyana stared at the map with an expression I couldn't read, and I'm good at reading expressions, it's how you stay alive on the streets—he looked like a man seeing the ghost of something he loved.

"That was the key," he said finally, hoarse. "That's the rite's opening. Six days early, sung by a child who shouldn't know it." He turned to me, and his voice went gentle in the way that made my stomach drop, because gentle voices in my experience are preambles. "Pip. How long have you known the verses?"

"Four nights."

"Where did you learn them?"

"In the dream. Under the harbor. There's a door, and it hums, and—" I stopped. Here it was. The part I'd been sitting on. The teeth under the saddle, so to speak. "There's something else I should say."

Ines's hand found my shoulder. Heavy, warm, steadying. "Say it."

"Last night I sang a verse back to it. To the door." I made myself look at all of them, because Mama taught me you take your punishments facing forward. "And it heard me. And it answered."

* * *

The answering happened again while we were all still standing there deciding what to do with me—which, for the record, rude—and this time it didn't wait for a dream.

Cassian noticed first. He's a priest; noticing dread is his trade. He cocked his head like a bird, toward the window, toward the harbor beyond the rooftops, and said, "Do you hear that?"

I did. Of course I did. I always do. But this time the others heard it too, which meant it wasn't the world's usual secret noise, which meant it was real, which meant my night terrors had company.

A hum. Low, vast, patient, coming up through the floorboards, through the foundations, through the bones of Veymar itself. The same tune. My tune. The verses I'd been learning, played back to me from underneath the city—slowed down, deepened, dragged through fathoms of black water, but unmistakably the same song.

It knew the verses because I'd sung them. It was learning them *back*.

Ulyana went white around the mouth. "It's echoing her," he said. "No—it's not echoing. It's *counting*. It's checking the verses against something." He grabbed the map, rolled it, shoved it inside his coat. "The seal. The original binding. If the key is a song, then the lock is a song too, and every verse she sings—"

"—it can compare," Cassian finished, and his voice had gone thin. "Every verse tells it how much of the key exists. How close the door is to opening."

The hum swelled, just slightly. Just enough. Somewhere below us, under the harbor mud, under the drowned streets, something enormous shifted its weight, the way a sleeper turns over, the way a debt comes due.

And then, threaded through the hum, clear as a bell through fog, it did something new.

It sang the next verse. The fifth one. The one I hadn't learned yet.

I felt it land in me like a hook set in soft flesh—the missing piece, offered freely, *come and get it, little singer*—and my mouth actually started to open, my throat actually started to shape the first note, because the song wants completing, that's its nature, a half-song is an itch the whole world scratches—

Ines clamped both hands over my ears.

Her palms were rough and smelled of rope and salt, and they pressed the hum down to almost nothing, and I stood there in her grip breathing hard, the unfinished verse dying in my throat, my eyes stinging for reasons I decided not to investigate.

"Don't," she said. Not to me—to the floor, to the harbor, to the thing underneath. Her voice was low and absolutely level, which anyone who works the docks knows is scarier than shouting. "Whatever you are. Whatever you think you're offering. She doesn't take gifts from strangers."

The hum held for a long moment. Long enough for me to notice my own heartbeat slowing to match it, which I did not enjoy.

Then it faded—unhurried, unoffended, like a fisherman reeling in slowly because he knows exactly where the fish lives—and the kitchen was just a kitchen again, smelling of tea and tar, ordinary and small and suddenly the most precious place in Veymar.

Cassian sat down heavily. Ulyana braced both hands on the table and hung his head, and when he spoke it was barely above a whisper, and I only caught it because catching whispers is my whole profession.

"Six days," he said. "It's teaching itself the rest. And it's using her voice to do it."

Ines took her hands off my ears. She crouched down to my level again, and this time the hardness in her eyes was gone entirely, and what was underneath was so fierce and so sad that I had to look at the teapot for a second.

"Pip," she said. "From now on, when it sings—you come get me. Day or night. You wake me. You don't listen alone."

"It's better if I learn them first," I said, because apparently my mouth was still taking orders from the part of me that wanted to help, consequences be damned. "If I know the whole key before it does—"

"*Pip.*"

"I'm the only one who *can* learn them! Ulyana can't hear the verses right, he said so, and it likes me, it *chose* me—"

"And that," said Ulyana quietly, and the whole kitchen turned to him, "is exactly what frightens me."

He crossed to the window and looked out at the harbor, at the gray-green water lying innocent over all that dark, and I watched his reflection in the glass and saw a man doing arithmetic he hated.

"The Veil doesn't choose randomly," he said. "It chose a courier to carry the map. It chose a sister with smuggler's routes. It chose a doubting priest. And now it's chosen a child who hears songs." He turned around, and his face was terrible and tired. "It's not testing our defenses. It's assembling us. We're not following the map, Ines."

He laid his hand flat on his coat, over the hidden roll of hide, and beneath his palm, faint as a pulse, the map began—very softly—to hum.

"We're carrying it exactly where it wants to go."

Down in the harbor, far below the keels and the chains and the sleeping city, something began, patiently, to sing the fifth verse again.
