## 14. The Veil Offers

I have never been a good sleeper. Swordspeakers are trained to sleep light and wake fast, the way soldiers are trained to march and priests are trained to kneel—until it stops being training and becomes the shape of your bones. Seven years of hiding had only sharpened it. I slept like a man sleeping in a stranger's house, which, in a sense, I was: Veymar had never once felt like mine.

So when I dreamed that night—and I dreamed deeply, wholly, the way I hadn't since I was nineteen—I should have known immediately that something was wrong. Dreams don't take you by the hand. Dreams don't wait for you to get comfortable.

This one did both.

* * *

I was back at the chapterhouse of Saint Verene's, the one that burned—or didn't burn; history is written by whoever survives to hold the pen, and none of us survived, so the pen went to the crown. The courtyard where I'd learned my first words of swordspeech was exactly as I remembered it: flagstones worn smooth by two hundred years of boots, the plum tree Master Serafina had planted the year I arrived, its branches heavy with fruit that never quite ripened because she liked them tart. The bell tower stood against a sky the color of unpolished silver. Somewhere, water dripped. That detail came later, I think—the harbor, the cathedral—but dreams don't care about chronology any more than they care about mercy.

Master Serafina was standing under the plum tree.

She looked the way she had looked on the last morning: gray braid coiled at her nape, ink stains on her fingers because she still wrote out every lesson by hand even though she could have spoken them into stone, that expression she wore when she was about to tell you something you didn't want to hear—a kind of patient sorrow, like weather waiting to happen.

"You're dreaming," I said. It seemed important to establish that early. "You're dead. And I'm dreaming."

"Am I?" she said, and smiled, and it was her smile, the exact one, the one that had made twelve-year-old me feel like the most promising student in a generation and twenty-five-year-old me feel like a disappointment with excellent posture. "You always did argue with everything, Ulyana. Even grief."

"I earned the right."

"You did." She picked a plum. Turned it over in her ink-stained fingers. "You've been carrying the map for eleven days now. You've barely slept. Your hands shake when you think no one is watching. And you've started saying things aloud in the shop when you're alone—little phrases, testing them, the way you used to test your edges against whetstones." She looked up. "You were always going to come back to it. I only wanted to see if I could make it easier."

The dream was very good. That's what frightened me most, in the part of me that was still awake enough to be frightened. Dreams are sloppy things, stitched from whatever your mind has lying around. This one had the smell of the courtyard after rain—wet stone and plum leaves and the faint iron tang of the practice yard. Dreams don't do smell. Not like this. Not unless something else is doing the dreaming for you.

"You're not her," I said.

"No," said the thing wearing my master's face, gently, the way you'd agree with a child who'd worked out that the man in red at midwinter isn't actually a saint. "But I remember her better than you do. I was there, Ulyana. Underneath. Listening. For seven years I have listened to this city through a hundred ears, and yours were among the ones I liked best. You hum when you work. Did you know? Old training songs. You think you've been silent all this time." The plum turned in her fingers. "You've been singing the whole while. Quietly. To yourself. Like a widow talking to an empty chair."

I woke—no. I tried to wake. I got as far as the edge of it, that thin place where the dream thins into gauze and you can feel your own body waiting on the other side, and then the courtyard folded itself around me again, patient as a net.

"Not yet," Serafina said—not Serafina, but the voice coming through her was almost tender. "Please. I've waited seven years to talk to you properly. Give me until the plums ripen."

In the dream, the plums were already softening toward dark.

* * *

We walked. That's what the dream wanted, I think—to walk with me, side by side, the way we used to walk the circuit of the walls at Saint Verene's when a lesson needed more air than a classroom allowed. My feet knew the path without being told. That was the cruelest part. Seven years of forgetting, and my feet remembered everything.

"You hate this city," the Veil said conversationally, in her voice. "I don't blame you. It's a hard city. It drowns people for selling luck-knots. But look at what it was before."

The courtyard changed. Or rather, we walked out of it, and the streets beyond were not Veymar as I knew it. They were Veymar as the histories painted it, before the Binding, before the inquisitors, before magic became a crime punishable by water. Market stalls where charms hung openly from strings like drying fish. A woman at a fountain healing a child's scraped knee with two words and a touch, and nobody stopping to stare, because it was Tuesday. Two swordspeakers crossing a square in their gray-and-silver, and a baker waving to them like neighbors, because they were.

"This is what your order built," the Veil said. "Two hundred years of it. And then the war came, and the oaths broke, and the killing started—magic against magic, city against city, and the children died first, they always die first—and so your order did the only merciful thing left. They bound me. They bound the king to me, and me to silence, and the war ended inside a season." She paused at the fountain, watching the memory-woman heal the memory-child. "Seven years ago, when the seal weakened and I began to wake, your master came to renew it. She failed. She stayed anyway. And the king's men purged your order to keep anyone else from finishing what she couldn't."

"That's not how I remember it."

"No," the Veil agreed. "You remember running."

There it was. No cruelty in it—that was the terrible thing. It said it the way Serafina would have said it, plainly, without flinching, because love doesn't flinch and neither does truth.

"I remember being ordered to run," I said. "She told us silence was the last duty. Scatter, hide, live. That was the mission."

"And you've done it beautifully. Seven years. Do you know how few men can erase themselves that thoroughly? Most people can't stop being themselves for an afternoon." We walked on. The market smells followed us—bread, tar, plums. "Here is what I want to show you next. This is the part I need you to see."

* * *

It showed me Veymar as it was now—but seen from underneath, the way it must look to something living beneath the harbor. And here is the confession I will carry longer than any other: it was beautiful.

Every small illegal charm in the night market glowed like an ember. Every whispered ward over a doorway, every smuggler's luck-sign scratched under a dock, every grandmother's knot tied wrong-on-purpose to keep the fever away—all of it fed down through the stones like rain finding a river, and the river ran to the drowned cathedral, and the cathedral glowed like a lantern held underwater. The Veil drank it, and grew, and spread its roots through the foundations of the palace, and the king sat above it all like a cork in a bottle, keeping the pressure contained.

"If you break the seal," the Veil said, "all of this releases at once. Do you understand what that means? I am not cruel, Ulyana. I am old, and I am hungry, and I have been fed carefully for a century so that I would stay asleep. Wake me fully and I will not be careful anymore. The city drowns people for magic. Let me show you what drowning really looks like, at scale."

And it showed me. Water in the low streets. The harbor climbing the seawall. Inquisitors' boats overturned, useless, because you cannot arrest a flood. Pip's face somewhere in the crowd, mouth open, no sound coming out. Ines—

I pulled back. The dream let me pull back, which I understood, dimly, was itself a kindness, or the appearance of one. Kindness is a tool like any other. I'd sold enough charm-work to know that the best lies are mostly true.

"You're lying," I said. "Or you're telling the truth in a way designed to break me. Either way."

"I have never lied to you," the Veil said, and there was something almost wounded in it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "Your order lied to you. Your master lied to you—she sent you away and called it duty. The king lies to his city every morning from behind his face. I am the only one in this story who has told you the truth: I am hungry, I am bound, and if you free me, people die. That is the truth. Now let me show you the other truth."

* * *

The second vision was smaller. That's how I knew it was the dangerous one.

A room. Not grand—a workroom, stone-floored, lamplit, with a long table scarred by generations of knife-work and ink. Students at it. Twelve of them, maybe fourteen, young, arguing about declensions of the old speech, laughing the way students laugh when they think no master is listening. At the head of the table, writing out a lesson by hand despite having a perfectly good voice to speak it with: Master Serafina. Alive. Ink-stained. Tart plums in a bowl beside her, deliberately underripe.

"The purge never happened," the Veil said quietly. "That is what I offer. Not a lie—I do not lie. A restoration. Your order rebuilt, in this city, openly, under my protection. Magic legal again, taught again, because a city that feeds me willingly needs no inquisitors, no drownings, no fear. Your exile undone—you could walk into Veymar market tomorrow and sell a real charm and be thanked for it. And her—" the dream-Serafina looked up from her lesson, met my eyes across fourteen years and one death, and smiled— "her, returned. I kept what I took. I keep everything. Ask me, and I will give her back to you."

The room smelled of lamp oil and paper and the particular chalk-dust sweetness of a schoolroom in winter. One of the students—Pip, older, robed, laughing at something—looked directly at me and waved.

"You can't do that," I said. My voice came out wrong. Thin. "The dead don't come back. Whatever you are, whatever you ate down there in the dark for a century, you can't—"

"I held the king for sixty years," the Veil said. "Do you imagine I spent that time doing nothing? I am a loom, Ulyana. Everything that passes through me, I can weave again. It would cost me nothing. It would cost you nothing. That is the whole of the bargain, and it is so small you should be ashamed of considering it at length."

"Which is?"

"Stop." The word landed in the dream like a stone in still water. "Don't come to the cathedral. Don't sing the map. Don't break the seal. Go on as you have gone on—hidden, careful, humming your little songs where no one hears. Keep the shop. Keep the girl and the child and the priest, if you like; they are good for you, and I bear them no ill will as long as they point you nowhere. Simply stop. Walk away. And in exchange: the order restored, the exile ended, the dead returned, the city safe from the flood. Everyone lives. Everyone comes home. All it costs is the same thing it has cost you for seven years already."

The dream-Serafina rose from the table and crossed the room and stood in front of me, close enough that I could see the gray in her braid, the ink on her knuckles, the small scar on her chin I'd never learned the story of because she died before I thought to ask.

"You were so tired," she said, in her voice, with her gentleness. "You are so tired. Seven years of being no one. Wouldn't it be enough, just once, to be someone again? To be *my* student again, in a world where that means something? I'm not asking you to fight. I'm asking you to rest."

And God forgive me—and I mean that literally, because Cassian would insist on the terminology—I considered it.

Not for a heartbeat. For a long, warm, shameful stretch of dream-time, I stood in that lamplit room and let myself want it. I let myself imagine walking into the night market with silver at my collar instead of ash. I imagined teaching. I imagined her voice saying *well done* and meaning it. I imagined putting down the weight I had carried so long I'd forgotten it was a weight and not just my spine.

The plums in the bowl ripened while I watched. That was the trick of it, I saw later. The dream moved whether I consented or not. Time passed in it, sweet and easy, and every moment I stood still was a moment the choice made itself.

* * *

What broke it wasn't courage. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of my recent life. What broke it was a detail.

The student who waved at me—the older Pip, robed, laughing—had a scar. A fresh one, along the forearm, the kind you get from an inquisitor's hook-chain. And the dream-Pip laughed anyway, and the dream-Ines bandaged it anyway, and the dream-Cassian prayed over it anyway, and I realized, standing in that perfect lamplit room, that the Veil had built me a paradise and hadn't been able to help including the wound. Because the wound was true. Because the Veil only knows what it has watched, and what it has watched is us bleeding and continuing.

The real Pip didn't laugh at wounds. The real Pip flinched, and went quiet, and watched the door for three days after. The real Ines didn't bandage gently; she bandaged like she was angry at the injury personally. The real Cassian prayed like a man arguing. The dream had gotten them *wrong*, softly, kindly wrong, polished into types instead of people—and that polish was where I found the seam, and the seam was where I put my fingers, and I tore.

"You don't know them," I said. "You've listened for seven years and you don't know them at all."

"They are yours," the Veil said, and for the first time the voice bent—not Serafina's, not anything's, something vast and patient and suddenly, genuinely puzzled. "I gave them to you as you love them."

"You gave me what I *miss*. That's different. That's a portrait, not a person." I looked at dream-Serafina—at the thing wearing her patience, her sorrow, her love, because it had watched her love me and learned the shape of it the way a mimic learns a birdcall. "And you gave me what I miss about her, too. But my master wouldn't have offered me this bargain. My master died kneeling at an altar holding a door shut so I could run. If she were here, she'd tell me to go to the cathedral. She'd tell me silence was never the last duty. Love was."

The courtyard came back. The plum tree. The silver sky. The thing that had worn my master's face stood beneath the tree with the fruit going dark overhead, and it regarded me for a long moment with an expression I will spend the rest of my life trying not to describe, because describing it makes it real: it looked *hurt*.

"She stayed," the Veil said at last. "She stayed and she starved me and she suffered, and for what? You ran, and hid, and sold luck-knots, and hated yourself, and the seal weakens regardless. Nothing she bought with her life has held. Come to the cathedral and you will only add your voice to hers. Stay away, and everything I showed you can still be true." The plums dropped, one by one, soft as bruises. "Six days, swordspeaker. The festival renews the binding either way—with your help or without it. Without it, the harvest continues. Children, Ulyana. It has always been children. Think about whose silence that serves."

The dream began to fold, edges first, like a letter being closed.

"One more thing," it said, from everywhere, in no one's voice now. "Tell no one. Not the dockwoman, not the priest, not the child. If you speak of this dream to any of them, the offer dies, and what remains is only the flood. Some gifts can only be given in secret. You of all people understand secrecy."

* * *

I woke in the back room of the shop with dawn graying the shutters and my heart going like a smith's hammer and the taste of plums in my mouth, which is not a thing a mouth should do.

For a long time I lay still and took inventory. Hands: shaking. Voice: present, regrettably. Map: under the floorboard, silent, or pretending to be. The dream clung to me like harbor fog. That was the worst of it—not the visions, not even the offer, but the residue. The warmth of it. Somewhere under my ribs there was still a room with lamplight and students in it, and my master alive at the head of the table, and a version of me who had never run, and I could feel that room wanting to be believed in.

*If he simply stops.*

It had dressed the cage in her face and called it rest. And the truly obscene thing, the thing I sat up in the dark chewing on like a bone, was that six days ago I would have taken the bargain without needing the dream at all. Hiding had been my answer for seven years. The Veil hadn't offered me anything new. It had offered me permission.

I got up. I lit the lamp. I took out the false papers—three sets, three names, seven years of careful nothing—and I laid them on the table and looked at them for a long time.

Then I heard the floorboard creak in the front of the shop, and Ines's voice, low and rough with sleeplessness: "Ulyana. You're burning something. Why are you burning something at dawn?"

Because I hadn't decided to. My hands had. There was a candle lit, and the first set of papers—*Marek Voss, licensed charm-seller, resident of the Tanner's Ward*—was already curling at its corner, my name-in-exile going up in honest fire, and I stood there watching it with no memory of striking the match.

"Ines," I said, and my voice came out hoarse and strange, like a man speaking after a long illness. "Sit down. There's something I have to tell you, and I've been told not to."
