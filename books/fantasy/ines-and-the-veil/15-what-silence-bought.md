## 15. What Silence Bought

*Ulyana*

The dream did not end when I woke.

That was the first sign something had gone badly wrong, because dreams are supposed to have the decency to dissolve like sugar in rain, leaving nothing but a vague sweetness and the sense you've forgotten something important. This one stayed. It sat in the corner of my room like a guest who'd arrived early and made itself comfortable, and it kept talking.

*I can give them back to you.*

I lay in my cot above the shop and stared at the ceiling beam and tried very hard to think about anything else. The price of bread. The loose shutter that needed fixing. The fact that Ines snored like a dock crane taking on cargo, which was audible even through two walls, and which I would never say aloud because she'd broken my nose once already over a lesser insult.

*Your master. Your brothers and sisters of the blade. The halls of Corvenne refilled with light. Seven years unwritten, as though they never happened.*

"It happened," I said to the ceiling. My voice came out rusted. "They're dead. You don't get to sell me back my own grief at a discount."

The presence didn't argue. That was the worst part. It simply *showed* me, the way a merchant lays out wares: Master Serafina at the head of the training hall, gray braid swinging, calling corrections across the floor in that voice like a whetstone on steel. The novices laughing. The smell of oil and cedar. Me, twenty-three years old and whole, magic running through me like blood through a body that had forgotten what circulation felt like.

And beneath it all, threaded through every image like wire through fruit: *peace*. Seven years without war. Seven years in which Veymar's children grew up not knowing the sound of siege bells. All of it bought with my silence, and all of it still standing, still real, still worth something — if only I didn't ruin it now by getting loud.

I got up. I dressed. I did not answer it, which I told myself was not the same as listening.

It was still dark when I went down into the shop, and I will be honest, because honesty is apparently the discipline of this entire miserable season of my life: I took out the false papers.

There were three sets. Ulyana Delacroix, dead seven years, officially drowned in the harbor incident — the purge's tidy fiction, since drowned men couldn't be interrogated. Then Tomas Wren, charm-seller, licensed, dull, the man who had sold luck-knots and weather-charms for nearly a decade without once making one actually work. And a third set, half-finished, for a name I'd been too cowardly or too tired to adopt: a man called Merric Hale, bound for the southern coast, where the Veil's reach thinned and a swordspeaker could maybe, possibly, grow old.

I laid them on the counter. The map sat beside them, folded into its leather case, humming its low patient note that only I and a nine-year-old orphan could hear. Even folded, even silent-seeming, it sounded like accusation.

"You knew him," I said to it. To Ines's brother, really, though he was ash by now or worse. "You knew what I was, and you walked across half the city bleeding out to put this in my hands. Why? There were easier men to die in front of."

The map hummed. Maps are terrible conversationalists.

Here is what the Veil had offered, in the language of dreams where everything arrives pre-justified: stop. Walk away. Take Merric Hale south. Live. And in exchange — no, not in exchange, it had said, *as a gift*, which is how devils and merchants both make you feel the knife is your idea — the order restored. Serafina alive. The students scattered seven years ago gathered home like sheep. Everything I had mourned, un-mourned.

It had even shown me Pip, grown, apprenticed, safe, under a banner that read *the new order*, and I had woken with tears on my face and hatred in my mouth, because I knew — I *knew* — that the children the Veil harvested at the Binding Festival would also appear in such a dream, if I asked nicely. Everything it showed me was true. That's the trick of it. Lies are cheap; the Veil dealt only in truths arranged like lies.

I picked up Tomas Wren's papers. Good forgery. I'd paid a man named Quill far too much for them, and Quill had earned it.

All I had to do was leave before dawn, before Ines woke, before Cassian returned from the palace archives, before Pip sang another verse and something under the harbor sang back. Leave the map. Leave the girl with her brother's ghost and the child with the singing in her skull. Let someone else be the last swordspeaker. Let someone else break an oath that had been breaking the city for a century.

My hand was steady. That frightened me more than trembling would have. Seven years of practice at being no one, and it turned out the skill transferred: I could erase myself out of this story as cleanly as I'd erased myself out of Veymar.

"Ulyana."

I didn't jump. I want that recorded somewhere. A woman standing in a dark doorway behind me, having moved through a shop I ward against exactly that, and I did not jump, because some animal part of me had heard her breathing ten seconds ago and decided, wisely, to pretend otherwise.

Ines stood in the kitchen doorway with a lamp unlit in her hand and her hair tied back for sleep, and she looked at the papers on the counter, and then at me, and her expression did the thing it always did — the thing where grief and fury share a face so completely you can't tell which one is driving.

"Packing light," she said.

"Ines—"

"Don't." She crossed the room and set the lamp down, unlit, deliberately, so that we'd do this in the dark like honest people. "Don't explain. I watched you sleep-walk down those stairs twenty minutes ago like a man going to his own funeral, and I thought, well. Either the Veil's got him or he's got himself, and either way I should put the kettle on. But there's no time for tea, so here's where we are." She planted both hands on the counter, on either side of the false papers, caging them. "You're leaving."

"I'm sparing everyone," I said, and heard how it sounded even as it left me.

"Sparing." She laughed, one syllable, no humor in it, a sound like a door slamming. "You know what sparing looks like? Sparing looks like my brother, six months ago, writing letters to every contact he had in this city asking one question — *who knows about magic, who can help, who isn't afraid* — and getting back nothing. Nothing, Ulyana. Do you know why?"

I said nothing. She took it as permission, which it was.

"Because the last person who could have helped was selling luck-knots under a dead man's name and had spent seven years teaching himself not to hear the knocking." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Ines Delacroix-adjacent rage — wait, no, that's wrong, her name was just Ines, but the point stands — her voice never rose. It got quieter and heavier, like water finding the crack in a hull. "Tam wrote to forty people. Forty. And the one man in Veymar who could have done something was *you*, and you were hiding so well that a dying courier had to fall through your door for the truth to find you, and even then—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, lower. "Even then you hid the body and almost burned the map."

"The map was a death sentence," I said. "It still is. Everything we've done since the night Tam died has painted a mark on all of us, and the Veil is offering me a way to take the mark off—"

"At what price?"

"Nothing that matters."

She went very still. "Say that again."

I should have lied. I was good at lying; I'd had practice. But she was looking at me in the dark with her brother's stubbornness written all over her, and the dream-guest in the corner of the room was murmuring *yes, tell her nothing matters, tell her they were always going to die, tell yourself whatever gets you out the door* — and I found I couldn't. Not to her. Not anymore.

"My voice," I said. "My magic. Permanently. And in exchange the order lives again, my master lives again, the war stays buried, and none of you ever have to see me again." I spread my hands. "It's not nothing, Ines. It's the best offer anyone's made me in seven years."

For a long moment she just looked at me. Then she reached across the counter, picked up Tomas Wren's papers, and held them up between two fingers like something she'd scraped off her boot.

"This," she said, "is a man who let a boy drown in the canal district because stopping to pull him out would've meant being seen. Did the dream show you that part? Did it show you the ones your silence already cost?" She dropped the papers. "Because I can. I keep a list. It started the night my brother died reaching for a stranger, and it hasn't stopped growing, and every name on it is a person who needed *someone* and found nobody, because the somebody was busy being nobody on purpose."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Fairness left this city a long time ago; we're just the cleanup crew." She leaned in. "You want to know what silence bought, Ulyana? I'll tell you, since you're shopping for it. Silence bought my brother dying alone on a stranger's floorboards. Silence bought Pip watching friends vanish off the streets while the whole city pretended the fog ate them. Silence bought a king rotting from the inside for thirty years while priests heard him scream in tongues and filed it under *piety*. Silence bought seven years of *nothing*, and now the thing that profited from all that nothing is in your head offering you a refund, and you're actually considering it, because it wrapped the bill in your master's face."

Every word landed. That was the problem with Ines — she didn't fight like a brawler, she fought like a surgeon, and she'd clearly spent the night identifying exactly which organs to cut.

"She'd be ashamed of me," I said quietly. "Serafina. If she saw this."

"Probably," Ines said. "But she's dead, and I'm not, and I'm telling you something better than shame." She straightened up and looked at me the way you look at a man standing on a ledge — not with pity, but with the flat expectation that he'll step back from it. "Hiding didn't save anyone. Not you, not Tam, not that boy in the canal. It just meant that when the bad thing came, there was no one *there*. That's all hiding ever does, Ulyana. It guarantees an empty room."

The dream-guest stirred. *She doesn't understand the arithmetic,* it whispered. *One man against a century of binding. Sentiment is not strategy.* And it was right, that was the awful thing, it was *right* — the odds hadn't changed, the plan hadn't changed, six days until the Binding Festival and a cathedral full of drowning-dark below the palace, and every rational ledger said run.

But I looked down at the papers on the counter — Tomas Wren, Merric Hale, and poor drowned Ulyana Delacroix, three men I had built to live in so I wouldn't have to — and I thought about Tam's blood soaking into my floorboards, and Pip's voice repeating swordspeech it had learned in dreams, and Cassian walking out of the palace with stolen scripture and a smile like a man stepping off a cliff on purpose, and Ines standing in front of me at four in the morning because she'd heard me get up and *followed*, because that's what she did, that was the whole of her: she followed people into the dark so they wouldn't be alone in it.

Seven years of careful invisibility, and it had taken this ragged, furious, grieving woman four days to see me more clearly than anyone ever had.

"Give me the lamp," I said.

She handed it over without a word. I lit it. The flame caught, small and ordinary, and threw our shadows huge against the shelves of harmless charms.

I took the papers to the stove. There was still banked fire in it from the evening — I'd kept the shop warm the way I kept everything, low and controlled and barely alive — and I fed the coals until they glowed, and then I held Tomas Wren over them and watched the license curl and blacken. Then Merric Hale, the man I might have been, gone in a breath of orange. Then the death certificate of Ulyana Delacroix, the official lie, and I held that one longest, because burning it felt less like destroying a document and more like signing one.

"There," I said, when the last scrap was ash. "Ulyana Delacroix, officially alive, and about to be extremely inconvenient."

Ines studied me for a moment. Then, because she was who she was, she didn't hug me or praise me or do any of the things a kinder story would demand. She jerked her chin at the map case on the counter.

"Good. Because Pip woke up an hour ago singing, and something under the harbor sang back loud enough to rattle the windows, and Cassian's due back from the archives before dawn with whatever he stole, and the festival's in five days." She was already moving toward the stairs, pulling her coat off its hook. "So whatever epiphany you're having, have it walking."

I took one last look at the stove, at the ashes of three lifetimes of hiding, and felt — not light, exactly. Lighter. The difference between drowning and swimming, maybe. Both of them mean you're in over your head.

I picked up the map. It hummed against my palm, warm now, urgent, like a heart that had finally been given permission to beat properly.

"Ines," I said, following her. "What was Pip singing?"

She paused at the top of the stairs, and the look she gave me over her shoulder made my stomach drop through the floorboards.

"The unbinding," she said. "Pip says it's the first verse. And Pip says something answered — and this time, it used *your* name."

*Ines*

He burned the papers. I'll give him that. I'd stood in that doorway with a speech loaded and ready — I'd been composing it since the night Tam died, honestly, all those pieces waiting for a target — and he'd taken maybe a quarter of it before folding like wet paper, which was either courage or exhaustion, and with Ulyana it was usually both.

But I'll tell you what I didn't say, because I'm not built for saying it: when I came down those stairs and saw him standing over the false names with that look on his face, my first feeling wasn't anger. It was the cold drop, the harbor-water feeling, the one I'd had at Tam's funeral-that-wasn't, standing over an empty coffin because there wasn't a body to put in it. The feeling of *again*. Of another person deciding to be absent.

Tam had died reaching for a stranger, and the stranger had almost reached back and then thought better of it, and I had stood in the dark of my own kitchen for six months knowing that shape of loneliness from the inside. So yes, I cut him deep. Yes, I listed the dead. But underneath all of it, what I meant — what I'd have said, if I were the sort of person who said things like that — was simply: *don't you dare make me stand in another empty room.*

He didn't. He lit the stove instead.

Now he came up the stairs two at a time with the map in his fist, wearing his own name for the first time since I'd known him, and asked the right question, which was more than I'd managed in my whole speech.

"Pip's in the back room," I said. "Cassian's not back yet, and I don't like it. He was supposed to be an hour, and palace priests don't get lost between a library and a door." I kept my voice level, but Ulyana heard the seam in it anyway, because he was learning to listen, which was either progress or a nuisance. "And Pip won't stop singing. I tried covering the kid's mouth — gently," I added, at his look, "and Pip just harmonized around my hands. Whatever's answering from under the harbor, it's getting louder each verse, and Ulyana—" I stopped at the back-room door, hand on the frame. "It knows the words. All of them. Better than Pip does. Like it's been waiting for someone to start the song so it could finish."

Ulyana went pale in the lamplight. Then he set his jaw in a way I was starting to recognize — the look of a man picking up a weight he'd sworn off carrying — and pushed the door open.

Pip sat cross-legged on the cot, eyes closed, small hands moving in the air like a conductor's, and the sound coming out of that little body was not a child's voice. It was old and layered and resonant, swordspeech in its pure form, the language my grandmother had whispered over her maps and I'd never understood until this week — and beneath it, rising up through the floorboards, through the foundations, through seven years of my brother's unanswered letters, something vast was singing the next line.

Ulyana stepped into the room and said, in a voice that cracked down the middle, the phrase every swordspeaker learns first:

*"I am here."*

The singing under the harbor stopped.

Then, soft as a tide turning, it began again — and this time, it was saying his name.
