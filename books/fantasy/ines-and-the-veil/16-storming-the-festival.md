## 16. Storming the Festival

**Ulyana**

The Binding Festival began, as all of Veymar's worst ideas did, with bells.

They rang from every spire in the city at dawn—a sound that should have been joyful and instead landed on my ears like a verdict. Seven years I'd lived under those bells for ordinary feast days, and seven years I'd learned to tell the difference. These were the deep ones, the ones cast before the purge, before the Veil had taught the founders of this city to fear a child's candle-spell. The old bells remembered what the festival was actually for.

Renewal. A century more of the thing wearing King Aldric like a coat.

I stood at the window of Cassian's borrowed rooms in the temple district, watching lanterns go up along the processional route, and tried very hard not to think about how many of those lanterns were lit by children who would be harvested tonight.

"You're doing the face," Ines said behind me.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're composing your own eulogy and finding it tedious."

"I'm a charm-seller, Ines. Tedium is my native tongue." I turned. She was lacing her boots with the quick, brutal efficiency of a woman who'd spent twenty years on the docks and expected at any moment to need to run, fight, or swim. Her brother's courier satchel hung across her chest—empty now except for the map, which we no longer needed. Pip's song had finished what the map started. We knew the way down.

We just didn't know the way back.

"Say it again," she said. "The plan. All of it. If you say it wrong I'll know you're lying to yourself about some part of it."

"The plan." I held up fingers. "Cassian triggers the diversion at the western colonnade during the Rite of Unveiling—fire, panic, a false relic, whatever he can manage without getting himself burned at his own altar. You go through the servants' galleries to the nursery wing where they're holding the taken children, and you get Pip out. I go down. Through the harbor gate, along the map's road, into the cathedral."

"And?"

"And I unbind the king."

"And?" she pressed, arms crossed.

I looked at her. She knew. Of course she knew; I'd confessed it all weeks ago with her blood on my hands—the price written into the original oath, the voice and the magic torn out together, the swordspeaker who breaks the binding left hollow as a cracked bell. She wanted me to say it anyway. That was Ines. She believed that things said aloud became real, and that real things could be fought.

"I lose everything I've spent seven years protecting," I said quietly. "And it will have been worth it."

"Better." She slung the satchel higher on her shoulder. "Now one correction. You don't *lose* anything. You spend it. There's a difference. Losing is something that happens to you. Spending is something you choose."

"When did you become a philosopher?"

"Docks'll do that to you. Lots of time to think between crates." But her eyes were wet, and she turned away fast, pretending to check the window latch. "Pip's going to ask for you. When I find them. They always do—you, or the map-song. Mostly you."

"Tell them I'm coming up after."

"Liar," she said softly. "Tell them yourself. Alive. That's an order from your employer."

I wasn't her employer. I hadn't been since the night I'd stitched her wound and told her the whole rotten truth of my order. Somewhere between triage and dawn she had quietly annexed me into her family, the way she annexed everything—without asking permission and without any intention of giving it back.

The bells stopped. Below, the first procession turned onto the processional way: white-robed inquisitors, their hoods back for once, faces painted with the silver ash the Veil favored. Behind them came the choir, and behind the choir—

Behind the choir walked the king.

Even from four floors up I felt it. The pressure of him, like a change in weather, like the moment before lightning when the air forgets how to breathe. Aldric moved with the slow grace of something that had practiced being human for so long it had almost gotten good at it. Almost. As he passed beneath a lantern, his shadow moved a half-step ahead of him, and then caught up, ashamed.

"That's our king," Ines said flatly. "That's the man we're saving."

"He didn't choose it," I said. "None of us chose what was done to us. Some of us just got better masks."

She grunted—an agreement, in dockworker. Then she pulled her hood up, kissed two fingers, and pressed them briefly to the empty satchel where her brother's letters used to live. For Ines. For the dead courier who had died reaching my door so that someone, anyone, would finally stop hiding.

"For Ines," I echoed.

* * *

**Ines**

The servants' galleries smelled like tallow and old bread, and I hated every corridor of the palace on principle. Rich places were all the same. Everything polished except the people carrying the polish.

I moved the way Da had taught me before he drowned—stay in the service rhythm, carry something, look bored. Boredom was invisible. Nobody in history had ever stopped a woman carrying a tray because she looked bored. I'd stolen a tray and a livery apron off a hook two floors down, and now I was the most forgettable person in Veymar.

Except the palace wasn't forgetting anybody today. The halls were thick with inquisitors, and worse, with that silver-ash smell underneath the tallow—like coins rubbed on a corpse. Every time I passed a knot of white robes I thought of Pip's small body pressed against mine two nights ago, shaking, whispering: *they came for the ones who hear it, Ines, they keep lists.*

Lists. I had a copy of one. Cassian had lifted it from the Rite offices, and I'd read Pip's name in a clerk's tidy hand—*subject twelve, auditory affinity, holding: nursery gallery east.* Subject twelve. My Pip, who liked their eggs burnt and hummed when they were scared, reduced to a number in a ledger.

I put the rage somewhere I could carry it. Rage was cargo too. You just had to balance the load.

The nursery gallery east was behind a door with a new lock and old hinges, and the hinges were the problem. New locks meant new money spent on keeping children in, but nobody had spent money on oiling the door, because nobody imagined a dockworker with a tray. I set the tray down, worked a thin pry-blade from my sleeve—smuggler's kit, grandmother's design—and eased the pin loose with the patience of twenty years of quiet doors.

Inside: cots. Twelve of them. Small shapes under blankets, breathing in that particular shallow way children breathe when they're pretending to sleep because sleeping is safer than being awake here.

"Pip," I breathed. "It's me."

A shape sat bolt upright. In the dark, Pip's eyes found me faster than any child's should have—they saw in the dark a little, our Pip, another gift on the list. They were across the room and into my chest before I could get my arms ready, small fists knotted in my apron, whole body trembling like a mooring line in weather.

"You came," Pip whispered into my ribs. "You came, you came—"

"Told you. Family finds family. It's the whole job." I checked the room. Eleven other children, watching me now with the flat, careful eyes of kids who'd learned hope was expensive. "All right, listen. Can all of you walk?"

A small girl near the window shook her head. "They gave us the tea," she said. "For the rite. Legs don't work right."

The tea. The harvesting draft. Something in my chest went cold and clean. "Then we carry," I said. "Pip, you're my eyes. Any white robes, you squeeze twice. Everyone else, hold on to whoever's next to you and don't make a sound unless the sound is praying, and if it's praying, pray quiet."

I got the smallest girl on my back, took Pip's hand, and opened the door onto the gallery—

Where the bells began to scream.

Not ring. Scream. Cassian's diversion, early, enormous, glorious. From the west colonnade came light like a second sunrise and a sound like the sky tearing, and every inquisitor in earshot turned toward it, and I turned the other way, and we ran.

* * *

**Cassian**

I had served the Church of the Unveiled Face for thirty-one years, and in all that time I had never once committed arson.

There is a certain freedom, I confess, in doing it for the first time at fifty-six. The reliquary fire I had built in the western colonnade was a masterpiece of heresy: a false relic of Saint Verene, soaked in lamp oil and salt-petre, rigged to blaze green—the color of true miracles, according to a text I had falsified myself three days ago. When it ignited, half the festival crowd fell to its knees and the other half fell over each other trying to flee, and the inquisitors, whose theology had no category whatsoever for a genuine-looking miracle occurring without authorization, did what bureaucracies do.

They formed a committee.

Well. They formed a perimeter and argued about jurisdiction, which is the same thing with more shouting. And in the heart of that beautiful confusion, I stood on the colonnade steps in my full vestments, arms raised, crying in a voice trained by decades of homiletics:

*"THE SAINT IS COME! THE VEIL'S LIE IS UNDONE! BEHOLD—"*

I will not record the rest of the sermon. It was magnificent and entirely fabricated, and if I burn in hell for it, I shall consider the trade fair, because every eye in the western quarter was on me, and every inquisitor's boot was pointed at my heresy instead of at a smuggler carrying children through the eastern galleries, or at a charm-seller walking calmly toward the harbor gate with murder and mercy both folded inside his coat.

They took me, of course. That was always part of the arithmetic. Four of them reached the steps as the false relic guttered out, and I did not resist, because a defector priest struggling is a story, and a defector priest kneeling peacefully with his arms open is a *symbol*, and symbols are harder to make disappear quietly.

"Brother Cassian," said the senior inquisitor, and there was grief in his voice, which surprised me. Ossian had been a novice beside me once, before the Veil's silver got into him. "Do you know what happens to you now?"

"I have a general idea," I said. "I've read the older rites. The ones from before the Veil edited them."

He flinched. Good. Somewhere under the ash, Ossian could still flinch.

"Take him," he said, not looking at me. And as they bound my wrists I bowed my head—not in shame, but in the old attitude of prayer, the one that lets you watch the ground—and whispered the words I had promised to deliver, the signal arranged with Ulyana in the grammar of our conspiracy:

*"The door is watched, but the watchers are watching me."*

Which meant: *go. Now. While the whole palace looks at the burning liar.*

I had told Ulyana once that faith cracking under weight either shatters or becomes something truer. Tonight mine had become this: a lie on fire, a truth in chains, and a prayer with no god attached to it, aimed at a flooded cathedral under the palace, for a man I barely knew and wholly believed in.

*Go,* I prayed, to whatever listened under the harbor. *Let him reach her. Let him reach the altar. Let the words still be in him.*

* * *

**Ulyana**

The harbor gate was exactly where the map's song had promised, behind a wall of festival storage crates that no one had inventoried since the last century's binding. The seal on the gate was swordspeech—my order's work, laid down generations ago, still humming faintly against my palm like a cat that remembers being petted.

I spoke the opening word, and hated how good it felt.

That was the trap of magic, the thing I'd never told anyone, not even Ines: it wasn't sacrifice, using it. It was homecoming. Every syllable of swordspeech fit in my mouth like it had been measured there. Seven years of silence hadn't dulled it. Silence had only made me hungry.

The gate swung inward on stairs going down into black water-smell, and I went, and the festival noise faded above me until it sounded like a party thrown by strangers.

Down. Down past the tide-line, down where the stones changed from palace granite to cathedral limestone, down where the air went strange and thick and glowed faintly silver without any source. The Veil's light. It seeped up through the flooded nave below like light through ice, and with every step the map-song in my memory grew louder, until it wasn't memory anymore—it was ambient, everywhere, the whole drowned church singing.

And something singing back.

I heard it clearly for the first time as I reached the last landing: beneath the map's melody, a counter-voice, vast and patient, the sound a glacier would make if it learned to hum. It wasn't threatening. That was the horror of it. It sounded *glad*. It sounded like a host who has waited a long time for a guest they are quite certain will stay for dinner.

*I know why you've come,* it seemed to say, in no language at all. *I have known since the courier bled on your floor. Come down, swordspeaker. I have kept your seat.*

My hand found the rail. Above me, muffled through fathoms of stone, the festival screamed and sang and burned. Somewhere up there, Ines was carrying children. Cassian was buying our minutes with his freedom. Pip was squeezing hands twice at the sight of white robes.

And below me, at the bottom of a staircase that had been waiting seven years and seven centuries both, the drowned cathedral opened its doors.

I thought of Ines's voice: *you don't lose anything. You spend it.*

I put my hand on the door of the cathedral, felt the Veil's gladness press against my palm like a handshake, and stepped through—to the sight of a figure kneeling at the far altar in robes rotted to lace, and a skeleton beside the altar kneeling too, and between them, rising out of the silver glow like a tide coming in, the thing that had eaten a king and was so pleased, so very pleased, to finally meet the last of the order that fed it.

"Master," I whispered, though the kneeling bones could not hear me, and descended.
