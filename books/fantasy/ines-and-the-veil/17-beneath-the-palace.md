## 17. Beneath the Palace

The water let me through.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left by then. The harbor did not part with drama—no walls of sea standing aside like courtiers. It simply stopped being where I was going. I walked down the flooded stair beneath the palace's oldest foundation and the water closed over my head and then, a few steps later, it wasn't there at all. My clothes were dry. My lungs were full of air that tasted of stone and old incense and something sweeter underneath, like fruit rotting in a locked room.

The Veil's hospitality. It knew I was coming. It had known for seven years.

* * *

The stair went down farther than any stair has a right to go. I counted two hundred steps before I gave up counting, which is the sort of thing a man does when he's afraid of what waits at the bottom and would rather do arithmetic than think about it. The walls changed as I descended. The upper reaches were palace masonry—Veymar granite, quarried and squared and smug about it. But somewhere past the hundredth step the stone grew older, rougher, marked with channels cut into the rock where water once ran in ritual patterns. Swordspeech glyphs, worn nearly smooth, spiraled down the walls like ivy.

I knew them. Of course I knew them. I'd traced them a thousand times as a boy, my fingers following Master Serafina's fingers following her master's, back and back, an unbroken chain of hands touching the same stone.

*The word is not the power,* she used to say, tapping the glyph for *oath*. *The word is only the door. Remember which side of it you're standing on.*

I remembered. That was the whole problem. Seven years of trying very hard to forget, and here I was, walking back down into the memory like it was a cellar I'd left a candle burning in.

The air changed too. Warmer. And there was light—not torchlight, nothing so honest. A glow came from below, pale and sourceless, the color of moonlight strained through milk. It didn't flicker. Nothing down here moved unless something made it move, and I had the growing, skin-crawling certainty that the only something down here was the thing I'd come to kill.

Or be killed by. The evening had been generous with options.

* * *

The cathedral announced itself the way cathedrals do: by making you feel small before you could see it. First the sound—or rather the absence of sound. My footsteps stopped echoing. Then the ceiling appeared above me, vaulted and vast, lost in shadow even though shadow shouldn't have survived that glow. Then the columns, rank upon rank of them, each one carved with the names of the dead.

Not carved. *Written.* In swordspeech, the old high dialect, the kind that took three years to learn to read and a lifetime to learn to mean. Every column bore hundreds of names, and every name had been spoken aloud once, by someone who loved the person it belonged to, because that was how our order buried its dead—you spoke them into the stone, and the stone held the sound forever. A cathedral of echoes. A building made out of grief given grammar.

I had trained here. I had *slept* here, in the novices' cells behind the transept, with the harbor singing through the stones. I had been fifteen years old and certain the world was a place where oaths meant things.

Then the water came—not the flood itself but the memory of it—and I understood why they'd called it the drowned cathedral. When the order sealed this place, they hadn't just locked the doors. They had pulled the sea in after them, a moat of salt and silence, so that no one could follow. The water was still up there, somewhere above the invisible ceiling of whatever working kept this air pocket breathing. I could feel it pressing. All the weight of the harbor, patient as a debt.

"Sentimental," I said aloud, and my voice sounded obscene in all that stillness. "Drowning your own church. Very dramatic. Master would have hated it."

Master would have loved it, actually. Master Serafina had a flair for the dramatic that she'd spent forty years pretending not to have. But talking to her ghost was a habit I'd have to break eventually, and apparently not tonight.

* * *

The nave stretched ahead of me, and I walked it the way you walk toward a thing you've been walking away from for seven years: too fast, heart hammering, feet knowing the way better than the rest of you wanted them to.

The altar stood at the far end, raised on seven steps—one for each vow, though most people only ever learned five. And on the seventh step, facing the altar, back straight, hands folded in the posture of vigil—

She was still there.

I stopped. I don't know how long I stood there. The glow didn't change; time down here was a rumor. But at some point my knees decided the question of standing was no longer theirs to answer, and I was on the floor, and the sound I made didn't have a word in any language, high or low.

Her skeleton knelt exactly as she must have knelt, decades ago, when the last of her strength went out of her and the stone accepted her the way stone accepts everything: completely, without comment. The robes had rotted to lace against the bones. Her skull bowed forward slightly, chin near her chest, and I thought—absurdly, uselessly—that she looked like she was praying. She'd told me once she didn't pray. *Prayer is asking someone else to hold the door,* she said. *We are the ones who hold doors.*

She had held this one until it killed her.

"You said you'd scatter us," I whispered. "You said the order ends and we live. You said—" My voice cracked and I hated it, hated the crack, hated the wet heat in my eyes, hated the seven years of carefully constructed numbness dissolving like sugar in rain. "You lied."

No. Worse. She hadn't lied. She'd told *me* to run while she stayed, and I'd been too young and too much a coward to notice the difference between an instruction and a sacrifice. *Silence is the last duty,* she'd said, pressing the seal-stone into my hand. *Go east. Don't look back. Live long enough to be ordinary.*

And I had. Oh, I had been magnificent at it. Seven years of luck-knots and false names and flinching at boots on cobblestones. Seven years of being no one, diligently, every single day, while she knelt here in the dark holding shut a door that led to a god-shaped hunger, alone, dying by inches, waiting for a student who never came back.

Until now. Until a dying courier bled out on my floorboards and put a map in my hands and said *the king is not the king*, and the past quit waiting politely and kicked the door in.

"I'm sorry," I told her skeleton, because the dead deserve honesty even when it arrives decades late and useless. "I'm sorry I ran. I'm sorry I was good at it."

The glow pulsed, once, gently. Like something exhaling. Like something pleased.

I got up off the floor. Wiped my face with a sleeve that had no right to be dry. And I climbed the seven steps to stand beside her, because there are apologies you make with words and there are apologies you make by staying, and I had done enough leaving for one lifetime.

* * *

Up close, I saw what I should have seen from the nave: her hands. The finger bones were wrong—worn, ground smooth at the tips, the way bone wears when it's pressed against stone for years without rest. She hadn't just knelt here. She had *worked*. Pressing her palms to the seal, speaking the binding verses over and over, renewing them day after day as the Veil gnawed at them, until the words wore her body down to this: a kneeling shape and ten ruined fingers and a silence that had finally, finally broken.

And beside her knee, half-buried in the dust of decades, lay the seal-stone's twin—the anchor half of the rite, the piece that should have gone with me east and never left her side. She'd left it here. On purpose. Because a seal needs both halves in reach to be renewed, and she had known—I understood it now with a clarity that felt like a knife turning—that someday, if the world was lucky or if her student found his spine, someone would need to come back down here and finish what the binding started.

She hadn't built a tomb. She'd built a doorway. And she'd trusted a frightened boy to walk through it.

"All right," I said. I picked up the anchor-stone. It was warm. Of course it was warm; everything down here was the Veil's body temperature, and I tried not to think about that. "All right, Master. I'm here now. Late, but here."

I set the stone on the altar. It fit a groove worn into the granite—worn by her hands, or by older hands, generations of them, going back to the war and the oath and the desperate arithmetic that had traded a king's soul for a generation's peace. The glow brightened around it, and beneath my feet, through the stone, through the drowned dark, I felt something stir.

It wasn't anger. That was the worst part. It was *attention*—vast, patient, almost tender, the attention of a thing that had waited a century and could wait another and was genuinely glad I'd come. Somewhere above me, past the water, past the festival crowds and the lantern-light and Pip's small stolen voice, the Veil turned its face toward the seventh step.

And it spoke. Not in words. In the space where words go—in the pause between my heartbeat and the next one, it poured meaning directly into the hollow of me:

*You brought the child's song with you. I can hear it in your blood. How thoughtful.*

My hand went to my chest as if I could pat the echo out of me. Pip's verses—the ones the map had taught, the ones that were a key and not a map at all—they lived in me now, carried up from the harbor's edge, and the Veil had heard them the moment I crossed its threshold.

*I know why you've come,* it said, gentle as a lullaby. *Come, then. Let us talk terms. I have waited a long time to make you an offer, swordspeaker. And I promise you—it is a kind one.*

The glow gathered itself, coiling up from the altar like smoke learning to be a shape, and I stood my ground on the seventh step beside my master's bones, and I did not run.

I was done running. Whatever it wanted to offer, I would hear it, and then I would speak the unbinding if it cost me my voice, my magic, and every year I had left.

But gods forgive me—as the light rose and the thing beneath the palace began, at last, to show me what it truly was—some small, traitorous part of me already wanted to say yes.
