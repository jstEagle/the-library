## 18. The Unbinding

The drowned cathedral did not feel like drowning.

That was the first thing I noticed, and I want it on the record that I noticed it while my lungs were still working, because afterward there wasn't much room in me for noticing anything at all. The water hung around us like a held breath—walls of it, a ceiling of it, the whole harbor standing on its head above our heads—and none of it touched us. It parted where we walked and closed behind us, courteous as a servant who has been told to be polite to the guest before the killing.

The light came from below. From the altar, from the thing coiled beneath it, came a glow the color of deep water over black stone—not blue, exactly, not green, but the color your eyes invent when you stare too long into a well. It moved. Light should not move like that, slow and muscular, like something turning over in its sleep.

Master Serafina knelt at the altar.

I knew her by the bones. Seven years had stripped everything else away—the gray braid, the ink-stained fingers, the way she tapped her knuckles twice on a doorframe before entering any room—but the posture was unmistakable. Kneeling. Hands folded. Spine straight as a drawn blade. She had not fled. She had come down here, all those years ago, while I ran through the night with my master's last command ringing in my ears like a coward's lullaby, and she had knelt here and held the seal until holding killed her.

*Silence is the last duty,* she'd told me. *Go, Ulyana. Live small. Live hidden.*

She hadn't meant it for herself. She'd never once, in all the years I studied under her, given herself a single kindness she wouldn't have refused anyone else.

"I'm sorry," I said. My voice came out strange in that airless place, flat, as if the water were listening and disapproving. "I'm late."

The bones said nothing. Bones are honest that way.

Behind me, far above, muffled through fathoms of held breath, the Binding Festival went on screaming. Drums. Bells. And under it, thinner, the sound I kept trying not to hear: Ines's voice, hoarse and furious, calling Pip's name through streets full of inquisitors. Cassian's deeper shout, and then—this was the part I would carry longest—a sound like a door being held, and then a sound like a door failing.

I couldn't go up. Every part of me wanted to; every part of me was already running up stairs that weren't there. But the map had sung us down here for one reason, and if I turned back now, then Ines's wound, Cassian's faith, Ines's brother dying on my floorboards with a stranger's name in his mouth—all of it would have bought nothing but a longer funeral list.

So I walked toward the altar, past my dead master, and I let the Veil see me coming.

* * *

It rose out of the floor the way ink rises through water when you drop it from a height—slow, deliberate, showing off. It didn't take a shape so much as suggest several at once. For a moment it wore the king's face, Aldric's face, kind and tired. Then it wore mine. Then it wore Master Serafina's, young again, smiling the smile she used when I'd gotten an answer right.

*You came,* it said, and the words arrived inside my skull rather than my ears, which is a violation I will never get used to no matter how many eldritch entities I negotiate with. *Seven years I waited. Seven years you hid, and hid well. I respect craft.*

"You're wearing my teacher," I said. "Your respect means nothing to me."

*Serafina,* it said, tasting the name. *She fought me for eleven days, you know. Alone. Her order scattered, her students run off like frightened birds. Eleven days she knelt there and sang the seal shut, and every day I offered her what I am about to offer you, and every day she refused.* A pause, and the glow brightened, warm now, almost tender. *You could be wiser than she was.*

"Bold of you to open with flattery after admitting you tortured her to death."

*I sustained her. Eleven days without food or water, kneeling, singing—I gave her the strength to do it. Ask yourself why, swordspeaker. Ask yourself what I wanted her to hold shut.* The light pulsed. *And ask yourself what happens to the city above when you do what she did instead of what I ask.*

And it showed me.

Not visions this time—memories. Mine, dredged up and polished and presented like a merchant laying out wares. Veymar's night market, seven years ago, the purge still fresh: neighbors dragged from their homes, a charm-seller named Odile drowned in the fountain square with her hands tied, children with the talent vanishing into the palace and not coming back. And then the years after. The quiet years. No more drownings, because no more magic to find—or so the city believed. Markets that stayed markets. Children who stayed children, mostly, mostly unharvested. Peace, of a kind. The peace my silence had bought, itemized and priced.

*This is what your hiding purchased,* the Veil murmured. *Every year you stayed quiet, fewer died. You think that was cowardice? I think it was mercy. I think you were the best thing that ever happened to this city, Ulyana Delacroix, precisely because you made yourself nothing.*

"That's a lie," I said.

*Is it?* The glow dimmed, thoughtful. *Then here is truth, freely given, no price attached: the binding is old. Older than your order. Older than the king. I was bound into this flesh by swordspeakers who thought they were ending a war, and they were right—it ended. A generation of children grew up who would otherwise have burned. Your oath worked. All I ask now is renewal. One voice, spoken freely, and I will release this tired man and take a new vessel—one that volunteers. Or—*

The light flared, and the offer changed shape, the way a con artist changes accents.

*Or refuse. Speak the unbinding. Tear me out of him. And yes—you will free King Aldric, and yes, you will be a hero in whatever story they tell. But the unbinding requires a swordspeaker's whole voice, Ulyana. Not some of it. All of it. Every word of power, every charm, every whisper of the art, gone forever. And the tearing does not stop there. Voices are fragile things. You may keep enough to order ale. You may not. And the city above—* the glow spread, and I felt it touch the edges of my mind like cold water finding the seams of a boot— *the city above will learn what I was holding back. The famine I softened. The plague I slowed. The riots I quieted. I have been the hand under this kingdom for forty years, swordspeaker. Cut me out and see how the body stands.*

There it was. The real bargain, laid bare at last: not *stop*, not this time. This time it only asked me to walk away. To climb back up those stairs with my voice intact and my magic intact and my cowardice intact, and let the king rot another century, and tell myself—forever—that the cost was too high, the risk too great, the timing wrong.

It was offering me seven more years exactly like the last seven.

"You know what's funny," I said. My voice shook. I let it. "For seven years I told myself I was being careful. Prudent. Sensible. And every single day, some small part of me knew I was just—" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "I was hiding. That's all. Hiding with excellent bookkeeping."

*Take the bargain,* the Veil said, and for the first time there was something underneath the silk, something urgent, something almost afraid. *Take it. You are a reasonable man. Reasonable men—*

"I'm not," I said. "I really am not. I'm a man whose brother-figure died on his shop floor holding a map he couldn't read. I'm a man who watched a dockworker take a crossbow bolt because I was too slow. I'm a man whose teacher knelt in this room for eleven days waiting for someone to come help her, and nobody came, because everyone she loved was busy living small." I stepped forward, onto the altar step, close enough to see the glow reflected in the water-walls. "You've been talking to the man I was. He's not here anymore. You're looking at the man she raised."

Above us—muffled, distant, unbearably far—came a scream that might have been Ines, and a smaller sound that might have been Pip, and I stopped listening to the entity in front of me and started listening to the map in my coat, which had begun, very softly, to sing.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about swordspeech: it isn't spells. Spells are recipes—words arranged, ingredients listed, power poured in like wine. Swordspeech is older and stupider and truer. It's the language the blade speaks when it leaves the forge, the language of edge and oath and consequence. You don't cast it. You mean it. Every syllable costs exactly what it says, and there is no haggling.

The unbinding is nine lines. I had learned them at fourteen, kneeling in a training hall that no longer existed, and Master Serafina had made me recite them a hundred times, and at the end of each recitation she'd said the same thing: *"Pray you never need them, and pray you're brave enough if you do."*

I took out the map. It was bloodstained and soft as cloth now, and it hummed against my palms, and Pip's dream-song rose in my memory to meet it, verse answering verse, key fitting lock. The seal beneath the altar shuddered. The water-walls trembled. Somewhere very far above, a bell began to toll the hour of the renewing.

*Last chance,* the Veil said, and its voice cracked like river ice. *Ulyana. Swordspeaker. Last of your order. I can give them all back. Your master. Your brothers and sisters of the blade. I can unmake the purge, unmake the graves, unmake the seven lonely years—*

"I know," I said gently. "That's the worst part. I believe you could."

And I meant the first line.

I won't write it here. Some things shouldn't live on paper, and besides, the words don't survive transcription—they were never really words, more like decisions shaped into sound. What I can tell you is what they did. The first line opened my throat like a window in a storm. The second took every charm I'd ever woven and unwove it, seven years of luck-knots and false-name sigils dissolving off my skin like frost in sunlight. By the third line I was on my knees beside my master's bones, because the words were pulling now, hauling the voice out of me the way a tide hauls a boat off a sandbar, and I gripped her skeletal hand—dust and patience—and kept meaning.

The fourth line hurt. I'll say that plainly. The fourth line hurt worse than Ines's healing, worse than the inquisitor's brand, worse than anything, because that was the line where the Veil understood what I was doing and threw itself against the words, and for a moment we were two forces in one throat, and it showed me everything—Serafina alive, the training hall rebuilt, students laughing, my name spoken without fear in a city that remembered me—and I had to look at all of it, really look, and mean the fifth line anyway.

*You will be nothing,* it screamed, in my own voice, in hers, in Ines's. *Nothing, nothing, NOTHING—*

"No," I said, and it was barely a whisper, most of me already gone. "I'll be found."

Lines six and seven went fast, the way the end of a fall goes fast. Line eight took the last of the magic, and I felt it leave—not torn, exactly. Surrendered. Like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying, except the weight was also my wings.

Line nine was the king's name.

I spoke it, and the world broke politely, the way a lock breaks when the right key finally turns.

* * *

The glow went out all at once, and the water came down.

Not on us—it had never intended to drown us, I understand that now; the cathedral had been holding its breath for seven years out of courtesy to its prisoner—but around us, settling, sighing, becoming ordinary harbor water again, lapping at steps that had been dry for decades. The dark rushed in where the false light had been. And in that dark, from somewhere above, came sounds: shouting, splashing, boots on stone, and a small voice piping a fragment of song that no longer needed to be sung.

"Ines!" I tried to shout. What came out was a rasp, a ruin, a whisper with ambitions. "Ines! Down here!"

The splashing redoubled. A lantern appeared at the top of the stair, bobbing wildly, and then Ines was descending into the flooded nave with Pip clinging to her back and blood on her sleeve that I prayed was not entirely hers, and behind her came Brother Cassian—alive, limping, one arm hanging wrong, his priest's collar torn half off, grinning like a man who had just been excommunicated and found it agreed with him.

"You're alive," Ines said, sliding down the last steps, catching me under the arm. She looked at my face and her expression changed. "Ulyana. Say something."

"Something," I whispered.

Pip slid off her back and stood in the ankle-deep water, staring at the altar, at the bones, at the dark where the glow had been. "It's quiet," Pip said. "Underneath. It's all quiet now." Then Pip looked up at me with those enormous eyes and said, in perfect, careful swordspeech, the phrase every child of my order learned first: *the blade remembers the hand that stays.*

"Yeah," I rasped. "It does."

Ines pulled me upright. Above us, faint and growing, came the sound of bells—not festival bells now, but alarm bells, and under them voices, hundreds of voices, the whole city waking to a king's scream that had just rolled across Veymar like thunder.

Because the king was screaming. Somewhere above us, in his chamber, in his own body for the first time in forty years, King Aldric had opened his eyes and remembered everything.

And everything, I was beginning to suspect, was going to want answers.
