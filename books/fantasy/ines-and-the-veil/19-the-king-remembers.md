## 19. The King Remembers

I want to tell you that I woke gently. That there was a moment of soft confusion, a slow surfacing, the way a man rises from an afternoon nap with sunlight on his face and no idea what year it is.

There was not.

Waking was drowning in reverse. It was forty years of water forced back into my lungs all at once, and every drop of it was memory.

One moment I was—wherever I had been. I still cannot describe it. A dark without edges. Not sleep, because sleep has dreams, and I had none. Not death, because death at least has the decency to be final. Call it a waiting room with no chairs and no window and no sense of how long the appointment had been delayed. And then, all at once, I was *in*, crammed into my own body like a man stuffed into a trunk by men who were in a hurry, and the trunk was on fire, and the fire had a name, and the name was everything it had done while wearing me.

The first thing I felt was my hands. They were shaking. They were old. The last time I had been properly present in them, they had been thirty-four years old and steady enough to sign a treaty. Now they were spotted and knuckled and trembling like a drunkard's, and I did not recognize them, and then I recognized them completely, because they were mine, and I had watched them do everything.

That is the part no one warns you about, being ridden. You don't get to look away. Whatever the thing does through your body, some sliver of you is strapped to the inside of your own eyes, watching, unable to close them, unable to scream, unable even to decide whether what you're watching is real. For decades I told myself it wasn't. That it was a fever dream. That the dark I floated in was mercy, and the flickers bleeding through its walls were nightmares, nothing more.

They were not nightmares. The nightmares would have been kinder, because nightmares end when you wake.

I woke standing in a drowned cathedral under my own palace, ankle-deep in black water, lit from below by a glow the color of a bruise going green. There was a dead god dissolving in front of me—or what passes for a corpse when the corpse is made of other people's stolen magic—and there was a man kneeling in the shallows before my master's skeleton, and he was making a sound I will hear until I die.

He was trying to speak and couldn't. The words came out of him as breath, as shape, as the ghost of language, and I understood—I understood with a horror that arrived ahead of any explanation—that he had just paid for my freedom with his voice. With the magic in his voice. Possibly with his voice entire.

I knew what he was. Of course I knew. Every king of Veymar for four hundred years has known what a swordspeaker is, because every king of Veymar is bound to the Veil by one, and the binding rite is written in their speech, and the first thing the Veil teaches its host king—teaches through your own mouth, in your own voice, to your own heir—is that swordspeakers are the enemy. Heretics. Traitors. The reason for the purges, the drownings, the law that turned a word of power into a death sentence.

It lies, of course. It lies the way water lies about being deep until you're in over your head. Swordspeakers are not the enemy. Swordspeakers are the lock, and the Veil is the thing the lock was built to hold, and for four hundred years my ancestors and I have been the door, and the door has been doing everything the thief asked of it while calling the locksmith a criminal.

This man—the last one, apparently, the last oath-bound swordspeaker in Veymar—had just broken the lock from the inside, at cost of himself, to free a king he had never met, who had spent seven years hiding from laws this king's own mouth had decreed.

I have been a monarch for most of my life, if you count the years I was only technically present. I know how kings are supposed to behave. Kings are gracious from a height. Kings bestow. Kings do not kneel.

I knelt.

My knees hit the flooded stone and the water soaked through my ceremonial robes—it was the night of the Binding Festival; I had been dressed for a ritual I could finally remember dreading—and I knelt in front of Ulyana Delacroix, whose name I somehow already knew, because the Veil had screamed it as it died, and I said the truest sentence I have spoken in forty years:

"I remember everything."

* * *

Here is what remembering everything means. Here is the inventory I took in the hours after the Veil unraveled, while the green light guttered out of the water and the cathedral went dim and honest around us, lit only by whatever phosphorescence lives in drowned places.

I remember signing the Edict of Stillness. My hand held the pen. I remember the ink, brown-black, drying slowly because the chamber was cold. I remember the inquisitor-general standing at my shoulder whispering the justifications, and I remember thinking, from somewhere far away inside myself, *this is wrong, this is wrong, this is wrong,* the way you think it during a nightmare where you can't move. Then the thought would be gone, smoothed over, and my mouth would say "So ordered," and the seal would come down, and another district would learn what happened to people who kept luck-knots behind their counters.

I remember the drownings. Do not ask me to describe them. I will describe them anyway, someday, publicly, every one I can recall, because I believe the families deserve to hear their names said by the mouth that condemned them—but not here, not now, not while my hands were still shaking too hard to hold a cup. I remember forty-one of them by face. There were more than forty-one. The Veil fed well in Veymar. Magic is food to it, and fear seasons the meat, and we had built a city where every charm-seller and hedge-healer and grandmother tying a knot against storms lived in a low simmer of terror, and the Veil ate three meals a day off that simmer for forty years and called it law and order.

I remember the children. I am going to say this once and then I am going to put it down, because I cannot carry it yet: I remember the harvests. The Binding Festival comes every twenty-five years, and each time, the binding must be renewed, and the renewal requires—required—innocent magic, young magic, the kind children make without knowing. I remember approving the lists. I remember my own signature on requisitions for children, filed under euphemisms so clean they should have shamed the devil that dictated them: *festival attendants. choir placements. wardships of honor.*

Some of those children died. Some were emptied and sent back hollow. And some—this is the thread I cling to, the splinter I refuse to stop digging at—are possibly, *possibly*, still alive somewhere beneath the palace, because the Veil hoarded the way all gluttons hoard, and hoards can be found, and found things can be returned.

I intend to find them. Write that down. Let it be the first decree of the reign of Aldric the Second, or Aldric the First Restored, or whatever name the historians settle on for the king who spent four decades as a haunted house: *the children first.* Before reparations, before trials, before the histories are corrected and the statues pulled down—though those too, gods, especially those—the children first.

But all of that came later. In the cathedral, in the first hour, there was only this: a ruined old man in festival robes, kneeling in black water, facing a younger man who had given up his voice to save him, and neither of us could manage much conversation.

Ulyana tried. I'll give him that. He opened his mouth and what came out was a rasp, a thread of sound, a whisper with ambitions. He got two words out—"Your Majesty"—and then stopped, and I watched him try again, and fail again, and something in his face closed like a shop pulling down its shutters, and I understood that he was grieving. Right there, in the moment of his greatest victory, he was grieving the thing he'd lost to win it, and he was doing it silently, because he no longer had any other way.

"Stop," I rasped. My own voice was hardly better—forty years of disuse will do that to a throat, even a throat the Veil used constantly. It turns out the parasite spoke *through* me but never bothered keeping the instrument tuned. "Don't spend... what's left... on ceremony."

He looked at me. And I want to record, for the historians who will inevitably sand the edges off this story, exactly what his face looked like: exhausted. Suspicious. Forty-one years old, give or take, with gray coming in early at the temples, and the particular expression of a man who has just done an enormous, irreversible, generous thing and is now braced for the world to punish him for it. Because that is what my government taught the magic-born of Veymar to expect. That is the country I ruled. Generosity punished. Hiding rewarded. Seven years this man had made himself small, sold harmless charms under false names, flinched from his own reflection—and the reward for surviving all that was to walk into my palace and pay the ultimate price for a stranger.

"You freed a king," I whispered. "The least the king can do is stand up."

And I stood. It took three tries. The Veil had worn my body hard—there were decades of damage in these joints, in this spine, debts I am only beginning to catalog—and the stairs up out of the drowned cathedral were long and slick and dark. But I climbed them. On my own feet. Which is more than I can say for anything else I've done in forty years.

* * *

What happened above, in the palace, in the festival hall, deserves its own account, and others will write it better than I—there was fighting, I'm told; there was a priest holding a door who should be canonized by any faith willing to have him; there was a dockworker with a knife and a fury who reached a stolen child before the inquisitors could finish their work. I arrived at the top of the stair to find the great hall in wreckage, the Binding Festival's white-and-silver banners trampled in spilled wine, and perhaps two hundred of Veymar's noblest citizens pressed against the far walls watching a handful of ragged strangers the way crowds watch a fire—as though it might spread to them.

The inquisitors were still standing. That was the problem. Six of them, in their grey coats, blades drawn, forming a wall between the crowd and the child—a small child, a scrap of a child with river-mud on their knees—while a woman with a bleeding arm stood over the kid with a fish-gutting knife and absolutely no intention of losing.

The inquisitors saw me enter and did the thing I had trained them, through forty years of my own mouth, to do: they looked to the crown for direction. Every eye in the hall swung to me. Two hundred nobles, six armed men, one bleeding woman, one silent swordspeaker, one half-dead priest propped in a doorway, one mud-streaked child—all of them waiting to see which way the king would fall.

Forty years ago, I would have said the words the Veil put in my mouth. *Seize them. The heretic, the smuggler, the defector.* I know the script by heart. I could recite it in my sleep, and for four decades, I effectively did.

"The inquisitorial order," I said, and my voice cracked across the hall like a whip, louder than I expected, louder than the body should have allowed, as though forty years of suppressed words had been banked somewhere and were paying out interest, "is dissolved."

You could have heard a moth land.

"As of this hour. Stand down. Sheathe your blades." The six grey coats did not move. One of them—Vessic, I think his name was, the tall one, the one with the reputation—said, "Majesty, the binding—"

"The binding is *broken*," I said, and heard the whole hall inhale at once. "By right of swordspeech, freely given, in the old cathedral, witnessed by the drowned and by me. Your warrant is ash. Your order is ended. And if a single one of you raises a blade tonight in its name, I will hang you with the ribbon of it myself. Go home. Look at your hands. Decide what you want them for."

They went. All six. I watched Vessic's face as he sheathed his sword, and it was not defeat on it, exactly. It was the expression of a man whose entire architecture has just been declared unsound and who is walking out of the building very carefully to see whether the sky holds. I expect we will see him again. Men like that do not dissolve as easily as orders do. But that is tomorrow's problem, and I have decided, as my first act of personal policy, to permit myself one night of problems I refuse to solve.

Then I crossed the hall—took maybe ten steps, though my legs billed me for a hundred—and knelt again, in front of a bleeding dockworker and a terrified child, in front of my own assembled court, in front of everyone whose family I had helped break.

"My name is Aldric," I said. "I have been absent from this crown for forty years. Everything done in my name in that time was done by the thing that wore me, but the name was mine, and I mean to answer for it. Starting now. Starting with her." I nodded at the child. "Is she hurt?"

"He," said the dockworker, flatly, not moving the knife. "And he's fine. He's mine now. That's not negotiable."

"Good," I said. "Keep him. Send me the bill."

She stared at me. So did the court. Somewhere in the wreckage, the defector priest started laughing—a cracked, disbelieving sound, the laugh of a man whose faith broke and who has discovered that what grew in the crack is sturdier than what it replaced. I would learn later that his name was Cassian, and that he had held a door against inquisitors with nothing but a censer and stubbornness, and I would also learn that he had spent months listening to my confessions through a screen, hearing the thing inside me speak in tongues, and doubting, and doubting, and choosing to act on his doubt. The church will excommunicate him for it. I intend to make him a duke. Let them weigh those two things in whatever scale they use.

But the person I needed to face was the one leaning against a fallen pillar, gray-faced, holding his throat as though checking the wound was still there.

I went to him. The court watched their king cross a wrecked festival hall to stand before a charm-seller in a patched coat, and I let them watch, and I hope the image burns itself into the city's memory, because it is the single most honest political act of my reign: the crown, bowing its head to the man it persecuted.

"Ask me," I whispered to him.

His brow furrowed. He couldn't ask. That was rather the point.

"Anything," I said. "Anything you lost, anything they took, anything the law stole from you and yours—ask it of me, and while I draw breath, it is yours. Your order's name cleared. Your dead named and mourned. Your magic lawful again, if any soul in Veymar ever wants to touch it after what we did to them. Ask."

And Ulyana Delacroix—who had every right to demand a mountain, who could have asked for my head and gotten a committee to debate it—he looked at me for a long moment with those tired eyes, and then he shook his head, once, slowly, and rasped out in that ruined whisper of his, barely loud enough for me alone:

"Just... listen to them. From now on. When people talk... actually listen."

Of all the prices ever charged to a crown, I think that may be the smallest and the hardest. Kings are not built for listening. We are built for pronouncing. But I gave him my word, there in the wreckage, in front of God and the court and a child with mud on his knees, and I have kept it every day since, and I will keep it until they put me in the ground—which, given the state of this body, may be sooner than my physicians would like me to acknowledge.

* * *

They tell me dawn is coming. I can see it starting at the harbor edge, gray going gold, and the city below my windows is awake in a way I have never seen it—not festival-awake, not riot-awake, but *talking*. People in the streets. People arguing, loudly, in the open, about magic, about the law, about what Veymar is supposed to be now. Arguing! Openly! Without drowning! It is the most beautiful sound I have ever heard, and I include in that assessment every anthem ever sung in my honor, all of which I now intend to retire.

There is so much to do. Trials—mine first, I insist, whatever the lawyers say about a king's immunity; I was the instrument, and instruments should be examined for what they played. Reparations. The search beneath the palace for the taken children, which begins this morning with lanterns and rope and my personal attendance. The rebuilding of a swordspeaker tradition that I nearly finished exterminating, from whatever few survivors Pip's map-song and Cassian's records can help us find.

And there is one more thing, a small thing, that I have already set in motion, because I am told the man who freed me keeps a little shop in the Tanner's Row district with a crooked door and a sign that says CLOSED, and I am told he has a family now—a smuggler, a priest, a child who hears songs in old paper—and I am told that when he speaks, which is rarely, people lean in to catch it.

I have instructed that no guard go near that shop. No tax collector either. No official of any kind. If the last swordspeaker of Veymar wants to sell luck-knots that are only knots, then by every power I possess, he will sell them in peace.

But I confess I hope he leaves the sign off CLOSED more often than not. A kingdom that spent forty years teaching its magic to hide owes its quietest hero at least one open door.

Mine, for instance. It stands open now. The morning is coming up the harbor, and the city is talking, and for the first time in forty years, the voice answering it is entirely, only, mine.

Now—if someone would kindly fetch me the list of every child who vanished, and a chair, and perhaps a strong tea—I have forty years of listening to begin.
