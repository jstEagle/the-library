## 20. Aftermath

The morning after the world ended, I woke up in a borrowed bed above a bakery and smelled bread.

That was the strange part. Not the king's proclamation nailed to every door in Veymar — though that was strange enough, a royal seal declaring that magic was no longer a drowning offense, pending "civic deliberation," which is what governments say when they haven't decided whether to be terrified or grateful. Not the ash still drifting off the harbor like gray snow. Not the fact that half the city had seen a light rise out of the drowned cathedral and felt something *lift* off their chests, like a hand they'd forgotten was there.

The strange part was the bread. Because the baker, a wide woman named Marta who'd never said two words to me in nine years of hauling crates past her door, had left a loaf outside our room with a note: *For the ones who did it. Whatever it was.*

We hadn't done it for them. We'd done it because my brother died on a shop floor with a map in his blood, because children were going missing, because a thing made of hunger was wearing the king like a coat. But Marta didn't know that. Marta knew bread and fear and the sudden absence of fear, and she answered it the only way dock people know how: she fed somebody.

I sat on the edge of the bed and ate half the loaf before Ulyana woke up, and when he did wake up and tried to say good morning, what came out was a rasp like a hinge in need of oil. He pressed his lips together. He'd been doing that all night — testing his voice, wincing at what came back.

"Don't," I said.

He looked at me.

"It'll either come back or it won't. Don't sit there auditioning."

He almost smiled. It was a bad smile, crooked and tired, the smile of a man who had traded his voice and his magic to unmake an ancient horror and gotten a hoarse whisper and a loaf of bread in return. I threw him the other half of the loaf. He caught it one-handed, which he shouldn't have been able to do anymore, and we both pretended not to notice that some things live in the body longer than they live in the soul.

* * *

They found the king sitting on the steps of the palace at dawn, in his nightshirt, barefoot, asking everyone who passed if they were real.

I know because Cassian told us, later, in the low steady voice he'd started using since the door — since the door he'd held shut in the cathedral while something on the other side screamed in forty voices at once. His arm was splinted to the shoulder. His face had a bruise blooming across it purple as a storm front. He looked, frankly, terrible, and also like a man who had set down a sack of stones he'd been carrying for twenty years.

"They stood down the inquisitors by noon," he said. We were in the shop — Ulyana's shop, the charm shop with its false back wall and its floorboards where my brother had bled out. Nobody had suggested going anywhere else. "Aldric himself gave the order. He remembered everything, Ines. Every child. Every confession he heard through the Veil's mouth and signed his name to." Cassian turned his cup in his hands. "He asked me to hear his confession. The real one, this time. I told him I wasn't fit to absolve anyone. He said that was why he wanted me."

"What did you say?"

"I'm saying it slowly." He drank. "It may take years. That seems fair."

Outside the shop window, Veymar was doing something I'd never seen it do: arguing about magic in the open. Two fishermen on the corner were having it out, loud enough to hear through glass — one saying the Veil's fall proved magic was a plague that had nearly eaten us, the other saying the Veil had *been* the plague and the magic was just what it ate, and weren't those different things entirely? A woman selling eels had joined in. A child was chanting something about swordspeakers. Nobody was being drowned. Nobody was even being arrested. The city was a kettle coming slowly to a boil, and for once nobody was holding the lid down.

"Deliberation," Cassian said, following my gaze. "The council wants a year of hearings."

"A year." I thought of Pip upstairs, asleep with the map under their pillow like a stuffed animal. "Children are still missing. The ones the inquisitors took before the festival. Somebody should be looking for them now, not after a year of old men clearing their throats."

"Somebody is." Cassian set down the cup. "Aldric remembers where they were kept. He's sending people today. He asked—" the priest hesitated, and there was something raw under the hesitation, "—he asked if the swordspeaker would come. To identify the ones who can still hear the songs. Apparently the children sing, now. Since the Veil broke. All of them, the same song."

Ulyana, who had been mending a net by the window with more concentration than the task required, stopped mending.

"He can't speak," I said.

"He doesn't have to speak," Cassian said gently. "He has to listen. And then point."

Ulyana looked at me. I looked at him. There was a whole conversation in it, the kind you learn to have with someone over weeks of shared danger and shared bread, and the short version of it was: *not yet.* Today there was somewhere else we had to be.

* * *

You can't bury someone properly in Veymar if they died unknown. The city takes unclaimed bodies to the potter's field beyond the tanneries and stacks them under markers that say only a number. Tam Delacroix would have been number four hundred and eleven. A courier who ran letters through smugglers' channels, no family listed, no trade guild, nothing.

But he had a sister. And a sister with a smuggler's network can get anything — a plot on the hill above the harbor, where you can see the water; a coffin from the undertaker who owed me three favors and now owed me considerably more, since my brother's last delivery had helped end a century of nightmare; a priest, or the next best thing.

The next best thing stood at the head of the grave in his cassock with the insignia torn off, because the Church had finished voting on Cassian by the time we got there. Excommunicated. Formally, ceremonially, with bells. He'd described it to me with something close to wonder: *"They rang the great bell, Ines. The one they ring maybe twice a century. For me. Do you know how long I served hoping to matter enough to be punished?"* He'd laughed when he said it, and then he'd gone quiet, and I understood that underneath the joke was a man standing in the ruins of the only house he'd ever had, discovering that the sky was bigger than the roof.

So there he was, defrocked, splinted, bruised, reading the burial words from memory because he no longer had the right to carry a book. And beside him, Pip, scrubbed pink, holding a sprig of rosemary they'd stolen off Marta's windowsill without asking, because grief apparently doesn't wait for manners.

And beside them, Ulyana, who had asked me the night before — in writing, on the back of a receipt, because he refused to waste his ruined voice on anything that wasn't necessary — *what were his words? Did he have words?*

Everyone has words. You just have to catch them. Tam wrote me letters for six years, from every port, complaining about the weather and the pay and the clients and the state of his boots, and never once saying anything that mattered, which is how I knew everything mattered. So I read the last letter. The whole thing. The part about the rain in Corvess and the part about a client who paid in buttons and the part where he said, in the postscript, the way he always put the true things: *Tell you what, Ness. When this job's done, I'm taking a season off. Come with me. We'll go somewhere the fog doesn't reach.*

The fog reaches everywhere. That was the joke of it, and I laughed, and laughing at a graveside feels like blasphemy until you realize it's the opposite — it's the sound of loving someone too much to let the ground have the last word.

Pip put the rosemary on the coffin. Then Pip did the thing that none of us expected and all of us, afterward, agreed we should have: Pip sang.

Not the map's song. Something smaller, newer, made up on the spot in a child's piping voice — a few notes, wordless, the kind of tune kids hum when they're walking. But the harbor below caught it. I swear this. The water carried it back up the hill a half-second later, softer, like something enormous and drowned singing along, and the wind moved wrong, gentle, circling the grave twice before it went out toward the sea.

Cassian crossed himself. Old habit. Then he lowered his hand and said, "Well. The Veil's dead, but the sea remembers music. That's not the same thing. That's not a bad thing."

Ulyana touched the coffin lid once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder so as not to wake them. His lips moved. No sound came out, or almost none. I've thought since about what he might have said — an apology, a swordspeaker's rite, a promise. I decided it doesn't matter. Some words are meant for one listener, and Tam had waited long enough.

* * *

Afterward we walked back down through the city, all four of us, and Veymar watched us go.

I don't mean that dramatically. I mean that word travels fast in a port town, faster than any courier — even mine, even Tam's — and by the time we came off the hill, people knew. The woman who sells eels pointed. The fishermen stopped arguing. Someone had chalked a sword and a broken circle on the wall by the tannery stairs, and a kid — maybe ten, gap-toothed — ran alongside us for half a block chanting "swordspeaker, swordspeaker" until his mother hauled him back by the collar, apologizing and glaring at the same time, because even liberation takes getting used to.

Ulyana kept his hood up. Seven years of habit. But halfway down the market road, an old woman stepped out of the crowd — a fishwife, hands like knotted rope — and pressed something into his palm and walked on before he could react. A luck-knot. Badly tied, honestly, all wrong, the work of someone who'd seen one once and tried from memory.

He stared at it. Then he closed his fist around it, and under the hood, I'd have bet my boat he was crying, and I decided I'd never check.

"That's going to take some adjusting," I said.

He rasped something. It might have been "*you have no idea.*"

Pip took his other hand, the one without the knot, and swung it like kids do, back and forth, back and forth, and the most dangerous man in Veymar let a nine-year-old swing his arm down the market road in broad daylight, and if you'd told me a month ago that this was how the story ended, I'd have called you a liar and sold you a fake route out of the harbor besides.

* * *

Evening found us back at the shop. Cassian had gone — back to the palace, to sit with a king's confession that might take years, and he'd paused at the door and said, "I'll want to see you all again. Not for plots. Just... dinner. Is that allowed, when there's no plot?" and Pip had said "you're allowed if you bring the honey cakes," and that was settled.

Pip was asleep on the cot in the back, the map finally set aside — its song finished, Ulyana had written to me, its work done, though Pip still wouldn't sleep a night without it. I stood at the counter pretending to inventory stock, which is to say I stood at the counter not thinking about my brother, which is to say I thought about nothing else.

Ulyana came and stood across from me. He laid a piece of paper on the counter between us, written in his careful hand:

*The king's men found the children. Eleven alive. They're asking for Pip by name.*

My chest did something complicated. "Then Pip goes tomorrow. With an escort. With Cassian, if he'll stand guard."

He nodded. Then he wrote again, slower:

*I owe you a debt I can't name. Your brother died to bring me the map. You bled to buy me the door. I had a voice and an order and a purpose, and I spent all of them, and what I have left is—*

He stopped writing. Crossed it out. Started again.

*Thank you isn't big enough. But thank you.*

"You're an idiot," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle of it, because grief and gratitude are the same muscle and mine had been lifting heavy all day. "Tam didn't die bringing you the map. He died doing his job, the stubborn way he did everything, and if he'd known what it was for he'd have charged double and complained the entire time. And I didn't bleed for you. I bled for him. For all of them." I wiped my face with my sleeve, furious at myself, not meaning it. "We're not even, Ulyana. That's not how family works. Family isn't a ledger."

He went very still. *Family.* I watched the word land on him like something thrown, like something he'd spent seven years dodging and had finally, accidentally, caught.

"The shop needs a new sign," I said, gruff, moving on before he could make it a moment. "The old one says DELACROIX CHARM & CURIOSITY, FALSE AND MISLEADING, and half the letters are burned anyway. You'll want something honest. And a second cot. And Marta will feed you whether you like it or not, so learn to say thank you in a whisper and save your voice for important things."

He picked up the pen. Wrote: *What are the important things?*

I looked at the back room, where Pip slept with a dead man's map under their head. At the door Cassian had left through, coming back tomorrow with honey cakes. At the hill where Tam lay under new-turned earth with the harbor singing softly below.

"Dinner," I said. "Dinners are important. Everything else is negotiable."

Ulyana smiled — the real one this time, crooked and tired and unbearably relieved — and under his breath, in a voice like gravel and embers, he spent three precious syllables on the only word worth spending them on.

"*Home,*" he whispered.

Outside, the tide was coming in, and far beneath the harbor, in a cathedral with no roof, something that was not the Veil — something older and kinder, the sea remembering music — began, very faintly, to sing.
