## 21. A Shop with New Signs

The sign took me three tries.

Not the carving—I could carve a luck-knot in my sleep, and had, for seven years, in wood cheap enough to burn if an inquisitor knocked. No, the trouble was what to *say* on it. The old sign had said DELACROIX CHARMWORKS, which was a lie twice over: once because Delacroix was a name I'd bought from a dying man's estate ledger, and once because nothing in that shop had ever been only a charm.

I'd burned that sign the night after the Binding Festival, along with the false papers, along with the second set of false papers I'd hidden inside the third set of false papers like a coward's nesting doll. Ines had watched me feed the fire and said nothing, which was her way of saying everything.

So now the new sign, and the problem of honesty, which turned out to be harder than forgery.

The first attempt read EMRYS BLACKWOOD, SWORDSWPEAKER — no. That was a door I'd nailed shut with my own voice, and besides, I couldn't have spelled it right anyway; the words wouldn't come anymore. The second said FINE CHARMS, HONESTLY PRICED, which Pip pronounced "the saddest thing ever written on wood," and Pip had once lived in a drainpipe, so their authority on sadness was considerable.

The third try hung above the door now, letters painted by Pip's own hand in gold they'd traded half a month of errands to afford:

**BLACKWOOD & FOUND FAMILY**
*Charms. Only charms. Come in anyway.*

Ines had objected to the ampersand. "We're not a business partnership."

"We're something," I'd said. "Ampersands are cheaper than naming it."

She'd rolled her eyes and bought the paint anyway.

* * *

Reopening day was the first of Thawmonth, because Ines insisted on auspicious dates and Cassian insisted that the Church no longer got a vote on his calendar. The morning came up gray over Veymar the way mornings do in a city still learning to breathe, and I stood behind my counter—my counter, my shop, my name on the door, all of it real now in ways that kept catching me off guard like stairs that aren't where your foot expects—and waited to see if anyone would come.

They came.

That was the strange part. Not crowds, not yet—the city hadn't decided how it felt about magic being legal again, or rather about magic having always been there, drowned under the harbor and under the law both. But people came. A fishwife wanted a knot against storms, and when I tied it she watched my hands the whole time, not afraid exactly, but watching, the way you watch a dog that used to be a wolf. A boy of maybe ten asked if I could make him fly. I told him honestly that I couldn't, that whatever I'd once been able to do had gone out of me like heat from a cracked kettle, and he looked so disappointed that I gave him a carved bird for free and told him the flying was his end of the bargain.

He held it up to the wind off the harbor and ran the length of the quay with it, and I'll tell you something I'd have denied under torture seven years ago: it flew better than anything I'd ever made with swordspeech.

Because here is what nobody warns you about losing your magic: the hands remember. Every knot, every carving, every fold of paper and twist of cord lives in the fingers independent of the power that used to run through them, the way a song survives in the throat after the words lose their meaning. My charms did nothing. They were string and wood and river-stone and wish. And people bought them anyway, more of them than ever bought the real ones, because it turned out that what Veymar had been starving for these long drowning decades wasn't magic at all. It was permission. Permission to carry a small hope in your pocket without wondering who saw you buy it.

I sold out of luck-knots by noon and had to tie more in the back room, standing at the window where I could see the street, and somewhere in the middle of the fourth knot I realized I was humming.

I don't sing. Couldn't, really—not since the unbinding took the deep registers of me, the ones where swordspeech lived, leaving my voice thin as winter light. But humming isn't words. Humming is just breath agreeing with itself. And the tune, when I noticed what it was, made my hands go still on the cord.

It was the map's song. Or the shape of it, the way Pip hums it, the way it had lodged in my head during those weeks beneath the palace when a child sang verses learned in dreams and something ancient answered up through the harbor stones. The map itself hung framed above my hearth at home, its ink faded almost to nothing now, its work finished. Silent, as promised. But songs don't die just because their singers stop. They move house. They take up residence in whoever was listening.

I finished the knot. I put it in the basket with the others. Outside, the fishwife's grandson was showing off his mother's storm-charm to a friend, and the friend's eyes went wide with the particular awe children reserve for things that are probably nothing, and I thought: *this*. This is what the order should have been, if we'd had any sense. Not oaths and purges and kings bound to hungry dark. Just this. Small hopes, honestly priced.

* * *

Pip arrived at midday, as they did most days now, bursting through the door with the specific energy of someone who has recently learned something and cannot physically survive without telling you.

"Master Ondrey says I'm ready for the Third Verse."

"Does he." Master Ondrey was one of four surviving swordspeakers in Veymar, a stooped man with ink-stained fingers who had spent the purge years disguised as a tax assessor, which he maintained was the greater crime. He'd come to the shop in the first week after the Festival, drawn by rumor of the man who'd broken the great oath, and stood in front of my counter looking at me the way you look at a legend that turns out to be tired and hoarse and selling charms.

"You're the one who unbound it," he'd said.

"I'm the one who got lucky and had help."

"The records say otherwise." He'd set a bundle of salvaged texts on my counter—training manuals, verse-cycles, the order's scattered bones. "The rebuilt order will need teachers. And students." His eyes had gone, deliberately, toward the corner of the shop where Pip sat cross-legged on the floor with the map's frame pulled down into their lap, mouthing sounds that weren't quite words. "Particularly students who can already hear it."

So now Pip studied under Ondrey three mornings a week, learning the verses properly instead of by dream-osmosis, and came to my shop every afternoon to do homework at my counter and eat everything in my pantry and narrate their progress at a volume that suggested the neighbors ought to be graded too.

"The Third Verse," they repeated, when I didn't react fast enough. "The binding one. Ondrey says even the masters argue about it. Some of them think it shouldn't be taught at all."

"What do you think?"

Pip considered this with the gravity they reserved for questions that mattered, which was most of them. "I think hiding it is how you got a Veil in the first place." They hopped onto the stool behind the counter—*their* stool, claimed within a week of the reopening, defended against all comers. "Ondrey says you used to be able to speak the whole cycle alone. Is that true?"

"It's true I was arrogant enough to try."

"What was it like? Speaking it. Before."

I tied off the knot in my hands before answering, because some questions deserve finished work. "Like holding water in your cupped palms," I said finally. "Like the whole city was thirsty and you were the well. It was—" I searched for it, the old feeling, and found the place where it had been, smooth and empty as a healed scar. "It was the best thing I ever lost."

Pip frowned. "That doesn't make sense."

"No," I agreed. "Most true things don't, at first. Give it thirty years."

"I'll give it ten." They slid off the stool and went to rearrange my display shelf according to some private taxonomy known only to children and archivists, and I let them, because the truth was the shop organized itself better when Pip had been through it, and the truth was also that I liked the noise.

Halfway through the afternoon a woman came in wanting a charm for her daughter's fever, and I sold her willow-bark tea and a carved bear and instructions to see the physician on Cordwainer Street, because we were honest now, all the way down. She left looking faintly disappointed until Pip explained that the bear was for the daughter to hold while the tea worked, and that this was medically sound, probably, and the woman laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind of laugh that would have gotten someone reported to the inquisitors two years ago, back when joy itself needed a permit.

I thought about Ines's brother then. I thought about him the way I thought about most things now, which was sideways, in flashes, grief arriving like weather. He had died on my floorboards reaching for a stranger, trusting a stranger, because someone had to be there and no one else was coming. The whole architecture of my hiding had rested on the belief that my silence cost nothing—that disappearing was a neutral act, a subtraction, leaving the world exactly as it was minus one swordspeaker. Ines had dismantled that argument in a single sentence while I stood packing to flee: *hiding didn't save anyone, it just meant no one was there.* Her brother had bled out proving it.

So the shop stayed open. That was the whole philosophy, such as it was. Be there. Answer the door. Sell the fever-charm, walk the child home, stand in the doorway where people can find you. It wasn't heroism. Heroism had nearly killed everyone I loved and actually killed Cassian's career, though he seemed oddly cheerful about the excommunication, wearing it like a medal he was too polite to mention. No—it was smaller than heroism and harder, the daily discipline of remaining visible.

The bell over the door rang. I looked up expecting another customer and found Ines instead, salt-wind in her hair, a courier's satchel slung across her chest with the new insignia stamped on the flap: the open-hand mark of the Veymar Letter Guild, the network she'd built out of her brother's smuggler routes once the smuggling became unnecessary.

"You're out of luck-knots," she said, reading the empty basket with a dockworker's eye for inventory. "There's a queue forming. Two women and a man who looks like he wants to ask about love charms, and you're going to have to let him down gently again."

"I have a speech prepared."

"You always have a speech prepared." She dropped onto the stool Pip had vacated, accepted the tea I poured without asking, and looked around the shop with an expression I'd learned to read as approval wearing disguise. "Busy day?"

"Sold out by noon. A boy tried to negotiate for a flying charm with two buttons and a dead beetle."

"Did you take the deal?"

"He drove a hard bargain. I threw in the beetle."

She laughed, and the sound filled the shop the way the map's song used to fill the cathedral, and I understood—for perhaps the thousandth time since the Festival, and each time as newly as the first—that this was the trade I'd made, voice for voices, magic for this. The ledgers balanced. I'd have signed it again.

* * *

Closing time came soft, the way it does in spring, the light going long and honeyed across the counter. Pip had gone home with the Ondreys' other student, arguing loudly about the Second Verse's proper cadence all the way down the street. Ines lingered, restacking a shelf that didn't need it, which meant she had something to say and was approaching it by water rather than by road.

"Spit it out," I said. "You're worse at preamble than I am."

"The Guild's first official route opens next month." She turned a carved bird over in her hands, studying its wings. "Veymar to the coast towns. Letters, mostly. People writing to family they haven't dared write to in years—there's a lot of that now, everyone surfacing at once like it's low tide." She set the bird down. "I want the first delivery to stop here. Officially. A Guild post at Blackwood & Found Family. You'd be the harbor district's letter-keeper."

I stared at her. "Ines. I sell knots."

"You sell *hope*, and you're trustworthy, and you're stationary, which is more than can be said for anyone else I employ." She met my eyes, and beneath the briskness was the thing underneath, the thing that had carried her through her brother's death and a wound and a war: the fierce, unsentimental conviction that people who had proven themselves belonged in the story. "My brother died getting a message delivered. Let me build the thing he was building. With you in it."

The bell over the door rang—one last customer, an old man wanting a charm against bad dreams—and I sold him lavender and a good night's instruction and watched him go, and when the door closed I found Ines still waiting, patient as tide.

"Yes," I said. "Letter-keeper. Put it in writing. I'll frame it next to the map."

She grinned and stuck out her hand, and we shook on it like smugglers, which technically we had both been.

After she left I stood a while in the emptying shop, turning the lamp low, listening to the building settle. On the wall above the hearth at home, the map slept its dreamless sleep. Under the floorboards of the world, the Veil was ash and memory. Somewhere across the city, a king who remembered everything was learning, slowly, publicly, painfully, to govern a country that had every reason not to trust him—and was, inch by inch, earning it back, which was more than I'd expected and exactly what Ines had demanded.

I locked the door. I touched the new sign once, feeling the grain of the wood, the gold paint already beginning to flake at the ampersand's edge the way paint does, honestly, imperfectly, in the weather.

*Come in anyway,* it said.

And tomorrow, they would.

I was halfway down the darkening street, thinking of supper and of nothing else, when I heard it—faint, unmistakable, rising from the direction of the harbor where the drowned cathedral lay quiet under its hundred fathoms:

Someone singing the First Verse. And not in Pip's uncertain child-voice, nor Ondrey's careful scholar's one.

In a voice I knew. A voice I had knelt beside for twenty years. A voice that had been kneeling, skeleton-still, at an altar beneath the sea.

My master's voice, singing up through the stones.
