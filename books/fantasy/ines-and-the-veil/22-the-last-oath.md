## 22. The Last Oath

The scaffolding around the cathedral's west tower looked like a birdcage built for something enormous and apologetic. I stood beneath it in the gray of an autumn morning, watching masons haul stones up on ropes that creaked like old men getting out of chairs, and I thought: seven years ago I would have lifted every one of those stones with a word.

I don't miss it. That's the strange part. Or maybe it isn't strange at all—maybe it only took me losing everything to learn what I'd actually been carrying all those years in Veymar, hunched over my little shop of harmless knots and charms. It wasn't power. Power was just what I used to keep people at arm's length.

What I'd actually been carrying was loneliness, and it was heavy as a cathedral.

"You're brooding," Ines said, arriving at my elbow with two cups of cider and the particular expression she wore when she'd already decided what I was brooding about and found it tiresome.

"I'm appreciating architecture."

"You're brooding about whether you deserve to be here."

I took the cider. Arguing with Ines was like arguing with the tide; you could do it, but you'd end up wet either way. "One of these days," I said, "you'll let me have a feeling without narrating it back to me."

"Not likely. You hoard them." She bumped her shoulder against mine—a gesture that had taken her the better part of a year to permit herself, and which still landed on me like sunlight through a window I'd kept shuttered for decades. "Come on. They want you inside before the bells. Cassian's been pacing the nave since dawn, and Pip's driving the masons mad asking questions about the acoustics."

"Of course they are."

"Of course we are," she corrected. "Say it properly. *We.*"

* * *

The cathedral had been drained for two years now, but the water had left its signature everywhere: tide-lines on the pillars like the rings of some vast drowned tree, salt blooming white in the carvings, a smell of brine and old stone that no amount of incense would ever fully evict. The restoration crews had argued long and hard about whether to scrub the marks away. In the end, King Aldric himself had ordered them left.

"We should remember what was done here," he'd said, standing ankle-deep in the shallows where his own prison had been, "and by whom. Including me."

The nave had been re-consecrated three months ago—the first legal act of magic performed in Veymar in living memory, if you counted consecration as magic, which the new laws did, grudgingly, under a great deal of paperwork. Cassian had officiated. He'd wept through half of it, which he would deny to his dying day, and which everyone present had agreed not to mention for exactly that reason.

Now he stood near the altar in his plain gray robes—not priest's vestments anymore; the Church had seen to that with impressive speed once he'd handed over the liturgical records—but the robes suited him better than the gold ever had. He looked like a man who had set down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"You came," he said, when I reached him.

"You sound surprised."

"I've spent my life around oaths, Ulyana. I know how much they cost to say aloud." He studied me with those pale, patient eyes. "Especially for a man who spent seven years teaching himself not to say anything true."

"It wasn't seven years of nothing true. I sold excellent luck-knots."

"The luck-knots were lies, Ulyana. You told people they worked."

"They *did* work. Placebo is a form of magic. Ask any priest."

He laughed—that startled, rusty laugh he still seemed surprised by—and then sobered, because below us, in the body of the nave, the doors opened and the witnesses began to arrive.

There weren't many. That was the point. An oath sworn before a crowd is theater; an oath sworn before four people is a promise with nowhere to hide.

Ines went down first, taking her seat on the front bench like a woman attending a trial she intended to win. Behind her came Pip, scrubbed nearly pink, fidgeting in clothes that were new and therefore hated, clutching the map.

Ah. The map.

It hung at Pip's side in its oilcloth wrapping, and even from the altar rail I could feel it—or imagine I felt it, which after two years amounts to the same thing. Since the unbinding, its song had faded verse by verse, like a lullaby winding down. Each month there was less of it. Pip had charted the decline with the grim precision of a child cataloguing a dying pet: *three lines last spring, one line at midsummer, half a line in the autumn.*

Today, Pip said, there was one note left.

* * *

They'd asked me, weeks ago, what I wanted the oath to say. I'd told them I'd know when I heard it. This was only partly mysticism; mostly it was because I couldn't write it myself. Every draft came out wrong. Too grand. Too small. Too much like the old oaths, the ones that had bound kings and broken orders and taught a generation of children—including one specific red-haired apprentice sitting in the third row—that silence was a virtue.

It was Pip who finally brought me the words, the way Pip brought me everything: sideways, at breakfast, without looking up from the porridge.

"You should say the things you never said," Pip had said. "You know. The ordinary ones. Everyone always swears the big stuff. Nobody swears the small stuff, and the small stuff is what actually matters."

And that was it. That was the whole oath. Children are unbearable that way.

Cassian raised his hand, and the nave went quiet—not empty-quiet but full-quiet, the kind of quiet a building makes when it has been empty a long time and is glad of company.

"We gather," he said, "not to bind, but to witness. Not to hold anyone to anything, but to stand beside them while they hold themselves." He glanced at me. "Ulyana Delacroix. Last swordspeaker of the Order of the Veiled Blade. Will you speak?"

I came down the steps. My legs knew the way; I'd walked this floor when it lay under forty feet of black harbor water, when the only light was the Veil's cold glow and my master's skeleton knelt at the altar keeping a seal with her dead bones. I had spoken the unbinding here. I had felt the words tear out of me like roots pulled from soil, and I had thought, in the last moment before the silence took me, *so this is what I was for.*

I had been wrong about that too. What I was for turned out to be smaller and harder and better.

I stopped before the bench where they sat. Ines. Pip. Cassian. And behind them, filling the doorway because he refused to sit anywhere and insisted on standing guard out of habit, King Aldric in plain clothes, looking like exactly what he was: a man who had been a passenger in his own life for forty years and was still learning to drive.

I opened my mouth. And here is the thing they don't tell you about losing your voice to magic: it doesn't take your voice. It takes your certainty. Every word I said now came out unarmored, unglamorous, at the volume of an actual human being talking to other actual human beings. There was no resonance. No power riding underneath. Just me, hoarse, in a big cold room.

"I, Ulyana Delacroix," I said, "swear nothing magical today. There's nothing left to swear with, and I've learned the hard way that magic was mostly how I avoided saying things plainly."

Pip grinned. Ines didn't smile, but her eyes did, which from Ines was worth more than any blessing.

"So. Plainly." I looked at each of them in turn. "I swear to be present. When you knock, I'll answer. When you're silent, I won't assume it means nothing. When you need someone in the room, I will be in the room—not hiding behind a counter, not behind a false name, not behind the comfortable lie that staying away keeps people safe. Hiding never saved anyone. I know. I wrote the book. It was a bad book."

A breath. The salt-smell of the old stones. Somewhere above us a gull complained about the scaffolding.

"I swear to be findable. Not just in the shop—in the sense of being someone you can look for and locate. If I go dark, if I go quiet, if I start doing that thing where I decide I'm a burden and start planning my noble exit—" Ines made a small noise of recognition, "—you have my permission, indeed my instruction, to come find me and be extremely annoying about it until I stop."

"That won't be hard," Ines said. "I'm very annoying."

"She is," Pip confirmed solemnly. "It's her best quality."

"And last." My voice cracked on it, and I let it crack, because that was rather the point of the whole exercise. "I swear to stay. Not because staying is easy—I'm quite good at leaving, historically, I have references—but because you three, plus one king who won't sit down, are worth more than every spell I ever cast. I lost my magic and gained a family, and if I could go back and make the trade again, I'd make it faster."

Silence. The good kind.

Cassian stepped forward. "These words are witnessed," he said. "By Ines Delacroix-Vey, sister of the courier who began this. By Pip, who heard the song when no one else could. By Aldric, king in fact as well as name. And by me, who has no church left and doesn't miss it." He paused, and something mischievous moved through his gravity. "No power binds this oath. Which means nothing can break it but you. We thought you should know that's how it works now."

"That's terrifying," I said.

"Yes," said Ines. "Welcome to it."

* * *

Afterward there was cider on the cathedral steps, because Ines had decided ceremonies without refreshments were just lectures, and because the morning had warmed into one of those thin gold autumn days that Veymar gets perhaps six of a year and spends the rest of the time apologizing for.

Aldric finally sat down—on the steps, next to Pip, which two years ago would have caused a constitutional crisis and now caused nothing but Pip scooting over to make room. The king ate bread and cheese like a man still astonished that food belonged to him.

"You know," he said to me, quietly, under the noise of Pip explaining acoustics to a mason who hadn't asked, "for thirty years I watched everything that thing did through my own eyes. I remember all of it. The drownings. The purges. Your order." His jaw tightened. "I remember your master coming to the palace, before the end. She begged to see me. The Veil wouldn't allow it—it sent her away with my own hands, made me turn her out myself. She knelt in the mud outside the gates for an hour. I watched her do it from a window I couldn't look away from."

I hadn't known that. None of us had. Master Serafina had left no letters, only a skeleton and a seal held past all reason.

"She stayed," I said. "She held the binding as long as her body allowed. She bought us the time."

"She bought *me* the time," Aldric said. "And I intend to spend it well. The schools reopen in spring. Magic instruction, legal, regulated, out in the open where it can't curdle into shame or fear." He looked at me sidelong. "They'll want teachers. People who remember what the old order got right—and what it got catastrophically wrong."

"I'm retired."

"You're thirty-eight."

"I'm *hoarse.*"

"You whisper beautifully," he said, and had the grace to grin when I threw a crust of bread at him.

Across the steps, Ines caught my eye. She'd been talking with a harbor official about the new courier routes—letters now, honest ones, though she'd confided to me that she missed the smuggling sometimes, the way a sailor misses storms. She crossed over and dropped onto the step beside me, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while we said nothing, which between us had become a fluent language.

"My brother would have liked this," she said eventually. "All of it. The stupid ceremony. The cider. You making a speech about feelings in public." Her voice did the thing it did when grief and warmth arrived in the same breath and she refused to choose between them. "He died carrying a map to save a city he barely knew. And the city's going to put up a plaque."

"He'd have hated the plaque."

"He'd have hated it so much." She leaned her head briefly against my shoulder, then straightened, businesslike again, because tenderness in bulk embarrassed her. "Pip wants to show you something. Before it goes."

My chest tightened. Right. Yes. That.

* * *

Pip led me up the side aisle, past the tide-marks, to the pillar where the map had first shown us the way down—where the singing had been loudest, where the drowned chapel door had answered. Pip unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of someone handling a sleeping animal.

The map lay open on both our palms. Two years of fading had bleached it almost blank: the ink of the streets gone ghost-gray, the seal of Ines's grandmother barely a watermark. But there, in the corner, one line remained—one thread of the old swordspeech, humming so faintly I felt it more in my teeth than my ears.

"One note," Pip whispered. "Listen."

I listened. And because I no longer had magic, I heard it the way ordinary people hear things—with my ears, and my memory, and whatever part of a person understands music without needing to understand words. It wasn't a direction anymore. It wasn't a key. It was just a phrase, the last phrase, the one the whole song had been walking toward, and translated—as I translated it, badly, in my head, the way you translate a language you'll never speak again—it said:

*Found. Found. Found.*

Not *go here.* Not *do this.* All that time, all those verses, all that dread and danger and answering darkness under the harbor—and the map had simply been repeating, in the oldest dialect of my order, the one thing every swordspeech begins with and every swordspeech ends with. The greeting. The roll call. The word we spoke across training yards to mean *I see you; you are not alone; you are accounted for.*

Found.

"It's waiting," Pip said softly. "It won't finish till you answer. That's how call-and-response works, silly. Even I know that."

My throat closed. Of course. Of course it was waiting. Everything in my life had been waiting for me to stop hiding—to answer the roll call instead of slipping out the back of it.

I bent close to the map. I had no resonance left, no power, nothing but a voice worn down to gravel and honesty. I gave it everything I had, which was very little, and it was enough:

"Found," I whispered.

The last note rose off the page like a bird off water. It circled the pillar once—Pip swore afterward it glowed, and I've stopped arguing with Pip about such things—and then it settled, and faded, and the map was only paper. Old, blank, beautiful paper. A grandmother's watermark. Nothing more.

Pip wrapped it up again, gently, and didn't cry, which was braver than anything I managed that day.

"What happens to it now?" I asked.

"Dunno." Pip considered. "Maybe it rests. Maybe it becomes a letter someday—paper's paper. Someone writes on it. Starts a new song." A shrug, philosophical, eight-going-on-eighty. "Songs don't die, Ulyana. They just wait for somebody to sing them."

From the steps, Ines's voice rang out across the nave, impatient and warm: "Are you two done communing with stationery? The cider's going warm and the king's telling embarrassing stories about Cassian!"

"We're coming!" Pip bellowed, and grabbed my hand, and towed me toward the light.

And I went. Present, findable, staying—no oath required, though I'd given one anyway, because some promises you make not because they bind you but because saying them out loud teaches your heart the shape of what it already knows.

Outside, the bells began. Inside, the silence where a song had been was not empty at all.

It sounded, if you listened the way Pip listens—

Exactly like home.
