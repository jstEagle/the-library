## 2. A Girl with Winter in Her Teeth

The girl arrived on the last day of Thawmonth, when the gutters of Vellamarch still ran brown with snowmelt and the guildhall's stone steps wept damp into the entryway. Orsen heard her before he saw her — a sound like something dragged, chains and boots and a third noise beneath both, low and rhythmic, that took him a moment to identify as growling.

He was at his desk in the Scriptorium, as he always was, hunched over the Ledger of Small Debts with his sleeves pinned back and ink to the second knuckle of his right hand. The ledger lay open before him, its pages soft as cloth from years of handling. In it he recorded the guild's collateral: the shadow pawned by a cooper from Lowbridge who'd needed coin for his daughter's fever; the name sold twice, once honestly and once not, belonging to a midwife in Harrow Fen; seven years of a fiddler's laughter, traded away in a card game the fiddler could no longer remember losing. Orsen wrote them all down in his small, precise hand, and told himself — as he told himself every morning, kneeling at his narrow cot before the bell rang — that the ledger only recorded what others had already done. Ink did not cause. Ink remembered. There was a difference, he said, and the difference was everything.

His ink disagreed.

It always disagreed, quietly, in ways only he could see. When he wrote a true thing — a thing that mattered, a thing with weight — the characters did not sit still on the page. They shivered. They leaned. Once, recording the sale of a storm-hag's second sight, he had watched the letters crawl toward the margin like men fleeing a burning house, and he had pressed his palm flat over them until they stilled, heart hammering, while Master Corvane's footsteps passed behind him without pause. No one else saw it. No one else could. That was the whole of his gift, the small shameful secret he kept folded beneath his tongue: ink moved when he wrote true things, and so he had learned, over eleven years of debt-bound service, to write as few true things as possible.

The growling came up the stairs.

Orsen set down his pen. Around him the other scribes bent over their work — pale men mostly, and two women, all of them debt-bound like himself, all of them wearing the gray collars of the Guild of Impossible Things, all of them pretending not to listen. Pretending was a skill the guild taught early. You learned to keep your eyes on your page while a debtor wept in the hall below, while a shadow was carried past in an iron box, while whatever was coming up the stairs made whatever sound it made.

Master Corvane appeared in the doorway first. He was a tall man built like a folded letter, all creases and corners, with a voice like dry paper tearing. Behind him came two guildsmen in leather aprons, and between them, held by a chain that ran from a collar at her throat to a bar they gripped between them like oarsmen, came the girl.

She was perhaps sixteen. Thin as a winter branch, with hair the color of wet slate cut raggedly at her jaw, and eyes that went around the room the way a wolf's eyes go around a firelit clearing — not afraid, exactly. Counting. Measuring the exits, the hands, the throats. Her wrists were raw where the manacles had been, and she walked with a loping, wrong-jointed gait, as though her body were a borrowed coat she hadn't yet learned to wear properly. And there was something else, something that raised the fine hairs along Orsen's arms: the air near her seemed colder. Not draft-cold. Winter-cold, a cold with intent behind it, rolling off her in slow waves so that the nearest candle guttered and the ink in Orsen's well grew briefly thick and sluggish.

"Here." Corvane snapped his fingers at Orsen without looking at him. "New entry. Collateral received against the Vellamouth shipping debt. Catalogue it."

*It.* Not *her*. Orsen rose, and his chair scraped, and the girl's head turned toward the sound with a speed that made one of the guildsmen flinch.

"What is she?" Orsen asked, keeping his voice level, clerkish, bored. Boredom was armor. He had learned that too.

"She's a wolf-gift," Corvane said, in the tone other men used for describing livestock. "Northern stock, taken at the border villages. The family ran a debt three generations deep and finally paid it in kind." His thin mouth curved. "Rare enough, these days. The southern courts pay beautifully for the old bloodlines. Fetch the catalogue ledger and the assessment kit. I want weight, age, manifestation class, and soul-depth. Full valuation."

"Soul-depth," Orsen repeated.

"The buyers ask." Corvane was already turning away. "Deep souls burn longer."

*Burn.* The word sat in the room after he left, the way smoke sits. The girl watched Corvane go with those counting eyes, and then she looked at Orsen, and Orsen found himself unable to look away. There was frost on the inside of her collar. He could see it, tiny ferns of ice blooming against the iron, melting and reforming with each breath she took, as if the metal itself couldn't decide whether to hold her or let her go.

"Sit," Orsen said.

"I'd rather not."

The guildsmen shoved her into the chair anyway. She went loose rather than resist — a boneless collapse, deliberate, the resistance of water — and then sat up again slowly, watching Orsen with an expression he would spend many nights afterward trying to name. It wasn't hatred. Hatred required hope. It was the look of someone watching a process begin that they had already decided not to survive politely.

* * *

The assessment took the better part of the afternoon.

Orsen had catalogued collateral for eleven years. Shadows, names, voices, memories, the ability to dream, the ability to weep, a woman's talent for making bread rise, a child's imaginary friend sold by practical parents to a collector in the capital. He knew the forms. Weight of soul, measured on the brass scale with its little pan and its counterweights ground from grave-glass. Manifestation class, marked by the guild's stamped sigils: minor, useful, potent, forbidden. Soul-depth, plumbed with a needle of black glass that you touched to the wrist, and which fogged white to a depth proportional to what the buyer would be consuming.

He began with the name.

"Name?"

"Ines."

"Just Ines?"

"Ines." Her voice was flat, northern-accented, each word bitten off short. "The rest of it they took already. At the border. You can check your papers." A thin smile showed teeth that were, he noticed, slightly too sharp. "You people are thorough."

He checked the papers. The family name had indeed been struck through in red — *reclaimed* — which meant that somewhere in the vaults below, her surname sat in a lead box, owned now by whoever had paid for it, and she would never speak it again because she no longer wholly possessed it. Orsen had written such entries a hundred times. He wrote this one, and his ink shivered on the page, and he ignored it.

"Age?"

"Old enough."

"Ines." He looked up. "The form requires—"

"The form can bite me."

One of the guildsmen laughed, then stopped when she looked at him. Orsen wrote *approx. sixteen* and moved on. The brass scale was worst. They made her place her hand in the pan — the scale didn't weigh flesh, it weighed what was under the flesh, and the needle swung and settled at a figure that made the senior guildsman whistle through his teeth. Orsen transcribed the number and felt something turn over in his chest, slow and cold, like ice shifting on a river.

Then the black-glass needle.

She jerked her wrist away from the guildsman's grip before he could touch her. "I'll do it myself," she said. "You don't get to touch me. Any of you. Write that down too, scribe. Put it in your book."

And because some part of Orsen — the part he didn't examine, the part that knelt every morning and recited *the ledger only records* — wanted suddenly, desperately, to give her one thing, he said: "Let her do it herself. It reads more accurately that way." Which was even true.

She took the needle. She pressed it to her own wrist with the steadiness of someone who had decided pain was just weather. The glass fogged white — then whiter — then whiter still, clouding from the tip down its whole length, deeper than Orsen had ever seen, deeper than the storm-hag, deeper than anything in the reference tables. Frost crawled out from under the needle across her skin in delicate branching lines, and the candle flames in the Scriptorium all leaned toward her at once, and for one clear moment Orsen felt what the instrument was feeling: depth without bottom. A soul like a well that had been dug down past the water table, past the rock, into the cold black places where old things sleep.

"Gods' teeth," the guildsman murmured. "Corvane'll buy her a new wing with this one."

Ines pulled the needle away. The frost faded. She looked at Orsen across the desk, and said, very quietly, in that flat northern voice:

"How much am I worth?"

Orsen's pen hovered. The honest answer was on his page: a figure with four zeroes, maybe five, a sum that would clear the Vellamouth debt twice over and dress the guildmasters in silk besides. He looked at the number. He looked at the girl with winter in her teeth, sitting straight-backed in a borrowed chair with iron cooling on her wrists, asking a scribe to tell her the price of herself.

"The ledger only records," he said, barely audible, to no one. To himself. The oldest prayer he had.

"What?"

"Nothing." He bent over the page. And then — he would think about this later, turning it over at night like a stone with something alive under it — his hand moved before his mind agreed to anything. He dipped his pen. He wrote the valuation line, and as he wrote it, he lied.

He adjusted the soul-depth figure downward. Not hugely — enough to pass a casual glance, not enough to rouse suspicion — but enough. Enough to drop her from the court tier to the provincial tier. Enough to mean weeks instead of days. Enough to mean that any buyer of consequence would pass her over, that she would sit in the guild's holding cells through the summer, unremarkable, unsold, alive. He wrote the false number in his small precise hand, and the ink did not move. Of course it didn't. False things sat perfectly still. Only truth made his ink dance, and so the lie lay quiet and clean on the page like a sleeping snake, and Orsen understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had just discovered the one use of his gift he had never dared imagine: the moving ink could tell him when he wrote true. Which meant its stillness could tell him when he didn't.

He had spent eleven years using that silence to hide. Now he had used it to falsify.

"There," he said, when it was done. His voice sounded far away to him. "Valuation complete. You'll be entered at provincial rate pending re-assessment in the autumn."

"Autumn," Ines repeated.

"The guild re-assesses all unsold collateral seasonally. It's procedure." Procedure. The word tasted like ash. He was giving her a season. He was buying her a summer with a lie, and calling it procedure, and the girl was looking at him with those counting eyes that had stopped counting exits and were now, for the first time, counting *him*.

"Why?" she asked.

Orsen capped his inkwell. "Because the form was misfiled," he said. "These things happen."

They were both silent. The guildsmen unlatched her collar from the chain and marched her out — not gently, but not cruelly either, because cruelty cost extra effort and the guild paid by the task — and at the door she turned and looked back at him once. Just once. Frost bloomed and died on the doorframe where her hand touched it, a handprint of ice that melted slowly into the wood long after she was gone.

* * *

That night Orsen did not kneel at his cot.

He sat at his desk instead, with the lamp turned low, and read back through the day's entries until he reached hers. *Ines. Approx. sixteen. Wolf-gift, northern stock. Soul-depth: 4.* The false number sat there, quiet, perfect, damning. Below it, in the margin, his pen had — he checked three times to be sure — left a single mark he did not remember making. A tiny flourish, half a character, the ghost of a stroke. His ink, moving on its own, the way it did when he wrote true things.

But he hadn't written anything true there. He had written a lie.

Unless.

Unless the true thing was not the number. Unless the true thing was the reason for it, the thing he had refused to say aloud in the Scriptorium, the thing that had no column on any guild form: *she is a person, and I helped sell her, and I will not do it cleanly.* The ink had recorded that. The ink always recorded what mattered. It had been doing it for eleven years, patiently, in margins and flourishes and half-strokes, keeping a truer ledger inside his false one, waiting for him to read it.

Orsen sat very still in the lamplight, listening to the guildhall settle around him — timbers ticking, rain beginning against the high windows, somewhere below the muffled clank of the holding cells where a girl with winter in her teeth was spending the first night of the summer he had bought her.

*Autumn,* he thought. *Re-assessment in autumn.*

That gave him one season. One season to learn how a valuation was corrected, how a sale was rerouted, how a soul might be struck from a ledger entirely — or, failing that, one season to decide what he was willing to become in order to keep lying. Because he understood now, with the terrible calm of a man reading his own sentence, that he would not be able to stop. The ledger only records, he had told himself for eleven years. But the ink had known better all along. Ink remembers. And tomorrow, when Master Corvane sent down the quarterly summaries to the southern buyers — when the court beneath the mountain's own factors came sniffing through Vellamarch with their deep pockets and their bottomless appetites — a misfiled form would be the only wall between that girl and the tithe.

Outside, the rain thickened. Orsen drew the ledger toward him, uncapped his ink, and began — carefully, quietly, in the space between one line and the next — to plan.
