## 3. The Crown's Proclamation

The bells woke Vellamarch before the sun did.

Orsen heard them from his cot in the guildhall's undercroft — six strokes, then silence, then six again, which was not any pattern the city used for fire or flood or the death of a guildmaster. It was the pattern for proclamation. He had copied enough civic ordinances to know it by heart, though he had never once heard it rung in earnest. In nineteen years of service to the Guild of Impossible Things, the bells had announced nothing but tax seasons and the occasional scandalous wedding.

He dressed in the dark, fingers finding each buckle by memory, and climbed the stairs into a morning that smelled wrong. Woodsmoke, yes — Vellamarch always smelled of woodsmoke in autumn — but beneath it something sharper, like iron left out in rain. Like fear given a scent.

In the great hall, the other scribes were already gathered at the long windows, and no one was working. That alone told Orsen how bad it was. Master Quillon had once made a junior copyist finish an inventory during his own father's funeral.

Below, in the square of Saint Aldhelm's Well, the heralds had assembled on the guild steps. There were three of them, which meant royal authority — one herald was a lord's whim, two were a magistrate's errand, but three came only from the crown itself. They wore the king's colors, silver and ash-grey, and their trumpets caught the first light and threw it back in long cold blades.

One of them unrolled a scroll the length of a man's arm and began to read.

Even from the third floor, Orsen heard every word. That was the trouble with proclamations; they were written to be heard through walls, through shutters, through the hands people pressed over their children's ears.

*By order of His Majesty, Aldric the Second of that name, let it be known throughout the marches and holdings of the crown: that all persons bearing the taint of gift — the shifting, the seeing, the speaking-to, the making-move, and all other workings unnatural to honest folk — shall present themselves at the nearest crown office within forty days, there to be registered, assessed, and bound to lawful service.*

*Those who conceal themselves, or who harbor the concealed, shall be accounted traitors.*

*Reward is offered for information leading to the taking of any gifted person: ten crowns the head, fifty crowns the whole and living body.*

*Let none say they were not warned.*

The herald's voice did not change once through all of it. That was the worst part, Orsen thought. Not fury, not triumph. Just procedure. The kingdom had decided, the way a mill decides, and the water would keep turning whether the grain screamed or not.

Beside him, young Pellam whispered, "Ten crowns. For a *head*." He said it the way boys say terrible things — testing whether the words will hold weight.

"Quiet," said Master Quillon, but softly, and he was looking not at the square but at the guildmasters' gallery above it, where the masters of Vellamarch's four great houses stood in a row like crows on a fence. Orsen followed his gaze and saw what Quillon saw: Guildmaster Hesse of the House of Small Debts already descending the steps toward the heralds, already bowing, already smiling the smile he wore when a bargain was about to be struck.

Orsen's stomach turned over slowly, like a page falling.

"The crown needs clerks," Quillon murmured, mostly to himself. "Registries. Assessment rolls. Someone must write down who gets taken." He glanced at Orsen, and there was something in the look that Orsen could not read — invitation, or warning, or both folded together like a letter never sent. "You balance accounts better than anyone I have ever taught."

*I don't want to write down who gets taken,* Orsen thought, and did not say it, because he had spent nineteen years learning exactly which thoughts stayed thoughts.

Down in the square, the crowd was doing the thing crowds do — pulling apart around certain people without quite knowing why. A woman with a birthmark shaped like a moth found herself standing in an empty circle of cobblestones. Two brothers who ran the ferry and were said to be able to call fish up from the mud suddenly had no neighbors near them. Nobody spoke. Nobody pointed. But the space opened around them the way water opens around a stone, and Orsen understood that the hunt would not need hunters, not at first. Neighbors would do the work for free, and then the ten crowns would arrive to make it official.

He turned away from the window and went down to the counting room, because if his hands were moving ink he did not have to think, and because — though he would not have admitted it even under torture — he wanted to see the ledger where her name was.

* * *

The entry still read as he had written it, eleven days ago:

*Item: one soul, female, age approx. sixteen years, wolf-touched, received as collateral against the debt of Rennick of Harrow Fen. Condition: damaged but viable. Disposition: held.*

*Held.* The smallest lie he had ever told, and the largest. The truth was that she had been sold the same afternoon she arrived — sold onward, upriver, to a buyer whose seal Orsen had recognized and wished he hadn't. He had entered *held* instead, and bought her perhaps a season of being unfindable, and hated himself for how little a season was.

Her name was Ines. She had come into the guildhall in chains that were too big for her wrists, biting the hand of the man who fed her, and when Orsen had been ordered to catalogue her — to stand close and *look*, the way the guild looked at everything, appraising — she had lifted her head and looked back, and her eyes had been winter-colored, grey going white at the rim, and she had said, in a voice scraped raw from disuse:

"You write things down. Write down that I'm nobody. Write down that you got the wrong girl."

And his ink had moved.

It always moved when he wrote true things — had done since he was a child, since the first time he'd copied out *my father is dying* and watched the letters crawl on the page like insects waking. It was the gift he hid the way other people hid scars: sleeves pulled down, ink kept covered, true things written rarely and burned after. But that day, cataloguing her, he had written *the subject shows no unusual properties* — a lie, safe, dead ink — and then, below it, in letters so small they might have been a slip of the pen, he had written *she is more afraid than dangerous,* and the ink had *moved*, had writhed like something alive trying to get warm, and he had slammed the ledger shut with his heart hammering and prayed to whatever governed ledgers that no one else had seen.

She had seen. Of course she had. Wolf-gifted; she saw the way wolves see, movement first. And in the moment before they took her away she had looked at him again, and this time there had been something else in the winter of her eyes — not gratitude, nothing so gentle. Recognition. *You're one of us,* her look had said. *You're just better at hiding.*

Now he sat with the ledger open to her entry and read *held* until the word stopped looking like mercy and started looking like what it was: a delay. A season. And the season had just been cancelled by three heralds and six bells.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not avoid, the sum that assembled itself in his head with the horrible neatness of all ledger-work: the crown was buying the gifted. Fifty crowns for a whole and living body. The guild dealt in souls — that was the trade no one said aloud, the trade the ledgers recorded in euphemism (*one shadow, pawned; one name, sold twice; one dream, repossessed*) — and the crown had just become the richest buyer in the market. Every debt-bound gifted person in every guildhouse in the kingdom was now inventory. And inventory that the crown wanted was inventory the guildmasters would sell *fast*, before the crown thought to ask why the guilds had been hoarding such valuable stock.

Including, Orsen realized with a cold that started in his teeth and spread downward, including the entry marked *held.*

Including, he realized a heartbeat later, colder still, *me.*

His own gift sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. Ink that moved when he wrote truth. Under the new law, that made him not a clerk of the hunt but its quarry. Nineteen years of careful, gray, invisible service — and one proclamation had redrawn the columns, moved him from the ledger's margin into its entries. He had spent his whole life believing that the ledger only recorded, never caused. Now the ledger was coming for its keeper.

Master Quillon appeared in the doorway of the counting room. He was an old man built like a filing cabinet, all drawers and corners, and Orsen had trusted him the way one trusts weather — impersonally, because it had always been there.

"Guildmaster Hesse wants the collateral rolls," Quillon said. "All of them. Gifted debtors flagged for transfer assessment." He set a fresh quill on Orsen's desk, unnecessarily, the way men offer a rope to someone already drowning. "By noon."

"Transfer to where?" Orsen asked. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, in a distant, sickened way.

"To the crown offices. And some—" Quillon paused, and Orsen watched him choose between honesty and habit, and watched habit win, as it always won, "—some to private buyers. There are those with standing offers. The mountain court pays well for wolf-touched, particularly."

The room tilted very slightly, the way a floor tilts in the last moment before a fall.

"When," Orsen said, "was the wolf-touched girl transferred?"

Quillon's eyes flicked to the open ledger, to the entry, to the word *held*. Old man; sharp old man; he had taught Orsen everything Orsen knew about reading entries. Something passed across his face that might, in a younger man, have been called guilt.

"Eleven days ago," he said. "Buyer's agent collected her the same evening. You knew that, Orsen. You wrote the receiving copy yourself."

Had he? Had he written the receiving copy, the duplicate that went to the buyer, confirming the sale he had falsified in his own book? He tried to remember and found the memory strangely soft-edged, like something read rather than lived. Eleven days ago. Eleven days of telling himself *held* meant *safe*. Eleven days of a lie he had told himself along with the ledger.

"The mountain court," Orsen said. "Under the mountain. What does a court want with—"

"What courts always want," Quillon said quietly. "To last." And then, briskly, terribly kindly: "Flag the rolls, Orsen. Do your work. Work is the safest place to be right now, for anyone."

He left. Orsen sat with the ledger open and understood, finally, completely, the shape of the machine he had served. It had never been neutral. It had never merely *recorded*. Every entry he had ever balanced was a gear, and the gears turned, and now the whole mechanism had been bought by the crown and repointed, like a weapon, at everyone it contained — the debtors, the collateral, the girls with winter in their teeth, and the scribes.

Especially the scribes. Because who better to find the hidden gifted than the man who kept their books? Who better to hunt than the one who knew every trick of concealment because he had invented half of them?

*Forty days,* the herald had said. Forty days to register, or be taken.

Orsen looked down at the ledger, at Ines's entry, and did the only thing his hands knew how to do. He dipped the quill. Below *disposition: held*, in letters small enough to deny, he added a line:

*Buyer: the court beneath the mountain. Destination: the tithe halls.*

The ink moved. It writhed off the page like something burning, like something screaming, black tendrils curling at the edges of each letter, and Orsen pressed his palm flat over it and felt it pulse against his skin like a second heartbeat.

True things. The ink moved for true things. And somewhere under the mountain, in a place called the tithe halls, a girl with grey-going-white eyes was about to learn what courts wanted in order to last.

He closed the book. Outside, the bells rang the proclamation pattern a third time, and in the square below, he heard the first voice of the new kingdom rise — someone calling out a neighbor's name, not in greeting, and the crowd surging toward the guild steps like water finding a drain.

Forty days. He had forty days, maybe fewer, and a falsified ledger that would hang him the moment anyone audited it, and a debt to the guild that was measured — he had never been allowed to see the figure — in years of his life.

Orsen Villalobos picked up the collateral rolls and began, with steady hands and a mind already running like a fugitive through flooded streets, to flag them.

And on the fourth roll, in the margin beside a debtor's name he did not recognize, he found a note in a clerk's hand that was not his own — small, hurried, and signed with a single initial: *M.*

*They know about you too,* it said.
