## 5. The Road of Borrowed Names

*Orsen*

The road out of Vellamarch was called the Pilgrim's Way, and it had never in living memory carried so few pilgrims.

Orsen walked it with his hood up and his hands buried in his sleeves, because his hands were the problem now. They wanted to write. Even without ink, even without paper, his fingers moved against his palms in small restless strokes, tracing words he did not dare speak aloud — *north, north, the river forks at Hallowmere, Maren walks too fast when she is frightened.* His gift had always been a quiet thing, a secret he kept folded like a letter in an inside pocket. Now every mile put distance between him and the guildhall, and the secret seemed to grow louder for the silence around it.

Maren walked ahead of him, her clerk's grey coat exchanged for a drover's oiled canvas that smelled of tallow and rain. She had stolen it from a fence post outside the city gates, along with half a loaf and a jar of pickled onions, and she had not apologized for any of it.

"You're limping," she said, without turning around.

"I'm favoring my left foot."

"That's what limping is." She slowed until they were side by side. The road bent here, following the old canal that had once fed the tanneries, its water gone green and still as bottle glass. "There's a culvert under the next bridge. We can sleep there if the water stays low."

"You've done this before," Orsen said. "Run."

"I've *moved quickly* before." Her mouth twitched, the ghost of a smile that didn't survive the daylight. "Different thing entirely. Running is when you don't know where you're going. Moving quickly is when you do."

"And which is this?"

Maren looked down the long grey reach of the road, at the crows walking the hedgerows like little clerks inspecting accounts, and said nothing, which was answer enough.

They had been two days out of Vellamarch. In that time Orsen had learned three things about the woman who had burned his confession and pulled him into the dark: she slept sitting up, back to a wall, one hand curled around whatever was nearest to use as a weapon; she counted things under her breath when she was afraid — steps, coins, birds, anything; and she had not once asked him about the moving ink, though she had seen it write a true sentence on his own skin and had chosen, deliberately, to say nothing.

He asked her about it on the third morning, over a fire no larger than a cupped hand, built in the lee of a fallen wall.

"Why didn't you turn me in? Back at the inspection. You could have. It would have been safer."

Maren fed a twig to the flames and watched it catch. "I spent eleven years in that archive," she said. "Eleven years shelving souls alphabetically. Do you know what I thought about, most days?" She didn't wait for him to guess. "I thought about how tidy it all was. How everything fit. And then one day I realized the tidiness was the horror — that we'd built a machine so clean you could forget it was eating people. And then your ink moved, and wrote something *true*, right there on your wrist, and I thought—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I thought, here is one untidy thing. Here is one thing the ledger can't hold flat. I wasn't going to be the one to file it away."

Orsen looked at his own hands. "It writes true things," he said. "That's all it does. It doesn't cause anything. The ledger only records—"

"The ledger only records," Maren echoed, and there was something in her voice that was not quite mockery and not quite kindness. "Yes. I told myself that too. For eleven years." She lay back against her pack and closed her eyes. "Sleep, scribe. Your shift ends at moonrise. Wake me gently — I startle loud."

*Maren*

The boy came out of the orchard on the fourth day, and Maren heard him before she saw him, which was strange, because the boy made no sound at all.

She had been walking point through a stand of bare apple trees, their branches silvered with lichen, counting her steps the way she did — forty-one, forty-two — when the count broke. Not because of noise but because of its absence. The birds had stopped. All of them, all at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters it.

Then, from behind a mossy trunk: "Don't come closer. Please. I'll hear it if you mean harm."

Maren stopped. Forty-three steps from the road, forty-one back. "Hear what?"

"What people are." A thin figure edged into view — a boy, twelve perhaps, maybe fourteen and starved down to younger, wearing a shepherd's coat cut for a much bigger man with the sleeves rolled six times. His eyes were enormous and red-rimmed, and they weren't looking at her face. They were looking somewhere near her chest, or through it. "You sound like a door that's been locked a long time. But the lock's rusted through. You'd open easy, if someone pushed." He swallowed. "The man behind you sounds like a book. A wet one."

"Maren," she said carefully. "And the wet book is Orsen. He's harmless. Mostly damp."

The boy's gaze flicked past her shoulder, and whatever he heard in Orsen made him take a step forward instead of back. "He's got a door in him that isn't shut all the way. Something's been coming out of it." His voice dropped to a whisper, awed and frightened in equal measure. "Ink. I can hear it scratching."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind them, Orsen's footsteps had gone very still.

"Your name?" Maren asked.

"Tobin." Then, quickly, as if paying a debt before it was demanded: "I didn't steal anything. I only listened. The tollhouse on the Fen Road — I heard them opening the strongbox before dawn, and I told the carter, and the carter sold me out to the crown men because there's a bounty now, did you know? Two crowns for a gifted child brought in alive." He laughed, a horrible small sound. "Alive. Like a chicken at market."

"We know," Maren said.

"They took my mother's house. Not arrested her — just, took it. Put a seal on the door and the door *screamed* when they did it, did you know doors scream? Nobody believes me about the doors." His chin was trembling now, and he locked it still with visible effort, the way children learn to do when crying has become dangerous. "Everyone's doors scream lately."

Maren looked back at Orsen. He stood among the apple trees with his hood down for once, and his face had gone the particular grey of a man reading his own name in a list of the condemned. She watched him weigh something — she would learn later that this was what he did, weighed and priced and balanced, the habit of a lifetime of ledgers — and then he stepped forward and knelt, slowly, to bring himself below the boy's eyeline.

"Tobin," he said. "Can you hear whether we're lying?"

The boy tilted his head, listening to some frequency beneath the world's ordinary noise. "No," he said. "Lies sound like nothing. That's how you tell. Truth has a hinge. It creaks." He pointed a dirty finger at Orsen's chest. "Everything you say creaks."

"Then hear this," Orsen said. "We have no food worth taking, no plan worth stealing, and no reason in the world to hurt you. But we're going north, and the roads south are full of men who pay two crowns for children. If you walk with us, you walk in the middle. Never first, never last. Can you live with those terms?"

Tobin looked between them — the woman with the rusted lock, the man full of scratching ink — and Maren saw him do the same arithmetic she had done four days ago in a flooded tunnel: not *can I trust them* but *can I afford not to.*

"Middle," Tobin agreed. "But I get to listen for doors. You'd be surprised how many things are doors."

*Orsen*

Sefa they found by accident, or Sefa found them by accident, depending on whose account you believed, and the disagreement itself became the first argument of their strange traveling household.

It happened at dusk on the sixth day, in the ruins of a waystation chapel where the roof had fallen in but one corner still held, sheltering a hearth black with old fires. Orsen arrived first with Tobin, laid the fire, and went to the broken doorway to watch the road for Maren — and found an old woman already standing there, watching *him*, with a basket on her arm and an expression of profound disappointment, as though he were a delivery that had come late and damaged.

"You're in my chapel," she said.

"It says abandoned on every map I've ever read."

"Maps." She sniffed and pushed past him into the ruin, setting her basket down with the authority of a general claiming high ground. "Maps are drawn by men who think a place is empty because *they* aren't in it. Sit down, boy, you're letting the cold in. Both of you. There's nettle soup and there's enough for three, and if a fourth comes along she'll go hungry, so let's hope your other one hurries."

"How did you know there was another one?"

The old woman — Sefa, though he didn't learn the name until later — was already hanging a dented pot over the fire with the ease of long practice. "Because you keep looking at the door like it owes you money," she said. "People with nobody coming don't look at doors like that."

When Maren arrived twenty minutes later, breathless and suspicious, sword-hand hovering near her knife, Sefa looked her up and down and said, "You'll do," and ladled out a fourth bowl anyway, from a pot that should not have held it. Orsen counted the bowls twice. There were four. There had been ingredients for three. When he mentioned this, Sefa fixed him with a look like a nail being driven.

"Don't count my soup, scribe. Some things refuse arithmetic, and soup is one of them."

She was a hedge-witch, though she preferred "herbalist with certain irregular competencies." She had lived thirty years in a cottage at the edge of the Fenwood, delivering babies, curing fevers, and quietly unhexing the occasional cursed well, and she had fled three days ago when a crown proctor nailed a proclamation to her own front door listing her name among the *Anomalous Persons of the Eastern Reaches*. She spoke of it without bitterness, in the tone of someone describing weather.

"Forty years I pulled their children into the world," she said, stirring the pot. "Pulled their mothers out of death's grip more times than I can number. And now I'm an anomaly." She tasted the soup, added salt from a twist of paper. "Well. Anomaly is just a word for a thing the ledger doesn't have a column for. I expect you know something about that."

Orsen said nothing. Across the fire, Maren was watching him with that careful, patient attention she sometimes turned on him, like a person watching a crack spread slowly across a dam.

They ate. Tobin fell asleep mid-chew, upright, exhausted past sense, and Sefa eased him down onto her own bedroll without waking him, murmuring something under her breath that made the boy's frown smooth out. Maren established the watch order — herself, then Orsen, then Sefa, then Tobin, though everyone privately agreed the boy would sleep through his turn and no one would wake him — and the chapel settled into the small domestic sounds of strangers becoming, without anyone announcing it, a camp.

Orsen took first watch by the doorway. The night was clear and hard, stars sharp as nailheads, and the road ran pale toward the northern hills where, somewhere, a girl with winter in her teeth was either dead or worse than dead. He found himself tracing words on his knee in the old unconscious way — *north, Hallowmere, the mountain* — and stopped himself, and stared at his fingers as if they belonged to a stranger.

"She's alive, you know."

Sefa had come to sit beside him, silent as her profession allowed, a blanket around her shoulders. "The wolf-girl. Don't look so startled. You talk in your sleep, and your hands talk even when you're awake. Whole conversations, your fingers have. Terrible gossips." She followed his gaze up the dark road. "Whatever they took her to buy, it hasn't finished her. Things that are finished stop pulling. She pulls. Like a tide. I felt it the moment you lot walked into my chapel — a thread running north, tight as wire, and every one of you tied to the end of it."

"You felt that?"

"I'm an anomaly," Sefa said dryly. "We feel things." She patted his arm once, briskly, the way she might splint a fracture. "Sleep when your watch is done, boy. Grief keeps. It'll still be there tomorrow."

* * *

*Maren*

On the ninth day, Maren understood that they had become a family, and it frightened her more than the proclamations did.

It happened at a ford, of all places — a wide brown sprawl of river running shallow over gravel, the kind of crossing you wade without thinking. But Tobin stopped dead at the bank, head cocked, listening, and went white.

"There's a door," he whispered. "Under the water. An old one. It's *angry*."

Maren had learned in nine days that Tobin's doors were not always doors. Sometimes they were wells, or cellars, or the sealed mouths of old mine shafts — any threshold that had been shut with intention, with feeling, with blood sometimes. The world, according to Tobin, was full of places that had been closed by people who meant it, and those closures hummed like struck bells for anyone with ears to hear.

"Sefa," she called. "The ford."

The old woman came and stood at the bank, and her face changed. "Ward-water," she muttered. "Someone drowned this crossing on purpose, years back. Sealed it with a grudge." She rolled up her sleeves. "Right. Everyone hold hands like children at a fair, and whatever grabs at your ankles, you do *not* let go of each other. That's the whole trick of it. Grudges work alone. They can't abide company."

They crossed in a line, Maren first, Orsen's hand clamped around hers, Tobin gripping his coat hem, Sefa bringing up the rear muttering in a language that predated the kingdom. Halfway across, the water went cold as ice in a band no wider than a cart track, and something brushed Maren's ankles — not weeds, something with *intention* — and Tobin made a small terrified sound, and Orsen's grip tightened, and Sefa snapped "*Together!*" like a drill sergeant, and then they were through, stumbling out onto the far gravel, soaked to the knees and laughing.

Laughing. All four of them, standing in the shallows, gasping and laughing like fools, and Maren caught herself doing it and felt the bottom drop out of her chest.

Because here was the thing she had not told any of them: she had run alone before. Twice. She knew the mechanics of it — the sleeping in ditches, the false names, the way you stopped speaking altogether after a while because there was no one to speak to and speech attracted ears. She had survived it. Survival was possible alone.

But she had forgotten this. The stupid, unbearable warmth of it. Four strangers dripping river water, arguing about whose fault the ford was, dividing a heel of bread into exact quarters with the solemnity of priests. Tobin teaching Orsen to whistle through a blade of grass, badly. Sefa mending the tear in Maren's coat without being asked, because "you walk like a woman whose coat bothers her, and life's too short."

That night, on her watch, Maren sat with her back against a stone wall and counted the sounds of the camp: Orsen's breathing, slow and even; Tobin talking in his sleep about hinges; Sefa's fire ticking down to embers. Four heartbeats' worth of noise, and every bit of it a liability — harder to hide, slower to move, more mouths, more grief available if the crown's men came in the night.

Every bit of it worth more than safety.

She was still turning that arithmetic over, unable to make it balance in any ledger she'd ever been taught, when Tobin sat bolt upright in his bedroll, eyes wide open and utterly blind with sleep, and said in a voice not quite his own:

"There's a door in the mountain. And it's been opened so many times it's worn smooth. And something is coming *through* it — something with teeth — and it's following the thread. It's following *us*."

The fire popped. Far off in the dark pines, too far to be anything, close enough to be heard — a sound rose and fell, long and low and unmistakable.

A wolf, howling. Where no wolves had lived for a hundred years.
