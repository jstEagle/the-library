## 6. The Wolf That Wasn't There

Maren had learned to read roads the way other clerks read ledgers — by what was written on them, and by what had been scratched out.

This one told a story in the language of mud and hoofprint. The ruts of the transport wagon ran north-northeast toward the mountain road, deep-cut and recent, no more than four days old by the softness of their edges. But half a mile back, where the pines thickened and the ground rose into stony shelves, the story changed. The ruts swerved. There were drag-marks. And there was blood — not much of it now, rain having done its patient work, but enough to stain the stones a rust-brown that no weather fully forgives.

"Here," she said, and crouched, pressing two fingers to the stone. Cold. The blood had gone cold days ago. She wiped her fingers on her cloak without thinking about it, then thought about it, then made herself stop thinking about it.

Orsen came up beside her, breathing hard from the climb. He still moved like a scribe, she'd noticed — like a man whose body was an afterthought, something he kept in a back room and visited occasionally. His eyes went to the drag-marks, then to the swerved ruts, and she watched him do the arithmetic she'd already finished.

"The escort stopped here," he said quietly.

"The escort stopped being an escort here."

Tobin crouched at the edge of the tree line, his whole body angled toward the wagon tracks the way it always angled toward doors — listening with parts of him that weren't ears. The boy hadn't spoken since they'd found the first body a mile back, and Maren didn't press him. Tobin's silences were load-bearing. You learned not to lean on them too hard.

Sefa arrived last, as Sefa always did, walking with the unhurried gait of a woman who had decided long ago that rushing was for people who believed they could outrun things. She looked at the blood, at the torn earth, at the way three young saplings had been snapped off at waist height — not cut, *snapped*, the wood splintered into pale fraying ends — and she said nothing for a long moment.

"Well," she said finally. "Someone objected to the journey."

* * *

They followed the wreckage north through the falling afternoon light.

The escort had been eight men, by the count of bedrolls and boots and the guild's own manifest, which Orsen carried folded inside his coat like a confession. Crown men, mostly — conscripted for the transport because the guild's own people had better uses — with two guild wardens riding shotgun, the kind who wore iron rings on every finger and never took them off, even to eat. Maren knew the type. She'd been desk-adjacent to that type for eleven years. They were the ones who signed the transfer orders without reading them, or worse, read them with pleasure.

The first body they'd found had been a crown man, face-down in a stream, and Maren had felt almost nothing, which frightened her more than grief would have. The second and third they found together at the base of a granite shelf: the two wardens, and this was where the scene stopped making sense.

They hadn't been killed so much as *unmade*. One of them was simply gone from the chest down, the armor peeled open like fruit skin. The other had been thrown — thrown — twenty feet into a pine, and the pine had lost. Iron rings scattered around him in the needles, every one of them split down the middle, sheared clean as if by a jeweler's blade.

"Iron doesn't shear," Orsen said. He was holding one of the rings, turning it over. His voice had that careful flatness he used when he was keeping himself level, the voice of a man balancing books. "Warden-grade iron is warded. It's supposed to hold against anything gifted."

"Supposed to," Sefa said. She was examining the splintered saplings again, running her thumb along the break. "And yet."

Maren stood over the ruined warden and tried to feel what she was supposed to feel. Justice? Horror? All she could find was a cold, crawling question, and she asked it out loud before she could stop herself:

"How strong does someone have to be to do this?"

Nobody answered. The pines answered instead, in their way — a long shudder of wind moving through them from the north, from the direction of the mountain, bending the tops together with a sound like the sea heard through a wall.

Tobin flinched. Maren saw it happen: the boy's head snapping up, his eyes going wide and fixed on nothing, on some interior door only he could hear.

"What is it?" she said. "What do you hear?"

His mouth worked. When Tobin spoke it always seemed to cost him something, like each word was a coin he had to dig out of his pocket.

"Not a door," he said. "A... breathing. Big. Far away." His hands curled into fists. "It's not coming closer. It's just — standing. Out there. Standing and breathing."

"Where out there?"

He pointed north. Then, slowly, his arm swinging, he pointed east. Then west. Then, with visible reluctance, south.

Maren's mouth went dry. "All of them?"

"It moves," Tobin whispered. "When we move. It stops when we stop. It's been doing it since the stream." He looked up at her, and his face was the face of a boy who had spent his whole life hearing things nobody else could hear and had finally, finally been believed, and wished desperately he hadn't been. "I thought it was my head. I hoped it was my head."

* * *

They made camp early, in a hollow ringed with boulders, because Sefa said you didn't push a day like this one into the dark, and because none of them wanted to say aloud the real reason, which was that if something was following them, they would rather meet it with firelight than without.

Maren took first watch, which was to say she took the watch nobody else wanted and pretended it was generosity. She sat on the highest boulder with her back to the fire's glow and her face to the black wall of pines, and she watched, and she listened, and she thought about Ines.

She'd seen the girl exactly once, in the guildhall at Vellamarch, through the slats of a holding-room door Maren had no business opening. A girl maybe seventeen, chained at wrist and throat, sitting very still on a bench built for grown men, with winter-colored eyes and a stillness that wasn't calm — it was coiled, it was held breath, it was a drawn bow pretending to be furniture. Maren had looked at her for perhaps four seconds. Ines had looked back. And in those four seconds Maren had understood two things with total certainty: that this girl was going to kill someone someday, and that whoever she killed would deserve it.

Then Orsen had falsified her entry — Maren hadn't known that then, wouldn't learn it until the night the ink betrayed them both and they fled through the flooded archive tunnels side by side, gasping in the dark while the water rose — and the guild had sold her onward anyway, to the court beneath the mountain, and the transport had come up this road.

And now the transport lay in pieces along it.

"Tell me what you're calculating," Orsen said, appearing beside her boulder with two cups of Sefa's bitter pine-needle tea. He handed one up. "You get a look. Like the ledger's not balancing."

"Everything balances," Maren said. "That's the problem. Look at the numbers, Orsen. Eight armed men, two of them warded, escorting one girl in chains. And every single one of them dead or scattered, and the chains—" She nodded toward the wagon bed they'd found overturned a quarter mile back. They'd examined it by daylight. The manacles were intact. The chain between them was intact. What wasn't intact was the anchor bolt, sheared through, and the iron collar itself, opened like a birdcage door, its hinge bent outward.

Not broken. *Opened.* Carefully, almost gently, the way you'd open a locket.

"She didn't tear free," Maren said. "She got out. Someone let her out, or she convinced the iron to change its mind, or—" She drank the terrible tea. "Or the thing that did all this isn't the girl anymore. It's whatever the mountain's tithe-takers were making her into when they fed on her soul. And it's been pacing us for a day, matching us step for step, and I cannot decide if that's the best news I've ever had or the worst."

Orsen was quiet for a while. Firelight moved on his face, and Maren noticed — not for the first time — how tired he looked in a way that sleep didn't touch. Guilt had a metabolism of its own. It ate all night.

"You think it's her," he said.

"I think I hope it's her. Hoping and thinking have stopped speaking to each other somewhere back on this road." Maren turned the cup in her hands. "Here's what I keep coming back to. If it were just a beast — some wolf-shape the tithe left behind, all appetite and nothing else — it would have hit us by now. We're slow, we're loud, we smell like fear and hedge-magic. A beast doesn't pace a camp for a full day. A beast doesn't open a collar like it's being polite." She looked at him. "Something out there is choosing. Every hour, it chooses not to come here. Either it can't, or—"

"Or it's deciding whether we're worth it," Orsen finished.

"Or whether we're *who* it's looking for." Maren set down the cup. "Orsen. We're the only people in this kingdom walking toward the mountain instead of away from it. If any part of that girl remembers the guildhall — remembers the ledger, remembers who lied in it to buy her time —"

"Then she might be following us," he said softly. "Because we're the trail that leads to her hunters."

"Or because we're the trail that leads to her," Maren said. "Both of those are the same footprints, Orsen. That's what scares me."

Below them, Tobin sat up abruptly in his blankets, eyes wide open, staring north into the trees.

* * *

It came at moonrise, or near enough that Maren would always afterward blame the moon.

She heard Sefa wake first — the old woman made no sound, but the fire shifted, a handful of something thrown on the coals, and the light leapt, and in the leaping light Maren saw the treeline change.

There was a gap in the pines. A wrongness in the pattern of trunks, the way a missing tooth changes a smile. Her eyes kept sliding off it, insisting it was shadow, insisting it was nothing, and she had to fight them, blink by blink, to hold the shape in place: low, broad, still. Shoulders higher than a horse's. Eyes that caught the firelight and gave it back the color of deep ice under black water.

Winter-colored eyes.

"Ines?" Maren said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of the name like a dropped plate.

The shape didn't move. The forest breathed around it. Somewhere behind her, Tobin was whispering — not words, more a counting, the way he counted when the doors got loud — and Sefa had risen to her feet with her arms slightly out from her sides, palms open, in the posture of a woman greeting something she wasn't sure she wanted to greet.

Orsen stepped forward. Of course he did. Maren grabbed for his coat and missed, and he walked out past the firelight's edge with his empty hands raised, a scribe in a nightshirt approaching a thing that had unmade warded iron, and he said, in the gentlest voice she'd ever heard him use:

"We know what they took from you. We're going to the mountain to take it back."

The shape's head tilted. Slowly. The movement had something in it that was not animal — animals don't hesitate, don't weigh, don't *listen for meaning*. For one suspended moment Maren let herself believe, wholly and stupidly, that the girl was in there, that the girl had been in there all along, pacing them through the pines because she remembered a lie told kindly in a ledger, because a stranger's small false mercy was the last warm thing anyone had ever given her—

The shape exhaled. Even at forty yards, Maren felt it move the air against her face, smelled it: snow, blood, wet fur, and beneath it something older and stranger, like the air in a sealed room, like paper left too long in the dark.

Then it turned, unhurried, and was gone between the trees without a single branch moving, and the forest closed behind it like water over a stone.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The fire popped. Tobin stopped counting.

"It knows us," Orsen said at last. His voice was steady; his hands, Maren saw, were not. "It heard me say *mountain*, and it turned north before we did. It's not hunting us." He turned back toward the fire, and his face in the light was the face of a man reading a number he'd hoped not to find. "It's waiting for us to lead it home."

Sefa sat back down heavily and fed the fire another handful. "Then we'd best be worth following," she said. "Sleep in shifts. Tomorrow we walk faster."

Maren stayed on her boulder until her watch ended, watching the treeline, holding the image of those ice-dark eyes against everything she feared they might belong to.

Hope, she decided, was just fear wearing its good clothes. And either way, they were all walking north together — the hunted, the haunted, and the wolf that wasn't there.
