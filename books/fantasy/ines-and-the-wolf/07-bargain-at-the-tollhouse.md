## 7. Bargain at the Tollhouse

The road to the tollhouse ran along the spine of an old aqueduct, and by the time they reached it the morning fog had burned off into a haze that made everything look like it was remembering itself rather than simply being. Orsen walked at the front because he had appointed himself there three days ago without anyone objecting — someone had to be first when the crown's men appeared on the horizon, and he had decided that someone would be him. It was a small vanity. He was aware of that. But a man who had spent eleven years recording other people's debts had very few possessions left to him besides his vanities, and he guarded them jealously.

Behind him came Maren, walking with her clerk's straight back even in a stolen fishwife's shawl; Tobin, who kept drifting to the edges of the road as though listening for something under the stones; and Sefa, who moved like weather, slowly and with an air of having been here long before any of them arrived.

"There," Tobin said, and stopped.

Orsen heard it too, then: the creak of a winch, the clatter of a chain, the particular bored murmur of men paid to stand in one place all day. The tollhouse sat where the aqueduct crossed the river gorge — a squat stone block with a timber gate stretched across the narrow bridge, red-and-gold pennants snapping above it in the wind off the water. Crown colors. Two soldiers at the gate, a third on the roof with a crossbow held loosely, the way a man holds a thing he has never once needed to use.

"Papers," Maren said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"We don't have papers."

"No," she agreed. "We don't." She looked at Orsen with an expression he had learned to read over the past week: not blame, exactly, but the careful accounting of a woman deciding whose fault this was going to be if it went badly. "You're the one who's good with documents."

"I'm good with *true* documents," Orsen said. "Forging is different. Forging requires confidence I've never possessed."

"Then we'll have to find some," Sefa said, "before you reach that gate."

But there was no time to find anything, because the soldier on the roof had already seen them — four travelers alone on an empty road, which in the weeks since the Proclamation meant four travelers worth looking at twice — and raised two fingers, and the gate did not open so much as become *available*, swinging inward just enough to suggest passage while making clear that passage was a privilege rather than a right.

They walked through it the way sheep walk through a gate they can smell on the other side.

* * *

The tollhouse interior smelled of tallow, wet wool, and ink — the last of which struck Orsen like a physical blow, because it was *good* ink, oak gall cut with iron salts, the kind the Guild bought by the barrel, and for one disorienting moment he was back at his desk in Vellamarch with the ledgers stacked around him like city walls. He caught himself reaching for a pen that wasn't there and folded his hands instead.

A sergeant sat behind a scarred table, eating bread and cheese with the unhurried thoroughness of a man who knew the food was the best part of his day. He looked them over without urgency. That was worse than urgency. Urgency meant fear, and fear could be worked with.

"Names," the sergeant said.

Orsen had prepared names. They were good names, borrowed from gravestones in villages they'd passed through, common enough to be unremarkable, dead enough to be uncheckable. He opened his mouth to deliver them.

And the door at the back of the tollhouse opened, and a man came out whom Orsen recognized instantly, completely, and with a sensation like stepping off a stair that isn't where his foot expects it to be.

He was unremarkable in every particular except his eyes, which were the pale gray of paper left too long in sun. He wore a merchant's coat of no guild and no heraldry, and he carried a small lacquered case the way a priest carries a reliquary — close to the body, angled away from strangers' hands. Orsen had seen him exactly once before, across the trading floor of the Guildhall at Vellamarch, being escorted out by four guards after offering to buy something the guildmasters insisted was not for sale. Orsen had never learned what. He had only remembered the man's parting words, delivered cheerfully over his shoulder to no one in particular:

*"Everything is for sale. The only question is the currency."*

"Well," said the memory-broker, smiling at Orsen as though they were old friends meeting at a funeral. "Here's a face I priced once and never got to collect on."

The sergeant looked up, annoyed. "Master Quill, if you're going to conduct business, conduct it outside. This is a crown post."

"And I am conducting crown business, sergeant, or near enough." The broker — Quill, apparently, though Orsen doubted the name belonged to him any more than the coat — set his lacquered case on the corner of the sergeant's table and turned to face their little company fully. His gaze moved across Maren, Tobin, Sefa, cataloguing them with the speed of a man flipping through a ledger, and settled on Orsen with the satisfaction of a collector finding the exact coin he'd been missing. "Four travelers. No papers — I'd wager my case on it, and this case is worth more than this whole tollhouse. Heading south, which means heading toward the mountain roads, which means either fools or pilgrims. And gifted folk are always pilgrims, aren't they? Always walking toward something they believe will save them."

"We're wool merchants," Maren said flatly.

"You're a burned clerk, a boy who flinches at doors, a hedge-witch older than the king's grandfather, and—" Quill tilted his head at Orsen, and his smile widened by a single tooth, "—a scribe whose ink moves. Which makes all of you, by the terms of the Proclamation, crown property pending assessment. The sergeant here knows what happens to posts that let property wander through unassessed. Don't you, sergeant?"

The sergeant had stopped eating. He looked at the four of them now with new eyes, and his hand had drifted, casually, to the ring of keys at his belt — among which, Orsen noted, hung the iron cuff-keys of a slave-taker, not a toll-keeper.

"The Proclamation says the gifted are to be taken to assessment stations," the sergeant said. "Nearest station's at Greyford. Three days east."

"Back the way we came," Tobin said softly. Nobody told him to be quiet. There didn't seem to be any point.

"So," Quill said pleasantly, rubbing his hands together. "Here is the shape of your morning. Option one: the sergeant cuffs you, and you spend three days walking east to a station where clerks far less sentimental than our friend Orsen will catalogue whatever it is you do, and then the crown decides whether you're useful or fuel. Option two—"

"You sell us a way through," Sefa said. Her voice was dry as seed husks. "For a price."

"For a price," Quill agreed, delighted. "I do love a witch. You people understand commerce."

* * *

They negotiated in the tollhouse's side room, which contained a cot, a shrine to some river god gone green with damp, and a window overlooking the gorge. The sergeant stayed outside, ostensibly guarding them, actually pretending not to listen. Quill set his lacquered case on the cot and opened it, and Orsen made himself look.

Inside, nested in velvet, lay dozens of small glass vials, each stoppered with wax and labeled in a fine, spidery hand. Each vial held a curl of silver-gray vapor that moved when you weren't watching it directly — slow, deliberate, like smoke that had somewhere to be. Like ink.

"What are they?" Tobin asked, from the doorway, where he'd stationed himself with his back against the frame.

"Memories," Quill said. "Extracted, preserved, portable. A lady's wedding day. A sailor's last sight of his home port. A boy's entire childhood, sold by a father who needed the money more than the son needed the past." He lifted one vial to the light, admiring it. "The court beneath the mountain pays handsomely for certain kinds. First loves. Faces of the dead. Grief, especially. Grief keeps." He glanced up at Orsen. "You know how this works, don't you? You've seen the trade from the other end of the pen. The Guild records souls as collateral. I merely make the collateral liquid."

"The Guild doesn't deal in memories," Orsen said.

"The Guild deals in *everything*, Master Villalobos. It simply prefers not to write down the parts that would upset its clerks." Quill smiled again. "Yes, I know your name. I know all sorts of things. It's the foundation of my business model."

Maren stepped between them, arms crossed. "What's your price for papers that'll get us past every toll between here and the mountain passes?"

"That depends entirely on which of you pays." Quill's eyes went around the room. "The boy has almost nothing worth taking — children's memories are thin gruel, mostly hunger and fear. The witch has too much sense to carry anything valuable in her head; you can see she's already emptied herself of everything soft. The clerk—" he nodded at Maren, "—might fetch a decent price for her years at the Guildhall, but institutional memories are common as rain. No." His gaze returned to Orsen and stayed there, warm as a hand on the back of the neck. "It's the scribe. Scribes hoard. All those years reading other people's lives, and all the while keeping his own locked away tight. There's gold in that skull. I can *smell* it."

"I'm not selling you anything," Orsen said.

"Of course not. Selling implies you keep something in exchange. This is a trade. Papers for payment. Fair market rate." Quill began packing the vials back into their velvet slots with delicate fingers. "Think about it while I speak with the sergeant about his discretion. He'll want paying too, of course. Everyone wants paying. It's the one honest thing about people."

He swept out. The door closed. For a long moment none of them spoke, and the river muttered below the window like a debtor being asked to settle up.

"He'll take the papers and give them to the sergeant anyway," Maren said finally. "After he takes whatever he takes from you. We'll be cuffed by noon."

"No," Sefa said. She had drifted to the window and stood looking down at the gorge, where the fog was burning away and the water showed green and cold and very far down. "He wants us through the gate. Not cuffed in his presence — a broker hates witnesses to his own transactions. If he sells Orsen something, he needs us gone and quiet before anyone asks questions." She turned. "The question is what we're willing to let him take."

"Nothing," Tobin said. "We go east. We run."

"East is the assessment station, love. West is the mountain, and Ines is in the mountain, and this man—" Sefa's eyes found Orsen's, and there was no softness in them at all, only the terrible clarity of the old, "—has been carrying her name in his ledger like a coal in his pocket since the day he falsified that entry. I've watched him walk for six days. He isn't running *from* anything. He's running *to*. So the question isn't whether he pays. It's what he pays with. And I'd rather he chose than be chosen for."

Orsen looked at his hands. They were steady. Eleven years of balancing impossible accounts had taught them steadiness the way a rope teaches a hanged man stillness.

"My mother," he said.

Everyone turned.

"When I was nine, she took me to the lantern festival at Vellamarch harbor. She bought me honey-bread and told me about my father — the one thing she ever told me — standing under the great lantern wheel with the light turning her hair copper. It's the only memory I have of her face. Everything else is..." He searched for the word and found it with a small, cold surprise. "*Ledger entries.* Dates. Debts. She owed the Guild forty crowns when she died. I know that perfectly. I can recite it. But her face—" He shook his head. "That's the price. Take that. Take the festival, the lanterns, the honey-bread, the story of my father. Take all of it, and give me papers that hold."

Quill, re-entering without knocking, heard the last of it and beamed like a man hearing music.

"Oh, *beautiful*," he said. "Do you know how rare it is, in this trade, to meet a seller who understands the product? Most of them haggle over scraps. 'Take the bad year, take the shame.' Amateurs. But a mother at a lantern festival — first-rate stock. Grief keeps, Master Villalobos. I told you." He set the case on the cot and drew out a single vial, empty, its wax stopper waiting. "Papers for four, sealed with a customs stamp I happen to carry, good from here to the Greywater passes. In exchange: one memory, extracted clean, no residue, no fragments. You won't feel a thing during. Afterward, you'll feel a hole shaped like a festival. That part's free."

"Orsen." Maren's voice cracked on his name. "Don't. There's another way. There's always—"

"There isn't," Orsen said, and was surprised to find he meant it gently. "There's the cheap way and the expensive way, and both ways cost, and I have spent my whole life watching other people pay prices I only wrote down. Let me pay one myself. Let me at least be fluent in the currency."

Sefa said nothing. Tobin stared at the floor. Maren looked at him for a long moment, and then, in a voice smaller than he had ever heard from her: "Will you remember *us*?"

"Yes," Orsen said. "You I keep. You're mine. She was theirs." He didn't say whose *theirs*. He suspected the Guild's. He suspected the debt had eaten her long before the fever did, and that the festival memory was the last piece of her the ledger had never managed to price.

Until now.

* * *

The extraction took less time than confession. Quill had him sit on the cot, tilt his head forward, and breathe. The broker's thumbs came to rest on Orsen's temples — cool, dry, precise as a compass — and he murmured something that might have been a prayer or an invoice number, and the world went silver at the edges.

Orsen felt the memory leave the way you feel a word leave your tongue mid-sentence: a fullness, a shape, a meaning — and then the shape of the absence where it had been, which is the cruelest part, because you remember *that there was something* even after you've forgotten what.

The lanterns. The harbor. Copper hair. Honey-bread—

Gone.

He opened his eyes. Quill was stoppering the vial, which now glowed faintly, a curl of silver light coiling inside the glass like a sleeping eel. The broker held it up to the window, admiring his purchase, and for one unguarded instant his face held something that was almost tenderness — the look of a man holding the last egg of a bird he'd plucked bare.

"Genuine article," Quill murmured. "Unspoiled. She'll fetch a fortune from a certain client of mine who collects mothers." He tucked the vial away and produced, from his coat, a leather folio, which he dropped into Orsen's lap. Four sets of travel papers, stamped, signed, aged convincingly at the corners. Wool merchants, licensed out of a town Orsen had never heard of, bound for the Greywater passes. Perfect work. It made him sick to admire it.

"A pleasure doing business," Quill said, gathering his case. At the door he paused, and looked back, and his voice lost its salesman's warmth for just a moment — became, briefly, something almost like pity, which was somehow worse. "A word of professional courtesy, scribe. You're walking toward the mountain, and the mountain buys what I sell. When you get there — and you will — ask yourself what else you own that you haven't counted yet." He smiled. "Everything is for sale. The only question is the currency."

Then he was gone, and the sergeant waved them through the gate with the studied blindness of a well-paid man, and the four of them walked south across the bridge with legitimate papers in their pockets and the wind off the gorge tearing at their collars.

Orsen walked at the front. He tried, as they climbed the far road, to summon her face — just to check, just to confirm the borders of the wound — and found instead a smooth, bright blankness, like a page written on and then scraped clean for reuse. He knew her name. He knew the debt: forty crowns. He knew she had died in winter. He knew, with absolute certainty, that he had loved her.

He could not remember what loving her had looked like.

Tobin fell into step beside him, silent for a mile, and then said, not looking at him: "There's a door ahead. Two days south, at the foot of the mountain. It's old, and it's angry, and it doesn't want to be opened." The boy swallowed. "But underneath the anger, it's *afraid*. And doors that are afraid... doors that are afraid can be talked to."

Orsen looked south, where the haze had thinned, and saw for the first time the mountain itself — vast, blue-black, crowned with cloud, with a city glittering on its flank like frost on a blade. Somewhere inside it was a girl with winter in her teeth, and a contract with his handwriting on it, and a tithe-hall where souls were poured out like wine.

"Good," he said, and heard his own voice come out flat and cold as scraped vellum. "So am I."
