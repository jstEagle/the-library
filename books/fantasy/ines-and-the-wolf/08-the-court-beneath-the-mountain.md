## 8. The Court Beneath the Mountain

Tobin heard the mountain before he saw it.

That was the strange truth of his gift, the one that had made his father nail shut every door in their house and finally send him away with a copper bit and a warning never to come back: doors spoke to him. Not in words, exactly. In moods. A door had a temper like a person did — sullen, sleepy, eager, afraid — and if Tobin stood close enough and let himself listen past the ordinary noise of wood and iron, he could feel what waited on the other side. He'd known the miller's wife was dead three days before they found her, because her front door had gone quiet as a held breath. He'd known which tavern cellar hid a smuggler's cache, because its trapdoor practically giggled.

The mountain hummed.

They'd been climbing for two days through pine country where the cold came down off the peaks like something poured, and Sefa had stopped complaining about her knees only because she'd started complaining about everything else instead — the wet, the wind, Maren's cooking, the way Tobin chewed. It was a comfortable noise, that complaining. Tobin had learned on the road that families weren't made of blood; they were made of people who complained at you like you'd still be there tomorrow to hear it.

But the humming had started sometime before dawn, faint as a fly against a window, and by midmorning it wasn't faint anymore. By the time they crested the last ridge before the high valley, it filled his skull like a hive pressed to his ear.

"Stop," he said.

Maren stopped. She was good about that. She'd learned early that when Tobin said *stop*, you stopped, because half the time there was a tollman or a tracker around the next bend that no one else had heard yet. Orsen looked up from the map he was pretending to read — Tobin had noticed that Orsen pretended a lot, that his hands needed to be holding paper the way other men's hands needed a drink — and Sefa leaned on her stick and squinted at him with her one good eye.

"What is it, boy?"

"I don't know." He swallowed. His mouth had gone dry. "Something's... singing."

* * *

From the ridge, the valley opened below them like a bowl of grey milk, and in the middle of it stood the mountain.

Not a mountain with a city on it. That was what Tobin had expected, some citadel crag with walls and towers clinging to the stone like lichen. But this was worse, or better, depending on how you loved the world. The city had been carved *into* the mountain, or perhaps the mountain had been hollowed to make room for the city — terraces of pale stone descending in rings toward a central shaft that plunged down into the rock's black heart, and every terrace bristling with towers and spires and buttressed walkways, all of it the same bone-white color, all of it gleaming under the thin autumn sun like the inside of a shell.

"It's beautiful," Maren said, and then, quickly, as if she'd caught herself: "It's horrible."

"It's both," Sefa said. "Most terrible things are."

Tobin couldn't speak. The humming was resolving now, sorting itself into layers the way a chord sorts into notes if you listen long enough. There were thousands of doors down there. Thousands upon thousands — doors of stone and bronze and glass and things that weren't either, doors set into living rock, doors that had been sealed so long they'd grown into their frames like trees into fences. And beneath all of them, underneath the whole ringing architecture of the place, one vast pressure that was not a door at all but something *held shut* — something the size of the mountain's own heart, straining against a lid.

He put his hand flat against the frozen ground, the way he did when he needed to think, and let the feeling pour through his palm.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, no."

Orsen crouched beside him. "What do you hear?"

"You know how a house sounds when everyone's home? Footsteps and pots and voices through the floor?" Tobin kept his eyes closed. "It's like that. Except—" He struggled. How did you explain that the footsteps he heard beneath the mountain were not feet? "Except nobody down there is walking anywhere. They're all just... *there*. Pressed up against the insides of the doors. Like moths at a window."

Sefa went very still. "How many?"

Tobin opened his eyes. Below them the city glittered, serene and white, smoke rising from ten thousand vents in straight, orderly columns, and somewhere down in that beauty were people who had been sold the way horses were sold, whose souls were being drunk slowly the way a landlord drinks a tenant's rent.

"I can't count that high," he said. "More than the whole town I grew up in. More than all the towns."

Nobody said anything for a while. The wind moved through the pines behind them with a sound like the sea, and far below, tiny as beetles, figures moved along the terraced roads — carts, guards, processions in bright colors winding down toward the central shaft, where the light seemed to fall in and simply not come back.

"That's where they took her," Orsen said at last. His voice was flat in a way Tobin had learned to distrust; it was the voice he used when the thing underneath the words was too big to say out loud. "Ines. That's where they brought her."

"Yes," Maren said quietly.

"And we're going down after her." Orsen folded his map with great care, creasing each fold precisely, as though precision could hold him together. "All four of us. Into that."

"Well," said Sefa, "I've been meaning to die somewhere impressive."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the ridge, out of the wind, and Tobin lay awake listening to the mountain.

He couldn't help it. Sleeping near it was like sleeping next to a sleeping giant — you felt every breath through the ground. And the more he listened, the more the shape of the place assembled itself in his head, unasked, the way a tune gets stuck. Doors liked to be understood. That was the secret nobody knew about doors: they were lonely. Every door in the world spent its whole existence being opened without being listened to, slammed without thanks, locked and cursed and kicked. When Tobin paid attention to them — really paid attention, the way you'd pay attention to a shy child — they told him things.

The mountain's doors told him everything.

There was a servants' way in, he realized somewhere past midnight. Of course there was; there were always servants' ways; palaces were the same everywhere, however high they built them. A low entrance on the northern flank where the trade roads wormed into the rock, all service corridors and stair-shafts, guarded badly because nobody important ever used them. The doors along that way were old and tired and mostly bored — they hummed with kitchen-smells and lamplight and the shuffling of tired feet, nothing worse. If you were small and quiet and willing to smell like a scullery, you could walk most of the way into the mountain's body without anyone asking your name.

But deeper in, the doors changed.

Tobin sat up in the dark, hugging his knees, and listened harder, and wished he hadn't. Past the servant ways, past the ring-terraces with their markets and barracks, the doors began to feel wrong. Sealed doors usually felt one of two ways: stubborn, like a fist, or dead, like a brick in a wall. These were neither. These doors were *full*. They had weight behind them the way a dam has weight, a soft enormous pressing, and every one of them carried the same mood, and the mood was the worst thing Tobin had ever felt in his short unlucky life.

Hope.

The doors behind the sealed galleries were full of hope. Faded, thinned, worn down to threads after years and years — but hope, still, stubbornly, horribly, alive. Whatever was stored behind those doors was not asleep. It was waiting. It still believed someone was coming.

"Tobin."

He flinched. Maren was sitting up across the dead fire, watching him with those steady dark eyes of hers. She didn't sleep much either; none of them slept well anymore, not since the proclamations, not since the hunt. "You're shaking," she said.

"I'm fine."

"Liar. Come here."

He came. She was the closest thing he had to an older sister, which meant she bullied him gently and he pretended to mind. She wrapped the shared blanket around his shoulders and didn't ask questions right away, which was another thing sisters were for.

"They're awake in there," he whispered finally. "The souls. The ones they bought. Everyone says it's like — like harvesting, like grain, like the souls go to sleep in the vaults. They don't. They're standing at the doors. All of them. All this time." His voice cracked and he hated it. "They're *waiting to be let out*, Maren."

Maren was quiet a long moment. Down the slope, the mountain city glowed faintly against the stars, a constellation pinned to the earth, beautiful as a lie.

"Then somebody should let them out," she said.

* * *

At dawn they studied the approaches, and Tobin drew them a map no cartographer could have made.

He scratched it into the dirt with a stick while the others watched: the northern service road, marked with a line of small circles for the bored, half-asleep doors. The gallery levels above, where the doors began to press and strain. The central shaft, which he would not draw at all — he tried twice and his hand shook too hard. Around the shaft he drew a ring, and inside the ring he wrote nothing, because there was nothing to write. That part of the mountain had no doors. It had a *mouth*.

"The tithe hall's down there," he said. "I think. Wherever they do the — whatever they do. I can't hear inside it. There are no doors between me and it, and that means—"

"That means it isn't a room," Sefa said grimly. "Rooms have doors. That's a throat."

"Wonderful," Maren muttered.

Orsen stared at the dirt map for a long time. Tobin watched him — watched the way his eyes tracked over the little circles, the servant road, the ring of nothing — and saw the scribe doing what scribes did, turning horror into inventory. It was almost comforting, watching him work. Almost like having a plan.

"There," Orsen said finally, tapping the northern road. "We go in as laborers. Carts go down at first light; Sefa heard the drivers talking at the waystation. We ride the freight lift to the third gallery, find the record-vaults—" He stopped himself. Tobin noticed he did that a lot lately, stopping himself, as if the plan had a shape he didn't want to look at directly. "Find out where they keep the contracts. Everything starts with the contracts. If Ines is sold, there's a paper that says so, and papers can be stolen."

"And if the paper says her soul's already been drunk?" Maren asked, gentle and merciless, because someone had to ask it.

Orsen's jaw tightened. "Then we steal her back anyway. However much is left."

Nobody argued. That was the rule they'd fallen into on the road, the found-family rule nobody had written down: you didn't argue with the desperate parts of each other's plans. You saved your arguing for the practical parts, like how many knives to carry and whether Sefa was allowed to make her blister-poultice again after what happened to Tobin's eyebrows.

Tobin looked back down at the shining city, at the smoke rising in its tidy columns, at the tiny bright processions winding toward the shaft like offerings sliding down a gullet. Somewhere down there was a girl with winter in her teeth whom Orsen talked about the way starving men talk about bread — carefully, and too often, and with his whole face changing. Tobin had never met Ines. But he'd heard her at the edge of the mountain's song, the way you hear one particular voice in a crowd: a door deep in the sealed galleries that didn't feel like the others. The others hoped. That door *raged*. It rattled and snarled and threw itself against its frame, night after night, and Tobin had taken to listening to it before sleep the way you'd listen for a friend's footsteps.

She was still herself in there. Half-eaten and sold and chained behind a door in a mountain, and she was still fighting.

"She's loud," Tobin said suddenly.

The others looked at him.

"Ines. Her door. It's the loudest one in the whole place." He managed a crooked grin, and found that it was almost real. "You'll hear her before you see her. I'd bet my gift on it."

Sefa snorted. "Keep your gift, boy. We'll need it to get out again."

They broke camp as the sun cleared the eastern peaks and lit the white city gold, so that for one moment it looked less like a shell and more like a crown — and Tobin understood, with a lurch, that it *was* a crown, that the kingdom wore this mountain the way a king wears his power, and that everything lovely about it was paid for with the currency of people.

They walked down toward the northern road, four small figures on a vast grey slope, and behind them the mountain hummed its thousand-doored song.

And as they came down out of the wind, close enough now to smell the woodsmoke and the ore-dust of the outer terraces, Tobin heard something new threading through the chorus — a sound that stopped him dead on the road and turned his blood to ice water.

A horn. Long, low, and rising from the depths of the shaft itself, rolling up through the galleries door by door by door, and every door it passed woke screaming with joy and terror at once, because every soul behind every seal knew what the horn meant.

The tithe ceremony was beginning.
