## 9. Ines, Running

They took her name on the third day.

Not all of it — not yet. They took the *saying* of it first, which was its own kind of theft. In the mountain city there were no names spoken aloud in her presence, only designations delivered by servants who would not meet her eyes: *the asset*, *the tithe-pending*, *the winter one*. The last had been coined by a pale woman with silver rings on every finger who had leaned close and breathed in through her nose like Ines was bread at a market stall.

"Winter," the woman had murmured. "Cold keeps. Cold keeps beautifully."

Ines had bared her teeth at her — the long canines, the ones that came when she let the wolf rise just to her gums — and the woman had laughed with genuine delight and written something down.

So Ines learned the shape of the place quickly, because learning the shape of a cage was the first thing any trapped thing did. The court beneath the mountain was not beneath it in the way a cellar lies beneath a house. It was *inside* it, grown through it like roots through a skull. The upper city — Vellamarch's rival, all white spires and trade-glamour — was only the crown of the thing. Below, galleries opened into galleries, halls into halls, all of them lit by lamps that burned without oil and gave off light the color of a held breath. The stone was warm. That was the worst of it, at first. The stone under her bare feet was warm as skin, warm as something alive, and it took her four days to understand why.

The mountain was fed.

* * *

The rituals came at dusk, or what passed for dusk down here, where the hours were kept by bells that rang in no order she could learn. A bell would sound — low, sweet, wrong — and the attendants would come for whoever was called. There were eleven others in the tithe-wing when she arrived. She counted them the way she counted everything now, the way the wolf in her counted: bodies, breaths, exits, weaknesses.

There was the old man with river-gifted hands who could make water climb walls; they took him twice in her first week, and after the second time his hands hung at his sides like things he'd forgotten he owned. There was the twin girls who shared one gift between them, dream-walking, and who were always taken together, always returned holding each other and smiling. The smiling was the part Ines could not look at. They came back from the tithe hall *smiling*, serene as saints, and their eyes had gone soft and far away like the eyes of deer in the last moment before the arrow.

And there was the boy. Pell, though no one said his name anymore either. Ten years old, maybe. His gift was small — flowers bloomed where he slept, little white ones pushing up through stone seams — and Ines had thought, in the first blind days, *they won't want much from him, he's so small, his soul must be small too.*

She had been wrong about that. She understood now that the court did not measure souls by size. It measured them by flavor.

The bell rang on an evening when the lamps had gone amber, and the attendants came for Pell. Ines heard him before she saw him — not crying, he never cried anymore, that was another thing the rituals took — just the small scuffing drag of his feet on warm stone as they led him past her cell. She pressed her hand flat against the bars. Her nails had come out long and dark without her asking them to.

"Pell." She made her voice the voice she imagined a mother might have, if she'd ever known one. "Look at me."

He looked. And he smiled at her, that terrible pre-emptive smile, the smile they gave you before they went, as if the ritual had already begun working backward through time.

"It doesn't hurt," he whispered. "That's the trick of it. It feels like being *thanked*."

Then he was gone down the corridor, between the two attendants in their grey livery, and Ines stood at the bars with her hand still raised and felt the wolf rise in her throat and howl against her teeth, silent, silent, because screaming brought the wardens and the wardens brought the rings and the rings brought the pale woman with her nose for cold.

He came back two days later. He smiled at everyone. Three mornings after that, Ines found white flowers growing in a line along the base of his cell wall, neat as stitches, and Pell sitting beside them with his hands in his lap, watching them with an expression of mild, pleasant curiosity, as though he could not remember planting them but supposed someone must have.

*It feels like being thanked.*

Ines sat down across the corridor from him, as close as the bars allowed, and began, in a low steady voice, to tell him about a guildhall in Vellamarch.

* * *

Here is what she kept. Here is the hoard she guarded in the den of herself, tooth and claw over every scrap of it:

A room that smelled of ink and tallow and old paper gone soft at the edges. Rain on a high window. A man at a desk with a scribe's hunched shoulders and ink stains to the second knuckle, who looked up when they brought her in chained and muzzled — *muzzled*, like a beast, because the guildmaster had insisted, though she hadn't bitten anyone in months — and whose face did something no face in this mountain had ever done.

His face flinched. For *her*. Not in fear. In something that hurt him.

They had ordered him to catalogue her. That was the word: catalogue. As if she were a shipment of dye or a bolt of cloth. He had asked her questions in a voice kept carefully level, and she had answered none of them, and growled instead, and snapped at the air near his wrist once — deliberately missing — and he had not even blinked. He had written. And later, when the guildmaster read the entry aloud, Ines had watched the scribe's still face and understood, in the slow way wolves understand traps, that the words did not match what he had seen.

*Subject docile. Gift minimal. Value negligible.*

Value negligible. She had heard the guildmaster grunt — *then we'll be rid of it cheap* — and she had looked across the room at the scribe, at Orsen, because yes, she had gotten his name by then, pried it out of a chatty clerk like a splinter, and Orsen had been looking very hard at his ledger, at the entry drying under his hand, and she had seen it.

The ink moved.

No one else saw it. No one else ever seemed to see anything in that room except ledgers and profit. But Ines had a wolf's eyes, and a wolf's eyes catch motion, and the ink of the word *negligible* had shivered on the page like a living thing, like a small black animal trying to make itself smaller, trying to hide the lie inside itself. He had lied. This man who recorded everything, who was the machinery's own pen, had bent the truth by one word to buy her time — a day, maybe a week, maybe nothing at all — and had done it knowing the cost if anyone caught the ink confessing.

Nobody bought time for her. Nobody had since her grandmother died. And this stranger, this tired ink-stained stranger, had spent a piece of himself on her without her asking, without her even being able to say thank you, because they muzzled her, and then they sold her onward anyway, up the chain, to the crown's brokers, to the mountain, to here.

So she kept him. That was the truth of it, however strange it sounded even inside her own skull. In a place designed to empty you, she had chosen one full room and locked herself in it, and the room smelled of ink, and rain hit its high window, and a man with careful hands wrote a merciful lie in moving ink, and as long as she could walk back into that room whenever the attendants came, as long as she could stand behind that desk in her mind and watch him flinch for her — they could take whatever else they liked.

They would try, of course, to take the room itself. That was what the rituals were for. Not pain — pain was crude, and the court disdained crude tools. The rituals were *gentle*. That was their horror. Warm water, sweet smoke, voices like a mother's, asking you to set things down. *Set down your anger. Set down your fear. You carry so much, little one. Let us help you carry less.* And every time you set something down, you felt lighter, blessed, *thanked* — and you never noticed what was missing until you reached for it and found smooth nothing where it used to be.

Ines went to the tithe hall seven times in her first month. She learned to do what the wolf does when the hunters come with dogs: she ran. Not with her body — her body lay obedient on the warm stone slab while the pale woman sang to her and the sweet smoke curled — but with everything else. She ran into the guildhall. She ran down the corridors of her own keeping, past the ledger desk, out the rain-lashed window, into pine forest, into snow, into the night she'd first shifted whole at thirteen and lain under the moon astonished at her own four feet. She ran and ran and the woman's voice followed her saying *set it down, set it down,* and Ines bared her teeth at the voice from inside the memory and growled, *no. This is mine. I paid for this. I bled for every mile of it.*

The woman noticed. Of course she noticed. On the seventh visit she paused in her singing and looked down at Ines with those ringed fingers steepled, and her expression was not angry. It was hungry in a new way.

"Remarkable," she said softly. "You hold your borders. Do you know how rare that is? Most of them scatter like spilled water." She leaned closer, and her perfume was cold, like a cellar, like coins. "What are you holding onto, little winter? What is it worth more than rest?"

Ines looked at her and let the wolf come all the way up to her eyes, and said the first full sentence she'd spoken in the mountain:

"Everything you'll never get."

* * *

After that they changed her regimen. She gathered that from the way the attendants' routines shifted, from the new heaviness of the wardens outside her cell, from the pale woman's visits, which became frequent and conversational and worse than any ritual. She wanted to know about the borders. What anchored Ines. Where the walls of her were built and out of what stone. Because a soul that holds its shape cannot be skimmed cleanly — it must be broken open first, or starved, or *convinced*.

So they tried starving. Not food — the body was an investment, they fed the body well. They starved the memory-room. They took away anything that might feed it: no windows, no weather, no paper, no ink, no books, no names. The lamps never changed color. The bells rang in patterns engineered, she suspected, to dissolve time itself, so that a prisoner could not mark a week or a season and think *I have survived this long, therefore I am someone who survives.*

Ines countered with counting. Wolves count. Heartbeats, breaths, steps, the seconds between bells. She built a calendar of her pulse. She recited the guildhall to herself every waking hour — the exact number of floorboards, the smell of the tallow, the scratch-scratch-scratch of Orsen's pen, the way he'd pushed his sleeve up when it fell, the way he'd said, once, quietly, when the guards weren't listening: *"I'm sorry. I'm going to fix the entry."* Fix the entry. As if her freedom were an accounting error he intended to correct. She held that sentence like a coal in cupped hands.

But she was honest with herself, in the dark, in the way wolves are honest: the coal was cooling. The room was fading at its edges. She could no longer conjure the exact color of the rain on the high window. She could not remember whether Orsen's voice was deep or light — only that it had been *careful*, careful the way a man is careful when he has spent his life handling things that break. The details were going. One by one, like lamps failing down a long corridor. And she understood with cold clarity that this was the true design, that the starvation was working exactly as built, and that someday soon she would reach for the room and find smooth nothing, and then she would lie down on the warm slab and smile like Pell, and be thanked, and be emptied, and never know what she had lost.

Unless she got out first.

* * *

She started running in earnest after that. Not in the rituals — she submitted to them now, pliant, dull-eyed, giving them the performance of a soul already half-spilled, because a compliant tithe was watched loosely and she needed loose watching. She ran with her ears instead. The wolf's ears, the ones no muzzle had ever truly silenced.

She learned the sounds of the mountain the way Tobin — no, she didn't know a Tobin, that name meant nothing, why did her heart stutter on it like a missed stair — the way a hunted thing learns a forest. She learned that the third gallery's lamps hummed a half-tone lower than the others, which meant older wiring, which meant older stone, which meant cracks. She learned that the wardens' boots had a rhythm, four-and-rest, four-and-rest, and that the rest was a door opening somewhere below. She learned that water moved under the eastern cells — real water, cold water, mountain water — and that where water moves, stone eventually yields.

And on the night the story begins in earnest — the night Ines would later mark as the first night of the rest of her life — she heard something new.

It came up through the warm stone, faint as a heartbeat through a wall: iron, many sets of boots, and under them a sound she knew from Vellamarch, from the guildhall, from the world above that she had begun to fear she'd dreamed.

Paper. A great weight of paper, dragged and rustling, being carried down into the mountain's record-vaults. Ledgers. Someone was bringing ledgers into the heart of the court.

And threaded through the shuffle of all that paper, so faint that only a wolf's ear could have caught it, only a girl who had memorized a single man's pen-scratch among ten thousand sounds —

— a voice, low and careful, asking a guard a question about entries.

Ines stood at her bars in the lampless dark, and every hair on her arms rose, and the wolf lifted its head inside her and turned toward the sound the way it turns toward the moon.

*You're dreaming,* she told herself. *Starving girls dream of rescue.*

But her heart was slamming like a fist on a door, and somewhere far below, past the humming lamps and the moving water, a scribe with ink-stained knuckles was walking deeper into the mountain — walking toward her, or toward the vaults, or toward his own destruction, she couldn't know which — and Ines pressed her forehead to the warm stone and made the wolf a promise in the oldest language she had, the one without words:

*I am coming. Hold on to yourself. I am coming, and neither of us dies in here.*
