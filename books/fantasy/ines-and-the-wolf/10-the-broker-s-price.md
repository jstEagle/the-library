## 10. The Broker's Price

The broker came back on the third night of rain, when the road had turned to porridge and the four of them were sheltering in the ribs of a ruined waystation that smelled of wet ash and old horses.

Orsen saw her first. He had learned to know her shape in a crowd — the stillness of her, the way the weather seemed to negotiate around her rather than fall upon her. Rain struck the broken roof above them all night, drumming through gaps in the slates, pooling in the corners of the floor where Tobin slept curled like a comma. But around the woman who stepped out of the treeline at the edge of the firelight, the rain fell in a perfect column, unbroken, as though she walked inside a glass bell that no one could see.

She did not knock. She did not need to. The door of the waystation — half off its hinges, swollen with damp — swung inward for her with a groan that Tobin felt in his sleep and flinched away from, murmuring something about *a door that doesn't want us here*.

"Your boy has good ears," the broker said.

Orsen was on his feet before he knew he'd moved, one hand out behind him as if he could shield Maren and Sefa and Tobin with his body alone. "We have nothing you want."

The broker smiled. It was not an unkind smile. That was somehow worse. "That," she said, "is the only lie you've told me, scribe, and I've heard you tell very few."

* * *

Her name — or the name she gave — was Vess. She had appeared once before, at the tollhouse below Greywater Ford, where Orsen had traded his mother's face for papers that said they were wool-factors out of Lowmarsh, licensed, taxed, dull as dishwater. He remembered making the trade. He remembered the sensation of it: a coolness starting behind his eyes and spreading down through him like water finding channels in sand, taking first the smell of bread baking in their kitchen in Vellamarch, then the sound of her voice calling him in from the street, then — last, and gently, the way one closes a book — her face itself. He knew now that his mother had been tall. He knew she had laughed more than she spoke. He knew these things because he had written them down afterward, frantically, by firelight, while the memory was still warm enough to hold: *tall, laughs, hands always floury, sang off-key on purpose.* But the face was gone. When he tried to summon it, he found a smooth blankness, like a page scraped clean for reuse, and the words he'd written sat beside it like a description of a house burned to its foundations.

He had sworn, after Greywater Ford, never to trade with her again.

Now she sat down across the fire from him as though she owned the ruin, and the rain outside bent around the doorway, and no one else seemed able to look directly at her. Maren kept trying — Orsen could see her doing it, dragging her gaze up from the flames to the woman's face and having it slide away again, again, again, like a hand failing to grip wet stone. Sefa didn't try at all. The old hedge-witch simply watched the broker's shadow, which behaved oddly, leaning toward the fire when the broker leaned back, moving a half-second before she did.

"You're wondering how I found you," Vess said.

"I'm wondering why you bothered." Orsen kept his voice level. Beside him, Tobin had woken and gone very still, the way he did when a door was about to open somewhere far away. "You got what you came for at the tollhouse."

"I got a down payment." She held her hands toward the fire without seeming to feel its heat. "I told you then that memories are currency. Let me tell you now what they're currency *for*."

"I don't want to know."

"Everyone says that." She tilted her head. "And then they ask."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the rain went on falling on everything in the world except her.

"The court beneath the mountain is sealed," Vess said. "Not guarded — sealed. Its gates do not open for force or cunning or bribes, because it has no need of anything the surface can offer. It has everything. It has time. Do you understand what I mean when I say that? The court has bought so much time that time itself has become its only appetite. Centuries of it, drunk down soul by soul, tithe by tithe. The people under that mountain do not age because they cannot afford to. Aging would mean spending what they've hoarded."

"And you sell to them," Maren said. Her voice came out rough. She had stopped trying to look at Vess and fixed her eyes on Orsen instead, as though he were the rope she was holding onto over a drop. "You're their factor. Their merchant of—"

"Of everything the gifted carry that the ungifted cannot take by force." Vess shrugged, elegant, unashamed. "A soul-contract takes the whole of a person. Crude. Wasteful. What I deal in is finer. A single memory. A face. A first love. The sound of a dead man's voice. The court pays magnificently for such things — not because they're useful, but because they're *rare*. Every gifted soul that goes into the tithe halls goes in whole. Whole things are common there. Fragments are treasure."

"Why are you telling us this?" Orsen asked.

Vess looked at him then, and for the first time since the tollhouse, he felt truly seen by her — felt the weight of her attention settle on him like a hand on the back of his neck. "Because you're going to the mountain," she said. "All four of you. You've been walking toward it for nine days with your heads down, pretending you aren't. The girl with winter in her teeth is under it. Your wolf-girl. And you mean to get her out."

No one denied it. There was no point. Orsen realized, distantly, that they must have been as legible as a ledger entry — the direction of their travel, the pace of their desperation, the way Tobin mapped every sealed gate they passed with his ears and Sefa muttered ward-craft in her sleep. Anyone reading them correctly would know exactly where they were going and exactly why.

"So," Vess said, and folded her hands. "Here is my offer."

* * *

She laid it out the way a merchant lays out cloth: smoothly, turning each fold to catch the light.

"The mountain's gates admit three kinds of visitors. The dead, who come as dust on the wind and are swept out again. The contracted, whose souls are already signed over and who walk in through the tithe doors without being asked. And bearers of payment." She paused. "The court does not accept coin. Coin is a promise, and the court stopped believing in promises before your grandmother's grandmother was born. It accepts only value already held. For the ungifted, that means years of life freely surrendered — most surface folk who go under never come back out, and those who do come out old. For the gifted—" her eyes moved across the four of them, unhurried, "—it means memory. Raw, living, freshly given. The gatekeepers taste it. They weigh it. They let in whatever is heavy enough."

"You want me to pay my way in with—" Orsen's mouth had gone dry. "With them."

"With what you remember of them," Vess corrected softly. "They'll keep existing. They'll keep loving you. You simply won't be in it. Think of it as—" she searched for the phrase, and found it with a small, terrible smile, "—an amputation. Clean, above the joint. You'll walk. You'll even run. You just won't feel the foot."

Tobin made a small sound. Orsen didn't look at him. If he looked at Tobin — at the boy who heard doors, who had taught them all to sleep in shifts, who called Sefa *gran* when he thought no one noticed — if he looked, he would see exactly what he would be selling, itemized and priced, and he wasn't sure he could survive the catalogue.

"There are other ways in," Sefa said suddenly. Her voice was gravel and smoke. "There are always other ways in. Old ways. The mountain wasn't always a court; it was a mine, and mines have throats."

"The old ways are watched by things older than the court," Vess said pleasantly. "Ask me how I know. Go on. Ask." She let the silence sit. "Or take the front door, paid honestly, and walk in past every ward and warden in the place as a guest. Four days' travel from here. I'll write the letter of credit myself."

"And the price?" Orsen asked.

Vess spread her hands. "Enough to be heavy. The gatekeepers will judge. I'd estimate—" and here she did something Orsen had watched her do at the tollhouse, something he had hated her for then and hated her for more now: she reached into her coat and produced a slim black book, and opened it, and read aloud, in the tone of a woman reading a shopping list, "—the mother, already spent, so we begin fresh. The clerk Maren: her kindness to you in the archive tunnels, the burning of the inspection record, the flight together, the road, the nights by fires, the trust. The boy Tobin: the finding, the naming, the teaching of shifts and watches. The witch Sefa: the warding lessons, the soup she makes from nothing, the way she calls you *inkling*. And the girl Ines—"

"No." The word came out of Orsen like a blade leaving a body. "Not her. Not one memory of her."

Vess closed the book. "Scribe. Be reasonable. The gatekeepers don't weigh individual entries. They weigh the sum. Either you pay in the family entire, or you pay in something equivalent — and the only thing in you that heavy, besides them, is her."

The rain filled the pause. Orsen became aware, in a distant way, that Maren had taken his sleeve. That Tobin had come to stand at his shoulder. That Sefa had not moved at all, but was watching him with an expression he couldn't read — not pity, not warning. Something older than either. Recognition, maybe. As if she had stood where he stood, once, in some other life, in front of some other merchant of the intangible, and was remembering what she had chosen.

"You said at the tollhouse," Orsen said slowly, "that you don't choose what you take. That the trade decides."

"I said the trade decides the *shape* of what's taken. You decide whether to make it." Vess set the black book down on the stone between them, open to a blank page. From her coat she drew a pen — a plain thing, horn-handled, unremarkable — and laid it beside the book. "You're a writer. Write the bill of sale yourself. Name what you give. My kind find that self-authored prices bind cleaner. More honest. Less likely to fester."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in four days you'll be standing in front of a sealed gate with four days of walking in your legs and nothing in your pockets, and the girl you crossed a kingdom for will spend another decade in the dark, another tithe, another year of herself poured down the court's throat until there's nothing left but a smiling shell." Vess rose. The firelight did not bend her shadow; her shadow bent the firelight. "I'll wait at the crossroads below the mountain until the new moon. After that, the price changes. Prices always change. It's the one honest law of my trade."

At the doorway she paused, half-turned, rain sliding down the invisible wall of her going.

"For what it's worth, scribe — and it isn't worth much — the ones who pay don't regret it. They can't. That's the mercy of it. The ones who *refuse*—" she shook her head. "Refusers remember. Refusers live with it. Choose whichever burden you'd rather carry."

Then the rain took her, and the door swung shut on its own, and Tobin whispered, "It didn't want to close. Something made it."

* * *

They argued by the banked fire until the grey hour before dawn, voices low so the words wouldn't carry into the trees.

Maren argued against it first, furiously, in a whisper that shook. "You are not selling us. You are not — Orsen, look at me. Whatever's under that mountain, we find another way. We dig. We starve outside the walls until someone opens a gate for a corpse. Anything. You don't get to decide to forget us like we're — like we're *entries*—"

"That's exactly what we are," Orsen said, and hated how calm his voice sounded, how much it sounded like the guildhall, like balancing accounts. "To the court, to the brokers, to the whole rotten machine — we're entries. The only question is who holds the pen."

"Then don't let it be her."

"It was always going to be her or me." He heard the truth of it as he said it, cold and clean. "I'm gifted. My ink moves. If the crown doesn't hang me for it, the mountain will drink me eventually anyway. Everything I am is priced already. The only thing I own outright — the only asset in my whole ledger that isn't owed to somebody — is what I remember. And she wants to spend it on Ines." He looked at his hands. Ink-stained, always ink-stained, the right middle finger calloused in a ridge the guildmasters used to check for, proof of a lifetime at the desk. "Do you know what I wrote after the tollhouse? About my mother? Three lines. *Tall. Laughs. Flour on her hands.* That's what's left. Three lines where a woman used to be. And I did that for *papers*, Maren. For the privilege of walking down a road. Imagine what I'd do for her."

"Then I'll go in as payment," Tobin said.

Every head turned. The boy sat with his knees pulled up, chin on them, eyes red-rimmed and steady. "My hearing's worth something. Doors talk to me. I could buy all of us in."

"You could buy yourself in," Sefa said, "and be emptied before the first tithe bell, and we'd have lost our mapmaker for nothing." She said it without softness, because Sefa never used softness where honesty would serve better. Then she turned to Orsen. "Inkling. Come walk with me."

* * *

They walked out into the rain, because Sefa wanted to, and Sefa was old enough that wanting was reason enough. The ruined waystation shrank behind them. Under the dripping pines, the hedge-witch moved with surprising sureness, barefoot in the mud, until they reached a fallen tree crusted with moss and she sat on it like a throne.

"Do you know what a hedge-witch's gift costs?" she asked.

"You've never said."

"Every spell takes a day of my life. Not metaphorically. I cast the ward that saved us at the ford, I lost a day. I mended Tobin's ankle, two days. I've been spending myself for fifty years, and I've perhaps a winter left in the purse." She looked up at him through the rain, unblinking. "So believe me when I tell you I know the arithmetic of sacrifice better than any broker alive. And here is what the arithmetic says, inkling: a life spent entirely is worth exactly as much as a life spent wisely, and not a copper more. The spent part isn't the achievement. The *wisdom* is."

"You think I should refuse her."

"I think you should stop talking to her and start listening to yourself." Sefa caught his wrist — her grip was iron under the papery skin. "You've been keeping ledgers since you were a child. You think in debits. You think love is a balance that can be zeroed out and the world goes on. But hear me, boy: the court buys souls because souls can be *held*. Memory can't. You carve it out of yourself and it doesn't sit in their vault like a coin. It *leaks*. It gets into the walls. It haunts the corridors asking after people who never come. The mountain is full of ghosts that used to be payments." Her voice dropped. "You wouldn't be giving them your family. You'd be giving them a haunting. And keeping the hole."

Orsen stood in the rain a long moment. Water ran down his face and he let it, because if he wiped it away he might find it wasn't only rain.

"If I don't," he said finally, "Ines dies by inches in the dark, and I live the rest of my life knowing I stood at a crossroads with the price in my pocket and haggled."

"And if you do?"

"Then I save her and lose everyone, including the version of me that cared enough to save her." He laughed — a short, awful sound. "There's no line in that ledger that balances, gran. That's the whole horror of it. Every column bleeds."

Sefa studied him for a long time. Rain dripped from the pine boughs between them, counting seconds.

"Then here's an old woman's counsel, worth what you pay for it," she said at last. "Whatever you give them — give it *last*. Walk in with your family as long as the road allows. Eat with us, argue with us, learn us while you still can. Because the memories they take are copies, Orsen. Did the broker tell you that? No? Of course she didn't. But I've seen her kind work, long ago, in another country. What the gatekeepers drink is the *living* of it. What remains in you is the husk — the facts, the dates, the names written down. Hollow knowledge. Enough to recognize us. Not enough to love us." Her grip tightened. "So fill yourself to the brim first. Make every memory heavy. Give them a feast so rich it chokes them. And write everything down — names, faces, stories — and give the pages to Maren. Because paper remembers what flesh is made to forget, and you of all people should know that a ledger can outlive its keeper."

Orsen stared at her. "How do you know all this?"

For the first time since he'd known her, Sefa looked away first.

"Because someone paid for me once," she said. "And I was the price. Now come back to the fire, inkling. We haven't got many nights left, and I intend to make you remember every single one."

* * *

They traveled the next four days as though the days were loaves to be broken and shared. Maren talked — endlessly, deliberately, telling him stories of the guildhall archives, of her childhood in the flooded quarter, of the first time she saw him falsify an entry and decided to say nothing. Tobin taught him door-speech, pressing his palm to Orsen's ear against inn doors and bridge-house doors so he could feel the faint vibrations of hinges and locks, the grammar of wood. Sefa cooked and cursed and taught him ward-marks in the dirt, laughing her cracked laugh when he got them wrong, and once, only once, when he brought her water without being asked, she looked at him with such naked tenderness that he had to turn away.

And Ines — Ines he carried the only way he could, in the fragments he had: the girl with winter in her teeth, catalogued in a guildhall ledger; the falsified entry, written in ink that moved because it was true; the chains torn like paper in the pine forest; the wolf-shape glimpsed between trees, watching, deciding. He turned these over nightly like a man counting coins he was about to spend, and found, to his astonishment, that the counting made them heavier, not lighter. That was the trick of it, he began to understand. Love compounded. Every retelling added interest.

On the fourth evening, the mountain filled half the sky ahead of them, black against a bruised sunset, its crown lost in cloud. At the crossroads below, where the pilgrim road met the tithe road, a figure waited beside an invisible lantern, and the rain bent around her, and the black book was already open.

Orsen handed Maren the packet of pages he had written — everything, all of it, in his careful guildhand, dated and signed — and kissed her forehead, and shook Tobin's shoulder hard enough to hurt, and let Sefa press her thumb, briefly, to the center of his chest, over the heart, like a seal on wax.

Then he walked down to the crossroads alone, and picked up the horn-handled pen, and Vess turned the black book toward him.

"Name your price, scribe," she said. "Make it heavy."

Orsen looked at the blank page. Behind him, three strangers who loved him stood in the last light. Ahead, beneath the mountain, a girl he could no longer afford to remember was waiting in the dark.

He set the nib to paper, and his hand — his true hand, the one that had lied in ledgers to save her — did not shake at all.

*What I surrender,* he wrote, and the ink gleamed wet and alive in the lamplight, *is every memory I hold of Maren, Tobin, and Sefa — given whole, given gladly, to open the sealed ways of the court.*

The ink drank into the page like blood into snow. Far overhead, faint as a heartbeat, something in the mountain began to open.
