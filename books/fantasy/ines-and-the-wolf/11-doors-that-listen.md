## 11. Doors That Listen

Tobin had been listening to doors since before he could name what he was hearing.

It wasn't a sound, exactly. It was more like the way you could feel weather coming in your bad knee, or know someone stood behind you by the prickle at your neck — except Tobin felt it through his palms and the soles of his feet and somewhere behind his eyes, a low hum of attention that every door gave off whether anyone was there to hear it or not. Doors were patient things. Doors remembered. A door that had been slammed in anger for twenty years felt different from one that had only ever been opened gently, and a door that was afraid — truly afraid, the way doors got when something on the other side of them wanted through — felt like a held breath against his skin.

The mountain's doors were all holding their breath.

He crouched at the mouth of the servant way with his palm flat against cold stone, feeling for the seam of the first hidden door, and behind him he heard the others breathing: Maren's quick shallow breaths, Sefa's slow deliberate ones, Orsen silent as paper. Above them, past a thousand feet of living rock, the court of the mountain city glittered and feasted and did whatever it was courts did with the souls they bought, and down here in the dark the mountain's bones were threaded with passages no noble had walked in three hundred years.

"Which way?" Maren whispered.

Tobin didn't answer yet. He moved his hand along the wall, an inch at a time, reading. There. A faint warmth under the stone, like a sleeping animal. Not a door — a vein of the mountain's heat, nothing more. He kept going. His fingers found a crack, traced it down, and there it was: the dull, drowsy pulse of old wood behind old plaster, a door that hadn't been asked to open in generations.

*Sleepy,* he thought. *Bored. A little lonely.*

"It's here," he said. "But it hasn't moved in a long time. It might stick."

"Doors stick," Sefa said behind him. "That's what crowbars are for."

Tobin almost smiled. The old hedge-witch had a voice like gravel steeped in tea, and she'd spent four days on the road telling them that everything frightening could be solved with either a crowbar, a lie, or a good stew, preferably all three. He pressed his palm flat over the seam and gave the door what he always gave them: a little of his attention, offered politely, the way you'd knock instead of battering.

*I'm not here to hurt you,* he thought at it. *I just need to come through.*

The door's mood shifted — surprise, then something like the pleasure of an old dog hearing its name after years. The mechanism inside the wall groaned, rust fighting rust, and a panel swung inward on a breath of air that smelled of dust and tallow and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a tooth.

"You talked it open," Maren said. She still sounded half-suspicious whenever he did that, as if politeness to doors were some new species of witchcraft she hadn't catalogued yet.

"They like being asked," Tobin said. "Nobody's asked this one since before my grandmother's grandmother." He squeezed through into darkness. "Come on. And step where I step. Some of these floors have opinions too."

* * *

The servant ways were a city beneath the city.

They walked single file through corridors cut rough and narrow, ceilings low enough that Orsen — the tallest of them — had to bow his head. Niches opened off the passages every hundred paces, some holding the black stubs of ancient candles, one holding a complete set of dishes stacked as though the washing-up had simply been abandoned mid-chore two centuries ago. Tobin read the doors as they passed: service doors, dumbwaiter shafts, the sealed mouths of stairways that once carried food and linens and, he suspected, other things up to the bright levels where the court lived.

Most of the doors were asleep. A few were dead — hollow silences where a door should have been, wood rotted to nothing. And twice, they passed doors that made his skin crawl: doors that were *awake*, and watching, and afraid of something deeper in.

"What's wrong with that one?" Maren whispered the second time, because Tobin had stopped walking without meaning to, his whole body gone stiff.

"It's scared," he said. "Not of us. Of what's behind it." He swallowed. "There are things down here the doors don't want to look at either."

"Charming," said Sefa. "Keep moving, boy. Fearful doors and fearful witches both do better in motion."

They made camp in a widening of the passage where an old guard-niche held a hearth long cold. Sefa let Tobin build the fire small, shielded, while she unpacked her bundle of charms and dried herbs and laid out the night's work: the warding. It had become ritual over the days of travel, and like all rituals it had grown more elaborate as the stakes grew higher. She chalked sigils at the corridor's mouth and the passage's end, hung her charm-braids from iron hooks she'd brought for the purpose, and finally sat cross-legged before the campfire with a bowl of salt water in her lap, muttering into it like a woman scolding a slow kettle.

Tobin watched her from across the fire and thought, not for the first time, that she looked smaller than she claimed to be powerful. Her hands shook now when she worked. She pretended not to notice. They all pretended not to notice.

"How far tomorrow?" Orsen asked quietly. He was sharpening a letter-knife against a whetstone, the small sound rhythmic and calming, a scribe's fidget turned weapon.

"The maps say the vaults sit below the tithe hall," Maren said, unrolling their stolen plans again even though she'd memorized them. "Two levels down from where we are. But the servant ways don't go straight there. They spiral. Everything in this mountain spirals."

"Like a snail shell," Tobin said. "Or an ear."

Maren glanced at him. "What?"

"Nothing." He pulled his blanket around his shoulders. "My mother used to say mountains were ears. That they heard everything the king's people said and kept it." He hesitated. "I think she was right. This one's been listening to us since we came in. The doors talk to each other, you know. Word travels."

Sefa snorted from her salt water. "Then let's hope the doors gossip kindly."

* * *

It went wrong on the second day, at a junction where five passages met like the fingers of a buried hand.

Tobin felt it before any of them saw anything: a pressure change, a wrongness in the air, the way every door within earshot — his earshot — flinched at once. He threw out an arm to stop the line and the others bumped into him in the dark.

"What?" Maren breathed.

"Something's coming. Something big. Through the main ways, not these." He pressed both palms to the wall, listening with his whole body. Far off, muffled by stone, came a rhythm he knew from stories the roads had told them: the tread of the Soulwarden's sentries, the ones in grey lacquer who didn't breathe right. "A patrol. Six of them. Maybe eight. They're sweeping the lower galleries."

"Can we hide?" Orsen asked.

"Not in a corridor. We need a door between us and them, and it needs to be a *good* door." Tobin's heart was hammering. He cast along the walls, reading, desperate. Most of the nearby doors were weak things, rotten or rusted, doors that would fold like wet paper. Then — there. Off the eastern passage, behind a fall of rubble: a heavy pulse, dense and stubborn, a door with iron in its bones and three hundred years of standing firm in its grain. "*That* one. Help me move the rock."

They dug like animals, no light, no voices, hands bleeding on sharp stone, while the sentry-tread grew louder in Tobin's skull. When the last boulder rolled aside, a slab of black iron-banded oak stood revealed, its surface carved with spirals worn nearly smooth.

"It'll hold," Tobin gasped. "But it's angry. It's been sealed angry a long time — see the marks? Someone chained it shut from the outside and left it. It hates being closed but it hates being *broken* worse. I can't promise which it'll choose."

"Then we give it a reason," Sefa said.

She was already kneeling, pulling the stopper from a clay flask at her belt, and Tobin saw with a lurch that it was the flask. The big one. The one she'd told them on the road she'd only uncork twice in forty years, and both times had cost her something she didn't name.

"Sefa—" Maren started.

"Hush, girl. Wards are promises, and promises cost." The old woman poured a thread of silver liquid into her salt bowl, and the firelight bent toward it like iron to a lodestone. "This will seal the door fast to its frame — stone to stone, grief to grief, nothing passing either way till dawn. But a ward is only as strong as the life poured into the words, and mine is running thin." She looked up at them, and her eyes in the dark were steady and dry and terribly calm. "So I'll spend it anyway. Get behind the door. All of you. Now."

"No," Tobin said.

"Tobin—"

"*No.*" His voice cracked. "You always say everything's got a price. Well, I'm not paying this one. Find another way."

"There isn't another way, boy, and the sentries are ninety seconds out." She smiled at him, and it was the kindest and worst thing he'd ever seen. "You listen to doors. So hear me: some doors are meant to be paid for. Now *go*."

Orsen took Tobin's arm — gently, firmly, the grip of a man who had spent his life being moved by other people's decisions and had finally decided to make one of his own — and drew him toward the doorway. Maren slipped through. Tobin fought, and then, hating himself, stopped fighting, because the sentry-tread was sixty seconds out and Sefa was already chanting in a language that made the salt water boil.

He got one arm through the closing gap. "You come through after. You *promise*. Doors keep promises, and so do you."

"Witches keep promises," Sefa said, not looking up, her voice fraying as the words of the ward wound tighter and tighter, "or they die trying. In you go, dear heart."

The door began to swing. And that was when Tobin understood, with the terrible clarity of the gifted, that the ward wouldn't leave a gap. Stone to stone, grief to grief, *nothing passing either way*. She had known. She had known when she knelt down.

He wedged his arm in the gap and the door bit down — not cruelly, the old oak was gentle as it could be, but iron bands are iron bands, and the pressure built and built until he screamed. Beyond the narrowing gap he saw Sefa rise to her feet, arms spread, silver light crawling up her body like frost climbing a window, her mouth still moving around words that were costing her everything—

—and then Orsen was there, shoulder to shoulder with him, forcing his own arm into the gap, and Maren behind him, and the three of them straining together against a door that weighed as much as grief.

"We're not leaving her!" Maren shouted.

"I know!" Orsen's face was white, the veins standing in his neck. "Tobin — ask it! Ask the door!"

And Tobin, sobbing, blood running from his crushed forearm, did the only thing he knew how to do. He stopped pushing and he *listened*, and then he spoke — not words, but the deep wordless language he'd used all his life, the one doors answered to.

*Please. She's ours. She's family. Family doesn't get sealed on the wrong side.*

The door went very still.

Then, slowly — grudgingly, the way an old dog moves when it has decided something against its better judgment — the pressure eased. A finger's width. A hand's width. Enough.

Through the gap they dragged her, Sefa collapsing forward into their arms mid-word, the unfinished ward snapping back along the corridor like a whip. The great door boomed shut of its own accord, and the ward she had already spoken sealed it — stone to stone, grief to grief — with all four of them breathless on the safe side, and the sentry-tread passing on the far side a moment later, close enough that Tobin could feel the doors beyond flinch, one after another, like birds going quiet.

* * *

She nearly died anyway.

The broken ward had rebounded into her, all that spent power with nowhere to go, and it settled into her chest like a stone dropped in a well. For six hours she lay by the small fire gray-lipped and shivering, and Maren did everything Maren knew — broths, poultices, the bitter root that forced breath into a failing body — while Tobin sat holding one of Sefa's hands and refusing, absolutely refusing, to cry.

"You listen to doors," Sefa rasped at one point, her eyes finding him through the gloom. "Did you hear it? What the door said?"

Tobin blinked. "Doors don't talk. Not in words."

"That one did." A ghost of her old grin. "It said: *about time somebody asked.*" Her hand tightened on his, feeble but insistent. "Three hundred years chained shut, boy, and we were the first souls in all that time to treat it like a neighbor instead of an obstacle. Remember that. Half of magic is just... manners. Being willing to ask."

She slept after that, a real sleep, and woke at dawn ravenous, demanding stew, and complained that Maren's broth tasted like dishwater, and called Tobin "dear heart" in front of everyone, which mortified him completely and pleased him more than he would ever admit.

But something had changed among the four of them, and Tobin felt it the way he felt everything: through surfaces, through contact, through the quiet hum of things that cared whether they were opened or closed. Before the junction, they had been four strangers bound by circumstance and fear. After it, they were something else. When Orsen handed him bread, their fingers lingered a moment. When Maren checked his bandaged arm, she scolded him like a sister. When Sefa walked, Tobin walked on her weak side without either of them discussing it.

Family, he thought, wasn't something you were born into. It was a door you agreed to hold open for each other.

* * *

On the third day, the servant ways began to change.

The passages widened. The stonework improved — still old, still rough, but deliberately shaped now, and the niches held not candle-stubs but iron brackets, and the brackets held not dust but fresh oil. Someone maintained these ways. Someone still used them.

Tobin heard the doors before he saw them: a cluster of them ahead, awake and alert and *nervous*, humming like a hive disturbed. He raised a fist and the line stopped.

"There's a door ahead that gets used," he whispered. "Recently. And it's frightened of whoever uses it."

"Frightened?" Maren frowned. "Of the servants?"

"Of what comes through it." He crept forward, palm to the wall, and the picture assembled itself in his head piece by piece, the way it always did: a wide landing, a stair descending, and set into the stairwall a double door of pale wood, banded in silver, humming with a mood he had never felt from a door before. Not fear exactly. Not sleep. Something worse.

*Reverence,* he realized, with a chill that started in his palms and climbed into his teeth. *The door is reverent. It's been taught to worship whatever walks through it.*

Beyond it, faint as a heartbeat through stone, he could feel the vast slow pulse of the tithe hall — hundreds of doors' worth of presence gathered into one place, dim and flickering, like candles seen through fog.

And threaded through all of it, one thread burning brighter than the rest. Close. Close and getting closer, the way a wolf runs when it has finally caught the scent of home.

He turned back to the others, and his voice came out steadier than he felt.

"She's down there," Tobin said. "Ines. I can feel her. And there's a ceremony starting — soon, maybe tonight. If we're going to reach her..." He swallowed. "We have to go through the door that worships them."

Behind him, in the dark of the mountain's ear, Sefa began, grimly, to unpack her charms.
