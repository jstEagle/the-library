## 12. The Tithe Hall

They came for her on the ninth day beneath the mountain, when the lamps in the corridor outside her cell burned a color Ines had learned to dread — not gold, not flame-orange, but that pale honeyed white the servants called *tithe-light*, as if even the fire here had been refined past its own nature into something sweeter and more obedient.

She had counted days by the changing of the water in her basin. Nine changes. Nine times she had watched a gray-faced woman with kind hands and dead eyes carry the pitcher in, set it down, and leave without once meeting Ines's gaze. The woman's kindness was real. Ines could smell it under the fear, a warm animal smell like bread and milk. But the woman would not look at her, and Ines understood why. You did not look at what was going to be eaten. It made the eating harder.

*Not yet,* she told herself, pressing her palms flat against the cold stone of the wall. *They haven't taken it yet.*

The wolf in her stirred at the thought, a low rolling heat behind her ribs, and she gentled it down the way you gentled a dog near strangers — not because it was wrong to growl, but because growling now would get them both muzzled. She had learned that much in nine days. The mountain did not fear the gifted. The mountain *farmed* them.

* * *

They dressed her for the ceremony. That was the part that frightened her most, in the end — not the chains, which were silver-woven and heavy enough to ache in her teeth, but the dressing. Two attendants with soft voices bathed her hair with something that smelled of juniper and snowmelt. They put her in a gown of white wool so fine it felt like wearing a cloud's underside. They braided flowers into her hair — little white stars of them, mountain blossoms she had seen growing wild in the cracks of the pass on the road up, blooming stubbornly out of nothing.

"You look lovely," one attendant murmured, and meant it, and Ines wanted to bite her.

"Why the flowers?" she asked instead. Her voice came out low and rough. She had been talking less each day, letting the wolf-words pool in her throat.

The attendant's hands stilled in her hair. "It's an honor," she said, in the tone of someone reciting. "To be chosen for the tithe is an honor. Your gift will live forever in the court. You will be remembered."

"Remembered how?"

The hands resumed their braiding. The question went unanswered, the way all her questions went unanswered, and Ines stared at her own reflection in the polished bronze mirror and thought: *they dress us for our own funerals and call it a wedding.*

But there was something else in the reflection, if you knew how to look. She had spent her whole life learning how to look — the feral girl from the border villages, the one who'd been sold twice before she was twelve, the one whose eyes caught light wrong when she was angry. And in the bronze, behind her own face, she saw the attendant's face soften for just a heartbeat into something raw and grieving, before the mask of serenity slid back over it.

*You've done this before,* Ines thought at her. *Many times. And every time, some small part of you dies doing it.*

She almost pitied her. Almost. Then the silver chain clicked shut at her throat, and pity went back in its cage.

* * *

The Tithe Hall had no doors so much as mouths — great archways carved to look like lips parted around darkness, and Ines felt Tobin's absence sharply as they led her through. The boy who heard doors would have known what these mouths were saying. She could only feel it: a pressure, a listening, the sense of walking down a throat.

Then the hall opened, and she forgot everything else.

It was vast beyond sense. The ceiling was lost in a glow like the inside of a pearl, and the walls — the walls were *full*. Niches upon niches rose in tiers toward that pearly dark, thousands of them, tens of thousands, and in each niche sat a shape of light. Not fire. Not crystal. Something softer and more terrible: a glow with texture, with movement, like breath seen through skin. Some glowed steady and warm. Some flickered, guttering, thin as candle-ends. A few — very few, high up near the ceiling where the oldest ones must be kept — burned with a radiance so dense it hurt to look at, light that seemed to have weight, to have *history*.

Souls. She knew it the way animals know weather, instantly and completely. Every niche held a soul, and the hall held ten thousand niches, and the court beneath the mountain had been filling them for longer than any kingdom above had existed.

Ines stopped walking. The chains sang taut. The attendants exchanged a glance but did not drag her; there was nowhere to drag her that mattered anymore.

"They're still *alive*," she said. Her voice echoed strangely, swallowed by the pearl-glow. "Some of them. The dim ones — they're still in there."

"The faded are at rest," said a new voice, smooth as poured cream. "There is no suffering in the tithe. Only continuation."

She turned. He stood at the edge of the dais — the Soulwarden, though she didn't know his title yet, only that everyone bent around him the way water bends around a stone. He was beautiful the way the hall was beautiful: too symmetrical, too luminous, ageless in a way that was not youth but *stasis*, a man preserved rather than living. His eyes were the color of the tithe-light.

"You are Ines," he said, tasting the name. "Wolf-gifted. Rare. The guild's ledger undervalued you badly." A smile, gentle as a hand on a child's head. "We will do better by you."

"What happens tonight?" she asked.

"Tonight you witness," he said. "Tomorrow, you are honored." He gestured toward the front rows, where benches of pale wood faced a raised platform draped in white. "Every gifted soul attends one tithe before their own. It is our custom. Understanding sweetens the gift."

*I'll bet it does,* Ines thought, and let herself be led to the bench, and sat among perhaps forty other chained figures in white wool and flower-braids, and watched the platform with the fixed stillness of a creature deciding whether to fight or flee.

The wolf in her had already decided. The wolf was patient. The wolf knew that patience was just hunger wearing a calm face.

* * *

They brought the child out at moonrise — or what passed for moonrise down here, a slow brightening of the pearl-ceiling that the court pretended was sky.

A boy. Eight years old, maybe nine. Small for his age, with a cowlick that refused to lie flat no matter how the attendants had tried, sticking up at the crown of his head like a tuft of feathers. He wore white wool like hers. He had flowers braided into his hair. And he was *smiling*.

That was the thing Ines would carry out of this hall or die carrying it: the boy was smiling. He waved at the assembled gifted as they seated him on the platform, a little wave, shy and proud, and several of the older captives waved back with expressions of such tender anguish that Ines's chest cracked open along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"He thinks it's a festival," whispered the woman chained beside her — old, brown-skinned, with storm-gray eyes and the smell of dried herbs clinging under the juniper soap. A hedge-witch's smell. Ines filed it away without knowing why. "They tell them it's a festival. They tell them they're being blessed."

"What are they actually doing?"

The old woman closed her eyes. "Watch," she said. "I'm sorry, child. Watch."

On the platform, the Soulwarden knelt to the boy's height and spoke to him — Ines couldn't hear the words, but she saw the boy nod solemnly, eagerly, the way children nod when adults promise them wonderful things. Then the Soulwarden produced a vessel. It looked like nothing: a plain clay cup, unglazed, humble as a beggar's bowl. He set it on a stand before the boy and stepped back.

And the boy began to give.

There was no cutting, no blood, no visible mechanism at all. The boy simply closed his eyes and smiled wider, and *light* began to move through him — rising through his chest, his throat, gathering at his lips like breath on a winter morning. It left him in a slow golden stream and poured itself, willingly, gracefully, into the clay cup. And the boy laughed. Ines heard it across the whole vast hall, a child's laugh, bright and delighted, the laugh of someone blowing bubbles or catching fireflies.

"That's the cruelty of it," the old woman whispered, and her voice shook. "The tithe feels good. That's how they built it, long ago — they made the giving sweet. The child empties and never knows. Feels full right up until there's nothing left to feel with."

The stream of light thinned. The boy's laugh softened. His shoulders, which had been squared with excitement, began to relax downward, and his smile changed — not fading, but *simplifying*, the way a complicated face simplifies in sleep. The light in the cup rose higher, denser, taking on that weighted quality Ines had seen in the highest niches.

"No," Ines breathed. She wasn't aware she'd said it aloud. Her hands had become fists against her thighs, nails biting through fine wool. "No, no —"

The boy opened his eyes.

They were still open afterward — that was the horror, that his eyes stayed open, clear and mild and looking at the crowd, at the flowers, at the pretty ceiling, seeing everything and *no one home behind the seeing*. His smile remained. It was a perfect smile. It was the smile of a painted saint, of a doll, of a door left open onto an empty room. The cowlick still stuck up at the crown of his head, absurd and heartbreaking, the last untamed thing about him.

He swayed gently on the bench, smiling at nothing, while attendants lifted the cup — heavier now, glowing like a captured sunrise — and carried it with reverence to the wall of niches, sliding it into an empty space where it settled and pulsed and began, slowly, to beat.

Like a heart. Like ten thousand hearts, beating in the dark, keeping the court alive.

Ines did not remember standing. She was upright, chains rattling, a sound coming out of her that was half growl and half sob, and half the hall turned to look at her, and the Soulwarden turned too, and his beautiful empty eyes found hers with an expression of mild, patient interest — the interest of a farmer noting which animal would need a stronger fence.

"Ah," he said pleasantly. "The wolf objects."

Something in Ines tore loose then, some final pretense of the girl who had learned to keep her head down, to survive, to wait. She let the wolf rise all the way to her eyes and she looked at him — at the thing that farmed children and called it honor — and she said, clearly, in a voice that carried to the highest niches:

"I will die before I smile like that boy. Do you hear me? Whatever you take from me, I will be *there* when you take it. I will make you look at me. I will make it cost."

A murmur ran through the chained rows. Somewhere behind her, the old hedge-witch whispered, fierce and low: "*Yes.*"

The Soulwarden studied her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the boy's smile, perfect and vacant, and somehow that was worse than any threat.

"Wolves say such things," he said. "The mountain has heard them all. Tomorrow, little wolf, you may show me your teeth. We do so enjoy the spirited ones." He turned back to the platform, to the hollow-eyed child being led away by gentle hands. "Take her back to her cell. Let her rest. The gift comes easier to the rested."

* * *

They chained her in the dark, and the dark was total, and Ines lay on the stone shelf and did not sleep.

Instead she did what she had done every night since the guildhall, since the scribe with ink-stained fingers who had looked at her across a ledger and lied — she had seen the lie happen, had smelled the shift in his sweat, had watched him write something false about her soul to buy her time. Orsen. That was his name. She held it like a coal in both hands.

*Orsen lied for me. A stranger, a debt-bound clerk, risked the guild's wrath for a girl he'd met once.* If a stranger could do that much, then she owed it to that much and more. She owed it to the boy with the cowlick. She owed it to every guttering light in those ten thousand niches, every one of them stolen while smiling.

She pressed her palm to the cold floor and felt, faintly, far below, the mountain's pulse — the tithe-engine turning, souls burning slow to feed the deathless. And she let the wolf come up beside her in the dark, not to rage, but to plan. Wolves did not weep in cages. Wolves measured the cage. Wolves remembered where the keepers' feet dragged with tiredness, which lock rattled loose, which hour the corridor emptied.

Escape or die whole. Those were the only roads out of this mountain, and she would take either one with her teeth bared.

Somewhere above her cell, boots paused outside her door. A key turned — not her keeper's tread, lighter, quicker, nervous. The door opened a hand's width, and a voice slipped through the gap, young and terrified and urgent:

"Ines. My name is Tobin. I hear doors — I heard yours asking to be opened. There are people outside the mountain looking for you. A scribe with moving ink. He's coming for you."

And Ines, in the dark, began to smile — not the court's smile, not the boy's hollow brightness, but the other kind, the kind with teeth.
