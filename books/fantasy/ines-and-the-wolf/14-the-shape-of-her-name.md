## 14. The Shape of Her Name

The vault beneath the tithe hall smelled of cold iron and old honey.

Orsen had expected rot. He had expected the stink of the guild's ledgers, that sour tang of vellum soaked in tallow and secrets. Instead the air was almost sweet, thick as syrup, and it clung to the back of his throat like something swallowed too fast. The Soulwarden's clerks had called it *the fragrance of preservation*. They said it reverently, the way farmers spoke of rain.

Orsen thought it smelled like a thing being kept from dying past its time.

He moved between the transfer alcoves with a scribe's slate in his hands and a courier's badge on his chest — Maren's forgery, good enough for the lower galleries where no one looked at faces, only at ink. Around him, the vault breathed. Rows of glass vessels rose from floor to ceiling, each one cradling a curl of light no larger than a moth: souls in suspension, labeled in a hand so fine it might have been embroidered. Orsen did not let himself read the labels. If he read them, he would have to think about what they meant, and if he thought about what they meant, his hands would shake, and shaking hands could not copy keys.

*Copy the key. Find her. Get out.* Three instructions. He repeated them the way he'd once repeated ledger sums, and there was a bitter comfort in that — that even here, in the belly of the court beneath the mountain, he was still doing arithmetic.

The master key hung in the Warden's antechamber behind a grille of black bronze. Orsen had watched the duty clerk unlock it twice that day, and both times he had written the motion into memory the way his gift demanded: fully, truly, *movingly*. Now, alone in the antechamber's shadow, he drew out his stylus and a scrap of wax-paper and began to write.

His ink moved when he wrote true things. It had always moved — a small, secret shame he'd hidden from the Guild of Impossible Things for eleven years, until the night it betrayed him before an inspector and cost him everything except the people who'd pulled him out of the flooded archive tunnels and refused, absurdly, stubbornly, to let go of him since.

Now the ink moved again. On the wax-paper, the copied sigils writhed like living things, rearranging themselves into the exact sequence the black bronze demanded. His gift had never been a curse. Here, in this place built on stolen souls and perfect records, it was a blade.

He was folding the paper when he heard the singing.

It came from the far end of the vault, down the corridor marked **TRANSFER — ACTIVE**, and it was not a hymn or a work-song. It was a girl's voice, thin and cracked, singing a counting rhyme in a northern dialect Orsen half-remembered from debtors who came down from the pine country. *One for the door, two for the snow, three for the wolf that walks below—*

His heart stopped, then started again, hard enough to hurt.

He knew that voice. He had heard it only once, years ago, through a guildhall door — a feral girl with winter in her teeth, brought in as collateral on her father's impossible debts, snarling at anyone who came near until Orsen had sat down outside her cell and simply *talked*, scribe to stranger, about nothing, about weather, about the terrible food, until she stopped growling and started listening. He had catalogued her soul for sale because he was ordered to. And then, in the only act of rebellion his careful life had ever contained, he had falsified the entry — understated her gift, misdated her assessment — to buy her time.

Time the guild had spent anyway. Time the crown had spent. Ines had been sold onward, down and down, to the court beneath the mountain, and Orsen had crossed half a kingdom and traded pieces of himself he could never get back to follow her here.

He should have gone for the key first. That was the plan. *Key, then Ines, then out.*

But the singing faltered mid-verse, and what replaced it was worse than any song: a sound like tearing linen, like ice cracking on a pond, like something between a scream and a howl.

Orsen ran.

* * *

The transfer alcove at the corridor's end was a hemisphere of white stone, lit by lamps that burned without flame. In its center stood a chair of silver filigree, and in the chair sat a girl.

No — not a girl. Not anymore, not entirely.

Ines had been fifteen when he last saw her, all sharp elbows and sharper eyes, dark hair chopped ragged by her own knife. The creature in the chair shared her bones but little else. Her skin had gone pale as birchwood, veined with lines of silver that pulsed in time with the machinery humming beneath the floor. Her hair had gone white at the temples. And from her shoulder blades, from her forearms, from the ridge of her spine, fur had come — grey and silver and winter-white, growing in patches like frost claiming a windowpane. One of her hands was still a hand. The other was a paw, long-toed and black-clawed, strapped to the chair's arm with bands of woven light.

Her eyes found him the moment he stepped into the alcove. They were amber now, wholly amber, wolf-gold, and they were *aware*. That was the worst of it. Whatever the tithe-engine was doing to her, whatever portions of her soul it had already drawn off into the waiting vessels, it had left her mind intact. Left her watching. Left her knowing.

"You're late," Ines rasped.

Orsen's legs nearly gave out. He crossed the alcove in four strides and dropped to his knees beside the chair, and for one unguarded moment he pressed his forehead against the silver armrest because he could not trust himself to speak.

"You're real," he managed. "I kept thinking I'd invented you. A guilt I made up to have somewhere to put itself."

"Guilt." She laughed, and the laugh broke into a cough, and the cough shook loose a thread of silver light from her lips that drifted up toward the ceiling vessels like smoke with intent. "You came all this way for guilt?"

"I came all this way because I lied in a ledger," Orsen said. "And it wasn't enough. And I've been trying to make it enough ever since." He was already working at the bands of woven light, fingers probing their weave. They were contracts — he realized it with a lurch. Physical contracts, spun into restraints, each strand a clause. "Hold still. This is going to be like unpicking a knot in the dark."

"It's going to be like unpicking a knot while the knot is awake," Ines corrected. Her ears — she had ears now, high on her skull, furred and swiveling — twitched toward the corridor. "The Warden comes at the turn of the hour. There's a bell under the floor; I can feel it winding."

"Then we have minutes." Orsen pulled out his stylus and the wax-paper with the copied key-sigils, and set them against the nearest band. "My ink moves when I write true things. Let's see if it can write a lie *true enough*."

He wrote: *This contract is fulfilled. This debt is paid. This soul is free.*

The ink crawled off the page and into the woven light, and for a heartbeat — one glorious, impossible heartbeat — the band flickered, dimmed, loosened—

And snapped back tight, burning a line of frost across Ines's wrist.

She didn't cry out. That frightened him more than screaming would have. She just bared teeth that were becoming less human by the hour and said, "They anchor the clauses in the tithe itself. As long as they're drawing me off, the contract holds. You'd have to stop the engine."

"Then I'll stop the engine."

"With what? Your pen?" But there was something in her voice beneath the scorn — wonder, fierce and disbelieving. "Orsen Villalobos. Ledger-keeper of Vellamarch. You hid under your numbers for eleven years and now you're going to fight the Soulwarden with a *pen*."

"I stole a master key with it an hour ago," Orsen said. "I'm told I make quite a good thief. Apparently I always was one; I just used to steal for the wrong side." He gripped her unbound hand, the human one, and her fingers closed around his with a strength that would have crushed a weaker man. "Listen to me. My friends are coming — Maren, Tobin, Sefa. Tobin hears doors. He'll find us. All I need is to keep you whole until they do."

"Whole," Ines repeated softly. She turned her amber eyes up toward the vessels overhead, dozens of them now, each holding a curl of light, and Orsen understood with a horror that settled into his marrow like groundwater that some of those lights were *hers*. Drawn off in installments. Stored. Waiting to be poured into the court's immortal veins. "Do you know what they take, scribe? Not strength. Not years. They take *remembering*. Every tithe, I lose a piece of my past. I've been rationing myself. I recite the guildhall every night — the smell of the ink, the bad soup, the man who talked to me through a door until I stopped wanting to bite him." Her grip tightened. "I remember you. Whatever else they strip out of me, I have made them leave *that*."

Orsen's throat closed. He thought of the tollhouse, of the memory-broker's gentle hands, of his mother's face dissolving like sugar in rain — a price paid once, and he had sworn never again. Yet here was Ines, paying that price in installments, and choosing, every single time, which piece to lose.

"Then hold onto it," he whispered. "Hold onto all of it. I'm getting you out of this chair if I have to rewrite every clause in this mountain."

"That's the spirit." Her muzzle-lengthening grin was wild and bright and utterly herself. "Terrible plan. Very you."

That was when the bell beneath the floor finished winding, and rang.

* * *

Ines had learned, in the months beneath the mountain, to measure time in losses.

The first tithe had taken the sea. She had grown up three days' walk from the grey northern water, and one morning she had reached for the memory of it — the smell, the roar, the way gulls hung crooked on the wind — and found a smooth blank place where it had been, like a page torn cleanly from a book. She hadn't cried. Crying wasted salt, and salt was strength, and strength was the only currency she had left.

The second tithe took her mother's voice. The third took the name of her village. The fourth, fifth, and sixth took, in order, the taste of honey, her brother's laugh, and the color green — that last one worst of all, because afterward the pines outside her cell window were merely *dark*, and she knew she had once loved them and could not remember why.

So she had begun to ration. Each night before a tithe, she chose. She laid her memories out like coins on a table and decided which ones the court could have and which ones she would hide, buried deep, wrapped in other memories like smuggled letters inside legitimate ones. The Soulwarden's clerks believed the tithe took whatever it touched, indiscriminately. They were wrong. The tithe took what floated highest. Ines had taught herself to sink what mattered.

And what mattered, always, at the very bottom, weighted with stones, was the guildhall. The bad soup. The ink-smell. The door, and the low tired voice on the other side of it, talking about weather to a girl who wanted to bite the world.

*Orsen,* the memory was named. She had asked his name through the door on the fourth night, and he had answered like it surprised him that anyone wanted to know.

Now he was kneeling in front of her, real as hunger, smelling of lamp-oil and fear and ink, and his hand was warm around hers, and Ines felt something crack open in her chest that had nothing to do with the engine.

Fury, mostly. Joy underneath the fury, the way ore runs under stone.

"You shouldn't have come," she said, because someone had to say it. "You should have stayed in Vellamarch and been small and safe and boring."

"I was never safe," Orsen said. "I just hadn't been caught yet. The hunt changed that." He was still working at the bands, stylus flying, muttering contract-language under his breath — she could see the words move on his wax-paper, actually *move*, and she stared. "Your ink is alive."

"My ink is honest," he said. "There's a difference. Hold on—"

The bells rang again, closer, and the alcove's white lamps shifted from white to red.

Ines felt the change in her own body before she saw it anywhere else. The engine, sensing interruption, sensing *theft-in-progress*, surged. Silver light climbed the chair's filigree and sank into her skin, and the change that had crept over her for months now came like a tide — fur racing down her arms, her spine arching, her jaw pushing forward into something between a woman's face and a wolf's. Pain went through her like a struck bell. She rode it. She had learned to ride it. The wolf was not her enemy; the wolf was the part of her the court couldn't digest, and every tithe that stripped away Ines-the-girl seemed only to feed Ines-the-wolf, as though her soul were dividing itself to survive.

*Let them take the girl,* she thought, teeth gritted. *The wolf remembers. Wolves don't forget the hand that fed them. Or the hand that lied for them.*

"Ines." Orsen's face swam above her, human and ordinary and the most beautiful thing she had seen in a year. "Stay with me. Talk to me. Tell me something true."

"The soup was bad," she gasped. "The soup in the guildhall was unspeakable. You complained about it through the door for an hour. That's how I knew you were a coward and a friend."

"I stand by both," he said, and his voice cracked, and his living ink was crawling up the bands of light now, unraveling them thread by thread, clause by clause, and one strap burst apart in a shower of dying sparks—

Footsteps. Many. Measured. Unhurried, which was somehow more terrifying than running would have been.

The Soulwarden entered the alcove the way winter enters a valley: gradually, and then completely. It wore a body — tall, robed in grey that drank the lamplight — but the body was a formality, like a signature on a document. What looked out through its eyes was older. Hungrier. Patient the way mountains are patient.

"So," it said, and its voice was the sound a quill makes on parchment, amplified. "The scribe from Vellamarch. We wondered when your guild's little error would walk itself home." Its gaze moved to the broken strap, to the moving ink still writhing on Orsen's wax-paper, and something like amusement rippled through the room. "Moving ink. How quaint. We haven't seen your gift in the vaults since the founding. Did you know, scribe, that the first master of your guild had the same talent? It was *his* hand that drafted the original tithe contracts. Honest ink makes the strongest chains."

Orsen went very still. Ines watched the blood leave his face and understood that the Warden had just handed him a truth heavier than any chain — and she hated it, hated the thing in the grey robe, with a ferocity that made her vision sharpen and the world go bright-edged and simple.

*Prey,* said the part of her that was wolf. *Threat. Pack.*

"Step away from the vessel," the Soulwarden said.

"She's not a vessel," Orsen said quietly. "She's a person. I've read enough of your records to know the difference, even if you've forgotten it."

"And you are a debtor." The Warden raised one grey hand. "Everything you are, scribe — your skill, your position, your debt, your *guilt*, so carefully cultivated — was engineered. Written. You are a document that walked. Do you really imagine you can amend yourself?"

"Yes," said Ines.

And she tore her remaining arm free.

The second strap parted with a sound like a shout, because she did not unpick it the way Orsen had tried to — she *pulled*, with the whole furious weight of the wolf, and the woven contract-light shrieked and failed, and Ines came up out of the chair half-transformed and entirely alive, silver light streaming off her like water off wet fur.

What happened next happened fast. Alarms. Red light. Clerks pouring into the corridor and stopping dead at the sight of the thing rising off the tithe-chair. Orsen grabbing for her hand. The Soulwarden raising both arms, and the air filling with the smell of cold iron and old honey gone rancid, and bindings of grey light snapping toward them both—

Ines got one arm around Orsen's waist. She felt him clutch the wax-paper with the master key to his chest — *good, thief, keep it* — and then the world contracted to a corridor of red light and running feet, her own four-and-two feet hammering the stone, his breath ragged against her shoulder, the Warden's voice rolling after them down the galleries like a landslide given language:

*"Seal the doors. Seal every door. Nothing leaves the mountain with what belongs to the court—"*

They rounded a corner and the passage ahead was already closing, black bronze slabs sliding down with a boom that Ines felt in her teeth, and behind them more boots, more light, the net drawing shut.

Orsen skidded to a halt, chest heaving, trapped between iron ahead and iron behind — and then he lifted his head, and Ines saw him do the thing she would remember for the rest of her life, however long the court left her a life to remember anything:

He smiled.

"Tobin," he breathed. "Tobin hears doors." He turned to her, wild-eyed, alive, gripping her hand like a vow. "Ines — listen. Somewhere in these walls, one of these doors is going to open *for* us. We just have to be standing in front of it when it does."

Somewhere deep in the mountain, faint as a heartbeat, a door sighed open — and held.
