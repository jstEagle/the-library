## 15. The Warden's Offer

They kept him in a room with no doors.

Orsen understood the architecture of it within an hour of waking there — a chamber carved from the mountain's living stone, its walls smooth as poured wax, its single entrance hidden behind what Tobin would have called a listening door. He could feel the seam in the rock if he pressed his palm flat against the eastern wall: a faint coolness, a held breath. The court beneath the mountain did not believe in locks. Locks admitted the possibility of opening. They preferred rooms that simply forgot they had ever been passages.

He had been given a bed he did not sleep in, a basin of water that smelled faintly of iron, and a table. The table was the insult and the invitation both. Upon it, arranged with a curator's precision, lay pen and ink and a single sheet of paper — good paper, Vellamarch stock, the kind the Guild of Impossible Things imported at ruinous expense for contracts that mattered.

They wanted him to write. That was the first thing he understood. The second thing, arrived at more slowly, in the long amber hours while the wall-lamps burned without flickering: they wanted to see *how* he wrote.

So Orsen sat with his hands folded and did not touch the pen, and thought about Maren's face, and tried not to think about Maren's face, because every time he did he saw her standing in the flooded dark of the archive tunnels with the burning inspection ledger clutched to her chest like an infant, saying *run, you idiot, run* — and his chest did something complicated and useless that a man about to bargain for his life could not afford.

The door opened on the third day. It did not open so much as resolve; one moment the wall was wall, and the next a tall figure stood in a frame of lamplight, and Orsen felt the air pressure change the way it changed before a storm came over the fells.

The Soulwarden was not what he had expected. He had imagined — from Ines's fractured accounts, from the ledgers' cold marginalia, from the tithe hall's liturgy — something vast, something wolf-adjacent, something with the mountain's hunger wearing a shape. Instead there stood a person of indeterminate middle age, grey at the temples, dressed in the plain charcoal wool of a senior clerk. Kind eyes. The kind eyes were the worst thing Orsen had seen since entering the mountain.

"Master Villalobos." The voice was warm, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once been interrupted. "You've been sitting with your hands in your lap for three days. Most scribes last half a day before they start writing. Guilt, usually. Or habit."

"I'm resting my hands," Orsen said. "They're my livelihood."

A smile touched the Warden's mouth without reaching anywhere near his eyes. "May I?"

He did not wait for permission. He drew out the chair opposite — the room provided furniture now, obligingly, the way a dream provides furniture — and sat, folding his own hands in unconscious mirror. Up close he smelled of nothing at all, which was its own kind of wrong. Every person Orsen had ever met smelled of something. Even the hollowed ones in the tithe hall had smelled of lilies and cold ash.

"You're wondering why you're alive," the Warden said.

"I'm wondering why I'm comfortable," Orsen said. "Alive I can account for. A dead thief tells no one where he hid the key."

"And where did you hide it?"

"In a place you'll never audit."

The Warden laughed — a genuine sound, which frightened Orsen more than rage would have. "There it is. Do you know how rare that is? Defiance with bookkeeping in it. We bought four hundred souls last year, Master Villalobos. Farmers, singers, a hedge-witch or two, children mostly. Not one of them bargained. They wept, or they prayed, or they went quiet as meat. But a *scribe*—" he tapped the table, once, beside the untouched pen— "a scribe understands that everything is negotiable, because everything is written, and anything written can be rewritten."

Orsen said nothing. His heart was behaving badly. He made it behave.

"I've read your file," the Warden continued. "All of it. The guild's copy and our own. You were bound at nineteen — a debt of eleven marks, four shillings, incurred for your mother's lung-fever treatment. Curious arithmetic, that debt. Eleven marks for a fever cure, when the going rate in Vellamarch was three. Did you never wonder why the guild overcharged you by eight marks?"

The question landed somewhere below Orsen's ribs and stayed there. He had wondered. He had wondered for fifteen years, in the small hours, and always concluded that the guild was simply the guild — that usury was weather, that debt was gravity, that no one had designed it.

"The answer," the Warden said softly, "is that a debt of three marks is paid off in six years. A debt of eleven marks is paid off never. And a scribe who writes true things is worth keeping forever. Your whole life, Master Villalobos, has been someone else's well-managed asset. I say this with admiration. Whoever structured your indenture understood the market."

*Betrayal later,* Orsen told himself. *File it. There will be time to bleed over it if there is a later.* He kept his face still, and was distantly proud of it.

"You want something," Orsen said. "People who want nothing don't bring up a prisoner's mother."

The Warden inclined his head, conceding the point like a chess player acknowledging a decent move. "I want the girl's contract returned. Willingly. Freely. Signed over by your hand, in your ink, with your gift bearing witness." He paused. "And in exchange, I am prepared to offer you the only two things you cannot steal."

"Which are?"

"Your freedom. And your past." The Warden leaned forward, and the lamplight moved across his kind, empty face. "Your debt, erased — not paid, *erased*, struck from every ledger in every archive in every kingdom where the guild keeps copies. You walk out of this mountain owing nothing to anyone. No guild, no crown, no hunt. The proclamation names the gifted; the proclamation can be amended. One line of moving ink, and Orsen Villalobos becomes a man who never existed in any record at all. Free. Anonymous. Untouchable." He let that sit in the amber light between them. "You have spent fifteen years being written about. I am offering you a life with no one holding the pen."

*Except you,* Orsen thought. *You'd hold the pen. You'd just write blank pages.*

"And the girl?" he asked. His voice came out level. He was almost impressed with himself.

"The girl is inventory." The Warden said it without cruelty, which was somehow more terrible than cruelty would have been. "Her soul has another sixty years of yield in it. The court takes a tithe each season — a spoonful, never enough to kill, just enough to keep two hundred immortals immortal. She will live comfortably. She will be fed, warmed, respected. She will simply be *less*, each year, by degrees so gentle she won't notice, the way a man doesn't notice going bald or going blind. Everyone in this mountain is less than they were. It's called civilization."

"She noticed," Orsen said. "She notices everything. She's half wolf. She counts the spoons."

Something flickered behind the Warden's eyes — the first honest expression Orsen had seen on him. Interest. Perhaps appetite.

"Yes," the Warden said. "She does. Which is why her yield has been disappointing, and why the transfer was scheduled to move to... extraction rather than harvest. The girl will be emptied entirely within the year. Smiling, probably. They usually smile at the end; the soul goes out through the face." He spread his hands. "Unless. Unless her contract passes willingly into our keeping, signed by a gifted hand, witnessed by gifted ink — because willing surrender triples the yield, Master Villalobos. A soul given freely burns brighter than a soul taken. That is not cruelty. That is chemistry. One signature from you buys her comfort instead of agony, and buys yourself a life. Refuse, and we take the contract anyway — we have already taken it, in the legal sense; you merely stole the physical instrument — and we take it *unwillingly*, and she suffers the difference, and you die in a room with no doors having accomplished nothing but a delay."

The lamp flames did not flicker. Somewhere far above, water was finding its way down through the mountain's bones, drop by patient drop, and Orsen fixed his attention on that sound because it was the only honest thing in the room.

*He's lying about some of it,* Orsen thought. *Not all. The best lies are ninety percent ledger-true. But some of it is bait.* The question was which part. If the freedom was real and the mercy was false, refusing was suicide that bought Ines nothing. If the mercy was real and the freedom was false, signing was murder with extra steps. If both were true—

If both were true, then the choice was arithmetic, and Orsen Villalobos had spent fifteen years doing arithmetic for other people's sins.

"Give me the night," he said.

The Warden studied him for a long moment. Then he rose, smoothed his clerk's wool, and smiled the smile that went nowhere.

"Of course. Deliberation is free. It's the only thing in this mountain that is." At the wall he paused, half-turned. "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, I price things for a living — I hope you sign. You're wasted on the guild. You'd have gone far here."

The wall closed behind him like water closing over a stone.

* * *

Orsen waited. He counted it out — a thousand heartbeats, then five hundred more — because whatever heard through these walls deserved to hear a man pace, and sigh, and sit, and suffer. So he paced. He sat on the bed's edge. He put his head in his hands and let his shoulders shake, and only some of it was performance, because the Warden had known about his mother, had read the arithmetic of her death like a market report, and grief is not punctual; it arrives when it arrives.

Then he went to the table.

The pen was goose-quill, cut by a careful hand. The ink was black and thick and smelled of oak galls and iron — proper ink, guild ink, the ink of his whole imprisoned life. He uncapped it and dipped the quill and did not write words.

He wrote the key.

It hung above the vault door in the record-levels, three galleries down and one gallery up from the tithe hall — Orsen had mapped it during the Warden's tour, that obscene guided stroll past shelves of soul-contracts filed like deeds to orchards. The lock was not a lock in any locksmith's sense. It was a *reading* lock: a plate of pale metal etched with characters that shifted when observed, a text that rewrote itself faster than any eye could follow. Only the master key — a rod of the same pale metal, carried on the Soulwarden's belt, Orsen had seen it, had *made himself look away from it* so as not to seem to look — only the key knew the text's next sentence before it wrote itself.

But Orsen's ink moved too.

That was the secret he had carried since he was nine years old and first watched a written truth crawl on the page like something warming itself in sun. Moving ink did not merely record. Moving ink *kept pace*. And for one hour, in the vault antechamber, pretending to faint from fear while guards dragged him past the great door, he had let his eyes rest on the shifting plate — not reading it, no one could read it — but letting his gift do what gifts do, which is to say, letting it *copy*. His right hand had twitched against his thigh, finger by finger, tracing strokes no one could see, and somewhere in the marrow of him the pattern had settled like a bird coming to roost.

Now he gave it paper.

The quill moved without his quite steering it. Black lines bloomed across the sheet — not letters, not numbers, but the key's own grammar, its self-rewriting logic rendered in ink that rewrote itself in sympathy, stroke answering stroke across the distance between vault and cell. The page looked like nothing. A child's scribble. A storm of loops. But if you held it against the lock's plate — if you brought the copy home to its original — the two texts would recognize each other the way a wolf knows its own name.

It took until the lamps dimmed for their long artificial dusk. When the last stroke settled, Orsen sat back, breathing hard, sweat cooling along his spine. The page writhed gently in the lamplight, alive, hungry, faithful.

He thought of Sefa teaching Tobin to sleep in shifts. He thought of Maren burning the inspection ledger and laughing in the flooded dark, wild with terror and relief. He thought of Ines in the tithe hall, counting spoons, refusing to forget herself.

Then he thought about what the Warden had offered, and did the only honest arithmetic available to him.

Signing would save him. Signing might even soften her suffering — the Warden had no reason to lie about yield chemistry; lies that cost the liar nothing are cheap, but this one would cost him product. And yet. And yet a soul surrendered willingly, tripled in brightness, would feed the court for decades. His signature would not spare Ines. It would *season* her. It would make her delicious. The Warden had offered him a knife and called it a door.

And the erased debt, the amended proclamation, the life with no one holding the pen — that part was true, Orsen suspected, entirely true, and precisely therefore worthless. A man with no records was a man with no witnesses. A man outside every ledger could vanish as thoroughly as the hollowed children, and no one would ever come asking, because there would be nothing left to ask about. The Warden was not offering freedom. He was offering erasure with better lighting.

Orsen folded the page into eighths, small as a seed, and considered where to hide it.

In a place you'll never audit, he had said. He almost laughed. There was exactly one archive in this mountain that the Soulwarden would never search, one repository beyond suspicion, one hiding place guaranteed sacred by the court's own greed.

Himself.

He pressed the folded page flat against his sternum, inside his shirt, over his heart, and thought: *every ledger-keeper knows the oldest rule. The best place to hide a document is in the body of another document.* Let them catalog his gift, weigh his soul, price his memories. Let them open him like a book. They would find what they expected — a scribe, a coward, a man for sale — and folded against his beating heart, disguised as the flutter of a frightened pulse, the key would go on copying itself, waiting for the day a thief walked back into the vaults.

Because that was the decision he had made somewhere between the pacing and the weeping, quietly, the way debts are decided: he would consider the offer. He would weigh it beautifully. He would let the Warden watch him struggle for a day, two days, letting hope curdle into calculation on his face.

And then he would say yes.

He would say yes with tears in his eyes, and ask for a ceremony, and request the honor of signing in the vault itself — where else, after all, should a contract of such weight be executed but among the archives? — and when they brought him before the great pale door with its unreadable, rewriting text, he would take out this folded scrap of living ink and press it home, and the lock would taste its own handwriting and open.

The scribe becomes the thief. He turned the phrase over and found it fit him better than any title the guild had ever given him.

Outside the doorless walls, the mountain hummed its vast digestion. Two hundred immortals slept on stolen years. Somewhere below, Ines was counting spoons.

Orsen picked up the pen one last time, and on the back of the key, in ordinary motionless script, in letters so small they might be texture, he wrote the only true thing left worth recording:

*I am going to betray everyone in this mountain, beginning with the man who trusts me most. Forgive me, whoever reads this. I priced it fairly.*

The ink shimmered once — his gift, assenting — and went still.
