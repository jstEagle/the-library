## 16. Everything I Priced

The broker was waiting for him in the dark below the tithe hall, as if he had always been there — as if the mountain had grown him out of its stone the way it grew its pale gardens of soul-glass.

Orsen found him by smell first: cold iron and old paper and something sweeter underneath, like fruit rotting in a locked room. Then by the small gold light that hung above the man's head with no lantern to hold it, swaying gently, though there was no wind this far underground.

"You came back," the broker said. He sat on a broken step with his hands folded in his lap, patient as a spider between meals. His coat was the color of wet slate, and his face was the kind of face Orsen had never once managed to remember afterward — pleasant, unremarkable, already fading even as he looked at it.

"I came back," Orsen said.

His voice sounded strange to him. Steady. That was the strangest part. His heart should have been hammering; instead it beat slow and even, like a metronome in an empty room. Somewhere above them, muffled through forty feet of living rock, the court's music played on — thin strings, a singer whose voice bent notes like light through water. The court did not know yet. The court would not know until it was too late, and Orsen intended to keep it that way.

"The key," he said. "The master impression. You said you could give me what the vault's lock wants to taste."

"I said I could." The broker unfolded one hand, palm up. In it lay a sliver of black metal no longer than a finger joint, etched so finely with lines that they seemed to move when Orsen wasn't looking directly at them. "And you said you'd pay my price. Full price. Not the tollhouse rate." A small smile. "You've thought about it, then."

"I've thought about nothing else since the tollhouse."

That was true, and the truth of it sat in him like swallowed stone. At the tollhouse he had paid a single memory — his mother, her face, her voice saying his name — and had walked away lighter and emptier, unable even to grieve properly because grief needs a shape and she no longer had one. He had told himself then: never again. One memory to survive. Never again.

But that was before the vaults. Before he'd knelt in the dust among the guild's oldest records and read, in his own guildmaster's hand, the founding charter of the tithe — *the Guild of Impossible Things proposes to the Crown a renewable harvest* — and understood that every page he had ever balanced, every entry he had ever inked, had fed this place. Before he'd found Ines's contract chained to the transfer engine, her name glowing faintly, being worn away letter by letter like a coin rubbed smooth by too many thumbs. Before the Soulwarden had smiled at him across a table of silver instruments and offered him freedom in exchange for her.

He had copied the master key in moving ink. But moving ink only *persuades* a lock; it does not *become* it. The vault wanted more than forgery. It wanted proof of payment from a gifted hand — and the only currency the gatekeepers accepted from the gifted was memory, drawn whole, at full weight.

"Sit," the broker said, gesturing to the step beside him. "This is better done sitting. People fall otherwise."

Orsen sat. The stone was cold through his trousers. Above them the music changed — something slower now, almost tender, which somehow made it worse.

"How much?" he asked. He made himself ask it plainly, the way he'd asked ten thousand questions about ten thousand accounts. *What is owed? What is the balance?* Numbers had always been safer than feelings. He clung to that now like a rope over a drop.

The broker tilted his head, and the little gold light tilted with him. "You know how much. You've known since you walked down here. Say it anyway. It's important that you say it."

Orsen looked at his hands. Scribe's hands, ink-stained at the cuticles, a callus on the middle finger where the pen rested. Hands that had written lies to save a girl and truths that moved on the page.

"Maren," he said. Each name cost something just to speak, and he felt each cost land. "Tobin. Sefa." His throat tightened around the last one, and he forced it out anyway, quiet as a confession. "Ines."

"All of it," the broker said softly. "Not summaries. Not the highlights. Everything. Every conversation, every meal shared, every night you slept in shifts on the road, every time Tobin heard a door before it opened and you pretended not to be afraid. Every moment you have of any of them, from the first word to the last. Drawn at full weight." He paused. "You will not know their names afterward. You will not know you ever knew them. You will look at four strangers and feel nothing but what a stranger feels."

"And the theft will still work," Orsen said. "I'll still be able to open the vault. Burn the contracts."

"Oh, yes. Memory is the finest key there is. The vault drinks it gladly." The broker's smile widened by a degree. "It's rather poetic, isn't it? The court feeds on souls; the lock feeds on love. Everything in this mountain eats something."

*Everything in this mountain eats something.* Orsen turned the phrase over and found, to his surprise, that it steadied him. Because that was the thing about ledgers — he had spent eleven years learning this — every debt has two sides. Something taken, something given. The guild had taught him to record both columns without flinching. He had told himself for years that the ledger only records, never causes.

Tonight he would make the ledger lie one last time. Tonight he would cause something.

"Before we begin," Orsen said, "I want terms in writing."

The broker laughed — a dry, papery sound. "Terms?"

"I'm a scribe. I don't enter agreements I can't audit." He pulled his notebook from inside his coat — the small one, the private one, the one with the moving ink — and uncapped his pen with his teeth. "One: you take only memories of those four people. Nothing else. Not my mother again — she's already gone, and whatever fragments remain are mine. Two: the extraction happens after the key is delivered and proven. Three: when it's done, you walk away and you don't sell what you took. Not to the court, not to the Soulwarden, not to anyone. What you take from me stays dead. Sworn on your own trade."

For a long moment the broker simply looked at him, and the gold light burned steady between them.

"You want me to swear not to profit from the richest haul I'll ever carry," he said. "Do you know what four people's worth of love weighs, scribe? Properly weighed? Most men pay me a face. A voice. A single afternoon. You're offering me years."

"I know what I'm offering." Orsen's pen hovered over the page. "Swear it or find another customer. There isn't time for another customer, and we both know it. The tithe is in three days. Her name is half gone."

Something flickered across the broker's unmemorable face — respect, perhaps, or its close cousin, appetite. He extended his hand, palm up again, and a thread of shadow rose from it and coiled in the air like a serpent considering.

"Sworn," he said. "On my trade, which is older than your guild and hungrier. What I take tonight, I keep. No selling, no trading, no whispering it into the wrong ears. Dead and buried." The shadow curled into a knot and sank back into his palm. "Satisfied?"

Orsen wrote the terms in his notebook, in his careful ledger-hand, and watched the ink settle black and still. True things. He wrote only true things, and the ink obeyed.

Then he closed the book, and put it away, and there was nothing left to do but the thing itself.

* * *

"Tell me about them first," the broker said. He drew from his coat a small instrument of glass and brass, shaped like an hourglass but with three bulbs instead of two, and set it on the step between them. Inside the glass, something stirred — a darkness with a faint grey shimmer, like smoke remembering it had once been wood. "It goes easier if you talk while I draw. The mind resists less when it's busy telling stories. And—" the smile again, gentle now, almost kind, which was the cruelest thing about him— "you might like to hear yourself say it. One last time."

Orsen stared at the glass bulb. He understood suddenly why people wept at this stage, why some begged, why some ran. It wasn't the pain — the tollhouse had barely hurt, just a cold pull behind the eyes like a tooth coming loose. It was this: the last hour of knowing. The border crossing. You stood on one side of it whole, and everything you said became a farewell whether you meant it as one or not.

*Maren,* he thought. *Tobin. Sefa. Ines.* Four names. He held them in his mouth like stones he refused to spit out.

"Maren burned evidence for me," he said. His voice came out low and rough. "First night we met properly. My ink betrayed me during inspection — wrote the truth right off the page in front of the guildmasters — and she swept the pages into the fire before anyone could read them, and lied with a straight face, and then ran with me into the flooded archive tunnels without asking why. She didn't even hesitate. She had a life there. A desk. A plant she watered every morning. She left all of it in ten seconds flat." He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "She complains constantly. About the mud, the weather, my cooking, Tobin's snoring. But she complained loudest the day I suggested she'd be safer alone, and she hasn't let me suggest it since."

The broker turned the hourglass over. The shimmering dark began, slowly, to fall.

"Tobin hears doors," Orsen went on, faster now, as if he could outrun the falling dark. "Any door. Locked ones, buried ones, doors that haven't been built yet. He heard the vault gates of this mountain from three ridges away and mapped half the servant ways from sound alone. He's twelve and he snores and he calls me 'ledger-man' like it's an insult and he cried the first night on the road and pretended it was rain. There was no rain." The cold pull started behind Orsen's eyes, gentle as a hand resting on the back of his skull. He kept talking. "Sefa is seventy if she's a day and she cheats at cards and she sealed a ward behind us in the mountain's throat with her bare hands and nearly died doing it, and when we pulled her back she said—" his breath caught— "she said, *well, someone has to keep you lot alive, you're hopeless.* She smells of woodsmoke and feverfew. She taught Ines to braid her hair so the wolf-shape wouldn't tear it out."

The pull deepened. Somewhere far away, Maren's voice began to go quiet — not gone, not yet, but dimming, like a lamp carried down a long corridor. Orsen felt the edges of her start to soften. The color of her coat. The exact pitch of her laugh.

"Ines," he said, and stopped.

The broker waited. The dark fell another grain.

"She arrived at the guildhall in chains," Orsen said. "Half-feral. They brought her to me to catalogue — her soul, for sale, itemized like grain. And I falsified the entry. First lie I ever wrote that mattered. I bought her three days with a forged valuation, and she looked at me through the bars of the holding pen with winter in her teeth and she knew — she *knew* what I'd done, and she never thanked me, because thanks would have made it a debt, and she was done with debts." His eyes were burning now, and he let them. "She remembers things. That's her armor. Whatever they do to her under this mountain, however much they take, she holds onto who she is by remembering. The guildhall. The road. Us." He laughed, wet and cracked. "She's holding onto *me*, down there. The scribe who lied in the ledger. And I'm about to sit here and give away my half of it. Both halves. All of it."

"You could stop," the broker said quietly. The dark was more than half fallen now. "Walk away. Keep them. Find another way into the vault. There's always another way — worse, bloodier, slower, but there's always another way."

"No," Orsen said. "There isn't. Not one that leaves her whole. I've run the numbers." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, briskly, like a man closing an account. "That's the difference between me and the guild, do you see? They priced other people's souls and never paid anything themselves. I'm paying. Full column. Balanced to the last line."

"What will you do," the broker asked, "when you don't remember why any of this mattered? When you open that vault and burn those contracts and feel — nothing? When you wake up tomorrow a stranger to everyone who loves you?"

Orsen considered it honestly, the way he considered everything: carefully, completely, without flinching.

"I won't know why it mattered," he said. "But it will have mattered. That doesn't undo itself. The burning will be real. Her freedom will be real. Love isn't only what you remember — it's what you *did*. The ledger keeps the deed even when the memory's torn out." He looked at the hourglass, nearly empty now, the last grains of dark falling soft as ash. "They'll remember for me. Maren will. She's good at keeping records too."

The last grain fell.

The broker reached out and touched two fingers to Orsen's temple, and the world went white and silent and utterly, perfectly empty —

— and then Orsen was sitting on a cold stone step in the dark beneath a mountain, next to a stranger with an unmemorable face, holding a sliver of black metal in his hand and wondering distantly why his cheeks were wet.

"Done," the stranger said. He was packing away a curious glass instrument. "The key is proven. The price is paid. We're square, scribe."

"Scribe," Orsen repeated. The word fit. It seemed to be the only thing that did. He looked at the black sliver in his palm — fine etched lines, shifting when he didn't watch them — and understood what it was for, the way you understand things in dreams. "There's a vault," he said slowly. "Contracts. Souls. I'm going to burn them."

"That's the idea." The stranger rose, brushed off his slate-colored coat, and paused. For a moment something almost like pity crossed his forgettable face. "You cried, near the end. Talking about them. You won't know who 'them' means, but — for what it's worth, whoever they were, they were worth crying over."

Orsen touched his wet cheeks, frowning. "I don't remember crying."

"No," the stranger agreed. "You wouldn't." He turned to go, then stopped again. "One more thing, free of charge, since you kept honest books. The girl — the one whose name you gave last, the one you saved for the end like the best bite of a meal—" he smiled thinly— "she's in the tithe hall, third transfer cycle, and her contract is wearing thin. If you mean to burn this mountain down, scribe, I'd hurry."

And he was gone, between one blink and the next, leaving only the smell of cold iron and old paper and rotting fruit.

Orsen sat alone in the dark. Above him the court's music played on, tender and obscene. In his hand, the key. In his chest, a hollow the size of four people, aching in a way he had no story for — grief with no object, love with no name, a debt recorded in a ledger he could no longer read.

He stood. He checked the notebook in his coat — his handwriting, terms in a careful ledger-hand, though the names in them meant nothing now — and he began to climb toward the vaults, calm as a man balancing accounts.

Far above, deep in the heart of the mountain, something felt the first memory strike the lock like a coin dropped in an empty cathedral — and the vault doors, ancient and starving, began to open.
