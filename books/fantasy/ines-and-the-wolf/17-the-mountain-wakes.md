## 17. The Mountain Wakes

The first thing Maren felt was the heat.

It came up through the stone like a fever rising through a sickbed — not fire, not yet, but the memory of fire, the promise of it, climbing through the soles of her boots and into her teeth. She had been crouched in the servant gallery outside the tithe hall for what felt like hours, Sefa's warding chalk crumbling between her fingers, when every lamp in the mountain guttered at once.

Then they flared.

Every flame in the Court Beneath the Mountain turned gold — not lamplight-gold but something older, richer, the color of souls burning where they stood. Maren had seen that color once before, in the guildhall at Vellamarch, the night she burned Orsen's confession-page and ran. She knew it the way you know a voice in a dark room.

"Tobin," she whispered. "Tobin, listen."

The boy was already listening. He had his palm flat against the gallery wall, eyes shut, and his face was doing something terrible — wonder and horror fighting for the same ground.

"They're screaming," he said. "All of them. The doors — every door down there is *screaming*." His hand trembled against the stone. "Not the doors. What's behind them. It's like — like a thousand rooms all opening at once, and everything inside them waking up—"

The mountain shuddered.

Dust sifted from the ceiling in long gray ropes. Somewhere far below — deeper than the tithe hall, deeper than anything Tobin's gift could map — there came a sound Maren would carry to her grave: a vast, groaning exhalation, as if the mountain itself had been holding its breath for centuries and had finally, finally let it go.

"Sefa," Maren said. "Is it him? Did he do it?"

The old hedge-witch didn't answer at once. She was standing very still, her gnarled hands spread wide, reading the air the way other people read pages. Her eyes were wet.

"The contracts are burning," she said softly. "Can't you feel it? All those poor penned things, all those souls sold and shelved and stacked like winter stores — they're *loose*, girl. Every one of them." A tear cut a bright line through the chalk dust on her cheek. "That foolish, brave scribe. He actually did it."

* * *

Orsen did not remember doing it.

That was the shape of the thing — the terrible, elegant shape of it. He stood in the record-vault with ash drifting around him like black snow, and he did not know why his hands were scorched, or why the master ledger lay open before him with its contracts curling into flame one by one, or why three strangers were pounding down the corridor toward him shouting his name.

He knew his name. That much the broker had left him. *Orsen Villalobos.* It sat in his mind like a coin at the bottom of an empty well — real, but nothing to draw up with.

The vault was coming apart around him. The great racks of soul-contracts, ten thousand ledgers deep, were burning from the inside out, and each contract that caught light screamed — a thin, human scream, a scream with a name in it — and then burst into motes of gold that swarmed upward through the shattered skylights like bees returning to a hive that no longer existed.

He should have been afraid. Some distant part of him noted this: *you should be afraid now.* But the fear wouldn't come. Fear needed a past to be afraid *for*, and his had been sold.

The corridor door burst open.

Three people. A tall woman with ink-stained fingers and a clerk's sharp face, half-collapsed against the frame. A boy of perhaps fourteen, wild-eyed, gripping the woman's sleeve. An old woman leaning on a staff crowned with river-stones, chalk dust to her elbows.

They looked at him the way people look at a house they grew up in, found burned to its foundations.

"Orsen." The tall woman's voice cracked on the second syllable. She crossed the vault in four strides and caught his face in her hands, and her touch was gentle and desperate and utterly familiar to every part of him except the part that kept memories. "Orsen, look at me. It's Maren. I'm Maren. We came back for you."

"I don't—" His voice came out strange to him. Flat. Polite. A clerk's voice. "I'm sorry. I think you have the wrong—"

"He doesn't know us." The boy's voice, small and horrified, from the doorway. "Maren, he doesn't *know* us. You said he might not, but—"

"He paid," the old woman said. She had not come closer. She was watching Orsen with an expression he couldn't read — grief and reverence tangled together like roots. "He paid everything. Look at his eyes, child. There's nothing behind them but today."

"There has to be something," Maren said fiercely. "There has to be. Orsen — the ledger fragment, Ines showed you, you *chose* this, you wrote it down yourself, you knew what it would cost and you—"

Somewhere above them, the mountain screamed.

* * *

Tobin heard it before anyone: a sound like a door the size of the sky being torn off its hinges.

"MOVE!" he shrieked, and grabbed Maren's arm, and hauled her away from the vault's central aisle just as the ceiling came down.

Stone the size of a cart-horse crashed through the space where she'd been standing. Dust exploded outward, white and choking, and through the dust came the light — gold everywhere now, pouring out of the broken racks, out of the walls themselves, as if the mountain's bones had been hollowed and filled with lantern-oil and someone had finally struck the match.

And in the light: *them.* The freed souls. Tobin saw them and would see them for the rest of his life — shapes made of that deep gold, some small as children, some vast and slow as weather, streaming upward through the ruin in silent rivers. One of them paused. It was the size of a sparrow and it hovered an arm's length from Tobin's face, and though it had no eyes he felt it looking at him, felt it *thanking* him, and then it spiraled up through a crack in the stone and was gone.

"They're getting out," he breathed. "Maren, they're getting *out*—"

"Not all of them." Sefa's staff cracked against the floor for attention. "Listen to me, both of you. When a cage breaks, not everything inside remembers how to fly. Some of those souls have been shelved so long they've forgotten their own names. They'll pool. They'll *pool*, do you understand, and pooled soul-light burns whatever it touches — and this whole mountain is about to be knee-deep in it."

As if to prove her right, a cascade of gold poured through a fissure in the eastern wall and splashed across the vault floor, and where it landed the stone glowed cherry-red and began, slowly, to run.

"The galleries," Maren coughed, hauling herself up. "The way we came — Tobin, can you still hear the doors?"

Tobin pressed his hand to the wall and listened through the roar.

Everything was screaming. Everything was singing. The doors of the mountain had spent centuries being obedient and silent, and now they were howling like wolves, and beneath the howl he could feel the deep structures shifting, settling, *failing* — staircases unspooling, corridors folding shut like hands.

But he could hear them. That was the thing about his gift; it had never once failed him, even when he'd begged it to.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, I can hear them. This way. And Maren — run when I say run. Don't ask. Just run."

* * *

They ran.

Through galleries where the gold light fell like rain through broken ceilings. Across a bridge over a cistern that had become a lake of slow-burning soul-light, its surface dotted with the pale, confused shapes of souls who had forgotten they were anything but light. Down stairs whose steps crumbled behind them, Tobin calling turns three beats before they came — "*left, left, the door here hates us, go soft, GO*" — while Sefa threw wards behind them that sealed collapsing passages with walls of hardened chalk-light, each one costing her something visible, a little more stoop, a little more gray.

Maren carried Orsen.

He could walk — he walked, mechanically, competently, a clerk following instructions — but he did not run *toward* anything. When the bridge swayed he gripped the rail because gripping rails was sensible. When Tobin shouted *run* he ran, because running was the instruction. Watching him, Maren felt something in her chest tear loose and refuse to stop bleeding.

She had known him for six years. Six years of shared tea gone cold over ledgers, of his dry voice reading entries aloud — *"a shadow pawned against Tuesday, redeemed never"* — of the night he'd falsified a line for a wolf-girl and looked up at her with guilt in his eyes like a man confessing a theft of bread. She had fled the guildhall with him. She had slept beside a road with him and learned the sound of his breathing. She had traded her own small hoard of silver to a broker in Vellamarch just to learn which mountain road the transport had taken.

And now he looked at her with courteous blankness, and thanked her when she pulled him out of the path of falling stone, and asked, once, mildly, in the lull between collapses:

"You seem to know me. Have I wronged you? If there's a debt, I'll want to settle it."

Maren had stopped dead in the middle of a burning gallery and laughed until she cried, and Tobin had thought she'd gone mad, and Sefa had put her hand on Maren's shoulder and said, gently, "Grieve later, girl. Grieve *later*."

So she grieved later. She grieved later at a dead run, tears cutting through the dust on her face, dragging the empty well of a man who had once been her dearest friend toward daylight he no longer wanted.

* * *

Ines found them at the third collapse.

Later, none of them could say exactly how — whether she'd torn her way up through the mountain's heart or simply *arrived*, the way weather arrives. But the corridor ahead of them, the last corridor, the one Tobin swore ended in a wall of fused stone, suddenly did not end in a wall of fused stone, because something enormous and white and furious had removed it.

The wolf stood in the wreckage with gold light sheeting off her fur like water off a waterfall. She was too big — wrong-big, a wolf the size of a horse, shoulders scarred with the pale seams of old chains, eyes the color of winter sunsets. For one heartbeat she looked at the four of them without recognition, a wild thing in a burning world.

Then her gaze found Orsen.

What passed across her face then — across the wolf-shape's face, which should not have been able to hold expression and yet held it, held it the way a cup holds water — was so naked and so human that Maren made a sound low in her throat.

"She knows him," Tobin whispered. "Look — she *knows* him, even like that, even after—"

"He lied for her," Sefa said quietly. "In the ledger. Before any of us. Whatever else was sold, girl — that wasn't. Debts of that kind don't clear."

The wolf lowered her great head and pressed her brow, briefly, against Orsen's chest. He went very still. Something moved behind his eyes — not memory, not quite, but the *shape* where a memory might live, the negative space of it, an ache with no object.

"I'm sorry," he said to her, helplessly. "I think—I think I was supposed to know you."

The wolf made a sound that was almost a word. Then the mountain bucked beneath them like a ship in a storm, and there was no more time for almost-words.

* * *

They came out of the mountain's mouth into night air so cold it burned, and behind them the Court Beneath the Mountain died loudly.

The peak shed itself in avalanches of gold. Rivers of freed light poured from every fissure and vent, rising into the dark sky in columns that could be seen, Maren knew, from a hundred miles away — seen by hedge-witches and hunted families and every debt-bound soul in the kingdom, seen and understood, or at least *hoped*. The guildhalls' great engines of record would be feeling it too, she thought. Every ledger in Vellamarch was, in its way, a tributary of this mountain. And tributaries run backward when the sea reverses.

Sefa sank down onto a boulder, gasping, her wards spent, her hands shaking. Tobin collapsed beside her, ears ringing, listening — even now, even here — to the doors of the dying mountain crying out their last. Maren stood with Orsen's sleeve gripped in her fist, as if letting go might lose him entirely, watching the light climb.

And Ines — the wolf folded smaller and smaller, bone sliding under skin, until a girl knelt in the snow where the wolf had been: thin, half-naked, chain-scarred, her breath steaming, her eyes still lit faintly gold at the edges.

She crawled to Orsen.

He watched her come with that polite, ruined blankness, and when she took his scorched hands in hers he let her, because he was kind — whoever he was now, whatever remained, kindness remained, Maren saw it and clung to the sight of it like a rope.

"Ines," the girl said. "My name is Ines. You wrote it in a ledger once, in ink that moved, because you wanted me to have time." Her voice was wrecked and fierce and steady. "You bought me a year with one lie. Then you bought me a whole life with everything you had." She lifted his hands and pressed them, flat, against her sternum, over her heart. "I don't care that you've forgotten. I remember enough for both of us. Do you understand? I will remember *enough for both of us*."

Orsen looked down at her. At the scars. At the gold fading in her eyes. And something happened in his face — not remembering, never that, but the ache finding its object at last, the negative space filling with the outline of a loss he could not name.

"I don't know you," he said, and his voice broke clean in half. "I don't know you, and I am grieving you like — like a country. Like a home that burned down before I was born. Why do I *grieve* you?"

"Because you loved us," Maren said, coming to kneel in the snow beside them, Tobin and Sefa closing in, a ragged circle against the burning sky. "Because you loved us, you impossible, stubborn fool, and love leaves marks that brokers can't price. That's what's left. That's what they couldn't take."

Above them, the last of the stored souls rose out of the mountain's broken crown — thousands upon thousands, a slow golden snowfall going *upward* — and among them, for just a moment, one small bright shape paused above the five of them, hovering, before it streamed away north toward the stars.

Orsen watched it go. And though he could not have said why, he raised one scorched hand and waved goodbye.

Behind them, deep in the settling ruin, something that was neither soul nor stone began — very faintly, very patiently — to laugh.

Sefa heard it first. Her head came up like an old hawk's.

"Get him on his feet," she said. "Whatever that was, it just lost its larder. And hungry things always follow the ones who emptied the pantry."
