## 18. Wolf at the Heart

The contract burned in her mouth.

Not paper — she had never held the paper — but the thing itself, the shape of her ownership, dissolving somewhere in the vaults below as Orsen's stolen fire ate through the master ledgers. Ines felt it go the way a dog feels a chain snap: not freedom first, but weight. The sudden terrible lightness of a body that belonged to itself again after so long belonging to arithmetic.

She stood in the tithe hall with ash drifting down like grey snow, and the wolf came up through her like water finding its old channel.

It had always been there. That was the secret the guild had never understood, the thing they had tried to price and parcel and sell by the ounce. You could not sell what was not property. You could only delay it. And delays end.

Her bones spoke first — a long grinding chorus, femurs lengthening, spine unspooling into something that ran low and heavy along the ground of her. Her skin split not in pain but in relief, fur rising through it silver-grey and winter-pale, the color of the pine forests she had dreamed of every night for three years in her cage of candlelight and hymns. Her jaw widened around teeth that had been waiting. Her eyes went gold, then deeper than gold, then to a color that had no name because no one who saw it lived long enough to name it.

The last human thought she carried into the change was small and absurd and precious: *he lied in the ledger for me.*

Then she was wolf, wholly, and the hall was full of screaming.

* * *

The Soulwarden's engine stood at the heart of the tithe hall like a cathedral built by something that hated cathedrals.

Ines had seen it every day of her captivity and had never been permitted to look at it directly — the attendants turned your head with gentle hands when you were led past, murmuring about the sanctity of the work. Now she looked. Now nothing in the mountain could make her look away.

It was a machine of glass and brass and worse things. Vessels in their hundreds rose in tiers toward the vaulted ceiling, each one holding a soft glow that pulsed slow as a sleeping heartbeat, and she knew — the knowing came to her whole, the way a wolf knows a wounded thing across a valley — that each vessel held a soul. Not metaphor. Not theology. Souls, drawn thin as syrup, stored sweet and slow-feeding, sipped at by the court above to keep their faces smooth and their hands steady and their centuries rolling on. Some of the glows were bright. Some were nearly out. One, near the top, flickered in a rhythm she recognized without wanting to: quick, stubborn, arrhythmic, like a child's heart trying to be brave.

The engine's core was a great turning wheel of bone-white rings, and seated within it, wired through with filaments of light, was the Soulwarden himself — if he could still be called himself. He had fed so much of his own humanity into the mechanism that he was more apparatus than man now, a figure of pale robes and patient machinery, his face the only part left untouched, and it was a kind face, which was the most obscene thing in the entire mountain.

He turned that kind face toward her as the doors blew inward and the mountain began to die.

"Ah," he said, in the tone of a physician greeting a difficult patient. "The wolf-girl. Your contract has been — irregularities are being addressed. If you will simply—"

She hit him at full run.

The engine shrieked. Glass vessels burst in cascading rain of light — freed souls going up through the shattered ceiling like reversed snowfall, like sparks climbing home — and the Soulwarden's machinery screamed in a register that cracked the marble floor. His kind face finally changed. It went through surprise, then calculation, then something rawer, older, the face under all the faces: fear.

Good, thought the wolf, in the wordless way wolves think. *Good.*

She tore the tiers down one by one. Brass bent like green wood under her jaws. Filaments of hoarded light snapped and whipped and discharged, burning lines across her shoulders, and she barely felt them, because grief was burning hotter. Every vessel she broke was a person someone had sold. Every glow that rose free and faded upward was a name she would never know, a debt she could not collect, a theft she could only interrupt, never undo. Fury was simple. This was worse than fury. This was fury with grief inside it, fury wearing grief as armor, and it made her tireless.

The wheel of bone-white rings she saved for last. She circled it slowly while the hall collapsed around them in groans of settling stone, while the freed souls thinned to a trickle overhead, while somewhere far above the court's beautiful people discovered what happened when the fuel ran out mid-sip.

The Soulwarden hung in his ruined cradle, half-unwound, filaments dangling from him like cut harp strings. He was still trying to speak. She let him.

"You don't—" he managed. "You don't understand what you're ending. The tithe is mercy. A soul drawn thin over two hundred years feels almost nothing. Without us they'd be hunted anyway — the crown's hunt, the guild's ledgers — we gave them purpose, we gave their suffering *structure*—"

The wolf put her head back and howled, once, and the sound went through the mountain like a blade through bread. When she lowered her head, some of Ines was in her eyes again, enough to answer, though her throat could no longer make words. The answer was this: she stepped forward and closed her jaws around the central ring, and pulled.

The engine did not scream. It sighed — a vast exhalation of spent light, centuries of stolen living leaving the machine all at once — and the Soulwarden went with it, unwinding, his kind face finally honest at the very end, which was to say: empty. What fell to the floor among the wreckage was small and dry and weighed nothing at all.

Ines stood over it, sides heaving, blood and light mingling on her fur, and found that triumph tasted like ash and cold iron.

* * *

She found him in the corridor outside the vaults, and for one black moment she thought she was too late after all.

Orsen sat against the wall with his knees drawn up, the burned ledger fragment still clutched in one hand — she could see the scorch marks, the ghost of his own handwriting, the entry he had falsified three years ago to buy a feral girl time she hadn't known she needed. His eyes were open. They were the worst thing she had seen in the whole collapsing mountain, worse than the tithe-engine, worse than the emptied children, because they were *blank* — not frightened, not pained, just vacant, a house with all the lamps out and no one home to light them.

He had paid everything. She understood it without being told, the way she now understood many things with the wolf's terrible clarity. He had walked into that broker's parlor and handed over Maren's laugh and Tobin's door-listening and Sefa's curse-words and Ines herself — the guildhall, the lie in the ledger, the girl with winter in her teeth — every memory that made him the man who had done this, traded away so that the vault would open and the contracts would burn. He had completed the theft knowing he would never know why it mattered.

And he had done it anyway.

The mountain was coming down. She could feel it in her paws, in the fine tremor running through the stone, in the dust sifting from the ceiling seams like flour through a torn sack. Somewhere above, Maren was shouting names into the chaos — shouting *Orsen*, mostly, over and over, a woman calling for a man who no longer answered to anything.

Ines shifted back. It hurt more going human than going wolf; the body was smaller, slower, poorer, and grief fit badly inside it. But she needed hands. Hands to lift him. Hands that had once been catalogued in a ledger as collateral, worth eleven crowns and a mule, and were now going to carry a free man out of a dying mountain.

"Up," she said, and her voice came out rough, half-wolfed still, gravel and wind. "You're walking if you can. I'm carrying you if you can't."

He looked at her with those lampless eyes and said, politely, the way one speaks to a stranger: "I'm sorry. Do I know you?"

Something in her chest tore cleanly, like wet paper.

"No," she said. "Not anymore. But you're going to."

* * *

She got his arm over her shoulders and took the servant ways, because Tobin wasn't there to hear the doors for them and someone had to guess. The corridors were filling with dust and panic — courtiers in their beautiful clothes stumbling blind without their borrowed centuries, servants fleeing with armfuls of nothing, the whole gilded organism of the mountain convulsing as its heart stopped. Ines moved through it low and fast, wolf-strength in her human legs, and where the ways were blocked she put her shoulder to stone and made new ways.

Twice the ceiling came down behind them. Once a fissure opened ahead, breathing hot air from some deep broken furnace-room, and she jumped it with him in her arms, and he did not cry out, only watched her with that awful polite blankness, and afterward said, "You're very strong," in the tone of a man making conversation at a tollhouse.

"I'm very angry," she said. "It's the same thing, most days."

They came out onto the mountainside as the sun was setting, and the peak behind them was breathing smoke. Below, scattered down the switchback trail, she could see them: Maren with soot to her elbows hauling Tobin upright, Tobin with blood in his hair listening to the mountain's death-rattle through the ground itself, Sefa leaning on her staff and swearing with magnificent fluency at the sky, the rock, the crown, the guild, and God, in roughly that order.

They saw her. They saw Orsen slung against her side. Maren started down the slope at a dead run, and Ines watched the woman's face do everything a face could do — joy and horror fighting for the same inches — and arrive, finally, at the expression of someone reaching for a door that has been bricked over.

"Orsen." Maren caught his face in both hands. "Orsen, it's me. It's Maren. We came back for you, we—"

He smiled at her. Kindly. The way one smiles at an earnest stranger.

"You have me confused with someone," he said gently. "But you're hurt, and it's cold, and — please, let me help. I'm good with accounts. I could keep track of your supplies, if that would be useful."

Sefa made a sound like a knife going into her own heart. Tobin turned away and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

Ines stood apart, letting the wind pull at her, and felt the wolf stir restlessly under her skin, offering its simple solutions. She declined them. The wolf could tear engines apart. It could not remember a man for him, and that was the only work left now, and it was human work, slow work, fire-and-story work, and it would take all winter and maybe all their lives.

She came and stood beside him at the trail's edge. Below, the mountain city was going dark, window by window, its ten thousand lamps of stolen life guttering out forever. Above, the first stars were arriving, indifferent and clean.

"They'll tell you who you were," she said to him. "All of them. Every night, if it takes years. Maren will start with the archive tunnels, probably, because that's where she found her courage. Tobin will tell you about doors. Sefa will lie about half of it to make it a better story, and everyone will let her."

He listened the way he must have listened to debtors once, patiently, attentively, a scribe's courtesy still living in him like a plant growing back after frost.

"And you?" he asked. "What will you tell me?"

Ines looked at the burned fragment of ledger still clutched in his hand, his handwriting ghosting through the scorch — proof, the only proof, that he had chosen this before he knew what choosing would cost.

"I'll tell you about a girl the guild sold," she said, "and a scribe who lied in the ledger to save her. And then I'll tell you the rest, night by night, until the stories take root. That's the bargain. That's the debt now — mine, not yours. And I pay my debts."

Behind them, the mountain shuddered and settled, and Maren called them both down to the fires, and Orsen Villalobos — blank-eyed, empty-handed, owed everything — followed the sound of strangers' voices toward the light, and Ines walked beside him, keeping watch over the most valuable thing she had ever carried:

a man at the beginning of being remembered.
