## 20. Strangers Who Stay

The fire was the first thing Orsen trusted.

Not the people around it — not yet, though they had carried him down a dying mountain and fed him broth for three days and asked nothing in return but that he stay where they could see him. Not the ruined town either, all black beams and sagging doorways, where ash still sifted from the sky like gray snow that refused to melt. But the fire itself, small and honest in its ring of stones, crackling with the smell of pine — that he could understand. Fire did what it said it would do. It burned.

He sat apart from the others, on an upturned cart wheel near the edge of the light, and watched them the way he supposed one might watch strangers at any crossroads inn: cataloguing, measuring, waiting for the lie to show its seams.

There were four of them who mattered, if their story was true. The clerk-woman, Maren, with her ink-stained cuffs and her habit of touching her collarbone when she spoke of things that frightened her. The boy, Tobin, who kept tilting his head toward doors that weren't there, listening. The old woman, Sefa, who hummed under her breath while she worked charms into the hems of their clothes, little stitches of protection that Orsen could almost feel humming against his own skin. And the girl.

The girl with winter in her teeth.

He didn't know how he knew that phrase. It had arrived in his head whole, like a stone dropped into still water, and it rippled through him every time he looked at her. She sat closest to him without sitting close — a careful distance, the distance of someone who had learned that approaching too fast made wild things bolt. Her eyes were amber and unsettlingly steady. Sometimes, when the firelight moved across her face just so, he thought he saw something else looking out of them. Something patient. Something that had run a very long way and was willing to run longer.

"You're staring," she said, without turning her head.

"I'm trying to remember you." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I'm told I knew you."

"You knew me." She turned then, and the firelight caught the pale seam of a scar along her jaw that made his chest ache inexplicably, a grief with no address. "You just don't remember knowing me. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"There's always a difference." She poked the fire with a stick, sending up a constellation of sparks. "That's what my mother used to say. Knowing is what happens to you. Remembering is what you choose to keep."

Orsen looked down at his hands. They were a stranger's hands, calloused in strange places — a writer's callus on the middle finger, a burn scar across the left palm he had no story for. Everything about him was like that now. A body full of evidence and no testimony.

"The others," he said carefully, "have been telling me stories. About us. About what we did."

"Have they."

"Maren says I burned the guild's ledgers. Tobin says I walked through the mountain's servant ways like I'd lived there all my life. Sefa says—" He stopped, because Sefa's story was the one that had unsettled him most: that he used to hum while he wrote, some tuneless little habit, and that she'd caught herself missing it before he'd even woken. "They say I paid for something. With everything."

Ines was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere beyond the ring of light, Tobin paused in his restless circuit of the ruined houses, head cocked toward a doorway, and moved on.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" Ines said at last. "It isn't that you don't remember us. It's that you look at us like we're the ones lying. Like we're the debt collectors come to collect something you never owed."

The words landed harder than they should have. Orsen flinched, and hated himself for flinching.

"I don't mean to—"

"I know." She said it quickly, and gently, and somehow that was worse. "You don't mean anything. That's what they took. You're not cruel, Orsen. You're empty. There's a difference there too."

* * *

He walked after that. He couldn't help it; the walls of the camp felt like the walls of a ledger, and he had — allegedly — spent his whole life inside ledgers. The ruined town gave him room. He picked his way down its gutted main street, past a well with a rope still hanging, past a shrine whose god-statue had lost its face to fire or time or both, and he let the cold air scour him clean of the fire's warmth and the warmth's attendant confusion.

Grief followed him anyway.

That was the thing no one had warned him about — or perhaps they had, and the warning had been priced into the bargain and sold along with everything else. He grieved constantly, pervasively, for people he could not name. It sat in his chest like water behind a dam. Certain things triggered it: the smell of ink. The sound of Sefa humming. The particular way Maren said *Orsen* — not the way a stranger says a name, testing it, but the way someone says a name they've said ten thousand times, worn smooth as a river stone. Each time, the grief surged, and each time he reached for its object and found nothing. A door in him had been bricked over, and he could feel the draft coming through the cracks.

*You chose this,* Maren had told him, the first night, when he'd accused them — gently, because even hollow he apparently couldn't manage cruelty — of holding him against his will. *You signed for it. We watched you sign.*

*Then show me the signature,* he'd said.

And Maren had gone quiet, and looked at Ines, and Ines had said, *Tomorrow.*

Tomorrow had become three days. Tonight, Orsen decided, walking back through the ash-quiet streets, tomorrow would end.

* * *

When he returned to the fire, Ines was alone. The others had withdrawn — he noticed that they did that, coordinated without speaking, giving him room the way you give a wounded animal room, and he resented it and was grateful for it in equal measure. She sat where he'd left her, mending a cloak with quick, sure stitches, and she looked up when he came into the light as if she'd heard him coming from much farther away than she possibly could have.

"Tomorrow's here," he said.

She set down the cloak. From inside her shirt she drew out something wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with the care of someone handling a relic, which, he would soon understand, it was.

A fragment of ledger page. Burned at the edges, scorched brown across half its face, but intact enough — and he felt his stranger's heart lurch before he even read it, because he recognized the hand. He recognized it the way you recognize your own reflection in dark glass: the slightly forward slant, the heavy downstrokes, the peculiar hooked *g* that no writing master would have approved of. His handwriting. Unmistakably, intimately his.

*Entry 4,417,* the fragment read. *Received: passage through the mountain, servant ways, ward-sealing included. Paid in full: memories of Maren Ondris, clerk; Tobin Hale, door-hearer; Sefa Vetch, hedge-wise; Ines—*

The name stopped there, eaten by the burn. But below it, crammed into the margin in smaller script, pressed hard enough to bite into the paper, was a single line:

*I know what this costs. Pay it anyway. Some debts are worth owing twice.*

Orsen read it four times. His hands had begun to shake somewhere around the second reading, and by the fourth he had to sit down on the cart wheel again, the paper held delicately between finger and thumb as though it might dissolve.

"This is my hand," he said. It wasn't a question, but he needed it said aloud, witnessed, entered into some record.

"It's your hand." Ines crouched in front of him, forearms on her knees, close enough now that he could see the fire reflected twice in her amber eyes. "I pulled it out of the vault myself. While the mountain was coming down around our ears. Everyone else grabbed gold, supplies, soul-contracts. I grabbed that." She tapped the margin line with one blunt fingernail. "Because I knew. I knew that someday you'd sit where you're sitting and look at us like debt collectors. And I wanted you to have proof. Not our word. Yours."

"I could have forged it." His voice sounded far away to himself. "If I'm the kind of man who falsifies entries—"

"You are," Ines said, and smiled, and the smile had winter in it and warmth underneath, and the grief behind the dam in his chest surged so hard he nearly gasped. "Maren taught you the guild's forgery marks when we were on the road. You're very good. But look at the pressure. Look at how deep you pressed. Forgers press lightly — they're afraid of being caught. Whoever wrote this wasn't afraid of anything. He was making sure it survived the burning."

Orsen looked. She was right. The strokes gouged the paper. Whoever had written those words had written them the way a man drives a stake into ground he expects to flood.

"Some debts are worth owing twice," he read aloud, slowly, tasting each word like a coin bitten for gold.

"That's what you said when you handed over the last of it. Sefa was there. She heard you." Ines's voice roughened. "You looked at each of us, one at a time, like you were trying to press us into the paper too. Then you said, *remember me wrong if you have to, but remember me.* And then you forgot."

The dam broke.

Orsen didn't sob — he was fairly sure, from the shape of his own body, that he was not a man who sobbed — but the grief came out of him in long shudders, silent and enormous, and he bent forward over the ledger fragment as if he could warm his hands at it, as if it were a fire smaller and more essential than the one beside them. Ines didn't touch him. She stayed crouched at his knee, present, steady, letting him break without trying to hold the pieces, and somehow that was exactly right, exactly what he needed, though he had no memory of ever needing anything.

"I'm sorry," he managed at last. "I'm sorry, I can't— I can't find you in there. Any of you. I've been looking every day since I woke and there's just—"

"Brick," Ines said softly. "We know. We've stood outside the wall too."

"Then why?" He lifted his head. "Why stay? Why feed me and mend my clothes and tell me stories every night? I'm not the man you knew. I'm the space where he was. You could walk away. No one would blame you."

Ines considered him for a long moment. When she answered, her voice was low and certain, the voice of someone stating a fact of nature.

"Because the wolf doesn't leave her pack."

"I'm not—"

"You are." She took his hand — the burned one, the one with the scar he had no story for — and turned it palm-up, and traced the scar with one finger. "You got this pulling me off a tithe-sled inside the mountain. The iron was hot. You didn't let go until the chain broke." She met his eyes. "I remember for both of us now. That's the arrangement. That's what we agreed, all of us, the night the mountain fell. Nobody gets forgotten just because forgetting was the price. So we carry it. Until the stories take root."

Orsen looked down at his scarred palm, at her finger resting on it, and felt something shift in him — not a memory returning, nothing so kind as that, but something adjacent. A settling. Like a house accepting new foundations.

"If I choose to trust you," he said slowly, "I want it understood that I know what I'm choosing. I'm trusting evidence. Your word. A scrap of paper. Not myself. I can't vouch for myself. I'm the one variable I can't account for."

"That's the most scribe thing anyone has ever said to me." Ines laughed — a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped a cage — and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Yes. Fine. Trust the evidence. Start there. The rest grows back. Like a forest after a burn."

* * *

Later — much later, when Tobin had finally stopped pacing and lain down with his head pointed toward the road, when Sefa's charms had gone quiet in their hems and Maren slept with one hand curled around a stub of pencil even in her dreams — Orsen sat by the embers with the ledger fragment open on his knee.

He read the margin line again. *Some debts are worth owing twice.*

And beneath it, in the same gouged, unafraid hand, he found something he had missed before — a final mark, half-eaten by scorch, tucked into the corner of the page where a proper accountant would have initialed his work. Not initials. A symbol. A small, hooked figure, like a wolf drawn in three strokes by someone who had only ever seen one in passing, or dreamed one.

His breath went out of him slowly.

He didn't remember drawing it. He didn't remember what it meant. But his hand remembered the shape of it — he could feel that much, a ghost of pressure in his fingers, the way a door remembers the wind that once blew through it — and when he glanced across the dead fire at Ines, sleeping in her cloak with her scarred jaw tipped to the stars, he understood, in the place where understanding lives when memory cannot follow, exactly whom the little wolf was meant to stand for.

He took out the pencil stub Maren had left beside his bedroll — she always left one, every night, a small deliberate invitation — and beneath the burned entry, in his own unfamiliar hand, he added a new line:

*Entry 4,418. Received: four strangers who refuse to be strangers. Payment pending: everything I am becoming. Terms: acceptable.*

Then, after a moment, he drew the wolf beside it. Three strokes. Unafraid.

Across the ashes, Ines opened her eyes — he hadn't heard her wake, hadn't heard anything, but she was looking straight at him as if she'd heard the scratch of the pencil in her sleep — and smiled.

"Keep going," she whispered. "Tell me what you write. I'll tell you what it means. That's how we start."

Beyond the ruined town, far to the north, a bell began to toll in some surviving village — slow, rhythmic, deliberate. Tobin sat bolt upright in his bedroll, head tilted, listening to a door none of them could hear.

"That's not a bell," Tobin said quietly. "That's a summons. Somebody's ringing in the hollowed."
