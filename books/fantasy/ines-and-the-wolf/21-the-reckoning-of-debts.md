## 21. The Reckoning of Debts

**Maren**

The tithe house at Coldharbour Row had been a counting office once, before the guild bought it and bricked over the windows. Maren stood in the street outside it on the first morning of the reckoning, her breath smoking in the grey light, and listened to the sounds inside: shuffling feet, the soft clink of chains, and beneath all of it a sound she had learned to dread above every other — the silence of people who had stopped expecting anything.

Tobin touched her elbow. "Three doors," he murmured. "The front one's angry. The back one's just tired."

"Everything about this place is tired," Maren said.

They had come to Coldharbour with a cart, a crowbar, Sefa's warding chalk, and Orsen — who had insisted on coming even though he still flinched when any of them said his name too warmly, as though warmth were a debt he couldn't account for. He stood now at the edge of the little crowd they'd gathered, hollow-eyed folk from the tenements who had heard rumors of what happened beneath the mountain, and he held his new ledger against his chest like a shield.

"Tell me again," he said quietly, "what we're doing here."

Maren had answered that question eleven times since the mountain came down. She would answer it a thousand times if she had to. That was the bargain none of them had spoken aloud but all of them kept: *we will remember for you.*

"You're going to open that door," she said, "and you're going to read out the names of everyone inside. And then we're going to give them back whatever pieces of themselves the brokers still hold." She paused. "You wrote the plan down yourself. Last night. By the fire."

He looked at the ledger. Opened it. His own handwriting stared back at him — cramped, precise, the hand of a man who had spent his life making numbers behave — and below it, in the same hand, a list of steps and a line at the bottom that made him go still:

*This is how we pay them back. Not in coin. In names.*

"I wrote this?" he said.

"You write everything true," Tobin said, grinning. "It's your whole thing."

* * *

The door was angry, but it was also old, and old doors could be reasoned with. Tobin pressed his palm flat against the wood and hummed low in his throat, some wordless negotiation, until the lock gave up its grudge with a click like a sigh. Inside, the air was thick with dust and lamp-oil and something sweeter underneath — the smell Maren had first noticed in the flooded archive tunnels, the smell of stored souls, like rain on hot stone.

Forty-one people sat in rows on benches in the dark. Some were chained; most weren't, because chaining was unnecessary. The hollowed did not run. They sat where they were put, hands in their laps, faces smooth as unscribed paper, waiting to be called for their next tithe.

Orsen walked down the aisle between them, and Maren watched him do the thing that had become his ritual: he read from the ledger, name by name, in a voice that carried.

"Aldric Vey, cooper. They took your memory of your daughter's laugh."

"Hesta Brann, laundress. They took the year you spent by the sea."

"Milo Fen—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Milo Fennick, tinker's son, nine years old. They took almost everything. We're going to get it back."

And one by one, the smooth faces turned toward his voice. Not understanding — not yet — but turning, the way plants turn toward a window. Maren had seen men weep at executions and women laugh at funerals, but she had never seen anything like this: forty-one people remembering, slowly, painfully, that they were people.

Sefa moved among them with her chalk, marking each bench with a ward that would keep what remained of them from leaking away while the brokers' fragments traveled home. Her hands shook these days — they had shaken since the ward nearly took her in the servant ways beneath the mountain — but her voice was steady as bedrock.

"Patience," she told them, over and over. "Patience. What was taken can be carried back. It only takes someone willing to carry."

* * *

**Tobin**

The brokers, it turned out, had not fled far enough.

Word had gone out across the kingdom in the weeks after the mountain woke — passed mouth to mouth, scrawled on walls, shouted from carts by people who had been in Coldharbour Row that morning and seen the door come open. *The guild is burning. The ledgers are ash. Come to the reckoning.* And the brokers, those gray little merchants of other people's lives, had done what merchants always do when the market turns: they had tried to sell their stock cheap and disappear.

Tobin found the first one behind a tavern in Marrowgate, haggling with a desperate woman over a stoppered glass vial that glowed faintly, the way a firefly glows, the way a soul glows when it's been decanted.

"He says it's her mother's voice," the woman was saying, her knuckles white around three copper coins. "He says there's an hour of her mother singing in here. He wants eight silver."

"It's worth eight silver," the broker said. He was small and damp-looking, with ink-stained cuffs, and Tobin hated him instantly and completely. "Memories degrade in storage. I'm doing her a favor—"

"No," Tobin said, "you're really not."

The broker turned, saw a boy of maybe fifteen standing in the alley with sawdust in his hair, and made the mistake of reaching for the vial. Tobin didn't fight him. He simply said, very clearly, "That door behind you has been wanting to fall off its hinges for six years," and the door agreed with him, tearing loose from its frame and catching the broker across the shoulders, pinning him gently but firmly to the tavern wall amid a shower of splinters and spilled ale.

The woman stared. The broker stared. Somewhere inside the tavern, a fiddler stopped mid-tune.

"How," the broker managed.

"People tell me things," Tobin said, taking the vial from the woman's hand and pressing it back into her palm along with her coppers. "Doors especially. Doors hear everything." He leaned close to the pinned man. "Here's what I've heard lately: every broker in the kingdom is going to be found, and every vial is going to go home, and nobody's going to hurt you — because we're not like you. But you're going to carry them yourself. Every single one. Door to door. And if you drop one—"

"I won't drop one," the broker whispered.

"Good," Tobin said cheerfully. "Because I know a wolf who checks."

* * *

By the end of autumn there were seventeen of them working the reckoning — freed debtors, runaway clerks, two defected guild guards, and the four of them at the center of it like the hub of a crooked wheel. They moved from town to town in a caravan of borrowed carts, and everywhere they went the pattern repeated: find the tithe house, open the doors, read the names, chase the brokers, carry the fragments home.

And everywhere they went, Orsen sat at night by the fire with his ledger, writing.

**Ines**

Ines watched him write, most nights, from the edge of the firelight where the shadows pooled around her the way they always did now, thicker than they should be, shaped like something with ears.

She was getting better at being a girl again. That was the truth of it, though it cost her more than anyone knew. The wolf lived close under her skin since the mountain — closer than before, permanent as a scar — and some nights the shape of her hands felt like a suggestion rather than a fact. On those nights she ran. The others had learned not to worry. They left the door unlatched and set no wards against her, and she always came back at dawn smelling of pine and frost, human again, or near enough.

But watching Orsen write — that she could do in any shape.

He had filled half the ledger already. Not debts. Never debts. Stories. He interviewed the hollowed as their fragments returned, patient as a priest, asking them to tell him what came back and what it meant to them, and he wrote it all down in his moving ink so that the pages shimmered faintly in the firelight, alive, the way true things are alive.

Tonight it was Hesta Brann, the laundress, holding the restored memory of her year by the sea like a cup of something warm.

"There was a boy," Hesta was saying. "I'd forgotten the boy. We used to dig clams at low tide and he'd eat them raw and make faces. I married someone else, years later, good man, dead now — but I'd forgotten I'd ever been seventeen and laughing." She wiped her eyes with her apron. "Why does it matter, scribe? It's such a small thing."

Orsen's pen paused. Ines saw him look up, and she saw the expression she had learned to read in him now — the careful face of a man assembling himself from other people's accounts.

"Small things are the whole cargo," he said. "I used to keep a ledger of debts. Souls weighed against coin. I thought numbers were neutral." He tapped the page. "Then I learned that every number is a person pretending to be a number. So now I keep the opposite ledger. Everything the takers called small, written large."

Hesta laughed through her tears, and Ines felt the wolf in her stir — not with hunger, but with something older and gentler, the pack-sense, the knowing that the den was warm and the pack was whole.

She should have been happy. Most nights she was.

But tonight, across the fire, Orsen caught her looking at him, and he smiled — polite, distant, the smile of a man for a kind stranger — and the wolf in her lay down and put its head on its paws and grieved.

He remembered the guildhall. He remembered lying in the ledger to save her; she'd shown him the entry herself, the falsified valuation in his own hand, and he had studied it like a stranger's clever forgery. But he did not remember *her*. Not the girl with winter in her teeth who had arrived in chains. Not the weeks of cataloguing, the smuggled bread, the single moment — she treasured it, hoarded it, told it to herself in the dark like a prayer — when he had looked at her across the ledger desk and said, quietly, so the guildmaster wouldn't hear: *"Hold on. I'll find a way to make the numbers lie."*

That man existed. She had proof in ink. But he was a story now, and stories have to be given, not taken.

Soon, she promised herself. When the reckoning reached Vellamarch. When the last tithe house stood empty. Then she would sit him down by a winter fire and begin at the beginning, and she would tell him about a girl who was sold twice, and a scribe who committed his first crime for her sake with nothing but a pen.

* * *

**Orsen**

Vellamarch smelled different than he remembered — which was to say, it smelled different than anything, because he did not remember it at all, and yet walking its streets he felt the strangest sensation, like a coat that fit perfectly and belonged to someone else.

"This was my guildhall?" he asked.

The building squatted at the end of Ledger Square, its windows bricked, its iron doors hanging open. Someone had already painted across the lintel in dripping letters: **THE LEDGER LIES NO MORE.** Inside, the great counting room stood stripped bare, desks overturned, the vault door torn from its hinges — he recognized the damage from the others' accounts of the mountain's waking, contracts burning across the kingdom like a contagion of freedom.

"The tithe system started here," Maren said beside him. "Your guild invented it. Sold the method to the crown. You found that out in the vaults under the mountain, right before—" She stopped, the way they all stopped now, skirting the edge of the thing none of them could say plainly: *right before you paid the broker everything.*

"Right before," Orsen agreed.

They walked through the hall together, the five of them — Maren brisk and efficient, Tobin listening to the building's moods, Sefa pausing to chalk a blessing over the threshold, Ines drifting at the edges where the light failed. And Orsen trailed his fingers along the master desk, the tall one by the window, and felt something turn over in his chest. Not memory. Memory was gone, sold, spent. But something adjacent to it — a groove worn into him, the shape of a habit.

"I sat here," he said. "For years. I balanced accounts here." He frowned. "I can almost feel the pen."

"You'll get it back," Tobin said. "Piece by piece. That's the deal."

"That's the deal," Orsen echoed, and opened his ledger on the ruined desk, and began to write.

*He wrote:* **On this day the Guild of Impossible Things was emptied of its impossible cruelty. Forty-one souls freed at Coldharbour Row. Sixty-three at Marrowgate. A hundred and twelve at Duncastle, and the brokers made to carry every fragment home on foot.** *His ink moved as it dried, swimming gently across the page like fish under ice, and he had long since stopped finding it frightening. True things moved. That was simply their nature.*

*He wrote:* **The hollowed are learning to be hollowed no longer. Hesta Brann remembers the sea. Milo Fennick remembers his mother's name, and then his own, and then, three days later, wept for the first time since his rescue — which Sefa says is the surest sign of a soul coming home.**

*He wrote:* **A boy named Tobin hears doors. A witch named Sefa holds back the dark with chalk and stubbornness. A clerk named Maren burned evidence to save a stranger and has never once regretted it, though she pretends to be sensible.** *He paused over that last line, feeling the strange vertigo of recording facts about himself that he could verify but not recall.* **These people say they are my family. The evidence supports them.**

And then, because the hall was quiet and the others were watching him with expressions he was learning to bear — hope, grief, patience braided together — he wrote the line he had begun ending every day's entry with, the line that had become his small liturgy, his way of paying forward a debt he couldn't remember owing:

**I do not remember them. I choose to believe them. Tomorrow I will ask them to tell me again.**

* * *

**Ines**

Winter came early that year, and with it the last house on the list.

It was a small one, a way-station tithe house on the road north of Vellamarch, holding only seven souls — but one of them, when Orsen read the manifests, made Ines go very still.

"Corvin Ashe," Orsen read. "Ferryman. Tithe record shows... ninety percent taken. Retained fragment: one memory, unclassified. Broker of record: Eland Voss, deceased." He looked up. "There's no broker to return it from. The fragment's still in the house strongbox."

They opened the strongbox together. Inside, nested in wool like a jewel, sat a single vial glowing soft as a coal.

Corvin Ashe sat on his bench, smooth-faced and empty, while Sefa unstoppered the vial and held it to his lips like medicine. For a long moment nothing happened. Then the ferryman's breath caught, and his hands came up slowly to his own face, and he began to speak — haltingly, wonderingly, a man reading words off the inside of his own skull.

"A boat," he whispered. "Green water. There's a girl on the bank and she's waving and I know her, I know her, she's—" His face crumpled. "My daughter. It's my daughter. They left me my daughter."

Ines turned away, because her eyes were doing something inconvenient, and found Orsen watching her across the room.

"What happens to them," he asked quietly, nodding at Corvin, "when only one memory comes back? Is one enough?"

Ines considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty. "One is a seed," she said. "Water it, and the rest grows back around it. Slowly. Crooked. But it grows." She hesitated, then added, before she could stop herself: "Someone told me that once. About seeds."

Something flickered in his face — not recognition, nothing so cruel as almost-remembering, but interest, the hunger he had for his own lost life. "Who?"

"You'll find out," Ines said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended, wolfish at the edges. "When the reckoning's done. I have a story for you, scribe. A long one. I've been saving it."

He studied her for a moment — this wild-edged woman who spoke to him like an old friend he couldn't place — and then he nodded, solemn as a signature.

"I'll be here," he said. "I'm told I always am."

Outside, snow was beginning to fall on the road north, thick and silent, erasing the tracks of the carts behind them and laying down clean ground ahead. Ines stood in the doorway of the last empty tithe house and breathed the cold in deep, and the wolf in her lifted its head and tasted the wind, and found — for the first time in either of their memories — nothing to hunt.

Only a fire to reach. Only a story to tell. Only a man with a new ledger and an old debt, waiting to learn who he had been.

She went in to sit by the fire, and began, at last, at the beginning: *"There was a girl with winter in her teeth..."*
