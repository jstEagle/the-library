## 22. Ines and the Wolf

The snow came down like ash that had changed its mind.

Ines sat with her back against the old stone chimney of the tollhouse they'd claimed — the same one where Orsen had once sold his mother's face for forged papers, though he didn't know it yet, and might never — and watched the fire throw its light across the faces of her family. Maren was mending a sock with the fierce concentration of a woman who had burned evidence for a living and found mending a gentler arson. Tobin lay on his stomach with his ear pressed to the floorboards, cataloguing the house's doors the way other boys collected river stones. Sefa dozed in the good chair, her hedge-witch hands folded over a cup of willowbark tea gone cold hours ago.

And Orsen sat closest to the fire, because he always did now, as if the flames were a page he was trying to read.

He still held himself like a scribe. That was the thing that broke Ines's heart most quietly — not his blankness when he looked at them, not the careful politeness with which he asked their names each morning as though checking whether they'd changed overnight, but the way his hands moved. Even empty of memory, they remembered ink. He turned his fingers around an invisible pen when he was thinking. He smoothed invisible paper flat before he spoke about anything difficult.

Tonight was story-night. They had been doing this since the mountain, since the ash, since the morning he woke among strangers and asked — gently, so gently, as if afraid of wounding them — *who are you people, and why do you look at me like I'm someone you lost?*

"Tell me one," he said now, into the fire. "Whichever one you want."

Ines looked at Maren. Maren looked at Sefa. Sefa opened one eye, considered, and closed it again, which from Sefa was a benediction: *yours tonight, girl.*

So Ines set down her cup and moved to sit beside him, close enough that he could feel the warmth coming off her — she ran hot these days, wolf-hot, even in human shape — and began.

* * *

"You were born in Vellamarch," she said, "in a room above a dye-shop, and the whole street smelled of madder root and vinegar, and your mother used to say you were the only baby who never cried at the smell because you liked it."

"I don't remember her," Orsen said. It wasn't a protest anymore. It was just a fact he laid on the table between them each night, like a coin, like a stone, like something he was learning to carry instead of mourn.

"No," Ines said. "But listen anyway. Listening is how it gets back in."

She told him about the boy who taught himself letters by copying the dye-shop's labels. About the debt his father took against his name — a debt Orsen would learn, much later, in a vault beneath a mountain, had been engineered by the Guild of Impossible Things before he could walk, a leash disguised as misfortune. She told it without softening, because he had asked them once, early on, not to make him kinder than he was. *If I was a coward,* he'd said, *tell me I was a coward.* (He hadn't been, mostly. But he had been complicit, and he needed to know it, and telling him that part was the hardest thing Ines did all winter.)

"And then," she said, "one autumn day, the guild brought me in."

His eyes came up. This part he leaned toward every time, the way a plant leans toward a window.

"A girl with winter in her teeth," he said. "That's how you said it. You've told me this one four times."

"Five," Ines corrected. "You fell asleep during the fourth. And you always say that line back to me, and it always does something to my chest, so I'm going to keep telling it until my chest gets used to it or stops breaking, whichever comes first."

The fire popped. Outside, the snow kept falling with that enormous hush snow has, the hush of a world holding its breath. Tobin had stopped pretending to catalogue doors; he was listening with his eyes shut, mouthing along, because he knew this story too, all of them knew all of them, and that was the point. A story known by one person is a memory. A story known by five is a fortress.

"They brought me in half-starved and snarling," Ines went on, "and they put me in front of your desk, and you were supposed to catalogue my soul for sale. Write down what I was worth. And you looked at me — really looked, not the way buyers look — and then you wrote down a lie. You wrote that my gift was weak. Dying. Not worth the tithe-price. You bought me time with a stroke of your pen."

Orsen's hand had drifted, without his noticing, through the motion of writing. Ines watched him do it and felt the ache and the warmth tangle together the way they always did now, grief and gratitude braided so tight she could no longer tell which strand was which.

"Why would I do that?" he asked. He asked every time. "I didn't know you."

"You did something better than knowing me," she said. "You saw me. There's a difference. Knowing takes time. Seeing takes a decision." She reached over and took his hand — the scribe's hand, ink-stained at the knuckles even now, even after everything, as if the ink had gotten into him past any broker's reach. "You decided. And then later, when the crown called the hunt and the guild sold me on to the court under the mountain, you burned your own life down to come get me. You paid a memory-broker with everyone you loved. With us. With yourself. And you did it knowing you wouldn't remember why it mattered."

"That's the part I can't make sense of," Orsen said quietly. "I sit here by your fire, and you're kind to me, and I believe you — I believe all of it, the way you believe a story in a book. But I can't feel it. There's a shape where it should be. Like a tooth gone from the jaw. My tongue keeps going back to it."

"I know," Ines said. "I know exactly what that's like."

Because she did. That was the secret engine of all these winter nights, the thing she had never said aloud in full: the court beneath the mountain had been drinking her too, sip by sip, tithe by tithe, and there were rooms in her own past that stood empty now. Her mother's voice — gone, drunk down by a Soulwarden's engine. The smell of the pine forest where she'd run as a child — gone. She knew they had existed the way Orsen knew Maren had once been his friend: by testimony, by ledger, by love's paperwork.

So when she promised him things, she promised as an expert.

"I'll be your memory," she said, the way she said it every night, because promises are like stories — they only take root through repetition. "Until yours grow back, if they grow back. And if some of them never do, then I'll hold those too, for as long as I live, and after that Tobin holds some, and Maren holds some, and Sefa — well, Sefa claims she intends to outlive all of us out of sheer spite, so we're covered for decades."

From the good chair, without opening her eyes, Sefa said, "Decades, girl. Write it down."

Orsen laughed. It startled him when he laughed — she could see it, the small surprise on his face, a man finding a door in a wall he'd thought was solid. Ines had learned to treasure each one.

* * *

Later, when Maren and Tobin had gone up to the loft and Sefa's snores had settled into their familiar rhythm — two long, one short, like a woman arguing in her sleep and winning — Orsen stayed by the fire, and Ines stayed with him, because she always did, and because the truth was she slept badly unless she could hear him breathing. Not for his sake. For hers. Some fears don't retire; they just take off their armor and sit quietly in the corner.

"Can I ask you something?" he said. "Something I've been turning over."

"Always."

"The night we met. The real meeting, in the guildhall. You remember it. I never will — that entry's been paid away, spent, burned in a vault. So it exists now only in you." He turned his invisible pen over in his fingers. "Doesn't that frighten you? Carrying the only copy?"

Ines considered the fire for a long moment.

"When I was in the mountain," she said, "they took pieces of me every week. And I survived by holding one memory like a coal in both hands — the guildhall, the desk, the scribe who lied in the ledger to save me. Every time the tithe came, I went back to that room. I memorized it harder. The scratch of your pen. The way you wouldn't meet my eyes until the moment you decided to lie, and then you looked right at me, and I understood that looking was the lie's twin brother." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "They couldn't take it, do you understand? Because I kept re-writing it. Every remembering made the ink fresher."

"And now you're doing that for me."

"Now we're doing that for each other," she said. "That's the whole trick, Orsen. Memory isn't a vault. It's a fire. It only stays lit if people keep feeding it. Alone, it goes out. Together—" she gestured at the ceiling, at the loft where the others slept, at the whole crooked household of hunted strays — "together it burns all winter."

He was quiet for a while. Snow ticked against the shuttered window. Somewhere below the floorboards, Tobin murmured in his sleep, counting doors even in dreams.

"Some faces have come back," Orsen said at last. "Did you notice? Your face — I dreamed it last week, before you'd described it to me that day. Just the eyes. Wolf-amber. And Maren's laugh — I heard it in a dream before I ever heard it awake, and then she laughed at breakfast and I nearly dropped my cup." His voice went strange, wondering. "It's like the stories are digging. Like somewhere under the ash there are coals, and the telling is bellows."

"Some," Ines agreed. "Some will come back. Some won't." She had learned not to flinch from this. "Your mother may stay gone. That price doesn't un-pay itself. But hear me—" she turned to face him fully, firelight catching the amber in her own eyes, the wolf looking out through the girl as it always did now, patient, permanent — "the ones who are here, in this room, feeding this fire every night? Those ones I refuse to lose twice. You gave up your memories of us. Fine. We're giving them back. Freely. Nightly. Whether you want them or not."

"That sounds threatening."

"It is threatening," Ines said, and grinned, and let a little of the wolf show in it, because he'd told her once — in a story, in a ledger-line she'd written herself into his new book of days — that he'd found her frightening at first, and she liked being a little frightening. It kept things honest.

* * *

Spring came late that year, the way spring does after a hard winter, all at once and apologetic.

On the first warm evening, they ate outside, and afterward Orsen brought out the new ledger — the one he'd begun keeping in the ashes of the old world, the one whose columns were not debts owed but stories told. He read from it sometimes now, haltingly, because reading aloud was part of the work, part of the bellows. Tonight he read the entries from midwinter:

*Maren Ovese, clerk of the Guild of Impossible Things. Burned the evidence that would have killed me. Has never once let me forget it. Laughs like a door coming unstuck.*

*Tobin, who hears doors before they open. Slept in shifts to guard strangers who became brothers. Afraid of deep water. Brave everywhere else.*

*Sefa, hedge-witch, keeper of wards and terrible opinions. Nearly died sealing a door behind us in the dark of the mountain. Would deny it if asked.*

*Ines.*

He stopped there, as he always stopped there, and Ines waited, as she always waited, heart doing its old wolf-quick patter.

"Ines," he read, "who was sold and refused to be sold. Who carried my name through a mountain that eats names. Who is teaching me who I was, one night at a time, and who I suspect will keep teaching me long after I've learned it, because she enjoys it."

"I do enjoy it," Ines confirmed.

"And here's tonight's entry," Orsen said, and dipped his pen — a real pen now, moving ink and all, his gift returned to him whole and unhidden, because a man who has paid everything for freedom ought to write with his whole hand. He wrote for a moment, then turned the ledger toward the firelight so she could see.

*Entry the hundred-and-somethingth. Told the story of the guildhall again. She says it's the fifth time. She says my chest does something when I repeat her lines back to her. She's right. It does. I am beginning to suspect that "remembering" was never the point — that the point was always this: the fire, the telling, the people who stay to hear it. I no longer remember choosing them. I choose them now. Perhaps that was the choice that mattered all along.*

Below it, in a different hand — quick, slanted, sharp-toothed — someone had already added a line. Ines's handwriting, entered while he slept sometime that week, because the ledger belonged to the family now and she took liberties:

*He's wrong about one thing. He does remember me. He remembers me every time he looks at me like that. The broker got the memory. He didn't get the wolf.*

Orsen read her addition. Read it twice. Then he did the thing that had become, over the long winter, his truest signature: he wrote *true* beneath it, in his moving ink, and the letters shimmered, and settled, and stayed.

Above the tollhouse, the first bats of spring stitched the dusk. Down the valley, in the ruins of Vellamarch, the guild's great seal was being pried off the guildhall door by citizens who no longer believed in impossible debts. Somewhere far off, a wolf sang — one clear note, answered by nothing, needing nothing, because the thing it had sung to all winter was sitting right beside the fire, holding the pen.

"One more story," Ines said, settling against his shoulder as the stars came out. "Tell me one this time. Your turn."

Orsen looked at the blank page. Looked at his family — Maren arguing cheerfully with Tobin about whose turn it was to haul water tomorrow, Sefa pretending to sleep and missing nothing. People he had bought with everything he was, and who had repaid him with everything they had, freely, nightly, refusing to stay forgotten.

"All right," he said, and set the pen to paper, and began.
