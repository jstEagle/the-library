## 1. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

Noor Pemberton had three rules, and she had carved them into the underside of her worktable where no one could read them but her, and only if she lay flat on the cold stone floor and craned her neck, which she did every morning, because rules you don't check are rules you've already broken.

One: count everything twice.

Two: trust no sum that comes out in your favor on the first pass.

Three: never, under any circumstances, do a favor for free.

She lay on the floor of her cellar that morning, breath fogging in the dark, and read them again by the light of a single tallow stub. The letters wobbled a little. Her hand had been less steady three years ago, when she'd carved them, the night after she'd climbed out of Maren's cottage window with a stolen primer under her coat and a debt of gratitude she'd decided she could no longer afford to pay.

Three years. Long enough for the guilt to wear smooth, like a river stone she kept in her pocket instead of her heart.

She got up, brushed the flour off her back — she slept in the cellar of her own bakery, because a bakery was the best disguise a smuggler ever invented, and because bread was the one honest thing she still made — and went upstairs to count.

The counting went like this: fourteen loaves proofing, of which six were ordinary and eight had hollowed centers. In the hollows: three vials of fever-tincture bound for Widow Cratch's granddaughters up in the terraces, two bundles of letters from a sailor's wife to a sailor's husband who was, technically, a deserter, and one small wrapped parcel of salted eel that Noor did not ask about because the man who'd paid for its passage had the eyes of a ferret and the manners of a worse one.

The arithmetic: the tincture paid her rent. The letters paid her conscience. The eel paid for the silence of Ferret-Eyes, which was its own kind of rent.

And the bread — ordinary bread, sold at honest prices to honest people who had no idea their baker could light a candle with a word if she were fool enough to say it — the bread paid for everything else, and everything else was the point.

Because Thornfell sat at the bottom of the king's high road like a knot at the end of a rope, and everything the crown wanted to strangle had to pass through the knot. Salt. Medicine. News. People, sometimes. And every wagon that came down from the passes was searched by men with white armbands and patient, terrible smiles, who were called witch-finders, and who were very good at finding exactly what they were paid to find and nothing else.

Noor had learned, from Maren, how to be nothing else.

* * *

The morning market in Thornfell smelled of wet wool, woodsmoke, and the particular desperation of fish that had traveled too far from the sea. Noor moved through it with her basket on her hip and her face arranged into the expression she thought of as *pleasantly forgettable* — the face of a woman who would help you find your lost goat and never once wonder why the goat was lost.

"Morning, Pemberton." Old Hesk at the cheese stall, who had a voice like gravel being polite. "Your loaves came out flat again Tuesday."

"My oven's temperamental."

"Everything in Thornfell's temperamental. That's what makes us honest." He wrapped her the rind of hard cheese she hadn't asked for and waved off her coin. "For the flat loaves. Consider it an apology on behalf of your oven."

Noor considered pointing out that this violated her third rule, then considered that refusing kindness was a kind of arithmetic too, and one that always came out lonelier. She took the cheese. "Thank you, Hesk."

"Tell your oven it's forgiven."

She was halfway down the fish row, calculating — Hesk's cheese meant one fewer purchase, which meant the coin she'd set aside could go toward the good lamp oil, which meant she could work later into the evenings, which meant the winter ledger might actually balance for the first time in — when the shouting started.

Not the ordinary shouting. Thornfell did ordinary shouting the way other towns did breathing. This was the other kind: the sudden, total, held-breath silence of a market deciding all at once to become very interested in its own shoes.

Witch-finders.

Three of them, white armbands bright against the grey morning, moving down the row with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no. The tall one in front carried a brass instrument Noor pretended not to recognize — a resonance-needle, Maren had called it, a thing that trembled near working magic the way a compass trembled near iron. Noor had never seen one up close. She kept her basket steady and her heart steady and her face pleasantly forgettable, and she walked, at exactly the speed of a woman with nothing to hide, toward the far end of the row.

The needle didn't so much as twitch. Three years of nothing, and it didn't twitch. She was a baker. She was a baker. She was —

At the end of the row, a woman was weeping quietly, and a witch-finder was patting her arm with the gentleness of a man comforting a lamb on its way to slaughter, saying, "We only want to help it, mistress. Whatever's been left at your door. We only want to help."

Noor's feet stopped moving without her permission.

*Left at your door.*

She did the arithmetic in the time it took to cross the square: the witch-finders were at the far end of town. Her cellar was at the near end. The needle had not twitched. There was no reason — no arithmetic reason at all — for the cold that slid down her spine like meltwater.

She walked home. She did not run. Running was a number that never came out in your favor.

* * *

Her door, when she reached it, was shut. That was the first strange thing, because Noor always latched it twice and the second latch hung open.

The second strange thing was the wards.

She'd laid them herself, hedge-wards, small and stubborn as burrs — Maren's design, Maren's patterns, though Noor had modified them over three years until they were hers, and better, and she'd never told Maren that either. They hummed under the threshold the way a kettle hums before it boils. Except now the kettle was boiling. Now the wards were screaming — not aloud, nothing a neighbor would hear, but Noor felt them through the soles of her boots, a high thin shriek of *wrong, wrong, wrong* that made her teeth ache and her eyes water.

Something on her doorstep was tearing them apart. Slowly. The way a child peels bark off a birch tree. Not attacking. *Unpicking.*

Noor stood in the lane, basket on her hip, and ran the numbers.

Option one: walk away. Go to Hesk's, drink someone else's tea, come back in an hour and see if the problem had solved itself or eaten through her door. Cost: her wards, her home, possibly her cover. Probability of the problem having solved itself: negligible. She was a smuggler. Nothing left unattended on a doorstep in Thornfell ever solved itself.

Option two: fetch the watch. Cost: explaining what was on her doorstep. Probability of the watch being useful: she'd have better luck asking the goat.

Option three: open the door.

She set down the basket. She told herself — in the firm, reasonable voice she used for clients who wanted to haggle — that this was not a favor for free, that her wards were an investment, that she was protecting her assets.

She unlatched the door.

The child was small. That was the first thing. Small and half-frozen, curled against her doorframe like a bundle someone had set down and forgotten, wearing a coat that had been fine clothing once, a hundred years ago, for someone much larger. Frost in pale hair. Lips the blue of deep ice. And awake — that was the worst part, the eyes open and calm and fixed on Noor with an attention that had nothing childish in it at all.

"You'll die there," Noor heard herself say. Which was, she reflected, an odd thing to say to what was very possibly a trap, a spy, a witch-finder's decoy, a curse with legs. "Inside. You'll die out here."

The child's mouth moved. What came out was not any language Noor knew, and Noor knew — or had once known, before the window and the primer and the running — a fair number she wasn't supposed to. This was older. This was the sound a language makes before it has finished becoming one: syllables like stones settling in a riverbed, vowels that seemed to arrive from a long way off, from under the ground, from under the *winter*.

And her wards — her good, stubborn, Maren-built wards — screamed.

Not the tearing shriek of before. This was different. This was the sound of the wards *recognizing* something, the way a dog recognizes a wolf, and Noor felt every hair on her arms stand up as the child spoke again, still calm, still freezing, and one word in the stream of it rang against her memory like a coin dropped on stone.

She knew that word. She had read it in a stolen primer, in a margin, in Maren's own cramped hand, with a note beside it that said only: *never say this aloud.*

The word meant *crown*.

Noor stood in her own doorway with the cold pouring past her and the arithmetic — three years of careful, life-saving arithmetic — lying in pieces at her feet, because there was no column for this. No route. No price. No number.

The child looked up at her and, in that old, old tongue, said something short and simple, and though Noor could not translate it, she understood it anyway, the way you understand a wound.

*Help.*

"No," Noor said, to the child, to the wards, to the morning, to herself. "No, absolutely not. I don't do favors for free."

But she was already reaching down, and her hands — traitorous, uncalculated things — were already lifting the child out of the snow.
