## 2. A Tongue Older Than Stone

The child weighed about as much as a sack of turnips and had roughly the same opinion of being carried.

Noor got them inside — over the threshold, which made her wards shriek like a kettle with opinions, and then settle into a grudging, muttering acceptance, the way a cat accepts a new kitten it intends to resent forever — and set them down by the hearth in the back room where she did her actual living, as opposed to the front room where she did her actual lying.

"Right," she said, rubbing her hands together. "Rules. Rule one: you don't touch anything. Rule two: you don't touch anything. Rule three—"

The child looked up at her. Snowmelt had gotten into their hair and turned it to dark spikes; their lips were still blue-tinged at the edges; their eyes were a gray so pale it was nearly silver, and they regarded Noor with the calm of someone who had watched empires fall and found it mildly educational.

"—rule three is you eat something," Noor finished, defeated. "Gods' teeth. Fine."

She put barley and salt pork on to boil, because barley forgives everything, and while it cooked she took inventory of her guest the way she took inventory of contraband: quickly, thoroughly, without sentiment.

Small. Maybe eight years old, maybe ten; underfed children lie about their age the way smugglers lie about their cargo. A coat that had been good once, generations ago, stitched with patterns Noor's eye kept snagging on — not decoration, she decided, *notation*. Boots two sizes too big. No pack, no knife, no token, no coin. Nothing that said *where I came from* and nothing that said *who sent me*, which in Thornfell was itself a kind of answer. Nobody arrived at Noor Pemberton's door by accident. The warrens didn't permit accidents. You came to her because someone told you how, and someone only told you how if they wanted something moved.

So who wanted this child moved? And from what?

And why, when the child opened their mouth and spoke — that same short, simple phrase again, pointing at the fire, then at Noor, then pressing one small hand flat against their own chest — did every ward-thread in the house pull tight as harp strings?

*Help,* Noor's bones translated again. She wished they'd stop doing that. Understanding without translation was hedge-magic of a low and uncomfortable order, the sort Maren had called "listening with your skin" and forbidden on the grounds that skin heard what it wanted to hear.

"Don't help me," Noor said. "That's the trick, isn't it. Little lost thing at the door, and suddenly I'm spending my stores, my fuel, my risk-margin. That's how they catch people like me. Not with chains. With kindness we can't do the arithmetic for."

The child considered this speech with grave attention, then reached out and tugged Noor's sleeve, and pointed at the pot, and said something else in that old tongue — longer this time, several syllables, rolling and formal, like a sentence read aloud from a very serious book.

It made the fire lean sideways.

Not much. An inch, maybe, all the flames tilting toward the child like sunflowers toward noon, before snapping back to honest combustion. Noor stood very still with the ladle in her hand and did not scream, mostly because screaming would have required breath and she'd misplaced hers.

"What," she said carefully, "was that."

The child shrugged. It was an eloquent shrug. It said, *I say things and sometimes the world listens, this has always been true, I assumed everyone's did that.*

Noor served the stew.

* * *

She owned exactly one book she shouldn't own, and it lived inside a false bottom beneath her floorboards along with three ledgers, a lockpick set, and a bottle of plum brandy she was saving for an occasion she secretly hoped would never come. Maren's primer. Stolen — no. *Taken.* Taken the night she'd walked out of Maren's cottage for the last time, seven years ago, after the argument that had ended with Maren saying, in that terrible quiet voice, *"You think rules are cages, girl. Rules are load-bearing walls."*

Well. Noor had been living happily in her rubble ever since, thank you very much.

She dug the primer out anyway, sat across the table from the child, and opened it to the first pages, where Maren's cramped handwriting catalogued the pre-kingdom tongue — the Old Speech, the Stone-Tongue, the language the first kings had supposedly spoken to the land itself, back when the land answered. Maren had taught her the alphabet as an exercise, years ago, the way you'd make a child memorize dead kings' names: useless, but it built discipline.

Useless. Right.

Noor wrote the child's phrase down phonetically, matched it against the letter-shapes, and began the slow work of cross-referencing. The child watched her do it, chin propped on small hands, occasionally offering corrections — a tap on the page when Noor transcribed a vowel wrong, a patient repetition when she asked them to say it again slower.

By lamplight, three hours later, Noor had a fragment. Not a whole sentence. A piece of one, assembled from half-remembered lessons and Maren's marginalia, and when the pieces finally clicked together in her head she read it aloud in common-tongue just to hear how ordinary it sounded:

*"Let the door be shut, and let the shutter be sealed, and let the key be given unto the crown, that the cold may sleep and not wake."*

Binding-liturgy.

She knew it was binding-liturgy the way she'd known *help* — skin-knowledge, unwelcome knowledge — but she also knew it in the ordinary way, the way any hedge-witch raised anywhere near Ironhollow knew it, because every child in the valley grew up on the story. King Aldric the First, the Winter-Crowned, who had bound the god Vaskr — Vaskr of the Long White, the Hunger Under the Snow — and caged him in his own iron circlet rather than let him devour the world. Every midwinter festival reenacted it. Children wore paper crowns and shouted *shut the door, seal the shutter* until their parents begged them to stop.

Nobody had spoken the actual liturgy aloud in five hundred years. The language had died with the binding. Scholars argued about whether anyone alive could even pronounce it correctly.

A mute child had just recited it over Noor's stew pot like a grace before dinner.

"No," Noor said to the room. "Nope. Whatever this is, it's not mine. I run cloth and brandy and the occasional fugitive tax clerk. I don't do gods. Gods are bad margins. Gods are *terrible* margins."

The child finished their stew, licked the bowl — Noor decided not to have feelings about that — and then looked at her and said another phrase, slow and deliberate, clearly chosen for her benefit, clearly wanting her to write it down.

She wrote it down. She hated herself for writing it down. She translated it anyway, because apparently she had no more self-preservation than a moth.

*"I am the word that opens, and I am the word that seals."*

The fire leaned sideways again. This time it stayed leaning, a full handspan, all evening, like a crowd craning to see.

Noor slept — badly, in a chair, with a knife in her lap — and dreamed of doors.

* * *

Two days later, the Hollis farm burned.

Everyone in the warrens saw the smoke; there was no missing it, a greasy black column standing up off the eastern fields against a white sky. Noor went up onto her roof under pretense of checking the chimney flue, because roofs were where you learned things, and what she learned was this: it wasn't soldiers. Soldiers burned fast and left. These burned slow. There were six riders in ash-gray cloaks, and they stood around the Hollis homestead in a loose ring while it went up, unhurried, and one of them held something long and thin that caught the light — an iron rod, tipped with a sigil Noor could see even at this distance because it *glowed*, a cold blue-white like frost catching sun.

Witch-finders. The king's new breed, the ones the taverns whispered about. Not the old corruptible constables who took bribes and looked elsewhere; these were true believers, and true believers couldn't be paid, only survived.

They worked the neighborhood for the rest of the day. Noor watched them ride to the Tarrow place, then the mill, then the ferryman's hut, and at each stop they dismounted and talked and held up the glowing rod, and the rod swung like a compass needle, and twice it swung toward the warrens' mouth before swinging away again.

Toward her. Almost toward her.

Down in the kitchen, the child sat on the floor playing with a wooden button, arranging it among pebbles in patterns that hurt Noor's eyes to look at too long — spirals within spirals, like frost on glass — and humming. The hum was the liturgy. Of course it was. The child hummed binding-spells the way other children hummed drinking songs.

When Noor climbed back inside, cheeks raw with cold, the child looked up at her with those silver-gray eyes and said, quite clearly, in the old tongue:

She caught enough of it now. Her ear was learning, the way an ear learns a song. *The seekers burn the field to find the seed.*

"Yes," Noor said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Yes, they do. That's exactly what they do."

The child tilted their head. Said something else, softer.

*Am I the seed?*

And there it was. Noor crouched down so they were eye to eye, and the arithmetic tried to reassert itself — *one child, high risk, zero return, cut losses, move her along, move yourself along* — and failed, failed utterly, because the child was looking at her with a question on their face that no child should ever have to ask, and because Noor had spent three years calculating her way through the world and had never once calculated her way out of being lonely.

"You're not a seed," Noor said. "Seeds get planted. You're—" She stopped. Started again. "You need a name, actually. Can't keep calling you 'the child,' makes you sound like a debt in a ledger. Where I grew up, ash meant the tree that bends in a storm instead of breaking. Also the stuff that's left after a fire, which, given current circumstances, seems apt either way." She exhaled. "Ash. How's that?"

The child repeated it — *Ash* — tasting the shape of it, the first word they'd worn that belonged to this country and this century. Something in their face eased, some tension Noor hadn't known was there.

Then, outside, faint but carrying across the frozen fields, came the sound of hooves. Many. Moving toward the warrens.

And Ash went rigid, head snapping toward the door, and spoke one word of the old tongue that Noor didn't need Maren's primer for at all, because the wards screamed it with them:

*Coming.*
