## 3. What the Crown Remembers

The candle had burned down to a stub the width of a thumb, and King Aldric Vane of Ironhollow could not remember lighting it.

That was the first entry in his journal that night. He wrote it carefully, in a hand he kept deliberately small — small enough to hide between the lines of grain accounts and border tariffs if anyone ever found the book. *I did not light this candle. I have been sitting here for some time — how long? — and I do not remember deciding to sit down.*

He turned the page and checked the date against the previous entry. Three days lost. Or two; the ink smudge made it hard to be certain. He wrote *three?* and underlined it twice, then sat back and looked at his own reflection in the dark window of the royal study.

The man looking back at him was sixty-one years old, gray at the temples, with laugh lines around his eyes that had been earned honestly over forty years of marriage to a woman who had been dead nine of them. A good face. A kind face, people said. It was important to Aldric that the face still looked like his.

Because sometimes lately, when he caught his reflection unexpectedly — in a polished breastplate, in the black ice of the fountain court — the face wore an expression he had never learned. Something patient. Something old. Something that looked at him the way a landlord looks at a tenant whose lease is nearly up.

"You're doing it again," Aldric told the reflection quietly. "Staring."

The reflection stared.

"Rude," he added, because humor was one of the things he had decided were his. He kept a list. It lived in the journal, on the first page, in letters pressed so hard into the paper they'd survive fire: *Things That Are Mine.* Dry humor. Love of pickled herring (Vaskr, whatever else it was, had no opinion on herring whatsoever, which Aldric found deeply reassuring). Grief for Elenna. The habit of tapping his ring twice before making a decision. His name.

*My name,* he wrote now, at the top of tonight's page, as he did every night. *Aldric. Not the other one. The other one has no name I can pronounce, which I take as a small mercy.*

Then, beneath it: *Inventory of the day.*

*Morning: Woke in my own bed. Good sign. There was frost on the inside of the windows despite the fire having been lit, and the fire was green. Green flame is not mine. I have asked Master Corvel about green flame three times now and each time he goes pale and says "the hearths are old, Majesty," which is either true or he is more frightened of something than of me, and I would very much like to know which.*

*Midday: Council session. I proposed the granary tithes for the northern villages — my own thought, I am fairly sure, though I noticed my hand sketching a spiral in the margin while Lord Hesse spoke, and I do not draw spirals. I never have. I am a squares-and-columns man. Elenna used to say I planned our garden like a tax ledger.*

*Evening: Lost time. Found myself standing in the reliquary corridor at full dark, holding the crown case, my forehead against the glass. I do not know how long. My feet are cold. I put the case down. I locked the corridor behind me and gave the key to Captain Brann, and told him if he saw me there again he was to knock me unconscious without ceremony and apologize at breakfast.*

He paused, quill hovering. Then, smaller:

*I am not sure the locking helps. I think the crown does not need doors anymore. I think it is learning to use me as one.*

* *

The trouble with being slowly eaten by a god, Aldric had decided, was the bookkeeping.

He had been a meticulous king. Twenty-two years on the throne, and he knew where every coin in the treasury slept and which of his lords owed what to whom. So when the lapses began — first hours, then half-days — he had done what any sensible administrator would do with an unmanageable liability: he had started a ledger.

The journal was now in its third volume. The first two were hidden in the false bottom of Elenna's old writing desk, beneath a layer of her unfinished embroidery that no servant would ever dare disturb. In them, in increasingly cramped script, was the map of a siege conducted entirely within one man's skull.

Some entries were almost funny. He allowed himself to find them almost funny, because the alternative was to stop writing altogether.

*Day 41:* *Today I insulted the ambassador from Serevan by greeting him in a language no one present recognized. It was not any tongue I have studied. The ambassador's interpreter went white as whey and left the room. I have since learned from the palace archivist that the words I spoke resemble fragments found on pre-kingdom boundary stones — the ones from before the Hollow Kings, from the age they do not teach children. I apologized to the ambassador with a gift of Serevani wine, which he accepted, and privately resolved to stop speaking in my sleep with the window open, as the night guard can apparently hear me.*

*Day 58:* *Dreamed of snow falling upward. Woke weeping and did not know for whom. Checked: grief for Elenna is mine. This weeping was not shaped like hers. Hers has edges. This was vast and smooth, like mourning the size of a country. Filed accordingly.*

*Day 63:* *I caught myself thinking, quite calmly, that winter is not a season but a debt, and that debts are collected. I want it noted — noted here, at least — that I do not believe this. Winter is a season. It is cold and it ends and there is herring.*

But other entries were not funny at all, and those he read aloud to himself sometimes, in the smallest voice he owned, just to hear a human sound in the room.

*Day 70:* *I no longer know whether my love for my people is mine or bait. It occurs to me that a thing wearing me would want me to love them — would want me gentle, trusted, permitted everywhere. How does a man audit his own heart? I built the orphanage on Copperlane. I remember choosing the site, arguing with the masons about the drainage. But I also remember wanting, last week, with a calm and total certainty that frightens me more than rage would, for everyone in Ironhollow to come home, all of them, every door closed, every fire lit, everyone safe and still and gathered in — the way a shepherd gathers sheep before shearing.*

*I do not know which memory is the trap. Perhaps both. Perhaps love is simply the shape the thing finds most convenient to wear, because I handed it that shape freely, years ago, and never asked for a receipt.*

He had shown the journal to exactly one person. His physician, old Master Quill, had read three pages, gone the color of porridge, and said, "Majesty, with respect, I treat gout."

"I'm not asking you to treat it," Aldric had said. "I'm asking you to witness it. If I wake up one morning and don't recognize my own handwriting, someone must know which thoughts were mine." He had smiled, and been surprised at how easily the smile came. "Consider it a will. Most men leave their boots to their nephews. I leave my self to posterity."

Quill had not laughed. Aldric had decided that meant the joke was still his, even if no one else appreciated it.

* *

Spymaster Dain Vess arrived after midnight, the way he always did, smelling faintly of river water and other people's secrets. He was a narrow man with the face of a disappointed schoolmaster, which he cultivated deliberately, and he had served Aldric for nineteen years without once asking a question he didn't already suspect the answer to.

"Your Majesty." He bowed precisely. "You sent for me."

"I did." Aldric gestured him to the chair across the desk, then — after a moment's consideration — rose and barred the study door himself. Dain's eyebrows climbed a fraction of an inch. Nineteen years, and the king had never barred a door against his own household.

"Dain," Aldric said, sitting, folding his hands. "How would you investigate a rumor that didn't exist?"

Dain considered this the way he considered everything: as though it might be trying to cheat him. "I'd start by asking who benefits from its nonexistence."

"Good. Here is your rumor." Aldric slid a scrap of paper across the desk. On it, in his own careful hand, he had written: *a child, north of Thornfell, who speaks the dead tongue.* Below it: *find out if this is real. If it is real, find the child. If you find the child, tell no one but me — and bring word to me in daylight, in my own study, and check my eyes first.*

"Check your eyes," Dain repeated.

"There will be a code phrase. If I give it correctly, proceed. If I give it wrong—" Aldric held his gaze steadily. "If I get it wrong, Dain, I want you to walk out of this room, forget you ever heard the word 'child,' and lose my message somewhere the river keeps things."

For a long moment the spymaster said nothing. Then, quietly, in a voice stripped of all its schoolmaster dryness: "Majesty. What is happening to you?"

And there it was — the question Aldric had spent seventy days not answering aloud. He found, to his surprise, that he wanted to answer it. That rationing himself included, apparently, rationing the truth, and that he was tired of eating alone.

"Something old is wearing my crown," he said. "It may be wearing me. I have reason to believe it came with the crown — or perhaps the crown is it, and we've all been bowing to the wrong thing for centuries. I have reason to believe it is waking further, and that something north of Thornfell has disturbed its sleep." He tapped the scrap of paper. "When I stood at the crown case last night, pressing my forehead to the glass like a beggar at a bakery window, I felt — *itching*, Dain. In the metal. Like a dog hearing a whistle only it can hear. Something in the north is a key, or a threat, or a child, and the crown wants it found. Which means I want it found first, because whatever the crown wants, I have recently learned to want the opposite on principle."

"The opposite," Dain said. "On principle."

"It's the only policy I can still guarantee is mine."

That got the ghost of a smile from the spymaster — quickly suppressed, but real. "And if the child is a weapon, Majesty? If finding it hands your enemy its own key?"

"Then I will hold it gently and decide slowly," Aldric said. "I've been a king for twenty-two years, Dain. I have signed death warrants. I have started one war and ended another. Whatever is left of me when this is finished — I would like it to be able to say it met a child as a man, not as a god wearing a man's hands." He looked at the candle stub, guttering now. "Besides. If the crown itches toward something, that something may be the only splinter in the world sharp enough to scratch back."

Dain rose, took the paper, and burned it in the candle flame without being asked, letting the ash fall into the dish. "Code phrase?" he said.

"*The herring is excellent this year,*" said Aldric. "*Though the winter is long.*"

"Charming," said Dain. "No one would invent it. No one sane would repeat it." He bowed — deeper than usual, Aldric noticed — and let himself out through the servants' stair.

* *

Alone again, Aldric opened the journal and wrote the last entry of the night:

*Day 71. Sent Dain north. Hands steady. Thoughts — as far as I can certify — my own.*

*One more thing, for whoever reads this, if anyone ever does. Tonight, walking back from the study door, I passed the long mirror in the west gallery, and the man in it raised his hand to me — not in mockery, not in menace. In salute. Like a soldier acknowledging relief watch. And I understood, with terrible clarity, that he was not threatening me at all.*

*He was thanking me. For holding the door so long. For keeping the post.*

*I saluted him back. It seemed polite. And because I am afraid that courtesy, too, might someday stop being mine, I am writing it down:*

*Whatever wears the crown next — remember that the man who kept it warm loved pickled herring, drew squares instead of spirals, and loved his wife Elenna with a grief that had edges. If you can find those things in yourself, be gentle with what remains of him.*

*If you cannot find them — then God help us all, and mind the frost on the inside of the windows.*

He blotted the page, hid the journal beneath the loose stone behind the woodbox, and went to bed.

In the morning, the frost on the inside of the windows spelled out a single word in the dead tongue, and Master Corvel, bringing the king's breakfast, went pale and dropped the tray.

By noon, a rider was on the north road to Thornfell, carrying the spymaster's seal — and by dusk, in a smuggler's warren beneath the town, a half-frozen child woke at Noor Pemberton's fire and spoke again in their sleep, and every ward on her door screamed at once.
