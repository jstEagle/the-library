## 4. The Rebel's Kind Offer

The mountain road, Noor had calculated, would take six days. It took four to reach the tree line and then stopped cooperating entirely, because mountains, unlike smugglers, did not care about arithmetic.

She had run the numbers a hundred times since Thornfell. Two mouths to feed from one satchel meant half-rations by day three, which she'd disguised as "variety." Ash ate what was offered without complaint and without expression, which Noor found either admirable or deeply unsettling depending on how hungry *she* was. The child walked when walking was required, slept curled like a comma against whatever stone was warmest, and spoke only in that old tongue — soft syllables that made the air feel older, like the words had been waiting under the snow for centuries and Ash was simply letting them out for air.

Noor had named them Ash partly because of the grey eyes, partly because naming things made them hers to protect, and mostly because Maren had always said an unnamed thing belonged to no one, and Noor had decided, somewhere between Thornfell and the first ridge, that this child belonged with her.

Not *to* her. With her. There was a difference, and it mattered, and she clung to it the way she clung to the rope-line over the scree: grimly, and with both hands.

"Nearly there," she said, though they were not nearly anywhere. Lying to children felt less like a sin if you called it encouragement. "There's a shrine past the col. Old watch-post. Dry roof, maybe even a hearth some pilgrim left stacked."

Ash looked at her. Said something in the dead tongue. The cadence was gentle. It might have been *I believe you* or it might have been *you are lost*, and Noor chose to hear the first one, because self-deception was also a kind of rationing.

* * *

They came down into the col as the light went copper, and that was where the arithmetic failed her completely.

There were people at the watch-shrine. A dozen, maybe more, in dun-colored cloaks with no livery, which on a mountain road meant exactly one thing: rebels. They had horses picketed behind the shrine and a cookfire going, and the smell of actual stew hit Noor like a blow to the knees. Her stomach made a sound that undermined every ounce of dignity she had left.

And at the center of them, warming her hands at the fire as though she'd been expecting company all day, stood a broad-shouldered woman with a scarred jaw and the easy posture of someone who had never once been ambushed in her life, because ambushes were things that happened to other people.

"Noor Pemberton," the woman said warmly. "Or should I say the Widow Quill? Or the tinker Vess? I've got four names for you. I collect them."

Noor's hand found Ash's shoulder and stayed there. "Then you've wasted good money. I'm nobody worth four names."

"Nobody walks the high passes in early winter with a witch-finder patrol two days behind them." The woman rose. Up close she was younger than her voice — thirty, perhaps, with laugh lines that hadn't quite finished lying about her age. "Sefa Corvane. And before you run — and I can see you're doing the math on running, don't insult us both — consider that my people found your patrol this morning and turned them around. You're welcome, incidentally."

"You turned them around."

"Convincingly." Sefa smiled. "Sit. Eat. I promise the stew is better than my manners."

It was, Noor reflected later, a masterfully built trap, if trap it was, because every single element of it was true. The stew *was* better than her manners. The patrol *had* been turned around — Noor could see the witch-finders' signal smoke drifting wrong, back down-valley. And Sefa Corvane talked exactly like a person with nothing to hide, which was itself the most alarming thing about her.

Ash sat when Noor sat. The child accepted a bowl and held it without eating, watching the fire with those grey eyes, and Noor noticed — filed away, calculated — that none of the rebels touched Ash. Not casually, not accidentally. They gave the child a small, unconscious berth, the way people give berth to a sleeping dog of unknown temperament.

"So," Sefa said, settling across the fire. "You have something the Crown wants badly enough to burn homesteads for. Something the witch-finders call a vessel." She said the word carefully, tasting it, disliking it. "And I have something you want. Passage over the pass before the snows close it, food, and people who shoot back when the king's men come asking questions."

"And in exchange?"

"The child comes to our loremaster. That's all. No cages, no rituals, no knives. Master Ellery just wants to *talk*. Understand what the little one is. There are people who'd say the binding on the old god is the root of everything wrong with this kingdom — the cold, the crown, the whole rotten inheritance. If the key to unbinding it is sitting in your satchel, Noor, keeping it hidden isn't protecting anyone. It's just hoarding."

It was a good speech. Noor had heard good speeches before; Maren had given them, right before explaining why Noor's instincts were wrong and Maren's rules were right. The difference, she told herself, was that Maren had usually been correct.

"I don't make decisions for Ash," Noor said. "But I decide what happens to Ash. Those aren't contradictory."

"They're exhausting is what they are." Sefa laughed, and it was a real laugh, and Noor hated how much she wanted to trust it. "Look. I know what you're doing. You're counting exits. You've counted three — the scree slope, the streambed, and the blind rise behind me. Am I close?"

"You forgot the fourth."

"There is no fourth."

"That's why it works," Noor said, and Sefa barked another laugh, and for one warm, stupid moment Noor thought: *this could be simple. This could be the easy road.* Hot food. Armed friends. Someone else standing between Ash and the world for a while.

She was so tired. That was the whole truth of it. She was tired down to the marrow, and kindness offered by strangers on a mountain is the most dangerous substance in the world, because it goes straight into the bloodstream.

"All right," Sefa said, softer now. "Don't answer tonight. Sleep. Eat. Decide at dawn. That's fair terms, isn't it? Fairer than the king gives anyone."

It was. That was the terrible part. It was fairer than anything Noor had been offered since she'd run from Maren's cottage with a stolen primer and a head full of forbidden liturgy.

* * *

She woke near midnight to the sound of someone being dragged.

Not killed — dragged. A low, muffled argument, boots on stone, coming from the far side of the shrine where the horses were picketed. Noor slid out from under her blanket, checked that Ash still breathed beside her, and moved along the wall the way Maren had taught her: heel first, weight low, breath through the nose.

Two rebels had a third man by the arms. He wasn't fighting anymore. In the firelight spilling around the corner of the shrine, Noor saw why.

He was branded. Both forearms, the sleeves cut away — sigils burned into the skin, old scars gone shiny with years. And Noor knew them. Knew them the way you know a face from a nightmare. Binding-sigils. The same ones inked in Maren's stolen primer, in the chapter Maren had made her memorize and then made her swear never to use, in the margins of which Maren herself had written, in that cramped furious hand:

*These marks are hunger dressed as righteousness. Any hand that brands them into living flesh has already begun eating what it claims to save.*

The prisoner saw her looking. His eyes were clear and terribly calm, the calm of a man who had stopped expecting rescue.

"You're the one with the child," he whispered. "Run. Whatever she promised you — the loremaster doesn't talk to vessels. He *opens* them. I've seen—"

One of the guards cuffed him hard across the mouth. The other looked up, met Noor's eyes, and there was a long, honest moment in which both of them understood that this changed everything, and then the guard said, quietly, "You were sleepwalking. Go back to your bed."

Noor went back to her bed. She lay down beside Ash. She stared at the underside of the shrine roof and ran the numbers again, honestly this time, the way Maren would have demanded: *safe passage, hot food, armed escort — weighed against branded prisoners and a loremaster who opens what he cannot close.*

Sefa Corvane might be sincere. Sefa Corvane probably *was* sincere, in the way of people who burn a village to warm their hands and grieve sincerely at the funeral. Hunger dressed as righteousness. Maren had never once been wrong about that chapter.

Beside her, Ash stirred. Grey eyes opened in the dark, already awake, already watching her — and Noor realized with a chill that the child had heard everything, and understood more than any child should, and had simply waited, silent, to see what she would do.

"What?" Noor whispered.

Ash said one word in the dead tongue. Low. Almost pitying. Noor didn't need the primer to translate it; some meanings arrive without translation, cold as well-water.

*Liar.*

"Yes," Noor agreed softly. "She is. So am I, sometimes. But I'm going to be a liar who keeps you alive, and apparently that's the best offer on this mountain."

* * *

They left an hour before dawn, taking the fourth exit — the one that didn't exist, the blind rise behind the shrine, which existed after all because Noor had spent the evening, while everyone watched the fire, cutting footholds into the frozen scree with her knife and a great deal of profanity.

Behind them, as the sky went grey, she heard the camp wake. Heard Sefa Corvane's voice carry across the col, not angry — worse than angry. Disappointed. Warm as ever, pitched to carry:

"Noor! The offer stands! The mountain won't be kinder than I am!"

Noor didn't look back. Looking back cost time, and time was the only currency she had left, and she was nearly broke.

Ahead, the high passes climbed white and empty into the dark, toward the abandoned watch-shrines, the starving weeks, the ford where — though she couldn't know it yet — the witch-finders would be waiting.

Ash's small hand found hers. Noor squeezed it once, and kept climbing.
