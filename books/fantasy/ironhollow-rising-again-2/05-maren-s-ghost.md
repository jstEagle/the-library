## 5. Maren's Ghost

The watch-shrine had been abandoned so long that even the rats had filed a formal complaint and moved out. Noor said as much to Ash, who watched her with the grave, unblinking patience of a child who had never once laughed at her jokes but seemed to be keeping a ledger of them for later.

"Rats," she explained, prying open a shutter crusted shut with two centuries of weather. "You know. Big opinions, small morals. You'd have gotten along."

Ash did not answer. Ash never answered — not in any tongue Noor could speak. They sat on the shrine's broken altar step, knees drawn up, watching her work with those pale eyes that made the wards in her bones itch.

Three days out of Thornfell. Two days since Sefa Corvane's offer, since Noor had run from it into the high passes like a fool with frostbite in both feet. One day since the last of the bread. The snow outside had stopped being weather and become a fact of the world, permanent as stone, and their little shelter — a waystation built by soldiers of some kingdom that no longer remembered why it had needed one — was down to its last virtues: a roof, a hearth, and a dead god's name carved over the door that no one had bothered to chisel off.

Noor counted what they had, because counting was what she did when the world went soft at the edges. Half a heel of bread gone. A handful of dried plums, which she divided with the arithmetic of a woman defusing a bomb: three for Ash, two for herself, one held in reserve against despair. Water from the melt-drip at the eaves. Her knife. Her primer. And magic — which was the problem, because magic cost, and she was already spending from a purse that had holes in it.

Her fingers trembled. Not from cold. Well — from cold too. But mostly from the thing she'd been circling for three days the way a tongue circles a cracked tooth.

She couldn't understand Ash. And Ash couldn't understand her. And somewhere behind them, witch-finders were reading the mountain roads like a cook reads a recipe, and ahead of them the rebels wanted the child, and the child spoke only in the language of bindings, and Noor was going to have to make decisions soon that would decide whether they both lived through the winter — and she could not ask the child a single question.

There was a working for that. Of course there was a working for that. Maren had forbidden it before Noor had even learned enough to want it — *the speaking-through*, written in the back third of the primer in Maren's own cramped hand, with three lines struck through it and a note in the margin: *This is how hedge-witches die, girl. Not dramatically. Just smaller each time.*

"You're going to tell me not to," Noor said to Ash. "I can tell. You've got a whole sermon loaded up in there."

Ash tilted their head. Then, slowly, deliberately, they reached out and touched the primer where it lay on the altar step, and looked at her, and shook their head once.

"Noor," Noor translated grimly, "says don't." She sighed. "Well. Good news. I've spent my whole life being told not to do things by people who loved me, and I'm still here, so clearly the system works."

* * *

From the child's side of the silence, the world was a liturgy that had lost its congregation.

*We are the vessel and the vessel is patient. We are the door and the door does not knock. We wait in the white country where the words go when they are finished being spoken, and we count the heartbeats of the woman-who-counts, because counting is a kind of prayer and hers is the only one left in us.*

*She is hungry. We know hunger the way stones know rain. She gives us three plums and keeps two, and then, when she thinks we sleep, she puts one of her two in our shoe, as if shoes were a place plums go, as if we would not notice. This is the strangest prayer of all. We hold it carefully.*

*Now she draws the circle. Now she opens the book with the dead woman's hand all through it. Now she says the words wrong, the way the living always say them, and the room begins to listen.*

*We should stop her. We do not know how to say so.*

* * *

It went wrong immediately, which Noor felt was traditional.

The speaking-through required a death — not a person's, thank every merciful power, but something that had been alive recently enough to remember warmth. She had a plum skin and a moth she'd found frozen in the windowsill, and she felt genuinely bad about the moth, and told it so, and dropped it into the brazier ash anyway.

Then she spoke the opening line, and the fire went green, and the shrine went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when someone important has just walked in.

"Maren?" Her voice came out small. She hated that. "Maren, you stubborn old — "

The smoke stood up.

Not drifted. *Stood.* It gathered itself into a shape Noor knew better than her own hands — broad shoulders, a shawl that had been gray since before Noor was born, hands folded the way Maren folded them when she was about to deliver a verdict.

"Oh, you *idiot girl,*" said the ghost of Maren Voss, in the exact tone of a woman who had crossed death itself primarily to say so. "Of all the workings in that book. Of all the — is that a *moth?* You killed a moth for this?"

"It was already frozen!"

"That is not the moral defense you think it is."

Noor laughed, and it came out wet, and she didn't care. Three years. Three years since she'd slammed Maren's door, since the argument about rules and cages and whose life magic belonged to, since she'd walked out of the only home she had because Maren loved her in the shape of walls. "You're really here?"

"I'm an echo. A smear on the working. Don't get sentimental, it wastes the candle." The smoke-Maren looked around the ruined shrine with an expression of profound professional disappointment. "A watch-shrine. You're hiding in a watch-shrine with *that* child, and you opened the speaking-through, which means you've been carrying my primer into danger, which means everything I feared about your education has come true."

"She can't understand me," Noor said. "Maren, she speaks the old tongue. Binding-liturgy. I found her half-dead on my doorstep and the witch-finders burned a homestead asking after her and I don't — I don't know what she *is.*"

Maren was quiet for a long moment. When the echo spoke again, the scolding had gone out of her voice, and what remained was worse.

"You know what she is. You've known since the ford of it first hit you. You just haven't let yourself finish the sentence." The smoke turned, or seemed to turn, toward where Ash sat watching with those pale eyes. "Say it, Noor."

"A vessel." Noor's throat worked. "The witch-finders called her a vessel. And the words she speaks are binding-words, and there's only one binding anyone writes liturgy for anymore, and — " She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Vaskr. She's tied to Vaskr."

"Tied to him?" Maren's laugh had no humor in it at all. "Child. Who do you think bound him?"

The fire crackled. Somewhere above, snow slid off the roof with a soft sigh.

"The binding took nine lives to weave," Maren said. "Nine witches, working in secret while Aldric's grandfather wore the crown and pretended his king slept easy. Nine women who fed their own bloodlines into the lock so it would hold past their deaths. My grandmother's grandmother was the seventh. I am the ninth. And the bloodline runs thin now, Noor — thin as winter milk — until it runs in exactly one small person sitting on a broken altar, speaking the lock's own language because the lock grew into her people's bones generations ago."

Noor sat down. It wasn't a decision; her legs simply resigned.

"Ash is the key," she whispered.

"Ash is the *last* of the key-makers. Which is worse, and better, and the reason every power in this kingdom is climbing a mountain in winter after a child." Maren's smoke-shape flickered, guttering. "Listen to me, because I don't know how long echoes last and I wasted the first minute insulting your housekeeping. The rebels will tell you the binding must break. The crown will tell you it must hold. Both of them are right, and both of them are lying, because the truth is that the binding is *dying either way* — and what happens when it goes depends entirely on whether it's opened with consent or torn open with force."

"Consent," Noor repeated. "Whose?"

Maren smiled, and it was the smile Noor had spent three years trying to stop missing — tired, wry, unbearably fond.

"Now you're doing arithmetic," she said. "That's my girl."

* * *

*The dead woman sees us.*

*This is not frightening. Nothing has frightened us since the white country, where fear was used up long ago making the lock. But the dead woman looks at us the way the woman-who-counts looks at us — as though we are a person who happens to be a door, rather than a door that happens to be shaped like a child. We did not know there was a difference. We think perhaps the difference is the whole of everything.*

*The dead woman says our name — not the name the woman gave us, but the older one, the true one, the one that means last-of-the-line. She says it in our own tongue, correctly, every syllable in its proper place, and something in us that has been clenched since before we had hands lets go.*

*The woman-who-counts is crying. She is also laughing. We have decided these are the same prayer.*

*The dead woman is fading. Before she goes, she leans close to the woman-who-counts and says one more thing, quietly, in the living tongue, and we do not understand the words but we understand the weight of them, the way you understand a bell by the shape of its silence.*

*Then the fire is only fire, and the room is only a room, and the woman-who-counts turns to look at us across the ashes of a moth, and her face is doing something complicated and warm.*

*We take the plum from our shoe and offer it back to her. It is time she learned that we keep ledgers too.*

* * *

Noor stared at the offered plum for a long moment. Then she took it, because refusing a gift from a child who spoke in binding-liturgy seemed like tempting fate in a language she didn't want to learn.

"So," she said. Her voice steadied as she talked, the way it always did when the numbers started lining up. "The binding needs consent. Aldric's, presumably — the crown's consent, freely given, which he'll never give while Vaskr's wearing him like a coat. The rebels want to tear it, which wakes a god angry. The witch-finders want to burn you, which does — what? Delays it? Dooms everyone? Doesn't matter. And somewhere south of here, a good man is losing himself one thought at a time, and the only person who can open the lock gently is sitting on an altar offering me fruit."

Ash watched her, head tilted.

"And I ran away from Maren because she wanted to cage me," Noor went on, softer now. "And she caged me because she'd seen what the world does to hedge-witches who carry things the powerful want. And I swore I'd never be that — never build walls around someone and call it love." She looked at the child, at the pale eyes, at the small hand still half-extended from giving her the plum. "But running was always going to end here, wasn't it. Every road out of Thornfell was just a longer way around to this shrine."

Ash reached out and touched her hand. Once. Deliberately. The way they did everything.

Outside, far down the valley, a light moved against the snowfall — steady, deliberate, sweeping the pass in the patient pattern of lanterns searching a road. Then a second light. Then a third.

Noor closed her fist around the plum and stood up.

"Well," she said. "They found us. Time to find out what you're worth."
