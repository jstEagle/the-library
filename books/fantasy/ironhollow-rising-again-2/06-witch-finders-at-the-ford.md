## 6. Witch-Finders at the Ford

The river called the Sorrow because some cartographer two hundred years ago had a bad week ran shallow and quick over grey stones, and Noor stood at its edge doing what she did best: arithmetic.

Ford crossing: half a mile of knee-deep water. Ash's weight on her hip when the current got rude: manageable. Distance to the high passes where witch-finders supposedly didn't bother going: three days, if the weather held, which it wouldn't. Odds that the patrol she'd spotted at dawn was still behind them: she'd stopped believing in good odds around the time she'd fled Maren's cottage with a stolen primer and a bruised heart.

"Right," she told Ash, who was crouched at the waterline poking a stick at a fish with the solemn concentration of a scholar defusing a bomb. "We cross here, we climb until our legs file formal complaints, and by tomorrow night we're above the snowline where even the king's hounds lose interest."

Ash looked up. Their face did the thing it always did — that terrible open attention, like a door left unlatched — and then they said something in the dead tongue, four syllables, falling cadence.

"I know," Noor said, though she hadn't translated it yet. She'd gotten better at not translating it. Some of the binding-liturgy, she'd learned, was just... small talk, in its way. *The stone remembers. The stone forgives.* A language built for keeping things, and apparently also for commenting on fish.

She swung her pack higher and stepped into the water.

It was colder than arithmetic had promised. It always was. That was the trouble with sums — they were honest about everything except how much things actually hurt.

* * *

Captain Ilsette Vane knelt in the ashes of the Kettleworth homestead and prayed, because that was what one did, and because someone ought to mean it.

The family had been taken alive, which was a mercy she'd argued for and mostly won. The house was another matter. Fire was doctrine; fire was clean. You could argue about a person's soul all day, but ash settled the question of what lived in the walls.

"She was a hedge-wife," said Brother Tam, her junior, kicking at a blackened kettle with more enthusiasm than sense. "Charms against chilblains. Love-knots. The usual filth."

"The usual filth," Ilsette agreed, and meant it, and grieved it, and would light the pyre herself if it came to that, because pity that flinched from its own work wasn't pity at all. It was vanity.

She rose. Her knees cracked. Forty years old and twenty-two years in the Order, and her knees had opinions now.

"The tracker says the vessel passed through the mill town," Tam said. "Heading for the Sorrow ford."

Ilsette looked north, where the mountains wore their clouds like old women wear shawls. Somewhere up there was a child — a *vessel*, the liturgy said, a jar with something holy and horrible sealed inside — and somewhere up there too, presumably, was whoever was carrying it. The Order's texts were clear on vessels. They were clear on the mercy required, which was the same merciless mercy as for any possessed thing: you burned the vessel so the prisoner inside could go home to the god it came from.

What the texts were less clear on was why, if the thing inside was so eager to go home, it fought so hard to stay.

"Mount up," she said. "Gently, if we can. The vessel is a child, Tam. Whatever else it is first."

Tam looked scandalized. Ilsette let him. Someone in the Order had to model the difference between cruelty and conviction, and apparently it was going to be her, on horseback, in the cold, forever.

* * *

They made the far bank by midday, and Noor allowed herself exactly eleven seconds of feeling clever before the world corrected her.

The correction arrived as a whistle from the ridge behind them — two notes, answered by three, the patrol-signals every smuggler in Thornfell knew the way they knew their own heartbeat. Noor turned, counted riders coming down through the pines, and revised her entire ledger in one sickening stroke.

Six. Six witch-finders, grey cloaks, white sashes, moving at an easy trot because they knew — the way wolves know — that running prey was already caught.

"Ash." She scooped the child up without asking; Ash weighed almost nothing, which was its own quiet horror. "Hold on to me and don't let go no matter what I say. Especially the swearing."

The river was behind them. The ridge was ahead, a wall of scree and pine, climbable in an hour they did not have. And between — the shingle flats, open ground, nowhere to be anything but visible.

Noor did the only sum left. It came out ugly.

Her magic was a smuggler's magic: small charms, misdirection, wards that screamed instead of fought. Maren had spent years trying to teach her that power had a price tag, and Noor had spent those same years pretending the lesson was about discipline rather than arithmetic. But she'd felt it lately, since the shrine, since Maren's ghost — every working took something now, something real. Warmth first. Then color. Once, after drowning a ward-net at the mill town, she'd tasted copper for a day and couldn't remember her mother's voice.

To stop six riders, she'd need a working big enough to empty her out entirely.

"There has to be another way," she muttered, which was a lie, because there wasn't, and because she was already reaching down into herself for the cold bright coin of it.

The riders fanned out across the shingle. A woman led them — broad-shouldered, silver-streaked braid, riding like someone who'd done it since before she could walk. Even at this distance Noor could see she wasn't shouting orders. She was holding her people back, hand raised, like a woman trying to keep a leash short.

Noor planted her feet in the wet stones, shifted Ash to her left hip, and opened the door in herself she'd spent her whole life pretending wasn't there.

The magic came up like floodwater. She shaped it fast and rough — not a bolt, nothing so honest as a bolt, but a *pull*, a great greedy downward drag on the river itself, hauling the Sorrow's whole cold weight toward the shallows where the riders sat their horses.

The river heard her.

It came off its stones like a released animal, six feet of brown thunder where there had been ankle-deep water, and the front rank of riders went down screaming. Horses thrashed. Grey cloaks bloomed and sank. The silver-haired captain had somehow turned her horse upstream and was fighting for the bank, dragging a junior officer by the collar —

— and Noor felt the price arrive, right on schedule, right on the nose.

Cold. Not river-cold. *Winter*-cold, pouring out through her own chest, her own ribs, as if the magic were paid for in body-heat and blood-warmth and the small essential fires that kept a person a person. Her vision went grainy. The world's colors drained like a tipped cup. She thought, distantly and with professional admiration, *that's a lot of interest on that loan,* and then her knees buckled and the shingle came up and met her, hard.

She was still holding Ash. That was the one entry in the ledger she refused to let red ink touch.

"Don't."

The word hit her harder than the fall.

Not the dead tongue. Not the binding-liturgy, not the stone-language, not anything out of Maren's stolen primer. Common tongue. Plain, flat, child-voiced common tongue — the first word Ash had ever spoken in it, in all these weeks of wards and flight and frozen mornings, and the word was *don't*.

Noor lifted her head. Ash was looking at her with those unlatched-door eyes, and their small hand was pressed flat against her sternum, over her hammering, stuttering heart, and their face was doing something new. Not fear of the riders. Not fear of the river.

Fear *for her*. Precise, specific, informed fear — the fear of someone who had watched the account balance and seen exactly what she'd just withdrawn, and from where.

"You—" Noor's teeth were chattering so hard the word came out in pieces. "You can talk. You could *always* talk."

Ash's mouth wobbled. They pressed their hand harder against her chest, as if they could hold the warmth in by main force, and said, again, wrecked and furious and tiny:

"*Don't.*"

*Don't spend yourself. Don't die for me. Don't.* Every meaning at once, the way the dead tongue worked, except this was plain speech and it cut plainer.

"No," Noor managed, and dragged herself upright, and discovered she could, barely, which was going to have to be enough. "No, listen — I decide what I spend. That's the whole point of being free, Ash. Nobody tells me what my magic costs. Not Maren, not the king, not you." She got an arm under the child and heaved them both toward the treeline, one staggering step at a time. "So the next time you want to say something useful, try *thank you*."

Ash, with the gravity of a judge, considered this.

"Thank you," they said. Then, after three more steps: "*Don't.*"

Despite everything — the cold eating her from inside, the riders regrouping downstream, the entire catastrophic shape of her life — Noor laughed. It hurt. Most true things did.

* * *

On the far bank, Captain Ilsette Vane coughed riverwater onto the shingle and watched two small figures vanish into the pines.

"They went up," Tam gasped beside her, gray-lipped, alive, which was more than she'd bet on five minutes ago. "Captain, the river — that was no hedge-charm. That was—"

"That was someone spending themselves to save a child," Ilsette said quietly. She got one boot under her, then the other. Her bones ached with cold and older things. "Mark the ford. We follow at dawn. And Tam — when we take them, we take them *gently*. I'll have no pyre lit on a lie of haste."

She looked up at the darkening peaks, at the snow already starting, and felt the pity settle into her chest where it always lived, patient as a coal.

Whatever was up there in the high passes — vessel, child, or both — it deserved better than the Order's mercy.

So, she suspected, did whoever loved it enough to drown a river.

Up in the trees, Noor leaned against a pine, shaking, emptied nearly to the walls, while Ash tucked themselves against her side like a small determined furnace and began, softly, in the old tongue, to speak to her heart.

This time, she translated.

*"Stay,"* it said. *"Stay, stay, stay."*
