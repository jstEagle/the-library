## 7. The Court of Broken Things

*In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was cold, and the Word was kept in a crown, and the crown was kept on a head that did not want it.*

*I am the small word. I am the word that was cut away so the rest could fit.*

*This is what I remember, which is everything, which is nothing anyone asked me for.*

* * *

The rebels came at dusk, when the light went thin as communion wine.

They did not knock. Knocking is a courtesy between equals, and Sefa Corvane's people had already decided — in the way adults decide things, quickly and without consulting the smaller parties present — that I was not an equal. I was a *relic*. A reliquary with legs. Something to be carried, not conversed with.

There were six of them. They came over the shrine wall like a psalm read badly: too loud in the wrong places, silent where they should have sung. Noor was asleep by the dead fire with her hands still curled around the last stub of candle, and I watched her wake — watched her go from sleep to arithmetic in a single breath, her eyes doing sums before her body finished sitting up. Six against two. One of the two could not speak their tongue. The other had spent her magic drowning witch-finders at a ford and now moved like a house whose foundations had been quietly removed.

Noor stood up anyway. That is a thing about Noor Pemberton that the liturgies do not cover, because the liturgies were written before her: she stands up anyway. It is not wisdom. Wisdom would have stayed down.

"Six of you," she said. "For one child and one half-dead smuggler. Corvane must be running short on confidence."

"We're running short on time," said the tall one, whose name I would learn was Brannic, and who had the particular gentleness of men who are kind right up until the moment they are not. "Nobody has to be hurt."

"Nobody," Noor repeated. She looked at me. In the common tongue she said, softly, "Ash. If I say run—"

I touched her wrist. *Do not*, I said, in the old tongue, the tongue of the Hollow Kings, the tongue that makes wards scream and grown witches flinch. *They will only chase you, and you have nothing left to run on. Let them carry us. Carried things are closer to the ground.*

She didn't understand the words. But she understood my hand on her wrist, the way one understands weather.

So we went with them. Down out of the high passes, into a valley where the rebel camp sat among the ruins of an old market town, tents pitched in the bones of shops, cookfires burning in doorways that had not sheltered anyone honest in a hundred years. The rebels called it the Freehold. The name was new; the stones remembered other names, older taxes, older masters. Stones are honest that way. They keep every ledger.

* * *

Here is what I saw, in the Court of Broken Things, on my first night:

A woman with a burned arm ladling soup and lying to every soldier who thanked her. ("It'll be fine." "We'll win." "You'll see your mother again.") Her lies were warm and given freely, like blankets, and I think they may have been the truest food in camp.

A boy of perhaps ten sharpening arrows because his father had died at the ford and grief needs a whetstone or it goes dull and dangerous.

Sefa Corvane herself, standing at the map table, beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful, listening to reports with her chin lifted as though the world owed her better news and she intended to collect.

And around it all, stitched through it all, the thing the adults could not see because they were standing inside it: the machinery. Turning. Oiled with hope. Every cause is a machine; every machine has a shape it is making of the world; and this one was shaping itself around me.

I am a small word, but I am an old one, and old words know what they are being used for.

* * *

They gave us a tent. They gave Noor broth. They gave me a blanket woven with a pattern I recognized from Maren's stolen primer — the binding-sigils, worn soft, decorative now, the way a cage becomes a pattern once everyone forgets it was ever a door.

On the second night, Sefa came to see me.

She dismissed her guards, which was either trust or theater, and knelt to my height, which was either kindness or strategy, and smiled, which was certainly strategy. Adults smile at children the way they check locks: thoroughly, and hoping to find nothing behind the door.

"You must be frightened," she said.

*I am never frightened*, I answered, in the old tongue, and watched her not understand me, and watched something flicker behind her eyes — impatience, banked and covered like a coal. She wanted me fluent. She wanted me *legible*. A key that cannot be read is only metal.

"She says she isn't frightened," Noor translated flatly, from the tent's mouth. "Which is true, and should worry you more than fear would."

"Does it translate anything else?" Sefa asked pleasantly.

"It translates whatever I ask it to."

"A loyal interpreter."

"I'm not loyal to you, if that's the question under the question."

"There's no question under the question." Sefa rose, smoothing her coat. "There's only a broken kingdom, and a child who might mend it, and the people who love both enough to act."

And here is the terrible thing, the thing I will carry even into mortality, even into forgetting: she meant it. Every word. Sefa Corvane loved her cause the way a fever loves a body — totally, consumingly, and with no thought at all for what remains when the burning is done.

That night I lay awake between sleeping Noor and the canvas wall, and I read the machinery the way I read everything: slowly, completely, without mercy.

They had a loremaster, yes. Old Veyran, white-bearded, ink-stained, who spoke of the binding in reverent tones. But I watched him take Sefa's orders, and I heard the shape of them. Not *free the god*. Never that. *Prepare the vessel. Prepare the rite. Ensure the transfer is controlled.* Controlled. Directed. *Worn.*

Sefa did not want Vaskr walking free across the world like a winter storm with a grudge. Storms cannot be commanded. Sefa wanted the god the way Aldric had been made to hold him — caged, close, and *hers*. A crown with a god in it is a throne that never grows cold. She had looked at a dying king and a captive divinity and seen, not a tragedy, but a vacancy.

The rebels sang songs about freedom in the evenings. Good songs. I hummed along inside, where no one could hear, and understood that most cages are built by people singing.

* * *

On the fourth day, a peddler came into camp.

He was unremarkable, which is the first mark of the trade — Noor had taught me that much in our weeks together, mostly by example, occasionally by lecture, once by catching me about to buy a "genuine dragonbone" comb. He sold needles, thread, ribbons, small tin mirrors. He worked the camp methodically, tent to tent, and when he reached ours he spread his wares with the weary patter of a man who has shown the same ribbon to four hundred women.

Noor bought thread she did not need. Her hand shook doing it — she was still hollowed out from the ford, magicless-feeling, moving through the days like someone reading by a guttering lamp — but her voice was steady and her haggling was vicious, and I felt a small fierce pride in her, the way one feels pride in a lighthouse.

The peddler packed up. At the tent flap he paused, and set down, among the ribbon-ends, a slip of paper folded the way merchants fold receipts.

Then he was gone, and Noor waited a full hour before opening it, and I read it upside down against her knee, because reading upside down is one of the few tricks my mortal fingers have learned.

*To the smuggler Pemberton, from a friend of the crown.*

*You are being kept. So is the child. You already know what Corvane intends — or you suspect, and suspicion is worse. The king offers this: safe passage out of the Freehold tonight, for you both, and a place at the table when the matter of the binding is decided. Not as prisoners. As parties.*

*Aldric is dying. He knows it. He wishes to die as himself, and to leave the world colder but freer than he found it. He asks only what he has never been able to ask in thirty years of wearing that thing: to be consulted.*

*Come to the north wall after the third watch. Ask for the man selling honesty. There is very little of it in camp, so he will be easy to find.*

*— V., spymaster, who apologizes for the theatrics*

Noor read it twice. Then she laughed — a small, cracked, astonished sound, the laugh of a woman discovering that the world contains one more betrayal than she had budgeted for, and that this one might actually be in her favor.

"The king's spymaster," she murmured, "offering to rescue us from the freedom-fighters." She looked down at me. "Ash. Is this a trap?"

I considered. I am made of consideration; it is most of what I am.

*Not yet*, I said, in the old tongue, uselessly.

But then I did something I had not done since the ford. I took her hand, and I pressed her palm flat against my own chest, over the place where the old words live, and I let her feel it — the faint cold turning there, slow as a millwheel, the sound of a lock considering its key.

When the binding is threatened, I turn. When lies come near me wearing the shape of truth, I turn faster. Noor had learned to read that tremor the way sailors read weather-glass.

"What does it mean?" she whispered. "What are you telling me?"

I looked up at her — this stubborn, calculating, candle-hoarding woman who had named me for the grey stuff left after burning, which was either grim humor or prophecy — and I did the bravest thing available to a creature of my limited vocabulary.

I pointed north. Toward the wall. Toward the man selling honesty.

Then I pointed at Sefa's tent, where the map-lamps still burned, where a beautiful blade of a woman was rehearsing a coronation she hadn't admitted to yet.

And I shook my head.

Noor closed her eyes. "Right," she said. "Right. Tonight, then." And she began, quietly, with the economy of a woman who has calculated her whole life long, to pack.

Outside, the rebels sang their evening song about freedom, sweet and rising, and the stones of the old market town kept their honest ledgers, and somewhere past the mountains a dying king sat lucid at his window, rationing his remaining hours like a miser's coins, waiting to learn whether the last innocent piece of the god in his head would choose him.

The third watch was coming.

The millwheel in my chest turned, and turned, and turned.
