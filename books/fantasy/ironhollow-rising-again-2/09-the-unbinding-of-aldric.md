## 9. The Unbinding of Aldric

The throne room had been built for a god's audience, and it showed.

Noor felt it the moment the guards marched her through the doors — the ceiling arched too high for any human comfort, the pillars spaced like the ribs of something enormous, and at the far end, on a dais of black ironwood, the crown of King Aldric sat on a cushion no one was allowed to touch. It looked small. It looked like a bird's nest made of knives. It looked, Noor thought with a lurch she refused to name, exactly like what it was: a cage, and inside it something that had been screaming for three hundred years in a language only one child alive could speak.

Ash's hand was cold in hers. They were not bound — Aldric's spymaster, a dry little man named Vetch who smelled of ink and bad decisions, had insisted on that much courtesy — but six guards walked behind them, and Captain Ilsette walked beside them, and Sefa Corvane walked ahead of them all like a woman arriving early to her own coronation.

"You're limping," Ash said. Their first full sentence in the common tongue, learned in a week of desperate lessons by firelight, and they'd spent it on an observation.

"I'm *dignified*," Noor said. "It looks similar from the outside."

"It doesn't."

"Keep your liturgical commentary to yourself."

But her hand tightened on theirs, and Ash squeezed back, and that was the whole of the conversation that mattered.

Sefa stopped at the foot of the dais and turned, and Noor saw that she was wearing grey and white — winter colors, Vaskr's colors — under a cloak cut like wings. She'd been dressing for this for years, probably. Maybe since before she had a reason.

"The king is dying," Sefa said, and there was real grief in it, which was the worst part. "And when he dies without consent, the binding shatters anyway, and everything within a hundred miles of this room dies with him. Unless we do it properly. Tonight. The first full dark of the long winter — the loremaster says the lock is thinnest now."

"Your loremaster," Noor said, "is a man who branded prisoners with hunger-sigils and called it righteousness."

"My loremaster is dead," Sefa said flatly. "I killed him when I found out what he intended for the child. Don't look so surprised, smuggler. I have always known exactly what I am. I simply refuse to be the *only* one."

She gestured, and the guards parted, and Noor saw the ritual circle already chalked on the floor around the throne — old sigils, corrected in newer chalk, and beneath the corrections, corrections upon corrections, generations of hands trying to make a cage kinder or crueler or merely more efficient.

"And if I say no?" Noor asked.

Sefa smiled, and it was almost warm. "Then I do it with the child and without you, and you can watch from wherever they throw you."

* * *

Aldric came into his throne room carried between two physicians, and insisted on walking the last ten steps alone.

He was fifty-one years old and looked ninety. The possession had eaten him from the edges inward — hair gone white as frost, hands trembling, one eye clouded where Vaskr pressed against the glass of him. But when he reached the dais he straightened, and Noor saw the man he must have been at thirty: tall, deliberate, kind in the specific way of people who have decided kindness is a discipline rather than a mood.

He looked at Noor. He looked at Ash. And then he did something no king had done in that room in living memory.

He knelt.

Not to them — to the circle, to the work, to the arithmetic of it. He knelt the way a man kneels at a graveside.

"You're the apprentice who ran from Maren of Thornfell," he said. His voice was gravel and patience. "She wrote about you. In the letters we burned."

"She wrote about me?"

"She wrote about everyone. It was very annoying." A ghost of humor moved through the ruin of his face. "She wrote that you calculated everything because trusting people had nearly gotten you killed twice, and that someday you would calculate your way directly into doing something selfless, and be furious about it afterward."

Noor's throat closed. "*That's* what she wrote?"

"There were also complaints about your handwriting." He turned to Ash, and his voice gentled further. "And you. The last piece of him that never wanted a crown."

Ash studied the dying king with the terrible frankness of children. Then they said, in the old tongue, four soft syllables.

"He says," Aldric translated, because of course he could, he'd had three hundred years of the voice whispering through him, "that I smell like snow about to fall. It's a compliment. In the old days they said that about good kings." He laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through into something else. "I was a good king. I want that noted somewhere. Before tonight, I was a good king."

"It'll be noted," Noor said. "By me. Loudly. To everyone."

"See that you are." He rose, joints cracking like river ice. "Now. The binding requires my consent — freely given, fully understood. That is why he took the crown by deception, all those centuries ago. Consent freely given cannot be faked, only stolen. So." He spread his arms. "Ask me."

* * *

Sefa moved first.

Noor would understand later — would lie awake understanding it for years — that Sefa had never planned to let the ritual happen cleanly. The loremaster's notes had included a variant, a forced unbinding that used the vessel's blood instead of the king's consent, and Sefa had learned it by heart the way she learned everything: completely, and for use.

The chalk circle flared white. Guards shouted. Ilsette — true-believer Ilsette, pitying Ilsette, who had burned homesteads with tears in her eyes — drove her blade through the nearest rebel officer's shoulder before anyone understood whose side she'd chosen, shouting "*the child lives, the child LIVES*" like a prayer she was finally allowed to finish.

And Ash screamed.

Not in the common tongue. Not even in the old tongue. In something underneath both, a sound like a glacier calving, and the temperature of the room dropped so fast that breath turned to smoke and the torches died in their brackets, and Noor felt Vaskr pressing outward against the crown like a fist against wet paper.

"*Noor.*" Aldric, still kneeling, still lucid, blood at his lip. "The liturgy. Maren's primer, page forty. *Now.*"

She had it by heart. She'd had it by heart since the watch-shrine, since Maren's echo had taught it to her with a patience the living Maren had never once displayed. She stepped into the broken circle, over Sefa's fallen officers, over Ilsette standing guard with a sword too big for her convictions, and she began to speak.

The words were older than stone. They tasted like snowmelt and iron. And they required payment — she'd known that too, since the shrine, since Maren's ghost had told her the truth with unbearable gentleness: *the binding runs on life given willingly, child. That was always the price. That was why I never taught it to you.*

Her hedge-magic woke in her chest like a second heartbeat. She let it rise. She let it pour.

* * *

From inside the crown, Aldric watched the girl work, and rationed his last hour the way he'd rationed every hour for twenty years.

*My name is Aldric. This is my throne room. The woman speaking is Noor Pemberton, and she has terrible handwriting, and she is saving my kingdom. The child is Ash, and they are afraid, and someone should be holding them, and I cannot, because my hands are needed where they are, empty and open, consenting.*

The winter god raged against the inside of his skull. Vaskr had been quiet for years at a time, once — had let Aldric rule, had sulked like weather — but now the cage was opening and the god knew it, and everything vast and starved in him was clawing for the crack in the world.

*I am lucid,* Aldric noted, in the journal of his mind. *This is mine. This hour is mine.*

"Noor," he said aloud, and his voice carried, because a throne room is built to carry a king's voice. "Tell the child — tell them I'm sorry we used their ancestors as a lock. Tell them a kingdom should never have been built on a person. Any person."

"They can hear you," Noor said, through gritted teeth, light streaming off her skin like she was burning from the inside. "They're *right there.*"

"Right." The laugh again, cracked. "Twenty years of talking to a god; I've forgotten how people work."

* * *

The binding tore.

There was no other word for it. Noor spoke the final line of Maren's liturgy and pushed everything she had through it — every trick Maren had forbidden, every hedge-charm she'd hoarded since Thornfell, the warmth in her hands that found fever-sores, the knack for knowing which road was safe, the small bright gift that had been hers and hers alone since she was six years old — and the liturgy took all of it, greedily, gratefully, the way winter takes a pond.

The crown split down the middle with a sound like a bell dying.

Aldric fell forward into the circle, and Noor caught him, because her legs still worked even if nothing else did, and for one moment — one clean, human moment — King Aldric the Last looked up at her with both eyes clear and brown and entirely his own.

"Good king," he whispered. "Note it."

"Noted," Noor said, and meant it with her whole ruined heart, and then he was gone — quietly, the way snow stops falling, without drama, a man dying free in a room full of witnesses.

The silence afterward lasted three heartbeats.

Then the doors blew open, and the cold came in, and out of the cold came something that had waited three hundred years to stand upright.

* * *

It was enormous. It was made of the space between snowflakes. It stood in the shattered doorway of the throne room with the long winter's first full dark pouring off its shoulders like a cloak, and every living soul in the room fell back except two: a magicless smuggler kneeling over a dead king, and a small mute child who walked forward through the wreckage, past Sefa on her knees, past Ilsette with her sword point-down in surrender, past all of it, and stopped in front of the god.

Ash looked up.

Vaskr looked down.

And the god — vast, wounded, starving, free — made a sound that was not rage. It took Noor a moment to identify it, because she had never heard anything that size do it before.

It was a sob.

Ash raised one small hand, the way Noor had raised hers a hundred times to offer a share of bread on a cold road, and spoke in the old tongue, softly, the way you speak to something hurt:

*Come down. Be small. Be done.*

And the god, trembling at the threshold of the world he had been caged above for three centuries, began — slowly, like a man kneeling at a graveside — to bend toward the offered hand.
