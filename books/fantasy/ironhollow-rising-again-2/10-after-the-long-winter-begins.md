## 10. After the Long Winter Begins

The snow stopped on a morning that smelled wrong.

Noor noticed it before she was fully awake — the particular silence of Thornfell's warrens, the absence of wind worrying the shutters, and beneath it something green and impossible. She lay in the narrow bed above the counting-house she'd half-leased with smuggler coin, listening to Ash breathe in the next room, and thought: *that's the smell of thaw. In midwinter. That's not right.*

Then she remembered what *was* right, now, and got up to put the kettle on.

The city had spent nine days holding its breath. Word traveled fast through the low roads — faster than any crier, because the smugglers carried it free of charge for once. The king was dead. The king had died human, lucid at the end, and had given away his crown with his own consent while a hedge-witch from nowhere burned herself hollow cutting a god loose. Half the stories were nonsense. Noor had heard three versions in which she died dramatically, one in which she was seven feet tall, and one — her personal favorite — in which she'd defeated Vaskr in single combat armed only with a ledger book.

She hadn't corrected that one. A woman earned a legend after the winter she'd had.

But the part everyone kept circling back to, the part that made grown men go quiet over their ale, was this: something vast stood unbound in the snow outside Ironhollow's walls, and no one knew what it would do.

Noor knew. Or she suspected, watching Ash eat breakfast with the solemn concentration of a child who took porridge seriously, that whatever came next would not be fire.

"You're staring," Ash said. They spoke the common tongue now, mostly, though sometimes words arrived in the old liturgy first and had to be translated through a pause, like goods going through customs.

"I'm allowed to stare. I paid for you in blood magic."

"You paid in *arithmetic*," Ash said, which was their way of saying thank you without having to survive saying thank you. "You counted everything until there was nothing left to count except me."

Noor turned to the window so the child wouldn't see her face do something undignified. Outside, the eaves were dripping.

Dripping. In the dead of winter. Water running down the glass in bright ropes, and beyond it the rooftops of Ironhollow steaming gently in the cold sun like the whole city had been baked fresh overnight.

"Ash," she said carefully. "Come look at this."

* * *

They went up through streets gone soft with meltwater, past people standing in doorways with their faces tipped up like they'd forgotten what sun felt like on skin. Someone had already dragged a fiddle into the square by the old granary and was playing badly and joyfully. A fishwife was weeping into her apron and insisting she wasn't. Children who had never seen rain were catching drips off the eaves in cupped hands and drinking them like wine.

And at the far end of the square, where the cobbles gave way to the frozen fields beyond the wall, *it* stood.

Noor had expected — she didn't know what. A giant out of the old stories. A storm given shoulders. What she saw instead was a figure made of the space between snowflakes, tall as a watchtower and thin as the hour before dawn, wearing winter the way other things wore shadows. It did not move. It did not need to. The air around it bent with attention.

The crowd kept its distance. The witch-finders — those few who remained, their captain Ilsette among them, looking smaller and more tired than her legend — had formed a ragged line between the god and the people, and were holding it with nothing but pikes and the desperate arithmetic of men who had run out of options.

Ash walked forward.

"Ash—" Noor caught their sleeve. Her heart was doing something complicated against her ribs. "We don't know—"

"Yes we do." The child looked up at her, and for a moment Noor saw the liturgy moving behind their eyes, the old tongue arranging itself like furniture in an empty house. "He's waiting for me. He's always been waiting for me. I'm the part he cut off so he could fit inside a crown." A pause. "I want to give him something back."

"Give him what?"

Ash smiled, and it was such a small, human smile that Noor's throat closed.

"The ending."

* * *

Noor stood close enough to hear, which meant close enough to die, and did not move. Some debts you pay by staying.

Vaskr looked down at the child, and the whole sky seemed to lean in. When he spoke, it was not sound so much as weather — a pressure, a cold that carried meaning the way rivers carry silt.

***LITTLE PIECE OF ME. LITTLE PIECE I LEFT IN THE WARM WORLD SO THAT SOMETHING OF MINE WOULD KNOW THE SUN.***

"You're angry," Ash said. Not afraid. Just precise, the way they were precise about everything, the way Noor had taught them without meaning to. "You've been angry for six hundred years. You were angry when they tricked you into the crown, and angry when Aldric wore you, and angry when the witch burned your cage open. But anger is just grief that hasn't found its shape yet."

***I AM A GOD.***

"You *were*. Now you're a wound walking around in the snow asking everyone to look at it." Ash's voice didn't waver. "I know what you are because I'm what you aren't. You're every winter you ever made. I'm the morning you never got to have."

The pressure in the air changed. Somewhere behind Noor, a soldier's pike trembled.

***AND WHAT WOULD YOU GIVE ME, MORNING-CHILD?***

"Mortality." Ash said it simply, the way you'd offer someone bread. "An ending. A real one. Gods don't get to finish — they just go on and on, doing the same winter forever, because nobody can stop them and so nothing they do means anything. But people end. And because they end, everything they do counts." The child spread their small hands. "You could rage for another thousand years and be exactly what you are now. Or you could stop. Be small. Be warm once. Be *done*. It's the only gift I have. It's the only thing you never had."

The silence went on so long that the meltwater had time to pool around Noor's boots, so long that someone in the crowd began quietly sobbing, so long that Noor understood she was watching a god learn to cry.

***IF I ACCEPT,*** said Vaskr, ***I CEASE.***

"Yes."

***THERE WILL BE NO MORE OF ME.***

"There'll be spring," Ash said. "That's better than more of you. That's *proof* of you — proof you could have been something other than cold, if anyone had ever asked."

And the vast figure bowed its head — bowed, to a child, to a beggar-thief's foundling in a coat two sizes too big — and the winter went out of it like breath from a mirror.

***THEN IT IS WELL,*** said Vaskr, in a voice suddenly, terribly small. ***IT IS WELL. LET ME BE DONE.***

He came apart the way frost comes off a window in a warm room — not violently, but all at once, everywhere, surrendering. The last of him was a shimmer in the air that might have been relief, and then the sun came through where he had been, and struck the wet cobbles, and the whole square blazed.

It began to thaw in earnest that afternoon. By evening the river ice groaned and split. By the third day, the first true spring thaw in living memory was running brown and triumphant through every gutter in Ironhollow, and people stood on bridges watching it go by like a parade.

* * *

Spring, it turned out, was a great deal of work.

Noor had imagined — in the rare moments she let herself imagine anything — that the hard part would be the god. It was not. The hard part was everything after: the granaries to count, the roads to reopen, the witch-finder question (Ilsette, stripped of office, had appeared at Noor's door with her pikes left behind and asked, in the flat voice of a woman renegotiating her entire soul, whether a former true believer could be useful hauling timber; Noor, who believed in second chances the way she believed in ledgers — reluctantly, but thoroughly — had said yes). Sefa Corvane was in custody awaiting trial, which meant the rebels needed a political settlement, which meant meetings, which meant Noor attended exactly one before declaring that freedom from kings apparently still involved an intolerable number of committees.

"You could leave," Sefa's lieutenant had told her, not unkindly, during the one meeting. "Go over the mountains. Start clean. Nobody would blame you."

Noor had considered it honestly. She'd done the arithmetic — routes, debts, risks, the whole familiar comfort of it. And then she'd come home to find Ash teaching Ilsette the names of birds in the dead tongue, and Ilsette writing them down in a notebook with the fierce concentration of a convert, and Noor had understood that the calculation had changed somewhere along the way without her noticing.

Some things didn't fit in ledgers. Maren could have told her that. Probably was telling her that, wherever echoes went.

So she stayed. She leased the whole counting-house, and then the shop beside it, and opened something she refused to call a school and everyone else called Pemberton's anyway — a place where children learned letters and sums and, if they happened to speak languages older than stone, where somebody could translate. She taught them the smuggler's first lesson, the one that had kept her alive: *count everything.* And then she taught them the second lesson, the one that had cost her everything to learn: *and then decide what's worth more than the count.*

Aldric's empty crown sat in the palace under guard, and there was talk of melting it down into church bells, which Noor privately hoped they'd do. Kings were finished in Ironhollow, or close enough to finished. What came next was being built by people with sore backs and strong opinions, arguing in squares that smelled of mud and cut wood, and if it was messier than a coronation, well. So was spring.

On the first genuinely warm evening, Noor sat on the counting-house roof with Ash, sharing a stolen — *requisitioned*, she maintained — apple tart, watching the sun go down over a city with no god in it and no king either.

"Do you miss him?" Ash asked. "Your magic."

Noor chewed. Considered. The honest answer was yes, some days, the way you missed a limb in cold weather — an ache where the hedge-magic used to hum. But the ache was quiet lately, and getting quieter, and today it was almost nothing at all.

"Sometimes," she said. "Do you?"

Ash thought about it with their whole body, the way they did everything.

"He was mine," they said finally. "The angry part. I think I'll miss being angry at him." A pause. "Mostly I'm just hungry. Is that allowed? After saving the world?"

"It's practically mandatory." Noor handed over the rest of the tart. "Saving the world burns calories. Ask anyone."

Below them, lamplighters were working their way down the street, and someone was laughing too loudly in the tavern, and the river ran loud and loose and free, carrying the last of the winter out toward a sea none of them would ever see.

Ash leaned against her shoulder, heavy and warm and mortal, and Noor Pemberton — runaway, smuggler, hedge-witch retired, guardian of one small ex-god — stayed exactly where she was, and counted her blessings, and found, for the first time in her life, that the number held.
