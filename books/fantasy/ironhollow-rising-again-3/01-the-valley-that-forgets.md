## 1. The Valley That Forgets

Ironhollow woke the way it always did: slowly, grudgingly, as if the valley itself resented being remembered.

Ada Blackwood stood at her window above the chandler's shop and watched the fog peel off the river like old linen from a wound. The mist never fully lifted here. It clung to the slate roofs, pooled in the gutters of Cobbler's Row, seeped under doors and into lungs until the whole town breathed it. The mountains on either side of the valley rose so steep and so close that from the street you could see only a knife-slit of sky, grey even at noon. Pilgrims used to say the gods had pinched the world shut here. Ada preferred a more honest accounting: Ironhollow was a place where things went in and did not come out — not light, not grief, not magic. Especially not magic.

She had lived here eleven years and had stopped counting after that. Counting was a kind of remembering, and remembering was currency, and Ada had learned early that you never spent your own coin if you could help it.

Downstairs, the shop smelled of tallow and beeswax and, beneath it, the faint copper tang of the vials hidden behind the false wall in the storeroom. Forty-one of them this morning. Forty-one little glass throats, each holding a swallow of someone's life. A first kiss. A mother's lullaby in a dialect dead before the Sundering. The smell of bread in a bakery that had burned twenty years ago with the baker's family inside it. Ada catalogued them the way other merchants counted bolts of cloth: by weight, by clarity, by how much a grieving heart would pay for one more hour of what it had lost.

That was the trade, and she was its finest practitioner, though *finest* was a word she kept locked away like everything else. In Ironhollow they called her the Tallow-Wife's niece, or the quiet woman above the chandler's, or — among those who knew and paid — the Gray Touch. She liked that last one least, because names were handles, and handles let people pull.

The mechanics of it were simple, which is why she distrusted them. Skin to skin, a breath of intention, and the memory came loose like a ripe fruit from a stem. It didn't hurt — that was the obscene part. The donor felt only a small coolness, a passing shadow, and afterward there was a soft place in their mind where the thing had been, smooth as river stone. They rarely missed it for days. Some never missed it at all. Grief did that; it hollowed people so thoroughly that removing one more stone from the pile changed nothing they could feel.

Ada took mostly from the dying and the desperate, and she told herself the ledger balanced. The dying wouldn't need their summers. The desperate would trade a bad year for bread. And the buyers — ah, the buyers. The widow who wanted one more evening of her husband laughing. The old soldier who'd sold his war to her and then, three winters later, come back weeping to buy a single vial of it back because he couldn't remember why he flinched at thunder. She had sold it to him at cost. She had told no one about that.

Cynicism was armor. Underneath, she suspected, was something softer and far more dangerous, but she hadn't looked under the armor in years and saw no reason to start now.

* * *

The client came at dusk, when the fog was thickest and the lamps along Cobbler's Row burned halos into the wet air.

His name was Master Ellery Vane, and he was a cooper by trade, a broad, careful man of fifty with hands like weathered oak staves. His wife had died in the fever two springs past, and since then he had come to Ada four times. He bought small things. A memory of his wedding feast. An afternoon of his wife singing while she planed wood beside him in the shop. He paid fairly, wept quietly, thanked her with the terrible courtesy of the bereaved, and left. Ada had marked him as an ideal customer: rich enough, broken enough, and too polite to ask where the memories came from.

Tonight he wanted something bigger. "I've heard," he said, turning his hat in those enormous careful hands, "that you have a childhood. A boy's summer. Fishing, they say. A river."

Ada's fingers went still on the shelf.

She knew the vial he meant. She had taken it eight months ago from a dying man up-country, a stranger brought through the pass by traders, feverish and far from home. He had pressed her hand gratefully, gratefully — *take it, take the lot, I don't want to be all these things when I go* — and she had walked away with thirty years of another man's life in her palm. Among them: a golden summer. A boy of nine on a brown river with a willow for a throne and a brother who splashed and shouted. She had priced it high, because joy was rarer than grief in her inventory, and joy sold to anyone, not just mourners.

"Such things are delicate," she said. "Provenance matters. I can't promise—"

"It's mine." Vane said it simply, without drama, the way a man says *it's raining*. "The river's the Sorrel, below my father's mill. The willow had a rope swing. My brother Tam fell off it and broke his arm and laughed the whole way home. That's mine, mistress. That's my summer."

The silence stretched between them like a wire.

Ada had rules for this. She had built them over eleven years, plank by plank, precisely so that moments like this would have somewhere to stand. Rule one: never sell a memory to anyone connected to its origin. Rule two: screen every buyer. Rule three: trust the fog, the distance, the valley's blessed amnesia — Ironhollow forgot, and forgetting was the whole foundation of her trade.

But the man had come through the pass. The dying man had come *from* somewhere, and somewhere, apparently, had a brother who grew up to be a cooper and followed a rumor down into a valley where you could buy back the years God had let slip.

"I think," Ada said slowly, "you are mistaken. These are collected works. Impressions. Dreams, some call them—"

"My brother is dead." Vane's voice cracked on the second word, and his hands stopped moving. "Tam died last spring. Fever, same as took my wife. And a trader told me — he'd seen a woman in Ironhollow selling summers in glass. I sold my cart. I sold my shop tools. I walked six weeks." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and absolutely steady, and that steadiness was worse than any rage could have been. "I'm not here to make trouble, mistress. I just want to hold it once more. Before I'm too old for it to fit."

Every rule Ada had said: deny, deflect, sell him the wrong vial and let him weep over a stranger's wedding. Every rule said: your survival depends on being a rumor, a shadow, a gray touch with no face.

But she thought of the vial in the back — the boy on the rope swing, the laughter, the willow — and she thought of the dying man's hand going cold in hers, grateful, *grateful*, and something under the armor shifted its weight.

"The price," she heard herself say, "is everything you have left."

He paid it. Of course he paid it. She watched him uncork the little glass throat with shaking fingers, and she watched the memory leave the vial the way it always did — a breath of silver, a warmth against the lips — and she watched Master Ellery Vane, cooper, fifty years old, become for one shining moment a boy of nine on a brown river with his brother laughing beside him.

He cried the whole time. He thanked her twice. At the door he paused and said, "You gave me back something the fever took twice over," and Ada closed the door on him before he could see what that did to her face.

* * *

Afterward she sat alone in the storeroom with the remaining forty vials glowing faintly in the lamplight, forty little captured sunsets, and she did the thing she never allowed herself: she counted what she had taken, not what she held.

A girl's first snowfall, lifted from a consumptive laundress. A sailor's homecoming, taken from a man who begged her to because the waiting had been sweeter than the arriving. Her own mother's face — no. Not that one. That one she did not count. That one lived in the deepest drawer, wrapped in wool, touched perhaps twice a year, and each time less, because each time the edges wore a little smoother and she was terrified of the day she'd reach for it and find only river stone.

Eleven years in the valley. Forty-one vials. One empty space where a cooper's summer used to be.

"You're a thief," she said aloud to the dark, testing the old familiar shape of the words. They fit. They had always fit. But tonight they sat strangely, like a coat grown suddenly tight across the shoulders, and underneath the armor something shifted again, patient as water finding a crack.

Outside, the rain began — a hard, driving rain, the kind the valley threw against its walls when autumn turned. Ada banked the lamp and climbed the stairs, telling herself the shiver at her spine was only the draft.

Three knocks came at her door just past midnight. Soft. Insistent. Wrong for the hour, wrong for the weather.

And when she opened it — lamp raised, thief's caution humming in her blood — there was no traveler, no client, no desperate mourner on her step.

There was a child.
