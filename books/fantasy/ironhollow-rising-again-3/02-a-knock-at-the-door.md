## 2. A Knock at the Door

The rain came down on Ironhollow like a debt being collected.

Ada Blackwood heard it first as a change in the sound of the town — the usual drip and mutter of eaves sharpening into a steady drumming on the slate roofs below her window, the gutters waking up with a gurgle. Then it arrived in earnest, hammering the shingles over her attic room, and she lay in the dark listening to it and thinking about the look on Master Corren Hale's face that afternoon.

She had been thinking about that face for six hours now. It was not improving with age.

He had come to her stall in the Shambles with his collar up and his eyes red-rimmed, the way they all did, wanting something warm to hold against the cold spot where a person used to be. His wife, he'd said. Died two winters past of the lung-rot. He wanted one good memory of her — a wedding day, an ordinary supper, anything — sealed in glass, something he could press to his brow at night and *be there* again.

Ada had sold him exactly that. A memory lifted off a widow in Lowmarsh who'd been drowning herself in ale to forget her own marriage; a wedding under a flowering hawthorn, sunlight through the petals, a girl laughing so hard she had to sit down in the grass. It was a beautiful memory. It had cost Ada nothing but a handshake and a half-hour's conversation about weather.

The trouble was what Corren Hale had done afterward. He'd gone home, pressed the vial to his brow like she'd told him, and lived someone else's wedding day — and somewhere inside all that borrowed sunlight, some stubborn scrap of himself had looked at the bride's face and thought: *that is not my wife.*

Except it was. That was the horror of it. The widow in Lowmarsh had been married to Corren Hale's brother, dead these ten years, and grief had worn both women's faces so smooth and similar that even memory couldn't always tell them apart. When Corren surfaced from the vial, weeping for a woman who wasn't quite his, he'd understood — slowly, the way ice understands it is water — that the sunlight on his skin belonged to someone else's happiness. That he had paid four silver marks for another man's joy.

He hadn't shouted. That was the worst part. He'd stood in front of her stall in the drizzle, holding the little glass phial up between two fingers like a dead moth, and said, quietly: "Is any of it real? Any of what you sell?"

And Ada, who had answers for everything, who could talk a magistrate out of his boots and a mourner out of his inheritance, had heard herself say: "It's real. It happened. Just not to you."

He'd set the vial down on her counter with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something he wants very badly to throw, and walked away without asking for his money back. That was worse too. She would have known how to be hated. She did not know how to be pitied.

Now it was midnight, and the rain was coming down like the sky meant to drown the valley, and Ada lay in her attic above the chandler's shop on Rope Lane, staring at the underside of the roof beams, and admitted to the dark what she never admitted to anyone:

She didn't know whose memories she carried either. Not really. She knew their shapes, their colors, the weight of them in her chest where she kept them banked like coals. But she had taken so many, from so many strangers, that when she reached for *her own* childhood — the flooded quarry, her mother's hands, the smell of tallow — she sometimes found other people's quarries, other mothers' hands, mixed in like river water poured into wine. Her gift kept no ledger. It only kept everything.

"Sentimental old fool," she told the ceiling, in her mother's accent, which was also not her mother's accent anymore, probably. "Corren Hale got what everyone gets. A pretty lie in a bottle."

The ceiling declined to argue.

* * *

The knock came at the worst hour, which was the only hour knocks ever came to Ada Blackwood's door.

Three strikes, small ones, spaced out like the knocker kept forgetting between blows. Not the fist-pounding of the Watch, not the coded double-tap Bram used when he had goods too hot to hold till morning. Small. Almost apologetic.

Ada sat up in the dark, and every instinct she owned stood up with her.

Rope Lane after midnight was either trouble or opportunity, and in her experience the two were conjoined twins. She slid from bed, pulled her knife from beneath the loose floorboard — not because she expected to use it, but because a thief without a knife is just a burglar waiting to be caught — and padded down the stairs barefoot, avoiding the third step that announced visitors to the whole street.

She put her eye to the peephole Bram had drilled for her, a brass ring set into the door at the height of a kneeling child.

There was a child.

A girl, six years old at most, standing on Ada's step in rain that fell like rope ends, soaked through a dress that had once been fine — good wool, embroidered at the hem, now grey with road-mud to the knee. Bare feet. Blue lips. Hair plastered flat to her skull, dark as wet ink. And clutched against her chest with both arms, held the way children hold things they have been told, in words or otherwise, that they must never let go: a locket. Silver, heavy-looking, old. Even through the peephole's distortion, Ada could see it was old the way bones are old.

No adult in sight. No lantern on the lane. Nothing but rain hammering the cobbles and this small drowned creature lifting her face toward the door as if she could see through it.

Ada should not open the door. Every rule she lived by said so. Children were complications; children were leashes around your neck that anyone could pick up and pull; children were the one thing in Ironhollow that could not be fenced, forged, or sold. She had spent twenty years making herself into a thing with no handles.

Her hand turned the latch anyway.

Cold air and rain rushed in, and with them the girl — not rushing, exactly, but swaying forward like a boat coming off its mooring, across the threshold and then stopping, three feet inside, dripping on Ada's clean floor, looking up.

Up close she was stranger than the peephole had made her. Too pale, as though she'd been ill for months rather than walking in rain for hours. Eyes a grey so light they were nearly colorless, ringed with exhaustion. And she was shivering in a way that had gone past cold into something else — a fine, constant trembling, like a plucked string that wouldn't still.

"Right," Ada said, because someone had to say something. "You can't stay—"

The girl opened her mouth.

What came out was not crying, though it had the shape of crying. It was *words*. A stream of them, low and urgent, tumbling over each other, the cadence rising and falling like a bird arguing with weather. The sounds themselves were soft, liquid, full of vowels that opened like doors — and Ada stood in her own doorway with rain blowing in past her ankles and felt the hair rise on her arms, because she knew, the way you know a face in a crowd, that she had heard this language before.

Never in life. In *glass*.

Three years ago, a dying antiquarian in Hollowbridge had sold her a memory he claimed was worthless — a scholar's memory, secondhand, of reading aloud from a cracked stone tablet in the university cellar. Ada had bought it cheap and mostly forgotten it. But the sound of it lived in her hoard still, filed away among a hundred other curiosities: a language like water over stones, older than the valley, older than the walls that kept the valley. The scholars had a name for it. Everyone had a name for it, and no one spoke it, because everyone who had spoken it had died or fled or been unmade on the same terrible year the histories all went silent.

The Sundering tongue. The language of before. Dead two hundred years, buried with the war, locked out of the world along with everything else that magic had touched.

And this six-year-old child was speaking it fluently, urgently, tearfully, to a stranger in a doorway, as if it were her mother's voice — as if it were the only voice she had.

"I don't understand you," Ada said, uselessly, in Common. "Little one. I can't—"

The girl stopped. Looked at her. And Ada watched something happen in that colorless gaze — a kind of folding shut, a door closing behind the eyes. The girl had understood *that* much, at least. She knew she wasn't understood. You could see how well she knew it; you could see how long she'd known it, how many doors had closed on her already, how many faces had gone blank and afraid.

Then the locket hummed.

Ada felt it before she heard it — felt it in her teeth, in the place behind her sternum where her gift lived, the deep quiet room where she kept everything she'd ever taken. The locket made a sound below sound, a vibration like a struck bell heard through a wall, and against every rule of her trade, every instinct honed over twenty years, Ada's gift *reached for it*.

It happened without her permission. It always happened without her permission — that was the nature of the thing; touch a mind and the room behind her ribs opened its door and began to drink. She had learned to govern it, to sip instead of swallow, but the reaching itself was involuntary as a blink.

The locket refused.

Not resisted — *refused*, the way a locked door refuses, politely and absolutely. Ada's gift pressed against it and slid off, again and again, like rain off oiled leather. And the locket hummed louder, and the humming climbed into the range where it became a feeling, and the feeling became a pressure, and the pressure pushed back along the thread of her own reaching and showed her, for one vertiginous instant, a glimpse of what it held—

Light. A wall of light. A plain of grass burning without smoke. A voice saying *hold the gate, hold the gate, whatever comes, hold the—*

Ada snatched her hand back. She hadn't realized she'd reached out at all. Her fingers were an inch from the locket, trembling, and the girl was watching them with those near-white eyes, and there was no fear in the child's face at all.

There was recognition. There was relief, terrible and total, the relief of someone who has walked a hundred miles in the rain to one specific door.

The girl said something else — one word this time, slow, deliberate, shaped carefully for a foreign mouth. She pointed at the locket. Then at Ada. Then, with a small mud-streaked hand, she pointed out the door, up through the rain, toward the dark shoulder of the mountain where the valley's walls rose sheer and old, where no road went and no one went, where the Wardens' watchtowers had stood empty for a generation.

Then she looked back at Ada and repeated the word. Whatever it meant, she said it like a warning. She said it like the last coin she had left to spend.

Outside, far off across the sleeping town, a bell began to ring — not the Watch bell, not the chapel bell. Something older, from the tower by the old gate, that Ada had never once heard ring in all her years in Ironhollow.

The girl flinched at the sound. And stepped closer to Ada — not away, *closer* — and took a fistful of her nightdress in one small hand, and held on.
