## 3. What the Locket Remembers

The locket sat on Ada's table all night like a guest who refused to leave.

She had tried to put it in the drawer with her tools — the little silver picks she used on minds instead of locks, the vials of dark glass, the wax seals stamped with a merchant's mark that belonged to no real merchant. The drawer would not close around it. Not physically; it closed fine. But every time she slid it shut, her skin prickled along the forearm, the way it did when someone nearby was remembering something too loud to ignore, and she had to open it again just to breathe.

So it sat on the table, and Wren slept in Ada's bed, and Ada sat in her one good chair and watched them both.

Rain still worked at the shutters. The girl had not stirred since midnight, when she'd murmured something in that dead tongue — two syllables, soft as a stone settling into a riverbed — and turned her face into the pillow. *Vessa.* Or maybe *vessah.* Ada had no way to know if it was a word or a name or nothing at all.

The locket hummed.

Not sound, exactly. It hummed the way a struck tuning fork hums inside your teeth rather than your ears. Ada had spent eleven years learning the texture of other people's memories — grief came in blues and slow water, joy in copper and heat, shame in ash. She had never once felt anything push back against her gift. Memories were passive things. They lay where they fell and let you take them.

This hummed back. This *watched.*

"You're a lock," she said quietly, to the locket, because talking to objects was what happened to thieves who lived alone too long. "And I don't pick locks I can't see the inside of."

But of course she did. That was the whole of her trade.

* * *

She waited until grey light seeped through the shutter cracks before she touched it. Not because she was afraid — she told herself that twice, which meant she was lying — but because whatever lived in that little silver case had been waiting centuries already. It could wait for daylight. Everything in Ironhollow waited for daylight; it was how the valley survived itself.

Wren slept on. Ada sat across the table, rolled up her sleeve, and laid two fingers flat against the locket's lid.

The world went out like a pinched candle.

* * *

Cold first. Then grass under her hands — real grass, wet, springy, alive in a way Ironhollow's scrubby valley turf had never managed. A sky overhead so blue it hurt, unbroken by the grey ceiling of cloud that hung over the hollow every day of Ada's life. And a woman's voice, warm and low, speaking from somewhere behind her eyes:

*They will tell you magic died, little bird. Remember this: nothing that was ever loved has ever died. It only goes somewhere it cannot be found.*

Ada knew the voice.

Her whole body knew it before her mind caught up — the way a scar knows rain coming. Her mother had died when Ada was seven, fever, the bad winter, and in twenty years Ada had sold off nearly every memory of her own that contained that voice, because keeping them hurt worse than hunger and hunger at least could be fed. She had kept exactly one: her mother singing while kneading bread, flour to the elbows, the kitchen gold with lamplight. She kept it in a vial she had never sold and never opened, buried at the bottom of her hoard like a coin she couldn't spend.

That was not this voice. This voice was younger. Clearer. And it was speaking a language Ada should not have understood — and understood perfectly.

*I am writing this into silver because paper burns and minds can be stolen,* the voice said, and here Ada felt something like laughter move through the memory, dry and rueful. *Though I suppose a thief might steal anything, given time. If you are hearing this, thief, then know I left it where clever hands would find it. Clever hands are the only kind that survive.*

The vision sharpened. Ada stood — floated, rather, a point of attention without a body — above a battlefield that stretched past the horizon.

Not a battlefield. A wound.

The earth was split in a seam miles long, and out of the seam poured light — not gentle light, not sun, but light with weight and intent, armies of it, shapes made of radiance wrestling shapes made of dark, and the noise of it was not sound but *meaning*, vast arguments conducted in fire. Men and women in grey robes stood along the seam's edges, arms raised, and where they stood the chaos bent away, held back, dammed.

*The Sundering,* her mother's voice said, quieter now. *We called it that because it is easier than calling it what it was. We broke the world to stop each other, and when neither side could win, we built a prison instead of a grave.*

The scene folded like a page turning. Now: a valley. Green, sheltered, ringed with hills. Ada recognized the shape of it with a lurch — those hills were her hills, worn down by centuries, but the bones were the same. The seam of light ran beneath the valley floor like a vein under skin.

*Here,* said the voice. *Here is where we locked it. Not in a tower, not in a sword — towers fall and swords get stolen. We locked it in forgetting. Every soul born inside the valley's rim would be born unable to hold magic, unable to carry it, unable even to believe in it properly. The valley itself is the lock, and its people are the tumblers, and the key—*

A pause. When the voice resumed it had changed, gone careful, gone quiet, the way people speak when children might be listening.

*The key is blood. One line of wardens carried the sealing in their veins, and one child of that line — one girl, in every generation, born knowing the dead tongue without being taught — is the living memory of why the door must stay shut. Kill her, and the knowledge dies. Take her, and the door opens. So we hid the line. We scattered it. We taught their daughters silence.*

*And I wrote down what I knew, and hid it in silver, and gave my life's last trick to make sure it would find the right hands.*

The battlefield again, dimming now, receding like a tide going out. Ada felt the memory begin to gather itself closed, and she clawed at it — not with her fingers, there were no fingers, but with the raw instinct of her gift, hauling fragments toward her the way a drowning woman hauls at driftwood:

*A fragment:* grey-robed wardens kneeling in a circle, chanting in the dead tongue, and the ground beneath them cooling like a forge left to die.

*A fragment:* a woman with Wren's face — older, tired, laughing — teaching a small girl words that made a hidden door swing open in a hillside.

*A fragment:* fire on the horizon, and her mother's voice saying, sharp with fear, *They're coming back. They never stopped wanting it. Promise me the valley forgets, promise me, promise me—*

And then, last, almost tenderly pressed into her grasp like a coin slipped into a palm:

*If you have taken everything else I hid here, thief, take this too: the lock does not want a hero. It wants a keeper. Someone stubborn. Someone who knows the price of things. I suspect you know the price of things very well indeed.*

*Keep her alive. Keep her free. And do not, whatever they offer you, give them the key.*

The locket went cold under her fingers.

* * *

Ada came back to her own kitchen gasping, both hands flat on the table, the chair tipped precariously behind her. Grey morning filled the room. Rain had stopped. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled, and a rooster complained, and Ironhollow went about its ordinary, forgetting day.

Wren stood in the bedroom doorway.

Ada didn't know how long the girl had been there. Long enough. Those dark eyes — old eyes, wrong eyes for a six-year-old face — moved from Ada's white knuckles to the locket on the table, and something passed over the small features that was far too weary to belong to childhood.

"Vessa," Ada said hoarsely. Testing it.

The girl's breath caught. Then, slowly, carefully, shaping each alien syllable like a mason fitting stones, she answered in Common for the first time since the night she'd appeared on Ada's doorstep:

"You heard her."

It wasn't a question. Ada sank back into her chair, and her hands, which had never once shaken in eleven years of thieving, shook now.

"I heard my mother," Ada said.

Wren crossed the room — small bare feet silent on the boards — and climbed onto the chair opposite, and looked at Ada with the terrible patience of someone who had been waiting a very long time for an adult to finally understand something.

"She said keepers," the girl said. "Not heroes." She reached out one finger and touched the locket, and it hummed, softly, contentedly, like a cat recognizing home. "The grey men are coming for me. They always come. You have to decide fast which one you are."

Outside, far down the valley road, a bell began to ring — not the chapel bell, but the watch bell, quick and urgent, the rhythm that meant strangers on the road.

Ada looked at the locket. At the child. At the door.

"Well," she said, to no one, to the ghost in the silver, to herself. "Damn you, Mum. You always did leave your messes for me to clean."

She was already reaching for her boots.
