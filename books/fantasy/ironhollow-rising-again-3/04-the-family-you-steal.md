## 4. The Family You Steal

Bram Halloway had a rule about trouble: it always arrived hungry, and it always ate the host first.

He stood in the doorway of Ada's back room, rain still dripping off the brim of his hat, and looked at the child sitting cross-legged on the floor like she'd been born there. She was small — six years old, maybe seven — with a smudge of soot on one cheek and eyes that watched everything and gave nothing back. In his forty years of moving stolen goods through Ironhollow's shadow economy, Bram had learned to read people the way merchants read ledgers. This child's ledger was blank. That frightened him more than any debt ever had.

"She came to the door," Ada said from her chair by the cold hearth. "Night before last. Rain like the sky was trying to drown us all."

"And you just... let her in."

Ada's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "She knocked."

Bram set his hat on the peg by the door, which was as close as he ever came to admitting he intended to stay somewhere a long time. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with hands that had once been a smith's hands and were now a fence's hands — good for counting coin, better for counting exits. He counted them now. Two windows, one door, one cellar hatch he already knew stuck in wet weather.

"Ada." He kept his voice low, though the child was watching their mouths move with the particular attention of someone who understood every word and could say none of them. "You know what I do for a living. I sell things whose owners want them back. And I'm telling you, free of charge, that whatever this girl is carrying, someone out there wants it back worse than anything I've ever fenced."

"I know."

"You *know*?" He rubbed his face. "Then give me something. Where's she from? Who brought her?"

"No one brought her. She walked down the Hollow Road alone, in the rain, barefoot for the last mile because her boots split. And when I asked her name, she said—" Ada stopped, and Bram saw something flicker across her face that he had never seen there before, not in ten years of partnership, not in the bad winter when the fever took half the lower town. Uncertainty. "She said a word. One word. And Tull — old Tull, who forgets where he puts his teeth but remembers everything else — went white as bone and told me it was Old Tongue. The language they spoke before the Sundering. The language that died with the war."

Bram looked at the child again. She looked back, unblinking, and then — deliberately, almost courteously — she lifted the locket hanging at her throat and tucked it inside her collar, out of sight.

His skin crawled. "She knows we've been talking about it."

"Of course she does. She's not stupid. She's just small, and alone, and speaking a dead language in a valley that kills magic on contact." Ada leaned forward. "So here's what I need from you, Bram. Not an opinion. A decision. Do we keep her, or do we hand her to the magistrates and let Ironhollow do what Ironhollow does?"

"And what's that?"

"Forget her," Ada said quietly. "Forget her fast, and clean, and completely."

The child made a sound. Small, sharp, involuntary — and though it was shaped in no language Bram knew, its meaning needed no translation. *No.*

Both of them turned. The girl had both fists pressed against her mouth, and her eyes were shining, and she shook her head once, hard, like a horse refusing a bit.

Bram exhaled through his nose. He thought about his shop above the chandler's, with its false wall and its honest ledger and its forty years of careful, cowardly profit. He thought about the road out of Ironhollow, the only road, the one that wound up past the grey standing stones and out into a world he hadn't seen since he was twenty-two and still had calluses instead of debts.

"Well," he said at last, heavily, settling into the second chair as if it were a decision itself. "I suppose I'd best hear the whole of it, then. Start from the knock."

* * *

Sefa arrived at midmorning, when the rain had thinned to a grey drizzle and the light through Ada's windows was the color of dirty pewter. She came in without knocking — she never knocked, on principle, having spent her girlhood in houses where knocking meant someone official — and stopped dead two steps inside.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, she's *real*. I thought Bram was drinking."

"Sefa," Ada warned.

"What? I'm delighted. Look at her." Sefa crouched down to the child's level, and there was nothing performative in it; she simply folded herself down the way water finds its level, quick and easy, until they were eye to eye. Up close she was all sharp angles and ink-stained fingers, twenty-six years old and wearing a coat with three different buttons because matching sets were for people who expected to keep things. "Hello, little mystery. Can you tell me your name?"

The girl studied her for a long moment. Then she opened her mouth and produced a sound like wind through a broken flute — soft, sibilant, rising at the end. A question, maybe. Or a greeting. It was beautiful and utterly alien, and Sefa felt gooseflesh climb her arms.

"That's the Old Tongue," she breathed. "Tull's right. My grandmother used to hum a lullaby her grandmother taught her, and she swore the tune was older than the valley, but the words—" She shook her head. "Nobody's spoken those words since the Sundering. Three hundred years. They burned the books, drowned the singers, and then the valley sealed itself and everyone agreed very firmly to remember nothing."

The girl touched her own chest. "*Wren,*" she said — and the word came out in Common, rough and rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter.

Sefa blinked. Then she laughed, sudden and bright, clapping her ink-stained hands together. "Wren! Did you hear that? She picked her own name. Well — half of it. Wren is what I called you in my head the moment Bram described you. Small brown bird, too clever for cages." She tapped the girl's chest gently. "Wren. It suits. Keep it."

"Wren," the girl repeated, tasting it. Her mouth curved — the first smile anyone had gotten from her, small and wondering, as if smiling were another language she was relearning.

And that was how the child got her name: not given, but chosen, borrowed from a forger's fancy and offered like a gift. Sefa would deny to her dying day that her eyes went wet over it.

They gathered around Ada's table — Bram, Sefa, Tull installed in the corner with tea he kept forgetting to drink — and Ada laid it out plainly: the locket that resisted her gift, the fragments of war she'd glimpsed inside it, the prison beneath the valley, the magic that hadn't died but been locked away. The room got quieter with every sentence.

"So," Bram said finally, when she finished. "Options. We keep her hidden here and hope whoever's looking gives up. We take her to the magistrates and wash our hands. Or we run her out of the valley, which means crossing the stones, which nobody's done in living memory, and delivering her to — whom, exactly? The people who lost her? The people who'll come looking?" He spread his hands. "Every road ends badly. I've checked my ledger twice."

"Then we don't pick a road yet," Sefa said. "We pick a question. What do *we* want?"

"We want to live," Bram said flatly. "That's my whole position. I have a shop. I have customers who'd notice if I vanished. I have—" He stopped, jaw working.

"Say it," Sefa said softly.

"I have nowhere else." The words came out like teeth being pulled. "You think I stay in Ironhollow because business is good? Business is *adequate*. I stay because thirty years ago I walked out those stones with my brother, going to make our fortunes in the low country, and I came back alone, and I have never once worked up the courage to walk out again and see if the road remembers him. There. Is that what you wanted? The valley doesn't just kill magic, Ada. It keeps things. It kept *me*." He stared at the table. "I can't leave. But I'll be damned if I hand a child to whatever's outside that wants her."

Tull spoke from his corner, and everyone turned, because Tull spoke perhaps four sentences a week. His voice was dry as old paper.

"I stayed for a woman," he said. "Forty years ago. She married a cooper instead. I stayed anyway. Silly reason to give a valley your whole life." He looked at Wren, and his ancient eyes were clear for once. "But I know locks, girl. I've opened every kind man ever made. And if what Ada says is true — that this whole valley is a lock — then that child isn't cargo. She's the key. And keys," he said, "always belong to somebody. Question is whether it's us or them."

Wren, who had followed none of the argument and all of the tone, slid off her chair and crossed the room. She stood in front of Tull, small and grave, and put her hand flat on his knotted old fist.

"*Stay,*" she said, in Common, carefully. Then, in the Old Tongue, something longer — a whole sentence, musical and strange — and though no one could translate it, no one in the room doubted what it meant.

* * *

It was near dusk when Sefa, watching from the window while the others argued logistics, went still.

"Ada," she said. "Come look at this."

Down the crooked lane, moving unhurried through the drizzle, came three riders in grey cloaks. Not soldiers — soldiers announced themselves. These moved like men attending a funeral, faces calm, eyes taking inventory of every window, every doorway, every face. At the well in the square they stopped, and the tallest dismounted and began, politely, to speak with the miller's wife.

Ada's hand found the window frame. Beside her, Wren made a sound like a small animal hearing a hawk, and backed away from the glass, and pulled the locket out of her collar and gripped it so hard her knuckles went white.

"They're here," Ada said quietly. "They're already here."
