## 5. Riders in Grey

Bram Halloway had spent thirty years learning to read people the way other men read weather, and the three riders who came down the mountain road on the first grey morning of autumn told him everything he needed to know before they'd even reached the town gate.

They rode fine horses. That was the first wrong thing. Ironhollow saw traders and pilgrims and the occasional tax-man from the lowland lords, and all of them arrived dusty, their animals lathered and footsore after the long climb through the pass. These horses stepped down the switchbacks like they were descending into a ballroom, coats gleaming, breath even, as though the mountain itself had been asked politely not to trouble them.

The second wrong thing was that there were three of them. Traders traveled in caravans. Tax-men traveled with soldiers. Nobody who meant Ironhollow well or ill had ever come in threes — but then, Bram thought, watching them pass beneath the gate's leaning arch, nobody had ever come wearing borrowed faces before.

He saw it because it was his trade to see it. A face, like a coin, could be minted well or badly, and these were minted *well* — so well that most of the townsfolk lining the street saw only three travelers: a broad woman with a farmer's sun-creased cheeks, a lean man with a scholar's stoop, and between them a small, neat person of indeterminate age whose smile sat exactly where a smile should sit and reached exactly as far as a smile should reach and no further.

But Bram had spent thirty years buying stolen goods from frightened people and selling them to desperate ones, and he knew what forgery looked like when it was done by something older than Sefa's cleverest hand. The woman's laugh lines didn't move when she laughed. The scholar blinked at intervals so regular you could have set a clock by him. And the small one in the middle — the one doing the talking — its eyes never once flicked toward the bakery, or the tavern, or any of the hundred small human things a traveler's eyes are drawn to. It looked only at faces. It read them. It moved on.

*Wardens,* Bram thought, and his mouth went dry as flour dust. *The Hollow Wardens have come to Ironhollow.*

He'd heard of them the way you hear of wolves — stories from traders who wouldn't meet your eye, rumors of an order that served no crown and answered to nothing, that walked out of the wastes beyond the Sundered Mountains in whatever shapes pleased them. His mother had used them to frighten him straight when he was a boy. *Mind your debts, Bram, or the grey riders will collect.* He'd assumed, in the comfortable way of grown men, that they were half legend, like the magic that couldn't cross the valley line.

The small one stopped in front of his stall.

Bram sold fence-goods out of a shop that officially dealt in "curios and restored furniture," which is to say a crowded den of chipped lockets, mismatched silverware, and chairs that had belonged to four dead households apiece. The little bell above his door had never once announced honest commerce. Now the Warden stood in his doorway without entering, and the bell didn't ring at all, and that was the third wrong thing, and Bram felt sweat start cold between his shoulder blades.

"Good morning." The voice was pleasant. Unremarkable. The kind of voice you forgot the moment you heard it, which was precisely the point of it. "We're looking for someone. A child."

"Children," Bram said, "are Ironhollow's chief export. Along with turnips."

The smile deepened by a measured degree. "A girl. Six years old, perhaps seven. Dark-haired. She would not speak Common — or anything else, we're told. She went missing from a caravan on the high road some weeks ago." The Warden produced a folded paper, opened it with two fingers, and held it up.

It was a drawing of Wren.

It was a *good* drawing. Not the flat, stiff likeness a town artist might manage — this caught her, the stubborn set of her chin, the wary dark eyes, the way her hair fell across her brow. Someone had studied that child closely enough to love the drawing, if such creatures loved anything, and Bram's stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking on water.

"Don't know her," he said.

"You haven't looked yet."

"I've got a good memory for faces. It's the trade I'm in."

"Is it?" The Warden tilted its head, and for just a moment the borrowed face seemed to sit slightly wrong on the skull beneath, like a mask hung a half-inch off its hook. "And what trade is that, Master...?"

"Halloway. Bram Halloway. Curios and restorations." He spread his hands over his cluttered kingdom. "You can see I'm a man of modest ambitions."

"We've heard otherwise." The Warden stepped inside at last, and the bell still did not ring, and the silence of it was worse than any sound. It drifted along the shelves, trailing one finger an inch above the goods without touching. "We've heard that goods pass through this shop that have no honest provenance. We've heard that a certain woman named Blackwood sells memories here, through you, in vials. We've heard—" the finger paused above a shelf of cheap clay pots, "—that strange things have been happening in Ironhollow lately. A child who speaks a dead tongue. A locket that hums."

"I sell pots," Bram said.

"You do." The Warden turned. Its eyes were brown and warm and utterly empty, and Bram understood suddenly why his mother's warnings had survived three generations: not because the Wardens were cruel, but because they were *patient*, and patience was so much harder to survive than cruelty. "Here is what will happen, Master Halloway. We are going to ask our questions in this town for a day, perhaps two. People will tell us things. People always do — kindness, fear, spite, coin; it makes very little difference to us which lever moves them. And if we learn that you knew of this child and said nothing..." The smile returned, exact as a ruler's edge. "Well. We are reasonable. There is always a price, and prices are simply a language, aren't they? You of all people speak that language fluently."

Bram's heart was hammering against his ribs like a prisoner at a door. And here was the thing about Bram Halloway, the thing thirty years of sharp dealing had carved into him deeper than any scar: when the price was named, he bargained. Even now. Even with his life sitting on the scale.

"What price," he croaked, "for me hearing nothing, saying nothing, and you finding your own way?"

The Warden regarded him for a long moment. Then it laughed — a real-sounding laugh, warm as bread, and somehow that was the worst thing yet. "Oh, I *like* you. Very well. A toll. For your silence and your slowness in answering us — you'll be surprised how slow a man can be when properly paid to be stupid."

It named a figure.

Bram's whole fortune — the shop, the stock, the careful hoard of twenty years of skimming and saving and the secret strongbox under the floorboards that was meant to buy his way out of this valley someday, out to the coast, to a little house where nobody knew what he was — the figure was his whole fortune, and the Warden named it the way a man names the cost of a meal.

"That's robbery," Bram whispered.

"Yes," the Warden agreed pleasantly. "It's rather our whole nature. Do we have an agreement?"

And Bram, who had lied to lords and cheated thieves and talked his way past every hard corner of a hard life, heard himself say: "I'll need a few days to raise it."

"Of course." The Warden moved to the door, and paused there, silhouetted against the morning light, and for one instant Bram saw the shape beneath the shape — something tall and grey and jointed wrong, something patient as stone — before the borrowed face settled back into place like a lid closing. "Two days, Master Halloway. And do give our regards to Miss Blackwood. We understand she's been keeping unusual company."

The bell rang as the door closed. Of course it did. It rang bright and cheerful, like a shop welcoming custom, and Bram stood alone among his stolen goods with his hands shaking and the drawing of a six-year-old girl lying on his counter, and understood with perfect clarity that he had just bought two days with everything he had ever saved, and that it wasn't going to be enough.

* * *

He found Ada at dusk, in the back room behind Sefa's workshop, where lamplight made the walls soft and the smell of ink and hot metal covered the smell of fear. They were all there — Ada with her arms crossed and her jaw set, Sefa grinding pigment with more force than the mortar deserved, old Tull whittling a peg he had no use for. The child — Wren, they called her — sat in the corner turning a wooden button over and over in her small fingers, and when Bram came in she looked up at him with those dark, ancient eyes, and he had to look away.

"They're here," he said. "Three of them. Borrowed faces, good ones. They've got a drawing of her." He laid the paper on the table, and watched Ada go white around the mouth. "They gave me two days. Two days before they start pulling this town apart, and they'll do it politely, door to door, smiling, and somebody *will* talk, Ada, because somebody always talks. I bought us time, but—"

"How?" Sefa said sharply. "Time isn't free. What did it cost?"

Bram looked at the floor. At his boots. At thirty years of cleverness arriving all at once at the end of its road.

"Everything," he said. "It cost everything I had. And it won't hold, because they don't want coin — coin was just the lever. They want *her*." He nodded at Wren, who had gone very still, the wooden button frozen between her fingers. "And they know she's here. They knew before they ever crossed the pass. This was never a search. It's a collection."

Silence, except for the lamp hissing and the wind worrying the shutters. Then Tull spoke, quiet as dust settling, from his corner:

"Then two days is what we've got. Best spend 'em moving."

Ada was staring at the drawing of Wren, and Bram watched something move behind her eyes — something he'd never seen there in all their years of sharp dealing together, something that had no place in a thief's arithmetic. It looked, God help him, like a woman deciding what she was willing to lose.

"Get Sefa's maps," Ada said. "The old mine shafts. Everything under the valley." She looked up, and her voice was iron. "Because in two days, we're gone — and we're taking her with us."

In the corner, Wren opened her mouth, and said — clear as a bell, in Common, her first word in it, her voice rusty as an unused hinge:

"Gone."

And outside, far up the dark mountain road, three riders turned their heads as one toward the valley, listening.
