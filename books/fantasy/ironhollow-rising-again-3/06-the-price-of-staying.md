## 6. The Price of Staying

Sefa had forged a hundred faces in her life — seals, signatures, the looping hand of dead merchants whose estates needed untangling — but she had never forged a person until she tried to make Wren look like nobody's daughter.

It was Tull's idea first, back when it was still a joke. *Dye her hair, mud on her face, send her out with the tinker carts.* The joke had died in his throat an hour ago, when the grey riders came down the switchback road and stopped at the well in Copperlane Square and began, politely, to take people.

Sefa watched from the shuttered window of Bram's counting house with Wren pressed against her hip and Ada's hand white-knuckled on the sill.

There were six of them. They wore the valley's own clothes — miller's smock, carter's coat, a widow's shawl on the one who might have been a woman — and that was worse than armor. Borrowed faces, Bram had called them last night, after he'd come home shaking from the bribe he couldn't afford. Faces like coats they'd taken off other people. Sefa had spent her whole life making false things look true, and she knew the sickness of it when she saw it worn so casually, so *well*.

The riders moved through the square without hurry. They didn't shout. They simply touched people on the shoulder — the cooper, the cooper's son, old Marta who sold eels, and then Tull, who had gone out at dawn to buy lamp oil because he was stubborn and frugal and seventy-one years old and had never once believed trouble would bother learning his name.

Tull didn't struggle. He looked up at the rider holding his shoulder, and then — Sefa saw it clearly, even across the square, even through the gap in the shutters — he looked directly at Bram's window. At them. And he winked.

Then he walked with them to the well and sat down on the stones beside the others like a man waiting for a ferry, and Sefa's chest did something she didn't have a name for.

"They're not taking him to kill him," Ada said quietly. "Not yet. Hostages are a promise you intend to keep."

"Then we give them what they want," Bram said behind them. His voice was wrecked; he hadn't slept since the Wardens had sat in his parlor asking polite questions about a missing child. "We walk her out the front gate and hand her over and—"

"No." It came out of Sefa before she could weigh it. She felt Wren flinch against her hip and softened her grip into something more like an embrace. "No. You saw what's in that locket. You felt it hum. Whatever they want her for, it isn't kindness."

"Neither is burning," Bram said. "They said dawn, Sefa. They said if the child isn't at the tollhouse by dawn, they start with the oldest hostage and work downward. That's Tull. That's *our* Tull, sitting on cold stones right now because we hid a girl under our floorboards."

Wren made a small sound. Not a word — or not a word Sefa knew. One of the dead-tongue syllables, low and round, and she lifted the locket in both hands and held it out toward the window, toward the square, as if offering it to the riders herself.

Ada took the locket gently and closed the girl's fingers back around it. "Not yet," she said, and there was something in her voice Sefa had never heard there. Something that wasn't cynicism. "Not ever, if I can help it."

* * *

They argued for three hours while the light went copper and then bruise-dark over the valley rim.

Bram wanted surrender dressed up as strategy: deliver the child, negotiate for the hostages, count their losses and survive. He kept saying *survive* the way a drowning man says *shore*. Sefa understood him. Bram had debts stitched through this town like veins through a heart — every favor he owed, every name he'd fenced goods under, every ledger line that would burn if the Wardens burned Ironhollow. He couldn't leave. None of them could, really, and that was the trap of the valley: it held you by everything you'd buried here.

Old Tull would have said the same thing, if old Tull weren't sitting hostage at the well. Tull had a wife in the churchyard and a son in the churchyard next to her, and he'd told Sefa once, drunk on festival wine, that leaving Ironhollow would mean leaving them behind twice.

And Sefa herself — Sefa had no one in any churchyard. What held her was smaller and stranger: the fact that every document she'd ever forged, every identity she'd built out of ink and nerve, only worked inside a town that half-recognized her. She was a forger of the particular, and Ironhollow was her particular lie. She had never admitted that aloud. She suspected Ada had known for years.

So they couldn't run, and they wouldn't hand Wren over, and the Wardens had set the arithmetic of dawn against them.

It was Ada who broke the silence, in the end. She came up from the cellar where she kept her hoard — the vials, hundreds of them, racked in straw like eggs — carrying a single memory in her palm, and she set it on the table between them.

"You know what I do," she said. "You've always known. I take memories. I sell them. I've got eleven years of other people's lives in that cellar, and I've never once asked whether that made me a thief or a merchant, because either way I ate." Her jaw worked. "Here's the unthinkable part. A memory isn't just a thing in a vial. It's a *trail*. If I pour enough of them into the ground along the east road — the pass road, the one that leads out toward Lowmark — anyone who hunts by blood or by gift will read it. They'll read a child's fear, a child's running, a child's voice. I've got fragments in my hoard from a girl who ran away from the miller's abuse two winters back. I can braid them together. I can make the trail say *she went east, alone, three days ahead of you*."

"And they'll believe it?" Bram asked.

"The Wardens hunt by something older than tracking," Ada said. "The locket hums against my gift — which means their gift is shaped like mine. They'll follow what the valley remembers. So we teach the valley to remember wrong." She looked at her own open hand as if it belonged to someone else. "The catch is that I can't fake it from nothing. I have to spend real memories. Real ones, poured out where I can't take them back. Every vial I use is gone forever — and everyone who *gave* me those memories loses them a second time, permanently, because I won't be able to return them even if I wanted to."

The room went very quiet. Rain had started again, soft against the shutters.

"That's your living," Bram said slowly. "That's eleven years."

"It's worse than that." Sefa heard her own voice come out flat and careful, the way it did when she was checking a forgery against the original. "Ada. Some of those vials are *paid for*. People bought them. Grieving people. Widow Corren bought her husband's voice last spring. If you empty the hoard, you're robbing them all over again, and this time there's no getting it back."

"I know." Ada's eyes were steady and terrible. "I've been doing the sums since the riders came down the road. Here's what I keep arriving at. Those memories are already stolen, whatever pretty word we put on it. I stole them. The question is only whether they stay locked in my cellar doing nothing, or whether they buy a child's life and four hostages' necks and maybe this whole stupid valley another century of forgetting." She laughed, short and joyless. "I always thought I'd die rich in other people's pasts. Turns out I'd rather spend them."

Wren, curled in the corner with a blanket around her shoulders, looked up at the word *spend* as though she understood it perfectly. Maybe she did. Children understand spending; it's adults who pretend otherwise.

"There's another cost," Sefa said, because someone had to say it, and guilt was a currency she dealt in too. "If you pour yourself out along that road — your gift, your hoard, hours of it, touching stone and earth and teaching them to remember — you'll be hollowed. You told me once what happens when you take too much in a day. You go gray at the edges. This is a thousand times that."

"I know what it costs," Ada said. "I'm the one who's been counting."

"It's not just the hoard," Sefa pressed, and hated herself for pressing, and pressed anyway. "Your mother's memories are in that cellar. The ones you took off her deathbed because you couldn't stand to lose her face. You told me — you were drunk, but you told me. If you spend the hoard, you spend *her*."

Ada didn't answer for a long moment. The rain filled the silence. In the corner, Wren rose, crossed the room small-footed, and stood beside Ada's chair without touching her — close the way a cat is close, near enough to warm.

"My mother's face is already gone," Ada said finally. "She's been dead nine years. All I've been keeping is the having of it." Her hand found Wren's hair, absently, the way you touch something you've decided to protect. "This girl has a face too. A future one. I find I care about that more."

* * *

They worked through the night, the three of them, because grief is no excuse for sloppy craft.

Sefa forged the documents first — a tinker's travel pass dated three days stale, a bill of sale for a mule that never existed, a scrap of a letter in a child's uncertain hand, *if I keep walking they can't find me, tell the miller's wife nothing*. She made each one imperfect in the correct ways: rain-blurred, tear-spotted, handled. Forging truth meant forging damage. She taught Ada the trick of it while Ada sorted vials by lamplight, laying out the braids of memory like a seamstress laying pattern-pieces: fear here, running here, a girl's voice crying a name that wasn't Wren's but could be mistaken for her in the dark.

Bram went out at midnight and came back with road dust on his boots and news in his mouth: the hostages were still at the well, under canvas now, guarded by two borrowed faces. Tull had sung something bawdy at his captors, loud enough to carry to the square's edges. Bram laughed when he reported it and then put his head in his hands, and neither woman pretended not to see.

An hour before dawn, Ada stood at the door in her traveling cloak with a satchel of vials that represented everything she had ever taken from anyone, and Sefa realized with a lurch that the plan had a hole in it the size of a person.

"You're going alone," Sefa said. "East road, pouring memories into the ground for six miles, finishing an hour before the deadline — alone, in the dark, with grey riders between you and the pass."

"Someone has to be here when they check the house," Ada said. "Someone has to be Wren's alibi. That's you and Bram. That's the trade."

"That's a terrible trade."

"It's the only one on the table." Ada smiled, and it was the old cynical smile, worn like a borrowed face, and Sefa hated how well it fit her. "Besides. If I'm caught, I'm just a memory-thief out walking. Nobody suspects the thief of giving anything away."

At the threshold Ada paused. She turned back, and for one unguarded second Sefa saw beneath the smile — saw the gray at the edges of her already, the place where eleven years of other people's lives had begun to drain out of her like dye out of cloth.

"Sefa. If this works — if the trail takes and they ride east — get Tull and go to the tunnels under the north shafts. Wren will know the way. Don't wait for me."

"Don't talk like a will," Sefa said sharply. "I forge those. I know how they sound."

Ada laughed — really laughed, once — and stepped out into the rain, and the door closed behind her.

Sefa stood with her hand flat against the wood, listening to footsteps fade, and Wren came and stood beside her and slipped her small cold hand into Sefa's. The girl spoke one word of the dead tongue, soft as an ember, and then — haltingly, like a lock turning — its Common twin:

"*Come back.*"

Down in Copperlane Square, the first grey rider turned his borrowed face toward the east road, and began, politely, to listen to the ground.
