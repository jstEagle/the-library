## 7. Tunnels Under the Lock

The dark under Ironhollow is not like other dark. Other dark is empty. This dark is full.

Wren knows this because she can feel it pressing against her skin like water against a window. The others cannot feel it. They walk with their little lights and their little fears, and the dark parts around them like it is being polite. But for Wren the dark leans close and whispers in her own tongue — the old tongue, the tongue that lives only in her mouth now — and it says: *you came back.*

She did not come back. She has never been here before.

But the dark does not know that. Or the dark does not care.

* * *

They go down through a door behind the tannery, a door that Bram swears was not there yesterday. It is iron, green with age, and it has no handle. Old Tull puts his picks to it and shakes his head and says words Wren half-understands now: *no lock, no hinges, no way.* Sefa holds the lamp high. Ada stands with her arms crossed and her jaw set, the way she always stands when she has already decided something but wants the world to argue first.

Wren steps forward. She does not decide to. Her feet decide, and her mouth decides after them.

"*Sulvenn ath,*" she says. *Open, please.* That is what her mother used to say to doors, back before, in the place Wren keeps folded up small inside her chest so it will not hurt so much. *Open, please,* said gently, the way you speak to something old and tired.

The door opens.

It does not creak. It swings inward smooth as water, and warm air breathes out of it, air that smells of stone and rain and something sweeter underneath, like flowers growing where no flowers should. The lamp flames bend toward the doorway as if leaning in to listen.

Nobody speaks for a long moment. Then Tull says, "Well," in the voice of a man whose whole life's work has just been made unnecessary, and Ada says, "Right," in the voice of a woman who has decided not to be afraid yet but is keeping the option open.

Wren touches the doorframe as she passes through. The iron is warm. Under her palm it hums, very quietly, the same note as the locket.

*Hello,* the iron says, or she thinks it says. *We remember you.*

*You are wrong,* Wren thinks back. But she is smiling, and she does not know why.

* * *

The tunnels are mine-shafts once. Sefa says so. She runs her fingers along the walls and finds the old tool-marks, the rails' rusted bones, the niches where lamps hung when there were men to hang them. "Ironhollow dug itself rich and then dug itself empty," she murmurs, "and then it dug too deep, and then—" She stops. She looks at the wall. There are marks there that are not tool-marks. They spiral, tight as snail shells, and they glow faintly when Wren walks past, one by one, like a row of candles lighting themselves for her.

"Then what?" Bram asks from the rear, nervous, his big shoulders brushing both walls at once.

"Then the Sundering," Sefa says. "And they sealed it all and wrote it out of every book and burned every book that wouldn't be written out." She glances at Wren, and her look is soft and frightened at the same time. "And apparently they left the key walking around in a six-year-old."

Wren does not like being called a key. Keys do not get tired. Keys do not get hungry. Keys do not miss their mother with a hurt so large it has its own weather.

But she does not say this. She saves her words. Words are heavy here; she can feel them working when she speaks, feel them land on things and change them. In the world above, a word is a breath. Down here, a word is a hand.

So she uses them carefully. And she watches the magic leak.

That is the truest name for it: leak. It comes through the stone the way water comes through a roof in bad weather. It pools in the low places of the tunnel, shimmering faintly, and when Tull steps in a puddle of it he yelps and hops and swears he heard his grandmother singing. When Ada steps in one she goes very still, and her face does something complicated, and afterward she walks faster for a while and does not talk. Wren guesses what the puddle showed her. Wren's own puddles show her her mother's hands, braiding hair. She steps around them now. Not because she doesn't want to see. Because she wants it too much, and wanting too much makes you slow, and slow gets you caught.

Above them, somewhere far, the Wardens are searching. They can all feel it, even the ones who cannot feel anything else: a pressure, a patience, a grey attention moving through the town like oil through cloth. The dawn is coming. The dawn is when the hostages die. Nobody says this. Everybody carries it anyway, the way miners carry gas — silently, carefully, knowing exactly which spark not to strike.

* * *

They come to the first sealed gate an hour down, maybe two. Wren measures time by her hunger, and her hunger says two meals.

This gate is different from the tannery door. It is black stone, seamless, taller than Bram, and carved over every inch with writing in her tongue. Not decoration. A plea. She reads it slowly, sounding the shapes with her fingers the way Sefa taught her to sound letters of Common.

*WE CLOSED THIS DOOR AND WE ARE SORRY. DO NOT OPEN IT UNLESS THE WORLD IS ENDING. IF THE WORLD IS ENDING, WE ARE ALSO SORRY ABOUT THAT.*

Bram reads Sefa's halting translation and laughs, one sharp bark, then stops laughing because nobody joins him. "Jokers, our ancestors."

"They weren't joking," Wren says.

Her own voice surprises her. It comes out low and certain, older than six. Everyone looks at her. The lamp gutters. Behind the black stone, something shifts its weight — a vast, patient shifting, like a mountain turning over in its sleep.

"The people who built this were sorry the whole time," Wren says. "Sorry while they did it. Sorry while they locked it. They left the sorry written everywhere because they wanted whoever came after to know they hadn't wanted to." She puts her hand flat on the stone. It is cold here, colder than the iron door, cold like the far side of a grave. "But they still did it."

"Can you open it?" Ada asks. Her voice is careful, gentle, the voice she uses on spooked animals and angry clients. "Or — do you want to? We can find another way if—"

"There is no other way," Wren says. She knows this the way she knows her own name, which is to say: someone told her once, long ago, in a voice she can no longer fully hear. "All the ways come here. This is the bottom of the valley. Everything drains downward."

She speaks the opening. It is not one word. It is many words, braided, and some of them taste like grief and one of them tastes like her mother's name, and when they leave her mouth they leave her a little lighter and a little emptier, and she understands suddenly why the dark whispers *you came back* — because the valley remembers everyone who ever spoke these words, and it has been waiting a very long time for anyone to speak them again.

The gate does not swing. It dissolves — edges first, like sugar in hot tea — and beyond it the tunnel changes.

* * *

Beyond the gate, the mine becomes something older than mining.

The passages widen into halls. The halls have pillars, and the pillars have carvings of people holding hands around a circle, and the circle is a door seen edge-on, and around the door the carvers left blank stone — a ring of nothing, a deliberate silence in the middle of all that talking stone. Wren stops before the blankness and feels her heart knock.

"That's where it is," she whispers. "Under us. That's the lock."

Magic is not leaking here. Magic is *rising*, slow as spring sap, visible now even to the others — threads of pale gold drifting upward through the air like pollen, like snow falling wrong-way-round. Tull catches a thread on his palm and weeps without seeming to notice he is doing it. Sefa's forged papers, tucked in her coat, begin softly glowing with every signature she has ever faked. Bram's shadow stands up straighter than Bram does.

"It's waking up," Sefa says. Her voice shakes. "Ada. It's been asleep since the Sundering and it's *waking up*."

"No," Wren says. "It never slept. Sleeping things dream. This is worse. It waited. There's a difference." She looks up at the adults, at their faces gone grey and gold in the rising light, and she gives them the truth because they have given her soup and blankets and names — Sefa named her Wren, a small brown bird that sings anyway — and because she is tired of carrying truths alone. "The Wardens don't want to put it back to sleep. My mother told me. They want to open it all the way and finish what their grandmothers started. And they need my blood to turn the key. So we must not let them catch me. Ever. Not once. Not almost."

Silence. Somewhere above, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, a bell begins to ring. Dawn-bell. Hostage-bell. Tull's bell.

Tull looks at the ceiling. He is very old and his hands shake even when they aren't picking locks, and he has taught Wren three card tricks and how to swear properly, and he says, "Well then. Best keep moving, little bird."

They move. And behind them, in the hall of the hand-holding people, the blank ring of stone around the buried door begins — very faintly, very slowly — to glow.

* * *

The last passage climbs toward a shaft of grey daylight, and Wren, who has learned to love the dark that whispers, feels terror arrive ahead of her like a cold wind: because in the light, the Wardens wait. She can smell them. Borrowed faces smell like nothing at all, and nothing-at-all is the worst smell there is.

She takes Ada's hand. Ada squeezes back, once, hard, an entire speech in a single squeeze.

"*Sulvenn ath,*" Wren whispers to the daylight — *open, please* — though no door answers, and though she is not sure anymore whether she is asking the world to open, or begging it, just this once, to stay shut.
