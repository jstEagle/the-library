## 8. The War That Never Ended

The valley's edge was not a wall and not a line. It was a feeling — a wrongness in the teeth, a pressure behind the eyes, like standing at the lip of a well that went down farther than the world should allow. Ada had felt it all her life without knowing what it was. Every child of Ironhollow knew it: the moment you walked far enough up the sheep trails toward the pass, something in your chest began to pull backward, gentle as a hand on your collar, saying *not yet, not yet, turn around.*

They had been walking since before dawn. Wren rode on Bram's shoulders with the locket tucked inside her coat, and every hundred paces she would touch his ear or tug his hair and say a word of her dead tongue, and Sefa would answer in her careful Common, and the girl would laugh — a small, startled sound, like she was still learning that laughing was allowed.

Tull brought up the rear, as he always did, leaning on his pick-staff and complaining about his knees in a voice pitched to carry. "Forty years I've picked locks," he said. "Never once had to *walk* out of one."

"The valley's not a lock," Sefa said.

"Everything's a lock, girl. Question is only what it's keeping in, and who's got the key." He spat companionably off the trail. "And I've been asking myself that since the night she turned up on our doorstep."

Ada didn't answer. She was watching the treeline where the pines thinned toward the pass, watching the grey light move through them, and trying not to think about how quiet it was. No birds. There were never birds near the valley's edge — another thing she'd known all her life and never questioned, because questioning things in Ironhollow got you looked at, and being looked at was bad for business.

"Stop," she said.

Bram stopped. The whole line stopped, four figures and a child on a mountainside, breath steaming.

"What is it?" Sefa whispered.

Ada didn't know how to say it. It wasn't a sound or a sight. It was her gift — the thing she'd spent fifteen years treating like a tool, like a knife, like a trade — and it was *singing*. The way a tuning fork sings when its twin is struck somewhere out of sight. Memories. Not in vials, not in heads, but loose in the air itself, caught in the pines and the stones like pollen, thousands of them, ancient and bright and screaming.

"They're here," she said. "Get down—"

The first arrow took Tull's staff out of his hands.

* * *

They came out of the trees without sound, which was the worst part. Men and women in undyed wool, faces plain as bread, the kind of people you'd sit next to at market without a second glance — and then they moved, and the borrowed-ness of their faces showed through like a mask slipping. The Hollow Wardens. Six of them fanning across the trail, and more in the trees; Ada could feel the memories clinging to them too, stolen or eaten or worn, a cold oily residue that made her skin crawl.

"Put the child down," said the one in front. Her voice was pleasant. That was worse than the arrows. "No one else needs to be hurt. We have been very patient."

"You shot Tull's stick," Bram rumbled, and did not put Wren down. He shifted her behind his broad back instead, and Ada saw him plant his feet the way he did when a buyer tried to shortchange Sefa's work — immovable, apologetic, doomed.

"We shot past him. There is a difference." The Warden tilted her head, studying Ada with an expression of mild professional interest. "You're the memory-thief. Blackwood. We read your file."

"I have a *file*?"

"You have a talent we've seen twice in three hundred years. You'll want to hear this before you die, so listen." The woman folded her hands, courteous as a clerk. "You believe we've come to kill the child. Everyone always believes that. Killing is crude. Killing loses information." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, and that was the most frightening thing Ada had ever seen. "We don't want the girl destroyed, thief. We want her *opened*. Her blood is the last unbroken line of the Sundering's architects. One drop on the gate, spoken over in the old tongue — the tongue she still carries — and the prison opens. And we finish what our grandmothers' grandmothers started."

Ada's mouth had gone dry. "Finish what? The war's over. It's been over for—"

"The war has been *paused*." For the first time the pleasantness cracked, and underneath it was something old and hungry and patient as stone. "Your valley isn't a refuge, Blackwood. It's a cork. Magic didn't die when the Sundering came — it was *chained*, and the chain was forged from a memory so heavy no single mind could carry it, so they broke it into ten thousand pieces and gave it to ten thousand sleepers and let the forgetting hold it shut. Clever. Beautiful. And temporary." She stepped closer. "Our order has kept the true war alive for nine generations. Waiting. And now the lock is walking around in a six-year-old's blood, and you are going to hand her to us, because if you don't, we will take her, and everything between us and her will be a smear on this hillside."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere above them, loose snow slid off a branch.

"And if the gate opens?" Ada heard herself ask. Her voice sounded far away. "What comes out?"

"Victory," said the Warden simply. "Order. A world with magic in it again — *our* magic, under *our* hand. The ones who built the prison were cowards and thieves. They stole wonder from the world because they feared it. We are only taking back what was ours."

Ada thought about her vials. About the grieving widow who'd bought her husband's laughter by the dram. About the client who'd recognized his own childhood in her wares and wept in her doorway while she lied to his face. She had spent her whole life telling herself that memories were just goods, that taking them was just trade, that nothing given to a thief deserved protecting.

She looked at Wren, peering around Bram's hip with those enormous dark eyes, and thought: *they'd open her like a letter.*

"No," Ada said.

The Warden sighed. "I was afraid you'd be sentimental."

"TULL!" Ada screamed, and threw herself sideways down the scree.

* * *

Later — much later, in the hollow place behind her ribs where grief would eventually set up housekeeping — Ada would try to remember exactly how it happened, and fail. Memory was her trade and she knew better than anyone how it lies, how it smooths and rearranges, how it gives you the parts you can bear and hides the rest in the dark.

Here is what she kept:

Tull's pick-staff whirling, his bad knees forgotten, the old man moving with a speed none of them had ever seen because none of them had ever needed to see it. Here is what she kept: the Wardens' borrowed faces flickering as Tull touched them — because that was the trick, that was the secret he'd never told any of them, that forty years of picking locks had been practice for picking *people*, that his gift was hers and older and finer, and he could reach into a mind and *turn the key*. Two Wardens dropped their weapons and stood blinking like men waking from a dream of home. A third sat down in the snow and began to cry.

Here is what she kept: Bram charging like the avalanche he resembled, Sefa dragging Wren behind the rocks, Ada scrambling up the scree with pine needles in her mouth and her heart slamming against her teeth.

And here is what she kept, because there was no hiding from it, because she had earned it: the arrow. Not the first one, the polite one. The second. It took Tull high in the chest as he turned to check on Wren — checking, even then, even bleeding, because the child was the job and the job was the family and the family was the point of everything, and he'd never once in forty years left a lock unpicked or a friend unwatched.

He went down in the snow like a coat falling off a hook.

"TULL—" Sefa's voice, cracking in half.

"Go." He was propped against a boulder, snow blooming red under him, and he was smiling, the old gap-toothed crooked smile, and his hand was already reaching for the nearest fallen Warden's collar. "Locks don't pick themselves, girl. Go. I've got the door."

"You're coming *with* us, you stubborn old—"

"Ada." He said her name the way he'd said it the first day, when she'd been eleven and starving and he'd caught her lifting coppers off his table and instead of turning her in he'd taught her which pockets were worth lifting. "Listen to me. I can't leave the valley. Never could. You know why." His eyes were going glassy but his grip on the Warden's collar was iron. "My wife. My girls. The fever year. I sold their memories to buy the medicine that didn't work, and I can't — I *can't* walk out of this place carrying nothing but the hole where they were. This is my valley. Let me keep it."

The Wardens were regrouping. The two he'd unlocked were shaking themselves free of the dream. Above the treeline, on the ridge, more grey shapes were moving into position, and Ada understood with terrible clarity that six had only ever been the courtesy call.

"Tull—"

"*Go.*" And then, softer, with the smile: "Tell the girl she speaks pretty. Tell her... tell her the valley remembers her name, even if nobody else does."

Ada ran. She hated herself for running. She would hate herself for running for the rest of her life, and she ran anyway, because that was the bargain Tull had bought with the last coin he had, and spending it twice would be theft, and she was many things but she had never once shortchanged a dying man.

Behind her she heard him laugh — actually laugh, delighted, the laugh of a man meeting a lock worthy of him — and then she heard the mountain itself groan, deep under the snow and the stone, as though some vast mechanism long rusted had been thrown into motion by an old man's final touch.

The pass ahead of them shimmered. The wrongness in her teeth sang louder. And the pulling feeling, the hand-on-the-collar feeling that had held every soul in Ironhollow for nine generations, *let go* — not opening the way out, but opening the way *down*, through the mountain's roots, toward something below that had begun, very softly, to knock.

Wren tugged Ada's sleeve. The girl's face was wet, her small fists clenched, and when she spoke it was half in her dead tongue and half in words she was only just learning, and both of them together made Ada's blood run cold:

"The gate knows we're coming. It says—" Wren swallowed. "It says somebody has to pay to close it. It says the price is *everything you remember.*"

Sefa stared at her. Bram's breath came out ragged. And Ada put her hand flat against the warm locket through the child's coat, and felt her mother's face waiting inside it like a coin at the bottom of a well, and understood what the mountain was asking, and what it would cost, and that she was already, had always been, going to pay it.

"Not here," she said hoarsely, turning back toward the dark mouth of the mine shafts below. "Not for them. Down. We finish this at the gate."
