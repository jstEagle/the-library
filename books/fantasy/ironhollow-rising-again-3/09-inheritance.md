## 9. Inheritance

The stairs went down farther than Ironhollow had ever gone.

Ada had lived her whole life in the valley and never known that beneath the cobbles of the Tanner's Row, beneath the flooded cellars and the old mine shafts where magic leaked like spring water, there was a throat of stone descending into the dark. Wren walked ahead of them with one small hand pressed to the wall, and wherever she touched, the rock remembered how to open. Doors that had been sealed since the Sundering sighed apart at the sound of her dead tongue — syllables like river-stones turning over, like a lullaby sung by someone who had forgotten how to grieve.

Bram carried a lantern in one fist and Sefa's arm in the other. Sefa was limping badly; the Wardens' crossbow bolt had torn through her calf at the valley's edge, and Tull —

Ada did not let herself think about Tull. Not yet. There would be time for grief later, if they lived, and if they didn't, then grief would be someone else's problem.

The air changed as they descended. It grew warm, then strange — thick with a pressure Ada felt against her gift like a hand pressed flat against her sternum. Her stolen memories stirred inside her, all of them, every vial she'd ever poured down her own throat rather than sell: a fisherman's last morning on the lake, a bride's terror on her wedding night, a soldier's dream of home. They shifted like minnows sensing a net.

"They know," Wren said softly, in her half-learned Common. She looked back over her shoulder, and in the lantern light her eyes were the grey-green of deep water. "The memories. They are *afraid*."

"Memories can't be afraid," Bram rumbled.

Wren only looked at him, and he stopped arguing.

* * *

The gate was not what Ada expected.

She had imagined iron — something forged, something with hinges and chains, something a lockpick like Tull could have laughed at. But the prison-gate beneath Ironhollow was not made. It had *grown*, or perhaps it had been grown, roots of pale silver stone spreading across the cavern floor and up the far walls like frost given permanence. At its center stood an archway, tall as three men, and through its frame Ada could see — not darkness, not light, but a kind of *waiting*. A stillness so complete it made her teeth ache.

Magic. All of it. Every spell ever cast, every wonder and horror, folded up small and shut behind this door, because the war it fed had grown too vast for the world to survive.

And the Wardens wanted to open it.

"They're coming," Sefa said from the base of the stairs, where she'd lowered herself onto a fallen slab. Her face was grey with blood loss, but her voice was steady. "I heard boots above us before we were twenty steps down. They'll have found the shaft entrance by now."

"Then we do this fast." Ada turned to Wren. The girl stood before the arch with her locket clutched in both hands, and the humming Ada had felt that first rain-lashed night filled the whole cavern now, resonating in the silver roots, in Ada's own bones. "Tell me again what your grandmother showed you."

Wren's mouth worked. Words came slowly, translated twice over — once from the dead tongue into her own understanding, once more into Common. "The gate eats a keeper. That is how it holds. One mind holds the lock, and one mind can be found, and one mind can be broken." She looked up. "The Wardens want my blood because my family were the keepers. But a keeper is not blood. A keeper is a *memory*. Whoever holds the memory of the gate holds the gate."

"And if they take you," Ada said, "they hold it forever. And the war starts again, and this time nobody gets a valley to hide in."

Wren nodded.

"So we don't give them one mind to break." Ada reached into her coat, into the inner pocket where she kept the things she could not bring herself to sell, and drew out the last of her hoard — not vials this time, but the raw weight of it, years of other people's yesterdays stacked behind her eyes like cordwood. "We give it ten thousand."

Sefa stared at her. "Ada. You can't mean—"

"I mean exactly what I've always done, Sef. I'm going to steal something." Ada smiled, and it hurt her face. "I'm just going to steal *from myself*."

* * *

It was Bram who understood first, because Bram was the fence, and fences understand distribution better than anyone alive.

"You scatter it," he said slowly. "Like coin. Like splitting a haul so no single thief can be traced."

"Ironhollow sleeps above us," Ada said. "Every soul in this town is dreaming right now, and dreams are just doors left unlocked. I pour everything into the gate — every memory I've ever taken, and the true memory of the gate itself, and the locket's — and the gate spreads it out. Thin. So thin no one knows they carry it. Ten thousand people each holding one thread of the secret, and no thread strong enough to pull alone." She looked at the arch. "No keeper. No claim. Nothing for the Wardens to steal or burn or bleed."

"And your mother?" Sefa asked quietly.

Ada's hand closed around the locket Wren held out to her.

She had touched it once before, in her kitchen, on that first terrible night — and been flooded with fragments of the ancient war, proof that magic hadn't died but been imprisoned, that the valley itself was the lock. But she had never dared touch it long enough to find the rest. She knew what else lived in there. Wren had told her, in her careful half-words: *your mother gave a memory to the locket, when she was young. Before you were born.*

Ada pressed her thumb to the silver.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of bread and rain.

Her mother stood at the window with flour on her hands, younger than Ada had ever known her, laughing at something out of sight — and then turning, and looking directly out of the memory as though she could see her daughter standing in it. As though she had known, thirty years ago, that someone would come looking.

*"If you're hearing this,"* her mother said, *"then you've got the thief's hands after all. I hoped you wouldn't. I hoped you'd be a baker, or a bore, or anything safe. But if you're here, at the bottom of the world, holding this — then you already know what has to happen, and I won't insult you by pretending it's small."*

She wiped her hands on her apron. Behind her, rain ran down the glass.

*"Everyone told me to hide it somewhere. Bury it, burn it, forget it. But hiding things is how we got here, love. Hiding is the whole disease. So I'm doing the opposite. I'm giving it away — to whoever loves me enough to look. That's the only vault I trust."*

She smiled, and it was Ada's own crooked smile, the one she saw in tarnished mirrors and had never known where she'd stolen it from.

*"You were never meant to inherit my face or my name, sweetheart. You were meant to inherit this: the choice. So choose well. And when it costs you everything —"* the memory flickered, her mother's voice softening — *"remember that empty hands can finally hold someone."*

The memory ended.

Ada opened her eyes in the dark beneath the world, and her face was wet, and she did not remember starting to cry.

"Well," she said, voice wrecked. "That settles it."

* * *

The Wardens came down the stairs while she was still unbuckling her coat.

There were six of them, grey-cloaked, wearing borrowed faces — the miller's broad jaw, a traveler's sunburnt cheeks, faces lifted off men and women who might never have consented. Their leader had worn the face of Aldous Crane, the town clerk, and it smiled with Crane's mild, apologetic features while its eyes burned cold and certain.

"Step away from the gate," it said. "Give us the child. No one else needs to die tonight. We have already buried enough of your people — the old man died bravely, if it comforts you."

Ada thought of Tull, who had taught her to pick locks with hairpins and patience, who had called her *sparrow* for twenty years, whose body lay somewhere back in the tunnels because he had stood in a doorway that needed standing in. Something in her chest went very quiet and very hard.

"You don't want the child," she said. "You want the memory. Fine. Come and take it."

She stepped up to the arch, laid both palms flat against the silver stone, and opened herself.

She had spent her whole life taking — lifting memories with a touch, siphoning other people's yesterdays into vials, selling grief by the dram. Pouring out was the same river run backward, and it nearly killed her. The hoard tore loose all at once: the fisherman's lake, the bride's terror, the soldier's homecoming, a hundred nights of strangers' laughter, a thousand small kindnesses she had stolen and never returned. And beneath them, deepest of all, her mother's face — the real one, the living one, flour-dusted and smiling in the rain-lit kitchen — and Ada held it a moment longer than the rest, one heartbeat, two, saying goodbye the only way left to say it.

*I inherited the choice,* she thought. *This is me choosing.*

Then she let go.

The gate drank it all. Light ran along the silver roots like fire through dry grass, up the walls, into the stone, into the mountain, into the sleeping town above — and Ada felt each memory leave her and split, and split again, threading itself into ten thousand dreaming minds. A baker in his bed dreamed suddenly of a lake he'd never fished. A widow dreamed of a war older than language. Children murmured fragments of a dead tongue into their pillows. The secret of the gate scattered like seed on wind, so fine, so wide, that no hand in the world could gather it again.

"No," the Warden in Crane's face whispered. It staggered forward, reaching — and found nothing to reach for. The claim it had carried for generations, the certainty, the *ownership*, simply wasn't there anymore. There was nothing left to inherit. Nothing left to steal. Its borrowed face sagged around its fury like a mask melting.

Behind it, Wren began to sing — the dead tongue rising clear and unafraid through the cavern — and the archway sealed. Not slammed. *Sealed*, gently, the way a book closes when the story is finished, the silver roots knitting together into seamless stone. Forever, Ada understood, without knowing how she knew. The waiting behind the door would wait until the end of the world, and no one, now, would ever be able to find the key, because the key was everyone, everywhere, and therefore no one at all.

The Wardens fled. Or crumbled. Ada never learned which; she was falling too, and Bram caught her, and the last thing she saw before the dark took her was Wren's face above hers, wet-eyed and luminous, saying in perfect, newly-broken Common:

"You gave it all away. Even her."

"Yeah," Ada breathed. "Turns out that was the inheritance."

* * *

She woke to birdsong, which should have been impossible underground, and realized she wasn't underground anymore. Sunlight through a familiar window. Her own narrow bed. Someone had washed the tunnel dust from her hands, and someone else was frying onions downstairs, and the smell mixed with the birdsong into something so ordinary it made her chest ache.

On the chair beside the bed sat the locket — open, empty, silent. Whatever had lived in it was everywhere now. In the walls. In the town. In her.

Ada reached for it and found, instead of the hollow she'd braced for, a strange new quiet — room, vast and clean, where the hoard used to be. Empty hands, her mother had said. She flexed her fingers, feeling the space, and heard footsteps on the stairs, and Wren's voice calling up, bright and imperfect and entirely her own:

"Ada! Bram says breakfast, and Sefa says hurry, and — Ada, there's something growing in the garden that wasn't there yesterday, and I think it's *magic*, and I didn't touch it, I promise —"

Ada Blackwood laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the rain-lashed night a lifetime ago — and got out of bed to see what the world was becoming without her guarding it.
