## 10. Ironhollow Rising Again

The first green thing came up through the floor of the tannery yard, and everyone in Ironhollow pretended not to see it.

Wren saw it. Wren saw everything now.

She sat on the step of Ada's house with her knees pulled up and her chin on them, watching a curl of moss push through the flagstones like a small green tongue tasting the air. In the old days — the days before the gate, before the dark under the hill, before Tull's name was carved into the memorial stone by the well — nothing had grown in that yard. Nothing grew anywhere in Ironhollow except what people carried in buckets from the river and coaxed with their hands and their worrying. Now the moss came up on its own, patient as breath, and no one had asked it to.

Magic, coming home gently. That was what Sefa called it. *Gently*, she said, like it was a cat returning to a house it had once been thrown out of.

Wren did not think of it as a cat. She thought of it as water finding its level. It seeped up through the seams of the valley the way it had seeped through the mine tunnels, the way it had pooled silver-bright around her ankles while Bram carried her on his shoulders and Sefa cursed at the dark and Ada walked behind them all with her face like a closed door. The lock held now. The lock would always hold. But a lock is not a wall, and through the keyhole of the world, things were leaking back in.

Small things first. Moss. Moths with dust on their wings that glimmered faintly when the lamplight caught them wrong. Old Marta's rheumatic knee stopped aching on the third morning of spring, and she wept into her bread and didn't know why. The river ran clearer than anyone remembered, so clear you could count the stones on the bottom, and twice Wren had seen fish in it that had no business existing — long and pale and patient, with eyes like dropped coins.

And remembering. Everyone remembered now. That was the hardest part, and the best part, and Wren watched the town learn to do it the way you watch someone learn to walk on a leg that had been asleep for years.

Because that was what Ada had done. Wren knew it the way she knew her own name, which was to say: she had been given it, carefully, by people who loved her. Ada had poured out everything she had ever taken — every stolen childhood, every grief bought and sold in little glass vials, every memory hoarded against the cold — and she had fed it all into the lock beneath the valley, and the lock had scattered it like seed into ten thousand sleeping minds. No single hand could hold the secret anymore. It lived in everyone now, folded down small and deep, the way a song lives in a town after the singer has gone. The Wardens' claim had shattered because you cannot steal what belongs to everybody.

Ada had given away her mother's face too. Wren understood that part better than anyone, because Wren had been there, holding Ada's hand in the dark, feeling the moment the last of it went out of her — not like dying, exactly. Like setting down something very heavy that you have carried so long your arms have forgotten how to hang.

Afterward, Ada had stood in the ruined gate-chamber with her empty hands open at her sides, and she had looked lighter than a person could look and still be alive. And then she had looked at Wren, and Wren had said — in Common, in her own new clumsy beautiful Common — *"You still have us."*

Ada had laughed. It was an awful laugh, wet and cracked, and it was the first one Wren had ever heard from her, and Wren decided on the spot that she would spend the rest of her life hearing more of them.

* * *

Spring got louder as it went.

By the time the plum trees along Tanner's Row bloomed — real blossoms, white and reckless, the first any living soul in Ironhollow had ever seen — the whole valley smelled like something waking up. The town did not know what to do with it. Ironhollow had spent three hundred years being a place where things endured, and endurance has a certain posture to it: shoulders in, eyes down, hands busy. Now the shoulders were coming down and the eyes were lifting, and people kept stopping in the middle of streets to stare at ordinary miracles like bees.

Bram said the town looked like a man who'd been told he was forgiven for a crime he couldn't remember committing.

Sefa said that was exactly what it was.

They argued about it every evening over supper, which was how they loved each other. Supper was at Ada's table now, all of them, every night, because the house had grown somehow — or maybe just because no one wanted to leave. Sefa had claimed the corner by the hearth for her forgeries-in-progress, though she'd started doing honest work too, restoring the old guild seals the town suddenly remembered it had. Bram ran his fence business out of the front room and complained daily that there was nothing left worth fencing, since half the town kept giving things away for free. Old habits. He still checked the window latch four times a night. He still flinched at grey cloaks. But he'd taught Wren to play knucklebones, and he let her win, and everyone pretended not to notice that he let her win, which was its own kind of tenderness.

Tull's chair stayed empty. They didn't move it. Sometimes Wren sat in it, and nobody said anything, and that was how they kept him.

Wren was learning fast. Her Common came easier every week, words stacking up inside her like the spring water rising in the wells. But some nights she dreamed in the old tongue — the dead language that wasn't dead anymore, that lived now in her mouth alone, the last door of the Sundering that had never quite shut. In her dreams she spoke to the valley itself, and the valley answered in growing things.

On the morning the plum trees bloomed, she walked down Tanner's Row barefoot — shoes were a battle she was losing on purpose — and touched the lowest branch, and a blossom opened under her finger like a small held breath released.

Old Marta saw her do it. Marta didn't scream or fetch the reeve. She just pressed her hand to her mouth, and then she smiled at Wren with her whole ruined, remembering face, and went inside to tell her daughter that the world was going to be strange again, and that maybe strange was not the same as terrible.

That was the thing about the secret living in everyone: nobody owned it, so nobody feared it quite the same way. Some people in Ironhollow were angry. Some were frightened. A few packed their carts and left for the low country, and good riddance, Bram said, though he watched them go a long time. But most of them simply began, slowly, to live differently — leaving offerings of bread at the old shrine stones they'd forgotten were shrines, teaching their children the names of the hills, being kinder to strangers for reasons they couldn't explain. Remembering changes a body. Wren watched it happen the way she'd watched the moss come: patiently, seam by seam.

* * *

Ada was different, and Wren studied her the way she studied everything, quietly and completely.

She was emptier. Anyone could see it. She moved through rooms like a woman who had taken off armor and hadn't yet learned where to put her elbows. She forgot things — small things, harmless things, where she'd set her cup, what day the market was — and each forgetting made her flinch a little, and then made her smile a little, because she had chosen these gaps. She had dug them herself and poured herself out into them.

But she was learning to be filled by other means.

Wren found her one evening on the back step, watching the last light go gold over the ridge where Tull had died, and she climbed up beside her without asking, the way family does. For a while neither of them spoke. Below them the town was lighting its lamps one by one, and above the lamps the first stars were coming out, and between the lamps and the stars the moths drifted with their faint borrowed shine.

"I used to know what every star was called," Ada said at last. "My mother taught me. Before." Her voice did something complicated on the word *before*. "I gave that up too. I keep reaching for the names and finding nothing there."

Wren considered this with the seriousness it deserved. Then she pointed up, at the brightest one hanging over the eastern ridge.

"That one's the Lantern," she said in Common. "Sefa told me. It's for people who walk other people home in the dark."

Ada looked at the star. Then at Wren. Something moved across her face that was not quite grief and not quite laughter but lived in the country between them, where most true things live.

"No," Ada said softly. "It isn't. But it is now."

She put her arm around Wren, and Wren leaned into the hollow space where all the stolen years used to be, and fit there perfectly, the way a key fits a lock that has finally, finally been turned the right way.

Down in the town, the lamps burned. Up on the ridge, something small and bright and green — a foxfire bloom, the first of the season — opened in the dark grass where no flower should grow, and no one saw it but the valley, and the valley kept it.

And far to the south, past the passes where the grey riders had come from, a letter was being written by candlelight in a hand that shook. It said only: *The gate holds. The child lives. Send no one.* It was signed with a name that had waited three hundred years to be spoken kindly again.

But that is another story, and the valley, for once, was in no hurry.

Spring rose in Ironhollow, and it rose again, and it kept rising — and Ada Blackwood, who had inherited nothing and lost everything and chosen both, sat on her own back step with her arms full of the only inheritance that had ever mattered, and watched the light come home.
