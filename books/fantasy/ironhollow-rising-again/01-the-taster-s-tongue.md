## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The bell over the kitchen door rang at half past five, as it always did, and Sable Quintana was already awake, as he always was.

He lay for a moment in the narrow dark of his room above the stables and listened to Ironhollow waking. The valley had a particular silence—not the silence of emptiness but the silence of something pressing down gently from all sides, the way snow presses on a roof. The mountains closed around the town like cupped hands, and whatever wild things lived beyond them—whatever songs and storms and strange bright hungers the old stories spoke of—none of it ever got in. No charms worked here. No curses took root. A hedge-witch from the lowlands had once tried to light a candle with a word in the market square, and the candle had simply sat there, wick and wax and nothing more, while the crowd laughed at her and she wept like a woman whose child had died.

Sable had watched that happen when he was nine years old, standing beside his mother's vegetable cart, and he had understood even then that the valley was not empty of magic. It was *proofed* against it, the way a cellar is proofed against damp. Something about the stone, the water, the air itself. The king's grandfather had chosen Ironhollow for exactly that reason, three generations back, after the Mage Wars had taught the world what a poisoned word could do. Here, if you wanted to kill a man, you had to do it the honest way. With steel, or with something he swallowed.

Which was why Sable Quintana had a job, and why the job had killed four men before him, and why he rose each morning before the birds and put his tongue to work.

* * *

The kitchens of Greyhollow Keep were already roaring when he came down. Cooks moved through steam and lamplight like fish through current, and the head cook, Marta Ilse—a broad, gray woman with forearms like hams and a memory for grudges longer than the king's reign—nodded him toward the tasting table without a word. She never spoke to him before the first dish. Superstition, she claimed. Bad luck to greet the taster too warmly; you might be greeting him for the last time.

Sable rather liked her for it.

The breakfast was simple by royal standards: barley porridge with honey and cream, smoked river trout, brown bread still warm enough to fog a knife, small beer, and a compote of preserved plums that the king's physician insisted aided digestion and which everyone else knew existed only because the king liked them. Seven dishes. Sable arranged them in the order they would be served and stood over them a moment, letting his eyes do the first pass.

Eyes first. Always eyes first. Poison that showed itself in color or sheen or an oily ring at the surface was poison meant for amateurs, and in twenty-two years of service Sable had caught perhaps forty such attempts, most of them lazy, two of them genuinely clever. There had been the morning someone had dusted the trout skin with powdered foxglove fine enough to look like pepper. There had been the plum compote, three winters ago, sweetened with honey cut from rhododendron hives—the madness-honey, the shepherds called it—and it had taken him two full minutes of staring to see that the honey poured just slightly too slowly, like syrup with a secret.

Nothing looked wrong today. He picked up the silver spoon—he owned six spoons, one for each course a king might be served, because wood held flavors and iron could lie—and tasted the porridge.

Barley, salt, cream, honey. The honey was clover, this batch, darker than the last. Warmth spreading across his tongue. He held it in his mouth and counted to thirty, feeling for numbness, for heat, for the faint metallic whisper that meant heavy metals. Nothing. He swallowed and waited another hundred heartbeats, watching the surface of the remaining bowl for any bloom or film.

Then the trout. Then the bread. Then the beer, swished and spat into the slop pail, because alcohol slowed the tongue and he could not afford a slow tongue. Then the plums.

It was the plums.

Not the fruit—the syrup. As he drew the spoon from his mouth he felt it, faint as a thought at the edge of sleep: a dryness at the very root of his tongue, a tightness in the hinge of his jaw. Most men would have called it nothing. Most men would have been dead within the month, because arsenic is patient. It does not announce itself. It moves in like a poor relation, quietly, and settles into the hair and nails and gut, and one day the host takes to bed with pains no physician can name and wastes away while the priests shrug and the heirs count spoons.

Sable set down the spoon with great care, the way a man sets down a sleeping infant, and looked at the compote.

Someone had laced the syrup. Not enough to kill quickly—enough to accumulate, dose by dose, until the king's death looked like age or illness. Which meant it was not a hot-blooded attempt. It was a *planned* one, planned by someone with access to the preserves over weeks, someone patient, someone who knew the kitchen rhythms well enough to doctor a jar without Marta Ilse catching them.

His heart did not speed up. That was the strangest thing, the thing he never told anyone: in these moments his heart went slower, his hands went quieter, as if some deep animal part of him understood that stillness was survival. He capped the jar, carried it to the cold room, and locked it in his own chest—the taster's private lockbox, warranted by the crown, opened by no one but him and the king's justice. Then he washed his mouth with vinegar water, chewed a heel of clean bread, and drank a full glass of the goat's milk he kept for himself alone, chalky and vile, because milk bound what little arsenic might have kissed his throat.

Twenty-two years of practice. Twenty-two years of dosing himself in increments so small his body learned each poison the way a scholar learns a language—one syllable at a time, until the whole alphabet sat in his blood.

* * *

By seven he was standing in the antechamber outside the king's morning room, hands folded, face arranged into its usual expression: pleasant, forgettable, slightly dull. It was a crafted face. Sable had spent years building it the way he'd built his immunities, adding a layer here, sanding a feature there, until what remained was a man nobody looked at twice. Average height. Brown hair going gray at the temples in a way that suggested tiredness rather than distinction. Eyes people described afterward as "some shade." He dressed well but not too well, spoke when spoken to, laughed at the right moments with the wrong amount of enthusiasm, and had perfected the art of ending conversations by seeming eager to continue them.

Invisibility, his mother had told him once, is not the absence of being seen. It is the presence of being *dismissed*. She'd said it while selling turnips, laughing, and he hadn't understood until much later that she was handing him the single most valuable lesson of his life.

"Quintana." The chamberlain, Lord Aldery, swept past without slowing. "His majesty is irritable. The joints again."

"The plums are off," Sable said. "I've sequestered the jar. Recommend bread and cheese and the small beer only, and I'll taste fresh everything within the hour."

Aldery paused. For a moment something flickered behind his long face—not gratitude, exactly. Relief, maybe, or the particular discomfort of a courtier reminded that the quiet man in the corner kept them all alive. "Off," he repeated. "How?"

"Badly," Sable said, which was true, and said nothing else, which was policy.

Poison in the royal household was politics, and politics was not the taster's business. His business ended at the tongue's edge. Whoever had laced the compote would learn within hours that the attempt had failed, and they would wonder how, and they would begin to watch the quiet man—and so the quiet man must remain exactly as quiet as ever, offering no clues, taking no sides, becoming again the furniture everyone forgot. He had survived four predecessors' worth of intrigue by understanding one thing: the taster who becomes interesting becomes dead.

King Edric the Second received his breakfast in the morning room with its tall windows facing the eastern wall of the valley, where the sun came late and left early, caught between mountains nine months of the year. The king was sixty-one, thick through the middle, fond of dogs and grievances, and he waved Sable to his customary place at the sideboard with the absent gesture of a man acknowledging a chair.

"Well?" the king said, around bread and cheese. "Marta says the plums turned."

"They did, your majesty. I'd not trust that supplier again."

"Mm. Send the bill to the steward and let him bleed the man." The king chewed, scowled at the window, at the mountains, at the gray-gold light. "You look well, Quintana. Better than me. What's your secret?"

"Goat's milk, your majesty."

The king snorted. Around them the morning room filled and emptied—secretaries, a captain of the guard, a nervous young lord with a petition. Sable tasted each new dish as it arrived, nodded or shook his head, and was, within ten minutes, entirely forgotten, which was precisely as he intended. He stood in the corner with his folded hands and his forgettable face and he watched the room the way he watched every room: cataloguing who touched what, who avoided whose eyes, who had come to breakfast angry.

He noticed the young lord with the petition glancing at the sideboard where the plums should have been. Noticed it, filed it away, and let it go. Not his business. Never his business.

* * *

The rest of the day unspooled in its familiar grooves. Midday meal for the king and six courtiers—tasted, cleared. Afternoon: the inventory of his lockbox, the ledger of doses, the careful maintenance of his own immunity the way a smith maintains his tools. This was the part of the work no one saw and no one thanked him for, and it was the part that mattered most.

Every poison was a debt, and the body paid interest forever. Arsenic required a maintenance dose monthly or the tolerance thinned. Foxglove, he refreshed quarterly, working up from grains of dust to leaves steeped like tea, his heart stumbling and steadying, stumbling and steadying, until it learned the rhythm of the drug the way a sailor learns a deck. Hemlock. Belladonna, which turned his vision silver at the edges for a day and made him lie in the dark listening to his own blood. Cantharides, aconite, the madness-honey, seven strains of mushroom toxin, and the slow gray family of metals—lead, mercury, antimony—that settled into bone and never fully left.

There were nights, in the early years, when he had curled around the cramps and sweats and wondered what he was becoming—a man so full of poison that anything that tried to kill him found the ground already occupied. His mother had been alive then. She had sat with him through the worst of the belladonna trials, cool cloth on his forehead, and she had said, in her turnip-cart way: *You're not filling yourself with poison, love. You're teaching your body the whole country so no stranger can ever draw a map of it.*

She had been dead eleven years now. Buried on the western slope under a stone he'd carved himself, because masons charged by the letter and she'd have hated the expense. He visited at the turning of each season and told her about the poisons, because she was the only one who had ever found the work interesting instead of grim.

At dusk he walked the wall path above the town, as he always did, and looked out at Ironhollow settling into evening. Lamplight kindling street by street. Smoke rising straight in the windless air. Somewhere a fiddle, badly played. The valley floor was a green ribbon eight miles long and two wide, and beyond it the mountains went up and up into rock and then into cloud, and beyond *that*—according to the stories, according to the peddlers and pilgrims who sometimes came through the blind pass and were searched and questioned and sent on their way—beyond that was a world where words could light candles and strangers could dream your future and kings fell to curses as easily as to knives.

Sable had never left. Forty-one years old, and he had never once crossed the border stones.

People assumed it was fear. It wasn't, quite. It was arithmetic. Outside, he was nothing—a taster without a king, a man whose entire fortune was locked in his bloodstream, useless against steel and spell alike. Inside, he was necessary. Inside, the valley's dead magic meant his particular genius was the only shield that counted, and the crown paid him well, and the routine held him like a hand. Breakfast at half past five. The wall path at dusk. The goat's milk. The ledger. The same dishes, the same faces, the same quiet pressing down like snow on a roof, keeping the world out.

Lonely? Yes. But loneliness, Sable had decided somewhere in his thirties, was simply the price of safety, paid in small daily installments, and he had never missed a payment yet.

He was halfway down the wall stairs when the storm broke—early, violent, the kind of mountain squall that turned the streets to rivers in minutes. Rain hit the flagstones like flung gravel. Lanterns guttered. And through the gate below, through the streaming torchlight, he heard the guards shouting at something that had just dragged itself out of the dark.

He went down toward it before he had decided to. Later he would understand that this was the moment everything turned—this one small failure of his discipline, this one step taken without counting the cost—but standing there in the rain with his collar up and his forgettable face streaming wet, all he knew was that the shape collapsed against the gatepost was a man, and the man was dying, and dying men made sounds that Sable had heard too many times to walk away from.

"Keep back," a guard was saying, halfheartedly, to no one in particular. "Some mad beggar. Burned half to death. Probably plague."

"He doesn't smell of plague," Sable said, kneeling, rain hammering his shoulders. "He smells of yew and something mineral. Hold the lantern higher."

The courier's lips were gray. His pupils were pinpoints. Under the burns and mud his skin had gone the color of candle tallow, and when Sable peeled back an eyelid and saw the tremor of the iris beneath, he felt the old cold recognition move through him like a hand through water.

He knew this poison. Or rather, he knew its *family*—and he had never once managed to build a tolerance to this branch, because the plant it came from didn't grow within a hundred miles of Ironhollow, and a man cannot dose himself against a poison he cannot obtain.

The courier's cracked lips moved. Rain ran into his mouth. His hand crawled across the wet cobbles toward Sable's wrist with the terrible patience of the dying, and his eyes—fever-bright, burned at one edge, sane at the center—found Sable's face and held it.

"You're the taster," the man whispered. "They said. They said find the man who eats poison for breakfast."
