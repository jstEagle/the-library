## 2. A Stranger at the Gate

The storm came up the valley an hour past dusk, and Sable felt it before he heard it—a pressure behind his teeth, a faint sourness on his tongue like copper left too long in water. Twenty years of tasting had taught him that weather announced itself chemically. Rain carried iron. Lightning carried ozone and something sharper, almost sweet. This storm carried all of it at once, and beneath it, faint as a whisper under a door, something he could not name.

He was washing the night's dishes in the taster's quarters below the keep when the first thunder cracked over Ironhollow's rim. The valley swallowed sound the way it swallowed everything else—magic, rumor, the outside world—and even thunder arrived here muted, polite, a lion taught to purr. Sable dried his hands, banked the fire, and told himself the ache in his chest was only the season turning.

He had learned long ago not to trust feelings that arrived without cause. In his trade, every sensation had to be interrogated. Numbness in the lips meant nightshade or its cousins. A bloom of warmth behind the eyes meant something worse. The body spoke honestly; it was everything else—kings, courtiers, friends—that lied. So he sat with the ache, named it loneliness out of habit, and dismissed it the way he dismissed all things that could not be weighed on a scale or held under the tongue.

The knock came at midnight.

Three blows, uneven, the last one sliding down the door as though the knocker had slid down with it. Sable went still, one hand drifting to the shelf where he kept his working vials—not the poisons, but the antidotes, chalk-white and amber and the bitter green he brewed himself from valerian root and something he'd never been able to identify, which worried him more than any poison ever had.

No one knocked on the taster's door at midnight. No one knocked on the taster's door at all. He was a function of the palace, like the well pulley or the gate chain. You did not knock on a well pulley.

The knock came again, weaker.

He opened the door on a wall of rain and a man who fell through it into his arms.

* * *

The courier—he would learn later that he had been a courier, though by then it hardly mattered—was a ruin of a person. His cloak had been burned off him in patches, the leather fused to the skin beneath in places, and what skin showed was the color of old candle wax. Rain steamed off him. That was the detail that stopped Sable's breath: the rain *steamed*, hissing faintly where it struck the man's shoulders, as if his body were a forge left banked overnight.

Sable got him onto the cot by the fire, and the man's eyes opened—white-rimmed, darting, fixed on nothing and everything—and he began to talk.

"Ember," he said. "Ember and hoard. Ember and—" A cough tore through him, wet and deep. "They're coming. They know. They know I know."

"Hush." Sable pressed two fingers to the man's wrist and read the pulse the way he read everything: patiently, without flinching. Too fast. Thready at the edges, like thread about to part. He peeled back the man's eyelids and found the pupils blown wide and unequal, the left lagging behind the right like a drunk trying to follow a conversation.

His stomach dropped.

He knew this picture. He had built a life on knowing this picture. Unequal pupils, waxy skin, sweat that smelled wrong—sweet underneath, like paraffin and rotting fruit—the tremor starting in the jaw and working outward. He had catalogued a hundred poisons in twenty years of service, had dosed himself with most of them in increments small enough to survive, and this one he knew by reputation alone because no one survived enough of it to study.

Thorn-wake. The slow toxin. The one they said the southern kingdoms used on couriers who might talk, because it killed over days instead of hours, and a man dying slowly could still be made to walk, still be made to lead his hunters wherever they wished.

"Who gave you something to drink?" Sable asked quietly. "Or eat. Think. Three days ago. Four."

The man laughed, and the laugh became another cough, and the cough brought blood at the corner of his mouth, dark as ink in the firelight. "Everyone gives you something," he rasped. "That's how you know you're carrying something worth taking. Nobody poisons a man with empty hands."

Sable had spent his whole life being careful precisely so that no one would ever say those words about him. Now a stranger had said them in his own room, and the truth of them settled into his chest like a stone into a well.

He should call the guards. That was the correct action, the safe action, the action that kept Sable Quintana invisible. A burned man collapsing at the gate in a storm was a matter for Captain Aldery and her watchmen, for the palace physician, for the machinery of the crown that ground slow and fine and noticed everyone it touched. If Sable reported him, Sable would be questioned. If Sable was questioned, Sable would be *seen*. And seen men were remembered men, and remembered men in Ironhollow ended up either promoted or dead, and Sable had worked very hard for twenty years to earn neither.

But the man's hand shot out and caught Sable's sleeve with a grip that had no business being that strong, and his fever-bright eyes found Sable's face, and he said:

"You taste things. I can smell it on you. Bitterroot, graycap, the arsenic they put in the king's wine last spring—you smell like all of it. You're the taster." His voice dropped to a whisper frayed to almost nothing. "Then you'll know what's killing me. And you'll know there's no cure. So don't waste my time pretending."

Sable did not move. The fire popped. Outside, the storm hammered the valley walls, and somewhere above, thunder rolled through the pass like a cart over cobblestones.

"I can ease it," Sable said at last. "Not stop it. Ease it."

"Then do that," the man said, "and listen while you work. Because I've got maybe a day in me, and I didn't crawl across half the world to die in a palace basement full of bottles."

* * *

Sable worked while the man talked, and the talking was mostly nonsense stitched through with sense, the way fever-talk always is.

He brewed willow bark and meadowsweet for the pain. He crushed charcoal for the stomach, though he knew it was like bailing the sea with a cup. He mixed his bitter green tonic—the mystery one, the one he'd never identified—and held it to the man's cracked lips, and watched some of the tremor drain out of the man's jaw like water from a punctured skin.

In exchange, the man gave him fragments.

*Ember.* Said like a prayer, once, and like a curse, twice. *The hoard.* Said with such longing that Sable's skin prickled. *Ashen Peaks.* *She's guttering, she's guttering, someone has to bring it back to her.* And once, very clearly, staring at the ceiling as though reading words written there: *"Magic isn't gone. It's just waiting. It's all just waiting for the fire."*

Sable had grown up on stories like that, except in Ironhollow the stories were forbidden, which made them better. Every child in the valley learned early that magic could not cross the border stones—that the valley was a hole in the world's fabric, a place where spells died and sorcerers were merely madmen and dragons were nursery tales. The elders said it plainly: magic cannot enter Ironhollow. They never said why. They never said whether magic existed anywhere else at all, and the not-saying was itself a kind of answer, and every child understood it, and every child grew up and stopped asking.

Sable had stopped asking at nine years old, the day his mother left. Or was taken. The story changed depending on which aunt told it, and eventually he'd stopped listening to that too.

"You're not listening," the courier accused, gripping his wrist again. "You've got your listening face on but you're not *listening*. Listen: they burned the others. All the other carriers. I'm the last one and I'm burning too, just slower, just from inside." He tried to laugh again and managed half of it. "Clever, isn't it? Fire outside for the ones who run. Fire inside for the ones who hide."

"The Ashcloaks," Sable said. The name surfaced from the same deep shelf of childhood stories—zealots who hated magic, who burned witches and mages and anyone the fire whispered against. He'd always assumed they were invented, like dragons. A story needed villains, and children needed warnings.

The courier's eyes sharpened. "You know them?"

"I know the word."

"Then you know more than the people hunting me think you do, and less than I need you to." He sagged back against the cot, exhausted by three sentences, and for a long moment there was only the storm and the wet rasp of his breathing. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—quieter, saner, a man surfacing briefly from deep water. "Taster. What's your name?"

"Sable."

"Sable." He tried it out, frowning. "Good name for a man who swallows death for a living. Salt preserves. Salt remembers." His eyes drifted shut. "My name doesn't matter anymore. Names are how they find you."

"Rest, then."

"No." The eyes opened again, fierce. "No rest. Rest is how they get the map." His hand crawled across the blanket toward the ruined satchel he'd collapsed wearing, a blackened leather thing buckled tight across his chest. "I need you to understand something, Sable-the-taster. I didn't come here by accident. The map brought me. It wanted to come inside the quiet place, where their fires can't follow, where their seeing can't reach. Nothing magic crosses your border—which means nothing that *tracks* magic crosses it either. Your valley is the one blind spot in the world." A ghost of a smile. "And blind spots are where you hide the things you love."

Sable's mouth had gone dry. "What map?"

The courier looked at him for a long moment, and whatever he saw in Sable's face seemed to decide something. "The kind of map," he said, "that a dying man gives to a stranger. Because a stranger is the only kind of person left you can trust. Everyone else already belongs to somebody."

* * *

Toward dawn the fever broke—or rather crested, which Sable knew was worse, the wave going up before it came all the way down. The courier raved about roads of ash and a queen made of embers, about a sister-city drowned in salt, about voices in mountains. Sable sat beside him and did the only thing he knew how to do: he monitored, measured, adjusted. Doses of willow. Cool cloths. Charcoal and honey. His hands moved with the calm of twenty years' practice while his mind ran in circles around a single question, over and over, like a dog pacing a kennel:

*Why am I doing this?*

Every instinct he had honed said give the man to the guards. Let the crown's machinery take him. Be a bystander in your own story; it's the safest role there is. He had watched a kitchen boy hanged once for stealing bread, and the lesson had never left him: in Ironhollow, involvement was the only crime that mattered.

And yet here he sat, wringing cloths at four in the morning for a stranger whose death would draw questions like a wound draws flies.

Because the man was poisoned, and Sable knew poison, and knowing a thing created obligations. That was the whole rotten architecture of his life. He had swallowed arsenic so the king wouldn't have to; he had memorized a hundred deaths so that others wouldn't die of them. Somewhere along the way the skill had grown roots, and now compassion grew out of it like a weed through flagstones, and he couldn't pull it up without pulling up everything else.

The courier surfaced near dawn, lucid for perhaps a minute, and caught Sable's hand.

"They'll ask questions," he said. "When they find me. Questions are their knives. Don't answer straight—straight answers are how they carve you into shapes they can use." His grip tightened, shockingly strong. "And Sable. Whatever happens. Whatever they offer you. Remember that the fire chooses its keeper, and it chose badly this time, and I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I'd have chosen a braver man—"

Then the fever took him again, and the words dissolved into muttering, and Sable sat alone in the grey light with his heart hammering and the phrase *the fire chooses its keeper* repeating in his skull like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

* * *

The storm broke mid-morning, and with the storm's breaking came the sound Sable had been dreading since midnight: boots in the corridor. Many boots. The particular cadence of soldiers walking with purpose rather than routine.

He had perhaps thirty seconds. He used twenty-nine of them.

The courier went into the old wine cellar behind the false shelf—a relic of some previous taster's paranoia that Sable had always assumed was excessive. The satchel went with him, wrapped in oilcloth. The bloody cloths went into the stove. The bitter green bottle went back on its shelf between the valerian and the chalk, one bottle among forty, anonymous.

On the thirtieth second, the knock came at his door—hard, official, nothing like the broken sliding knocks of the night before.

Sable opened it to find Captain Aldery herself, rain-damp, flanked by two watchmen, and behind them, at the end of the corridor, a figure he didn't recognize: a woman in travel-stained grey with the stillness of a drawn bow, watching him with eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"Beg pardon, Master Quintana." Aldery's face was carefully blank, which from Aldery meant trouble. "There was a man seen at the gate last night, during the storm. Burned, half-dead, raving. The gate watch lost him in the rain." Her eyes moved past Sable's shoulder, into the room, methodical as a searchlight. "You didn't hear anything? See anything?"

The lie assembled itself in Sable's mouth before he consciously decided to tell it, smooth and complete, the way an antidote assembles from memory: "Storm woke me around midnight. Thunder in the pass. I checked my stores and went back to sleep. I hear nothing in this room but my own heart, Captain—it's how I stay alive."

It was, technically, true. Every word of it. He had simply arranged the truths into a shape that concealed, the way a poisoner arranges harmless ingredients into something lethal.

Aldery studied him a moment longer. Then she nodded, curt, and turned away—and as she did, the woman in grey at the end of the hall stepped forward, unhurried, and looked at Sable directly for the first time.

"Master Quintana," she said. Her voice was pleasant. That was the worst thing about it. "Dame Corvane. I'm investigating a theft. Perhaps you'll have time for a few questions later, when your duties allow."

Behind the false wine shelf, in the dark, a dying man began softly to sing.
