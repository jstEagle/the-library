## 4. Questions in the Great Hall

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a page who wouldn't meet Sable's eyes.

"His Majesty's investigator requests the presence of all household staff in the Great Hall. All of them, Master Quintana. Even the taster."

*Even the taster.* As if his office were a courtesy rather than a necessity. Sable dressed in his plainest grey — he owned nothing else — and checked on the courier one last time before he went. The man lay in the root cellar beneath the pantry, cold now, wrapped in the sacking Sable had meant for turnips. He had died in the night with the map's scorched corner pressed against Sable's palm like a brand, and Sable had spent the small hours deciding where to hide it. The answer had come to him the way most answers came: somewhere no one would ever willingly look.

He had swallowed it.

Not the paper itself — that would have killed him faster than any poison, and besides, he needed to read it again. But he had hidden it inside the one place in Ironhollow that belonged wholly to him and that no sane person would search: his poison cabinet. Forty small drawers, each labeled in his own cramped hand, each holding death in measured grains. He'd slid the map flat into the drawer marked *aconite — do not open without gloves*, and locked the cabinet, and stood there in the dark listening to his own heartbeat and thinking, *this is what it feels like when your life stops being safe.*

Now the page was waiting, and the king's men were already in the hall.

* * *

The Great Hall of Ironhollow Keep had been built by people who feared nothing, which is to say by people who had never lived anywhere else. Its windows were tall and unshuttered; its doors stood open summer and winter alike. Why bar a door in a valley where magic could not enter? What sorcery could reach past the border stones? The architects had never imagined a threat that walked in on two legs wearing a crown's insignia.

Dame Corvane sat at the high table as though it were hers.

She was not what Sable expected. He had imagined an investigator as a large man with a loud voice, someone who filled rooms by force. Corvane took up almost no space at all. She was slight, grey-eyed, perhaps forty, with her hair bound back so tightly it looked painted on. She wore no armor, only a travel-stained coat of royal blue, and she had arranged before her on the table three objects: a scrap of burned cloth, a broken courier's seal, and a single boot, scorched black along one side.

She did not look up when the household filed in. She let them stand in rows — kitchen girls and gate guards, the steward, the under-cook, Sable at the end near the wall where he always stood — and she studied her three objects in silence until the silence itself became a question none of them could answer.

Then she looked up, and her gaze went down the line of them slowly, the way a physician's fingers go down a body feeling for the break.

"Three nights ago," she said, "a rider crossed the border stones into this valley. His name was Aldous Merrin. He carried a royal dispatch case and a wound that should have killed him a day sooner than it did." She touched the boot. "These were found in the river gorge, four miles east of here. The river runs toward Ironhollow. Rivers are honest things — they carry what they're given." Her voice never rose. It didn't need to. "So. Someone in this room gave him shelter, or watched him die in the street, or dragged his body into the hills. I will know which."

No one spoke. The under-cook began to cry quietly, which Sable supposed was honest, though useless.

Corvane waited out the weeping with the patience of a woman who had outlasted worse. Then: "I'll speak to each of you alone. In order of proximity to the gate." Her eyes moved down the row and stopped — not on Sable, not yet, but near enough that he felt the heat of it. "We'll begin with the gate watch."

* * *

They took the guards first, then the gate sergeant, then the steward, each summoned into the antechamber off the hall while the rest stood waiting in enforced silence. Sable used the time the way he used all time: cataloguing.

He had spent nineteen years making himself unremarkable, and he knew precisely how the work was done. Stand at the edge of rooms. Speak when spoken to, briefly, in a voice pitched neither high nor low. Keep your hands still. Never be the first to finish eating or the last. Wear grey. The trick, he had learned early, was not to hide — hiding drew the eye — but to be *already seen*, already categorized, already dismissed. The food-taster. The quiet one. The man who swallows poison for a living and therefore cannot possibly be interesting.

But Corvane had said *even the taster*, and something in the way she'd arranged her three little relics on the table told him she did not dismiss anyone. That was the trouble with investigators. They were paid to find the interesting in the dull.

"Sable Quintana."

He followed the guard into the antechamber. It was a small room, windowless, with two chairs and a brazier. Corvane had taken the chair facing the door. She gestured to the other and he sat, keeping his spine easy, his hands loose on his knees.

"You've served the crown eleven years," she said. No preamble, no warmth. "As taster."

"Eleven years in the spring," Sable said. "Yes, Dame."

"And before that?"

"I apprenticed to the apothecary on Tallow Street. He taught me herbs. The crown found the knowledge useful."

"Mm." She opened a leather folio on her knee. Inside, he glimpsed pages of close writing — names, dates, sums. His wages, he realized. Someone had written down what he earned every quarter for eleven years, and here it was, in another woman's hands. "You dosed yourself. To build tolerance. That's the practice, isn't it? Small poisons, increased over years, until the body learns."

"It's the practice," Sable agreed. "Though 'learns' flatters it. Mostly it endures."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile. "And you've never been sick. Never failed. Eleven years, and the king has eaten every meal you've approved."

"He has."

"That's remarkable discipline."

"It's remarkable fear," Sable said. "Discipline is just fear that's learned manners."

This time the flicker stayed a moment longer. She closed the folio. "Where were you on the night of the storm?"

"The night the courier would have arrived." He let the pause sit, then answered plainly, because he had decided in the antechamber doorway that plainness was his best armor. "In my quarters above the pantry. I heard the storm. I heard the commotion at the gate later — everyone did. I did not go to it."

"Why not?"

"Because it wasn't my post. Because a crowd at the gate in a storm is either a drunk or a corpse, and both are the guard's problem." He shrugged slightly. "Because I'm careful, Dame Investigator. You seem to have read that about me already."

"I have." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time her grey eyes fixed on him fully, and Sable understood why the household came out of that room looking scoured. There was no cruelty in her gaze. That was what made it terrible. It was simply thorough, the way fire is thorough. "Aldous Merrin was poisoned, Master Quintana. Slowly, over weeks, with something subtle. Something that mimics wasting sickness until the last day. Do you know what that suggests to me?"

"A patient enemy."

"A knowledgeable one." Her head tilted a fraction. "There can't be more than a handful of people in this valley who could design such a thing. You may be one of them."

"I could design a great many deaths," Sable said. "It's my trade. So could any army surgeon, any herbalist, any desperate wife with a garden. Knowledge isn't guilt, Dame. If it were, you'd have to arrest yourself first."

The silence stretched. Somewhere beyond the wall, the hall full of servants held its breath; he could feel it through the stone, a hundred heartbeats trying to be quiet.

Corvane rose. "Thank you for your time, Master Quintana. I may have more questions."

"I'm always available," Sable said, and stood, and bowed exactly as deeply as protocol required, and walked out of the antechamber with his pulse hammering so hard against his ribs that he marveled no one in the hall could hear it.

*That was the first lie,* he thought, standing back in his place against the wall while the next name was called. *Every word of it.*

Because he *had* gone to the gate. He had carried the courier in through the rain. He had held the man's head while the poison finished its slow arithmetic, and he had buried the body in a root cellar like a secret instead of a man, and the map was sitting in a drawer of aconite not thirty feet from where he stood.

And the worst of it — the part that made his stomach turn even now — was how easily it had come. The steady voice. The shrug. *It wasn't my post.* Nineteen years of swallowing poison had taught him more than immunity. It had taught him that the body can be trained to hold anything, even falsehood, even shame, if you dose it small enough and often enough.

He was good at lying. He hated that he was good at it. And underneath the hate, quieter and colder, something else was waking up — the same thing that woke when he lifted a cup to his lips and tasted hemlock in the wine. Not panic. Calculation.

*If she finds the cellar, I'm dead. If she finds the cabinet, I'm dead. Therefore the cellar must stop being a cellar, and the cabinet must stop being mine.*

Guilt, he discovered, was just resolve that hadn't picked a direction yet.

* * *

The interrogations lasted until well past noon. When they finally released the household, the corridors felt stripped bare, as if Corvane's questions had been a wind that blew through every room and rearranged everything slightly, so that home no longer fit quite right.

Sable went down to the kitchens because going anywhere unusual would be unusual. He ate bread and dripping standing up, tasting nothing, while around him the kitchen girls whispered in fragments.

"—asked me what the storm sounded like, why would she care what the storm—"

"—she knew Marta's sister's name. How does she know—"

"—the taster was in there longest, I heard, the taster was in there—"

He set down his bread. *The taster was in there longest.* Of course they'd counted. Of course they'd noticed. A valley full of people whose whole culture was noticing — that was what living without magic made of you, he thought suddenly. When nothing could be warded or charmed or foreseen, you learned to watch. Ironhollow's safety had bred a nation of sentries, and he had spent nineteen years among sentries pretending to be furniture.

He washed his plate, dried it, put it away. Then he climbed to his quarters, unlocked the poison cabinet with hands that did not shake — he made sure of that, made himself watch them not shake — and stood a long moment with the aconite drawer open, the map's charred edge just visible beneath the folded cloth of gloves.

He didn't take it out. Taking it out was what a guilty man would do, and a guilty man was what Corvane was hunting. Instead he memorized it again, the way he'd memorized it in the dead hours: the valley's border stones drawn as a circle of small crosses, the road east through the blind pass, the mountains rendered in faded ink that seemed almost to smolder. Names in a hand that trembled — the courier's, or someone else's. *Emberhoard.* And beneath it, smaller, half-burned away, words he hadn't been able to read until tonight, when memory supplied what the fire had eaten:

*The fire chooses its keeper.*

His mother's phrase. His mother's exact phrase, in the cadence she'd used for the stories she told him at night, the ones about the world outside the valley, the ones he'd assumed were inventions, comforts, the embroidery of a woman who missed a home she never named.

Sable closed the drawer. Locked the cabinet. Sat down on his narrow bed in his narrow room above the pantry, in the safest valley in the world, and felt the safety close around him like a lid.

Below, in the courtyard, he heard horses. Many horses. He went to the slit window and looked down and saw Corvane's escort doubled — new riders in royal blue, mud-spattered, leading pack animals loaded with equipment he couldn't identify at this distance except that some of it gleamed, and some of it was very long, and none of it looked like luggage for a short stay.

She wasn't passing through. She was settling in.

And pinned to the notice board by the gate, where every soul in the keep would walk past it before supper, was a fresh sheet of vellum bearing the royal seal and, in a clerk's neat hand, a proclamation:

*By order of Dame Corvane, Investigator of the Crown: a reward of fifty crowns for information concerning the whereabouts of the courier Aldous Merrin, alive or dead. Any person harboring knowledge of his passage and concealing it shall be judged guilty of treason against the realm.*

Fifty crowns. Half a lifetime of a taster's wages, offered to whoever remembered seeing a dying man carried through the rain.

Sable stepped back from the window. His heart was very calm, which frightened him more than pounding would have. He was already thinking like a liar — already weighing the servants below, guessing which of them might crack, calculating whether the root cellar's flagstone would look disturbed to a careful eye and deciding that yes, it would, and it would have to be moved tonight.

*The fire chooses its keeper.*

"Then it chose badly," he whispered to the empty room. But even as he said it, he was reaching for his cloak.
