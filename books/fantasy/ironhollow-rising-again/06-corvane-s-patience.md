## 6. Corvane's Patience

Dame Ilse Corvane had learned long ago that the truth of a household revealed itself not in its great rooms but in its small ones—in the scullery where the knives were sharpened, in the linen closet where the count never quite matched the ledger, in the narrow stair behind the kitchens that servants used when they did not wish to be seen.

So it was there she began her work in Ironhollow's royal keep: on her knees before a linen closet, counting sheets with a clerk who resented every minute of it.

"Forty-one," said Clerk Doss, licking his pencil. "Ledger says forty-four."

"Or the ledger lies," Corvane said. She ran her thumb along the shelf's edge and found no dust. Someone cleaned here often. Someone diligent. She made a note—not about the sheets, but about the diligence. Diligence was a kind of signature. People left it everywhere they went, like fingerprints in wax.

Three days she had been in the valley now, and three days the stranger at the gate had refused to resolve into anything useful. A burned courier, dead within hours of arrival. No name anyone could produce. No belongings beyond what the storm had soaked into him. And one member of the household—the king's own taster—who had been seen carrying the man inside through the postern, against all quarantine custom, against all sense.

The guards had reported it readily enough. Guards always did; they were paid to notice and punished for noticing too much, so they noticed loudly and often. But the report had sat strangely in Corvane's mind since the moment she'd read it. *The taster took him in.* Not the physician. Not the almoner whose charity was famous and tax-deductible. The taster—a man whose entire profession was suspicion, whose palate was a weapon, who tasted death for a living and was therefore the last man in any keep who should have opened his door to a dying stranger carrying who-knew-what in his blood.

Unless he knew what was in the blood.

Unless he wanted to know.

She closed the linen closet and stood, brushing dust from her grey skirts. Her joints ached; forty years of riding and kneeling and standing watch over other people's crimes had written their ledger into her body, and the entries were coming due. It did not matter. The body could be spent. What mattered was the work, and the work had a shape now, and the shape was a man named Sable Quintana.

* * *

She watched him first from the gallery above the great hall, on the morning of the fourth day.

He moved through the breakfast service with the unhurried precision of a man performing a ritual older than the crown itself. Silver dish, silver spoon, silver needle for the wines. He tasted each course with the same small economy of motion—lips to spoon, a pause, a swallow, a breath held for the count of ten. Ten seconds. Long enough for certain fast venoms to announce themselves. Not nearly long enough for others. Corvane knew this because she had studied poisoners the way other investigators studied thieves or traitors: as a grammar, a language with rules, and the tasters were its translators.

Quintana finished the service, bowed to the king, and withdrew. He did not linger. He did not speak to anyone longer than courtesy required. He walked the corridor back toward his quarters below the kitchens with his hands folded behind him and his eyes down, and if Corvane had not known better—if she had not spent twenty-two years learning the difference between innocence and performance—she would have called it the most innocent walk in the world.

But she did know better.

Innocence did not move like that. Innocence fidgeted under observation. Innocence glanced up at galleries, at windows, at the faces of strangers in its corridor, because innocence had nothing to hide and therefore no discipline about hiding anything. Quintana glanced at nothing. His gaze touched the floor six feet ahead of his boots and never lifted, and yet he navigated the crowded passage without once altering his pace—slipping between a kitchen boy and a laundress with an inch to spare on either side, threading the traffic of the keep like water finding its channel.

That was not a man who didn't notice people. That was a man who had trained himself, over years, to be unnoticeable—and who had gotten so good at it that only another invisible person would ever catch the seams.

Corvane had been invisible once too. She remembered the craft of it. She remembered why people practiced it.

She wrote in her journal that evening, by lamplight, in the cramped cipher her order used:

*Subject displays habitual concealment behaviors consistent with long-term self-effacement. No overt anomalies. Instinct assessment: high threat. Evidence assessment: none. Proceed with patience.*

Patience. Yes. Her old mentor, Dame Roshan, had told her once that investigation was mostly gardening—you planted small questions and waited to see which ones flowered, and you did not dig up the roots to check on them, because that killed the plant and taught you nothing. Corvane had buried her first question that very afternoon, casually, in the ear of the paymaster's clerk.

"I'll need to audit the household disbursements," she had said, sorting papers that needed no sorting. "Standard procedure after an unexplained death. Nothing personal."

The freeze on Quintana's wages would take effect within the week. It was a small thing. A trickle dammed. Most men would not even feel it for a month. But a man with hidden expenses—a man paying for something, buying something, bribing someone—felt a frozen wage the way a drowning man feels a hand release his collar. And how he reacted to that slow suffocation would tell her everything his careful face refused to.

* * *

The neighbors were easier.

Ironhollow town clung to the valley floor in the tight, gossipy way of towns built in shadowed places, where the sun came late and left early and people filled the dark hours with talk. Corvane spent two evenings in the tavern at the foot of Tanner's Row, drinking small beer she barely touched, listening to the woman who kept it narrate the lives of everyone on her street.

"Sable Quintana? Quiet as Sunday. Been in that room above the chandler's going on nine years, and I couldn't tell you his favorite meal." The tavernkeeper, Marta Hesk, wiped the same spot on the counter she'd been wiping since Corvane arrived. "Pays on time. Tips fair. Never drunk, never loud, never brings anyone home. You'd forget he lived there if the rent didn't come."

"Never brings anyone?"

"Not once." Marta leaned in, lowering her voice with the relish of a woman being handed exactly the story she'd been waiting years to tell. "There was a girl, maybe six years back. Pretty thing, worked the dye-works. Took a liking to him. Followed him around half the winter. And you know what he did?"

"Tell me."

"Nothing cruel. That's the strange part. He was kind about it. Walked her home, bought her a ribbon at midwinter, and then—" Marta spread her hands. "Gone. Kindly gone. Like smoke. She cried a fortnight and married a cooper in the spring, and Sable Quintana just kept being quiet as Sunday."

Corvane turned her cup slowly on the counter. "A man alone is either sad or hiding."

"Or both," Marta said, delighted, and Corvane let her believe she'd said something wise.

Both, then. Both was useful. Sadness was a lever; hiding was a door. She spent the next day walking Tanner's Row herself, making the small courteous calls that an investigator of her rank could make without alarming anyone—the chandler below Quintana's rooms, the widow across the lane, the night-soil man who saw more of a neighborhood's true life than any priest.

The picture they assembled was almost perfect. Almost.

The chandler: "Never a sound out of him past the ninth bell. I sleep light, mind, and I'd know."

The widow: "He carried my coals up two winters running when my hip was bad. Never asked thanks. Never stayed for tea."

The night-soil man, hesitating, scratching his jaw: "Funny thing, dame. His slops. I empty for the whole row, and most folk's pots are just pots. His—I don't know how to say it. They're *clean*. Not washed clean. I mean there's never a scrap, never a rind, never a fish bone. Man eats everything he buys, right down to the stalks. Every last bit."

Corvane thanked him, tipped him, and walked away with her heart beating a little faster than the conversation warranted.

A man who wasted nothing. Who ate everything down to the stalks. Who dosed himself, perhaps, dish by dish, year by year—

No. She stopped herself at the edge of that thought and made herself stand on it instead of leaping. It was a theory. Theories were plants too, and she had just dug this one up bare-rooted in the middle of the street. She put it back in the ground, patted the soil down, and marked the spot.

But that night, reviewing her notes, she allowed herself one line beneath the entry for the night-soil man:

*A taster who fears no plate has either never been poisoned—or has made himself unpoisonable.*

* * *

On the sixth day, she requested an audience with the king's steward and asked, with bureaucratic blandness, to see the records of the courier's death. Who had certified it. Who had prepared the body. Where the body lay.

"The paupers' crypt," the steward said, "pending the bishop's—"

"Show me the certification."

It was signed by the keep physician, dated correctly, thorough in its way. Cause of death: burn trauma compounded by exposure. And beneath it, in smaller script, a note Corvane read four times: *Attended in final hours by Sable Quintana, at patient's request.*

At patient's request. A dying stranger, half-mad with burns, delirious with storm-cold, and in his last lucid hour he had asked for the food-taster. By name? The record did not say. The physician, when Corvane found him in his surgery amid the smell of vinegar and cut flesh, could not recall whether the man had spoken a name or simply pointed.

"He pointed?" Corvane said.

"At Quintana, yes. Or past him. Hard to say. Delirious men point at walls sometimes." The physician shrugged, already turning back to his mortar. "The taster sat with him till the end. Decent of him. Most folk won't sit with the burned—they smell wrong, and the dying of them sounds like dogs."

"Did you hear what he said to him? In those final hours?"

Another shrug. "I was attending a colic case. When I came back, the courier was dead and Quintana was washing his hands. Very thoroughly." The physician paused, and something flickered behind his tired eyes—the flicker of a man remembering a detail he'd decided not to think about. "Come to think of it, he washed them a long while. Longer than the dead man required."

"Thank you, physician. You've been most helpful."

She left the surgery and stood a moment in the corridor, letting the cold stone breathe against her thoughts.

A dying courier asks for the taster. The taster sits with him. Washes his hands like a surgeon afterward. And three days later, when Corvane requests an inventory of the courier's effects, she finds the inventory short by one item: a leather satchel strap, buckled, noted in the gate-guard's original report—and absent from every list made since.

Someone had taken something off that body. Something small enough to vanish between reports. Something worth lying for, or at least worth the risk of silence.

She did not yet know what. She did not need to know what. Gardening, she reminded herself. Plant, water, wait.

* * *

Her conviction, when she finally spoke it aloud, she saved for the seventh day, and for the ear of Captain Aldery in the gatehouse, because Aldery was honest and honest men made good sounding boards—they rang true, and their cracks showed.

They stood on the wall walk above the valley mouth, looking out at the pass where the mountains pinched the sky to a grey ribbon. Below them, Ironhollow lay in its bowl of fields and slate roofs, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys in the windless air. It looked, Corvane thought, like a painting of peace. Paintings lied.

"You've served in the border forts," she said to Aldery. "You've seen what comes out of the wild country."

"Some of it." The captain was a broad, weathered man who spoke the way he walked—carefully, watching his footing. "Reavers. Plague sometimes. Once, a thing we didn't talk about after."

"A sorcerer."

"We didn't talk about it," Aldery repeated, but he nodded.

"My order keeps the records they don't talk about," Corvane said. "Every village that burned when one angry child learned to snap her fingers and make fire. Every town that knelt, afterward, because kneeling was cheaper than ash. I have read ledgers of the dead, Captain, that would turn your stomach inside out. Whole valleys. Names by the ten thousand. And do you know the common thread in every account?"

Aldery said nothing. The wind moved his cloak.

"It never starts big," Corvane said. "It starts with one person who is a little bit special and very reasonable about it. Who uses it kindly, at first. To heal. To help. To carry coals for a widow with a bad hip." She watched the captain's face change slightly—he hadn't expected her to know the neighborhood gossip, and she let him wonder how much else she knew. "And then the kindness becomes habit, and the habit becomes entitlement, and the entitlement becomes rule, and one morning there are men flying over your fort on things that are not birds, and the talking is over. Magic does not stay small, Captain. It is the only force in creation that grows by being fed mercy."

"That's a hard doctrine, dame."

"It's the only one that holds. Half-measures are how it survives. Tolerate the hedge-witch, pardon the hedge-witch's son, and in three generations you have a dynasty of them, and in five you have the Old Tyrannies again, and the ledgers open to fresh pages." She turned from the view and looked at him directly. "I am not cruel, Captain. I want you to understand that. Cruelty is waste. What I am is thorough. If magic must die—and it must, completely, or it will merely sleep and wake hungry—then it should die cleanly, quickly, mercifully, with as few graves as possible. That is not zealotry. That is surgery."

"And your suspect?" Aldery asked quietly. "Your taster. Is he the disease?"

Corvane considered the question honestly, which was more than most suspects ever got from her.

"He is a symptom I can't yet diagnose," she said. "Something entered this valley wearing a dead man's skin, and it went to him—to *him*, specifically, out of four thousand souls—and whatever passed between them, he is carrying it still. I intend to find out what." She smiled, and knew from long practice that the smile reached nothing above her cheekbones. "Slowly. Kindly, even. A man's life comes apart very quietly if you take it apart in the right order. First the wages. Then the reputation. Then the friends, who drift when the questions start. Then the options, until there is only one door left open—and you are standing in it when he walks through."

"That doesn't sound like surgery," Aldery said.

"No," Corvane agreed. "Surgery is for the sick. This is pest control." She pulled her cloak tighter against the wind. "But I'll grant you this, Captain. If he's innocent, the process will prove it, and he'll be the better known for it. And if he isn't—"

Below them, far down in the town, a figure emerged from the chandler's shop on Tanner's Row and turned up the lane with his hands folded behind him and his eyes on the ground six feet ahead. Even at this distance, even at this size, Corvane knew the walk. Water finding its channel.

"—then every day I spend being patient," she finished softly, "is a day he spends getting ready."

* * *

The answer came sooner than she had planted for, which was itself instructive. Impatience was a crack in the glaze, and cracks showed the color of what was underneath.

On the eighth day, the paymaster's clerk sought her out, pale and apologetic. "Dame Corvane—the taster. Quintana. He came asking about his wages this morning. Why they were held."

"Naturally he would. What did you tell him?"

"What you instructed. Audit delay, standard procedure, resolution within the fortnight." The clerk wrung his cap. "Only—dame, he thanked me. Thanked me kindly, wished me good morning, and walked out calm as you please. And I've been doing accounts thirty years, and I'll tell you what I saw, because I don't like it and I'd rather it were said."

"Say it."

"He wasn't surprised." The clerk swallowed. "Not annoyed, not worried, not curious even. He heard 'your money is frozen' the way a man hears weather. Like he'd already planned for it. Like some part of him had been waiting years for someone to come along and squeeze, so he'd know how much air he had left."

Corvane dismissed the clerk and sat for a long time in the little room they'd given her, with its narrow window and its view of the inner courtyard, writing nothing.

A man who planned for siege. Who counted his stores. Who ate everything down to the stalks and washed his hands like a surgeon and moved through crowds like water and had never once, in nine years, brought a living soul across his threshold.

Somewhere in that careful, invisible life was a thing worth sieging for. A satchel strap's worth of something, gone from a dead man's effects. A last request from a dying courier. A map, perhaps. A message. A key.

She did not know yet. But she knew the shape of the hole it left, the way you know a missing tooth by the tongue.

That evening she wrote the letter she had been composing in her head for days, addressed to her order's chapterhouse beyond the mountains, requesting the old files—the Keeper trials, the border archives, everything the crown preferred forgotten about the families who had once guarded the roads between worlds. Because instinct had finally given her a word for what Sable Quintana's invisibility reminded her of, and the word was *inherited*, and inherited things had lineages, and lineages had paper trails.

She sealed the letter with black wax and set it aside for the morning rider.

Then she blew out her lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the keep settle around her, and permitted herself, briefly, the pleasure of certainty.

Soon, taster. Patience is a blade too. I have simply been holding it by the flat.

Outside her window, in the courtyard below, a servant crossed through the moonlight carrying a covered dish toward the king's apartments—and walking beside him, unasked, unnoticed, hands folded behind him, went Sable Quintana, tasting the night air of Ironhollow like a man checking a broth for what had been added to it.

Corvane watched until the dark took them both.
