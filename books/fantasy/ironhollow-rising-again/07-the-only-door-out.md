## 7. The Only Door Out

The smuggler found Sable before Sable could find him, which was the first thing that told him Bren Oskar was good at what he did.

Sable had spent three days asking careful questions in careful places—the kind of questions a man asks when he wants to know something without seeming to want it. He'd lingered at the tannery stalls where hides came in from beyond the pass. He'd bought watered wine from a carter who complained about tolls on the blind road. He'd listened, the way he'd been trained to listen by twenty years of tasting: not for flavors, but for the wrongness underneath them.

And then, on the fourth morning, a man sat down across from him at the Drowned Kettle—a tavern so disreputable it didn't even bother pretending otherwise—and said, "You're the king's taster."

Sable didn't look up from his cup. "I'm nobody."

"Nobody." The man laughed, low and rough as gravel in a sack. He was broad through the shoulders and narrow through the luck, with a face like a door someone had kicked open too many times—crooked nose, scarred brow, a jaw shadowed two days past shaving. His eyes were the only clean thing about him: pale gray, sharp as frost. "Funny. I've been nobody my whole life. It's amazing how many somebodies want to talk to us."

"I don't know you."

"No," the man agreed. "But I know you. Or I know your shape. A man who's been asking after the blind pass without asking. Who pays with coin but watches doors. Who hasn't touched his drink since he sat down." He tapped the table once. "That last one's how I knew for certain. Nobody drinks nothing in the Kettle unless they're afraid of being poisoned or they're the one doing the poisoning."

Sable finally raised his eyes. "Which are you?"

"Both, most days." He grinned, and there was something almost honest in it. "Bren Oskar. And you're either the bravest man in Ironhollow or the stupidest, coming to me with whatever's got Dame Corvane pulling the city apart at the seams."

The name landed in Sable's chest like a swallowed stone. *Corvane.* Three days of quiet dread, and here it was spoken aloud in a tavern by a stranger.

"You've heard of her."

"Heard of her? She froze the wages of every servant in the keep yesterday. Questioned the fishmonger's boy about who buys eel on Thursdays. That's *you*, friend. Thursday eel. Somebody in that kitchen talks too much." Bren leaned back, arms crossing over his chest. "So. You're running. Question is—from her, or toward something worth more than she is?"

Sable turned his cup slowly on the table. Every instinct honed over a lifetime said *deny, deflect, disappear*. But denial would send this man walking, and Bren Oskar was, as far as Sable could tell, the only door out of Ironhollow that didn't have Corvane standing behind it.

"What do you charge to take a man through the blind pass?"

Bren's grin widened. "Now we're talking. Depends. One man, no cargo, no questions—fifty crowns. One man carrying something—" his eyes flicked, quick and weighing, over Sable's coat, his hands, the flat of his back where a map might be hidden against skin, "—that costs extra."

"Extra meaning what?"

"A share. Whatever you're carrying, whatever it's worth—I get a cut. Ten parts in a hundred. Non-negotiable." He held up a hand before Sable could speak. "And before you lie to me about what it is, understand: I've moved everything out of this valley that can be moved. Silk, salt, stolen relics, runaway brides, one very angry bear. I'll know if you're lying, and liars make bad passengers. They panic. They run at the wrong moment. They get us both killed on a road where there's nowhere to run *to*."

"And if I say the cargo is worthless?"

"Then you pay fifty crowns and we part friends at the border." Bren shrugged. "But men don't burn their lives down and flee their own funerals for worthless cargo, taster. So. What is it?"

Sable held his gaze for a long moment. Somewhere behind him, a drunk was singing badly; somewhere ahead of him, Corvane was dismantling his life plank by plank. The map lay against his ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from his body, heavier than paper had any right to be.

"It's a map," Sable said. "To a place outside the valley. That's all you need to know."

"That's the least I need to know, and exactly enough." Bren stuck out a hand. "Fifty crowns, ten percent of whatever the map leads to, and we leave before the sun does. You bring the money tonight. I bring the mule."

Sable shook it. The smuggler's grip was dry and strong and entirely without warmth, and Sable thought: *this is the first honest bargain anyone has ever offered me.*

It should not have felt like relief. It felt like relief anyway.

* * *

He went home—or to the room that had served as home for nineteen years, above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Row—and began to kill himself.

Not truly. But carefully, convincingly, the way he did everything.

The records were the problem. Nineteen years of a life left a trail: wage ledgers in the treasury, ration tallies, the chandler's rent book with his signature in its columns, the apothecary's list of customers who bought weekly doses of bitterleaf and ashroot—the regimen that kept a taster's blood a fortress. Corvane had already frozen his wages. It would take her a day, maybe two, to pull the rest of the thread.

So Sable sat at his little table by candlelight and did what he knew best. He worked with poison.

The apothecary's ledger page bearing his name came first—he'd bought the book itself weeks ago, when the plan was still a half-formed fear. A tincture of foxglove and rust-wine, brushed thin along the ink lines, aged with heat until the entry browned and crumbled as though decades old. Then the rent book: a drop of distilled rot-lichen, the kind that ate vellum, applied so the page tore away clean, leaving only a ghost of glue and a gap where a tenant named Sable Quintana had lived for nineteen years.

By midnight, on paper, Sable Quintana had never existed.

It was strange, watching his own history dissolve under a brush no wider than an eyelash. Strange and terrible and—there was no other word for it—freeing. For nineteen years he had built himself into the records the way a mason builds a wall, stone by stone, each entry another proof that he was small and safe and no one's enemy. Now he was unbuilding it, and beneath the wall he found not emptiness but a door.

The savings were harder. Not because of the money—because of what the money was. Four hundred and twelve crowns, accumulated crown by crown over two decades of a servant's wage, wrapped in oilcloth inside a loose floorboard beneath his bed. Fifty for Bren. The rest he carried because a man walking into the world needed to eat, and Sable had never in his life walked into the world.

He counted it one last time by candlelight. He let himself remember earning it: the first year, when he'd been sixteen and shaking after every meal he tasted for the king, hoarding coins like talismans against a death he was certain was coming. The tenth year, when he'd stopped counting the near-poisonings. The nineteenth, when he'd realized he hadn't thought about dying in months, and understood that somewhere along the way the fortress had become a tomb.

Four hundred and twelve crowns. Nineteen years. He weighed it in his palm, then wrapped it away.

Then he took the candle to the floorboard, and to the letters beneath it.

His mother's letters. Seven of them, brittle as moth wings, written in a hand that slanted left like wind-blown grass. He had read them a hundred times. He had memorized them past the point of needing them. *The fire chooses its keeper,* she had written once, in words that had meant nothing to a boy and everything to a hunted man. He pressed each letter to his lips, one by one, the way he might kiss a sleeping child goodbye, and fed them to the flame.

They burned fast. They burned blue at the edges, though the candle flame was ordinary yellow, and Sable told himself he imagined it.

When they were ash, he swept the hearth clean, packed a single bag—clothes, coin, the map, a small kit of antidotes and toxins that would look like a physician's traveling case to anyone foolish enough to search him—and stood in the middle of the room that had been his whole world.

Nineteen years. One bag.

He blew out the candle and did not look back, because he knew that if he looked back, he would see the bed, and the table, and the window that looked out on Candlewick Row, and he would sit down on the bed and not get up again, and Corvane would come for him in a week, and everything his mother had died to give him would die with him in a rented room above a chandler's shop.

Some doors you have to walk through without turning around. His mother had known that. Now, finally, so did he.

* * *

The last thing he did in Ironhollow was die.

He did it in the river, in the hour before dawn, where the water ran black and fast beneath the Old Mill bridge. He stripped to his shift, weighted his boots and coat with stones from the bank, and left them folded neatly on the far pilings—neatly, because a man who staged his own drowning ought to be tidy about it, and tidiness read as truth. Into the coat pocket he tucked a vial of hemlock extract, stoppered but cracked, so that when they found the clothes they would find the poison too, and draw the obvious conclusion: the taster, cornered by the investigator's questions, had chosen the gentler exit.

Corvane would not believe it. He suspected she was too clever to believe it fully. But cleverness required evidence, and he had just destroyed hers, and doubt—even a thin, persistent doubt—moved slowly. Doubt would buy him days. Days were all he needed.

He stood in the shallows in his wet shift, shivering, and looked up at the valley walls rising black against the graying sky. All his life those walls had been protection. Magic could not cross them; within them, he had been safe from everything except the slow poison of a small life. He had hated them and loved them the way you hate and love a parent who kept you alive by keeping you caged.

Beyond them, magic waited. Real magic, fading magic, the ember his mother had died to map. And things that hunted it: zealots, dragons, the crown.

*I'm coming,* he thought at the mountains, and could not tell if it was a promise or a threat.

* * *

Bren Oskar was waiting at the tannery gate with a mule, a cart of stinking hides, and an expression of profound disappointment.

"You look terrible," Bren said.

"I'm supposed to be dead."

"Dead men look better than that. Here." He tossed Sable a bundle of rough wool—a drover's cloak, hooded, reeking of tanbark. "Put that on. You smell like a clerk. On the blind road, clerks get robbed and drovers get ignored. Be a drover."

Sable pulled the cloak on. It hung heavy on his shoulders, and the stink of it settled into his lungs, and somehow that helped—that and the gray light creeping up over the eastern wall of the valley, touching the peaks with a color like old iron.

"The pass closes behind us at midday," Bren said, checking the cart's axle with practiced hands. "Gate wardens rotate at noon, and the new watch has eyes sharper than the old. We go now, we move steady, we say nothing. You're my sister's widower, mute since the fever, and I'm a saint for taking you on. Nod if you understand."

Sable nodded.

"One more thing." Bren looked at him straight, and the frost-gray eyes were serious now, all the tavern humor gone out of them. "Once we're through the pass, there's no coming back cheap. The valley lets people in easy. Out is another matter—they check papers going both ways these days, and yours are ash, I'm guessing. So this is the moment. Last chance to turn around, taster. Whatever's chasing you—it keeps chasing once you're outside. Walls won't stop it anymore."

Sable looked back down the street, just once. Candlewick Row slept on, chimneys cold, shutters dark. Somewhere in the keep, the king's breakfast was being prepared, and some other tongue would taste it now, some other invisible man would swallow the arsenic meant for a crown and live, and no one would ever think of Sable Quintana again except as a sad story told over ale: drowned in the mill race, poor devil, and him the king's own taster.

Good. Let them think it. Let the records stay dead and the name stay buried. He had spent nineteen years learning how to survive by disappearing, and now he was going to use every lesson to do the opposite—to walk into the wide, magical, murderous world and be seen.

"Grief later," he said quietly. "Road now."

Bren studied him a moment longer, then snorted—almost, nearly, a laugh. "Grief later, road now. I'll paint that on the cart." He clicked his tongue at the mule, and the cart rolled forward through the tannery gate into the waking streets.

They went east, always east, through streets still blue with night, past the fish market shuttered and silent, past the keep where torches burned in Corvane's window—she rose early, then; she was hunting early—and out along the river road toward the western wall, where the blind pass split the mountains like a wound that never healed.

The gate wardens waved Bren through with the bored familiarity of men paid too little to care. The cart rattled onto the mountain road. Behind them, Ironhollow shrank—rooftops, spires, the grey ribbon of smoke rising from the palace kitchens where breakfast was being laid for a king whose food no longer needed tasting.

Sable did not watch it shrink. He watched the pass ahead, the walls of stone closing in on either side, higher and higher, until the sky was a blade of paling gold overhead and the air smelled of pine and snow and something else—something faint and strange and electric, like the moment before lightning.

"Bren," he said. "What's that smell?"

The smuggler glanced back at him, and for the first time since the Drowned Kettle, he looked uncertain.

"That," he said, "is the border. Half a mile more, and you'll feel it too. Everyone does, their first time." He faced forward again, and his voice dropped, almost gentle. "Brace yourself, taster. The world outside your valley isn't empty. It's *full*. And it's been waiting for you a long time."

The mule plodded on. The blade of sky widened. And Sable Quintana, dead man, drover, keeper of a scorched map and a burning inheritance, walked out of the only home he had ever known—toward the hum at the edge of hearing, toward the fire that chose its keepers, toward everything his mother had died to show him.

The sun cleared the peaks behind him. He did not turn around.
