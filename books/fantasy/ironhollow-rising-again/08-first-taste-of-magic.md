## 8. First Taste of Magic

The border of Ironhollow was not a wall. That was the first thing that surprised him.

Sable had imagined a gate, a line cut into the earth, some visible seam where the world's quiet ended and whatever lay beyond began. Instead there was only a granite shelf above the tree line, a cairn of stacked stones gone green with lichen, and Bren Oskar standing beside it with his arms crossed, watching Sable the way a man watches another man step off a cliff.

"This is it?" Sable said.

"This is it." Bren spat over the edge of the shelf, into the pines far below. "No fanfare. The valley doesn't announce itself. It just... stops."

Sable put out his hand and touched the air past the cairn.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened—air was air—but he felt foolish anyway, and he felt Bren's eyes on him, cataloguing the foolishness for later use or later sale. Behind them, Wren sat on a boulder with her knees drawn up, chewing a strip of dried apple she'd stolen from Bren's pack without apology. She'd been following them since Marrowgate, and neither of them had found a way to make her stop that didn't involve leaving her in a ditch.

"You're stalling," Wren said around the apple.

"I'm being careful."

"You're stalling *carefully*."

Bren laughed, short and rough. "She's got you there, taster."

Sable looked back down the pass one last time. From here, Ironhollow was a gray-green bowl cupped in mountains, smoke rising from its chimneys in perfectly straight lines because no wind ever dared to bend them. Somewhere down there was the king whose food he'd tasted for eleven years. Somewhere down there was Dame Corvane, who would find the falsified death records soon—the fevered body he'd poisoned to resemble himself, the grave he'd paid a gravedigger to dig shallow enough to be suspicious—and when she did, every road out of the valley would grow teeth.

He had spent his whole life being invisible. It occurred to him now, standing at the edge of everything safe, that invisibility had never been a gift. It had been a cage so comfortable he'd mistaken it for a home.

"Right," he said, and stepped across.

* * *

Nothing happened for three heartbeats.

Then it happened all at once.

It came up through the soles of his boots like water finding a crack in stone—a pressure, a warmth, a hum that was not sound but lived in the same place sound lived. It climbed his shins, his spine, poured into the hollows behind his eyes, and Sable made a noise he was not proud of, a small strangled thing, and dropped to one knee on the far side of the cairn.

"Sable?" Wren was off her rock instantly. "What's wrong? What do you see?"

"Nothing," he managed. "That's—it's nothing I can *see*. It's—" He pressed a hand flat against the ground, as if he could hold himself down. "There's something *in* everything."

Because there was. He could feel it now, feel it everywhere: in the granite under his palm, a slow mineral patience; in the wind coming off the peaks, a restlessness like a living breath; in the pine roots threading the soil below, an ancient green attention turning toward him, curious, the way a dog turns toward a stranger at the gate. The world beyond Ironhollow was not empty. It had never been empty. It was crowded, humming, awake, and he had spent thirty years living next door to it without once hearing it breathe.

In the valley, magic could not enter. His mother had fled into that silence pregnant, trading wonder for safety, and Sable had been born into the hush and raised in it, dosed on poisons instead of spells, learning the tongue of toxins because it was the only tongue the valley allowed. He had thought the deadness was the natural state of things. He had thought the world outside was simply more of the same, more sky, more dirt, more indifferent matter.

He had been wrong. The world outside was *full*, and it was pouring into him through every seam of his body like light through a cracked jar.

"Breathe," Bren said, crouching beside him. His voice had changed—flatter, warier. "Some people take it hard, their first time over. Like surfacing too fast from a deep dive."

"You've brought people across before."

"I've brought everyone across before. That's the job." Bren studied him. "But most of them don't look like they're *listening* to it."

"I am listening." Sable got his feet under him and stood. The hum settled as he rose—not gone, never going to be gone again, but settling, finding a rhythm. It thudded faintly beneath his own pulse, slightly offset, slightly slower, like a second heartbeat walking beside his own. Terrifying. Intoxicating. He understood suddenly how people became addicted to this, how kingdoms had burned for it, how his mother had run from it and still, dying, had kept a map to its last ember hidden in the lining of her son's inheritance. You could hate it and crave it in the same breath. He already did.

Wren was staring at him with an expression he couldn't read. "You went white," she said. "Then you went *bright*. Just for a second. Like a lamp behind your skin."

"That's not possible."

"Neither am I, according to half the people in Marrowgate." She shrugged, but her eyes stayed on him a beat too long, and Sable filed the moment away with the other moments he was choosing not to examine: the way she'd claimed to hear the mountains humming, the way the hum in his own blood seemed to lean toward her, faintly, like a plant toward a window.

They walked. The pass descended through scree and then pine, and by midday Sable had learned to walk with the second heartbeat instead of against it, the way a man learns to sleep beside a snoring companion. Bren led them along a smuggler's track—unmarked, unloved, switching back down the mountain's shoulder—and spoke little, which Sable had learned was how Bren spoke when he was thinking about his sister.

They camped that night in a hollow ringed by lightning-blasted firs, and Sable lay awake for hours feeling the forest attend to him. Not menacingly. Attentively. As if the trees were polite strangers pretending not to stare.

He fell asleep listening to two hearts, and dreamed of his mother's kitchen, and in the dream the kettle sang in two voices.

* * *

The Ashcloaks came at dawn, and they came with smoke.

Sable woke to Wren's hand clamped over his mouth and her whisper in his ear: "*Men.* In the trees. Lots." Below the hollow, on the track, gray shapes moved between the pines with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the country. They wore ash-smeared robes over mail, hoods up, faces streaked with white bone-paint, and they carried braziers—actual iron braziers, slung on poles, trailing ribbons of sweet-smelling smoke that curled upward through the cold morning air.

"What are they burning?" Sable breathed.

"Ashcloak censer-weed," Bren whispered back. He'd gone very still, his hand resting on the knife at his belt the way other men rest hands on prayer books. "Zealots. They call themselves the Order of the Clean Flame. They burn anyone magic-touched—first with the smoke, which takes the fight out of you, then with actual fire, which takes everything else out of you." His jaw worked. "We're downwind. Bad luck. Or they followed us from Marrowgate, which is worse luck."

"How many?"

"Counted eight. Probably twelve. They travel in twelves."

Wren's fingers were digging into Sable's arm hard enough to bruise. "I heard them an hour ago," she whispered. "I told you—I *told* you I heard boots on the north trail and you said it was deer—"

"And I was wrong," Sable murmured. "Noted. Later."

The smoke reached them before the men did. It came in a slow gray tide through the firs, sweet and resinous, like incense cut with honey, and Sable watched Bren's face go slack and Wren sway on her heels, and he understood what the weed was: not a poison exactly, but a sedative, a softener, something to make the burning easier on everyone involved. The Order were zealots, but they were *tidy* zealots.

And Sable Quintana had spent eleven years drinking arsenic with breakfast.

The smoke washed over him and slid off. It was well-made—he could taste the monkshood in it, the dreamshade, a base of poppy resin, cleverly balanced, honestly a respectable piece of work—but it was a poison, and poisons had been his mother tongue since he was nineteen years old. His body read the smoke the way a scholar reads a familiar sentence, noted its intentions, and declined them. He stood in the gray tide and felt nothing but a mild sweetness at the back of his throat, like licking a candied spoon.

The first Ashcloak came around the fir at the head of the hollow and stopped dead.

Sable was standing upright in the center of the smoke. Both hands empty. Eyes clear. Watching him.

"*Untouched,*" the man said, and his voice cracked on the word. He was young—younger than Sable—with bone-paint smeared across his cheeks and terror underneath it. "Brothers! The stranger stands *untouched!*"

They came fast after that, six of them converging through the trees, and Sable had perhaps four seconds to understand that he was about to die, and then perhaps one more second to understand that he was not.

Because the smoke was his.

It had always been his, this whole category of things—this entire language of substances that unmade bodies. Every poison was a key, and he had spent a decade cutting copies of every key that existed, and here were men who had built their whole terrible faith on the assumption that no one held these keys. The smoke that knelt them was, to Sable, a recipe recited aloud. And a man who knows a recipe can do more than refuse the dish.

He could cook.

"Down," he snapped at Bren and Wren—"both of you, *down*, breathe shallow"—and then he did the bravest and stupidest thing of his life: he walked toward the braziers.

The nearest one hung from a pole planted at the hollow's edge, its coals banked low, its weed smoldering in a perforated iron basket. Sable had watched the wind work all morning; he knew the drift, knew the eddy behind the great lightning-split fir, knew that the smoke pooled in the hollow's low places because cold air is lazy air. He wrenched the brazier free of its pole—hot iron biting his palms, and he barely noticed, adrenaline burning hotter than coals—and swung it in a wide arc, scattering the smoldering weed in a fan across the eastern side of the hollow, directly into the path of the oncoming brothers.

They walked into their own cloud.

He saw it happen with a clarity that frightened him afterward: the way the lead zealot's stride shortened, the way the second man's knife hand drifted, the way all six of them slowed together like puppets whose strings had thickened in cold weather. Within twenty breaths, three of them were on their knees. Two more leaned against trees with the serene, stupid smiles of the deeply drugged. The last—the young one, the first—staggered, fought it, actually fought it, biting his own lip bloody to stay upright, and came at Sable anyway with a flensing hook raised, screaming something about the Clean Flame.

Sable caught the man's wrist.

He didn't strike him. He simply held on, and turned the young zealot gently until the man's face was inches from the drifting smoke, and held him there, almost tenderly, while the fight drained out of him like water from a pierced skin. The hook fell. The man folded. Sable lowered him onto the needles as carefully as if he were setting a drunk cousin into bed.

Behind him, Bren and Wren stood blinking in the thinning haze, alive, untouched, staring at him as if he'd grown a second head.

"Well," Bren said finally. "That's a new one."

"They'll wake in an hour," Sable said. His voice sounded strange to him—steady, clinical, the voice he used in the king's hall when reporting on a dish. "Headache, nausea, no lasting harm. Bind them if you want. Take their boots if you must, though I'd rather we didn't; cruelty compounds." He looked down at his own hands. They were steady. They were always steady. But they smelled of censer-weed and hot iron, and they had just done something no taster should be able to do, and both of his companions had seen it.

"The smoke should have taken you," Wren said. Not a question. Her eyes were enormous. "It took Bren in about ten seconds. It should have taken you."

"It's monkshood and dreamshade in a poppy base," Sable said. "With a carrier resin I don't recognize—something local. Cleverly balanced, but balanced for ordinary constitutions." He heard himself talking and could not stop; this was what he did when he was afraid, he retreated into the chemistry, the liturgy of doses. "I've built tolerance to the first two over eleven years. Deliberately. Dish by dish. It's why I'm alive, and it's why the king eats breakfast."

Silence. The forest hummed around them, that vast attentive hum, and Sable realized with a lurch that the second heartbeat in his chest had quickened—that the magic in the world had *noticed* what he'd done, had leaned toward him the way Wren leaned toward windows, and that some part of him had leaned back.

Bren picked up one of the fallen braziers, turned it over in his hands, and set it down with exaggerated care, like a man handling evidence.

"Taster," he said slowly. "When we struck our bargain, you told me you were fleeing the crown over a map. You told me you were nobody—a servant, a poison-eater, a ghost." He wiped his mouth. "Ghosts don't walk through Ashcloak smoke like morning fog. Ghosts don't disarm six of the Clean Flame's chosen with a *brazier*. So before we go one more mile down this mountain, you're going to tell me what you actually are, and what's actually on that map, and why every power in the world seems to want it—and why I'm starting to think my sister died chasing the same thing you're carrying."

The sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck through the firs, and in the light Sable saw, far down the switchbacks, a thin line of gray figures descending toward them. Twelve more. The rest of the company. Coming to find their brothers.

"We have until they arrive," Sable said. "I'll tell you everything. But we tell it walking."

He picked up his pack. The second heartbeat pounded in his ears, faster now, eager, and for the first time since crossing the cairn, Sable could not tell whether the magic in his blood was a gift, a disease, or a summons—and whether, somewhere ahead in the Ashen Peaks, the thing that had sent it was waiting to collect.
