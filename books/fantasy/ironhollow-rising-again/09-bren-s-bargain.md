## 9. Bren's Bargain

The fire had burned down to coals by the time Sable finally slept, and Bren Oskar sat with his back against a lightning-split pine, turning the problem over in his mind the way he turned everything: slowly, and looking for the catch.

Three days out of Ironhollow. Three days of watching the taster move through a world that had tried to kill him twice already—the Ashcloaks on the pass, then that miserable stretch of marsh where the water ran bitter and Wren had gone gray-faced before they found a clean spring. And through all of it, Sable Quintana walked like a man carrying nothing heavier than a change of clothes. Bren had smuggled fugitives before. He knew what guilt looked like draped over a man's shoulders. This was different. This was cargo.

He glanced at the boy—girl? Wren had never said, and he'd stopped guessing—which one was it—Wren, curled up on the far side of the fire with her stolen knife under her hand even in sleep, and at Sable, flat on his back with his palms open toward the sky like a man who'd made peace with whatever fell into them.

Then Bren did what he'd been doing every night since the pass. He took out the map.

Not Sable's map. His own.

It wasn't much to look at anymore—a square of oilskin gone soft as cloth from handling, ink faded to brown ghosts. His sister Maren had drawn it, or copied it, or dreamed it; she'd never been clear on which, and Bren had never cared enough to ask while she was alive to answer. It showed the Ashen Peaks from the east, the way no honest mapmaker drew them, because no honest mapmaker had ever come back. There were markings along the spine of the mountains that matched nothing in any atlas: a spiral here, a broken circle there, and at the center, pressed so hard the stylus had nearly torn the skin, a single word in Maren's cramped hand.

*Emberhoard.*

Bren had spent eleven years telling himself it meant nothing. That his sister had chased a story into the mountains and the mountains had eaten her, same as they ate everyone who went looking for dragons. That the word was a fever-dream, a con she'd bought from some dying fool the way Sable had bought his own map from a dying fool. That was the comfortable version. The comfortable version let him sleep.

Except now there was a taster from Ironhollow sitting three feet away with a scorched map of his own, headed for the same peaks, muttering in his sleep about embers and keepers, and the comfortable version was starting to smell like a lie left out too long in the sun.

Bren folded the oilskin away and looked at the stars. Beyond the valley's border, the sky felt bigger—he'd noticed it the first night out and still hadn't gotten used to it. In Ironhollow the stars came through flat and dim, like coins seen through dirty glass. Out here they burned. Out here everything burned a little brighter, and Bren didn't trust it. Bright things were bait. He'd learned that young, on the salt roads, watching his father's crew chase lights over the horizon that turned out to be a revenue cutter's lanterns.

Maren had loved the bright things. That was the whole trouble with her.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, frost furring the tent canvas, and Wren woke first as always—Bren heard her moving around the dead fire, quiet as a cat, pretending she wasn't checking whether he was awake. She thought he hadn't figured her out yet. Girl picks your pocket Tuesday, saves your life Wednesday, acts like neither happened Thursday. He'd known smugglers with twenty years on her who weren't half so good at their work, and none half so bad at lying about why they did it.

Sable woke last, the way he always did—no transition, just eyes open and fully present, like a man surfacing from deep water. Bren had watched soldiers do that. Watched men who'd survived things do that. It was another entry on the list of things about the taster that didn't add up.

"Water's boiling," Wren said. "Such as it is. Tastes like a swamp's armpit."

"You've tasted a swamp's armpit?" Sable asked.

"I've tasted worse."

They ate in silence, hard bread and smoked fish Bren had bought off a river trader who'd charged him double and lied about the freshness. Sable chewed slowly, methodically, and Bren watched him do the thing he did with every mouthful—some small ritual of the tongue, cataloguing, testing. A man who tasted poison for a living probably couldn't turn it off. Probably tasted betrayal in his bread and rot in his beer and never got a meal that was just a meal.

"So," Bren said, when the fish was gone and Wren had wandered off downstream with the waterskins. "The Ashen Peaks."

Sable looked up. "What about them?"

"How much do you actually know about what you're walking into?"

"The map shows a route. Through the Ashen Pass, down into a valley the cartographer marked with—" Sable hesitated, and Bren saw him decide, deliberately, how much truth to spend. "With bones."

"Bones." Bren laughed, but there was no humor in it, and Sable heard that, because Sable heard everything. "That's one word for it. My sister called it something else."

Something shifted in the taster's face—not surprise exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has been waiting for a door to open and hears the latch lift.

"Maren Oskar," Bren said. "My sister. Four years older than me and twice as stubborn, which if you knew me is saying something. We ran the salt routes together out of Marrowgate—her, me, our father before he drowned. She was the one with the head for stories. Every port, every tavern, she'd find the oldest drunk in the room and buy him ale until he talked. Dragons. Old magic. The wars before the treaties. I used to tell her she was wasting coin on ghost stories." He picked up a stick and drew lines in the ash, not looking at Sable. "She stopped laughing at my jokes around the time she started drawing maps."

"The Emberhoard," Sable said quietly.

"That's the one. She heard about it from a dying sailor in Saltreach—a man who claimed his grandfather had served a dragon-keeper, back when there were such things. Said the last dragon hoarded more than gold. Said there was an ember at the heart of it, the first fire, the fire all other fires came from. Magic's hearthstone." Bren snapped the stick and dropped the halves. "I told her it was nonsense. I told her magic was a story we told children so they'd behave, and anyone selling a map to a dragon's hoard was selling the same bridge twice. She went anyway."

"When?"

"Eleven years ago. Spring." The number came out of him smooth from use, worn round like a stone he carried in his pocket. "She took two companions and a mule and told me she'd be back before the snows. I waited until the snows. Then I waited until the next spring. Then I stopped waiting and started drinking, which lasted about a year, and then I started smuggling, which paid better than grief."

Sable said nothing. That was the thing about him most people would miss, Bren thought—he knew when silence was worth more than words. Most men would have offered sympathy by now, that soft-mouthed pity that made a man want to break something. Sable just sat with it, the way you'd sit with a sick friend.

"She sent one letter," Bren went on. "From the last settlement before the peaks. A place called Cinderhold, if it even still exists. The letter said the mountains hummed. Actual humming, like a struck bell that wouldn't stop. She said she could feel it in her teeth. She said—" and here Bren's voice did the thing it always did, the small crack he'd never managed to sand smooth, "—she said she understood now why people believed. And then nothing. Eleven years of nothing."

"And you think she's dead."

"I know she's dead." Bren met his eyes. "What I think doesn't enter into it. Nobody comes back from the Ashen Peaks. The peaks don't give refunds."

"But?"

"But." Bren exhaled. Here it was, then. The thing he'd been circling for three days, the confession he'd sworn he'd take to his grave, pried loose by a soft-spoken poison-eater and a girl who could pick a lock with her eyelashes. "But I've done business with a lot of liars, Sable, and I've learned to read the shape of a lie. Your courier—the one who gave you that map. You said he was poisoned. Slow toxin, the kind that takes days."

"Ashcloak work, most likely. Or someone using their methods."

"Right. And a man that far gone, that deep in the dark—men say true things then. Dying men haven't got the energy to lie." Bren leaned forward. "So tell me again, and tell me straight this time. What did he say the ember was?"

For a long moment Sable didn't answer. Bren watched him weigh it—watched the calculation move behind those careful eyes, the same arithmetic Bren himself had been running all night. How much truth does a man need before he'll follow you into a dragon's graveyard?

"He said it keeps magic alive," Sable said at last. "That outside the valley, magic is real, and it's fading, and the ember is what's left of its source. He said the dragon Vaelithra guards it. He said—" Sable's jaw tightened. "He said the fire chooses its keeper."

"There it is." Bren sat back. "Vaelithra. Maren had that name too. Scratched into the margin of her map, over and over, like she was trying to wear a groove in it. Queen of the last hoard. Mother of ashes." He pulled the oilskin out again, unfolded it, and laid it on the ground between them beside Sable's scorched parchment. For a moment neither spoke. The two maps didn't match—not quite—but the marks rhymed. The spiral on Maren's eastern ridge echoed a spiral on Sable's western approach. The broken circle appeared on both. Two different hands, years apart, drawing toward the same hidden heart.

"If your sister followed these marks," Sable said slowly, "and the courier's information is true—"

"Then she died within sight of the thing she was looking for. Yeah. I've done that math every night for eleven years." Bren's laugh came out rough. "You want to know the worst part? Some nights the math comes out different. Some nights I'm lying in the dark and I think: what if she made it? What if she reached the hoard and the dragon didn't kill her, because why would it—she wasn't there to steal, she was there because she *believed*, and maybe that's the toll, maybe belief is the coin—and she's been there ever since. Alive. At the dragon's side. Waiting for someone to come get her, and her useless brother spent a decade running salt and stolen brandy instead of growing a spine."

"That's not—"

"It's not rational. I know it's not rational. I'm a smuggler, not a philosopher." He folded Maren's map away, carefully, the way you'd fold away a wound. "Here's my bargain, taster, and I'll only make it once. I got you out of Ironhollow. I got you past the Ashcloaks. I'll take you to the Emberhoard—all the way, no more renegotiating the price at every crossroads, I know how that game goes. But when we get there, if there's any chance—any—that Maren Oskar is alive in that place, you help me find out. You don't leave without knowing. Swear it."

Sable studied him for a long time. Bren had the uncomfortable sense of being tasted—of the man sorting through him the way he sorted through the king's soup, hunting for the arsenic, the bitterness, the thing that didn't belong.

"You understand what we might find," Sable said. "A valley of bones, you said yourself. If she reached the hoard eleven years ago—"

"Then she's bones too, and I'll bury her myself, and that's a better ending than not knowing. That's the whole point." Bren heard his own voice go quiet. "I've buried her a hundred times in my head, Sable. Every version except the real one. I just need the real one."

Wren came back up the bank then, waterskins sloshing, and stopped short when she saw the two of them sitting over the maps like conspirators. Her eyes went narrow and quick, reading the scene the way she read a mark's pockets.

"What'd I miss?" she demanded.

"Bren's coming all the way," Sable said. "No more renegotiating."

"I could've told you that." Wren dropped the skins and flopped down by the fire, but her gaze lingered on Bren, and there was something in it that wasn't the usual mercenary appraisal. Something almost gentle, quickly buried. "Anyone with eyes can see you're not in it for the gold. You haven't mentioned the gold once since the pass."

"No," Bren agreed. "I haven't."

* * *

They broke camp within the hour and took the high trail north, where the land began its slow climb toward the line of gray peaks that ruled the horizon like a row of filed teeth. Bren rode point out of habit, though there was little traffic this far from anywhere to rob. By midday the air had changed—thinner, cleaner, with an edge underneath it that he remembered from the letter, from Maren's scrawled words: *the mountains hummed.*

He'd thought that was poetry. Drunk-sailor nonsense passed down a chain of taverns until it gleamed.

But as they crested a granite shoulder in the early afternoon and the Ashen Peaks opened before them—vast, white-crowned, silent—Bren felt it. Faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. A vibration in the stone under his boots, in the fillings of his teeth, in the hollow of his chest where grief kept its rooms.

He reined up. Behind him, Wren had gone rigid in her saddle, one hand pressed flat against her own sternum, her face lit with an expression he'd never seen on her—wonder, naked and unguarded, the look of a child at a window. And Sable—

Sable had his eyes closed. His lips moved slightly, as if counting, or praying, or listening to something the rest of the world couldn't hear.

"It's real," Wren whispered. "The humming. I thought I imagined it yesterday. I thought I was going mad."

"You're not mad," Sable said softly. "We're getting close."

Bren said nothing. He sat his horse and let the mountain's voice move through him, eleven years deep, and somewhere beneath the fear and the doubt and the long scar tissue of not-knowing, a small stubborn coal of the thing his sister had died chasing flickered awake in his chest.

Belief. Maybe that was the toll. Maybe that was the coin.

He dug his heels in and led them toward the peaks, toward the bones, toward whatever waited at the end of two maps and one impossible hope—and behind them, far down the trail they'd left, dust rose where no wind raised it.
