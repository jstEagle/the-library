## 10. The City of Salt

Marrowgate announced itself by smell before sight: brine and tar, fish rot and frying onions, the sweet-sour reek of a thousand lives pressed close together. Then came the sound—a wall of it, after weeks of mountain silence—and then, cresting the last hill, the city itself.

It rose from a gray bay like something grown rather than built. Salt-crusted walls the color of old bone, rooftops stacked at drunken angles, a forest of masts beyond the harbor where ships from a dozen nations leaned against each other like tired travelers. Gulls wheeled and screamed overhead. Somewhere a bell was ringing, though whether for a wedding, a funeral, or a fire, no one on the road seemed to care.

Sable pulled his hood lower and felt the familiar tightening in his chest that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with habit. In Ironhollow, invisibility had been a discipline—shoulders down, eyes soft, voice pitched to be forgotten the moment it finished speaking. Here, in this crush of strangers, he should have been safer than he'd been in years.

Instead, every crowd felt like a noose waiting to be tied.

"Three days' ride and I still smell the pass," Bren said beside him, guiding the cart with one hand and scratching his beard with the other. He looked better than he had any right to—the smuggler's weathered face split by an easy grin as he took in the harbor. "Marrowgate. Saltiest city on the coast. You can trust nobody here, which is why everybody comes."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be *true*." Bren clicked his tongue at the mule. "We need three things: a guide who knows the Ashen Peaks, supplies, and a place to sleep where nobody asks names. In that order. And Sable—" His grin faded into something more careful. "Keep your hands in your pockets. Not because of thieves. Because of your hands."

Sable glanced down at them. Taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips from years of pricking them against needles and thorns and the occasional blade, testing everything before it touched his lips. Hands that knew poisons the way priests knew psalms.

"I know," he said.

Wren had been walking ahead of them since the ridge, drifting through the crowd like smoke through fingers. She'd stopped asking to come along; she'd simply never left. Now she vanished between two fishmongers' stalls, and Sable watched the space where she'd been with a feeling he refused to name. The girl could hear mountains humming. She was magic-touched, exactly what the Ashcloaks burned people for, and she was alone in a city that sold anything—including people.

"Watch her," he murmured.

"Can't watch what won't stay still." But Bren's eyes were already moving, cataloging exits the way other men cataloged faces.

They found lodging first, because Bren insisted on it despite his own ordering: a rooming house above a chandlery near the salt market, run by a woman with a lazy eye and a ledger she kept in three different scripts. She asked no names. She asked coin instead, and Bren paid it without haggling, which told Sable how nervous the smuggler actually was.

The room was narrow, smelled of tallow and rope, and had one window overlooking an alley where the tide of the city flowed past all day—porters, sailors, beggars, children. Sable stood at it a long time, watching, learning the rhythm of Marrowgate the way he'd once learned the rhythm of the king's kitchen: who belonged, who didn't, who moved like they were hunting.

He saw her within the hour. A small figure in a patched coat, working the crowd below with quick, light-fingered grace—not Wren. Someone else entirely. A boy lifting a purse, a girl cutting a strap, a dozen small cruelties performed with the indifference of weather.

This was a city that ate the careless. He'd known that when they came. What he hadn't known—what he could not have prepared for—was the poster.

Bren found him there, and neither spoke for a moment. Below, nailed to the chandlery's post at eye level, fresh ink still sharp-edged:

*By order of Dame Corvane, Investigator of the Crown: One thousand crowns royal for information leading to the capture of a man answering to no name, traveling with a smuggler called Bren Oskar. Distinguishing marks: taster's scars upon the fingertips. Approach with caution. The man may be poisoned.*

"The man may be poisoned," Sable read aloud, and heard the absurdity of it crack out of him as something too close to laughter. "She knows me too well."

"She's spent your wages, questioned your neighbors, and now she's bought my name." Bren's jaw worked. "A thousand crowns. That's not a bounty, that's a kingdom's ransom dressed in copper. Half this city would sell its mother for a tenth of that."

"Then we leave tonight."

"And go where? Into the Peaks without a guide? You saw the map, same as me. There are passes up there that don't exist twice in the same season." Bren rubbed his face. "No. We need the guide more than we need caution, which means we need to move fast and quiet and pray the salt dries before anyone tastes us."

*Pray.* Sable almost smiled. In Ironhollow, prayer was a formality, like bowing—words said toward a heaven that couldn't hear, because magic couldn't enter and gods, presumably, traveled by magic. Out here, under a sky that hummed at the edges of Wren's hearing, prayer might actually mean something.

That thought frightened him more than the poster did.

* * *

They went looking for a guide at dusk, in the taverns nearest the eastern gate, where the caravans bound for the high country hired their muscle and their maps. Bren did the talking. Sable kept to the shadows and listened, and it was in the third tavern—the Salt Cellar, low-ceilinged and loud—that he heard the wrong sentence.

"—taster's scars, I heard. Fingertips all nicked up. My cousin saw the bounty board at the customs house—"

He turned slowly. Behind him, wedged into a corner booth, a knot of dockworkers argued over ale, and among them sat a girl of perhaps twelve with sharp eyes and sharper hands, listening the way children listen when their survival depends on information. Wren. She'd followed them again. She'd been following them all day, and now she was hearing exactly what Sable most needed her not to hear.

Their eyes met across the smoky room.

He expected her to look away. Instead she tilted her head, considering, and mouthed something he couldn't read. Then she slipped from the booth and out the door, and Sable made the mistake of letting her go, because Bren had just clapped a hand on the shoulder of a scarred mountain man named Hesk who claimed to know the Ashen Peaks "like my own teeth," and negotiations had begun.

The price was steep. The departure was dawn. The guide wanted half up front, and Bren counted it out with the grim face of a man watching his savings walk away wearing someone else's boots.

It was nearly midnight when they returned to the rooming house, and the alley window was open.

Sable froze on the stairs. Bren, behind him, went very still.

The room had been searched—not ransacked, but *read*. Their packs had been opened and repacked, the map's hiding place beneath the floorboard disturbed and replaced, the false bottom in Sable's satchel discovered and left empty. Whoever had done it had been good. Better than good.

On the table, weighted under Sable's own eating knife, lay a folded scrap of paper.

Bren reached for it. Sable caught his wrist.

"Wait." He picked up the knife first, turned it over, sniffed the blade, touched the flat of it to his tongue. Nothing. Only then did he unfold the paper.

One line, in a child's uneven hand:

*Customs house. They know about the guide. Dawn is a trap. —W*

Below it, pressed into the wax seal of a broken signet ring, the mark of the crown investigator: a black key.

"She sold us," Bren breathed. "The little thief sold us to Corvane—"

"No." Sable stared at the paper. "If she'd sold us, we'd already be in chains. This is a warning." He looked up. "But she *did* talk to them. Which means they talked to her. Which means—" He stopped, because the rest arrived coldly, all at once. "Which means Corvane offered her something. And whatever it was, she chose us instead."

"Why would she? She doesn't know you. She doesn't owe you."

"No," Sable agreed quietly. "But she heard them describe you. And she heard them describe me." He folded the paper closed. "Corvane doesn't just want the map, Bren. She wants everyone who's ever helped me. Wren figured out what being found *with* us means. We're not a payday anymore. We're a contagion."

* * *

Dawn found them not at the eastern gate but crouched in the loft of a tannery two streets from the customs house, watching the trap they weren't in.

Hesk waited at the gate with two men who were very bad at pretending to be porters. A crown warrant officer stood behind them, arms folded, wearing the gray-and-black of Corvane's service. When no taster appeared, the officer's expression curdled, and he began questioning Hesk loudly enough to be heard across the square.

Sable watched the mountain man sweat, watched him deny, watched him finally—wisely—walk away from the whole arrangement, abandoning his advance pay rather than be tangled in whatever this was. By full sun, the trap had caught nothing but its own tail.

"She saved us," Bren said, wonder and unease fighting in his voice. "The little pickpocket saved us. Why?"

"Because someone taught her that crowns buy knives, and knives come for whoever holds the purse." Sable settled back against the tannery wall, amid the reek of hides and lime. "Or because she's magic-touched and she's finally met people the hunters hate more than her."

"You're guessing."

"I'm always guessing. It's what keeps me alive." He paused. "We find her. Before Corvane does."

Finding Wren proved easier than finding honesty. She was in the salt market by midmorning, working the fish stalls with her quick hands, and when Sable set himself deliberately in her path she didn't even flinch—just looked up at him with those sharp gray eyes and said, "You're slow. I've been waiting since the bell."

"You warned us."

"I warned you." She shrugged, defiant. "Doesn't mean I'm sorry I sold you first. A girl eats."

"What did they offer you?"

"A hundred crowns and passage off the coast." She said it flatly, daring him to judge. "For pointing at you. That's all I did—point. Said you had nicked fingertips and traveled with Oskar. Didn't say nothing about any map, because I don't know nothing about any map." Her chin lifted. "And then I asked who was paying, and the gray-coat woman's man laughed and said, 'the lady who burned the witch-village at Hollowmere,' like it was a joke. Like burning a village was a *joke*."

The market noise seemed to recede around Sable. "Hollowmere."

"My gran's village." Wren's voice didn't waver, but her hands, buried in her coat pockets, had gone white-knuckled. "Ashcloaks did the burning. Crown did the looking away. Gran didn't make it out. So." She shrugged again, too hard. "I pointed at you for money. Then I heard whose money. And I un-pointed."

Sable crouched to her level, ignoring the stares of fishwives and the distant jingle of the customs bell. Every instinct honed in Ironhollow screamed at him to keep moving, keep hidden, trust no one—the discipline of a lifetime. But he had left Ironhollow. He had swallowed a courier's dying secret and walked out of the only safe valley in the world, and somewhere on the poisoned road he had stopped being a man who survived by staying invisible.

"Wren," he said. "I can't promise you gold. I can't promise you safety. Anyone traveling with me is hunted, and the hunting will get worse before it ends. If you come with us, you come knowing that."

"I already know that." She scowled. "You think I'm stupid?"

"I think you're twelve, and brave, and that those aren't the same thing."

Something flickered across her face—too fast to catch, gone before he could name it. Then she thrust out her hand, grimy and quick, the way merchants sealed deals in the salt market.

"Half up front," she said. "Everyone pays me half up front."

Bren, arriving breathless behind them, groaned. "We're going to die in the mountains, and we're hiring a child."

"We're hiring the only person in Marrowgate who's beaten Corvane twice in one day," Sable said, and shook the small hand. It was calloused, and cold, and it gripped like a promise.

Above the market, gulls scattered screaming from the customs house roof, and Sable looked up to see a gray-cloaked figure standing at the rail, watching the crowd below with patient, terrible attention. Too far to see clearly. Close enough to feel seen.

Dame Corvane's reach had crossed the sea and the mountains and a poisoned road to nail his name to a post in a city of strangers. She was patient. She was thorough. And now she knew he had a guide, a smuggler, and a child.

He turned back to his strange, small, newly-sworn company.

"Get what we need," he said quietly. "We leave Marrowgate before the next bell."
