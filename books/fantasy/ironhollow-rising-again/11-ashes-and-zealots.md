## 11. Ashes and Zealots

Dame Corvane had burned her first heretic at fourteen.

Not with her own hands—she had only held the torch while the Ashcloak fathers did the work—but she remembered the smell of the smoke, sweet and wrong, like honey left too long over a flame. She remembered how the crowd had gone quiet, not in horror but in relief. That was the thing no one understood about burning a witch: afterward, the air felt clean. The village could sleep again. Children stopped waking screaming from dreams that were not their own.

She had been one of those children once. Before the fathers came. Before the pyres.

She stood now at the window of the Wayhouse of the Sacred Flame, three days' ride south of Marrowgate, watching pilgrims shuffle toward the great ash-heap where the order's faithful knelt to pray. The Ashcloaks kept their temples beside their graveyards; it saved everyone a journey, the High Father liked to say. Corvane found she appreciated the economy.

Behind her, someone was praying aloud. It was the sound that had woken her an hour before dawn, a man's voice cracked raw with repetition, and she had lain in the dark listening until she could bear it no longer and rose to dress.

"You pray loudly," she said, without turning.

The voice stopped. Then, after a moment, it resumed, softer, as if she had asked for a courtesy rather than issued an insult.

Corvane allowed herself the ghost of a smile. She liked the stubborn ones. They were harder to break, which meant they were worth more when broken.

She finished buckling her sword belt—the crown's steel, though she had not drawn it in eleven years of investigation work—and went down to find breakfast and, if the gods were generous, the man she had ridden three days through freezing rain to meet.

* * *

High Father Malachi Voss received her in a room with no roof.

It was a deliberate thing, she knew. The Ashcloaks built their audience chambers open to the sky so that heaven could watch every bargain struck beneath them. In winter it made negotiations brisk. Snow had drifted across the stone floor overnight, and a novice was sweeping it into a corner with the grim diligence of someone who would be doing the same task again within the hour.

Voss sat on a plain wooden chair at the chamber's center, wrapped in his namesake cloak—grey wool gone stiff with old ash—and he was thinner than his reputation. Reports described him as a mountain of a man. The reality was a scarecrow with burn-scarred hands and eyes the pale grey of wood that has given up being anything but fuel.

"Dame Investigator." His voice was gravel poured slow. "You bring a royal seal into the house of a god who does not recognize kings."

"Then we understand each other," Corvane said. She did not sit, though he gestured to a second chair. "I'm not here as the crown's hand."

"No?"

"I'm here because we want the same dead thing."

A novice brought tea—bitter, smoked over the same fires that cured the cloaks—and retreated. Voss wrapped his scarred fingers around the cup without drinking. Waiting. He was good at waiting; she could see it in him, the same patience she practiced, the patience of people who had learned that haste was how innocents got spared and guilty men escaped.

"The Emberhoard," Corvane said.

Something moved behind those grey eyes. Not surprise. Confirmation. "You've read the deep archives."

"I've read everything the crown keeps locked." She set a folded paper on the swept stone between them—a copy, the original never leaving the vault beneath the palace where three locks required three different men's keys. "Treaty of the Sundering Vale. Signed four hundred years ago by the last Dragonqueen and the first king of the realm you and I both serve, whether or not your god approves of him."

"Read it to me," Voss said. "I want to hear which parts you think matter."

Corvane almost smiled again. He was testing whether she'd come to trade or to lecture. She crouched—old habit, keeping her hands visible, keeping herself below a seated man's eye line—and unfolded the paper against her knee.

"Clause one," she recited. "'So long as the Undying Ember burns at the heart of the hoard, magic shall remain bound beyond the borders of the Sundering Vale, and the realm shall know peace.' Clause two: 'Should the ember perish by any hand, the binding dies with it, and all covenants sworn between dragonkind and mankind are void.'" She looked up. "There is no clause three. There is no provision for what happens *after*. Because four hundred years ago, no one believed anyone would be fool enough to end it."

"And yet," Voss said, "here you sit."

"Here I sit."

He drank his tea at last. Outside, the snow had begun again, falling straight down through the open roof, settling on the stones between them like the world's slowest siege.

"The crown's fear," Voss said, "is that if the ember dies, the dragons come back. Or worse—that everything else comes back. Every hedge-witch, every blood-priest, every thing that used to wear a human face and wear it badly." He tilted his head. "That is the crown's fear. What is yours, Dame Corvane? You didn't ride three days through sleet to protect a treaty. Men who love treaties send clerks."

Corvane rose. She walked to the chamber's edge, where the wall began, and put her palm flat against the cold stone.

"When I was six," she said, "a mage lived in our village. A good one, by every account. He healed cattle. He blessed the harvest. He pulled a drowning boy out of the millpond with water that moved like it loved him." Her voice stayed level. It always stayed level; that was the discipline her whole life was built on. "Then the fever year came, and his magic turned. Or he turned. The histories disagree. What I remember is my mother holding the door shut with her body while something wearing Master Ellery's face asked, very politely, to come in and make us well. My father died making sure it couldn't. My mother followed him within the year—not from wounds. From what she'd seen."

She turned back to face him.

"The Ashcloaks tell villages that magic is a disease. The crown tells its investigators that magic is a weapon. Both lies. Magic is a *gamble*, High Father. Every generation it stays dormant is a generation the wager holds. And somewhere out there right now is a food-taster carrying a map to relight the stake."

Voss set down his cup. For a long moment the only sound was snow.

"You know about the taster," he said.

"I know a courier died in Ironhollow with a map in his effects. I know the taster lied to me in the great hall—beautifully, fluently, a man lying for the first time in a life built on truth, which makes it the most dangerous kind of lie there is. And I know he's gone. Vanished the same week. Through Oskar's pass, if the smuggler's ledgers can be believed, and they can, because I bought the smuggler's landlady." She let that sit. "Your order lost two cells trying to intercept him on the northern road. Burned wagons, dead brothers. Your network is loud, High Father. Mine is quiet. That's why I'm here."

"We don't need your quiet," Voss said. "We have fire."

"Fire is why you have dead brothers." She crossed back toward him. "Listen to me. Sable Quintana is not a mage. He's not a zealot. He's not even brave, by the look of him—he's a careful man, a small man, a man who spent thirty years learning to be unnoticeable. Which means he cannot be frightened into speed or flattered into error. But he *can* be outrun, outspent, and outlasted. And unlike your faithful, my warrant lets me do all three legally."

"And what does the crown get?"

"The ember destroyed before it can be used. Quietly. Completely. No relics, no fragments, no 'sparks preserved for study.' Ash, and then silence." She paused. "And the dragons die unwitnessed, which is a mercy your order preaches and never practices. You burn the innocent along with the guilty because you can't tell them apart. I'm offering you precision."

Voss laughed then—a dry, rusted sound, like a gate opening after a long war. "Precision. From the woman whose order hanged a healer in Duskmarrow because he set bones too well."

Corvane didn't flinch. "Yes. That was done under my warrant. I reviewed the evidence after. He was innocent." She met the pale eyes steadily. "I signed the order knowing the review might say exactly that, because in a famine you cut the grain even if some of it's still green. I have never pretended to be clean, High Father. I'm offering you effective. You can take it or keep losing men to a smuggler and a pickpocket girl."

That landed. She watched it land—the slight stillness in him, the recalibration. So they knew about Wren already. Good. It meant her information was ahead of theirs, not behind.

"The council will resist working with the crown," Voss said at last. "Four generations of Ashcloaks have died calling the throne soft."

"Then don't call it partnership. Call it what it is." She took up the treaty copy and held it out to him. "Two people who have both seen what magic does when it wakes up, agreeing that this time it doesn't get the chance. I'll give you the taster's route, his likely contacts, and my authority to hold any town that shelters him. You give me your fire when I ask for it—and only when I ask."

Voss took the paper. He read it longer than its length deserved, and she understood: he wasn't reading clauses. He was reading her.

"My sister was taken by a blood-cult," he said abruptly. "When we were children. Up in the Thornlands, before the order reached that far. They used her for—" He stopped. His scarred hands closed slowly around nothing. "They found what was left of her in a circle of salt, and the mages who did it were never caught, because the local lord *protected them*. Paid them for weather-charms." He looked up. "You gave me your childhood. Fair trade. Now you have mine."

"I'm sorry," Corvane said, and meant it, which surprised her. Grief recognized grief across any distance, even between people who would burn each other's worlds if the wind shifted.

"Don't be sorry. Be fast." Voss stood, unfolding to his full height, and the scarecrow became something else—something with a spine of iron under the ash-grey wool. "My riders say the taster passed through Marrowgate nine days ago. Bought horses, bought a guide. There was trouble at the Saltmarket—a bounty, your bounty, posted wide enough to wake every thief in the free cities. Wide enough, Dame Corvane, to teach the man exactly how much danger he's in and exactly whom to trust." His mouth twisted. "Your quiet methods."

"The bounty flushed him into the open," Corvane said. "Now I know his face is known, his coin is counted, and his companions are a liability he'll hesitate to spend. Fear narrows a man's options. Narrow options follow predictable roads." She pulled her cloak tight. "He's heading for the Ashen Peaks. There's nothing else on that map worth dying for."

"Then you know where he's going."

"I know where he's going. What I don't know—" and here she hesitated, the first hesitation she'd shown him, deliberately, because a negotiator who seemed omniscient made allies nervous— "is what happens when he gets there. The archive says the Dragonqueen still lives. Four hundred years old, guttering, mad with waiting. If Quintana reaches her with the map, she'll read him like a letter. And whatever she finds written in him, I need to know it before he does."

Voss studied her. "You sound as if you fear the dragon less than the taster."

"I fear certainty," Corvane said. "In him, in her, in your council, in myself. Certainty is what magic breathes. Doubt is the only ash that doesn't burn back." She moved toward the chamber's exit, then paused at the threshold, snow gathering on her shoulders from the open sky above. "One more thing, High Father. When we find him—if we find him—I'll be the one who decides how he dies. Not your fire. My warrant. Swear it."

"And if he's already given himself to the dragon?" Voss asked softly. "If he stands at the hoard with the ember in his hands and magic rising off him like heat off a forge—what does your warrant do then, Dame Corvane?"

She looked out past the temple walls, south and east, toward mountains she had never seen and had been dreaming about since she was six years old and a door wouldn't stay shut.

"Then," she said, "we burn it all."

* * *

She rode out before noon, alone, against Voss's offer of escort. The road north climbed through pine country where the snow lay blue in the shadows, and Corvane rode it thinking not of the taster, nor the dragon, nor even the treaty folded against her breast, but of Master Ellery's face at her mother's door—polite, patient, *kind*—and how the worst things in the world never announced themselves with teeth.

Kindness was how it started. Mercy was how it spread. A healing here, a blessing there, and twenty years later a village was kneeling to something with too many joints in its smile.

That was why the ember had to die cleanly. Not vengeance. Surgery. Cut the rot before the body learned to live with it.

By dusk she reached the crossroads shrine where her informant had promised word, and found it: a strip of linen tied to the stone marker, coded in the dye-patterns her network used. She read it twice by the light of her hooded lantern.

*Sable Quintana passed through the bone-valley approach three days ago. Party of three. The girl sings to the mountains.*

Corvane stood very still in the falling snow.

Sings to the mountains. Magic-touched. A child, by the report's tone, and traveling with the taster—which meant either hostage or convert, and neither possibility improved her night.

She fed the linen to her lantern and watched it curl, and thought, with something uncomfortably close to tenderness, *another door that won't stay shut.*

Then she mounted, turned her horse north into the dark, and rode toward the peaks where the last dragon waited—and, riding, did not see the second rider who broke cover half a mile behind her, cloaked in ash-grey, sent by a High Father who trusted warrants exactly as far as he could throw their ashes.
