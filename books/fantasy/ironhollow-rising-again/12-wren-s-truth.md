## 12. Wren's Truth

Wren had stolen from priests, smugglers, a duke's mistress, and once—her proudest hour—a tax collector's entire strongbox while he was still sitting on it. She had never stolen from anyone she liked.

That was the problem. She'd only known Sable Quintana for six days, and Bren Oskar for five of those, and somewhere between the salt-locks of Marrowgate and the first hard miles of the mountain road, they had stopped being marks and started being something else. Something with a name she refused to say out loud because names made things true.

So she did what she always did when a thing got too true: she counted her coins in the dark and told herself the story that kept her warm. *You were paid. That's all this is. You were paid by both sides, and you're still owed by one of them, and when this is over you'll walk away rich.*

The story had a hole in it the size of a food-taster who drank poisoned smoke without flinching and then asked if *she* was all right.

She sat up in the lean-to they'd built against a fallen pine, fire down to coals, Bren snoring like a landslide two arm-lengths away. Sable had the last watch. He sat with his back to a stone, the scorched map across his knees even though there was no light to read it by, turning it over the way other men turned worry-beads.

Wren watched him for a long time. Then she got up, quiet as her own shadow, and crossed the camp before she could talk herself out of it.

"I was paid," she said.

Sable didn't startle. She'd noticed that about him—he startled at nothing, which in her experience meant a man had been frightened so often he'd worn the fear smooth. He looked up at her, coal-light catching the flat gray of his eyes.

"It's cold," he said. "Sit down."

"That's not—" She crouched anyway, arms around her knees, because her legs had decided for her. "Both sides. Do you understand? The Ashcloaks paid me to find where you slept in Marrowgate. Corvane's people paid me to find where you *bought passage*. I took both purses. I sold your inn to the zealots and your smuggler to the crown, and then—" Her voice snagged on something. She pushed through it the way she'd push through a crowd. "And then I saw the Ashcloaks' faces when they talked about burning you. Not killing you. *Burning.* And I saw yours, when you breathed their smoke and didn't die, and the first thing you did was check if Bren and I could breathe."

"You saved our lives," Sable said. "In the alley off Tanner's Row. Whatever else you did, that's also true."

"Both things are true." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "That's the worst part. Both things are true, and I can't pick one and be done with it. So." She dug into her coat and pulled out the second purse—the Ashcloak silver, thirty pieces stamped with a flame—and set it on the ground between them. Then the crown's coin, heavier, colder. "There. Everything I earned off you. I want it back to be yours, but I don't know how to give a man back his own blood, so just—take it. Burn it. Feed it to a goat."

Sable didn't touch the money. He watched her the way he watched the king's wine, she thought—like a man reading poison in the surface tension.

"Why tell me now?" he asked. "We're four days from anywhere. You could have waited until the bounty men caught up and pointed at us from a safe distance."

"Because you'll be safer knowing." The words came out faster than she meant them to. "Because if they take me—if either side takes me—I'll talk. Everyone talks eventually; I've made enough people talk to know. And because—" Here it was. The hole in the story, wide open, wind howling through. "Because I don't want to run anymore. I've been running since I was nine and I'm tired, and you two are going somewhere that matters, and I want to come. Please. I'm fast, I'm quiet, I can read a crowd better than anyone you'll ever meet, and I already know every way this road can kill you because I've been paid to learn them."

The coals ticked. Somewhere upslope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"No," Sable said.

It wasn't cruel. That was somehow worse. It was the gentle, practiced no of a man who had spent his life refusing things carefully.

"Wren. You've been paid by the people hunting us. If I let you walk beside me, every mile we go, I have to wonder. And worse—you'd have to wonder about yourself. What you'd do when it got hard. That kind of doubt gets people killed on this road."

"I wouldn't—"

"You might not mean to. But doubt doesn't need you to mean anything." He gathered the map, folded it away into his coat. "Take the money. Take the head start. By morning you'll be glad, or you won't, but you'll be alive either way, and that's more than I can promise you with us."

He went back to his watch. Wren stood there with two purses of blood-money at her feet and the specific, exquisite misery of having done the right thing and been punished for it.

Bren hadn't been asleep after all. She heard him shift, heard the wet rasp of him clearing his throat, and didn't look over. She wasn't ready for whatever was in the old smuggler's face.

* * *

She gave them until dawn. It was the decent interval, she decided—the time it would take them to break camp and get moving, so that when she fell in behind them they couldn't send her back without losing half a day arguing. She knew these mountains' skirts better than they did; she'd grown up in the warrens beneath Marrowgate and learned the high paths from a goatherd who'd traded lessons for her silence about his still. There was a cut above the river gorge that would let her keep pace without ever being seen, and if they thought they'd lost her, well. People believed what was convenient.

They broke camp at gray light. Sable said nothing about the purses—she'd left them stacked under a stone where he'd find them, minus nothing, plus the little brass charm she'd lifted off an Ashcloak sergeant, because spite was free. Bren squinted around the trees like a man missing something he couldn't name, shrugged, and started walking.

Wren took the high path.

By midday she was a quarter mile ahead and parallel, moving through pine shadow and scree, eating the heel of bread she'd saved. The road below bent along the river, and she could see the two of them small as toys—Bren rolling like a barrel, Sable precise as a needle. She felt, absurdly, like a shepherd. Or a ghost. Or a girl following the only family she'd found in eleven years of fending for herself, which was the truth of it, and she made herself look at that truth straight-on because she'd promised herself no more comfortable stories.

That was when she heard the humming.

At first she thought it was the river. But the river was below and behind, and the sound came from everywhere—from the rock under her boots, from the air, from the ridge of black peaks to the north like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. A low, vast note, felt in her teeth and the backs of her knees and somewhere behind her sternum where she kept the things she didn't say.

She stopped. The humming didn't.

It rose and fell, slow as breathing. And—it was mad, she knew it was mad—but it seemed to notice her. To bend toward her the way a fire bends toward a draft. The hair on her arms lifted. Warmth spread through her chest, golden and terrifying, and for one suspended heartbeat Wren felt like the most important thing on the mountain, like the whole humming world had turned its face toward her and smiled.

Then she remembered what the Ashcloak sergeant had said, that night in Marrowgate, counting out her silver: *The touched ones hear it first. The mountains sing to their own. We burn the touched ones, girl, and we pay well for their names.*

Her name was worth thirty pieces of flame-stamped silver.

Wren ran. Not away—down, toward the road, crashing through brush with thorns opening her forearms, because the only two people in the world who might believe her were down there, and one of them had drunk poison smoke like spring water and lived.

She burst onto the road twenty yards ahead of them, wild-haired, bleeding, and both men went for weapons—Bren a knife, Sable something small and dark from his sleeve, gods, what did the taster carry?—and she threw her hands up.

"It's me! It's Wren, don't—just listen, please, you have to listen—"

"You followed us," Sable said. No surprise. Of course no surprise.

"The mountains are singing." The words tumbled out of her, graceless, enormous. "Right now. Right now, this minute. Can't you hear it? It's like—like a bell under the world, and it got louder when I came up the high path, and it *looked* at me, I know how that sounds, I know, but the Ashcloaks told me—they told me the touched ones hear it, they hunt the ones who hear it, and I hear it, Sable, I've heard it my whole life and I thought everyone did, I thought it was just what mountains *were*—"

She ran out of air. The road was very quiet. The river muttered below. And underneath everything, steady and patient and vast, the hum went on, and she could see on their faces that they heard nothing at all.

Bren lowered his knife slowly. "Girl," he said, gentle in a way she'd never heard him, "there's nothing but wind up here."

"There's not." Her voice shook. She hated that it shook. "Sable. You drank their smoke and didn't cough. You told me it rolled off you like rain off wax. You're not afraid of poison because poison can't find a hold in you. Why? Why is that?"

Something moved behind Sable's eyes—something old and guarded shifting its weight.

"Because I built it," he said. "Dish by dish. Year by year. That's all."

"Is it?" Wren stepped closer. The hum swelled as she moved, sweet and low, and she saw him hear it—not the hum itself, but the change in her, the way the air around her seemed to lean. "Then why do the zealots call you untouched? Why did their sergeant cross himself when he said your name? Sable, I sold information about you to both sides. I read every scrap of paper that crossed those tables. Corvane's investigators wrote that you were born in Ironhollow, where magic dies, and that you left it *six days ago*, and that the smoke didn't touch you. Six days. Poison-immunity takes years. I know—I used to sell antidotes to men who bought them too late." She swallowed. "Whatever you are, you've been it longer than you've been out of that valley. And whatever I am—" she pressed her fist against her sternum, where the warmth still glowed, "—I think it's the same thing. I think we're the same thing. And I think that's why the map picked you, and why I can't make myself leave you alone, and why the mountains are singing at me like I'm somebody's daughter coming home."

The silence stretched. Bren looked between them like a man watching a fuse burn.

Sable took off his pack, sat down on a boulder at the roadside, and was silent for so long that Wren started composing her retreat. Then he patted the stone beside him.

"Sit," he said. "Tell me about the humming. All of it. When it started, what it sounds like, whether it changes near water, near iron, near people." A breath. "All of it. Because there's a dragon at the end of this road, Wren, and a hoard full of fire older than any kingdom, and I am beginning to suspect that neither of us understands what we're carrying."

Wren sat. The stone was cold through her trousers, and the hum wrapped around her like a blanket, and Bren grumbled and built a fire nobody needed because some comfort was older than sense.

"So," she said, and told him everything.

* * *

They walked the rest of the day together, three abreast where the road allowed, single file where it narrowed along the gorge. Wren catalogued the hum aloud as they went—louder near the river's source, quieter where an old landslide had buried the road in dead gray rock, gone entirely for ten paces beside a shrine to some forgotten god whose icon had been chiseled away. Sable listened the way she imagined he tasted: completely, missing nothing, asking questions that cut to the joint.

Bren mostly walked behind and watched the back-trail, but once, passing a cliff face veined with quartz, the old smuggler stopped and frowned and said, "Huh," and when Wren asked, he said, "Nothing. Felt like someone walked over my grave," and she filed that away with a small fierce hope.

They made camp early, in a hollow with the river's noise for cover. While Bren cooked, Sable drew the map out again and laid it flat, and Wren leaned over his shoulder, and for the first time she saw it whole—the peaks, the passes, the valley of bones marked in faded ink, and at its heart a symbol like a flame inside a circle that matched, exactly, the brand on the Ashcloaks' silver.

"They inverted it," she said. "Their flame. Look—it's the same mark, but the fire's pointing down instead of up."

Sable was quiet a moment. "They didn't invent their hatred," he said finally. "They inherited it. From something that knew exactly what this symbol means." His finger traced the road ahead, the switchbacks climbing into the black teeth of the Ashen Peaks. "Which means they've always known where this leads. They're not chasing us, Wren. They're herding us—or waiting for us to lead them somewhere they've wanted to go for a long time."

"Then we shouldn't lead them," Bren said from the fire.

"We can't help it. They'll follow the map whether we carry it or not." Sable rolled the parchment closed. His jaw was tight; Wren was learning to read the small weather of his face. "What we can choose is who's beside us when we arrive."

He looked at her when he said it. Not past her. At her.

"I'm still deciding about you," he said. "I want that honest. But you told me the truth when lying was cheaper, and you came back when running was smarter, and you hear a song the rest of the world has gone deaf to." He almost smiled; it was a strange, rusted thing, like a door opening after years shut. "So for now: you walk with us. And Wren—if the Ashcloaks ever get close enough to touch you, you run. Don't argue, don't warn us, don't be brave. You run, and you live, and you let us be the fools."

"Nobody's ever told me to run *from* danger before," Wren said. "Usually it's run *with* it."

"Well." Sable fed a stick to the fire. "Get used to disappointment."

She laughed—really laughed, for the first time in days—and the sound went up into the dark, and far to the north, under the black ridgeline of the Ashen Peaks, the humming answered.

It was louder than it had been that morning. Closer. And threaded through the great slow bell-note now, unmistakable, was something new: a second voice, thin and ragged, like a singer at the end of a long song who refuses to stop.

Wren went still. Sable saw her go still, and read her, because he always did.

"What is it?"

"She's dying," Wren whispered. Her hand found her sternum again, pressing against the golden warmth. "The one who's singing. Sable—the song is ending. We have to hurry."

Down the valley, miles behind them, a red spark kindled in the dark: torchlight, many torches, moving with the patient discipline of people who believed the world belonged to them.

Nobody spoke. Nobody needed to. They began striking camp in the dark, quick and silent, three people who had been strangers a week ago learning the shape of each other's hands by firelight—because whatever was coming up that road behind them, and whatever was waiting at the end of the song ahead, they were going to meet it together.
