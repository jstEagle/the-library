## 13. The Poisoned Road

The water was wrong before anyone drank it.

Sable knew it the way he knew most things—through his tongue, through a lifetime of small betrayals learned by heart. They had found the stream in a fold of hills two days out of Marrowgate, a thin silver thread running over grey stone, and it should have been clean. Mountain water. Snowmelt and granite. But when he crouched at its edge and dipped two fingers, he caught it: a faint sweetness clinging to the surface of the current, like honey stirred into wine, so delicate that anyone who hadn't spent twenty years training their mouth to be a graveyard of poisons would have missed it entirely.

He spat into the grass and did not drink.

"Problem?" Bren asked, working the buckle on his pack. The smuggler had a way of asking questions that made them sound like accusations.

"Someone's been upstream." Sable rose, scanning the tree line where the pines crowded close and dark. "Bitterroot extract. Maybe hemlock cut with something sweeter to hide the smell. It's clever work—not meant to kill outright. Meant to slow you down."

Wren crouched beside him, peering at the water with the flat suspicion she gave everything. "Slow us down for what?"

"For whoever's following." He looked at her. "For whoever's *ahead*."

They filled their skins from the rain barrel of an abandoned shepherd's hut instead, and Sable told himself the tightness in his chest was only the altitude. But he tasted the sweetness again that evening, faintly, in the air itself, and understood that they were being herded. Poisoned streams behind them. Open ground ahead. Someone wanted them moving along a particular road, tired and thirsty and predictable.

*Corvane*, he thought. Or the Ashcloaks. By now it hardly mattered which; both wanted him stopped, and both had learned that Sable Quintana could not be taken by force alone.

* *

It happened at dusk on the third day, in a place called the Hollow Ford, where the road dipped between two ridges and crossed a stream over stones worn smooth as knuckles.

Later, Sable would replay it a hundred times and never find the moment it went wrong. There had been no arrow, no shout, no ambush in any shape he recognized. Just Bren kneeling at the ford to refill his flask—the same flask, Sable realized with a cold drop in his stomach, that Bren had filled from the poisoned stream two days ago because he'd been too impatient to wait for the rain barrel—and then Bren simply not getting up.

"Bren?" Wren's voice cracked high across the water.

The smuggler was on his hands and knees in the shallows, and even from ten paces Sable could see the wrongness of him. The tremor in his arms. The grey creeping up under his jaw. When they dragged him onto the bank he tried to swear at them and produced only a wet, rattling whisper.

Sable took his wrist. The pulse was there but wandering, stumbling like a drunk trying to walk a chalk line. He peeled back Bren's eyelid and saw the pupils contracted to pinpricks, and beneath the man's ears, along the line of the jaw, the skin had begun to flush a bruised violet.

"No," Sable said quietly. It wasn't bitterroot. Bitterroot killed fast and ugly, but it didn't do *this*. This was slower. This was the kind of poison that waited days in the body, coiled around the blood like a snake in tall grass, striking only when the victim was far from help. "This is widow's mercy. Who taught these people pharmacy?"

"What?" Wren's voice pitched toward panic. "What does that mean? Is he dying?"

"He's dying," Sable said, because lying to her would cost more time than the truth. "Unless I can stop it."

He turned Bren on his side, checked his breathing—shallow, with that sweetish odor rising off his skin that Sable had smelled once before, years ago, on a courtier who'd died over three terrible days while three physicians shook their heads. Widow's mercy. A taster's nightmare: rare enough that most healers never saw it, slow enough that its victims always died somewhere inconvenient, far from any remedy.

But Sable had seen it. And more than seen it—he had spent one long winter of his apprenticeship, back when he was still building his immunity dish by dish, dosing himself with trace amounts of every toxin the palace apothecary could source, including a vial of widow's mercy so old its label had crumbled. He remembered the taste of it. He remembered the antidote his master had forced down his throat afterward, foul as grave-dirt, and he remembered the recipe because remembering recipes was the whole of who he was.

"Hemlock ash," he said aloud, cataloguing. "No. That's for the cramping. I need—" His mind moved through the formula like fingers through a familiar drawer. "Milkthistle seed, crushed raw. White horehound. Something bitter enough to make him vomit the rest of his bile. And yarrow, a great deal of yarrow, boiled down to tar." He looked up at the darkening ridges. "All of it grows within a mile of here. It has to. It's the right season."

"You're going to *forage* him back to life?" Wren demanded.

"I'm going to try. You're going to keep us alive while I do it." He nodded up-slope, where the road climbed away north. "Because whoever poisoned that stream will want to know if it worked. They'll come to check."

Wren went very still. Then she unslung the short bow she'd carried since Marrowgate without once explaining where a pickpocket had learned to use it, and something in her face closed like a door. "How long?"

"An hour. Maybe less if the plants are close."

"Then hurry."

* *

Sable ran.

Not the blind panicked running of a man fleeing—he had done that once, leaving Ironhollow at dawn with his savings burned and his records poisoned to fake his death, and he still dreamed of it—but the controlled, furious running of a man racing a clock inside another man's veins. Widow's mercy gave him perhaps four hours before the paralysis reached the chest. Two of those were already gone. That left him time to find five plants, build a fire hot enough to reduce them, and pray that memory was a thing you could brew.

The hillside above the ford was sheep-cropped grass and gorse, and the gorse saved him, because gorse loves poor soil and so does milkthistle. He found the first stand of it in a hollow below a leaning boulder, the purple-veined leaves unmistakable even in the failing light. He dug the seeds out with his knife, filled his pockets, kept moving. Horehound grew along the old wall that wandered the slope like a fossilized snake—of course it did, horehound loved ruins, loved the places where people had suffered and left. Yarrow he found last and most abundantly, white flower-heads nodding in the wind like a congregation, and he harvested armfuls of it, stems and all.

By the time he scrambled back down to the ford, full dark had come, and the camp had changed.

Wren had dragged Bren beneath the overhang of the far bank and built the fire small and shielded, a coin of light visible only from directly overhead. She herself was nowhere in the firelight—and then Sable saw her, thirty yards up the near ridge, a shadow against shadows, bow half-drawn, watching the road.

"How is he?" Sable breathed as he passed her.

"Worse. He's talking to someone who isn't there. His mother, I think." Her voice was steady but her knuckles on the bowstring were white. "Go."

Bren lay curled on his side, shivering violently now, lips grey, the violet flush spread down his neck. His eyes rolled toward Sable and focused with an effort that seemed physical. "*Taster,*" he rasped. It might have been a plea or a curse.

"Hold still." Sable built the fire up, set the yarrow boiling in their smallest pot, and began to work.

This was the part no one understood about his trade. They thought a food-taster was a man who swallowed and lived or swallowed and died, a coin flipped at the king's table. But the truth was that tasting was the smallest part of it. The real work was knowledge—a library of symptoms and countermeasures carried in one man's skull, refined over centuries by tasters who had died learning them, passed hand to hand like a candle flame through a dark house. Sable had memorized two hundred antidotes before his twentieth year. He had tested forty-one of them on himself. He had watched six fail.

He crushed the milkthistle seeds with the flat of his knife, smelling the green bitterness bloom. Shredded the horehound. Pulled the yarrow decoction off the fire when it had gone thick and black as pitch. Combined, adjusted, tasted—

—and there it was. The flavor unlocked the rest of the memory like a key: his master's kitchen, the winter cold, the boy he had been swallowing the antidote and weeping from the bitterness while the old man held his head and counted his pulse. *Again,* Master Corlo had said. *A remedy you can't reproduce from taste alone is a remedy you don't know.* Sable added a pinch more horehound. Tasted again. Nodded.

"Sit him up. Carefully."

Wren had come down silently at some point; she helped him prop Bren against her pack, the smuggler's head lolling, breath coming in shallow sips. Sable got the cup to his lips.

"Don't," Bren whispered.

"It tastes like a swamp's funeral. Drink it anyway."

He poured it in slowly, letting Bren swallow in his own time, wiping what spilled with his sleeve. Then they waited. Sable kept two fingers on the man's pulse and counted, and counted, and the pulse wandered and stumbled and—

Vomited. Bren convulsed suddenly and brought up a flood of sour bile and half-digested supper, and Sable held his head through it and felt something in his own chest loosen by a single degree. The purge was the sign. The antidote worked by forcing the body to expel what it could and burn out the rest, and the burning was the bad part—there would be fever, hours of it, the poison and the cure fighting over the territory of one stubborn smuggler's blood.

"More," Sable said, mixing a second dose. "Half strength, every hour until dawn."

Bren drank this time without being told. Before his eyes rolled shut, he caught Sable's wrist with a grip that still had iron in it. "*Knew* you were worth the share," he slurred, and slept.

* *

The attack came an hour past midnight.

Sable heard nothing—his world had narrowed to the fire, the pot, the rhythm of doses—but Wren's whistle split the night, two sharp notes, and then the ridge erupted.

There were four of them, maybe five, shapes flowing down the slope with the unhurried confidence of men who had expected no resistance. Ashcloaks. Even in the dark Sable knew them by the pale sashes at their throats, the ash-white marks painted across their faces. They came with blades and a net and one carried a clay jar that Sable, watching from below, identified with a lurch: smoke-pot. Burning ashweed. The fumes dropped a grown man in minutes and left the survivors pliable.

Wren met them at the top of the bank.

He had never seen her fight. In Marrowgate she had been all quick fingers and quicker lies, a child playing at danger. This was different. She loosed from cover, dropped the net-carrier with an arrow through the meat of his shoulder, and was moving before he finished screaming—sliding down the scree, loosing again, not to kill but to herd, bending their approach away from the fire, away from Bren, buying seconds the way she'd once bought bread with other people's coins.

But there were too many. Sable saw the moment it tipped: two of them flanked wide through the gorse, and Wren couldn't watch all three lines at once, and the one with the clay jar broke free and came down the slope straight toward the fire, toward Bren, toward Sable.

The jar shattered at the fire's edge. Ashweed smoke rolled out, sweet and acrid, the smell of zealotry, and the lead Ashcloak advanced through his own cloud with his blade low, certain.

Sable stood up inside the smoke.

He heard the man's breath catch—the little human stumble of a predator whose prey has done something impossible. Sable breathed deep, deliberately, filling his lungs with the fumes that had killed a dozen of the king's enemies and would have dropped any ordinary man where he stood. Twenty years of dosing. Twenty years of swallowing death in increments so his body would learn its face. Every dish, every trace, every winter morning of bitter powders and held breath—it all came due at once.

The smoke tasted like nothing. Like a lie someone else believed.

"Your poison," Sable said, stepping forward out of the cloud, voice rough but level, "is amateur work. Hemlock base, ashweed carrier, cut with honey to mask it. I've had better in soup." He picked up the boiling pot of yarrow tar, still steaming, and held it out like a lantern. "Now. You can take that blade to my friend and learn what I do to people who threaten my patients. Or you can carry a message back to whoever sent you."

The Ashcloak stared. Behind him, Wren's third arrow took the flanker in the thigh, and the man's scream broke whatever spell held the leader. They withdrew the way water withdraws, fast and shapeless, dragging their wounded, and the night folded shut behind them.

Wren slid down the bank and landed beside Sable, breathing hard, bow still raised, eyes enormous. "You *breathed* it."

"I did."

"And you're fine."

"I'm fine tonight." He looked down at his hands. They were shaking now, late and honest. "That's the trouble with immunity, Wren. It isn't a wall. It's a purse. Every time you spend it, there's less."

She frowned. "Less what?"

"Less margin." He crouched by the fire and checked Bren—feverish, muttering, but breathing deeper, the violet flush receding from his jaw like a tide going out. Alive. He would live. Sable felt the relief hit him sideways, almost buckling, and let himself sit down hard on the cold stone.

The price, though. The price was real. He had burned through reserves that took years to build—the smoke-poison tolerance, the deepest layer of his armor, the one he'd paid for with a week of blindness and a month of nightmares when he was seventeen. Rebuilding it would mean dosing himself again, deliberately, with things that could kill him if his arithmetic slipped by a hair. And his arithmetic had never slipped. But he had never been this tired, this far from Ironhollow, this hunted, either.

Wren sat beside him. For a long time neither spoke. The fire ticked. Bren dreamed loudly of his mother.

"They'll come back with more," Wren said finally. "Zealots always do. It's the whole point of them."

"I know."

"So what's the plan, taster?"

Sable looked east, where the sky was beginning to bruise toward dawn, and beyond it, invisible in the dark, the Ashen Peaks waiting like a held breath. Somewhere up there was a valley of bones and a dragon guttering toward extinction, and a map pressed into his hands by a dying man who had chosen him for reasons Sable was only beginning to understand.

"The plan," he said, "is that we stop traveling like people who can be ambushed." He fed another branch to the fire. "And we move faster. Because I just spent the last of what keeps me safe, and I don't intend to be defenseless when Corvane finds us."

Wren snorted softly. "You're not defenseless. You're terrifying. You drank their poison like tea."

"That," Sable said, watching the flames, "is exactly what worries me."

Behind them, Bren stirred and murmured a name that wasn't his mother's—a woman's name, foreign, spoken with a tenderness the smuggler had never once allowed into his waking voice—and Sable, who noticed everything, filed it away. Whatever had driven Bren Oskar onto this road, it wasn't gold.

Dawn came grey and cold, and with it, on the northern wind, a sound Sable had never heard but knew instantly, the way he knew poison: a distant, vast, weary exhalation, like a bellows the size of a cathedral drawing breath.

Wren's head snapped up. "Tell me you heard that."

"I heard it," Sable said. His heart was hammering, and for the first time since the courier died in his arms, it wasn't fear. "We're closer than I thought."
