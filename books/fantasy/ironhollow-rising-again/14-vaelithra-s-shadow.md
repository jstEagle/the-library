## 14. Vaelithra's Shadow

The Ashen Peaks did not look like mountains so much as the memory of them—shapes worn down by wind and time, gray as old scars, their summits lost in a haze that was not quite cloud. Sable had climbed for three days since the poisoned road, his boots wrapped in strips of blanket because the leather had split, and still the peaks seemed no closer, only larger, the way grief grows larger the longer you carry it.

Wren walked ahead of him, humming under her breath without knowing she did it. She had been humming since the pass. *Hear that?* she'd said the first night, pressing her palm flat against a boulder like it was a horse's flank. *It's louder here.* Sable heard nothing. He had never heard anything. But Bren had gone quiet in a way that wasn't his usual quiet, and when Sable caught him looking at the peaks, the smuggler's face wore an expression Sable recognized from mirrors—the look of a man walking toward something he could not turn away from.

"Snowline's behind us," Wren said now, crouching to touch the ground. "And there's no snow up here anyway. Feel how warm it is?"

Sable knelt beside her. The soil was black and fine as sifted flour, and it was warm—not sun-warm; the sky was the color of dirty pewter—but warm from beneath, faintly, steadily, like the flank of a sleeping animal. He thought of the courier, burned half to death on Ironhollow's threshold, muttering about embers. He thought of the map's scorched edge, which never cooled no matter how long he kept it from flame.

"It's breathing," Wren whispered.

"Don't say things like that," Bren muttered from behind them.

"Why not? It's true."

"Because the last person who said true things on this mountain was my sister, and she didn't come back." Bren shouldered past them both, taking the lead with the aggressive stride of a man who would rather be angry than afraid. "Come on. If we're going to die, I want it done before dark."

* * *

They found the first bone at midafternoon.

Sable saw it before he understood it—a pale curve rising from the black soil like the rib of a buried ship. Then another behind it, and another, and then the valley opened below them and he stopped walking entirely, because there was nothing in his careful, poison-steeped life that had prepared him for this.

Bones. A whole valley of bones.

Ribs vast as cathedral arches, furred with gray moss. Skulls the size of cottages, their eye sockets pooled with shadow, their horns worn smooth by centuries of wind. Some of the great skeletons lay curled, tails wrapped around their own bodies, as if they had lain down to sleep. Others were stretched out facing the same direction—all of them, every one, facing deeper into the valley, toward a darkness at its heart where the air shimmered like heat above a forge.

"They came here to die," Sable said. His voice sounded small and absurd in all that space.

"Not die," Wren said softly. She had stopped humming. Her hand found his sleeve without her seeming to notice. "Look at which way they're facing."

He looked. Every skull, every hollow eye, turned toward the shimmering dark. They had not come here to lie down. They had come here to be near someone.

"To die *beside*," Sable corrected himself quietly. "Beside their queen."

Bren had gone very still. "Mara described this," he said, and his voice cracked on the name like ice on a pond. "In her letters. She wrote—" He stopped, pressed the back of his hand against his mouth, mastered himself the way he mastered everything, badly and at cost. "She wrote that she'd seen a valley of bones and that the bones were warm. I thought she'd gone mad. I thought the sun had cooked her letters' meaning out of her." He stepped forward, off the trail, toward the nearest rib cage, and laid his palm against it the way Wren had touched the boulder. "It's warm," he said. "Gods help me, after twenty years, it's still warm."

Sable did not touch the bones. He had spent his whole life putting things in his mouth and body that would have killed anyone else, and he knew better than most that some warmth is not comfort but fever. Yet he stood among those vast graves and felt something move through him that was not fear—or not only fear—and hated himself a little for the awe of it. In Ironhollow, wonder was a disease you vaccinated yourself against. Here, the vaccination had run out.

"We should make camp," he said, because routine was the last tool left in his kit. "Before dark. We don't know what lives here besides bones."

"Nothing lives here," Bren said. "That's the point of a grave."

But Wren was looking at the shimmering dark at the valley's heart, and her face, lit by that strange heat-haze light, held neither grief nor fear. It held recognition. "Something lives here," she said. "I can hear her thinking."

* * *

They camped in the lee of a skull whose horn curved over them like a shepherd's crook. Sable built no fire—there was no need; the ground breathed its slow warmth up through their bedrolls—and they ate cold salt pork and hard bread while the light failed into a deep violet dusk. The shimmer at the valley's heart did not fade with the sun. If anything it brightened, a smoldering gold visible through the dark, steady as a banked hearth.

Sable took out the map.

He had carried it against his chest since Ironhollow, wrapped in oilcloth, and it had grown warmer each day of the climb until now it nearly burned through the layers. By its own faint glow he traced the lines his mother had drawn—or that had been drawn for her, he still didn't know which—mountains rendered in a hand that shook, valleys marked with symbols he had finally begun to understand: the Emberhoard, the source, the place where magic had been gathered and hidden and hoarded like a dying man's last coin.

His mother. The word had been rearranging itself inside him for days. Not *dead woman who told stories*. Not *the reason I have no family*. But: keeper. The map chose him, Vaelithra would later tell him—no. He didn't know that yet. He only knew that the map was warm, and that he was cold, and that somewhere in the dark ahead something vast was guttering like a candle in its final inch.

"Sable." Wren's voice, barely a breath. "Sable, wake Bren. Wake up. Something's coming."

He felt it before he saw it—a pressure, like the moment before a storm breaks, like the silence in the king's hall just before poison was announced. The warmth in the ground quickened. The bones around their camp seemed to lean inward. And out of the dark between the skeletons came a light, low to the ground, moving with a slowness that was somehow more terrible than speed.

Then the dark itself stood up.

Sable had imagined dragons his whole life the way everyone in Ironhollow imagined them—in storybook miniature, big as a barn, big as a church. Nothing had prepared him for scale that broke the mind's instruments. She rose out of the valley floor like a hill deciding to be alive, wings unfolding wider than the great hall of the king's castle, wide enough to shadow the entire camp, wide enough that Sable instinctively ducked though she was fifty yards away and rising still. Her scales were the gray of ash over embers, and beneath them, along the seams of her throat and the membranes of her wings, ran veins of dull orange light—guttering, flickering, failing, the way a fire fails when the wood is spent. She was vast and she was dying and she was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and every story he had ever been told about magic being dead outside the valley curdled in his chest and burned to nothing.

Vaelithra. The last dragon. And she was looking directly at him.

Her eyes were the size of cartwheels and the color of molten gold going dark at the edges, and they fixed on Sable with an attention so total it felt like being flayed. He could not move. He did not especially want to. Beside him Wren made a sound halfway between a gasp and a prayer, and Bren had gone white as the bones around them, whispering, "Mara, Mara, you were right, you were right," like a man reciting penance.

*You are the taster.*

The voice did not come through his ears. It arrived inside his skull, vast and slow and ancient, with the weight of centuries behind each word, and it was not a question.

"Yes," Sable said. His voice cracked. He cleared his throat and tried again, because if he was going to stand in front of the last dragon in the world, he would do it with a steady voice or die trying. "Yes. I taste the king's food. I've tasted worse than you, probably."

Something moved across her immense face. It might have been amusement. It might have been pain.

*Little poison-eater. You built your armor dish by dish. Arsenic in the morning wine. Hemlock in the honey. You taught your body to welcome what would kill it, because safety was the only country you were ever granted citizenship in.* Her head lowered, closer, close enough that her breath washed over him, hot and smelling of ash and something sweeter underneath, something like the air after lightning. *I know you. I have read you the way you read a cup of wine.*

"You can't—" Sable's hands were shaking. "Those are my thoughts. Those are mine."

*All thoughts are mine, here, in the last mile of my life.* But she drew back a little, and the pressure in his skull eased to something almost gentle. *Forgive me. I have been alone a long time, and your mind is loud with your mother.*

The world went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to stop.

"My mother," Sable said carefully, "has been dead for twenty years."

*Your mother has been dead for twenty years,* the dragon agreed, *and yet here you stand, holding her map, warmed by her keeping, in the valley where she was born.*

The words landed one by one, each with its own small detonation. Born. Not fled *to* somewhere. Fled *from* here. Sable's knees felt unreliable. He sat down in the warm black soil without deciding to, the map glowing against his chest like a second heart.

"She told me stories," he said. "When I was small. Before—" He stopped. Started again. "She told me about a dragon queen who kept a fire that fed all the fires in the world. I thought they were just stories. Everything in Ironhollow is just stories. That's what the valley *is*. A place where the stories can't reach you."

*And she made certain of it.* Vaelithra's great head swung slowly, and the guttering light along her throat pulsed once, dimmer than before. Sable watched it happen and understood, with a taster's clinical clarity, that he was watching a death in progress—slow, enormous, measured in years instead of hours, but a death all the same. *Your mother was the last Keeper of the Emberhoard. The bloodline that tended the fire, that carried it when it needed carrying, that hid it when the world grew too hungry for it. When the treaties broke and the hunts began, she took the map and ran. She traded her magic for your safety, little taster. She poured it out of herself at the valley's border so that no hunter could follow her scent into the dead lands. And she left this map as insurance—for the day the fire grew so low that hiding it would become the same as killing it.*

"That day is today," Wren said quietly. She had crept forward to stand beside Sable, and Vaelithra's golden gaze moved to her, and softened—if a gaze the size of a cartwheel could soften. "Isn't it. That's why the ground is warm. That's why I can hear you thinking. You're burning your last wood to keep the room from going dark."

*You hear truly, small one. You are touched. You know what that makes you to the men in ash-cloth.* Wren flinched, and Sable put a hand on her shoulder without thinking, and the dragon saw, and something ancient and tired moved through her expression. *And you, taster. Do you understand why the map came to you? Why it burned its way across a kingdom to land in the hands of a poison-drinker in a valley where magic cannot live?*

"Because I'm her son," Sable said. His voice came out flat. "Because of blood."

*Because you are her son.* The confirmation should have felt like an answer. Instead it felt like a door closing behind him. *The ember does not choose randomly. It is not a coin to be found. It is a trust to be inherited. Your mother bound it to her line so that it would wait, patiently, through hunger and hiding, until a Keeper came who could carry it.*

"Well, she shouldn't have," Sable said, and heard his own voice rise, and did not care. Twenty years of careful invisibility were cracking open all at once, and everything he'd kept pressed down came up hot. "I didn't ask for this. I didn't ask to be born to a fugitive. I didn't ask to spend my life eating poison so no one would ever look at me twice. I didn't ask for a map, or a bounty, or a zealot army, or a dying dragon reading my mind like a tavern bill. My mother made choices for me before I had a name, and now everyone wants me to finish paying for them—your fire, Corvane's war, the whole rotten inheritance. I'm a food-taster. I keep people alive. That's all I ever wanted to be good at."

The silence after his outburst was enormous. Somewhere downslope, Bren had turned away, his shoulders shaking with something that was not quite laughter and not quite weeping.

Vaelithra regarded him for a long time. The light in her seams guttered, steadied, guttered again.

*Yes,* she said at last, and her voice was gentler than the wind. *That is exactly what a Keeper is. One who takes what would kill others into his own body, and survives it, so that others may live. Did you think your immunity was an accident of trade, little taster? Did you think your mother's blood chose a stranger?*

Sable stared at her. The warm soil seemed to shift beneath him.

*Every dish you survived prepared you. Every poison you swallowed taught your body the shape of holding what must not be spilled. You have been in training your entire life, Sable Quintana, and you never once knew for what.* She lowered her head until her vast eye was level with him, close enough that he could see his own small reflection floating in the gold-and-darkness of it. *Tomorrow, when the sun rises, I will show you the hoard. And you will understand what your mother saved, and what it asks of the one who keeps it. Sleep now. No harm will come to you in this valley. These bones died so that you would have a safe place to decide.*

She withdrew into the dark between the skeletons, folding down and down until she was only a shape again, only a smolder, only the largest gravestone in a valley of them. The pressure lifted from Sable's mind, and he discovered he was trembling, and that Wren's hand was on his shoulder now, mirroring his own gesture from moments before.

"Inheritance," he said to no one. "She calls it an inheritance. Like it's a house. Like it's silver spoons."

"What is it, then?" Wren asked.

Sable looked down at the map glowing against his chest, at the scorched edge that never cooled, and gave the only honest answer he had.

"A debt," he said. "And I don't know yet who holds the note."

Above the valley, the first stars were coming out, and at the heart of the Ashen Peaks, the Emberhoard's glow pulsed once—twice—like a heartbeat counting down.
