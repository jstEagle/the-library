## 15. The Keeper's Blood

The dragon's eye was a window and Sable was falling through it.

Not falling—*sinking*, the way a stone sinks into deep water, slow and unstoppable, the light above him narrowing to a coin, then a spark, then nothing. He had no body here. No tongue with which to taste the air, no hands to clutch at. Only memory, and it was not his own.

He stood in a corridor of green fire.

It stretched away in both directions, impossibly long, its walls made of flame that did not burn but *remembered*—he could feel that much even without skin. Each tongue of fire held an image the way amber holds an insect. He drifted past them because he had no choice, because something vast and patient was carrying him, and the images turned their faces toward him as he passed.

A woman in a valley of wheat, laughing, her hands full of light that spilled between her fingers like water.

The same woman older, running through snow, one hand pressed to her belly, her breath tearing white ribbons behind her.

A border of standing stones, gray and silent, and the moment she crossed it the light went out of her hands—snuffed like a candle pinched between wet fingers—and she screamed, and the scream had no echo, because Ironhollow swallowed echoes too.

Sable knew her face.

He had spent thirty years not thinking about that face, keeping it folded away in the same dark drawer where he kept his mother's voice, the lullaby she'd hummed off-key, the way she'd said his name—*Sable, little shadow, come inside before dark.* He had been seven when she died. He had built his whole careful life on top of her grave like a house built on a well, never looking down, never listening for the water.

Now the water rose around him and there was nowhere left to stand.

*You see her,* said the voice that was not a voice, the thought that moved through him like heat through iron. Vaelithra's mind touched his the way dawn touches a mountaintop—first the highest point, then everything. *Say her name, keeper. Names are doors.*

"Maerin," he said, and the word came out broken. "Her name was Maerin."

*Maerin.* The dragon tasted it. *She chose it herself, you know. The name she wore in your valley. Her true name was longer. It had fire in it.*

The corridor of flames bent, and Sable understood that they were moving—not him, the memories themselves, streaming past like a river seen from beneath. He stopped fighting it. There was a strange mercy in surrender; he had learned that long ago, swallowing poisons, letting each new venom do its work while his blood quietly learned the shape of it. So he let the visions do theirs.

* * *

He saw Ironhollow as it had been before he was born—or rather, as it had been everywhere else, because the vision showed him the world outside first, and the world outside had *color* he had no names for.

Magic, in those years, had not yet begun to fade. It moved through the world like weather. He watched a village where the wells sang back when children sang down them. He watched roads that rearranged themselves overnight to spare travelers the rain. He watched dragons—he watched *dragons*, dozens of them, wheeling over green mountains in formations like living constellations, their shadows crossing the valleys in slow tides, and every field they flew over came up greener that spring, and every child born under their passage dreamed true dreams for a year.

And he watched the fear grow alongside the wonder. He watched kings count dragons the way misers count coins. He watched the first Ashcloak bonfires, small then, almost apologetic—a hedge-witch here, a singing well sealed with stones there. He watched the fires learn confidence.

Then the vision narrowed, the way a fever narrows the world to a single burning point, and the point was his mother.

Young Maerin, walking out of the mountains with a satchel and a secret. She had been a Keeper—not *the* Keeper, one of nine, wardens of the ember's outer shrines, women and men who tended the small fires that fed the great one. He saw her shrine: a cave behind a waterfall where a sliver of ember-light burned in a bowl of black glass, and Maerin feeding it drops of her own blood twice a season, the old covenant, keeper and kept.

He saw the night the Ashcloaks found the waterfall.

He saw her run.

* * *

The running took years. That was what he had never understood, could never have understood—that her flight was not a night but a decade, a long bleeding migration across half the world, always west, always toward the one place in creation where magic could not follow.

Because magic could not enter Ironhollow. Everyone knew that. What no one in the valley knew—what Sable himself had never known—was *why*.

The vision gave him the why, and it was worse than anything he'd imagined.

Ironhollow was not naturally dead ground. It had been *made* dead—unmade, rather—by the last great working of the last great age, when the old kings of the valley had begged the dragons for sanctuary from the purges. And the dragons, dying by the dozen in those years, had paid for the valley's silence with a piece of the ember itself. They had driven a splinter of their own heartfire into the roots of the mountains, and the splinter drank all magic that came near, the way his own blood drank poison. A quarantine. A wound cauterized. Inside the hollow, no spell could live, no dragon could land, no Ashcloak pyre could catch with anything but ordinary wood.

His mother had not fled to a refuge. She had fled into the *scar*.

And she had known the cost. He watched her stand at the border stones, heavy with him, six months gone, and he watched her take the shard of ember-light from her satchel—the heart-shard of her shrine, the thing she had sworn to die protecting—and hold it over the boundary, and weep, and step across anyway. The magic bled out of her like warmth out of an open hand. He felt it go. Even secondhand, even in memory, he felt it: the sudden terrible quiet, the world going flat and flavorless, every color dimming one shade. She pressed her hand to her belly and whispered, "Better this than the fire, little one. Better dull and alive."

But she had not surrendered entirely. Keepers were not made to surrender.

In the vision, on the far side of the stones, in the gray safe silence of Ironhollow, Maerin knelt in the mud and did the last work of her life. She took the heart-shard—the ember's own seed, the fragment from which, in the old days, new dragons had been kindled—and she broke it into two pieces with her bare hands, and the act tore a sound out of her that Sable would hear for the rest of his nights.

One piece she pressed into the earth at the border, a seed buried against the day the world grew safe again.

The other piece she kept. Ground small. Mixed with ochre and ash and the tallow of a rendered candle. Painted onto a sheet of cured hide in lines that looked, to any ordinary eye, like the markings of a merchant's road map.

The map that a burned courier had died pressing into his hands.

*Insurance,* Vaelithra's voice said, gentle as ashfall. *That is what she called it, in the letter she sent us, the one that took eleven years to reach me through hands I dare not count. 'If my son ever comes to you,' she wrote, 'it will mean the world has grown dangerous enough that even dullness cannot save him. Give him the choice I never had.'*

* * *

The fire-corridor opened, and Sable fell out of memory into the present so hard his knees struck stone.

He was in the hoard. He had forgotten the hoard. The cavern spread around him in the ember-light, mountains of gold and bone and glassy scale, and above him Vaelithra's head hung low, her eye level with his face, vast as a harvest moon and guttering like a lamp out of oil. The light in her had dimmed since yesterday. He could see the darkness moving in her the way smoke moves under a door.

"You *knew*," he said. His voice came out raw. "All of it. You've known since the gate, since the courier—"

*I have known since before your birth, keeper. I have waited thirty years for the map to move.*

"Then why—" His hands were shaking. He looked at them as if they belonged to someone else. "Why give it to a *taster*? Why not send for me? Why let me be hunted across half the world, why let Bren's sister die, why let Wren—"

*Would you have come?*

The question landed like a blade laid flat against his neck.

"If a dragon's summons had arrived in Ironhollow," Vaelithra continued, and her thought-voice was very quiet now, very careful, the way he spoke to frightened animals and dying men, *if a royal writ had named you Keeper and commanded you to leave the only magic-dead ground in the world—you would have burned the letter. You would have told yourself it was a trap. You are a man who tests everything, Sable Quintana. Your king's food. Your own blood. Would you have swallowed a destiny served to you whole?*

"I might have," he said. "I'll never know, will I? Because instead you let a man burn half to death to carry it to me. You let—" His voice cracked and he hated it, hated the crack, hated the wet heat behind his eyes. "Bren's sister is dead. Or worse. And Wren hears humming in the mountains, and every Ashcloak from here to the sea wants her on a pyre, and Corvane is somewhere behind us with an army, and you're telling me this was *insurance*. That my whole life was a letter someone mailed thirty years ago."

*Yes.*

"No." He got to his feet. His legs held, barely. "No. You don't get to dress it up. My mother didn't choose this for me—she ran from exactly this. She cut the magic out of herself and walked into a hole in the world so her son would never have to stand where I'm standing. And you want me to believe she *planned* this? That burying a seed in the dirt was a death sentence with my name on it?"

*She planned for every future she could see,* Vaelithra said. *Including this one. Including the one where you curse her name. She was a Keeper, Sable. We do not get to be loved for our planning. Only thanked, if we are lucky, after everyone we planned for is grown enough to understand.*

"Well, I'm not thankful." The words came out of him like vomit, hot and ugly and honest. "I didn't ask for this. I didn't ask to be born on the wrong side of a border, or to spend my life eating poison so a fat man in a crown could sleep at night, or to carry the last hope of a dying species in my coat pocket like contraband. I am a *taster*. I taste things. I tell people whether it's safe. Nobody tastes *me*. Nobody asked whether I wanted—"

He stopped. Breathed. The hoard breathed with him, the vast slow respiration of gold and bone.

Vaelithra waited. That was the worst of it—she waited with the patience of a creature who had watched empires compost, and her patience made his rage feel small, and he refused, absolutely refused, to let it feel small.

"The ember," he said finally, when he could trust his voice. "Show me. Not visions. Truth. What does it actually do—what happens if I give it back to you, and what happens if I destroy it? All of it. No more doors, no more corridors. I want the ledger. Both columns."

The dragon's eye closed. Opened. In the depths of it, the guttering light steadied for one moment, gathered itself, and became honest.

*If you return it to me,* she said, *I can reignite the line. Not quickly. Not surely. The bones in this valley—some are too long cold. But many sleep only lightly. With the ember's heart beating again, I could wake perhaps forty. Perhaps sixty. Dragons enough to matter. Dragons enough that the world must reckon with us again—and the world, little keeper, has grown cruel in its cleverness. The last time dragonkind rose, the purges followed within a generation. Magic returned means the pyres return. The Ashcloaks will multiply. Kings will hunt. Children like your Wren will burn by the hundreds before the balance settles—if it settles.*

"And if I destroy it?"

*Then it ends cleanly.* No hesitation. She had clearly turned this over ten thousand times. *No rebirth, but no war either. The old treaty dies with the ember—the crown knows it, which is why Dame Corvane hunts you—and the realm will hold together or shatter on its own merits, without our fire tipping the scales. Dragonkind ends. Quietly. With dignity. The bones in this valley rest, and the world forgets us, and wonder goes out of it like warmth out of a room with an open door. Slowly. So slowly most people won't notice. Most people never notice.*

"And my mother's seed? The piece she buried at the border?"

Something moved through the vast frame above him—not quite a flinch. Grief, maybe, or its dragon-equivalent.

*Buried in dead ground, it sleeps. It cannot wake while the hollow drinks all magic near it. If the ember is destroyed, the seed dies with it. If the ember lives, the seed remains a locked door. Either way, keeper, the choice is yours alone. That is what your mother bought with her exile. Not safety. Choice. The one thing the fire never gives its keepers.*

Sable laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, half sob.

"A choice," he said. "Between waking a war and killing wonder. Between burning children and burying beauty. Some insurance. She traded everything so I could stand in a hole full of gold and pick which world gets to die."

*Yes,* Vaelithra said simply. *That is inheritance. Everyone receives one. Most people merely never learn the terms.*

He turned away from her. He couldn't look at that eye a moment longer—it was too much like being seen by a country—and he walked instead to the edge of the hoard, where the gold gave way to bone, where the smallest of the sleeping dragons lay curled like a dog at its master's door. A young one, once. He put his hand flat against the skull. Cold. But not *stone*-cold. Not yet.

Behind him, faint through the mountain's throat, came a sound he had learned to dread on the road: the far-off cry of a hunting bird. Corvane used them. Three days out, maybe two.

Wren's voice echoed up from the lower passage, sharp with alarm, and Bren's answer, rougher still.

They were out of time, and the ledger lay open in his hands, and both columns ended in ash.

Sable closed his eyes, and for the first time since the courier died against his chest, he prayed—to nothing, to anyone—that his mother had left him one more door she hadn't shown the dragon.
