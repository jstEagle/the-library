## 16. Betrayal at the Hoard

Bren Oskar had spent thirty years learning to read a trail the way other men read scripture. Snow told him weight. Broken fern told him height. The angle of a snapped twig told him hurry or calm. He could stand at the mouth of a pass and know how many riders had come through it, how long ago, and whether they'd been afraid.

So when he crouched at the tree line above the Ashen Peaks and saw the fire-scar on the eastern ridge—fresh, deliberate, burned in a pattern no lightning ever made—he knew the truth before his mind let him say it.

They had been followed. And he had led them here.

* * *

It had started three days back, in the last lowland village before the climb began, the one with the salt-crusted well and the shrine to some dead god whose name the locals had forgotten. Bren had gone in alone for provisions while Sable and Wren waited in the trees with the mules. That was the arrangement. Sable's face was worth more than a cargo of silver now; Corvane's bounty notices hung in every market from Marrowgate eastward, and Bren's own face was known in half the free ports along the coast. So he went in hooded, bought flour and salt pork and a skin of vinegar, kept his eyes down, and left.

He had done everything right. He was sure of it. He had counted the eyes in that village twice.

But there had been one man he hadn't counted—a lean figure by the far wall of the shrine, wrapped in a traveler's gray, who never looked at Bren directly. Not once. Bren remembered him now with the terrible clarity of hindsight: the way the man's boots were wrong for a pilgrim, too good, too quiet. The way he'd been whittling a stick and never once looked down at what his knife was doing.

A tracker. One of Corvane's hounds. And Bren had walked straight past him, out into the open, up the mountain road—and the man had simply risen from his bench and followed, patient as weather.

"You're certain," Sable said now. It wasn't a question, but Bren answered it anyway, because not answering would have been worse.

"Fire-mark in a spiral. Three spirals, actually, spaced a day apart." Bren wiped his palms on his coat. "That's signal code. Old military. My father used it running contraband through the Vellish wars. You burn your position at intervals so whoever's behind you can follow the line even if you lose them in the rocks." He swallowed. "I've seen it used exactly twice in my life. Both times by crown trackers."

Wren stood a little apart, arms crossed tight against the mountain cold, her breath steaming. She'd been quiet all morning—quieter than usual, which for Wren was saying something. Now she spoke without looking at either of them.

"How many?"

"Can't tell from the marks. But the woodsmoke I smelled yesterday, north of the switchbacks—that wasn't ours, and it wasn't hers." He nodded upward, toward the peaks where the dragon slept in her guttering vastness. "Corvane travels with forty men minimum when she leaves the capital. Zealots travel in packs of twelve. If they've joined forces like we think—" He stopped. There was no point finishing a sentence whose end everyone already knew.

Sable turned to look back the way they'd come, down the long white throat of the valley, and Bren watched him do the arithmetic on his face. The food-taster had a stillness about him that Bren had learned to respect and, occasionally, to fear. Most men panicked loudly. Sable panicked inwardly, quietly, and came out the other side with a plan.

"We're half a day from the hoard," Sable said. "Maybe less, if we push. They're a day behind us, maybe two."

"Then we run," Bren said.

"No." Sable's voice was flat. "We run, and they follow our tracks through snow that hasn't melted in a hundred years. We run, and we arrive exhausted with an army at our backs, and Vaelithra wakes to find humans swarming her dying ground with torches." He shook his head slowly. "Running is what they want us to do. Running is how a hunted thing gets tired."

"Then what's your answer, taster? Stand and fight? You've got a smuggler, a pickpocket, and whatever's left of my antidote reserves. Against forty."

"I don't know yet." Sable said it plainly, without shame, and somehow that was worse. "But I know we don't decide it standing in the open. Move. Now."

They moved.

* * *

The Ashen Peaks did not feel like mountains. They felt like a graveyard pretending to be one.

Bren had crossed the Spine of Korrath twice in his smuggling years, had climbed passes where the wind screamed hard enough to peel skin, and none of it had prepared him for this. The higher they climbed, the quieter the world became—not the muffled quiet of snow, but something older, something deliberate. No birds. No insects. Even the wind seemed to move respectfully, the way mourners move past an open coffin.

And the bones.

They found the first skull at midday, half-buried in a drift: enormous, longer than Bren's whole body, the eye socket big enough to put his head inside. Wren made a sound he'd never heard from her before—small, involuntary, the sound of a child seeing the sea for the first time. By afternoon they were walking between ribs arched like cathedral rafters, and the snow beneath their feet was stained faintly gray, as if the stone itself had been burned from within and never quite recovered.

"They came here to die," Sable said softly. He walked with one hand pressed flat against a rib taller than he was, and Bren saw, for the first time since the valley, that the man was trembling. "All of them. Every dragon that felt her fading—they came home to die beside her."

"That's not natural," Wren whispered.

"No," Sable agreed. "It's love. Which is worse."

Bren said nothing. He was watching their backtrail, and what he saw there turned his stomach. Their tracks stitched across the bone-fields like a scar. In fresh snow, in open ground, with a professional tracker behind them, those tracks were a written invitation.

They camped that night in the hollow of a fallen jawbone, a cave of teeth overhead, and Bren took first watch because he couldn't have slept anyway. Guilt is a strange cargo. He'd carried bales of it over the years—men he'd abandoned, debts he'd drowned, a sister he'd failed—but this was different. This wasn't a sin he'd committed. It was a door he'd opened without knowing, and everything coming through it was coming because of him.

Near midnight, Wren crept out of the shelter and sat beside him without asking. For a long time neither of them spoke. Then she said, "You didn't know."

"I should have known."

"You couldn't have." She pulled her knees up. "You did everything right. Sometimes doing everything right isn't enough. I learned that young." Her voice had a strange brittleness in it, and Bren glanced at her, and in the starlight he saw her looking up at the peaks—at the place where, she claimed, the mountains hummed. Only she could hear it. Only she had ever heard it.

"Wren," he said carefully. "What aren't you telling us?"

She was quiet so long he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "When they catch us—and they're going to catch us, Bren, you know it as well as I do—they're going to find three people. A wanted man, a smuggler, and me." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were older than her fourteen years, older than the bones around them. "Two of those people are worth taking alive. One of them is worth burning."

"Don't talk like that."

"Why not? It's true. I've heard the Ashcloak priests preach in Marrowgate squares. I've seen what they do to magic-touched children." She hugged her knees tighter. "They make a festival of it."

Bren reached over and gripped her shoulder, rougher than he meant to. "Listen to me. Nobody burns anybody. Not while I'm breathing."

"That's the problem," Wren said, very quietly. "That's exactly the problem. Everyone keeps saying things like that, and then someone has to pay for it." She stood, brushed snow from her coat, and went back inside the jawbone shelter, leaving Bren alone with the stars and the silence and the growing certainty that she had already decided something he didn't understand yet.

* * *

They heard the horns at dawn.

Not war-horns—the sound was thinner than that, almost gentle, a long rising note that rolled down the valleys and set the ancient bones humming in sympathy. Wren clapped her hands over her ears and doubled over, gasping, and Bren understood suddenly that for her the sound must be ten times louder, a scream pitched to the frequency of whatever lived in her blood.

"There," Sable said, pointing.

Far below, at the mouth of the bone-valley, torchlight moved in formation. Even at that distance Bren could read the order of it: outriders fanning wide, a disciplined column behind, and at the center a cluster of figures in ash-gray robes whose torches burned a color torches shouldn't burn—white, hungry, wrong. Two armies marching as one. Corvane's steel and the Ashcloaks' fire, bound together by a shared hatred.

"They found the spiral marks," Bren said numbly. "They're following our line exactly. I did that. Every step of it, I drew them a map."

Sable was already moving, scrambling up the slope toward a shelf of rock where they could see the whole valley floor. Bren and Wren followed. From the shelf, the picture was worse than he'd feared: the column wasn't hurrying. It was advancing at a marcher's pace, unhurried, inevitable, the way tide advances. And spread among the soldiers were things Bren recognized from his worst years—cages on wheels, iron-banded, empty.

Empty cages meant they expected prisoners.

"They'll reach the hoard by tomorrow night," Sable murmured. "Vaelithra can't rise. You've seen her. She's a candle in a windstorm. If forty armed men walk into that valley—"

"Then we go down there tonight," Bren said, "and we die slowing them down. It's what we've got."

"No." Sable's jaw worked. "No. There has to be another—"

"There isn't," Wren said.

They both turned. She was standing at the edge of the shelf, and she had taken off her pack, and she was setting it down very carefully, the way you set down something you're giving away. Bren's stomach dropped before she said a single word, because he had watched enough people leave things behind in his life to recognize the posture.

"Wren. No."

"Somebody has to slow them down, and somebody has to get you two to the hoard, and I'm the only one of us who can do the first thing." She said it fast, the way you rip off a bandage. "They're not hunting you anymore, Sable. Look at the column. The Ashcloaks are leading it. They're hunting *me*. They've probably known about me since Marrowgate—since I sold you out, since the priest paid me, since whatever their spies saw. A magic-touched child walking willingly into their hands?" Her chin came up, and Bren hated how much it cost her to hold it there. "They'll stop. They'll celebrate. They'll argue about whether to burn me at dawn or drag me back to their High Father. Zealots are predictable that way. I've watched them my whole life from the outside."

"You're fourteen years old," Bren said, and his voice cracked on it, and he didn't care.

"And I hear mountains sing, and I've been running from that fact since I could walk." She picked up her pack again—no, Bren realized, she was emptying it, pressing the dried meat and the flint into Sable's hands. "This way, nobody dies. That's the trade. That's the best trade anyone's offered in this whole stupid story."

"It's not a trade, it's a sacrifice, and I forbid—" Sable stopped himself, and Bren saw the exact moment the taster understood what she'd already understood: that forbidding a person to give themselves away is just another way of using them. His hands closed slowly around the supplies. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't," Wren said again, gentler now. "You taught me that on the poisoned road, remember? When you saved Bren. You said survival always costs something—you just choose what currency you pay in." She smiled, and it was the crooked, streetwise smile he'd first seen across a Marrowgate market stall, and Bren thought: *she practiced that smile. She's wearing it so we'll remember her laughing.* "Mine's cheap. I'm just time. Time is the only thing I've got plenty of."

She hugged Bren first, quick and fierce, her face buried in his coat for exactly two heartbeats before she pulled away. Then she took Sable's hand and pressed something small into it—a bent copper coin, Marrowgate-minted, the kind street kids bit for luck. "So you don't forget you owe me," she said. "And you do owe me. Both of you. Finish it."

Then she stepped off the shelf and went down the slope, small and straight-backed, walking openly toward the torchlight, and Bren had to physically turn away because watching it was a wound that wouldn't close.

"She'll be all right," Sable said. It sounded like a prayer he didn't believe in.

"No, she won't," Bren said. "But she might be alive. And that's more than I deserve to hope for." He shouldered his pack. "Come on, taster. We've got a dragon to wake and a debt to pay, and I swear on my sister's grave—if they hurt that girl, there won't be enough zealots in the world to hide behind."

Below them, the horns sounded again, and this time Bren could have sworn the whole mountain range answered—low, vast, grieving, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The hoard was close now. Whatever waited there—mercy or extinction, ember or ash—they would reach it by nightfall.

And behind them, in the valley of bones, a child walked into the fire so that they could.
