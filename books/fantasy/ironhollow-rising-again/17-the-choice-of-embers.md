## 17. The Choice of Embers

The hoard's heart was not what the stories promised.

Sable had grown up on tales of dragon-hoards—mountains of coin, crowns stacked like cordwood, gems spilling from broken chests in rivers of color. What he found instead, at the center of the Ashen Peaks, beneath a ceiling of stone so high it vanished into darkness, was a sea of ash. Fine grey ash, drifted into dunes, and rising from it like islands, the bones.

Dragons. Dozens of them. Some no larger than horses, curled tight as sleeping cats. Others vast enough that their ribcages formed cathedrals he could have walked through, if he'd had the heart to walk anywhere at all. They had come here to die beside their queen, one by one across centuries, folding themselves down into the grey until the grey swallowed them.

And at the center of it all, cradled in a basin of fused glass where Vaelithra's body curved around it like a wall against the world, the ember burned.

It was small. That was the first obscenity of it. A coal the size of a plum, glowing a deep orange that shifted toward gold when Sable moved closer, as if it noticed him. The heat it gave off wasn't the heat of fire—it was older than that, a warmth that arrived not on his skin but somewhere behind his ribs, in the place where a second heartbeat had lived since the day he'd crossed Ironhollow's border and felt magic flood him like a tide breaking a dam.

*The fire chooses its keeper.* The courier's last words. His mother's map, scorched at every edge, had led them here through passes that shouldn't have been passable, past the valley of bones, to this glass basin and its single living coal.

Sable knelt at the basin's edge and did not touch it yet.

Behind him, Bren Oskar stood with his hand resting on his knife—not drawing it, just resting there, the way a man touches a railing in rough seas. Wren was farther back, near the entrance tunnel, her thin frame silhouetted against the grey light. She hadn't spoken since they'd entered. She said she could hear the mountains humming. In here, Sable suspected, the humming must be deafening.

He could feel it too now. The ember pulsed, slow as a sleeper's breath, and each pulse pressed against something inside him that answered.

"You should know," Bren said quietly, "that I didn't bring them here."

"I know."

"The tracker—I never saw him. I swear it on my sister's name."

"I know, Bren." Sable kept his eyes on the coal. "You led with your feet, not your head. Same as the rest of us."

He heard the smuggler exhale, long and unsteady. Then boots on ash, and Wren's voice, barely above a whisper: "They're coming up the pass. Corvane's soldiers. And the ones in grey." Her voice caught on the word *grey*. The Ashcloaks. "Maybe an hour. Maybe less."

An hour. Sable looked at the ember, and the ember, he would have sworn, looked back.

* * *

Dame Corvane came alone.

That was the thing about her that had unsettled him from the first day in the Great Hall, when she'd dismantled his careful life question by question: she never wasted movement. She could have brought fifty soldiers up the pass. She brought herself, walking out of the tunnel into the bone-cathedral dark with her sword sheathed and her hands visible, her grey cloak dusted with ash, stopping twenty paces from the basin as though approaching an altar.

Behind her, at the tunnel mouths, the shapes of soldiers and Ashcloaks waited. He counted torches. Eleven. Not enough to storm a dragon's hoard. Enough to make sure no one left it.

"Sable Quintana," she said. Her voice carried strangely in the vaulted dark, flat and clear, without echo—as if the mountain drank sound the way Ironhollow drank magic. "Food-taster. Poison-keeper. And now—" her eyes went to the ember, and for just a moment something moved behind them, something almost like grief "—Keeper."

"You knew about this," Sable said. "The crown knew. That's why you came yourself. Why the bounty was quiet instead of loud. You weren't hunting a thief, Corvane. You were hunting a treaty clause."

"Sit, if you like." She gestured at the ash. "We have until my escort grows impatient, and I'd rather spend it honestly. You've earned that much. You lied to me beautifully in the Great Hall—it seemed only fair to return the courtesy."

He stayed kneeling. The ember's pulse was stronger now, or his awareness of it was; each beat landed in his chest like a second heart learning his rhythm.

"Here is the truth," Corvane said. "Four hundred years ago, the dragons and the first kings made a treaty. Magic stays beyond certain borders. Dragons keep to the peaks. Men keep to their fields. The ember is the seal on that treaty—and the treaty holds only while the seal lives." She paused. "Vaelithra is dying. When she dies, the ember gutters within a generation regardless. The old men who signed that knowledge away are dead. But the crown has read the letters. We know what's coming either way."

"So destroy it now," Sable said. "On your terms. While it's weak."

"On my terms, yes." No shame in her voice. He'd expected none. "Destroy it cleanly, deliberately, with witnesses and rites, and the realm survives the transition intact. Or let it die ragged, in some future year, with half the world's zealots and kings scrambling to claim whatever leaks out of it. I have seen what magic does to borders, Sable. I grew up in a town that learned."

Ashcloak territory. He remembered what she'd told the High Father, or what Wren had overheard: a childhood where magic had been the whip, not the wonder.

"And your offer?"

"Pardon. Full pardon—for you, for Oskar, for the girl. Escort home. Your position restored, your wages unfrozen, your records cleaned of everything I've spent two months writing into them." She took a step closer, and stopped, respecting the basin's radius as if she too felt the heat behind her ribs. "In exchange, you place the ember in the destruction rite. One touch. The Ashcloaks have the means—the same means they use on witches, refined. It will not even hurt you. It cannot hurt you. Your blood doesn't carry it anymore; you've poisoned yourself past its reach. Isn't that the joke of you? The one man alive who can handle it safely."

Sable looked down at his hands. Taster's hands. Scarred knuckles, bitten nails, steady as a surgeon's from twenty years of lifting cups that might kill him. He thought of the ledger of doses in his memory—arsenic in the king's wine, nightshade in the ambassador's broth, the slow education of his own body, dish by dish, poison by poison, until he'd become a fortress with a man inside it.

Safe. Yes. He was very good at safe.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we take it from you. I would prefer not to. I've watched enough people die in service of other people's certainties." Her gaze flicked, briefly, toward the tunnel where the Ashcloaks waited. "But the High Father's patience is shorter than mine, and his methods are less clean. If it comes to force, the ember may be damaged in ways none of us intend. Damaged how, no one knows. That is also part of the arithmetic."

* * *

Sable reached into the basin.

Not to take it. To feel it. His fingers hovered a hair's breadth above the coal, and the heat rose to meet them—not burning, *recognizing*. Like a tongue tasting him back.

And the visions came, because of course they did. Vaelithra had shown him this in the bones-valley, but here, at the source, it was not a showing. It was a remembering.

He saw the ember young. Saw the sky over the Ashen Peaks black with wings—thousands, tens of thousands—and the world below green and strange and full of small magics: rivers that remembered names, forests that walked in their sleep, children born humming. He saw the treaty signed in fire and ink. He saw the slow retreat, the shrinking, the valleys going quiet one by one. He saw wars—the wars Corvane feared—kingdoms tearing each other apart over scraps of power while dragons burned cities rather than yield their hoards. He saw Ironhollow founded, that blind valley, that blessed nothing-place, built by people who wanted the quiet more than the wonders.

His mother was in some of those visions. Fleeing, heavy with him, trading her Keeper's blood for a dead valley's safety. Hiding the map inside a lullaby. Dying ordinary, in a house that smelled of bread, having saved the extraordinary for a son who never asked for it.

And then he saw the other future. The one Corvane offered.

The ember destroyed. The world continuing. Fields planted, harvests gathered, children raised who would never hear mountains hum. Peace—the real kind, the durable kind, bought at the price of everything his mother had bled to preserve. He saw himself old in Ironhollow, tasting the king's food, safe behind walls that kept out wonders, telling no one what he'd held in his hands. It was not a nightmare. That was the terrible part. It was livable. It was, by every measure he'd been raised to honor, *wise*.

Against it, the other arithmetic: give the ember back, and what? Vaelithra might live another century—or another week. Dragonkind might rekindle—or gutter out anyway, the ember's gift arriving decades too late. There were no guarantees on that road either. Only a dying queen, a sea of bones, and a taster's gamble that renewal outweighed extinction.

Corvane wanted certainty. The Ashcloaks wanted purity. His mother had wanted safety. Everyone, it seemed, had a plan for the fire except the fire itself.

"Sable." Corvane's voice, quieter now. Almost gentle. "I have read your file. Every page I wrote. You are the most careful man I have ever hunted. You built a life out of never being surprised. Don't end it gambling on a lizard's promise."

"That's exactly why," Sable said.

She frowned.

"Careful men know the cost of things," he said. "That's the whole trade. I taste the cup so the king doesn't have to. I know precisely what safety costs, Dame Corvane, because I've paid for it my entire life, dose by dose. It costs everything except breathing." He lifted his eyes to hers. "You're offering me a pardon to go back to a life where I was already dead. I just hadn't filed the paperwork."

For a moment—just a moment—something in her face wavered. He saw the girl from the magic-ruined town looking out through the investigator's discipline, and he understood that she was not his opposite. She was what he might have become, if the poisons had gotten into his heart instead of his blood.

"The rite," she said. "Now. Please. Before the High Father comes up that tunnel, because if he does, I will not be able to protect any of us—including you."

Sable closed his hand around the ember.

It did not burn. It sank into his palm like a key finding its lock, and the second heartbeat in his chest roared into a choir, and every bone in the cathedral-dark around them shivered in its bed of ash.

Corvane drew her sword.

No—the motion was wrong. She wasn't drawing it at him. She'd turned, blade half-out, toward the tunnel mouth, where torchlight had begun to climb, and voices were rising through the dark—chanting, many-throated, the Ashcloaks' hymn coming up the pass ahead of their fire.

"They were never waiting for your answer," Sable breathed.

"No," Corvane said grimly. "They were waiting for the ember to wake. It wakes, it can be killed. Pray your queen rises faster than their faith burns."

Above them, deep in the mountain's throat, something vast exhaled.

The ash began to move.
