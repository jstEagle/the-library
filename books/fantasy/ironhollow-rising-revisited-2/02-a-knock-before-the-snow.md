## 2. A Knock Before the Snow

The storm came in from the west like something with a grudge.

Ulyana Sterling had watched thirty-one winters arrive at Ironhollow, and she had learned to read them the way other people read faces. This one had murder in it — a low gray bruise of cloud pressing down on the peaks, wind that smelled of iron and old ice, the kind of cold that didn't just kill you but took its time about it, politely, like a creditor.

*Like a creditor,* she thought, and almost laughed at herself. The ledgers were getting into her head again. She'd spent three days on the winter audit, hunched over the keeper's desk with her fingers stained blue-black from ink, cataloguing favors owed and favors owed to her, and now every thought came out in columns.

She stood at the gatehouse window with a cup of tea going cold in her hand and watched the last light die on the Thornfell road. By tomorrow morning the pass would be shut. Not closed — Ironhollow's gate never truly closed; that was rather the point of her whole miserable, beloved post — but *shut*, impassable, buried under drifts until thaw. Anyone caught on the road tonight would be dead by dawn or lucky enough to reach her door.

The road was empty.

It was always empty. That was the arrangement she'd built, stone by patient stone: the smugglers paid their small tolls in news and salt and silence, the honest travelers never came this far north in winter at all, and Ulyana kept the seal intact and told the Crown's surveyors, once a year, that everything below Ironhollow was exactly as quiet as it had been for fifteen years.

Fifteen years. Long enough that the mountain's old court had become, in her own private accounting, a fairy story. Something grandmothers used to frighten children away from the deep roads. Something her grandmother had believed in so fiercely she'd written a primer in a language no kingdom had ever claimed, and hidden it under the floorboards like contraband.

Ulyana drank the cold tea anyway. Habit was a kind of armor too.

* * *

She made her rounds by lamplight, as she did every night, though tonight the ritual felt less like comfort and more like counting stitches in a sweater she suspected had a hole somewhere.

The gate itself sat in a cleft of black granite, twenty feet of ancient iron-banded timber faced with bronze that had gone green as riverweed. Behind it, the wardstone — a pillar of veined white rock older than any mason's memory — hummed its low note that only keepers could feel, a vibration more in the teeth than the ears. Ulyana pressed her palm flat against it, the way her predecessor had taught her, the way his predecessor had taught him, back and back into the years.

Steady. Steady. Steady.

Except.

She pulled her hand back and pressed it again, slower, the way you'd press a bruise to be sure it was real.

There. Under the hum, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall: a second note. A hair off true. A crack in a bell.

Her mouth went dry. She lifted the lamp and walked the pillar's circumference twice, slowly, letting the light rake across the stone. On the third pass she found it — high up, near where the wardstone met the living rock of the mountain, so thin she could have slipped one of her own hairs into it and not known whether it had gone in. A line of darkness no wider than a whisper, running diagonally for perhaps the length of her forearm before it vanished into shadow.

A hairline crack. In the wardstone. The thing that had not cracked in four hundred years, through earthquakes and sieges and one spectacularly ill-advised attempt by the Crown to widen the pass with blasting powder.

Ulyana stood very still and listened to her own heart doing arithmetic.

If she reported it, the surveyors would come in spring. They would bring engineers. The engineers would bring priests, because everyone above a certain rank knew what the wardstone actually was, whatever they pretended in their reports, and priests brought protocols, and protocols meant the gate would be sealed *fully* — which meant the smuggler roads closed, which meant Bramble Ostwick and forty people like him losing their livelihoods, which meant questions, official ones, about what exactly a keeper at Ironhollow had been trading all these years in exchange for silence and salt.

And if the crack grew before spring—

She made the decision the way she made most decisions: quickly, alone, and with a mental note to hate herself later. She fetched the fine chisel and the pot of gold-leaf amalgam from the repair kit, climbed the scaffold ladder with her teeth gritted against the wind, and worked the soft metal into the crack by lamplight, sealing it cosmetically, the way you'd paint over damp on a wall. It wouldn't hold the magic together. It would hold the *appearance* together until she understood what she was looking at.

Below her, the gate hummed. Off-key. Patient.

"Nobody asked you," she told it.

The mountain, characteristically, said nothing.

* * *

She was banking the fire when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind of knocks that don't demand entry so much as apologize for asking.

Ulyana went still, poker in hand, and listened. Wind, and the tick of cooling iron, and beneath it all the wardstone's wrong little note, humming on.

Three more knocks. Softer. As if the knocker were losing strength between attempts.

Anyone on the Thornfell road tonight was either a fool, a corpse-to-be, or coming to her door deliberately — and in fifteen years, the people who came to her door deliberately arrived with cargo, or warnings, or debts. None of them knocked like that. None of them knocked *small*.

She unbarred the door and the storm shoved its way in ahead of whoever stood behind it, a fist of snow and shrieking air that guttered the lamps and filled the entryway with white swirl. She squinted into it, one hand raised against the cold, and for a moment saw nothing but night.

Then the night moved, and there was a child on her doorstep.

Seven years old, maybe eight. Small enough that the snowdrift at the threshold came up to the child's chest. Barefoot. *Barefoot*, in a storm that would take toes off a grown man in an hour, standing on her stone step with snow piled on thin shoulders and dark hair frozen into ropes, wearing nothing but a shift of some pale woven stuff that Ulyana's eyes kept sliding off of, refusing to settle on its color.

The child looked up at her. The face was blue with cold, the lips cracked, the eyes —

The eyes stopped Ulyana's breath. They were calm. Not the glassy calm of hypothermia, not yet — she knew that look, had dug two frozen travelers out of drifts over the years and knew exactly how long the calm lasted — but a settled, deliberate calm, the calm of someone who has walked a long way on purpose.

The child opened cracked lips and spoke.

Not the trade-tongue. Not the lowland dialect, not the northern burr of the Thornfell villages, not anything Ulyana had ever heard spoken aloud. The sounds were round and strange and old, vowels that seemed to come from deeper in the chest than speech should, and each syllable landed in the entryway with a weight out of proportion to its size, the way a small stone dropped down a well takes a long time to make its sound.

Ulyana understood none of it. And yet some animal part of her brain, some part older than language, flinched — the way you flinch when lightning strikes too close, when a floorboard groans in an empty house. Because the words felt *recognized*. Not by her. By the house. By the mountain.

By the gate.

She felt it happen — felt it through the soles of her boots, up through the bones of her legs. The wardstone's broken little note shivered, wavered, and bent toward the child's voice like a plant toward sun. For three heartbeats the whole gatehouse hummed in harmony with a seven-year-old's voice, and then the child fell silent, swayed, and Ulyana lunged forward and caught a body that weighed almost nothing and burned with cold like a banked coal.

"No," she said uselessly, hauling the child inside, kicking the door shut behind her. "No, no, you're frozen, you absolute — whose child are you? Whose—"

The child's eyes found hers one more time, calm and dark and unbearably steady, and then rolled back, and the small body went limp in her arms.

* * *

She worked without thinking, because thinking was a luxury and the body on her table couldn't afford it. Fire built high. Wet clothes off — the strange shift peeled away with difficulty, reluctant, as if it had grown there — and the child wrapped in her own spare blankets, the good wool ones, the ones she'd been saving against her own hard winter. Snow rubbed from skin. Toes checked: blue but not black, thank every merciful power, blue but not black. She chafed the small hands, the small feet, muttering the whole time, curses and prayers and fragments of the field-medicine every border keeper learns, and the child never stirred, breath shallow but even, heart fluttering under her palm like a bird in a sack.

Only when the immediate dying had been called off did Ulyana step back and let herself look.

A girl, she decided. Seven, maybe eight. Underfed in the way of children who've been hungry a long time rather than briefly — the leanness worn into the frame, not laid over it. Old scars on the soles of the feet, healed crooked. Whoever she was, she'd been walking barefoot long before tonight.

And the shift. Ulyana picked it up from the floor where she'd dropped it and turned it in the lamplight, and her skin prickled. It wasn't cloth, quite. It was fine as spider silk and strong as rope, and it had no seams, and when she held it up the weave moved like water and refused, still, to hold a color — gray, then green, then the pale bone-white of the wardstone itself.

She put it down carefully, the way you put down a snake.

Then she stood in the middle of her own kitchen, in her own lamplight, surrounded by her own familiar things, and felt the ground tilt under the ordinary.

Because here was the accounting, and she could not stop the ledger-mind from tallying it: a child arrives barefoot through a killing storm on the last passable night of the year, on a road that had been empty at dusk. The child is half-frozen but alive, which means the child had been walking in that storm for hours, which should have been impossible, and had crossed the pass, which should have been impossible, and had passed the outer wards — the ones Ulyana had inherited and maintained and trusted for fifteen years, the ones that sang alarms at stray goats and turned back armed men.

They hadn't sung. They hadn't turned. And when the child spoke, the wards had *hummed along*, like dogs greeting a master.

Ulyana looked at the sleeping girl, at the frost melting in her dark hair, at the small chest rising and falling, and felt two feelings rise in her at once, tangled past separating.

The first was dread. Cold, specific, old dread — the kind that lived in the same cellar of her heart as her grandmother's forbidden book under the floorboards, as stories of the court beneath the mountain that bought and sold and never slept, as the word *debt* in a language no kingdom claimed. Something had sent this child. Or something had *let* her come. Either way, the mountain had opened a door it had kept shut since before Ulyana was born, and doors like that did not open by accident.

The second feeling was simpler and worse.

The girl shifted in her sleep, frowning, and murmured something — those round, ancient syllables again, softer now, slurred with exhaustion — and reached one small hand out from under the blanket, patting the air beside her as if searching for someone who should have been there.

And Ulyana Sterling, who had spent fifteen years perfecting the art of needing no one, who had built her whole life into a fortress of routine and small favors and locked doors, felt her heart do something it had no ledger-entry for.

She sat down in the chair beside the bed. She took the small cold hand and tucked it back under the wool, and then, because the hand immediately escaped again, she simply held it, rough palm around tiny fingers, and told herself she was checking the pulse.

"You're safe," she said aloud, foolishly, to someone who couldn't understand her. "Whoever you are. Whatever you are. You're safe tonight."

Outside, the storm screamed against the gatehouse walls, and beneath it, faint and constant and wrong, the wardstone hummed its new off-key note — and if Ulyana strained, and she hated herself for straining, she could almost believe the note had changed again. Had settled. Had become, unmistakably, a lullaby.

* * *

Near midnight, the fever came.

It swept in fast, the bad kind, the kind that makes small bodies burn and twist. Ulyana dosed her with willow bark and snowflower tincture, sponged her down, held her through the worst of the thrashing, and all the while the girl talked — a stream of that ancient language pouring out of her, faster now, urgent, dream-speech or prayer-speech, Ulyana couldn't tell.

But somewhere in the third hour, in the middle of the torrent, the girl said a single word that Ulyana's ear snagged on like a nail in cloth.

Not because she knew it. Because she *almost* knew it. Because her grandmother's voice, twenty years dead, rose up unbidden in her memory, saying that word — or its cousin, its ancestor — in the dim kitchen of another lifetime, while a much younger Ulyana sat at her feet learning letters she was forbidden to write down.

One word. Out of hundreds. But it was a thread, and Ulyana had spent her whole life pulling threads.

The fever broke toward dawn. The girl slept, truly slept, her breathing slow and even, her hand still curled trustingly in Ulyana's grip. Outside, the storm was spending its last fury against the pass; by morning the road would be gone under ten feet of snow, and whatever had sent this child would be cut off from following, and Ulyana would be cut off from everything else, sealed in with a mystery until thaw.

She looked at the floorboards by the cold hearth. At the loose plank she'd stepped over a thousand times without stepping on.

"Well," she said quietly, to the sleeping girl, to the humming gate, to the mountain at large. "I suppose we'd better find out your name."

Under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, her grandmother's primer waited — and for the first time in fifteen years, Ulyana intended to open it.
