## 3. Words Older Than Kingdoms

The child slept in Ulyana's own bed, and Ulyana sat awake beside it, listening to the mountain breathe.

That was the old keepers' phrase for it — *the mountain breathes* — and for fifteen years she had treated it as poetry, a pretty way to describe the way the wind moved through Ironhollow's vents, the slow expansion and contraction of stone as the cold deepened and relented. Tonight it sounded like something else. Tonight it sounded like lungs.

The child had eaten half a bowl of barley porridge, drunk three cups of water, and fallen asleep sitting upright, the way soldiers and other people accustomed to sleeping in bad places do. Ulyana had eased her down onto the narrow bed beneath the window, wrapped her in the grey wool blanket that still smelled faintly of cedar, and then had not moved from the chair since.

The wards had hummed when the child crossed them. Ulyana kept turning that over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Wards did not hum. Wards *held* or wards *failed*, and hers had done neither. They had *acknowledged*. Like a doorkeeper bowing to someone expected.

Outside, the snow came down in the fat, windless flakes that meant the pass was closing. By morning, Thornfell road would be a rumor. Whatever had walked up her mountain tonight had done so on the last night the mountain would permit anything at all.

In the firelight the child looked ordinary enough: seven, perhaps eight years old, sharp elbows, a scrape healing on one knee, hair the color of wet slate cut short and unevenly, as if she had done it herself with a knife. Her feet had been cut — not frostbitten, *cut*, old cuts, healed wrong, the kind you got walking barefoot on roads that were never meant for bare feet. Ulyana had cleaned them, salved them, wrapped them in strips of linen. The child had watched her do it with an expression Ulyana could not read, an expression far too old for the face it lived on.

Now the child dreamed. And in her dreaming, she spoke.

It started as a murmur, low and rhythmic, almost like counting. Ulyana leaned forward. The sounds were soft-edged, liquid, built on vowels that opened like doors into dark rooms. They were not the gutter-speech of the smuggler roads, not the high trading-tongue of the lowland merchants, not the clipped soldier-cant Ulyana had learned in her brief, disastrous year in the Duke's garrison. They were none of the languages she knew.

They were, she realized with a cold that started in her spine and spread outward, *almost* none of the languages she knew.

Almost. Because there was a book in the bottom of the cedar chest under her window that knew them.

Ulyana sat very still for a long time, telling herself she was wrong. The mind played tricks. Fifteen years alone made a woman hear patterns in wind and meaning in murmurs. She was tired. She was rattled by the crack in the wardstone, which she had not told a soul about, and by the wards humming, and by the bare feet in the snow.

The child murmured again, and this time Ulyana caught a word — a whole word, unmistakable, its shape burned into her memory by a woman thirty years dead.

*Vess.*

Her grandmother had said that word exactly once, in Ulyana's hearing, and had paid for it with a beating from Ulyana's father that Ulyana still could not think about without her hands curling into fists. *Vess* — and her father had struck her grandmother across the mouth and called it a devil-word, a deep-folk word, a word from before the kingdoms, before the walls, before the light, and her grandmother had spat blood on the floor and said, in the trade-tongue so the children would understand: *Words are older than walls, boy. The walls will go first.*

Ulyana got up. Her knees cracked. She crossed the room on legs that felt borrowed from someone older, knelt at the cedar chest, and lifted out the blankets, and the spare candles, and the coil of tarred rope, until her hands found the oilcloth bundle at the bottom, wrapped in three layers and tied with waxed cord.

Her grandmother's primer.

She had not opened it in fifteen years. Not since the day she took the keeper's post and swore the oath on the wardstone, an oath that included, in its long archaic list of renunciations, a clause she had always assumed was boilerplate, the kind of legal fossil every old office accumulates: *and I forswear the tongues of the under-earth and all books that teach them.*

She untied the cord anyway.

The oilcloth fell open around a book small enough to fit in two hands, bound in leather gone dark and soft with age, its spine stitched with sinew. No title. No gilding. Just a symbol pressed into the cover — a spiral, turning inward, the kind of mark a child might draw to mean a shell or a whirlpool or the way water goes down a drain. Ulyana had spent her whole childhood being told never to draw that spiral, never to scratch it in dirt, never, ever to say the words inside this book aloud.

Her grandmother had taught her from it anyway, in the years before the beatings started, in the years when her grandmother still had a tongue to teach with. *Not to speak, little owl. To recognize. A keeper who cannot recognize what comes to her gate is a blind woman guarding a door.* And then her father had found the lessons, and the lessons had stopped, and her grandmother had died in a bad winter with the book hidden under the floorboards and her son believing it burned.

Ulyana carried it to the chair beside the bed and set it on her knees.

The child slept on. The fire ticked. Somewhere far below, so far below that Ulyana might have imagined it, something in the mountain made a sound like a page turning.

She opened the primer.

* * *

The first pages were a child's introduction, because it had been made for a child — her grandmother's own handwriting marching across the opening leaves in a careful, schoolroom hand, translating the deep-tongue into the trade-speech word by word. *This mark is the sound ah, as in the opening of the mouth. This mark is the sound of falling. This mark means debt, and it is also the mark for river, and also the mark for blood, because to the deep folk these are the same thing: that which flows, and must flow, and is owed to the low places.*

Ulyana read with the primer balanced on one knee, her eyes moving between the book and the sleeping child, and slowly, syllable by syllable, she began to hear the dream-speech resolve into sense — not fluency, nothing like fluency, but the way a melody half-remembered becomes a melody again when someone else plays it.

The child's words had shapes. Ulyana could find those shapes now in the primer's tables. She could not have spoken them to save her life. But she could *recognize*.

Her grandmother's voice, thirty years dead, murmured the translations in her ear.

*That phrase, little owl — the one she keeps returning to — means "the gate remembers."*

The child murmured. Turned her head on the pillow. Her brow furrowed in sleep like a much older person's, someone carrying a ledger of worries.

*And that one means "the snow is a door."*

Ulyana's hands had gone cold. She pulled the blanket from her own bed and wrapped it around her shoulders without taking her eyes off the pages.

An hour passed. Two. The fire burned down and she fed it without standing, feeding it by habit, by the same competence that had kept her alive and sane through fifteen years of solitary winters. And all the while the child talked in her sleep, and all the while Ulyana matched the sounds against the dead woman's careful letters, and the sense of it assembled itself in her like something rising through dark water.

The child was not raving. The child was *reporting*. The fragments came with the cadence of a message being carried — repeated, structured, the same phrases returning at intervals like a courier checking her bearings. Ulyana had listened to smugglers pass coded words over her kitchen table for fifteen years. She knew what a message sounded like, even when she couldn't read it.

And then, near midnight, the child said a phrase that Ulyana did not need the primer for, because she had already seen it, three pages back, in her grandmother's handwriting, underlined twice.

*The debt comes due.*

The child said it the way people say prayers. Not frightened. Not pleading. The way you say something that is simply, immovably true — *the sun rises, the river flows, the debt comes due* — a fact about the world that no one's wishing would change. She said it, and turned over, and slept on with her cut feet sticking out from under the blanket, and Ulyana sat in the firelight with the forbidden book open on her knees and felt the past put its hands around her throat.

Because she knew that word. *Debt.* Not from the primer — well, yes, from the primer, the mark that meant debt and river and blood — but from somewhere else too. From a kitchen table twenty years ago, when her brother Tam had sat across from her with a stranger's coin-purse in his fist and a shine in his eyes that she had mistaken for triumph and had spent two decades understanding was terror. *It's just a loan, Ulla. Everyone borrows. Everyone pays.*

Everyone pays.

Ulyana closed the primer.

The room was very quiet. The snow ticked against the window. The child breathed, small and steady, and the mountain breathed around them both, and Ulyana sat in her chair and let herself remember what she had spent twenty years not remembering, because the primer was open in her lap and her grandmother's voice was in her ears and the past had stopped accepting her excuses.

* * *

Tam had been twenty-two. She had been seventeen, and she remembered it because it was the last winter they had been a family of three instead of a family of one.

Their mother had been dying by inches that year — the lung-rot, the miner's curse, the slow drowning on dry land — and the medicine that might ease her came from the lowland apothecaries at prices no miner's daughter could do sums about. Tam had done the sums anyway. Tam had always been the one who did sums, quick and bright, the clever one, while Ulyana was the steady one, the one who could sit still through anything. Their mother used to say Tam had a head for numbers and Ulyana had a head for doors, and it was meant as praise for both, and Ulyana had spent fifteen years as a keeper of a door wondering which of them it had praised more.

The travelers had come up the Thornfell road in late autumn, out of season, out of reason — three of them, wrapped in good grey wool, with the smooth faces of people who had never done a day's honest labor. They had stopped at the keeper's cottage — her father was keeper then, before the cough took him too — and asked for water and directions, and Tam had talked with them by the fire for an hour, and Ulyana, seventeen and bored and chopping turnips, had not listened.

She had noticed only one thing, and she had noticed it because her grandmother, dying that same winter in the back room, had lifted her head from her pillow when the travelers passed the window, and made a sound Ulyana had never heard a human being make. Not a word. A sound like a door closing.

Later — much later, years later, when Tam was gone and the coin-purse was the only evidence anyone ever found — Ulyana had understood what her grandmother had heard. The travelers had spoken to each other in the yard, briefly, thinking themselves unheard. And the old woman in the back room, keeper's widow, keeper herself in her day, forsworn tongue or no, had recognized the language of the under-earth coming up her mountain like water finding a crack.

Tam had borrowed from them. Ulyana had never learned how much — the medicine had arrived within the month, and their mother had lived two more years on it, two years she would not otherwise have had, and Ulyana had never once regretted those two years and had never once stopped paying for them. And then in the spring, Tam had gone down the mountain to "settle the account," and he had kissed the top of her head — he had never done that before, he had laughed at her for being small her whole life — and he had said, *I'll be back before the thaw, Ulla. Mind the door for me.*

He had not come back before the thaw. He had not come back.

Their father had gone down to the lowlands asking questions until the questions got him looked at sideways, and then he had come home and taken to his bed and coughed himself into the ground within three years, and the last thing he had said to Ulyana, gripping her wrist with a strength his body no longer owned, was: *Take the post. Keep the gate. Whatever comes to that door, you let nothing through.* And she had sworn it on the wardstone at nineteen years old, and she had kept it for fifteen years, and she had told herself the story the whole time: Tam ran. Tam owed bad people and ran. Tam is alive somewhere in the lowlands, ashamed, comfortable, married, fat. It was the kindest story and she had built her life inside it like a woman building a house on a cliff edge and refusing to look down.

But her grandmother had known. Her grandmother, in her last lucid hour, gripping Ulyana's hand with the same fierce grip her son would use three years later, had said: *The court under the mountain buys against winter, little owl. It has always bought. Your brother's name is in a ledger down there, and ledgers do not forget, and neither must you — but you must pretend, do you hear me, you must pretend until the debt comes due.*

The debt comes due.

The child said it in her sleep, and Ulyana's grandmother said it across thirty years, and the two voices fit together like the two halves of a broken seal.

* * *

Ulyana did not sleep. She read the primer cover to cover as the night wore down toward its coldest hour, and what she found in it rearranged the world.

The deep folk had not been conquered. That was the kingdoms' story, the story carved on the lowland monuments: *driven out, sealed away, the gates shut upon the dark.* The primer told it differently. The deep folk had *sold*. Piece by piece, winter by winter, in the hungry centuries before the kingdoms rose, the deep court had bought and sold and borrowed against everything — land, names, faces, years, souls — until the debt-magic had grown so vast that it had to be caged, and the cage was the gate, and the keepers of the gate were not soldiers but *auditors*, sworn to ensure that nothing crossed in either direction that would reopen the accounts.

And the primer's last pages, in her grandmother's shakiest handwriting, were not language lessons at all. They were warnings.

*If the wards sing, the seal is thinning. If a child of the deep tongue walks the snow, the court is reaching. If the debt comes due in your lifetime, little owl, remember: the court cannot take what is freely given, and it cannot refuse what is freely given, and that is the only power a keeper has ever had.*

*Freely given.* Ulyana looked at the words a long time.

The fire was down to coals. The window had gone from black to the deep blue that comes before the grey that comes before the light. And in that hour, the child woke.

Not gradually. Her eyes simply opened — dark eyes, far too calm — and found Ulyana across the room, and she did not startle, did not cry, did not do anything a lost child woken in a stranger's house should do. She looked at Ulyana the way a messenger looks at the door she has finally reached.

Then she said something. Not the dream-speech. Slower, deliberate, each syllable placed like a stone in a stream — a sentence built for Ulyana to follow, and Ulyana, primer open on her knees, caught enough of it that her blood went to ice water.

She knew two of the words for certain. *Vess* — the child's own name, offered like a key. And the phrase her grandmother had translated three pages back, the phrase that meant *you are the keeper of the door.*

The child — Vess — waited. Watched. As if she had said everything that needed saying and the rest was Ulyana's work.

Ulyana closed the primer. Her hands were not quite steady, but her voice, when it came, was the voice she used at the gate, the one that did not shake because it was not permitted to.

"Vess," she said. "My name is Ulyana. This is Ironhollow, and I keep the gate."

The child's eyes did not change. But outside, faint through the stone walls, through the snow, through fifteen years of Ulyana's certainty that the old court was a fairy story — the mountain exhaled. And far below, in the dark where the ledgers never close, Ulyana would have sworn she heard a page turn, and a pen scratch, and a dry, patient voice begin to read a name she knew.

*Tam Sterling. The debt comes due.*
