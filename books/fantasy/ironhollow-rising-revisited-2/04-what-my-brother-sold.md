## 4. What My Brother Sold

The primer did not sleep on the shelf where Ulyana had left it.

She had put it back precisely as she'd found it — spine to the wall, wrapped in the oilcloth her grandmother had used for preserving fish, wedged behind the jar of pickled rowan berries that no one had eaten in fifteen years because no one but Ulyana knew they were there. She had checked twice that night while Vess slept curled in the keeper's bed like a comma of bone and hair. Both times the book had been exactly where she left it.

The third time she checked, near dawn, it was lying open on the table.

Ulyana stood very still in the grey light with her hand on the latch of the door she had not opened. The stove ticked. Vess breathed in the other room, a small steady sound like water finding its way through stone. And the primer lay open at a page Ulyana had never read, its vellum gone soft as cloth at the edges, the old script crawling across it in ink that looked less written than grown.

She did not remember opening it. She was almost sure she had not.

"Right," she said aloud, to the room, to herself, to whatever patient thing had turned the page. "We'll do this properly."

She lit the lamp, sat down across from the book like an opponent across a chessboard, and pulled it toward her.

* * *

Her grandmother had forbidden the primer in the way that grandmothers forbid things: not with anger but with a kind of ceremonial dread, the word *no* pronounced like a name of the dead. Ulyana had been nine. Tam had been twelve and already too clever for his own good, all elbows and questions, and he had been the one who found the book first, tucked into the false bottom of the cedar chest with the wedding linens.

*"It's just words,"* Tam had said, turning pages by lamplight while their grandmother's footsteps creaked overhead. *"Words can't hurt you."*

Their grandmother had come down the stairs without hurry, taken the book out of his hands with two fingers as if lifting a dead bird, and said, *"These words were here before hurting was invented, boy. They're what hurting was made from."*

Tam had laughed. Tam had always laughed. That was the whole trouble with Tam — he met the world laughing, and the world, being what it was, took notes.

Ulyana had forgotten most of that night. Twenty years will sand a memory smooth. But sitting now over the open primer with Vess asleep one room away, the details came back not as memory but as presence: the smell of the cedar chest, the particular squeak of the third stair, her grandmother's hands closing the lid. And underneath it all, faint as a watermark, the sound of Tam's laughter going on a beat too long after everything else had gone quiet.

She bent over the page.

The script was the same script that came out of Vess's sleeping mouth — she recognized it now, the way you recognize a face under a hood. Her grandmother's marginal notes, in a hand gone brown and cramped, translated in fits and starts. Ulyana read slowly, moving her lips like a child, and the mountain listened.

*On the buying of souls against winter,* the heading said, in her grandmother's blunt gloss beneath the older text.

* * *

Here is what Ulyana remembered of the winter Tam disappeared, and here is what she had spent twenty years refusing to remember, and the two lists were not the same list.

What she remembered: a hard season, the hardest in living memory, snow to the eaves by the feast of Saint Corvin. Their mother coughing blood into a rag she hid under the mattress until the rag could have stood up on its own. The medicine man from Thornfell charging sums that made no sense, sums that changed every visit, sums that climbed like smoke. Tam going out more and more, coming home late, coming home with coin he wouldn't explain. Coming home once with his boots soaked through and frost in his eyebrows and a look on his face Ulyana had never seen before and would not see again for twenty years — the look of a boy who has knocked on a door he cannot unknock.

What she refused to remember, until now: that the last night, she had heard him talking to someone in the yard. Two voices. His, high and bright with relief. The other one low, and pleasant, and wrong — wrong the way a portrait is wrong, everything in its place and nothing alive. She had been fourteen and tired and she had pulled the blanket over her head and told herself it was the wind off the pass.

In the morning Tam was gone. In the spring, so was their mother, though the medicine had worked — that was the bitter arithmetic of it, the sum Ulyana had carried twenty years without ever once setting it down. The medicine worked. Their mother lived four more years because of it. Whatever Tam had paid, it bought her time, real time, whole seasons of her laughing again in the kitchen.

And then the travelers had come asking after him, polite men with pleasant faces, and Ulyana had told them nothing because there was nothing to tell, and they had smiled and thanked her and left coins on the table that she threw into the river, and that was the end of it. That was the story she had kept, polished smooth as a river stone: Tam ran off. Tam owed bad people money. Tam couldn't face it.

The primer told a different story. The primer didn't care what she could bear.

*The court beneath buys against winter,* her grandmother's note read. *Not gold. Gold is only the shape the debt wears on this side. What they buy is the self entire — the part of a person that answers when their name is called. They hold it through the cold months and lend it back at interest, and the interest is memory, and warmth, and the face you make when someone you love walks into the room.*

Below that, smaller, pressed hard enough into the vellum to tear:

*Tam. If you are reading this, come to me before midwinter. Do not sign anything. Do not say your full name aloud below the snowline. There is still time if you come to me.*

The note was dated the winter he vanished.

Ulyana sat back. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere above the keep, wind moved along the gate and the wards answered with their low held note, and beneath that note — she was certain of it now, had been certain since the child arrived — ran a second note, thinner, older, like a crack through a bell.

Her grandmother had known. Had written to him, begged him, and he had — what? Not come? Come too late? Or come, and signed anyway, because their mother's cough had gotten worse and the medicine man's prices had climbed again, and Tam had never in his life met a door he didn't knock on.

*He was sixteen,* Ulyana thought. *He was sixteen years old and our mother was dying and someone offered him a way, and I have spent twenty years being angry at a child.*

The anger didn't leave. Anger at that age doesn't leave; it changes address. It moved out of Tam and into the thing that had lent a dying woman's son the price of a few more springs, and it settled there, and it began, quietly, to sharpen itself.

* * *

Vess woke near noon, silent as always, and stood in the doorway watching Ulyana work. Ulyana had the ledger out — the keeper's ledger, the honest one, columns of tolls and favors and weather — and beside it the primer, and between them a sheet of rough paper where she was copying out the phrase the child repeated in her sleep.

*The debt comes due.* Four words in the old tongue. She had them now, or close enough. She wrote them large and drew lines connecting them to the primer's glosses, and the connections held, and each one that held felt less like scholarship and more like a noose drawing tight.

Vess padded over and looked at the paper. Then she reached out one small finger and touched the largest word — *due* — and looked up at Ulyana, and her eyes were the color of deep ice, and in them was something no seven-year-old should be able to carry: recognition. Pity, even. As if the child were sorry for *her*.

"I know," Ulyana said. Her voice came out rough. "I know what it says."

Vess shook her head slowly. She pointed at the word again. Then she pointed at Ulyana's chest.

"No." Ulyana heard her own voice go flat and hard, the keeper's voice, the voice that turned smugglers around at the pass. "No. Whatever's owed, it isn't mine. It was never mine. My brother—"

She stopped. Because Vess had gone to the window, and was looking out at the gate, and making a shape with her hands — two hands, cupped, then opened, then closed again, empty. Over and over. Hands giving something away. Hands receiving nothing back.

Then the child pointed at the primer, and at the word *winter*, and then she opened her mouth and spoke — not the dream-language, not the sliding half-words of sleep, but clearly, deliberately, three syllables she must have saved up like coins, spending them all at once:

"*Sterling.*"

Ulyana's whole body went cold. "How do you know that name?"

But Vess was already shaking her head, frustrated, hands fluttering — not *how*, her gestures said, not *that*. She pointed at Ulyana. *Sterling.* She pointed at herself. *Sterling.* She knotted her small fists together, binding the two names into one, and then she pointed down. Through the floor. Through the stone. Through two hundred feet of mountain and the sealed gate and everything Ulyana had spent fifteen years pretending was a fairy story.

Down to where names were kept.

"You know him." Ulyana's voice cracked on it. She crouched to bring her eyes level with the child's. "You've seen him. Down there. You've seen my brother."

Vess's face did something complicated and terrible — the face of someone carrying a message too heavy for their size. She nodded. Once. Then she took Ulyana's hand, turned it palm-up, and traced on it, slow and careful, letters Ulyana could not read but whose shape she knew better than her own heartbeat, because she had watched them being carved into a headstone that never got used, on a grave that never got filled, when she was fourteen and the world went quiet.

Two letters. *T. A.*

Tam.

* * *

That night Ulyana did not sleep. She sat at the table with both books open and made lists, because lists were what she had instead of prayer, and the lists went like this:

*What I owe:* Nothing. Fifteen years of clean ledgers. Every favor traded square, every toll logged, every debt paid on the day it fell due. She had built her whole life into a wall of paid accounts, and she saw that now for what it was — a superstition, a charm against the thing that had taken Tam. As if the court kept books too, and might overlook a household with nothing outstanding.

*What I am owed:* Three favors from Bramble Ostwick, smuggler, liar, and the closest thing to a friend the pass allowed. One favor from the widow Hark at the Hollow Inn, if she still lived. And one enormous, unpayable, twenty-years-compounding debt from whatever wore pleasant faces in the dark and taught a sixteen-year-old boy to sign.

*What I have:* A gate. A failing seal. A grandmother's forbidden book. A mute child who knew her brother's name and carried it down out of the snow like a coal carried cupped against the wind.

And under all the lists, in the space where the total goes, the thing she had been circling all night finally set itself down and would not be argued with:

*They buy souls against winter. He sold his in winter. Which means the ledger is still open. Which means somewhere below, in whatever counts for ink down there, his name is still written — and anything written can be crossed out.*

Ulyana looked at the fire, and past the fire at the door of the keep, and past the door at the mountain holding its long black breath.

"I'm coming, Tam," she said. It was the first time in twenty years she had said his name aloud, and the room took it the way snow takes a footstep — completely, and without giving it back.

In the next room, Vess slept, and did not talk in her sleep tonight. She had said what she came to say.

Tomorrow, Ulyana thought, she would need a guide who knew the roads that weren't on any map, and there was exactly one man foolish enough and loyal enough and indebted enough to walk them with her — and he was due at the pass within the week, because Bramble Ostwick always ran the winter route before the snows closed, always, the one reliable thing about him.

She blew out the lamp and began, in the dark, to pack.
