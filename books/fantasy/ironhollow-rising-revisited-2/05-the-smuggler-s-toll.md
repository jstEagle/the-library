## 5. The Smuggler's Toll

Bramble Ostwick had three rules, and he'd kept them for twenty years without breaking a single one.

One: never carry anything you can't eat, drink, or deny. Two: never owe anyone more than they owe you. Three: never, under any circumstances, go back down the Thornfell roads after the first hard frost.

He was breaking rule three in his head before he'd even finished his tea, which was how it always started with Ulyana Sterling. She didn't ask for things. That was the trouble with her. She just sat there across the table in the back room of the Drowned Bell with her hands wrapped around a cup she wasn't drinking from, and she let the silence do the asking, and Bramble — fool that he was, fool that he'd been since the winter she'd looked the other way on four crates of unmarked brandy and a sick man hidden in the fifth — found himself owing her things he hadn't agreed to owe.

"You've heard something," she said.

It wasn't a question. It never was, with her. Ulyana asked questions the way a gatekeeper counted heads: you either passed through or you didn't, and there was no pretending you were somewhere else.

"I hear lots of things," Bramble said. "I'm a smuggler. Hearing's half the trade."

"Then tell me the half that matters."

He turned his cup on the table. The Drowned Bell's back room smelled of tallow and old smoke and the river-damp that got into everything below the cliff road, and outside the shuttered window the wind was already carrying that particular dry bite that meant the passes would close within the week. Last passable night. He'd been planning to spend it drinking somewhere warm and far away from mountains.

Instead here he was, being counted through a gate.

"The deep court's woken." He said it low, though there was no one to hear but the mice and the ghost of a card game long disbanded. "That's the word coming up the Thornfell roads, anyway. And before you make that face — I know what you'll say. Fairy story. Old wives' tale for people who drink too much. But Ostwick hears things, and Ostwick is telling you: three separate caravans have come up the root-side passes this month, and all three paid tolls they don't usually pay, and all three came up quiet. Not robbed-quiet. *Paid*-quiet. You understand the difference?"

Ulyana's face did the thing it always did when she was working through something — nothing at all. A stillness like held breath.

"Paying upward," she said. "Something below is buying passage."

"That's my read of it too." Bramble rubbed his jaw. His beard needed trimming; everything about him needed trimming; he'd been meaning to get around to himself for years. "And it's not coin they're paying in, if you follow me. One of the caravans — the Marlow outfit, you remember Marlow, big fellow, laughs like a landslide — well. Marlow doesn't laugh anymore. Came through Greywater two weeks back and his whole crew walked like men who'd signed something. Wouldn't say what. Wouldn't say to who. Just kept touching their throats like they were checking their pulses hadn't been replaced."

"And the child." Ulyana said it flat, but Bramble heard the floorboards creak under the weight of it. "The one at my door. Have you heard anything about a child on the roads? Coming up alone?"

He had, as it happened. He'd heard it three days ago from a mole-catcher at Thornfell's edge who spoke it like an apology, the way people speak things they wish they could unhear. A little girl walking the high snow without footprints. Barefoot. The mole-catcher's dog had refused to go near her, had lain down flat and whined like the animal remembered something its bloodline should have forgotten.

"There's talk," Bramble said carefully. "Talk of a small traveler on the high ways. Talk that the snow didn't take her."

Ulyana closed her eyes for exactly one second. When she opened them again they were keeper's eyes, gatekeeper's eyes, the eyes of a woman who had spent fifteen years standing between a mountain and everything it wanted to give birth to, and Bramble felt the familiar sinking in his stomach, the one that arrived whenever he realized he was about to be made part of something larger than a man's life ought to contain.

"I need to go down," she said. "Into Thornfell. Toward the deep roads."

"No."

"You haven't heard where—"

"I don't need to hear where. There's no *where* worth going down there in winter, Ulyana. The Thornfell roads shift. Everyone knows it. You walk them in summer with a guide and a prayer and you might come out where you meant to. In winter they walk *you*." He leaned forward. "You're the keeper of a sealed gate. Sealed means sealed. Whatever's woken down there, your job is the same as it's been for fifteen years: stand on top of it and pretend it's a fairy story."

"It stopped being a fairy story when it knocked on my door barefoot."

* * *

They argued for another hour, which by their standards was brief. Bramble laid out every reason, and Ulyana absorbed every reason the way stone absorbs rain, and at the end of it she stood, put on her coat, and said, "You owe me three favors. I'm calling one."

Bramble stared at her. "That's not how favors work."

"How do they work?"

"They—" He gestured vaguely at the air, at twenty years of careful accounting. "They sit there. They accrue interest. They're a *reserve*, Ulyana. A man keeps favors the way he keeps coin buried under a floorboard — against disaster. You don't spend a favor on a suicide walk into the Thornfell roads in midwinter."

"I'm not spending it on the walk." She paused at the door, hand on the latch, and for a moment — just a moment — the competence cracked, and he saw underneath it the thing he'd suspected for fifteen years and never once mentioned: a woman profoundly, catastrophically tired. "I'm spending it on you saying yes. The walk I'll manage myself if I have to. But you know those roads better than any living soul, Bramble, and you know the old signs, and if there's even a chance the deep court has woken, then the gate at Ironhollow isn't holding back a story anymore. It's holding back a creditor. And creditors, you of all people should understand, always come to collect."

She left him sitting there with his cold tea and his three rules, two of which he'd already broken in spirit and the third of which was about to die screaming.

* * *

He caught up with her at the stable behind the Bell, because of course he did. Twenty years of interest, compounding.

"Partway," he said. She turned. Snow had begun to fall, fat and slow, the kind of snow that meant business. "I'll take you partway. To the Hollow Inn, maybe past it if the roads behave. After that you're on your own, and I'll be telling everyone who asks that I tried to stop you, which will even be true, which is the best kind of lie."

Ulyana studied him. "What's your price?"

"Don't have one."

"Everyone has a price. You taught me that."

"I taught you no such thing. I taught you that everyone has a *rate*." He sighed, and the sigh came out of some cellar in him he kept locked, and it dragged up things he'd rather have left in the dark. "Fine. My price. Here it is, and you're going to hate it, and I want you to hate it properly, with your whole heart, because that's the only way this works."

"Say it."

"When we come back up — *if* we come back up — you burn the ledger page." Her expression flickered, and he pressed on before sense could reassert itself. "Your ledger, Ulyana. The one with my name in it. Three favors, fifteen years of entries, every small kindness you ever did me written down in that neat keeper's hand. Burn it. All of it. I don't want to owe you anymore, and I don't want you to owe me, and I especially don't want the deep court — which buys debts, if the rumors are half true — getting its hands on a document that says Bramble Ostwick owes the Keeper of Ironhollow three favors and counting."

"That page protects you," she said quietly. "If anyone ever comes collecting on you, that page is proof of service rendered. Proof you're mine to answer for."

"Aye. And proof is exactly what a court of creditors loves best." He pulled his collar up against the snow. "Debts are currency down there, or so the stories say. I'd rather be a poor free man than a rich marked one. So that's my rate, keeper. One favor, spent on guiding you partway. Price: the erasure of the rest. We settle clean, or we don't settle at all."

For a long moment the snow fell between them, filling the spaces where words should have gone.

"You understand what you're really buying," Ulyana said at last. "Not freedom. Distance. Once that page burns, I have no hold on you, and you have none on me. If you die down there, I won't owe your ghost a tear."

Bramble grinned, and it hurt his face, because it wasn't entirely a grin. "Wouldn't want your ghost crying over me anyway. Terrible for the reputation." He stuck out his hand. "Do we have terms, Sterling?"

She took it. Her grip was cold and strong and final, a gate closing.

"We have terms," she said. "Partway. Then clean."

"Then let's go break rule three properly." He swung up onto his mule, Hesper, who gave him the look of profound grievance that had been her lifelong gift to him. "And Ulyana — the child. The one the snow wouldn't take. Where is she now?"

"Sleeping in my bed. Under three blankets and a wardline I drew myself." A pause. "She talks in her sleep. In the old tongue. And last night, Bramble — the wards answered her. Hummed. Like they knew her voice."

Hesper shifted under him. Somewhere up the valley, muffled by miles of falling snow, the mountain made a sound that was almost certainly rock settling and almost certainly nothing else.

Bramble Ostwick looked at the dark line of the Thornfell passes, thought about ledgers and interest rates and the exact weight of a burned page, and understood with perfect clarity that he was about to spend the last of his careful life on a woman and a child who were themselves spent coins in someone else's ancient account.

"Well," he said. "No time like midwinter to ruin a spotless record."

He turned the mule toward the mountain, and the snow closed over the road behind them like a page being turned.
