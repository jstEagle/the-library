## 8. The Hollow Inn

Bramble Ostwick had a rule about inns: never sleep in one where the fire doesn't crackle.

It wasn't superstition, not exactly. A fire that burned silent was a fire being *watched*, and a fire being watched meant the room it warmed belonged to somebody else. He'd learned that rule on the Thornfell roads at fourteen, from a one-eyed carter named Doss who'd died warm and unbothered in an inn exactly like this one, and Bramble had spent thirty years since then trusting Doss's rules over his own instincts, because Doss had died comfortable and Bramble intended to do the same.

The fire in the common room of the Waymeet House did not crackle.

It burned low and orange and utterly, perfectly silent, like a painting of a fire rather than a fire itself, and every patron in the room sat facing it with the stillness of furniture. There were perhaps fifteen of them — drovers, peddlers, a pair of miners still dusted grey to the elbow — and none of them were talking. None of them were eating, though bowls sat steaming untouched before most of them. And when the innkeeper's boy crossed the room to fetch a pitcher, every head turned to follow him. All together. Smooth as a flock of birds changing direction.

They didn't blink right, either. Bramble noticed it within his first minute inside, standing in the doorway with snow melting down his collar. People blinked in rhythms — quick, quick, slow, a little flutter when they thought about something else. These patrons blinked like hinges. Once, deliberate, all the way down, and open again. On the beat. Together.

"Room for three?" he said to the innkeeper, because turning around and walking back into the blizzard was no longer an option. The pass had closed behind them an hour ago. He'd watched it close.

The innkeeper was a broad woman with flour on her apron and a smile that arrived a half-second late, like a messenger who'd gotten lost on the way. "Always room," she said. "For travelers on the old road." Her eyes flicked past him to Ulyana, and something moved behind them — recognition, or its counterfeit. "Keeper Sterling."

"You know me?"

"Everyone knows the keeper of Ironhollow." She wiped her hands. "Supper's included. The stew is mutton."

Bramble looked at the stew. The stew looked back, in the sense that there was something in it, submerged, that he chose not to identify. He took the bowl anyway. Survival on the Thornfell roads was mostly a matter of deciding which indignities you could digest.

Ulyana took the bench against the wall, back to the timber, with the sightline to both doors — she'd done it without thinking, the way she did everything, and Bramble felt the familiar ache of loving a woman built entirely out of duty and refusing to call it what it was. Vess climbed up beside her and pressed herself small against Ulyana's side, the way she'd done since the steward's shadow had crossed their road two days back. The child hadn't spoken — couldn't, in any language Bramble knew — but she'd been humming under her breath since the pass. A little three-note phrase, over and over, tuneless as wind through a keyhole.

The patrons didn't look at Vess. That was the worst part, Bramble decided, halfway through the mutton. Fifteen people in a room, and not one of them so much as glanced at a barefoot child in a dead man's coat. In any honest inn on any honest road, a child like Vess would draw every eye like a wound draws flies. Here she might as well have been invisible.

Or — and Bramble's stomach turned over the thought like a wagon in a rut — here she was *known*. Expected. The kind of guest you don't gawk at because you were told she was coming.

He finished the stew. It tasted of nothing much, which was better than it tasting of something.

* * *

The rooms upstairs were clean and cold and had no locks, only latches, and Bramble sat on the floor between the two doors with his back against the wall and his knife across his knees and told himself this was normal caution, standard practice, nothing to do with the way the floorboards below them had gone quiet at sundown. An inn full of people made noise. This one made silence, and the silence had weight, like snow on a roof.

Near midnight, he heard Ulyana's door ease open.

He didn't move. She crouched beside him in the dark, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the only sound in the world was Vess breathing slow through the wall.

"The barkeep," Ulyana whispered. "He came up an hour ago and stood outside our door. Didn't knock. Just stood."

"How long?"

"Long enough. Then he went down." She was quiet a moment. "Bramble. I need to ask you something, and I need you not to argue."

"That's never once worked."

"I'm going down to talk to the innkeeper. Alone."

"No," Bramble said, with feeling.

"Bramble—"

"You want to trade something. I can hear it in your voice. You've got your trading voice on, the one you use on me, and I know what it costs you every time, and I am telling you—" he kept his voice down but barely, "—whatever you're thinking of giving, this place will take more than you mean to pay. Look at the fire downstairs, Uly. Look at the guests. This isn't an inn. It's a *mouth*."

"I know what it is." Her voice didn't waver. It never wavered; that was the whole problem with her. "That's why it has to be me. If it goes wrong, you take Vess out the stable door and down the drift road to the charcoal burners' camp. They owe me. You'll be safe by dawn."

"And you?"

She didn't answer, which was an answer, and Bramble pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes and swore softly at the ceiling, at the mountain, at the entire rotten architecture of the world that kept putting brave women in front of hungry doors.

"Three favors," he said finally. "You remember. You've been sitting on them for six years like a dragon on a hoard. I'm spending one. You don't go down alone — we go down together, and if the innkeeper wants a price, we *both* hear it, because you've got a keeper's habit of paying things quietly and telling nobody until it's too late to stop you."

Ulyana was silent long enough that he thought she'd refuse. Then, in the dark, almost too quiet to catch:

"She'd have liked you, my mother. Terrible judge of character, but loyal to a fault."

"Your mother would've hated me."

"She did hate you. For years." But there was warmth under it, thin as winter sun, and Bramble held onto that all the way down the stairs.

* * *

The common room at midnight was worse than the common room at supper.

The fire still burned without sound. The patrons were gone — all fifteen of them, bowls still steaming on the tables, benches pushed back as if they'd risen all at once — and the innkeeper stood behind the bar polishing a glass that was already clean, and when she looked up at them her smile arrived on time this time, which was somehow far worse than it arriving late.

"Keeper Sterling," she said. "And the smuggler. Sit. The tea is fresh."

There was a pot on the bar, steam curling off it, and Bramble thought with distant horror: *it was waiting for us.* The whole night had been waiting for this, the empty room, the silent fire, the hour when honest folk were asleep. This was the inn's true face, and it had put on the crowd earlier the way a fisherman puts on bait.

Ulyana sat. Bramble stood behind her, which was as much defiance as his legs would manage.

"I want information," Ulyana said. No preamble. She poured the tea herself, three cups, and pushed one toward the innkeeper, and Bramble saw the woman's hands go still over her glass — a flicker of something, surprise maybe, or respect. You didn't get to be keeper of Ironhollow by forgetting that hospitality ran both directions, even with monsters.

"Information is the dearest thing on this road," the innkeeper said. "Cheaper things are available. Beds. Stew. Passage south, before midwinter closes everything."

"I'm not going south."

"No," the innkeeper agreed pleasantly. "I don't suppose you are. Word came up the root roads two nights ago. The steward's riders passed through asking after a woman and a child. Descriptions were precise." Her eyes settled on Vess's empty chair upstairs, then returned. "Precise descriptions cost money to obtain, Keeper. Everything does. That's the first law under the mountain, and lately it's become the law above it too."

"What do you want?"

The innkeeper set down her glass. Folded her hands. And for just a moment — a blink that lasted too long, a hinge swinging wide — Bramble saw something else looking out of her face. Something patient and old and terribly polite.

"Something you'd miss," she said. "Something true. Not coin — coin is a promise, and promises are cheap down here. Not blood — blood regrows. Something given freely that cannot be taken back."

Ulyana's hand went to the ring before Bramble could stop her.

He'd seen it a thousand times over fifteen years of favors and freight and one unforgettable night neither of them ever mentioned: a plain band of Thornfell silver, worn smooth, on the third finger of her left hand where a wedding ring would sit if she'd ever married, which she hadn't. He'd assumed, once, years ago, that it had been Tam's — a brother's keepsake. He'd never asked. Keepers and smugglers survived by not asking.

"My mother's ring," Ulyana said, and her voice was level, and Bramble wanted to break something. "Silver from the Thornfell seam. Smelted before the kingdom came. It's true. It's mine. Given freely."

The innkeeper's gaze fixed on it with an appetite that had nothing to do with silver.

"For what?" Bramble cut in. "Name the price *before* she pays it. What does she get?"

"The rolls," the innkeeper said, without looking away from the ring. "The debt-rolls of the court beneath, as they stand tonight. Every name. Every sum. Every term." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Including whether a certain name remains upon them. A name the Keeper has been carrying twenty years like a stone in her boot."

The silence stretched. The fire said nothing, because the fire said nothing always.

"Tam Sterling," Ulyana said quietly. "Is his debt still owed? Is he still—" Her voice did something then, the smallest fracture, hairline as the crack in her wardstone. "Is he alive?"

"He is *held*," the innkeeper said. "Which under the mountain is a form of life. Whether it's a form worth having — well." She extended one hand across the bar, palm up, fingers curled slightly inward, and Bramble thought of every trap he'd ever seen sprung and how they'd all begun with an open hand. "The ring, Keeper. And the rolls. Both names, both sums, both truths. That is the toll."

"Don't," Bramble said.

"It's a ring."

"It's your mother's ring and you know it and I know it and *she* knows it, whatever she is, and that's why she wants it. It's not the silver. It's that it *hurts*." He stepped forward. "Take my ledger instead. My debts — the real ones, the ones the court holds on me. Wipe them. That's worth more than a ring and you know it."

The innkeeper finally looked at him, and her expression was almost gentle, which frightened him more than anything yet.

"Smuggler Ostwick. Your debts are not yours to sell. They belong to the court, and the court does not discount." Her palm stayed open. "The ring, or nothing. The steward collects at midwinter, four days hence. After that, no roll in this house opens for love or silver."

Four days. Bramble watched Ulyana's face do the arithmetic — four days to reach wherever the steward collected, four days with a mute child and a coward and a dead man's name — and he watched her decide, the way she always decided, instantly and alone, as if consulting anyone were a weakness she couldn't afford.

She pulled the ring off. It caught on the knuckle — her hands were shaking, just slightly, just enough — and then it was free, and she set it in the innkeeper's palm, and the innkeeper closed her fingers around it the way a mouth closes.

"Thank you," the innkeeper said, and meant it, which was the most horrible thing she'd said all night.

She reached beneath the bar and brought up a book.

It was black-bound and swollen with use, its spine cracked white, its pages soft as cloth at the edges, and when she opened it the smell that rose off it was old paper and cold iron and something underneath both, something organic, like the inside of a root. The columns were written in ink that shifted color when Bramble tilted his head — brown, then rust, then a red too dark to be ink at all.

"Read quickly," the innkeeper said. "The rolls resist being known."

Ulyana read. Bramble watched her lips move, watched her finger travel down column after column of names in scripts he half-recognized — kingdom letters, pre-kingdom letters, and some that weren't letters at all but marks like claw-scars. The list went back centuries. Some entries were struck through in that dark red, debts paid or souls consumed, he couldn't tell which, and the struck-through names outnumbered the living ones ten to one.

Her finger stopped.

Bramble leaned in. The entry was older than the others around it, the ink faded to the color of dried blood, but legible enough:

*Tammas Sterling, of Ironhollow. One soul, whole, sold against winter. Term: service perpetual, interest compounding. Balance after twenty years:* 

The number that followed had too many digits. Bramble's eye slid off it twice before he forced himself to count, and when he understood what he was counting he felt cold all the way down, colder than the pass, colder than the grave. Twenty years of interest on a single soul, compounded, and the sum now exceeded every debt on the page combined.

"They can't collect that," he whispered. "No soul's worth that. Nobody could ever pay it."

"No," the innkeeper agreed. "That is rather the point. Debts that cannot be paid make excellent chains." She closed the book, gently, reverently, and the smell of roots faded. "He serves in the archive, deep beneath. The archivist of the court. He has served... faithfully. As much as anything hollow can serve."

"Can I buy him out?" Ulyana's voice was terrible and steady. "Whatever the balance is. Name it."

"You could not pay it in a hundred lifetimes, Keeper, and the court would not accept payment if you could. A debt that size is not a debt anymore. It's an *investment*." The innkeeper smiled her late-arriving smile, and touched two fingers to the spot where the ring now sat hidden in her apron pocket, the way a person touches a relic. "But balances can be *transferred*. Terms can be renegotiated. Collateral can be substituted." Her eyes moved, very slowly, from Ulyana's bare left hand to the ceiling, toward the room where a child slept humming her three-note prayer. "Under the mountain, everything is negotiable. That is the second law."

"Get her out of your eyes," Bramble said, and his voice cracked on it, and he didn't care.

The innkeeper inclined her head, gracious as a duchess. "Of course. The toll is paid; the truth is yours. Now hear the last of it free, as courtesy between traders." She leaned across the bar, and up close her breath smelled of nothing at all, which was wrong, everyone's breath smelled of something. "Midwinter, four days hence, the steward comes to the crossroads shrine below Thornfell to collect. He will expect the child delivered to him there — the riders made that plain enough. But the steward is not the throne, whatever he pretends. And the throne has been empty a long, long time." Her blink came down slow, a hinge oiled in the dark. "Some of us, Keeper, have been waiting twenty years for someone foolish enough to walk down there with nothing left to lose."

The fire went out. Not guttered — *went*, all at once, like a held breath released, and in the sudden dark the innkeeper's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, pleasant as ever:

"Sleep well. Breakfast is included."

* * *

Bramble lay awake in the cold room until the grey hour, knife in hand, listening to the inn breathe.

Around the fourth bell, the sounds started. Below the floorboards, beneath the cellar, deeper than any cellar should go — a slow rhythmic knocking, patient as a heartbeat, like something very large counting. Or like something very large *keeping time*. And threaded through it, faint and rising, Vess's three-note hum, coming through the wall, matching the knocking note for note.

The child wasn't having nightmares.

The child was answering.

Bramble got up before dawn, woke Ulyana with a hand over her mouth before she could gasp, and told her what he'd heard. She listened the way she listened to everything — completely, immediately, without wasting a second on disbelief — and then she looked at the door, and at the sleeping shape of Vess, and Bramble watched the last of the keeper of Ironhollow fall away and something harder take its place.

"Four days," Ulyana said. "Then we don't wait four days. We leave now, before the steward's riders double back. Down the drift road, to the shrine."

"You heard her. That's what they *expect*."

"Yes." Ulyana was already pulling on her boots, already moving, already becoming the thing that walked into mouths on purpose. "So we'll bring them something they don't expect."

Bramble looked at her, and at the child stirring in the blankets, and at the window where the snow had stopped falling for the first time in three days — stopped all at once, everywhere, as if the sky had been paid to hold its breath.

"And what's that?" he asked.

Ulyana picked up her grandmother's primer and slid it inside her coat, over her heart, where the ring used to sit.

"A debt of our own," she said.
