## 9. Midwinter's Steward

The innkeeper's warning had been simple enough: *when the candles gutter all at once and the fire burns blue, go to your room, bar the door, and don't answer.* Ulyana had thanked him, paid for the information with the last of Bramble's coin — he'd grumbled about that for an hour — and then spent the rest of the evening pretending she hadn't heard it.

Now the candles guttered.

All nine of them at once, in the common room of the Hollow Inn, as if a great breath had been drawn through the walls. The hearthfire didn't die; it changed. The orange light sank into a deep, cold blue, and the shadows of the patrons stretched long and wrong across the floorboards, though none of them moved. None of them even looked up from their cups. That was the worst part, Ulyana thought. Not the blue fire. The stillness. A dozen people sitting in a burning-cold room and not one of them turning their heads, as if they had all agreed, silently and long ago, exactly what they would not see tonight.

Vess's small hand found hers under the table. The child didn't flinch. She simply gripped, hard, the way she did when her dream-speech came close to the surface, and Ulyana felt the tremor running through that little arm and understood: Vess knew what was coming too. Knew it the way she knew the roads before they appeared.

"Upstairs," Ulyana murmured. "Now."

Bramble was already on his feet, hand nowhere near a weapon because he was smart enough to know weapons were useless here, his face gone the color of tallow. "That's him," he breathed. "That's the steward. I told you he collects at midwinter. I told you—"

"You told me." Ulyana lifted Vess onto her hip — the child was seven, far too old to be carried, and neither of them acknowledged it — and moved for the stairs. Behind her, the blue fire made no sound at all. Fire should crackle. This one hummed, low and patient, like a held note in a song that never ended.

They got the door barred. They got the shutters closed. They sat in the narrow room with its one bed and its water stain shaped like a weeping woman, and they waited, and Ulyana counted her heartbeats because counting was something keepers did, and keepers' arithmetic had always been the only kind of magic she trusted.

She reached two hundred when the knock came.

Three knocks. Soft. Courteous. The kind of knock a neighbor pays when returning a borrowed tool.

Nobody spoke. Vess pressed her face into Ulyana's shoulder. Bramble stood against the wall with his eyes shut, lips moving through what might have been a prayer or an inventory of his sins.

Then the voice came through the door, and it was warm. That was the horror of it. It was warm the way a banker's handshake is warm, the way a loan is warm when you're freezing.

"Mistress Sterling." A pause, perfectly measured. "I know you're awake. Keepers sleep badly — it's in the nature of the work. May I come in? I promise you, on every ledger I hold, that no harm will come to you or yours while we speak."

Ulyana looked down at Vess. The child looked up at her, and in the dark her eyes caught some light that wasn't there, faint and silver, like frost forming on glass.

*Don't,* said nothing at all. But Ulyana heard it anyway, or imagined she did, and found she agreed.

"No," she said clearly. "Whatever you are, you can say it through the door. Doors are older than courts. Even yours."

A silence. Then laughter — soft, genuine-sounding, delighted. "Oh, *good*. Tam's sister. He said you'd be stubborn. Well — he said it once, twenty years ago, before we filed most of him away. But the shape of it remains. Stubbornness keeps beautifully. Like salted meat."

Ulyana's whole body went cold, colder than the Thornfell night outside. *He said.* Tam had spoken of her. Some crumb of her brother, buried under twenty years of interest, had mentioned her name to this thing.

"Come to the door, then," she said. "If you're so courteous."

The door didn't open. It simply stopped being closed — there between one blink and the next, the doorway stood empty of wood, and the steward stood in it, hat in hand, bowing slightly at the waist.

He wore a face assembled from borrowed features. That was Ulyana's first coherent thought, and it stayed with her the way a brand stays. His left eye was brown and kind and human, crow's-feet at its corner, the eye of someone's grandfather. His right was grey and lidless and set slightly too high, the eye of something that had lived underwater for a very long time. His mouth was full and generous, a mouth built for laughing, but it sat crooked on his jaw, half a finger off true, as if the face had been assembled by someone working quickly and without a mirror. His skin didn't match itself — smooth at the throat, weathered at the brow, the knuckles of his hands young and unlined while the backs of them were mapped with age spots. He was tall. He was neatly dressed in a coat of dark green wool, cut in a fashion two centuries out of date, and he smelled faintly of ink and cold cellars and, underneath it all, of snowmelt over old bones.

"Forgive the intrusion," he said, replacing his hat. "Midwinter is a busy season for me. Collections, renewals, the occasional repossession. One does one's rounds." He glanced around the shabby room with polite interest, taking in the water stain, the barred shutter, the child. "You've been difficult to find, Mistress Sterling. Which is remarkable, given that you have spent your entire life standing in one place."

"I move when my work moves," Ulyana said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "State your business."

"My business." He smiled with the borrowed mouth, and the smile fit it better than anything else had. "Your business, rather. Or perhaps I should say your *family's* business, which has been my business these twenty years, accruing quietly, the way good debts do." He drew a slim book from inside his coat — black binding, silver clasps, pages that whispered when they moved as though each leaf were alive and unhappy about it. "Tamriel Sterling. Entered into our rolls on the ninth night of winter, twenty years past. Principal: one soul, entire. Collateral received: memory, name-recall, the color of certain childhood summers. Interest schedule: compounding." He looked up, and both mismatched eyes fixed on her with terrible attentiveness. "The account is in arrears, Mistress Sterling. It has been in arrears since the day it was opened. Your brother has been paying with what he has — hours, service, small pieces of himself — but pieces are not payment. Pieces are only delay."

Vess made a sound against Ulyana's shoulder. Small. The steward turned his head toward her with the smoothness of water finding its level, and the brown eye softened, and the grey one did not.

"And here is the little foundling," he said gently. "Vess. Do you know, we searched for you for a very long time? There are those below who never stopped looking. And there are those—" the smile thinned by a degree, the first honest expression he'd worn "—who hoped you'd stay lost. Children are such *complications* in a well-ordered ledger."

"She's not in your book," Ulyana said.

"No," the steward agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

The word landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. Ulyana heard it fall and fall without hitting bottom.

"But I am not here for the child," he continued, waving one young-knuckled hand as if brushing away smoke. "Not directly. I am here, Mistress Sterling, to make you an offer. The court beneath sends its compliments and its regrets — the regrets are traditional; the compliments are sincere. We understand you have come down the Thornfell roads seeking your brother's soul. Ambitious. Foolish. Touching. All three, usually, in family matters." He stepped further into the room, unhurried, and Ulyana noticed that the floorboards did not creak beneath him. Nothing about him touched the world quite enough to disturb it. "So. The offer."

He opened the black book to a marked page and turned it so she could see. The writing was in the pre-kingdom tongue, flowing right to left, and beside it, in newer ink, a translation had been added in a clerk's careful hand. Tam's name. Tam's debt. Twenty years of entries, each one a little smaller than the last, the handwriting degrading year by year until the final lines were barely legible — scratched, desperate, the script of a man writing with what remained of himself.

Ulyana's throat closed. She had prepared herself for a monster. She had not prepared herself for evidence.

"The offer is this," the steward said. "Surrender your claim."

"My claim?"

"On the soul. On the account. Legally speaking — and everything, *everything*, beneath the mountain is legal speaking — the debt belongs to Tamriel. But claims may be transferred, contested, inherited. You are his blood. Standing here in this room, you hold a claim on his soul's remainder that even I cannot simply override." He closed the book with a soft clap. "Renounce it. Sign it away, freely, before witnesses. In exchange, the court returns the child Vess to the surface unharmed, escorted, provisioned, delivered to Ironhollow gate by spring thaw. Safe passage for the smuggler as well — his accounts with us are, shall we say, extensive, and we are prepared to forgive them entirely. Everyone walks away. Everyone lives. The mountain goes back to sleep, and you go back to your ledger of small favors and your quiet gate, and in twenty years this will feel like a strange dream you had at midwinter."

The offer hung in the blue-lit air, complete and reasonable and monstrous.

Ulyana looked at the page she'd seen. At the shrinking handwriting. At Vess, whose grip on her collar had gone white-knuckled. At Bramble, who had opened his eyes and was staring at the steward with the expression of a man watching his own funeral being scheduled.

"And Tam?" she asked. "In your generous arrangement. What happens to my brother?"

The steward tilted his head, and the borrowed face arranged itself into something meant to be sympathetic. It nearly worked. That was the skill of it — the near-working.

"Tamriel remains in the court's service. As he has been. As he will be. His debt stands; only your claim upon it is released. He keeps the archives. He is... useful. Contented, insofar as his kind are contented." A pause. "You would be trading a claim you cannot collect for the life of a child you can save. Any reasonable person—"

"I'm not reasonable," Ulyana said. "I'm a keeper."

The steward's smile flickered. Just for a heartbeat, the two mismatched eyes went flat and ancient and hungry, and Ulyana saw beneath the courtesy to the thing wearing it — saw the shape of the appetite that had assembled this face feature by feature from people who had owed and could not pay. Then the warmth returned, smoother than before, and somehow that was worse.

"A keeper," he repeated. "Yes. Fifteen years at a sealed gate, keeping a seal you've never tested, guarding a court you insist is a fairy story. And yet here you are, three days down the Thornfell roads, in an inn where the fire burns blue, negotiating with the fairy story's accountant." He spread his hands. "Believe what you like about us, Mistress Sterling. But the mathematics do not care what you believe. One soul against one soul. A brother already spent, against a child who is — forgive my bluntness — worth considerably more than either of you can imagine. Refuse, and you lose them both. Accept, and you lose one and save two. I have worn many faces, keeper. I have never yet met the face that could look at that arithmetic and choose wrongly."

"Then you haven't been wearing faces long enough," Ulyana said, "or you'd have met mine."

She set Vess down. The child stood beside her, small and silent, and Ulyana put her hand on the girl's head — not protectively now, but the way a keeper puts a hand on a wardstone. Anchoring. Claiming.

"Here is my answer," she said. "I don't surrender claims. I *keep* things. That's the whole of my trade, and I've done it fifteen years in the wind and the dark for wages no sane person would take, because keeping is what I am for. My brother's soul is kept. My claim on it is kept. And this child—" her hand tightened, gentle, immovable "—this child is kept too. By me. You want her? You want what's left of my brother? Come to Ironhollow and try to collect at my gate, steward, and learn why the deep folk sealed themselves under a mountain in the first place."

Silence. The blue fire hummed. Somewhere below the floorboards, in the cellar of the Hollow Inn, something shifted its weight — a vast, patient shifting, like a body turning over in sleep.

And then the steward laughed again, and this time there was no performance in it at all. It rang out bright and startled and almost young, and for one absurd moment he looked like whatever man he had been before the borrowing began.

"Ohh," he breathed, wiping the borrowed mouth with one knuckle. "*Oh*, that's the stuff. Twenty years I've waited for someone to talk to me like that. Do you know what they say to me below? *Yes, lord steward. Of course, lord steward. How much, lord steward?*" He shook his head, savoring it. "Your brother said something similar, once. First week. Before we began the deductions." The mirth drained out of him by degrees, like water through sand, until the courteous terrible thing was back, regarding her with something that might, in a kinder creature, have been respect. "Very well, keeper. No signature tonight. But understand what you've chosen. Midwinter ends in six days. The court collects at midwinter — all of it, everything outstanding, everywhere. When the reckoning comes, your claim will be called, your brother will be called, and the child..." He looked at Vess, and the grey eye gleamed. "The child will be *found*. She is already found, mistress. She has been walking toward us since the day she walked out of the snow. You haven't been rescuing her. You've been *escorting* her."

He replaced his hat. He bowed — a full, formal, courtly bow, deeper than any he'd given before, and Ulyana realized with a lurch that it was not mockery. It was ceremony. It was the bow one gives before a war formally opens.

"Six days," the steward said, straightening. "Rest well. Eat well. The roads ahead are long, and the root roads longer, and I would hate for you to arrive at the court hungry. We are monsters, Mistress Sterling, but we are *hospitable* monsters. It's in the ledgers."

The doorway filled with wood again. The blue fire leapt back to orange. Around the common room below, Ulyana heard the inn exhale — cups clinking, voices resuming mid-sentence, a dozen patrons remembering how to blink — as if the whole building had been holding its breath and had only now been permitted to release it.

Bramble slid down the wall until he was sitting on the floor, hat over his face. "Six days," he said hollowly. "He gave us six days. That's not mercy, Uli. That's a hunting season with a start date."

But Ulyana wasn't listening. She was kneeling, gathering Vess against her chest, and the child was trembling now — trembling hard, all the stillness shaken out of her at last — and against Ulyana's ear, in the pre-kingdom tongue, in that dream-voice that was older than any kingdom, Vess said one word.

Ulyana knew it. She'd read it a hundred times in her grandmother's forbidden primer, tracing the angular letters by candlelight, never guessing she'd need it.

It meant *faster.*

Below them, beneath the inn, beneath the road, beneath the roots of the Thornfell mountains, something vast began to move — and every candle in the Hollow Inn leaned toward the floor, all at once, like grass bending under wind, pointing the way down.
