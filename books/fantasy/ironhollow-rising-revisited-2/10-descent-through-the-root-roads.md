## 10. Descent Through the Root Roads

Bramble Ostwick had been down a great many holes in his life, and he had a professional rule about all of them: know your way out before you go in.

He broke that rule at the mouth of the root road, and he knew it, and he went in anyway, because the alternative was standing very still in the snow while a man wearing eleven borrowed faces counted Bramble's debts aloud like a butcher naming cuts.

"Down," Ulyana Sterling had said, and down they went.

The entrance wasn't a door. That was the first thing wrong with it. It was a seam in the mountainside where the rock had been persuaded to part — not cut, not quarried, *grown* apart, the way a mouth opens. The edges of it were smooth as the inside of a shell, and warm, which no hole in Thornfell had any business being in midwinter. Pale roots laced across the opening like the ribs of something enormous sleeping on its side.

Vess walked straight up to it and put her small bare hand flat against the largest root, and the root — Bramble would swear this on his mother's grave, if he ever managed to get one — the root curled toward her fingers. Just slightly. Like a dog lifting its head when someone it knows comes through the gate.

"That's not natural," Bramble said.

"Neither is she," Ulyana said. "In."

"In where? I don't know this road. Nobody knows this road. The root roads aren't on any map I've ever bought, sold, or stolen, and I have stolen maps from people who steal maps for a living—"

"Bramble." She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; that was the whole terror of her. "He's coming back with more of them. Do you want to argue about cartography, or do you want to be alive tomorrow?"

He wanted, honestly, to be drunk yesterday, but there was no road to that either. So he hitched his pack higher, muttered an apology to every god of thresholds and doorways he'd ever shortchanged, and stepped into the dark after a mute child and a keeper who'd abandoned the only post she'd held for fifteen years.

The seam closed behind them without a sound.

That was the second thing wrong with it.

* * *

Inside, the road was alive, and Bramble hated it immediately and completely.

Smuggler roads were supposed to be honest in their dishonesty: stone, dust, old torch-brackets, the bones of some fool who'd taken the wrong turn in 1803. You could read a tunnel. A tunnel told you its age by its drips and its drafts, told you where the collapses were, told you how long since anything living passed through. Bramble had made his entire living off reading tunnels the way other men read ledgers.

This place refused to be read. The walls were root and packed earth and pale wood grown in braids thick as oxen, and they gave off a faint grey-gold light from somewhere inside themselves, like the memory of sunlight kept too long in a cellar. The air was warm and smelled of loam and, faintly, of apples — apples gone over, fermenting sweet in some far-off dark. There were no forks. There were no markers. The passage simply went, curving gently, always downward, always the width of three men walking abreast, as though the mountain had grown a gullet and they were all of them well along the way to being swallowed.

And it moved.

Bramble tested this three times before he let himself believe it. He'd drop a pebble behind him, count fifty paces, and look back: the pebble would be gone, or the wall beside it would have shifted a hand's width closer, or a new buttress of root would stand exactly where he'd walked. Not fast. Nothing so honest as fast. Just slow, patient rearrangement, like furniture moved while you slept.

"The road's shifting," he said, for the fourth time, because saying it out loud was the only thing keeping it from being madness.

"I know," Ulyana said.

"It's following us. Or leading us. Those are different problems, Keeper, and I'd very much like to know which one we've got."

Ulyana looked back at him. In the rootlight her face was all planes and shadow, fifteen years of winter carved deep. "Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously! If it's following us, we can outrun it. If it's leading us—" He stopped. He looked ahead, down the long gentle curve of the passage, to where Vess walked with her hand trailing along the wall the way a child trails a stick along a fence.

She wasn't hesitating. That was the thing he couldn't stop noticing. Every forkless curve, every place where the braided roots split and rejoined, every stretch where the floor rose or fell — she never slowed. Never checked. She walked the way Bramble walked the back ways out of Hollowbridge at night: knowing it. Having *always* known it.

A seven-year-old child who'd arrived barefoot in the snow nine days ago, who couldn't speak a word of any tongue Bramble knew, was navigating a road that grew rather than stood, and navigating it better than he could navigate his own cellar stairs.

"She's expected," he said.

Ulyana's stride didn't break, but something in her shoulders did — a small tightening, like a rope taking weight. "What?"

"Think about it." His voice came out lower than he wanted. "Keeper, I've run goods past your gate for six years. I know what the deep court's waking looks like from the outside — lights under the ice, wards humming, folk dreaming in languages they don't own. And now we're running *from* the court's own steward, down a road nobody's mapped, and the foundling walks it like she's coming home from market." He swallowed. "We're not escaping. We're being *received*. This whole road is a hallway, and hallways lead somewhere, and somebody built it because they wanted guests."

Vess stopped walking.

She turned around — slowly, the way you turn when you already know what you'll see — and looked up at Bramble with those eyes that were too old and too calm for her face. Then she lifted her hand and pointed down the passage, deeper, toward whatever waited at the end of the growing dark.

Then she pointed at Bramble.

Then she pointed at herself, and at Ulyana, and made a gesture he couldn't parse at all except that it made his skin crawl: both hands closing slowly, like something folding shut. Like a ledger closing. Like a trap.

"She says you're right," Ulyana translated quietly. Her grandmother's primer was tucked in her coat, and she'd been reading the child's hands the way she read wardstone cracks — patiently, dreading what she'd find. "More or less. The word she keeps using is *waiting*."

"Wonderful," Bramble said. "I love waiting. I wait professionally. Waiting is my favorite thing to do in a hole in the ground with a face-stealing steward behind me and a growing road beneath me." He dragged a hand down his whiskers. "Right. New rule. Ostwick's Fourth Rule of Holes: if the hole wants you somewhere, you ask it *why* before you arrive."

"How do you propose we ask?" Ulyana said dryly.

But Vess answered. She crouched, pressed both palms flat against the root-floor, and hummed — two notes, low and high, low and high, a sound that wasn't language and wasn't music but sat in the chest like a second heartbeat. The floor shivered. Along the walls, the braided roots brightened, pulse by pulse, in time with her humming, and Bramble watched the light travel away from them down the passage like a signal sent ahead of travelers.

Like a runner going forward to announce them.

"Oh," he said faintly. "Oh, that's not good. That's a bell. She just rang the dinner bell."

"No," Ulyana said, and her voice had gone strange and soft. "Look again."

He looked. The light ran ahead of them, yes — but then it came *back*, wave after wave of it, returning up the passage faster than it had left, and where it passed, the roots changed. Brambles of thorn erupted across the walls behind them, dense and black and interlocking, sealing the road they'd come by. Ahead, the passage widened, smoothed, opened like a throat making room.

It was clearing the way forward and closing the way back.

For them. For *her*.

"It's protecting her," Ulyana whispered. "Or delivering her. With this family of magic, I'm learning those are often the same word."

* * *

They camped when the road offered a chamber — a hollow where the roots arched overhead into something almost like a vaulted ceiling, and a spring of warm water seeped from a wall and pooled clean over white sand. Bramble got a fire going from his own tinderbox, because he trusted flint and steel and nothing else in this place, and the wood he'd carried in burned sullen and orange, the only ordinary color for a mile in any direction.

Vess drank from the spring and fell asleep almost mid-swallow, the way children do, folded against Ulyana's side with her thumb near her mouth and her breathing gone deep and even.

Within a quarter hour, the walls began to answer her dreams.

Bramble had seen it once before, their first night below — flickers of light moving along the root-veins in rhythm with the child's sleep-talk, that silent pre-kingdom murmuring she did with her lips. But here, deep in the grown places, it wasn't flickers. Here the whole chamber lit and dimmed with her breathing, gold and grey, gold and grey, and shapes moved in the light: long processions of figures, tall and crowned, walking root-corridors that might have been memory or might have been prophecy. Once, unmistakably, the light formed a shape above the sleeping girl — a small figure standing on a throne of woven roots, and around the throne, kneeling, hundreds.

Hundreds of the tall ones. Kneeling. To a child who drooled in her sleep.

"Keeper," Bramble said, very quietly, "what in the nine cold hells did you bring down my mountain?"

Ulyana was watching the light-throne with an expression Bramble had only seen on gamblers watching the last card turn. "Her," she said. "I think I brought *her* down my mountain. Everything else came along on its own."

"And you trust her."

"Yes."

"You trust a child who rings bells in the dark and dreams thrones, down a road that eats its own tail, on our way to a court that buys souls by the pound?" He jabbed a finger at the glowing walls. "You saw what I saw back there. We're not fleeing, Ulyana. We're freight. Something ordered us delivered, and this road is the bill of lading, and I am *begging* you to tell me you have a plan beyond walking bravely into the parcel."

Ulyana was quiet a long moment. The fire cracked. Vess murmured in her sleep, and the walls pulsed gold, and somewhere far below — far enough that it might have been imagination, but Bramble had survived twenty years of smuggling by never trusting that particular excuse — something vast exhaled.

"I have a brother down there," she said at last. "Twenty years sold. I have a debt I mean to collect, and a seal I mean to keep, and a child I mean to see safe, and I will spend every favor, coin, and year of my life doing all three. That's the plan, Bramble. It's not a good plan. It's the only one where I can still look at myself in the morning."

He should have argued. He was, by trade and temperament, an arguer; he'd talked his way out of three hangings and one marriage. But he looked at her — at the keeper who'd rigged impossible wards and walked away from the only thing she'd ever been sure of, carrying a primer and a child and twenty years of guilt like stones in her pockets — and he heard himself say:

"Well. Good thing you owe me nothing and I owe you three favors, or I'd be worried about my motives."

"You've spent two," she said.

"I'm spending the third right now, and I'll thank you to remember it cost me my last exit." He pulled his blanket around his shoulders and settled against a root that was, at least tonight, stationary. "Ostwick's Fifth Rule of Holes, Keeper, and you'd best learn it: when you're expected, you don't fight the welcome. You change the terms of it. Whatever's waiting at the end of this road thinks it knows what it invited down here." He nodded at the sleeping child, at the throne of light dissolving slowly above her like breath off water. "It's in for a surprise."

Above them, the roots arched and gleamed, patient as a held breath.

And far ahead, down the long dark of the growing road, the passage bent — and in the bend, for just a moment before the light followed it away, Bramble saw that someone had hung lanterns.

Dozens of them. Burning green.

Waiting, at intervals, all the way down.
