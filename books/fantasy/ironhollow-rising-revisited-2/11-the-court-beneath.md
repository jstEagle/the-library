## 11. The Court Beneath

The root road ended the way a held breath ends — not with an ending at all, but with a release.

One moment Bramble was leading them through a passage so narrow that Ulyana had to turn her shoulders sideways, her palms flat against walls that were warm and faintly pulsing, like the flanks of something sleeping. The next, the passage simply opened, and she stepped out of the dark and into light that had no source she could name.

She stopped walking.

Bramble bumped into her back and swore under his breath. Vess did not stop. The child walked past Ulyana's hip with the unhurried certainty of someone coming home to a house she'd only ever seen in dreams, bare feet silent on stone that was not stone.

Ulyana had guarded Ironhollow for fifteen years. She had read every account of the deep court that smugglers would sell her and scholars would trade for her silence, and every one of those accounts had been wrong, or cowardly, or both. They described a cavern. A hall. A throne of black iron.

What lay before her was a cathedral built by roots.

They descended from a ceiling lost in shadow like the columns of some drowned temple — pale roots, thick as oaks, grown in spirals and buttresses, fused into arches that soared upward into darkness no torch could reach. Between them grew walls of woven living wood, and set into those walls, shelf after shelf after shelf, rising out of sight, were books.

Not books. Ledgers. She knew them on sight, because she had spent half her life keeping one herself: leather spines, brass corners, the particular sag of volumes written in daily and never read for pleasure. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The roots had grown around them, through them, holding them the way amber holds flies.

And the light — it came from the ledgers themselves, or from the air around them, a pale gold luminescence like lamplight through honey, pooling in the aisles, dripping from the arches. It made everything look preserved. Everything looked as if it had been kept.

*Kept,* Ulyana thought, and her stomach turned. *Everything down here is kept.*

"It's bigger than the stories," Bramble whispered. He had gone gray around the mouth. In twenty years of running Thornfell's underside, Ulyana had never once seen Bramble Ostwick afraid of a room. "The stories say it's a cave. This is a *city.*"

"This is an archive," Ulyana said quietly. "The city is somewhere else. Or there is no city anymore." She took Vess's hand. The child's fingers were cold but did not resist. "Stay close. Whatever happens, whatever anyone says — you stay where I can feel your hand."

Vess looked up at her. In the honeyed light her eyes were the color of the deep water under ice, and they were not frightened. That frightened Ulyana more than anything else she had seen since the knock at her door.

Then the nobles came.

They emerged from between the root-columns without hurry, the way frost comes across a window — gradually, and then all at once. There were perhaps twenty of them, arranged in no formation Ulyana recognized, wearing robes of dyed root-silk in colors that hurt to hold in the eye: bruise-purple, arterial red, a green like the last light before drowning.

Their faces were what stopped her breath.

They were beautiful. Every one of them. Perfectly, terribly beautiful — smooth-skinned, symmetrical, eyes like cut gemstones. And they were wrong in a way her mind kept trying to reject, the way a tongue worries a loose tooth. One woman's face was too narrow for her skull. One man's jaw belonged to someone twenty years younger than his hands. Another's features seemed assembled from three different people who had never consented to meet: a brow from here, a mouth from there, cheekbones borrowed from a third.

Bought faces. Centuries of bought faces, worn like heirloom jewelry, refitted across the generations.

"Keeper," said the one at their center, and its voice was pleasant, and it used her title the way a man uses a knife he intends to return to you later, handle first.

"I'm not keeper tonight," Ulyana said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Fifteen years of practice at sounding calm in front of things that wanted something from her. "I'm just a woman asking after family."

"Everyone who comes here," the noble said, tilting its flawless head, "is asking after family. That is rather the point of the place."

It gestured, and the aisle opened before them like a throat swallowing.

* * *

They walked. No one forced them; that was somehow worse. The nobles fell in around them at a courteous distance, gliding more than walking, and Ulyana counted her breathing the way she counted the wardstone's pulse each morning — four in, six out, four in, six out — because if she let herself think about where she was, her legs would stop working.

The archive went on forever. Aisles branched off aisles. Somewhere far above, or perhaps below, something dripped with the slow patience of water carving kingdoms. The ledgers hummed. She began to hear it after a while — not sound exactly, but pressure, a low harmonic in her teeth and behind her eyes, and she realized with a lurch that she was hearing the debts themselves. Every ledger a life. Every life still singing its balance owed.

Vess's hand tightened in hers.

"What is it?" Ulyana murmured, kneeling slightly, keeping her voice under the hum.

The child pointed with her free hand — not at a shelf, not at a noble, but at nothing, at a stretch of empty aisle ahead — and made a small sound in her throat. Then, in that old language that predated every kingdom Ulyana knew, she said something soft and certain.

Ulyana didn't need the primer to understand this time. Some phrases translate themselves straight into the blood.

*This is where I was promised.*

Bramble heard it too. He made a noise like a man swallowing a stone. "Promised to *who?*" he whispered, and no one answered him, and the nobles smiled their purchased smiles and said nothing at all.

* * *

They found him in the long gallery at the heart of the archive.

Ulyana saw him before she understood what she was seeing — a figure at a great desk of fused root-wood, writing. Just writing. The most ordinary act in the world, and it nearly brought her to her knees, because she knew the set of those shoulders. She had watched those shoulders across a kitchen table for nineteen years, hunched over sums he was bad at, over letters he wrote to a girl in Thornfell whose name Ulyana had teased him about until the day he disappeared.

He was thinner. His hair was longer, shot through with gray he shouldn't have earned yet, tied back with cord. He wore robes the color of wet slate, plain among all that splendor, the uniform of a servant. But it was the way he moved that broke something open in her chest: precise, economical, without a single wasted motion. Twenty years of practice at being a tool.

"Tam," she said.

Her voice cracked down the middle of the word. She hadn't meant to say it yet. She had planned — on the whole climbless descent, through the steward's courtesy, through the humming aisles — to plan first. To find out what was left of him before she spent her own name in this place.

But her mouth had betrayed her, the way mouths do, and the word *Tam* rolled down the long gallery and struck him like a thrown stone.

He looked up.

His face — his real face, the one face in this entire cathedral that had never been bought — turned toward her, and Ulyana felt twenty years collapse and rebuild themselves in the space of a heartbeat. He was thirty-nine years old. He had her mother's nose. He had ink on his fingers, always ink on his fingers, even now, even *here.*

He looked at her the way a man looks at a stranger who has addressed him on the road. Polite. Slightly puzzled. Already returning to his work.

"You have me mistaken," he said. His voice was level and dry and quiet, a clerk's voice, a voice that had been sanded down until nothing caught on it. "My name is Archivist. I keep the rolls of the court. If you have business with the records, state it and I will enter it."

Ulyana did not move. She was distantly aware of Bramble's hand hovering near her elbow, of Vess pressed against her side, of the nobles watching from the edges of the gallery with the patient interest of people observing weather.

"Your name is Tam Sterling," she said. "You were born in the high valley above Ironhollow, in the year the mill burned. You are afraid of deep water and you go swimming anyway every summer to spite it. You sold our mother's good spoons when you were fourteen and told her gypsies took them, and I covered for you, and you never thanked me, and I never let you forget it."

Nothing moved behind his eyes. That was the horror of it — not emptiness, which might have been peace, but *nothing moving,* a face behind glass, a lit room with no one home.

"You are mistaken," the archivist said again, gently, as one corrects a child. "I have no mother. I have no sister. I have the rolls." He lifted his pen. "Shall I enter your business?"

Something in Ulyana's chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there.

She had imagined this moment for twenty years — in the bath, on watch, in the dead hours before dawn when the gate hummed and she allowed herself the luxury of grief. In every version, Tam wept, or raged, or ran to her. In every version there was a reunion, and reunions, however painful, put things back together.

No version had prepared her for being *filed.*

"Look at me," she said, and crossed the gallery floor, and took his face in both hands — his cold, ink-stained, unbought face — and made him look at her. "I am Ulyana. Ulyana Sterling. You taught me to skip stones on the Merrow pool. You carried me two miles on your back when I broke my ankle on the Thornfell pass, and you complained the entire way, and you never once put me down. You are my brother, and I have come forty miles underground in midwinter to take you home, and you will *look at me.*"

He looked at her.

For one instant — one — something flickered behind the glass. His brow creased. His lips parted. The pen trembled in his ink-dark fingers, and somewhere in the humming dark of the archive, a ledger, one ledger, shivered on its shelf.

Then it passed, like a cloud crossing the sun, and his face smoothed back into clerk's calm, and he said, with terrible gentleness:

"That will be entered as a claim. Claims require standing. Do you have standing, mistress?"

Ulyana's hands dropped to her sides. She became aware that she was crying — silently, without drama, tears simply falling off her chin onto the archive floor, where they vanished between the root-woven stones as if the floor were drinking them. As if the court collected everything. Even this.

Especially this.

"She has standing," said a new voice, silk over gravel.

The nobles at the edge of the gallery bowed — all together, all at once, a field of wheat bending under wind. And Ulyana knew, before she turned, before she saw the tall figure descending the far stair with his face of borrowed parts and his hands folded in patience, exactly who had come to collect.

The steward. Midwinter's own.

He smiled at her with a mouth that had belonged to someone else, and behind him, in the humming dark, ten thousand ledgers sang their balances owed — and one of them, she would have sworn it, sang hers.

"Come, Keeper," the steward said. "Let us discuss your brother's account. I find that families negotiate best," and the smile widened by one perfect, purchased degree, "*when they understand the terms.*"

Behind the great desk, the archivist bent back to his work, pen scratching, and did not look up again.

But as Ulyana turned away — as she gave her arm, numbly, to the steward's courteous hand — she heard it. Faint. Almost nothing. A rhythm beneath the scratch of the pen, tapped against the desk's edge, four beats and a pause, four beats and a pause.

The rhythm she and Tam had drummed on kitchen tables their whole childhood, the secret code of two children against the world.

*I know you,* the tapping said. *Whoever I am now — I know you.*

And the chapter of Ulyana's heart that had torn open in the gallery tore again, differently, and she followed the steward into the dark with her brother's ghost-tune beating in her ears like a second pulse.
