## 12. Twenty Years of Interest

The ledgers do not sleep, and neither, strictly speaking, does Tam Sterling.

He knows this the way he knows the weight of a quill in his hand, the way he knows the smell of the archive — cold stone and old ink and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft in a cellar. He knows it without knowing *why* he knows it, because that is the shape of his life now: knowledge without origin, habit without history. He is a vessel for the court's records, and a vessel does not ask where its water came from.

He wakes — if waking is the word for the moment when the fog thins enough to let him stand — at his desk in the third gallery of the archive, cheek resting on a ledger open to an entry he does not remember writing. The candle beside him has burned down to a coin of wax. Around him the shelves rise into darkness, root-grown and endless, each one heavy with books bound in bark and vellum and, in the older cases, things he prefers not to look at too closely.

*Entry 4,411,* says the page under his cheek. *One soul, adult male, sold against winter by the bearer's own hand. Price: continued warmth for one dying woman through the turn of the season. Interest accruing.*

His hand is resting on the entry as though he had been holding it closed. As though he had been hiding it.

Tam lifts his head slowly. The archive is quiet in the way the deep places are always quiet — not silent, never silent, but hushed, the way a cathedral is hushed, full of small sounds that might be wind or might be breathing. Somewhere far off, water moves through the roots. Somewhere nearer, a page turns itself.

He should log the candle. He should trim the wick, note the hour, begin the day's copying. There are debts to record. There are always debts to record. The steward collects at midwinter, and midwinter is close — Tam can feel it in the stone, a tightening, like a fist closing around something soft.

Instead he looks at the entry again.

*Price: continued warmth for one dying woman.*

There is a splinter in that phrase. A small, bright wrongness, like a sliver of glass in bread. He reads it three times, four, and each time the splinter goes deeper, and each time something behind his eyes — some room he did not know he had — grows a degree warmer and more painful.

A woman was dying. Someone bought her warmth. Someone paid with —

With what?

The question arrives whole and terrible, and then, as all such questions do, it begins to be answered by the fog. The fog is kind, in its way. The fog smooths everything over. It tells him: *you are the archivist. You were always the archivist. You have always served.* And the pain recedes, and the splinter works itself loose, and Tam Sterling picks up his quill and logs the candle and does not think about dying women for another hour.

But the hour ends. The splinter comes back. It always comes back now.

* * *

It started — the surfacing, though he has no word for it yet — nine days ago, when the mountain shivered.

Tam had been filing. That is most of what he does: filing, copying, carrying ledgers from the record hall to the counting rooms and back. His body performs these tasks with a smoothness that should frighten him and does not, because fear requires a self to be frightened *for*, and his self was sold fair and square, entered in a book, stamped with the seal of the deep court. What remains is competence. He is very competent.

Nine days ago, mid-filing, the whole mountain had groaned. Not an earthquake — deeper than that, older. A sound like a vast throat clearing after centuries of silence. Dust sifted from the root-ceilings. In the counting rooms, the nobles stopped their murmuring and lifted their borrowed faces toward the dark, and Tam saw, for the first time in — in how long? — something like fear move across features that had been assembled from other people's fears.

And in the archive, every ledger on every shelf had hummed.

Not metaphorically. Hummed. A low, resonant tone, as if the books were struck bells, as if somewhere a tuning fork of impossible size had been touched to the world. Tam had stood among ten thousand humming records with his hands over his ears, and the sound had not come through his ears at all. It had come through the hollow place inside him, the place where a soul used to live, and it had rung him like a cracked cup.

And in that ringing, for half a heartbeat, he had remembered snow.

Just snow. Falling past a window, fat and slow, catching orange light from inside a house. A kettle on a stove. Someone laughing in the next room — a girl's laugh, quick and bright, the laugh of someone who has not yet learned what things cost.

Then the hum faded, the fog rolled back in, and Tam filed his papers.

But the snow stayed. That is the thing about the fragments: they do not leave the way they arrive. They settle. They accumulate, like interest.

* * *

He begins keeping a private ledger.

This is dangerous, and he knows it is dangerous, which is itself a strange new sensation — danger implies stakes, stakes imply a gambler. But the archivist's hands know how to make entries that do not appear in indexes, how to bind a slim book of scrap vellum and slip it behind the false spine of a tax roll no one has audited since before the steward took power. The court watches its records obsessively. It does not watch its *archivist*. A tool does not need watching.

In the slim book, in a hand that trembles only slightly, he writes down the fragments as they surface:

*A window. Snow. Orange light.*

*A name that starts with U. I lose it every time. It is short. It is warm. Saying it feels like putting my hand near a fire.*

*A debt table. Three travelers in grey-green cloaks, faces like folded paper. Their coins were warm. Why were their coins warm?*

*A medicine bottle, brown glass, bitter smell. A bed. A woman in the bed, her hair gone thin and white though she cannot be old. Her hand around mine. She says: "Tam, love, what did you do?" And I say: "Nothing, Mam. I fixed it."*

*I did not fix it.*

He stares at that last line for a long time after writing it. The ink dries. The archive breathes around him.

*I did not fix it.*

Because here is the arithmetic, surfacing now in pieces, assembling itself in the dark behind his eyes like something crawling out of a cellar: a woman was dying. Winter was coming. Medicine cost more than a ferryman's son could earn in ten years, and the travelers in grey-green cloaks had appeared at the tavern door as if they had smelled the desperation on him, and they had made an offer, and the offer had seemed so simple, so *painless*.

*Not your life,* the paper-faced one said. *Nothing so crude. We deal in finer goods. A portion of yourself. The part you'll miss least.*

And twenty-year-old Tam Sterling — frightened, loving, stupid with hope — had asked which part that was, and the traveler had smiled with a mouth that unfolded like a letter being opened, and said:

*You won't know until it's gone. That's rather the point.*

He signed. Of course he signed. The pen was warm in his hand, warmer than any pen had a right to be, and the ink sank into the page and kept sinking, down and down, as if the book drank it. And the travelers gave him a purse of warm coins, and he bought the medicine, and his mother lived through the turn of the season — lived long enough, in fact, to watch him begin to fade.

That is the part the fragment will not give him cleanly. He keeps reaching for it and finding smoke. But there are edges of it: his mother's face changing as she realized what he'd done. The way she stopped saying *what did you do* and started saying *oh, my boy, oh, my boy* in a voice like a wound. The way Ulyana —

The name almost comes. It rises in him like a bubble through deep water, growing as it climbs, and he braces for it, *wants* it, wants it the way a starving man wants bread —

And the fog takes it. Gently. The way you take a knife from a child.

Tam sits back from his hidden ledger and finds that his face is wet. He touches his cheek and examines his fingers with mild, distant interest, the way he would examine a leak in the ceiling. The archivist does not cry. The archivist has nothing to cry *with*.

But someone does. Someone with a girl's laugh, in a house with orange light, who called him by a name starting with U.

*Ulyana.*

The bubble breaks the surface. For one full second — one second out of twenty years — Tam Sterling is entirely present in his own skull, and the grief that floods in is so total, so physical, that he doubles over the desk and grips its edge and makes a sound like a hinge that has not been oiled since the world began.

Her face. Round-cheeked, freckled, scowling at him across the supper table. Fifteen years old and already acting like the keeper of everything — the household, the accounts, *him*. *You spend money we don't have, Tam. You promise things you can't deliver. One day someone's going to collect.* She'd said it as a joke. She'd laughed when she said it.

She had been right.

The second ends. The fog pours back in like cold water filling a boot print in snow. But this time — and this is new, this is terrifying, this is *hopeful* — this time the fog leaves a residue. It leaves the name sitting in him, plain as a pebble in a shoe.

Ulyana. His sister. Whom he sold himself away from, one warm coin at a time.

* * *

The trouble with surfacing, Tam discovers over the following days, is that the court notices ripples.

It begins small. A noble pauses in the archive doorway — one of the lesser ones, wearing the face of a drowned fisherman grafted onto the shoulders of a soldier — and regards him a beat too long. "Your copying has slowed, archivist."

"Has it." His voice comes out level. Some skills survive the selling of a soul; steadiness, apparently, is one of them.

"The steward counts pages as well as souls." The noble's borrowed eyes blink sideways, like a goat's. "Midwinter approaches. Be only what you are."

*Only what I am.* Tam bows, precise and empty, and the noble moves on, and Tam stands in the aisle between the shelves with his heart hammering in a chest that technically belongs to the court, thinking: *I am not only what I am. I am also what I was. And what I was had a sister, and she is coming.*

He does not know how he knows she is coming. He simply does, the way he knows the weight of a quill. The mountain groaned nine days ago. The ledgers hummed. Something old turned over in its sleep up there beneath the frostline, and whatever it was, it wears his sister's frown.

Or perhaps that is only the fog, teasing him. Perhaps hope is just another form of interest, compounding daily against a principal he can never repay.

Either way, he returns to his hidden ledger that night and adds a line:

*If she comes — and she will, because she is Ulyana and she has never once left a debt unpaid, including the ones owed to fools like me — then I must be worth finding. I must remember enough to help her. The steward collects at midwinter. Whatever bargain brought her down here, it will end at midwinter, one way or another.*

*So: remember. Quickly. Before the fog remembers first.*

He turns to the original entry — Entry 4,411, the one he had been sleeping on, the one his hands had been hiding — and forces himself to read it to the bottom for the first time. Below the price, below the terms, below the accrual schedule that has been quietly devouring him for two decades, there is a clause written in the fine, cramped script the court uses for things it does not want read aloud:

*Bearers of voided or discharged obligations retain residual claim to memory of consideration given. In event of ledger irregularity, audit may be demanded by the debtor or his blood.*

*By the debtor or his blood.*

Tam reads it five times. His hands, when he looks at them, are shaking — actually shaking, alive, *his*.

An audit. A demand. Blood.

Somewhere above him, past miles of root and stone and frozen earth, the snow is falling past a window, and a woman with a girl's laugh is packing a bag, or arguing with a smuggler, or holding a mute child's hand in the dark — and she is coming down the Thornfell roads with his name in her mouth like a blade between her teeth.

And the ledger that never closes has just told him, in its own cramped and ancient hand, exactly where its seams are.

Tam closes the false spine over his hidden book, trims his candle, and sits in the dark of the third gallery, listening to the mountain breathe, waiting for the sound of footsteps that will mean his sister has finally come to collect.

Far above, faint as a heartbeat through the stone, the wards of Ironhollow gate begin to sing.
