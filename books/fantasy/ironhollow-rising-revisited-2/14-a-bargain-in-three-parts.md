## 14. A Bargain in Three Parts

The steward's hall had no ceiling that Ulyana could find. She looked up twice and twice her eyes found only root-work braiding into darkness, thick as cathedral pillars, hung with lanterns that burned a color she had no name for — a pale gold, almost the gold of lamplight, but colder, the way snow is cold even when it glows. The light did not flicker. Nothing down here flickered. Everything held its breath.

She stood at the center of a floor of polished black stone — not stone, she corrected herself; wood, grown smooth as glass by centuries of feet that no longer existed — and she made herself stand still while the court assembled around her in silence.

They came out of the dark the way frost comes across a window: gradually, then all at once. Nobles of the deep folk, or what had been nobles once. They wore faces the way other people wore coats, and some of them wore several, layered, shifting at the edges like reflections in disturbed water. One had Ulyana's own jawline. Another had her mother's hands. She kept her eyes on the middle distance and counted her breaths, because if she looked too long at any single face she would start cataloguing which pieces belonged to whom, and she was afraid of what she'd recognize.

Vess stood pressed against her side, small and warm and trembling. Above them, somewhere in the dark rafters of roots, Bramble was supposed to be finding another way out. Below them, behind a desk of living wood in the hall's far corner, Tam bent over his ledgers with his head down, writing, always writing, his pen scratching like an insect against the silence.

He still hadn't looked at her.

*Good*, she told herself. *Better this way. You can't bargain well when your hands are shaking.*

The steward came forward alone.

He had chosen a face for tonight that was almost handsome — a broad, calm face with grey eyes and a mouth built for patient smiles — but the seams showed where the light caught it, fine hairline joins at the temples and along the throat, like porcelain that had been broken and mended by someone in a hurry. He stopped three paces from her, which was closer than courtesy required and farther than trust allowed, and he inclined his head with the exact degree of respect one shows an equal.

"Keeper Sterling," he said. His voice was layered too, faintly, two tones speaking in unison a half-beat apart. "You've come a long way down."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know what you *want*," he said. "Wanting and knowing are different ledgers. Shall we open yours?"

He gestured, and a table rose from the black floor between them — grown, not built, its grain swirling in patterns that hurt to follow. Two chairs. He sat. After a moment's calculation, she sat too, drawing Vess onto her lap despite the child's age, despite the watching court. Vess went willingly, hiding her face in Ulyana's shoulder, and Ulyana felt the child's heartbeat hammering quick and small against her own ribs, and thought: *whatever this costs, it isn't going to be enough, so make sure you get everything.*

"The terms," Ulyana said. "Three parts. I'll say them once, plainly, so there's no room for interpretation later. I know how your kind loves interpretation."

A ripple went through the assembled faces. The steward's smile deepened by a fraction.

"First," she said. "Tam Sterling. His soul comes back to him. Whole. Not the scrap of it you keep on a shelf to make him compliant — all of it, every year of interest, every piece you've been feeding on. He walks out of here a man, not an archivist."

"Second. Vess goes free. No claim, no debt, no inheritance enforced, no throne waiting for her whether she wants it or not. She leaves this mountain when she chooses, and nothing under it follows her. Ever."

"Third." Her mouth had gone dry. She made herself finish. "The gate at Ironhollow gets sealed from beneath. Properly. Permanently. And I am the one who seals it — my oath, my keeping, transferred below. I stay. I hold the gate from your side until the mountain itself rots, if that's what it takes. That's the price. Those are the three parts."

Silence. The lantern-gold light lay on the table between them like something spilled.

She had rehearsed it on the road, in Bramble's grumbling company, in the dark of the root roads with Vess asleep against her chest. She had rehearsed it until the words wore smooth. But saying them aloud, in this place, before this audience, made them real in a way rehearsal never could. Somewhere in the last hour she had crossed from *considering* to *committed*, and now the commitment sat in her chest like swallowed stone.

The steward laced his borrowed fingers together and regarded her over them.

"An interesting offer," he said. "Generous, even. A keeper's oath is old magic, Keeper Sterling. Older than your kingdom, older than my court's ascension. It anchors. It binds. Do you understand what you're offering?"

"I've kept that gate for fifteen years. I've bled on those wards. I've said the words every dawn and every dusk until they're written into me deeper than my name. I know exactly what I'm offering."

"Do you?" He tilted his head, and the seam at his throat caught the light. "Your grandmother held the post before you. And hers before. Your family has fed that gate for four generations. An oath like yours isn't a rope, Keeper. It's a root. Pulling it up will leave a hole in you shaped like everything you've ever been."

*You have no idea,* she thought, *how many holes I already have.* Twenty years since Tam vanished. Fifteen since she'd stopped expecting anyone to come back down the smuggler roads. She had made a life out of holes and called it duty.

"Then it should be worth a great deal to you," she said aloud.

"It is worth a great deal to me." Something moved behind the steward's grey eyes — hunger, maybe, or satisfaction, or both wearing each other's faces. "But let us speak of the second term. The girl." He turned his attention to Vess, and Ulyana felt the child flinch like a struck bell. "Do you know what she is, Keeper? Truly?"

"She's a child who walked barefoot through a snowstorm to find me. Whatever else she is can wait."

"She is the last true heir of the deep throne." The steward said it flatly, without drama, the way a man reads a figure off a ledger. "Hidden above by loyalists before the usurpation — before *me*. Insurance against exactly this day. Every noble in this hall has spent centuries positioning for a succession that couldn't happen without her. And you propose to walk her out of here as though she were a stray cat you'd taken in for the winter."

"That's the term."

"And if she doesn't wish to go?"

Ulyana looked down. Vess had lifted her face from Ulyana's shoulder. Her eyes — dark, ancient-dark, older than any seven-year-old's eyes had a right to be — met the steward's without blinking.

"I go with her," Vess said.

It was the longest sentence Ulyana had heard from her since the gate. The pre-kingdom tongue had given way, sometime on the root roads, to halting common speech — as though proximity to the court was unlocking her, or as though she'd simply decided the time for hiding was done. Her voice was small and clear and it carried further than it should have.

The court stirred. Faces rippled. Somewhere above, a lantern guttered — the first thing Ulyana had seen flicker since she came below.

"She stays with me," Ulyana said, "until she's grown. Then she chooses. That's the term, and it's not negotiable."

The steward studied Vess for a long moment. Then, to Ulyana's surprise, he inclined his head again — deeper this time.

"Accepted."

*Too fast.* The word arrived in her mind with the cold clarity of a wardstone cracking. *That's too fast.*

"Third term," the steward continued smoothly. "Tam Sterling. His soul returned entire. Yes. We can do this. Though I confess—" and here the patient smile widened just slightly, showing teeth that were slightly too regular, "—I'm curious. Twenty years of interest on a soul-debt is considerable. Most men would be a puddle by now. Your brother is... durable. Sentimental value, perhaps. Family resemblance."

Ulyana didn't answer. She wouldn't give him her grief to wear.

"So," the steward said, rising. The table sank back into the floor. "All three terms accepted. The archivist's soul restored. The heir freed, with guardianship granted to the keeper until her majority. The gate sealed from beneath by your transferred oath, binding you to this court and these halls forever." He extended his hand. "Shall we formalize?"

Every instinct she had honed in fifteen years of dealing with smugglers, liars, and desperate men screamed at her. *Nobody accepts that fast. Nobody accepts all three terms without haggling. He wants something more than he's showing, and you're about to hand it to him.*

But she had known that coming down. She had weighed it on the road, sleepless beside Bramble's dying fire. Whatever the steward's hidden design was, the alternatives were worse: Vess claimed by the court, Tam hollowed forever, the gate failing season by season until something poured through it into the world above. She had run the sums a hundred times. There was no version of this ledger where everyone walked away whole. Someone had to be the payment.

She had simply always assumed it would be her.

"One condition of my own," she said, standing without taking his hand. "I say the transfer words myself. My grandmother's phrasing, from the old primer. Not your scribes' version. If I'm binding myself to this place, I'll use words I trust."

Something flickered across the steward's mended face — annoyance, quickly smoothed. "Sentimentality. Very well. Tomorrow at midwinter's turning. The court will witness."

"Tonight," Ulyana said. "Before I sleep in whatever hole you put me in. Before anything else happens. I don't sign contracts that sit overnight in a house full of lawyers."

The smile again, wider. "Tomorrow, Keeper. Some things require preparation. The oath-transfer is not a candle you light on a whim. Unless—" the grey eyes glittered, "—you'd prefer to reconsider the timing entirely?"

And there it was. The hook inside the offer. Refuse the delay and look afraid; accept it and spend a night in the dark with a court that wanted her heir and her brother and her oath, with all night to change the terms.

"Tomorrow," she said evenly. "At midwinter's turning. But Vess stays with me until it's done. And Tam—" she raised her voice, letting it carry to the corner desk, to the bowed head and the scratching pen, "—Tam sits with us tonight. All three terms witnessed, or no terms at all."

For a moment the steward said nothing. The lantern-light lay cold on his seamed face, and Ulyana had the sudden, vertiginous sense of looking at something very old wearing a very new mask, and of having just agreed to hand it the key to everything she loved.

"Arranged," he said at last, softly. "The court provides. Rest well, Keeper Sterling. Tomorrow, we conclude our business — all of it."

He turned and the court parted for him, faces folding back into the dark like water closing over a stone. Servants — things that might once have been servants — appeared to guide them to guest chambers grown into the root-walls, warm-lit and soft-floored and unmistakably cells.

Tam came when called. He walked to her slowly, pen still in hand, ink on his fingers, and he stood before her with the polite blankness of a man waiting for his next instruction, and Ulyana's heart broke all over again, quietly, in a place where nobody could see.

"Hello, Tam," she whispered.

He frowned faintly, as if the sound of her voice had stirred dust somewhere far back in his skull. "Do I..." he began, and stopped, and shook his head, and the frown smoothed away. "The steward says I'm to attend you tonight. Where do you want me?"

*Here*, she almost said. *Twenty years ago. At Mother's bedside. Anywhere but this.*

"Just sit with us," she said instead. "That's all. Just sit."

They settled in the guest chamber — Vess curled against her side, Tam cross-legged at the foot of the bed like a servant awaiting orders, the root-wall glowing faintly around them. Ulyana lay awake long after the others slept, staring at the ceiling of woven dark, running the bargain over and over in her mind like a tongue probing a cracked tooth.

*He accepted too readily.* Three terms, no haggle. A keeper's oath — the anchor of the gate, the oldest binding she knew of — handed over with barely a pause. And tomorrow at midwinter's turning, at the precise moment the seal above was weakest, she would speak her grandmother's words and pour fifteen years of keeping into the court's hands.

Unless.

She sat up slowly. Across the chamber, Tam watched her with those half-remembering eyes, and Vess breathed small and steady against her hip, and far above them both, through half a mile of frozen rock, the Ironhollow gate hummed its failing song into the winter night.

*My grandmother's phrasing,* she had demanded. Words she trusted. Words from the primer — the same primer that held, in its final pages, in her grandmother's cramped hand, a variant of the transfer rite. A variant the court's scribes had never seen.

She didn't yet know what it did. But tomorrow, when she opened her mouth to bind herself forever, she intended to find out.

Above, in the dark of the rafters, something shifted — a soft scrape of boot on root, quickly stilled. Bramble, keeping his watch. Or something else, keeping its own.

Ulyana closed her eyes and began, silently, to memorize.
