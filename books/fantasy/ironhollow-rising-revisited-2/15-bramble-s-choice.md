## 15. Bramble's Choice

Bramble Ostwick had spent thirty years learning every way a man could owe something, and he had learned them all the hard way, which was the only way worth learning, in his opinion. He owed Ulyana Sterling three favors — two of them large, one of them the kind you didn't speak about in daylight. He owed the Hollow Inn a week's lodging he'd never paid because the innkeeper had stopped keeping accounts around the same time the innkeeper stopped blinking right. He owed a fence in Lowmark for a horse that had died on him, and a widow in Thornfell for bread she'd given his father during the famine winter, though she'd been dead twenty years and her grandchildren didn't know Bramble from a stranger.

And now, it turned out, he owed the court beneath the mountain.

He hadn't known that until the steward's clerk unrolled the page in front of him with hands too long and too many-jointed, and read out his name in a voice like dry paper tearing.

"Bramble Ostwick," the clerk said. "Son of Marta Ostwick. Debts outstanding: one." The clerk smiled, or did something with its face that the word *smile* would have to stretch very far to cover. "One debt. Do you know what it is?"

Bramble knew. He'd known since he was nine years old, though he'd spent thirty years telling himself he didn't.

* * *

His mother had been a smuggler before him, and her mother before that, back until the family name was just a word for people who knew the mountain's back doors. And when Bramble was nine, the winter had come down hard — the kind of hard that killed cattle in their stalls and old men in their beds — and his mother had gone down into the root roads with a sack of silver and come back up with medicine for his baby sister, who was coughing herself blue.

She'd come back wrong, though. That was the part nobody talked about. She came back with the medicine and the sister lived, but his mother sat by the fire for three days without speaking, staring at her own hands like they belonged to somebody else, and on the fourth day she'd taken Bramble aside and said, "Boy, if anyone ever comes asking after our name, you run. You don't ask what they want. You don't count what you're carrying. You run."

Then she'd burned a page in the fire — he'd only ever seen the corner of it, a corner with writing that moved when you looked at it — and she'd screamed, and after the screaming she'd been quiet and light, like something had been lifted off her, and she'd also been less. Less than she'd been. He could never say how. Just less.

She died the next spring, ordinary as anything, a fever, nothing strange. But Bramble had grown up knowing that somewhere under the mountain there was a page with his family's name on it, and that pages like that didn't burn themselves.

* * *

Now here was the page, whole and unburned, unrolled on a table of black root-wood in a chamber somewhere below the Hollow Inn, and the steward standing over it with his borrowed face arranged into an expression of paternal kindness.

"You've been useful," the steward said. "I want to be clear about that. The keeper trusts you. The child travels easier when you carry her. These are not small things." He tapped the ledger-page once with one long finger. "This is everything you owe. Everything your family has ever owed. One page. I can burn it for you, Bramble Ostwick. Clean. Painless. Your mother's debt, your debts, all of it — ash. You walk out of this mountain owing no one anything, ever again."

Bramble looked at the page. It was written in ink that moved, and beneath his name were lines he couldn't read but could feel, the way you feel weather coming in a bad knee.

"And what do I do?" he asked, because he already knew, and some stubborn smuggler's instinct made him want to hear it said aloud.

"You tell me where they'll run," the steward said pleasantly. "The keeper will take the child through the old toll-road when the court turns on itself — oh, yes, it will turn, we are a court, turning is what courts do. You know the roads better than any living soul. Tell me which one, and when, and this page burns tonight."

The chamber was warm. That was the worst part, Bramble thought. It should have been cold, a place like this, cold as a grave, but instead it was warm as a kitchen, warm as a lie told by someone who loves you.

He thought about Ulyana Sterling, who had torn up his smuggling fines twice and never once mentioned it. Who had stood at her gate for fifteen years doing a job nobody thanked her for, alone, so that the rest of the world could go on not believing in fairy stories. Who had looked at Vess — a mute child who spoke in dreams — and decided, without hesitation, without arithmetic, that the girl was hers to protect.

He thought about his mother, sitting light and less by the fire.

Three favors. Two large. One unspeakable. And here was the steward offering to wipe clean every other debt he'd ever carried, if he'd just add one more — the kind of debt that doesn't sit on a ledger but sits on a soul.

"No," Bramble said.

The steward tilted his head, the borrowed face rearranging itself into curiosity. "No?"

"I'm a smuggler, m'lord. Thirty years. You know what my whole trade is? Knowing what things cost." He nodded at the page. "That price is wrong. Any road cheap enough to buy with a man's whole family is a road that eats the buyer too. I've seen your ledgers work. My mother showed me." His voice cracked, and he hated it, and he kept going anyway. "You're not offering to erase my debt. You're offering to move it. Onto her. Onto the child. And I'd sooner owe you till the sun burns out."

For a moment — just a moment — the warmth in the room went out, like a hand cupped over a candle. The steward's face held still, all its borrowed features holding very carefully still, the way water holds still before it goes over the edge.

"A pity," the steward said. "Your mother chose better."

"My mother chose exactly the same," Bramble said. "That's where I got the idea."

* * *

They didn't throw him in a cell. That wasn't how the court worked; cells were for people who might still be useful later. They simply put him back in the corridor outside the chamber, and the corridor was dark, and the page was gone from the table, and Bramble understood with a cold clarity that the offer had been the mercy. What came after the refusal would not be offered politely.

But he'd spent thirty years in the mountain's pockets, and there was one thing the court had never understood about smugglers, because the court thought in ledgers and smugglers thought in exits: nothing in the deep roads stays where you put it. Not goods. Not people. Not pages.

He'd seen the clerk's satchel. He'd seen where the loose records went — a niche in the third gallery, where the root-wood grew soft and paper-things were filed between the living wood like leaves pressed in a book. He'd been carrying contraband through these passages since before the steward's current face was born, probably. The court kept its records close, but close wasn't the same as safe.

Getting to the niche took him four hours, two lies to a thing wearing a guard's shape, and one moment flat against the root-wall while something vast and patient breathed past in the dark — something that smelled of cold iron and old snow, something that paused beside him for the length of ten heartbeats before moving on. Bramble counted those heartbeats and thought about his mother's hands and did not move.

The niche was there. The pages were there, dozens of them, slotted into the soft wood like letters into a dead man's coat. He found his by feel — the ink moved under his fingertips, restless, alive, and when he pulled it free the whole gallery shivered, roots groaning somewhere far above like a ship in a storm.

He had no fire down here. Fire was death in the deep roads; everyone knew that; the air ate flame and gave back worse. But his mother hadn't used a torch either. He remembered now, properly remembered, kneeling at her knee at nine years old: she'd held the page to her chest, and she'd said a word — a word in the old tongue, the deep folk's tongue, the one the child Vess spoke in her sleep — and the page had burned itself, from the inside, with a light like the last coal of a dying fire.

He didn't know the word. But he'd heard it. Thirty years ago, once, through a wall of sleep and grief, and it had lodged in him the way such things lodge.

He held the page to his chest. He said the word as best a smuggler's tongue could shape it — rough, half-wrong, more breath than sound.

The page caught.

It didn't burn like paper. It burned like a wound. The pain went through his chest and out his back and down both arms, and Bramble Ostwick dropped to his knees in the dark gallery and made a sound he would deny to his dying day, a high animal sound, because the debt was leaving him and it was taking its toll on the way out, tearing free of whatever deep place it had rooted in a nine-year-old boy's fear and a grown man's denial. The light of the burning page lit the niche red, and the roots around him writhed away from it, and somewhere far off something began to scream — not a person, he was almost sure, not anymore — and the page curled and blackened and went to ash in his fists.

And then it was done, and he was on his knees in the dark, breathing like a bellows, lighter and less, lighter and less, just like his mother by the fire.

"Sorry, Mum," he whispered. "Should've asked what the word meant first."

* * *

He found them an hour later, or they found him — Ulyana's blade at his throat before he could speak, which was fair, given where they were — and when she saw his face she lowered it slowly.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Felt worse," Bramble lied. "Twice."

Vess was watching him with those old, old eyes, and she reached up and touched his hand, and where her fingers landed the ache went quiet — not gone, but quiet, like a storm moved offshore. Then she looked at him harder, and her eyes went wide, and she tugged Ulyana's sleeve and pointed at Bramble's chest and made a rapid series of gestures: fire, page, ash, and then — urgently, twice — *run.*

"She says they know," Ulyana translated grimly. "The burning rang through their ledgers like a bell. Every debt in this mountain felt it." Her jaw tightened. "Bramble. What did you do?"

"What I should've done thirty years ago." He pushed himself upright. His knees shook. His chest felt hollowed and new, like a house after the furniture's been sold. "M'lady — Keeper — we need to move. Now. Because I know how much noise that made, and I know what a court does when it hears a bell." He managed something like his old grin, thin as it was. "It comes to collect."

Ulyana was already moving, pulling Vess close, checking the dark tunnels ahead. "Which way?"

"The root roads fork ahead — the fast way and the deep way. They'll watch the fast way." He spat, tasting ash. "So we go deep. And Keeper — when we get wherever we're going, and whatever happens down there—" He hesitated, thirty years of deflection rising in his throat, and swallowed it. "Thank you. For the favors. All three. Consider us square."

"We're not square," Ulyana said. "You still owe me one."

Bramble laughed — a real laugh, rusty and surprised out of him — and followed her into the dark.

Behind them, far below, the mountain began, very softly, to ring.
