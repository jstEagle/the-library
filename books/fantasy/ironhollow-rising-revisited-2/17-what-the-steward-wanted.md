## 17. What the Steward Wanted

The fire in the record hall should have been louder.

Ulyana had imagined it a hundred times since Bramble came sprinting up the root road with soot on his knuckles and something like triumph cracking his face open — imagined a roar, a collapse, the whole cathedral of ledgers coming down like a felled tree. Instead the sound that reached the throne chamber was a low, wet crackle, distant and almost polite, as though the mountain itself were clearing its throat before speaking.

She stood at the base of the dais with Vess pressed against her side and Tam — half-himself, ash-streaked, breathing hard — one step behind her, and she watched the steward's face do something no borrowed face should have been able to do.

He smiled.

Not the courteous, terrible smile he had worn on the road when he offered her safe passage for the child. Not the patient smile of a creditor watching interest accrue. This was older and uglier, the smile of a man whose trap had finally closed on the right animal.

"Ah," the steward said softly. "There it is."

"There *what* is?" Ulyana's voice came out steadier than she felt. Her hand was on Vess's shoulder, and beneath her palm the child was trembling — not with fear, Ulyana realized, but with recognition. Vess knew what was coming. Some part of her had always known.

"The sound," the steward said, "of my property burning."

Around the edges of the chamber, the court's nobles stirred. They were fewer than they had been an hour ago; the fire was already eating its way through the record hall, and every ledger-page that curled and blackened took something out of the room. One noble near the far pillar flickered like a candle in wind, her bought face sliding for a heartbeat into something raw and unfinished before settling again. Another simply wasn't there anymore — only a drift of grey dust where he'd stood, and a ledger binding falling open on the floor.

But the steward did not move toward the fire. He did not send anyone. He stood on his dais of woven roots with his assembled face — a jaw taken from one century, eyes from another, a mouth that might have belonged to a king or a beggar or both — and he let it burn.

"You're letting it burn," Ulyana said. She heard her own confusion sharpen into dread. "Your records. Your power."

"My records." The steward descended one step. His boots made no sound on the roots. "Keeper Sterling. Do you know what a ledger actually is? Not the paper. Not the ink. What is it, fundamentally?"

"A debt," Tam said behind her, hoarse. "Written down so it can be held."

"So it can be *carried*." The steward's stolen eyes found him, and there was something almost fond in them, which was worse than cruelty. "Yes. Your sister's brother. Half-returned and already lecturing. A debt written down can be carried anywhere. Sold, traded, inherited, forgiven — all of it theater, all of it *paper*. But the debt itself—" he raised one long finger, "—the debt lives in the one who owes it. Burning my hall burns my *copies*, Keeper. It does not burn what I hold."

Ulyana's stomach turned over slowly, like a stone rolling downhill.

"The souls," she said.

"The souls. The oaths. Every promise ever made in this court's hearing, still living in the chests of the people who made them." He smiled again, and this time she saw the teeth under the courtesy. "Including yours."

* * *

They had come to the throne chamber because there was nowhere else to go. That was the truth of it, ugly and simple. The record hall burned behind them, the root roads had sealed themselves — Vess had touched the living wood and whispered, and the passages had closed like wounds — and the only way out of the deep court ran through the gate above, which was sealed, which had always been sealed, which could only be opened by—

By a keeper.

Ulyana understood it then, all of it at once, the way you understand a fall only once you've already left the ledge. She thought of the steward finding them on the road, too easily, too precisely. She thought of the root roads opening for Vess like a house opening for its heir. She thought of the bargain she had struck three days ago in this same chamber, laying her keeper's oath on the root-table like a card she believed was trump: *Tam's soul returned, the child freed, the gate resealed — and I stay below, bound to the court, forever.*

And she remembered how readily he had accepted.

Too readily. She had told herself it was greed, that the court hungered for a keeper the way it hungered for everything, that she had simply driven a hard enough bargain that even a steward would snatch at it. She had walked away from that table feeling like a woman who had paid a fair price in the only coin she had left.

"You never wanted Vess crowned," Ulyana said.

The nobles nearest the dais went very still. Even the fire seemed to lean closer through the stone.

"Oh, I wanted her crowned," the steward said mildly. "Crowned, and small, and mine. A true heir on the deep throne is a fine thing, Keeper — legitimacy is a kind of currency too, and I am nothing if not a collector. But no." He stepped down again, closer now, close enough that she could see the seams in his face, faint as hairline cracks in wardstone. "The child was insurance. Hers." He nodded, very slightly, toward the shadows at the edge of the chamber, where the old queen's faction had stood before the fire scattered them. "Hidden above ground, beyond my reach, growing up ignorant and safe. A blade kept in a scabbard for twenty years. And then—" his stolen eyes glittered, "—she walks herself down to me. Barefoot. Through snow. Carrying a soul in her chest like a lantern. Do you know how long I have waited for the mountain to make a mistake?"

Vess made a small sound against Ulyana's hip. Not words. Something older.

"And the oath," Ulyana said. Her mouth had gone dry. "My oath. You didn't take it to keep me here."

"I took it because your oath is not a promise, Keeper. It is an *anchor*." The steward spread his hands, and for a moment he dropped the courtesy entirely, and what was underneath was vast and cold and patient as pressure in a mineshaft. "Fifteen years you have sat above that gate, feeding it your word. Every dawn. Every audit. Every small faithful act of keeping. The gate's magic does not live in the stone — stone is stupid, stone forgets. It lives in *you*. In the shape of your keeping. You are the keel the gate balances on, and last week, you handed me the tiller."

The chamber tilted. Or she did. Tam caught her elbow, his grip sudden and human and real, and she clung to it without shame.

"The seal," she said. "You can unmake it."

"I can *carry* it." The steward's voice softened into something almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing yet. "An oath freely given cannot be forced — old law, deeper than this court, older than me. But an oath freely *given away*..." He let the sentence hang. "You gave it, Ulyana Sterling. Willingly. Knowingly. To save your brother and the child. There is no law in root or stone or kingdom that can call that back. You did it yourself. That is the beauty of it. That is why I let you walk every step of the road here, why I let the child lead you through doors I could have opened anyway. I could not take your oath. I could only wait for you to *offer* it. And you are the sort of woman who offers."

* * *

The fire reached some deeper gallery while he spoke. A shudder went through the floor, and somewhere far off, a shelf of centuries collapsed. Two more nobles came apart quietly, like wet paper. The remaining ones had begun backing toward the walls, and Ulyana saw it in their flickering faces — the dawning arithmetic of creatures made entirely of borrowed time, watching their collateral burn.

The steward saw it too. He ignored it.

"Here is what happens next," he said. "Tonight, while the old blood scrambles to salvage their ledgers, I will walk up the Thornfell road wearing your oath like a key. The gate will know me — the gate knows nothing but you, and I will be carrying you. And behind me will come every soldier, every creditor, every hollow-eyed thing this court has bought in four hundred years, because the long winter is ending, Keeper, and a court that feeds on debts cannot survive a thaw unless it finds new debtors." He paused. "Yours is a rich world up there. Full of people who borrow. They always borrow. It's the one thing the small folk never learn."

"No," Ulyana said.

It came out flat and small and useless, and she knew it, and the steward inclined his assembled head as though she had made a fine point in a debate.

"'No' is not a currency here," he said. "You of all people should understand. You've been trading in it for fifteen years — favors, tolls, small kindnesses banked against need. You built a little economy of debts above my gate and called it keeping the peace. Don't look shocked. Who do you think taught the smugglers the roads? Who do you think let Bramble Ostwick run his first cargo past your window, hmm? A gate that opens sometimes is worth ten gates that never open. I have been cultivating you since before you took the post, Keeper. Your grandmother knew. Why do you think the primer survived?"

The floor dropped out of the world.

Her grandmother's primer. The forbidden book, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a cedar chest, the book she had been told never to read and had read anyway, the book that had taught her just enough of the old tongue to hear Vess say *the debt comes due*. She had thought it contraband. She had thought it dangerous. She had never once thought it was *bait* — a lure set fifteen years deep, waiting for a keeper curious enough, desperate enough, to pick up the thread.

Every choice she had made since the child knocked on her door. Every step down. The favors called in, the ring traded, the oath laid on the table. She had thought she was descending into the dark.

She had been walking down a throat.

* * *

"You're lying," Tam said.

His voice cracked on it. He stepped forward, ash still on his sleeves, and Ulyana saw what the first payment of her bargain had bought him — not the man he'd been, but the outline of him, a man-shaped space filling slowly with light. His hands shook. His eyes didn't.

"You needed the archivist," Tam said. "That's why you restored me halfway. Someone had to start the fire. Someone had to void the pages so the old blood would scatter and leave you alone with her." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, grief wearing humor like a mask. "I forged the entries myself. I handed you the empty room."

"I did," the steward agreed pleasantly. "And I thank you for it. You were always the best of the Sterlings at paperwork."

Ulyana moved before she decided to move. She got a fistful of the steward's collar — silk gone stiff with age, or with something older than age — and dragged him down toward her level, and for one instant his borrowed face was inches from hers and she looked into eyes that had been someone's mother's, someone's enemy's, someone's, someone's, someone's.

"Then take it back," she said. "Unmake the transfer. Give me my oath."

"I can't." For the first time, something moved behind the stolen eyes that was not patience. It might have been hunger. It might have been fear. "That is the law, Keeper. The same law that protects you protected me. Freely given, freely held. The oath is mine until the thing it's written *on* is destroyed." His gaze slid, deliberately, toward the burning galleries. "And you burned my copy."

Hope flared in her chest, treacherous and bright.

"But the oath isn't written on paper," the steward continued, and the hope died the way a match dies. "Paper is only the receipt. The oath is written in the gate itself — anchored in the wardstone you've polished for fifteen years, in the crack you found last winter and told no one about." He watched her flinch, and savored it. "Yes. I know about the crack. I put it there. A seed, planted the day you took the post. All these years it has been growing toward your anchor, and tonight, when I stand beneath the gate wearing your word, it will finish the work. The seal won't break, Keeper. It will *turn around*. Open for me and mine, and close behind us on a world that will never know winter ended for any reason but our coming."

He straightened his collar out of her fist with two fingers, unhurried, restoring the courtesy like a garment.

"You gave me a weapon," he said. "You forged it yourself, over fifteen years of faithful service, and you handed it to me hilt-first because you loved your brother. I want you to carry that up the mountain with me. I want it to be the last thing you think about, for a very long time."

* * *

The chamber was nearly empty now. The old blood had fled to whatever holes remained to them, taking their flickering faces and their failing claims. The fire roared properly at last, eating the deep galleries, and the heat of it reached the throne room like breath.

Ulyana stood very still.

Later — in the years after, in the telling and retelling — she would try to describe what happened inside her in that moment, and she would never quite manage it. It was not despair. Despair is a soft thing, a surrendering thing. This was harder. It was the specific, crystalline fury of a woman who has audited her own ruin line by line and found the arithmetic correct. He was right. Every entry balanced. She had done it all herself, freely, knowingly, lovingly — and that was exactly the shape of the trap, because the court beneath the mountain had never once in four hundred years managed to *take* anything. It could only ever wait to be *given*.

Fine, she thought. Then we stop giving.

Beside her, Vess had stopped trembling.

The child stood with her bare feet on the root-floor, her small hands open at her sides, and she was looking at the steward with an expression Ulyana had never seen on a seven-year-old's face — ancient, level, and terribly awake. The wards of the deep court, the ones woven into the living rock older than the ledgers, older than the court itself, began to hum.

The steward heard it. For the first time, he turned to look at the child instead of the woman.

"That tongue is dead," he said.

Vess opened her mouth.

Above them, through a mile of stone and ice and fifteen years of faithful keeping, the mountain began to answer.
