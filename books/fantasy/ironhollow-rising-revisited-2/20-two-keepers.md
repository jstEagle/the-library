## 20. Two Keepers

The mountain was quieter than it had ever been.

Ulyana felt it in her teeth first — that absence of pressure, like a hand lifting from the back of her neck after twenty years of pressing. The deep roads no longer hummed with borrowed voices. The ledgers were ash. And in the great root-cathedral where a court had fed on debts for centuries beyond counting, there was only the sound of water moving through old wood, patient as anything that had learned to wait out an age.

She found Tam in the record hall.

What remained of it, anyway. Half the shelves had burned before Vess's unmaking finished the work, and the rest stood scorched and empty, their bindings crumbled to black flakes. Tam had swept a corner clean. He'd built a small desk from a fallen shelf-plank and set it on two stones, and he was sitting at it with a stub of charcoal, writing — not in the court's ledger-script, not in the tight devouring hand she'd seen him use as archivist, but in their mother's alphabet, the one she'd taught them both on winter evenings with flour dusted on the table so they could practice letters they'd wipe away and write again.

He looked up when her boots scraped the ash.

"Ulyana." He said it carefully, the way a man says a word he's relearning. His face was still wrong in places — too smooth along the jaw, as if some of the years hadn't come back when his soul did. But his eyes were his own. They were their mother's eyes, gray as thaw-water, and they knew her.

"You're writing," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"Inventories." He turned the page toward her. *One lamp, whole. One lamp, cracked. Three hundred and eleven souls, freed. Two hundred and six departed upward through the gate. One hundred and five remaining below.* "Some of them can't leave yet," he said quietly. "The deep folk who stayed loyal to the mountain instead of the steward. The ones who were born down here and have nowhere else. Someone has to count them. Someone has to make sure they eat this spring, now that the court isn't... feeding them."

"Feeding on them," Ulyana corrected.

"That too." He almost smiled. It was a strange thing to watch, that smile — like watching frost melt off a window and finding glass beneath. "I'm not asking you to stay. I know what's above you. Spring. A child who calls you sister whether you've agreed to it or not."

"She hasn't called me that yet."

"She will." He set down the charcoal. "Tam Sterling knows things. It's good to be him again."

* * *

They walked the lower gate together that afternoon.

It was nothing like the gate at Ironhollow — smaller, older, a seam in the living rock where the mountain's roots met the world's deep water. Before the unmaking, it had been barred with debt-magic, sealed by oaths and interest and the weight of every soul the court held. Now it was just stone, and cold air breathing through it, and a scatter of pale lichen that Vess said was the mountain's own writing, older than any kingdom, older than any bargain.

"The seal is failing here too," Ulyana said, running her fingers along the seam. She couldn't help herself; fifteen years of keeper's habit didn't dissolve with a ledger. "Slower than Ironhollow's was. But failing."

"I know." Tam crouched beside her, and she saw that he'd already been working at it — thin wedges of river-stone driven into the crack, packed with clay, arranged in a pattern she recognized from her grandmother's primer. The forbidden book. The pre-kingdom tongue. "Vess showed me. Before you two went up." He paused. "Before I asked her to teach me."

Ulyana sat back on her heels. "You asked."

"I asked." He laughed — short, disbelieving. "Twenty years I couldn't ask for anything. Twenty years I didn't have a self to do the asking with. So yes, Ulyana. I asked a seven-year-old to teach me a dead language, and she patted my hand like I was the child, and then she taught me."

"She would."

"She would." He looked at the wedges, at the slow careful work of them. "This gate can hold. Not the way yours holds — not with fear and wards and a keeper who never sleeps. It'll hold the way a riverbank holds. Gently. With tending." He glanced up at her. "That's what keepers do, isn't it? Tend?"

"It's what we're supposed to do," Ulyana said. "Most of us mostly fail."

"You didn't fail."

"Fifteen years at Ironhollow and I never once checked under the wardstone until it was nearly too late. I kept the gate the way a miser keeps coin — afraid to look at it too closely in case I found it counterfeit." She shook her head. "Don't make me braver in memory than I was, Tam. I spent fifteen years pretending your story was a fairy tale because the truth hurt."

"And I sold my soul for a bottle of fever-tincture because I couldn't bear to sit at Mother's bedside and do nothing." He said it flatly, without flinching, and she realized he must have been saying it to himself for days, wearing the guilt smooth like a river stone. "We were both cowards about different things. We're both still here. That's more than the steward can say."

"What happened to him, exactly?" She'd seen it — everyone had seen it — but seeing and understanding were different countries. "When Vess unmade the debts. Every soul he ever collected—"

"Came home." Tam's voice went quiet. "All at once. Through him. There were thousands, Ulyana. Thousands of stolen selves, and every one of them remembered his face." He looked at his hands. "I helped carry some of them out afterward. What was left. The mountain took the rest into itself. Vess says it's holding them kindly. She says the mountain is better at grief than people are."

"The mountain is better at most things than people are."

"Amen," said Tam Sterling, and for a moment they were children again, saying grace over their mother's table, and Ulyana had to turn away and pretend to examine the lichen until her eyes stopped burning.

* * *

In the evening, the deep folk held something between a funeral and a festival.

There wasn't much left to burn — the court's regalia had crumbled with its owners — so they burned the last of the ledgers' covers, the iron-bound boards that had outlived their pages. An old woman of the root-roads whose name Ulyana finally learned was Maren sang in the pre-kingdom tongue, a low looping melody without beginning or end, and the others joined where they knew the words, and those who didn't hummed, and the sound rose through the cathedral of roots and out through passages Ulyana would never see.

Vess sat in Ulyana's lap through all of it. She was heavier than a seven-year-old should be, or perhaps Ulyana was simply tired down to the marrow. The child watched the fire with eyes that had seen too much before she could speak of it, and when the singing ended she reached up and touched Ulyana's cheek — one finger, deliberate, the way she did everything.

"You're sad," Vess signed. Her voice was still coming slowly, word by word, like a door opening on stiff hinges. Speaking aloud cost her effort; signing came easier. Ulyana was learning the signs faster than she'd learned anything since her grandmother's primer.

"I'm not sad."

"You are." Vess's small hand flattened against Ulyana's sternum, right over the heart, as if she could feel the shape of the grief there. "But it's a good sad. Like the snow going."

Ulyana huffed. "Is that how the mountain talks? Everything is a season?"

"Everything is a season," Vess agreed solemnly, and then ruined her own gravity by yawning enormously, and Ulyana carried her to the pallet of woven rush that Maren had made for her, and the child was asleep before she was fully laid down.

Tam was waiting outside the sleeping alcove. He had his hands in his pockets and the awkward posture of a man who has rehearsed a speech and lost his nerve.

"Walk with me?" he said. "There's something I want to show you. Or — tell you. Both."

* * *

He led her up through the root roads to a ledge above the lower gate, where the mountain opened onto a gallery of pale stone veined with quartz. In the dark, the veins gave off a faint light, blue-white, like moonlight caught in ice. Below them the gate glimmered. Beyond it, faintly, the sound of the deep water.

"This is where I used to come," Tam said. "When I was still... hollow. The archivist wasn't allowed preferences, but the body remembered liking high places. I'd stand here and feel the light and not know why it mattered to me." He touched the wall. "First thing I remembered clearly, after. Standing right here, thinking: *my sister would love this.* And not knowing if I had a sister. Just knowing the thought was mine."

Ulyana leaned against the stone beside him. The quartz-light lay across his face and softened the wrongness of it, filled in the missing years, and for a moment he looked exactly like the boy who'd left Thornfell with a satchel and a promise to be back by planting.

"Stay," she said.

It came out before she'd decided to say it. Twenty years of unsaid words crowding behind one syllable.

Tam closed his eyes. "Ulyana—"

"No. Hear me out, because I've earned one speech and I'm taking it." She turned to face him fully. "You could come up. You know you could. Bramble would put you up in his spare room and complain about it every day for a decade, which is how he says *you're family*. Vess needs—" She stopped. Started again. "I need you up there. I've needed you up there for twenty years and I told myself I'd buried that with Mother, and I lied. So I'm saying it now, plain, no bargains, no ledgers: stay. Come up. Be my brother where I can see you."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far below, the deep water moved.

"I thought about it," Tam said at last. "Every day since the fire. I lie awake in that alcove and I imagine the sun. Actual sun, Ulyana. I'd forgotten what it does to your eyes." He smiled, and it hurt to look at. "And then I get up, and there's a hundred and five people down here who spent centuries being eaten by a court, and the court is gone, and nobody is coming to help them. Nobody even remembers they exist. The kingdoms up top wrote the deep folk out of their histories before the histories were written." He spread his hands. "Except us. Except our grandmother, who kept the primer. Except you, who kept the gate. Our family has always been the ones who remember what everyone else agreed to forget."

"So you'll be the keeper."

"So I'll be the keeper." He laughed softly. "Me. The man who couldn't keep a promise to himself for twenty years. But it turns out keeping isn't a talent, it's a habit. You taught me that. Fifteen years of letters I never answered, and you kept writing anyway. That's keeping. That's the whole job."

"I stopped writing after year nine."

"I know. Bramble told me. He also told me you started again in year twelve, on the anniversary, and never sent those either." Tam's eyes were bright. "You never stopped, Ulyana. You just stopped sending. Same thing, down here where it counts."

She looked away, at the gate, at the slow breath of cold air through the seam. "It won't be the same," she said. "Letters. If you write."

"It won't be the same," he agreed. "It'll be better. Because now I'll have things worth telling. How many lambs Maren's people birthed. Whether the eastern passage flooded. What new word Vess taught me." He bumped her shoulder with his, the way he had when they were children walking to market. "And you'll write back and complain about Bramble's tea and Vess's table manners and the mud in Thornfell in spring, and I will read every word twice, and I will be exactly where I'm meant to be, doing exactly what our family does."

"Which is?"

"Keeping what everyone else forgot."

* * *

They said goodbye at dawn, or what passed for dawn below the mountain — the hour when the quartz-veins brightened and the deep folk stirred and the root roads filled with the smell of Maren's flatbread.

No speeches. They'd spent all their speeches in the night. Tam hugged her hard enough to creak ribs, and Ulyana let him, and when he pulled back he pressed something into her hand: a scrap of vellum, salvaged from the fire, with their mother's alphabet across it in his careful new-old hand.

*Home,* it read. *Stay. Sister.*

"Vess's primer," he said. "She started teaching me the words that matter first. Thought you should have a copy."

Ulyana folded it into her coat, over her heart, next to the space where her mother's ring used to be. "Write to me," she said. "Through the gate. Bramble swears he knows a courier who owes him."

"Bramble knows a courier who owes everyone. That's how couriers work." Tam stepped back. Behind him, the lower gate breathed its cold breath, and the wedges of river-stone held the seam, and a hundred and five people began another ordinary day in a mountain that had finally stopped being hungry.

"Goodbye, Tam," Ulyana said. And then, because twenty years of silence deserved one more sentence: "Welcome home, brother."

He smiled — the whole smile this time, the real one, the boy's smile grown into a man's face. "Go up, keeper. Your other half is waiting."

* * *

The climb out took three days.

Bramble complained about all of them — the footing, the rations, the way Vess insisted on naming every stalactite, the fact that Ulyana had cried at the goodbye and then denied crying and he'd had to pretend not to notice, which was *work*, Ostwick work, unpaid. But on the second night, when Vess slept between them with the answering lights drifting gentle as pollen through the dark, Bramble said quietly, into the silence:

"He'll be all right, you know. Your brother. Seen a lot of men hollowed out by that place. Never seen one fill back in. He's filling back in."

"I know," Ulyana said.

"And you did that."

"We did that." She nodded at the sleeping child. "All three of us. And a mountain."

On the third day, the air changed. Warmer. Wetter. Green-smelling. Vess scrambled ahead up the last switchback, and Ulyana followed, and then they were standing at the mouth of the Thornfell road with actual sunlight pouring down on them, late-spring sunlight, thick as honey, and the valley below was so green it looked newly invented.

Vess grabbed her hand. Pointed down the slope, where a thread of smoke rose from a familiar crooked chimney.

"Home?" the child said aloud — testing the word, weighing it, spending it like her first coin.

"Yeah, sprout," Ulyana said, and her voice broke on it, and she didn't care. "Home."

They started down together, into the thaw.
