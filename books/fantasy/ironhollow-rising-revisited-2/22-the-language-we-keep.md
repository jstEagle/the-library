## 22. The Language We Keep

The spring audit of the Ironhollow gate took three days instead of one, because Ulyana did it slowly on purpose.

She had spent fifteen years learning to hurry through the ledger — count the wards, test the seal, note the smuggler tolls in her neat columned hand, and close the book before the mountain could remind her how alone she was at the top of it. Now she walked the gate's length like a woman visiting an old friend who had nearly died, touching each wardstone as she passed, listening to the hum of it. The hairline crack she had hidden all winter was gone. In its place ran a seam of pale new stone, grown smooth as river ice, as if the mountain had knitted its own bone back together while no one was watching.

*While a child was sleeping against it,* she thought. *While it listened to her dream.*

She wrote that in the margin of the ledger, because the ledger deserved honesty now. *Wardstone self-repaired. Cause: Vess.* Then, after a moment, she added: *Cause: us.*

The word looked strange there among the columns of tolls and tallies and small favors. *Us.* She had kept accounts for so long in the singular.

Below her, past the gate's iron ribs, the deep road descended into green dark. Tam was down there somewhere — her brother, mostly himself again, learning to be an archivist of nothing, since the ledgers he had tended for twenty years had burned to ash and honest blankness. He had sent up his first letter two weeks ago, carried by a root-road courier who blinked exactly the right number of times, and Ulyana had read it four times and slept with it under her pillow like a girl of twelve.

*Dear Ula,* it began, in handwriting she had not seen since she was nineteen years old. *I am relearning my own name. It fits better than I expected. The gate is quiet. I am not. Come down when the thaw allows. Bring the primer. There are words here I want to say properly. — Tam*

*Bring the primer.*

She glanced at the window of the keeper's house, where Vess sat cross-legged on the sill with her grandmother's forbidden book open on her knees, lips moving, tracing letters older than any kingdom with one blunt fingernail.

Ulyana closed the ledger, and went inside to teach.

* * *

The primer smelled of cedar and forty years of hiding — behind loose stones, under floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth during the years when speaking the old tongue aloud could get a border family named traitor. Her grandmother had taught her from it in whispers, one word a night, as if language were contraband and every syllable had to be smuggled past the light.

In a way, Ulyana thought now, watching Vess sound out the first page, it had been. Everything worth keeping had been smuggled somehow. Love. Names. Souls.

"Again," she said. "Slowly."

Vess frowned at the glyph — a spiral opening into a line, the sign for *water* — and said something in the deep tongue that made the kettle on the stove shiver and the fire lean toward her like a dog hearing its name.

"That's the old word for it," Ulyana said, laughing despite herself. "But this is grandmother's word. The border word. The one we were allowed." She touched the gloss beneath the glyph, the cramped translation in her grandmother's hand. "*Ael.* Water."

"*Ael,*" Vess repeated, and got it right on the second try, and beamed.

It had been like this for six weeks. The child learned the way rivers learn downhill — inevitably, and with a great deal of noise. Some nights Ulyana suspected the lessons were theater, that Vess could have read the whole primer in an afternoon if she'd wanted, that the slow sounding-out was a gift the child was giving her: the pretense that Ulyana still had something to teach. Other nights she watched Vess struggle genuinely with the border-tongue's crooked grammar and understood that the deep folk's language and the surface languages were truly different countries, and that the child was crossing between them one bridge-word at a time, for Ulyana's sake, because Ulyana had asked.

Because asking was what families did. Because debts were over, and what remained was choice.

They worked through the morning. *Stone. Root. Door. Cold. Warm.* Vess collected words the way Bramble collected grudges — greedily, and with visible satisfaction. When they reached the page of household words, the ones grandmothers used for children and kitchens and small domestic mercies, Vess went still.

She tapped a glyph Ulyana had never needed before. A circle with a hearth-flame drawn inside it, and beneath the flame, a small mark like a person standing.

"What's that one?" Vess asked, in her careful new accent.

Ulyana looked at it for a long moment. Her grandmother had never taught it to her. She had found it herself, years later, flipping through pages that smelled of cedar, and had assumed it was archaic — a word for a world where people stayed anywhere long enough to need it.

"Home," she said. "It means home."

"Home," Vess said. The fire leaned toward her again. Outside the window, the last dirty drifts on the pass shrank a little, as if embarrassed. "Say it in the old way too."

"*Doma,*" Ulyana said. "*Doma-vess.* Hearth-and-people. Home is the fire plus whoever stands beside it. That's the literal reading. Grandmother would have said that's sentimental. Grandmother would also have written it, or it wouldn't be in the book."

Vess traced the little standing figure with her fingernail. "That's you," she said. "And that's me. And that's Tam, when he comes up. And Bramble, when he stops pretending he's just passing through."

"Bramble has been 'just passing through' for nine days," Ulyana said. "He's rebuilt my woodshed twice. Once out of spite."

"He says the first one was badly built."

"The first one was built by me, in a snowstorm, in an hour, and it stood for eleven years."

"He says that proves his point."

Ulyana laughed, and the laugh turned into something else halfway through, the way it sometimes did now — a loosening, a letting-down of weight she had carried so long she'd mistaken it for her spine. Vess watched her with those old, old eyes and did not look away, because the child had learned already that grief and joy could share a room without either one having to leave.

"Next word," Vess said gently, and turned the page.

* * *

Bramble arrived at midday with a haunch of salt pork, a barrel of nails he had absolutely not acquired through legitimate channels, and the expression of a man preparing to complain about the weather as a substitute for saying anything true.

"Thaw's early," he announced, dumping the nails by the door. "Earliest in thirty years, if the Thornfell traders are right, and they're usually wrong, but optimistically so. Roads'll be mud till midsummer. Good year for staying put."

"You've stayed put very thoroughly," Ulyana said. "Nine days."

"I'm resting. Smuggling is hard work. You wouldn't know; you sit behind a gate signing papers."

"I signed your pardon papers. Twice."

"And I remain grateful, which is why I'm here rebuilding your shed instead of south where the wine is." He said it lightly, but his eyes went to Vess — quick, involuntary, the glance of a man checking that a candle was still burning — and then away, out the window, toward the deep road. "Any word from below?"

"A letter. He's remembering more every week. Last week it was our mother's face. This week it's the name of the dog we had when he was six." Ulyana smiled. "He hates the dog's name. Says no one should name a dog Turnip."

"Sound man," Bramble said gravely. "Hollowed for twenty years and his taste survives intact."

Vess, who had been pretending to read, looked up. "When does he come up?"

"When the thaw allows," Ulyana said. "His words."

"But the thaw allowed weeks ago."

"Yes," Ulyana said. And then, because the ledger deserved honesty, and so did the child: "He's afraid, Vess. Twenty years is a long time to be gone from your own face. He wants to come up when he can do it as himself, all the way through, and not flinch at mirrors on the way."

Vess considered this with the terrible seriousness she brought to everything. "Tell him the mirror part gets easier. I know about mirrors."

Bramble made a noise that was almost a laugh and almost not. "Kid talks like a grandmother."

"She is a grandmother," Ulyana said. "Several centuries' worth, folded into eight years. Haven't you noticed?"

"I've noticed everything about that kid," Bramble muttered, "and I'd thank her not to notice me back." But when he thought neither of them was looking, he fished something from his coat and set it on the table by Vess's elbow: a carved wooden bird, crude, unmistakably whittled by someone with strong hands and no talent. "Found it in my pack. Don't know how it got there. Probably fell off a cart."

Vess picked it up and held it the way you hold a thing you intend to keep forever. "Thank you, Bramble."

"Don't mention it. Literally. If anyone asks, I've never been north of Thornfell in my life."

* * *

That evening, after Bramble had loudly failed to leave and had installed himself by the fire with the salt pork and a bottle of plum brandy he claimed was payment for services rendered, Ulyana opened the primer to its last pages.

There were only a few glyphs left untaught. She had been rationing them, she realized — saving the rarest words the way you save the last of anything precious. But the child had earned them, and besides, Ulyana had decided something that afternoon, walking the gate, and decisions made at wardstones had a way of sticking.

"Three more," she said. "Then the book is done, and we start writing our own."

Vess sat up straight. Even Bramble pretended not to listen, which meant he was listening with his whole body.

The first glyph was a door, open, with light coming through it. "*Kella-dai,*" Ulyana said. "Welcome. Literally: the door is glad. Old border custom — you said it when anyone crossed your threshold, even enemies. Especially enemies. Grandmother said it was the most dangerous word in the language, because once you've welcomed someone, you're responsible for them."

"Like a keeper," Vess said.

"Exactly like a keeper."

The second glyph was two lines running side by side into the distance, never touching, never separating. "*Sennu.* Road-together. Two travelers going the same way. Not the same as friendship. Deeper. It means your roads are one road now, even if you walk it apart."

Bramble looked into his brandy bottle very intently.

The third glyph Ulyana did not read from the page, because it wasn't on the page. She took the pencil from behind her ear — the same stubby carpenter's pencil she had used for fifteen years of ledgers — and drew in the margin, slowly, carefully: a circle, and inside it, two small standing figures, side by side, holding the flame between them.

"This one isn't grandmother's," she said. "I'm making it. That's allowed now. The court that punished this language is ash, and the kingdom that feared it has forgotten why, and the mountain itself speaks it better than either of us ever will. So we get to add words." She set the pencil down. "This is *sister.*"

Vess went very still.

"In the old tongue it would be *selith*," Ulyana said. "Blood-of-my-blood, root-of-my-root. But grandmother's word — the border word, the plain word — is simpler. Sister. That's all. Just sister. No debt in it. No bargain. Nobody sold anything, nobody paid anything. It's free." Her voice caught, and she let it. "Do you understand? For the first time in this whole story, it's free."

Vess looked at the glyph for a long time. The fire leaned toward them both. Somewhere below their feet, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the mountain hummed.

"Sister," Vess said at last, trying it in the border tongue, tasting it. Then, softly, in the deep speech, "*Selith*" — and every wardstone from the gate to the pass rang once, gently, like a bell struck by snow falling off a branch.

Bramble jumped. "Does it always do that?"

"No," Ulyana said, heart hammering, grinning like a fool. "No, it really doesn't."

* * *

Later — much later, when Bramble had finally, ostentatiously, decided to stay the night "because the roads are mud, don't make it a thing" — Ulyana sat with Vess on the keeper's bed, the primer open across both their knees, the lamp turned low.

The child was drowsy. Six weeks of surface living had not entirely worn the deep habits off her: she still slept curled like a seed, still woke before dawn to touch the wall and listen to the mountain's mood. But tonight her eyes were heavy and human and eight years old, and Ulyana felt the fierce, terrifying tenderness of it rise in her chest like water finding its level.

"One more word," Vess murmured. "Before sleep. Please."

"We finished the book."

"There are always more words. You said so. We write our own."

Ulyana smiled. "Greedy."

"Grandmother's rule?"

"Our rule. New rule. Greed for words is permitted in this house."

Vess nestled against her shoulder. "Then teach me *stay.*"

Ulyana's breath stopped for half a heartbeat. Of all the words. Of course, of all the words.

"It's not in the primer," she managed. "Grandmother never wrote it. I think—" She stopped. Started again, honestly, because the ledger deserved honesty. "I think it hurt too much. To write a word for something nobody in our family had ever gotten to do. Everyone left. Mother died, Tam was taken, I stayed at the gate and everyone else moved through it. There was no one to say it to."

"Say it to me," Vess said. "Make it up. You're allowed. You made *sister*."

So Ulyana closed her eyes and reached for it, the way you reach for a word in a dream, and found it waiting — perhaps it had always been waiting, unwritten, in the blank space at the back of the book where her grandmother's courage had run out.

"*Enda*," she said quietly. "Stay. In the old tongue. And in ours—" she pressed her lips to the top of Vess's head, "—stay."

The fire settled. The mountain hummed. Outside, the last snow of the long winter let go of the eaves and fell as ordinary water.

And Vess, half-asleep, in the border tongue, in the plain words, in a voice with no magic in it at all except the kind that changes everything, said:

"I was sent to find you."

Ulyana held her and did not ask who sent her, or why, or what the sending had cost. Those answers could keep. They had a whole life of ledgers ahead of them, and for the first time in fifteen years, every column in it balanced.

"Found," she whispered. "You found me. Write that one down tomorrow."

But Vess was already asleep, smiling, home.
