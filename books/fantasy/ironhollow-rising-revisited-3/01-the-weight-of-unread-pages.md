## 1. The Weight of Unread Pages

The trouble with bottling a storm, Ines Iversen had learned in his third year of apprenticeship, was not the catching. The catching was easy. You waited for the squall off the Saltreach and held the flask up like a lantern, and the storm poured itself in, grateful to be somewhere small. The trouble was the interest.

A bottled storm fermented. Left too long on a shelf it grew heavier, the way grief grows heavier, and by year ten a two-pint flask could weigh as much as an anvil, and by year twenty you had to decide whether to open it—and lose everything inside—or keep paying for stronger shelving forever. The Ledgerkeepers' ledger, volume forty-one, page three hundred and six, recorded this dilemma in Ines's own careful hand: *One storm, minor, coastal. Accrued weight: 41 lbs. Storage cost to date: 9 years of shelf-life, 2 shelves, 1 apprentice's back.*

No one would ever read that line. No one had read any line in eleven years.

Ines blew out his lamp at dawn, because dawn was when the counting-house's single window went from black to grey and there was no point spending candle-ends pretending otherwise. He was twenty-nine years old and he had spent fourteen of those years keeping accounts for a guild that officially did not exist, in a country that officially did not exist, on behalf of clients who were mostly dead, mostly gone, or—in the case of the free cities—mostly pretending very hard that neither fact concerned them.

He ate his bread standing up. He always ate standing up; sitting felt like something a man did who expected to be at the table long enough for it to matter. Then he climbed the stairs to the vault room, where the ledgers lived, and began the day's work, which was the same as every day's work, which was the reason he was still alive.

* * *

The Ledgerkeepers traded in impossible things. That was the whole of the guild charter, written four hundred years ago by a founder whose name had been deliberately scratched out of every copy—a founder who had apparently believed that even a name was a kind of debt, and had declined to owe one. Bottled storms. Borrowed years—young people sold a decade of their old age to rich men who wanted to be young again, and someone had to track when those decades came due, and they always came due. Luck, weighed and portioned. Names, held in trust. One entire winter, according to volume nine, purchased by a northern town that had since stopped existing, and which the ledger nonetheless insisted still owed its instalments.

Impossible things required impossible bookkeeping, and impossible bookkeeping required a particular kind of person: one who could look at a column of figures that said *one borrowed year, due 14th of Frostmonth, debtor deceased, estate: none* and not flinch. Ines had never flinched. This was not strength. It was more like being made of the same material as the work—dry, exact, unimpressed by anything, including himself.

The free cities had stopped sending clerks forty years ago. They had stopped sending money thirty years ago. Ten years ago, when Aldric Vane had taken the old country apart and declared the free cities sovereign—which meant, in practice, that each city now belonged to whichever merchant family could afford its council—the last official notice had arrived at the counting-house. Ines kept it framed above the vault door. It was a single line:

*The Guild of Ledgerkeepers is hereby dissolved. All obligations are void.*

Void. As if obligation were a thing a proclamation could reach. Ines had read the notice a hundred times and it still struck him as the funniest document he had ever seen, though he had never once laughed at it. You could dissolve a guild. You could not dissolve what the guild was owed. Somewhere out there, storms sat fermenting in cellars. Somewhere out there, men who had bought extra decades were living on time that was not theirs, and the interest compounded nightly whether or not anyone was watching.

Someone had to watch. That was the whole argument of Ines's life, and he had never won it, and he had never conceded it either.

* * *

Midday brought the only visitor the counting-house ever got: a rat, which crossed the floor of the front room with the confidence of a creditor and left through a gap in the wall that Ines had been meaning to fix since before the dissolution. He logged the visit. Volume fifty-eight, marginal note: *Premises deteriorating. Recommend repairs. Recommendation ignored, as usual.*

He was halfway through reconciling the luck accounts—heavy work, luck, because it moved—when the light in the window changed. Not to dusk. Dusk came slow and grey along the coast. This was a shadow, quick and wrong, crossing the glass from the street.

Then came the sound. A body hitting a door is a specific sound, and Ines had heard it exactly once before, when old Master Corvane had died at his desk and slid sideways into the doorframe on his way down. He knew it the way you know your own name.

He opened the door and the stranger fell across the threshold onto the counting-house floor, which was fitting, Ines thought later, in a way he did not appreciate yet. People who owed the guild always ended up on that floor eventually. This one simply arrived early.

The stranger was a courier—you could tell by the boots, road-worn past all decency, and by the way his hand went immediately to the satchel at his chest even while his other hand pressed uselessly at the wound below his ribs. Blood had soaked through his coat in a shape like a spread wing. Someone had cut him deep and angled, someone who knew how to make a wound that killed slowly enough for the victim to run.

"Ledgerkeeper," the man said. His eyes found Ines's face and fixed there with terrible relief, as if Ines were a harbour and the man had been swimming toward him for miles. "You're real. Gods. They told me you'd be real."

"Who told you?" Ines asked, kneeling. Pressure first. He put both hands on the wing-shaped wound and the man hissed. "Who sent you?"

"The Doorway." The word came out cracked, reverent, obscene. "It's real. It's been real the whole time. And Vane knows."

Ines's hands went still for half a heartbeat. Half a heartbeat was all he allowed himself. "You're bleeding on a floor that took me six years to clean properly," he said. "Be specific or be quiet."

Something like a smile crossed the courier's face. Dying men, Ines had read, often smiled at the stubbornness of the living. "They said you'd be like this. Cold as a column of figures." The man's hand fumbled at the satchel's wax seal. "Listen. There's a map. The Doorway—it isn't a way out. Do you understand? Everyone thinks it's a way out. It isn't. It's—" A cough took him, and blood came with it, bright and shocking against the grey floorboards. "It's a court. It's still sitting. Four hundred years and it never adjourned. And the debtor king—" Another cough. "Vane bought his crown. On credit. From behind the Doorway. And the payment is coming due, and he'll burn every city on the coast before he lets the court sit in judgment on him, and the map finds the Doorway, and you're the last one, you're the *last one*, do you hear me—"

"I hear you," Ines said. "Breathe between sentences."

But the man was past breathing between sentences. He seized Ines's wrist with both hands, and his grip was astonishing, the grip of a man spending his final coin, and he pressed the satchel—not handed it, *pressed* it, the way you press a debt into someone's palm—against Ines's chest.

"Take the account," he whispered. "Take the account. Take the account."

Then the grip failed, all at once, like a shelving bracket giving way under a storm-flask grown too heavy, and the courier slid back against the doorframe and was dead before his head finished tipping.

* * *

Ines sat on the floor beside the body for a long time. Outside, Meridian Freeport's evening noises went on—carts, gulls, a drunk singing three streets over—and none of it paused, because none of it knew. That was the thing about invisibility, Ines thought. It protected you completely right up until the moment it didn't, and then it offered nothing at all.

He should have been afraid. He noted, distantly, that he should have been afraid, the way he noted discrepancies in a ledger: *fear, expected, not yet posted.* Instead he did what he had trained fourteen years to do. He opened the satchel.

Inside was a map, folded eight times, sealed in wax stamped with a sigil he knew better than his own handwriting: the crossed quill and key of the Ledgerkeepers, pressed in wax so old it had gone the colour of dried blood. Beneath the map, wrapped in oilcloth, a single loose page torn from the guild's oldest ledger—volume one, the founder's own hand, the hand with the scratched-out name. Ines read it twice by lamplight, and the second reading changed nothing, which meant it was true.

*Aldric Vane. Principal: one crown, purchased on credit. Collateral: the Doorway. Terms: known to the debtor. Interest: compounding. Status: PAST DUE.*

Below it, in fresher ink, someone had added a line that was not bookkeeping at all:

*If you are reading this, the guild is not dissolved. It is waiting.*

Ines looked at the dead man. He looked at the map. He thought about the wound—angled, expert, patient—and about the words *Vane knows*, and about the fact that whoever had cut the courier open would very soon want to know what the courier had been carrying, and would ask that question with the same expertise.

For fourteen years, no one had read a single line Ines wrote. Tonight, someone had crossed a country to die on his floor holding a line with his guild's seal on it.

Quietly, without letting himself feel anything about it, Ines rose, fetched the guild's oldest ledger from the vault, and entered the night's transaction in a steady hand:

*Received: one map, sealed; one debt, royal; one corpse, unnamed. Debtor unknown. Creditor: the old country. Due date: imminent.*

He blotted the page. Then, from the street outside, came the sound of boots stopping in front of the counting-house door—three pairs, unhurried, deliberate—and a voice, polite as a clerk's, asking the dark:

"Is this where the Ledgerkeeper lives?"
