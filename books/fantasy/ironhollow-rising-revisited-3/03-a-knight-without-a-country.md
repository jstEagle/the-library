## 3. A Knight Without a Country

The salt road out of Corvane was the kind of road a man took when he had nothing left to lose and wanted company that matched.

Ser Brannoc Hale crouched behind a leaning milestone, watching the traveler come. He'd been watching for an hour, since the figure left the last waystation—a thin man in a gray coat too fine for the dust he walked through, carrying a satchel like it held his own heart. Brannoc had robbed three travelers on this stretch in as many weeks. Not because he liked it. Because a knight without a country still needed bread, and honor didn't keep a man fed once the heralds had read your name off the rolls.

His name had been read off the rolls eleven months ago. He could still hear the cadence of it: *stripped of rank, stripped of arms, stripped of standing.* They'd let him keep his sword only because the executioner's fee for a disarmed knight was considered wasteful. He'd walked out of Vanehold with his spurs in his fist and hadn't put them on since. They sat now at the bottom of his pack, wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than any weapon he owned.

The reason for it all was a village called Miren's Ford, which no longer existed, and an order signed by a captain with a clean doublet and a dirty soul. *Burn it. Make an example.* Forty households. A granary. A shrine to whatever gods farmers kept. Brannoc had stood in the square with a torch in his hand and understood, quite suddenly, that everything he'd been trained for—every drill, every oath, every scar—had been preparation for exactly one moment, and this was it.

He'd put the torch in the well.

They'd called it treason. He'd called it arithmetic. Forty households against one career. The math had never frightened him.

Now the thin man on the salt road was close enough to count freckles. Youngish. Careful walk, like someone used to being overlooked and had made peace with it. No guard, no horse, no visible weapon. Easy pickings, and Brannoc hated how easily the thought came now, how little friction there was between the man he'd been and the man stepping out from behind the milestone with his sword drawn.

"Your purse," Brannoc said. "And the satchel."

The traveler stopped. He didn't scream or run. He looked at the sword, then at Brannoc's face, and something in his expression shifted—not fear, exactly. Recognition of a kind. Like a clerk seeing a number that didn't balance.

"You're the knight," the man said. "Hale. The one who wouldn't burn Miren's Ford."

Brannoc's grip tightened. "My reputation travels faster than I do."

"It does when people can't stop talking about it." The traveler's voice was quiet, unhurried, the voice of a man reading aloud from a ledger. "They tell it wrong, though. In the taverns they say you wept. I don't think you weep. I think you counted."

Brannoc felt that land somewhere under his ribs. "Who are you?"

"Ines Iversen. Last apprentice of the Ledgerkeepers." He said it the way other men said *orphan*—a fact, not a boast. "You want the purse. Take it. It has four silver marks and a button I keep meaning to fix. But the satchel stays with me."

"Everything stays with me. That's what robbery means."

"Yes," Ines said. "I'm familiar with the concept. My guild spent two hundred years cataloguing it."

Something about him—the flat calm, the terrible precision—made Brannoc laugh, short and bitter. "You're either very brave or you don't understand your situation."

"Neither. I'm very tired, and I've been followed since Corvane, and I've decided that if I'm going to die on this road, I'd rather do it arguing about terms." He adjusted the satchel strap. "Two men came into the counting-house three nights ago asking after a map. They asked by name. Mine. Do you know what that means, Ser Hale? It means somewhere, someone opened a book, found my name in it, and decided I was worth more dead than alive. That has never happened before. I have spent my entire life being worth nothing to anyone. It's very disorienting."

Brannoc stared at him. "You're mad."

"I'm an accountant."

"They're not mutually exclusive."

"No," Ines agreed. "They rarely are."

Brannoc should have taken the satchel. Every practical instinct said so: a lone man, a heavy bag, a road with no witnesses. Instead he heard himself ask, "What's in it?"

"A map. And a debt." Ines looked down the empty road in both directions, then back up at Brannoc, and for the first time his composure flickered—just at the edges, like paper near flame. "A debt owed by Aldric Vane."

The name went through Brannoc like cold water. The usurper. The man whose captains burned villages and whose heralds struck names from rolls. "That's treasonous talk."

"Everything I own is treasonous now. It came with the map." Ines hesitated—and then, visibly deciding something, worked the clasp of the satchel open and drew out a folded leather folio, stamped with a seal Brannoc hadn't seen since he was a boy kneeling beside his grandmother's chair while she told stories of the old country.

A circle of interlocking lines, like a knot that had no beginning. The Ledgerkeepers' seal.

Brannoc lowered the sword.

* * *

He'd been seven years old the winter the tax riders came to Thornhollow, his family's village in the borderlands, back when the old country still existed on maps if nowhere else. The riders wore Vane's colors before Vane had a crown—mercenaries collecting debts that had already been paid, because no one dared audit them. His father had paid twice. They were going to take the third payment along with the roof beams and, his mother feared, along with her.

It was his grandmother who walked nine miles to the nearest counting-house, a gray building everyone ignored, and laid out receipts on a counter worn smooth by centuries of hands. She couldn't read. She didn't need to. The Ledgerkeeper on duty—an old woman with ink-stained fingers—read them, cross-referenced them against a ledger older than the kingdom, and wrote out a single line of correction.

Three days later, a rider in gray delivered that line to the mercenary captain. Brannoc never learned what it said. He only knew the riders left, and never came back, and his grandmother lived to be ninety and died owing nothing to anyone.

*The ledgers spared us,* she used to say, tapping his chest with one crooked finger. *Remember that, boy. Swords make countries. Ledgers unmake them.*

He'd forgotten. Or he'd buried it, the way you bury things that don't fit inside a soldier's life. Now a half-starved clerk stood on a salt road holding the seal that had saved his family, and Brannoc's sword arm felt suddenly, absurdly ashamed of itself.

"The old country," Brannoc said slowly. "Your guild served it."

"We were its memory. Its law, when the law couldn't reach." Ines closed the folio. "Vane bought his crown on credit. From the old country. And the collateral—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "I've said too much to a man who was robbing me ninety seconds ago."

"You have." Brannoc sheathed the sword anyway. "So here's my offer, and you'll forgive its shape, because I haven't made an honest offer in a year. You're being hunted, you can't fight, and you're walking alone through country full of Vane's outriders toward—I assume—the Doorway."

Ines went very still. "I never said—"

"You didn't have to. Everyone who runs runs toward the Doorway. It's the only door anyone remembers." Brannoc slung his pack higher on his shoulder. "I know these roads. I know where the patrols cross. I can cook, mend armor, and kill men efficiently, which is a grim résumé but an honest one. In exchange—"

"Name it."

"Not coin." He almost laughed at himself. What did he want? He turned the question over and found only one thing at the bottom of it, small and stubborn as a stone in a boot. "When this is done—if it's ever done—I want my name written back. Somewhere official. One line. *Ser Brannoc Hale, restored.* Your guild kept records, yes? Records have weight. I've seen what one line in a ledger can do."

Ines studied him for a long moment, and Brannoc had the uncomfortable sense of being audited—of every lie he'd ever told being weighed and found negligible.

"That's all?" Ines said. "One line?"

"One line is how my family survived. It seems fitting it's how I might too."

Something moved behind Ines's eyes—recognition, maybe, of a fellow creature reduced to essentials. Then he nodded once, briskly, like closing an account.

"Then we're aligned, Ser Hale. You protect the ledger; the ledger protects you." He started walking, then paused. "There's a woman in Saltmere. Wren Calloway. She has a ship, and she hates me, and she's our only way across the Ashfen coast. Fair warning: she may try to rob you too."

"She'd be the third this year."

"Did the first two succeed?"

"The first one did. The second one is walking beside you."

For the first time, Ines smiled. It was a small thing, quickly gone, but Brannoc found himself thinking that the old country had sent stranger ambassadors than a tired clerk and a disgraced knight.

They walked east together as the light went long and gold across the salt flats. Behind them, far off on the ridge, Brannoc caught movement—riders, three of them, moving with the deliberate patience of men following a scent rather than a trail.

He didn't mention it. He just shifted his pack so the sword drew easier, and matched Ines's pace, and began, quietly, to count.
