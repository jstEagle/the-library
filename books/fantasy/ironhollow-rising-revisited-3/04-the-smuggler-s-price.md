## 4. The Smuggler's Price

The *Saltwife* sat low in the water at the drowned quay of Corrow's Reach, her hull patched with tar and prayer, and Wren Calloway stood at her rail like she'd been waiting for me for six years.

She hadn't. I knew that. But my heart didn't believe it, and it hammered against my ribs like a debtor at the door.

I came down the rotted stairs slowly, because there was no fast way to do this. Brannoc waited at the top with the horses, which was a mercy—he filled doorways the way weather fills a sky, and this conversation needed to be small enough to hold in two hands.

"Ines." She said my name the way you'd read a figure in a ledger you already knew was wrong. "Someone told me a Ledgerkeeper died in Saltmarsh. I thought, good. One less."

"One less what?"

"Debt." She smiled without warmth. "Yours or mine, I never worked out which."

The harbor smelled of kelp and creosote. Gulls argued over something dead on the pilings. Behind her, three of her crew watched me with the flat professional interest of people deciding whether I'd be trouble, cargo, or both.

"I need passage," I said. "South. Past the Ashfen."

"Everyone needs passage. The Ashfen's a graveyard for ships that aren't mine." She came down the gangplank, and up close she was thinner than memory had made her, wind-scoured, a new scar splitting one eyebrow. Six years is enough time for a face to become a stranger's. It wasn't, quite. That was the problem. "What are you carrying?"

"A map."

"To where?"

"You know where."

Her jaw tightened. The Doorway. Everyone knew where; nobody believed it existed, the way nobody believes in a country that officially stopped being a country ten years ago. The old country hadn't died. It had been foreclosed on, and Aldric Vane held the deed, and everyone pretended the paperwork was legitimate because the alternative was admitting they lived in a stolen house.

"No," Wren said.

"You haven't heard the price I'm offering."

"I don't care about your price. I cared about you once, Ines, and look what it bought me. You chose a guild of ghosts over—" She stopped. Her hand had curled into a fist against her thigh, and she made herself uncurl it, deliberately, the way she did everything. "No. Find another smuggler."

"There isn't another smuggler. There's you, and there's Vane's navy, and Vane's navy doesn't take passengers."

"Then swim."

"Wren—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a whip over still water. "Don't say my name like that. Like an entry in one of your books. Like something you're balancing."

* * *

Here is what I had never told her, and what I told her now, standing on a rotten quay with assassins somewhere behind me and the end of everything somewhere ahead:

Six years ago, the guild gave me a choice. They always frame it as a choice—that's how institutions launder cruelty into procedure. Master Ellery laid two papers on the table. One was my apprenticeship bond, completed, with a posting to the Meridian archive: steady work, a stipend, a life. The other was a single line from the oldest ledger, redacted, concerning a ship called the *Saltwife* and a debt incurred by its owner's father—a debt the guild could call or forgive, at discretion.

My father had been a smuggler too. He'd died owing the guild four hundred crowns and a favor, and the favor had passed to his daughter like a birthright neither of them wanted.

"If you take the posting," Ellery said, "the Calloway account stays dormant. Indefinitely. If you refuse it—if you run off with the girl—the account comes due within the year."

He wasn't threatening Wren. Not exactly. He was showing me the arithmetic, and arithmetic has no malice in it, which is somehow worse. I could keep her safe by leaving her, or endanger her by staying, and either way the ledger balanced and the guild went on not caring whether I lived or wept.

So I left. And I let her believe it was ambition. Because if she believed I'd chosen the guild over her, she'd hate me—and hatred keeps people away from danger far better than love does.

"I didn't choose the guild," I said now. "I chose you. That was the shape of the choice. I just couldn't tell you which thing I was choosing."

Wren stared at me. A gull screamed. Somewhere below decks, a man laughed at something.

"That's a beautiful story," she said finally. "You've had six years to polish it."

"It's true."

"So is mine. Mine is that you walked out of my bed before dawn and left a note written in ledger-hand—you know you write in ledger-hand even your apologies?—and I spent a year waiting for the guild to come collect my father's debt, sleeping with a knife under my pillow. Then I stopped waiting and started working, and I became very good at not needing anyone." She stepped closer. She smelled of salt and tar and, underneath it, some oil she used on her hair that I would have known blindfolded. "And now you come back with a map to the Doorway and ask me to sail into the one place Vane wants closed more than he wants anything else in the world. Why should I believe a word?"

"Because I'm going to show you half the map," I said. "Right now. Before you agree to anything."

That surprised her. I saw it land—the flicker behind her eyes, the recalculation. Smugglers live on information; giving it away freely is the one sin their trade doesn't forgive.

"The map shows the Doorway's approach through the Ashfen," I said. "Tide-channels, the drowned causeway, the marker stones. Half of it. The other half—the part that matters—is a set of entries in the guild's first ledger. Names. Debts. What the Doorway actually is." I held her gaze. "You get the navigation half regardless. If you take me south, I'll give you the rest when we make landfall. Not before."

"Why not before?"

"Because knowledge is collateral, Wren. I'm a Ledgerkeeper. It's the only currency I have, and I'm learning to spend it carefully."

Something moved across her face—not softness, exactly. Recognition. She'd spent six years becoming hard in all the places I'd been soft, and here I was, arriving hardened in the one way she'd never managed: willing to trade what I loved most.

"And the assassins?" she said. "The ones asking after you in Saltmarsh. Vane's people."

"Two of them found me. Brannoc dealt with one. The other is probably reporting our heading right now."

"So every day I carry you south, I'm carrying a price on my head too."

"Yes."

"You're asking me to bet my ship, my crew, and my neck on a story about a door."

"I'm asking you to bet them on me. The story's just the interest."

* * *

She turned away and looked out at the gray water for a long time. I let her. There's a discipline to silence—I'd learned it among ledgers, where the most important figures are the ones left blank—but watching her stand at the rail, I understood it differently. She was doing what I'd done six years ago. Weighing accounts. Deciding what a person costs.

"My father's debt," she said, without turning. "The real one. Four hundred crowns and a favor. Is it still open?"

"Dormant. I marked it dormant myself, after I took the posting. It was the only thing I could do from inside."

"Can you close it?"

"I can mark it paid. Whether the old country honors my marks—" I shrugged. "Ask me after the Doorway."

She laughed then, short and sharp, no humor in it. "Gods. You really are the last one. The whole guild boiled down to one tired man with a satchel full of paper." She turned back. "Half now, half at landfall. And one more thing."

"Name it."

"Honesty. From here to the Ashfen, whatever you know, whenever I ask. No dormant accounts, no redactions, no ledger-hand apologies. You lie to me once, Ines, and I put you ashore wherever we happen to be, assassins or no."

It was a fair price. It was also the most frightening one anyone had ever asked of me, because honesty, in my trade, is the one commodity that doesn't compound. You spend it and it's gone.

"All right," I said.

"All right?" She raised an eyebrow. "That's it? No negotiation? Where's the man who once haggled me down to a fair price for a crate of Vellorine brandy?"

"He learned what things cost." I pulled the map case from inside my coat and held it out. "Half now. As agreed."

She took it, and for a moment our hands were both on the leather, and neither of us let go, and the gulls went quiet the way gulls do when they're waiting for something to break.

"Brannoc Hale," she said. "Your knight. He robbed three of my runners last year."

"He was hungry. He'll apologize."

"He'd better. And Ines—" Her grip tightened on the case. "If this map leads us anywhere but where you say it does, the old country won't be the only thing that gets foreclosed."

We cast off within the hour. Brannoc came aboard with the horses stabled below and an expression suggesting he'd rather fight another assassin than ride a ship, and Wren put him to hauling lines before he'd finished introducing himself, which he bore with the grim patience of a man who has decided his penance includes rope burns.

By dusk the free cities' lights fell behind us and the *Saltwife* ran south under a rising wind, toward the Ashfen and the drowned coast and the door at the end of all the ledgers.

I stood at the stern and watched the water, and Wren came to stand beside me, not touching, closer than touching.

"The entries in the other half of the map," she said quietly. "Vane's debts. How bad are they?"

I thought about lying. I'd promised not to, and the promise sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"They're not money," I said. "Wren, what he owes isn't payable in coin. And the creditor is coming to collect."

Ahead of us, far off through the dark, lightning flickered over the Ashfen—silent, repeated, like a signal no one wanted to answer.
