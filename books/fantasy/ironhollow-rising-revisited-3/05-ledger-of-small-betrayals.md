## 5. Ledger of Small Betrayals

The letter sat in Wren Calloway's coat like a stone she'd swallowed whole.

*Amnesty. Full pardon, sealed by the Crown of Aldric Vane, in exchange for the map and the man who carries it.*

She'd read it eleven times since leaving the drowned coast's last safe harbor, and each reading made it heavier. The spymaster's agent had pressed it into her hand in a tavern that smelled of tar and rotting kelp, three days before Ines ever came asking for passage. *You'll be rich or you'll be free,* the agent had said. *Choose before you make port.*

She hadn't chosen yet. That was the lie she told herself, anyway—the comfortable one, the one that let her sleep in the captain's bunk while Ines slept on deck among the salt crates, and Brannoc stood watch with his sword across his knees like a man guarding a grave.

Wren climbed the companionway into grey morning light. The *Kestrel's Debt* ran close along the shore where the old country had drowned—rooftops breaking the surface at low tide, church spires leaning like drunkards, whole streets visible beneath the green water when the sun angled right. Her passengers stood at the rail looking down at them.

"You grew up near here," Ines said. Not a question. He always knew things about her she'd never told him, which was either love or accounting, and she'd stopped being sure which.

"Saltmouth," she said. "Before it went under. I was six when the sea took the market square." She nodded at a dark shape below. "My mother sold eel pies there. Best on the coast."

Brannoc grunted. "The old country let it drown."

"The old country was already dead by then." Wren checked their heading, more for something to do than because it needed checking. "It just hadn't finished paying its bills."

Ines flinched. She saw it—a small thing, quickly buried, but she'd spent years learning to read him and some skills don't rust. She'd meant it as an ordinary piece of bitterness, the kind sailors traded like coins. But to him it landed somewhere else entirely.

*He knows what he's carrying,* she thought. *And he thinks I don't.*

That was the arrangement, wasn't it? He trusted her with his life and his secret both. And she held a letter in her coat that would sell them both.

* * *

She started testing him that afternoon. She couldn't help it; testing people was how smugglers loved them.

They put in at Gullwatch, a half-drowned fishing village strung along a seawall, to take on water. While the crew worked, Wren watched Ines walk the shingle with his ledger under his arm—of course he'd brought the ledger, the man probably slept with it—and stop at a fishwife mending nets by her door.

Wren drifted close enough to hear, pretending to inspect a mooring line.

"—your grandmother's account," Ines was saying. "Held with the Ledgerkeepers, sixty years. Storm-bottling fees, paid through the bad winters. There's a credit remaining."

The fishwife squinted at him. "The guild's dead. Everyone says so."

"The debts aren't." He opened the ledger, showed her a page, counted out silver from his own purse—not guild funds; Wren knew the guild's coffers were dust—and pressed it into her hand. "This is what remains owed to your family. It isn't much. But it's yours."

The woman stared at the silver like it might bite. Then she looked up at Ines with an expression Wren recognized from churches and funerals: the face of someone who has just been reminded the world contains more than it advertises.

When Ines walked on, Wren fell into step beside him. "That was your money."

"It was owed."

"To her grandmother. Who is dead."

"Debts survive the people who hold them. That's rather the point of debts." He said it mildly, the way he said everything, but his jaw was set. "Her grandmother bottled a storm for the guild in the famine year. Saved three villages. The guild paid her half and then the country fell and nobody paid the rest. I'm paying the rest."

"With what? You fled Meridian with nothing."

"With what I have." He didn't look at her. "It isn't a large sum. It doesn't need to be examined."

But Wren examined it anyway, all evening, as they sailed on. Because there was a second reason he wouldn't look at her, and she'd seen it years ago when he chose the guild over her: Ines Iversen could not bear to be seen doing good, because being seen made it real, and real things could be taken away.

Or—and the thought arrived uninvited, cold as ballast water—because being seen made it harder to betray.

* * *

At Cormorant Reach, two days later, she tested him properly.

A harbor official boarded them at the chain, a soft man with Vane's colors stitched inside his collar where he thought no one would look. Wren paid the toll, smiled the right smiles, and got rid of him. But he'd left behind a question, asked casually: whether they'd seen a clerk traveling south, a man named Iversen, wanted for sedition against the Crown.

Afterward, Ines came to her at the wheel. "You lied to him."

"I lie to everyone. It's cheaper than the truth."

"He offered you something. I watched his hands. Men who want only money keep their hands still. His hands were offering."

Wren kept her eyes on the channel markers. "You're imagining things, ledger-keeper."

"I imagine things professionally." A pause. "If someone has made you an offer, Wren, take it."

She turned so fast the wheel spun a quarter-point. "What?"

"If my capture buys your safety, take it. Brannoc will see me partway. I've accounted for—" He stopped himself, and she watched him hear his own words, watch the horror arrive. "That came out wrong."

"Did it." Her voice had gone flat and dangerous, the voice she used on men who shorted her cargo. "You've *accounted* for me. Like cargo. Like a line in one of your books."

"That's not—"

"I know exactly what I am in your ledgers, Ines. I've known since you walked out of Saltmouth with your guild seal and your grand purpose and left me standing on the dock." She heard herself getting loud and forced it down, because the helmsman was ten feet away and loose talk sinks ships. "Get off my deck."

He went. And she stood at the wheel hating him, and hating more the thing underneath the hate, which was that he had offered to let her sell him. That even now, even knowing what she might be, his first instinct was to settle her account fairly.

Nobody fair had ever loved her. She'd built her whole life on that principle. And here was this impossible man dismantling it one silver coin at a time, paying dead women's debts on a drowned coast, offering himself up like a balance to be struck.

The letter in her coat weighed as much as an anchor.

* * *

That night she found Brannoc at the stern, keeping his endless watch, and sat beside him because she couldn't stand her own thoughts below decks.

"Your knight," she said. "Why are you really here?"

He considered the question longer than most men would have. "When I was a boy, plague came to my village. The old country's assessors came through after—counting the dead, marking the fields, settling what was owed to survivors. My mother got a payment. Not charity. An accounting. It bought our winter." He turned the sword across his knees. "Vane ordered me to burn a village that owed him taxes it couldn't pay. I refused because I'd seen what honest accounting looks like. It looks like a woman being able to feed her children because someone wrote the truth in a book." He glanced at her. "Your clerk carries that book. Whatever else he is, that's what he is."

"And if what's in the book gets us all killed?"

"Then we'll have died solvent." The ghost of a smile. "Better than most."

She laughed despite herself, and the laugh cracked something, and she went below and took out the letter and read it a twelfth time.

*Amnesty. Full pardon.*

She thought about the fishwife's face. She thought about Ines saying *take it*, meaning it, having already entered her betrayal into whatever internal book he kept and forgiven the entry in advance. She thought about Vane's spymaster, whose hands had been still, whose offer had been clean and simple and required nothing of her except becoming the kind of person who sells a man who pays dead women's debts.

Small compromises. That was all corruption ever was. A lie at a harbor chain. A letter kept instead of burned. A choice deferred until deferring became choosing.

She held the letter over the lantern.

Her hand shook. Not from fear—from arithmetic. Because burning it meant choosing Ines, and choosing Ines meant admitting she'd spent seven years telling herself a story about what he'd done to her, and if that story was wrong then she'd wasted seven years, and people will do almost anything rather than admit they've wasted time.

The lantern flame bent. The paper did not burn.

Footsteps on the companionway ladder. She shoved the letter back into her coat as Ines appeared, hair salt-stiff, eyes shadowed, holding two cups of the terrible tea he insisted on making.

"I owe you an apology," he said. "For the word *accounted*. And for—" he paused, recalibrating, the way he did, "—for leaving Saltmouth. I've had seven years to write the entry and I still haven't found the figure that makes it balance. I don't think one exists. I think some debts you just carry."

Wren looked at the tea. At the man. At the drowned coast sliding past the stern windows, its rooftops catching moonlight like the bones of a country refusing to stay buried.

"Drink your tea, Ines," she said quietly. "We make port at Ashfen in two days."

She said it evenly. She said it like a woman who had not spent the last hour holding a pardon over a flame. And when he smiled—that small, relieved, unbearably trusting smile—she smiled back, and hated herself, and did not burn the letter.

Not yet.

But below decks, in the dark, she took out her chart and marked the Ashfen approach, and next to it, in pencil small enough to erase, she wrote the words she'd been refusing to write since the tavern:

*Decide by then.*
