## 6. The City That Signed Itself Away

Brannoc Hale had burned exactly one thing in his life, and he had refused to burn the second. Everything since—the stripping of his spurs, the letters after his name struck through in red ink by men who had never held a sword in rain—came from that refusal. So when Meridian Freeport rose out of the morning haze with its white walls and its hundred flags and its harbor thick with trading ships flying colors of every nation that still existed, he felt something old and bitter turn over in his chest.

Cities like this had signed themselves away while he was still kneeling on cold stone, listening to a tribunal read his crimes aloud. They had traded their independence for favorable tariffs and called it wisdom. Now they would trade it for survival and call it necessity.

"They'll surrender," Wren said beside him at the rail of the *Kindly Gull*, watching the city grow. "Look at them. Fat and frightened. Vane's envoys have been here a month, buying councilmen the way you buy fish—fresh ones in the morning, cheaper by noon."

"You know this port."

"I grew up three streets from that customs house." Wren's voice was flat as slate. "Before I learned that ships pay better than honesty."

Ines stood apart from them both, as he always did, holding the ledger against his chest like a man carrying an injured bird. He had barely slept since the Ashfen road. Brannoc had watched him read it by firelight, page after page, his lips moving, his face going grayer with each entry.

"It's all there," Ines had said last night. "Every payment. Every signature. Vane didn't take the throne—he bought it. On credit. From a country that officially doesn't exist."

"Then we show them," Brannoc had said.

He was beginning to understand how naive that sounded.

* * *

They came through the Salt Gate at midmorning, and Brannoc hated how easy it was. No one looked twice at a big scarred man with a sword, a smuggler with a sailor's roll to her walk, and a thin clerk clutching a book. Meridian's guards were busy. There were banners going up along the Processional Way—not Vane's black-and-gold, not yet, but the plain white of parley. The city was dressing itself for its own funeral and calling it a festival.

"The council sits at noon," Wren said. "Public gallery. Anyone can speak if a magistrate sponsors them." She glanced at Ines. "Nobody will sponsor a nameless clerk."

"Then I won't be a nameless clerk," Ines said.

Brannoc heard something in his voice he hadn't heard before. Not fear. Not the quiet dread that had followed the man since the courier died at his door. Something harder.

They found a magistrate by accident, which Brannoc later decided was not an accident at all. An old woman named Corvessa Dain, gray-robed, sharp-faced, sitting alone in a wine shop near the council hall because, she said, her colleagues no longer spoke to her. She had voted against the parley. She had voted against everything for ten years, and she wore her irrelevance like armor.

"A Ledgerkeeper," she said when Ines showed her the guild seal, and her hand shook—not with fear, Brannoc thought, but with something older. Recognition. "My grandmother told me about your guild. She said they kept accounts for a country that died. I thought she was senile."

"She wasn't," Ines said.

"No," Corvessa said. "I don't suppose she was." She looked at the ledger without touching it, the way you look at a snake you've been warned about. "You want to speak before the council."

"I want to read one entry."

"One entry." The old woman laughed, dry as ash. "One entry will do what ten years of my votes couldn't?"

"Yes," Ines said simply, and Brannoc believed him, and could see that Corvessa Dain believed him too, and hated herself for it.

She sponsored him. God help them all, she sponsored him.

* * *

The council chamber of Meridian Freeport was built to make citizens feel small. A semicircle of tiered benches for the magistrates, forty-one of them, above a floor of polished marble where petitioners stood in a chalked circle like defendants. The public gallery wrapped around the upper walls, packed today—Brannoc counted soldiers in the crowd wearing no livery he knew, which meant Vane's men already walked the city openly.

Corvessa Dain stood, her voice cracking across the chamber: "The gallery brings a witness. Under charter, section nine, any citizen-sponsored testimony must be heard before a vote of surrender."

Groans. Laughter from the higher benches. The Lord Magistrate, a soft man named Pellin Ostra with rings on every finger, waved one hand as though brushing away a fly. "Hear the witness, then. Briefly."

Ines stepped into the chalk circle.

Brannoc had seen men stand in circles like that before trials. He had stood in one himself. He knew what fear looked like from the inside, and Ines Iversen should have been drowning in it—a clerk, alone, in front of forty-one magistrates and a gallery full of spies. But Ines opened the ledger with steady hands, and when he spoke, his voice carried the way a small bell carries: not loud, but impossible to ignore.

"My name is Ines Iversen. I am the last apprentice of the Ledgerkeepers. This is our oldest ledger." He turned pages. "I will read one entry."

"Get on with it," someone called.

"Entry four thousand one hundred and twelve. Year of the Doorway, final accounting." Ines looked up. "It reads: *Received from Aldric Vane, one crown of the old country, claimed by conquest. Issued to Aldric Vane: legitimacy, term indefinite, interest compounding annually. Collateral held: the Doorway. Note appended by Guildmaster Sarel Voss: the debtor has not asked what happens when the debt comes due.*"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on the marble and the rings and the white parley banners.

Then the chamber erupted.

"Lies!" A magistrate on the third tier, fat and purple, on his feet. "Forged! The guild is extinct—who authenticated this book?"

"The guild authenticated itself for three hundred years," Ines said. "Its seals are verifiable. Its entries are countersigned. Bring any scholar of the old script—"

"There are no scholars of the old script!"

"I am one," Ines said, "and I am standing in your circle."

Brannoc watched the Lord Magistrate's face, and it was the face he remembered from his own tribunal—not cruelty, exactly. Worse than cruelty. The face of men calculating cost. Pellin Ostra looked at the ledger and saw not truth but trouble; saw not a king exposed but a treaty endangered; saw forty-one careers balanced against one clerk's book.

"Magistrate Dain," Ostra said slowly, "you sponsored this... testimony. You vouch for this document?"

"I vouch for the man," Corvessa said. "Read the seal yourself."

"I don't need to read seals." Ostra's voice was gentle now, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing in the room. "I need to ask what this testimony is *for*. We are negotiating peace. Peace, sir, that will save forty thousand lives. And you stand there and tell us our king—our *king*—bought his throne. Even if true—and I grant nothing—even if true, what would you have us do? Die for a footnote?"

"Die for the truth that your surrender is a payment," Ines said. "This city isn't negotiating. It's settling an account. And accounts, my lord, come due."

That was the moment. Brannoc saw it happen and could do nothing. Ostra's eyes went cold and certain, the way a man's eyes go when he chooses the rope over the truth because the rope is cheaper.

"Guards," the Lord Magistrate said. "Take the seditious clerk into custody. Charge: bearing false witness against the crown, incitement against the state's peace. Magistrate Dain will answer for her sponsorship."

The gallery roared. Soldiers moved—not the city's white-cloaked watch but the liveried strangers, moving fast, moving like men who had been waiting for exactly this. Brannoc's hand went to his sword and Wren's hand went to his wrist, iron-tight.

"Not here," she breathed. "Not here, you idiot. Forty of them."

They took Ines between them, and Brannoc watched his friend go—watched him look back once, not at the magistrates, not at the guards, but at the gallery, at the faces of ordinary Meridian citizens who were already looking away, studying their hands, their shoes, the ceiling. Polite. It was all so polite. The arrest, the charges, the murmured relief. Justice failing not with a crash but with a cough, an apology, a vote.

Corvessa Dain stood in the chalk circle alone, straight-backed, as they led her away too.

* * *

They found the old magistrate two days later—or rather, she found them, in the back room of the wine shop, bruised at the temple but unbowed, having talked her way out of house arrest on the strength of forty years of friendships that no longer mattered to anyone in power.

"They'll hold him until the parley concludes," she said. "Then hand him to Vane's people as a gift. A gesture of good faith." She drank her wine like medicine. "I'm sorry, knight. I believed the charter would protect him. I believed the circle meant something."

"It used to," Brannoc said.

"Everything used to." She set down the cup. "Listen to me. The cells under the council hall are old—older than the charter. There's a drainage gallery from the plague years that runs out under the customs house. My grandmother's brother helped build it. Smugglers have used it for generations." Her eyes flicked to Wren. "Some of them may even remember it."

Wren smiled without humor. "I remember it."

"Three nights," Corvessa said. "After three nights they move him to the envoy compound, and then no power under heaven gets him back." She stood, wincing, and gathered her gray robe around her like a flag she refused to lower. "I spent ten years losing votes and calling it duty. Get your clerk out. Make the ledger mean something. Because if it doesn't—" her voice caught, just once— "then everything I was, was nothing at all."

* * *

The night before the escape, Brannoc sat on the wine shop's roof watching Vane's campfires bloom on the far hills like a second constellation. Wren climbed up beside him with two cups of something rough and passed him one.

"You're doing arithmetic," she said.

"I'm counting fires. Fourteen thousand, give or take. And in three days they march on this city whether the council signs or not." He drank. "We get Ines out of a cell and into what? A siege?"

"We get him out and across the Ashfen. That was always the plan."

"The plan was to show the ledger to the world and let the world do the rest." Brannoc shook his head. "I stood in that chamber and I believed it. One entry, and the whole rotten structure falls. That's what the old country taught me—that law is stronger than armies. I watched forty-one magistrates choose surrender over a single sentence of truth, and I still want to believe it."

Wren was quiet a while. Below them, the city glowed with parley lanterns, dressing its wounds in white.

"Maybe the law that lives behind the Doorway is different," she said finally.

"Or maybe it's the same law, older and hungrier."

"Either way," Wren said, "we need him out tomorrow. And Brannoc—" she hesitated, and in the firelight he saw something flicker across her face, guilt or fear or both, gone too fast to name— "there's something you should know about how I got the drainage gallery so easily. About who else knows we're coming."

Brannoc turned. "Wren."

But she was already climbing down toward the dark, saying only, "Be ready at the third bell," and leaving him alone on the roof with fourteen thousand fires and the growing certainty that the price of this rescue had already been paid—to someone.
