## 7. Debt Collectors

The cell in Meridian Freeport's magistracy was built for drunks and debtors, which meant it was built for me twice over. The stone smelled of herring brine and old arguments. Someone had scratched a tally into the wall—forty-one marks, then a forty-second abandoned halfway, as if its maker had lost count or lost hope. I understood both.

They had taken my boots, my belt, and the ledger. Not the map—the map was sewn into the lining of my coat, which they had also taken, but apparently no one had thought to check the seams. Small mercies. Or large ones. In my experience they were usually the same thing wearing different clothes.

I had been in the cell for two days when the screaming started.

Not from my cell. From above. Boots on stairs, the clang of the grate, a guard's voice cut short with a sound like a sack of wet sand hitting a floor. Then the door swung open and Wren Calloway stood in it, holding a ring of keys in one hand and a magistrate's own dagger in the other, looking like every bad decision I had ever loved.

"You," she said, "are the single most expensive man I have ever known."

"Wren—"

"Don't." She tossed something at my chest. My coat. "Put that on. We have maybe four minutes before the night watch realizes their sergeant is asleep in a supply closet and not asleep at his post."

I fumbled the coat on. Her hands were steady; mine were not. Behind her on the stairs stood Ser Brannoc Hale, filling the doorway the way he filled every doorway, holding an unconscious guardsman under one arm like a rolled carpet.

"The magistrate signed your transfer papers this evening," Brannoc said. His voice was flat as slate. "To Vane's provosts. You were leaving at dawn."

The cold went through me like well water. "Then we're later than I thought."

"Later than anyone thought." Wren was already moving down the corridor, keys jingling, unlocking cells as she passed—not to free anyone, just noise, confusion, a trail of open doors for the watch to waste time on. It was a smuggler's trick and a good one. "There's a fish cart at the north gate. You're going to smell like mackerel until the Ashfen, and you're going to be grateful about it."

* * *

We made the gate by the last bell before dawn, wedged among barrels, and made the salt marshes beyond it by full light, and only then did Wren let us stop, because even she wouldn't push horses—or herself—past a certain line. She dropped onto a fallen sea-wall and started cleaning the dagger with the methodical calm of someone who intended to keep using it.

Brannoc stood facing back toward Meridian. From here the city was a gray smudge against a grayer sky, and above the smudge, faint but unmistakable, smoke. Not one fire. Many.

"They're burning the archives first," Brannoc said. "That's what Vane does. Takes a city, burns its records, issues new ones."

"He doesn't need to burn anything," I said. "He owns the council. They'll sign whatever he puts in front of them."

"Signatures are cheaper than fires," Wren agreed. "But he likes the fires. Sends a message." She looked at me over the blade. "So. Days away, you said. How many?"

I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the jail, the arithmetic I hated. Vane's vanguard had crossed the Sere at Corrow Bridge—that much the jailer had gossiped about, because everyone gossips about armies the way sailors gossip about weather. From the Sere to Meridian: six days for infantry, four if he pushed his horse. He would push his horse.

"Three," I said. "Maybe four."

"And the Doorway?"

"Ashfen. Nine days' walk. Six if the fen lets us." I met her eyes. "It won't let us. That's rather the point of it."

Wren sheathed the dagger. "Then we've already lost, and everything from here is theater."

"No," Brannoc said quietly. "We've never been ahead. Every step of this has been losing ground slowly. That's what it means to carry something valuable. Everyone behind you wants it more than you want to arrive." He turned from the city. "We go tonight. Rest now. Argue later."

We rested. I didn't sleep. I lay in the salt grass watching the smoke over Meridian thin into morning haze and thought about the founder.

Every Ledgerkeeper learned his name in the first year: Aldous Ferren, who wrote the guild's first entry and its strangest. Before the Doorway was a court, the story went, it was a wound—and Ferren was the one who stitched it shut, or tried to. The oldest ledgers don't say what he sealed there. They only say what it cost him, and what it cost him was written in a hand that deteriorated across three pages until the final line was barely legible: *Do not collect this debt. Some accounts exist so that they will never be settled.*

For ten years I had read that line as a warning against greed. Lying in the grass with Vane's army three days behind me, I finally heard it correctly. It wasn't a warning against greed. It was a warning against *me*—against whoever came next, carrying the map, needing the Doorway, willing to pay whatever the crossing asked.

* * *

The letter found us anyway. Of course it did.

It was the second night out from Meridian, camped in the ribs of a wrecked tollhouse where the drowned coast gave way to fen. A rider came up the causeway alone, unhurried, carrying a white banner and a lantern, and stopped exactly fifty paces out—the distance of parley, the distance of courtesy, the distance a man keeps when he wants you to know he could be closer if he wished. He set a letter on a milestone, bowed to all three of us equally, and rode back the way he'd come without a word.

Wren wanted to burn it unread. Brannoc wanted to read it and then burn it. I took it, because it was addressed to me—in ink, in a fine secretary hand, with my name spelled correctly, which almost no one managed—and because some part of me, the part I liked least, wanted to know what a king would say to a clerk.

*Iversen,* it began.

*You do not know me, though I have known of you for years. The Ledgerkeepers kept accounts on everyone worth keeping accounts on, and I paid attention to what they wrote. Your master thought you mediocre. He was wrong, as he was wrong about most things, which is why his guild is dying and mine is not.*

*Here is what I know. You hold the map. You have read the ledger. You understand that my crown was purchased, and you believe this makes it illegitimate. Let us dispense with the fiction: every crown is purchased. Mine simply has better documentation.*

*What I offer is not a bribe. It is a position. Royal Ledgerkeeper. The office has been vacant since I burned the last man who held it, and I find I miss having one honest account of myself in the world. You will live in comfort. You will have access to every record of the old country—which I am told you want—and you will be the only man in my kingdom whose books I cannot audit. You will be invisible no longer. You will be necessary.*

*Consider what happens otherwise. My army reaches Meridian in three days. After Meridian, the Ashfen. I will reach the Doorway whether you open it or not—I have other maps, worse ones, and men to die testing them. But you can spare those men. You can spare the free cities a hard occupation. One signature from you, and I become legitimate by the only law that ever mattered, and everyone lives.*

*Come to me. Or send the map. Either will be accepted. Neither will be forgotten.*

*—A.V.*

I read it twice. Then I handed it to Wren, because I couldn't trust my own voice.

She read it faster than I had, lips moving slightly. When she finished she didn't burn it. She folded it along its creases, very precisely, and put it in her coat, and I understood that she was keeping evidence, and I understood what kind, and I said nothing, because I still owed her honesty I hadn't yet paid.

"He's clever," Brannoc said. "The offer is real. That's the trap of it. A lie you can refuse. This—" he tapped the letter's memory through Wren's coat—"this is a door left open on purpose."

"It's worse than that." I stared into our small fire. "He's right. About all of it. Every crown is bought. The ledger proves nothing except that Vane is honest about the one thing every other king lies about. If I take his offer, I'm not betraying anything. That's what makes it rot. It would feel like coming home."

Wren looked at me across the fire, and for a moment the years between us weren't there. "And the other thing? The thing you're not saying?"

I should have lied. Ten years of keeping secrets had made me excellent at it. Instead I heard myself say: "He offered me visibility. Importance. A place where the work matters. And some part of me wanted it, Wren. The same part that chose the guild over you. It always wants the same thing—to matter to something larger than myself. And it will kill me, or worse, it will make me useful to men like him until I die of it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the fen, something large moved through water and went quiet.

"So we refuse," Brannoc said. Simply. As if refusal were a wall you could lean against.

"We refuse," I said. "And we cross the Ashfen. And when the Doorway opens, we learn what Ferren sealed, and we pray it's a court and not the thing the ledgers were afraid of."

Wren pulled the letter back out, held it over the fire, and let it catch. We watched Aldric Vane's careful handwriting curl and blacken and lift apart into sparks.

"One condition," she said, when the last of it was ash. "When this is done—if any of us is done—you tell me the whole truth of why you left. Not the version you rehearsed. The real one. I've earned it. So have you."

"You'll have it," I said. "On the other side of the fen."

"Then we'd best get to the other side."

Beyond the dead fire, the Ashfen waited under the stars—a black country of reeds and standing water, breathing slow fog, hiding whatever Ferren had bound beneath its surface. Three days behind us, an army marched on a burning city. Ahead of us, the oldest debt in the world sat waiting to be collected.

I banked the coals and picked up my pack, and we walked into the dark together.
