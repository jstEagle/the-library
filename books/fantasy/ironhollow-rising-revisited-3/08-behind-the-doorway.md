## 8. Behind the Doorway

The Ashfen did not want us crossing, and it made no secret of it.

We walked three days through country that had burned a generation ago and never bothered to heal. The ground was grey ash packed hard as slate, cracked into plates that shifted underfoot with a sound like crockery. Nothing grew. Nothing even tried. Once, near dusk of the second day, we passed the ribcage of something enormous jutting from the fen like the frame of a ship, and Brannoc said quietly, "That's not an animal," and none of us asked him what else it might be.

Wren Calloway does not spook easily. I have run cargoes past navy cutters in fog so thick you could chew it; I once talked my way out of a Vane prison with nothing but a forged manifest and a smile. But the Ashfen got under my skin the way debt gets under yours—slowly, then all at once. Because I knew what I was carrying besides the map's secret. I knew about the letter sewn into my coat lining, the one Vane's spymaster had given me before we ever sailed: *Bring me the map and the man who carries it, and your name is clean.* Every mile east, it weighed more. Every mile east, Ines paid some small kindness to a stranger—a coin to a scavenger child, a word of comfort to a dying hermit—and every kindness was another stone on the scale against me.

He never mentioned it. That was the worst part. He just kept paying his debts like the world was still a ledger worth balancing, and I kept my hand pressed flat over my coat lining like a woman guarding a wound.

On the third day, the map led us to a door standing alone in the ash.

* * *

It shouldn't have been there. That was the first thing. A free-standing doorframe, tall as two men, black wood gone silver at the edges, set into nothing—no wall, no ruin, no foundation. Just a doorway in the middle of the dead fen, its lintel carved with characters older than any alphabet I could read, though Ines read them aloud in a whisper like he was reciting a prayer he'd only half-learned.

"The guild's first mark," he said. "Before they were Ledgerkeepers. Before there was a guild." His fingers hovered over the carving without touching it. "This is where they started counting."

"Counting what?" Brannoc asked.

Ines didn't answer right away. He'd gone pale in a way I recognized—I'd seen it on men reading their own death warrants. "Everything," he said finally. "I think they started by counting everything."

The map's final instruction was simple: *Open as a debtor opens—by admitting what is owed.* Ines stood before the door for a long moment, and I watched him gather himself the way a man gathers himself before confessing to a priest. Then he spoke, quiet but clear, to a door in a wasteland:

"I owe a debt I cannot pay. The last apprentice of the Ledgerkeepers requests entry to settle accounts."

For a heartbeat, nothing. The wind moved ash across the fen in long grey snakes. I remember thinking, absurdly, *good, it's just a door, we can turn around, I can burn this letter and—*

Then the space inside the doorframe filled with light.

Not firelight, not sunlight. Something older and more patient, the color of parchment held up to a window. It poured out across the ash without warming anything, and where it touched, the ash *remembered*. Grass ghosted up green and faded. A road surfaced beneath our feet, cobbles worn smooth by centuries of feet, running away through the doorframe into country I had no name for.

Brannoc drew his sword. Old habit. Ines put a hand on his arm.

"It's not a trap," Ines said. "It's a summons."

"There's a difference?"

"A trap wants something from you. A summons wants you *there*."

I looked at Ines—at this thin, careful man who had spent ten years invisible in a counting-house nobody visited—and felt something twist in my chest that had nothing to do with the letter in my coat. He looked terrified. He also looked like he was going to walk through anyway, and he was going to do it because it was owed, and gods help me, I loved him for exactly the thing that was going to break him.

"Well," I said, "someone has to keep you out of trouble." And I stepped through first, because if the old country wanted a debtor, it could damn well start with a smuggler.

* * *

Inside was the country that had officially stopped existing ten years ago.

Hills. Actual hills, green as a promise, rolling down to a river valley where a city stood whole and lit—towers, bridges, market squares strung with lanterns, smoke rising from ten thousand hearths. The sky above it was evening, though behind us the Ashfen sat under a grey noon. Air moved through the doorframe in one direction only: inward, a slow breath the land took and never gave back.

There were people. That undid me worse than the magic. Farmers driving carts along the ghost-road, nodding to us as they passed as if three strangers stepping out of a burning wasteland were a Tuesday. Children chasing a dog. An old woman selling pears who pressed one into Ines's hand and refused payment, saying, "You'll be wanting your strength for the sitting."

"The sitting?" Brannoc asked.

"The court," she said, as if we were fools. "It's been in session eleven years. They're waiting on the case."

We followed the road down because there was nowhere else to go, and because every step inward, I felt the Ashfen letter burning colder against my ribs. The city resolved as we came closer into something both familiar and wrong—the architecture of the old country, the one from the histories Brannoc muttered over at campfires—but *maintained*. Fresh paint. Swept streets. Bells ringing the hour from towers that should have been rubble and memory.

"They never left," Ines breathed. "When the country 'fell,' when Vane took the throne—they didn't die out. They just closed the doors and kept the courts running."

"Waiting for what?" I asked, though I already knew.

"For someone to bring the case," Ines said. "Eleven years, and no one with standing ever came. You can't sue a king from inside his own kingdom. You need someone outside the law—" he laughed, a small broken sound, "—and apparently that's me."

The courthouse stood at the city's heart, and it was less a building than a verdict given architecture: white stone, columns like raised fingers, doors open onto a hall where a hundred benches rose in tiers toward a high seat. And the benches were full. Judges—hundreds of them, robed in grey, faces lined and patient as tree bark—sat in perfect silence, and every head turned when we entered.

Not hostile. Worse than hostile. *Expectant.*

A judge descended—old, woman, eyes like the inside of a ledger, blank pages waiting. She stopped before Ines and inclined her head.

"You bring the case of Aldric Vane."

"I—" Ines's voice cracked. He steadied it. "I bring his account. Purchased crown, unpaid principal, interest accruing eleven years. The Doorway stands as collateral."

"Then the court accepts jurisdiction." She turned slightly, addressing the tiers, and her voice carried without effort to every ear. "Let the record show: after eleven years and one hundred and forty days, a claimant has come. The debtor king will be summoned to answer."

A sound went through the hall—not applause, something deeper, a settling, like a house whose foundations had finally stopped shifting. Brannoc's hand found my shoulder. I realized I was shaking.

But the judge wasn't finished. She turned back to Ines, and now her face did something I will remember until I die: it softened, the way you soften toward someone you're about to hurt.

"Understand the terms, claimant. The old country's law lives because someone tends it. For eleven years, the bench has tended itself, but a self-tending court cannot *act*. It can judge only when a case is brought—and cases are brought by officers of the law." She paused. "Whoever brings the case must remain. As the country's final officer. Until the law is either restored to the world or formally ended. There is no term. There is no successor provision. Forever, or the death of the law, whichever comes last."

The silence afterward had weight, like deep water.

"That's the price," Brannoc said flatly. "You walk in here, you carry the case, and you never walk out."

"The claimant may withdraw," the judge said. "The court will wait another eleven years. It has waited longer."

And I watched Ines stand there in that impossible hall, in front of all that patient, buried justice, and I saw him do the arithmetic he was born to do. I saw him weigh forever against a usurper's armies marching on the free cities. I saw him start to arrive at the only answer his kind of man could reach.

Over my dead body.

"No," I said.

Every grey head turned to me. Fine. I'd been stared at by worse.

"He doesn't decide alone," I told the judge. "He travels with people. People who—" my voice did something humiliating, wobbled, "—people who owe him. And people he owes. That's how ledgers work, isn't it? Debts run both directions?"

The judge regarded me for a long moment. Something flickered behind those blank-page eyes—amusement, maybe, or recognition.

"The court hears all claims," she said. "Even irregular ones."

"Then hear this one." I pulled the letter from my coat lining, and my hands shook, and I let them. "Because if he's staying forever, he deserves to know what kind of people he's staying with. This is a warrant from Aldric Vane's spymaster. My amnesty, signed and sealed, in exchange for delivering him and the map to Vane's men." I held it out. "I never spent it. But I carried it. Enter it in whatever record you keep."

The hall was absolutely silent. Ines was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read—hurt and relief tangled together so tightly I couldn't tell which was winning.

"Why?" he asked quietly. "Why tell me *here*?"

"Because in there—" I nodded back toward the door, toward the Ashfen, toward the world, "—I could still have lied my way clear. Here, lying feels expensive." I swallowed. "And because if you're going to trade your whole life to fix a broken country, you shouldn't do it for a smuggler who was already halfway sold."

The old judge took the letter, read it, and did something extraordinary: she filed it. Produced a ledger from her sleeve, wrote a line, sealed it. "Entered," she said. "A confessed debt weighs lighter than a concealed one. The court notes the claimant's companion has settled with herself, if not yet with others." Her gaze slid to Ines. "The terms stand. The claimant must decide."

"And the summons?" Brannoc asked. "How does a dead country summon a living king?"

The judge almost smiled.

"The same way any creditor summons a debtor. We send notice." She looked at Ines. "The court requires an officer to serve papers. One journey, outside these walls, to the debtor's own threshold. After that—" her voice gentled, "—the officer returns, and remains."

One journey. One last walk in the world, carrying a summons to a king who commanded armies and murdered couriers and would very much like Ines Iversen dead before any paper touched his hand.

Ines looked at me. At Brannoc. At the hundred benches of patient judges and the green hills visible through windows that had no business existing.

"One journey," he repeated. "To serve Vane himself."

"To serve Vane himself," the judge agreed. "Choose your escort wisely, Officer. The debtor does not welcome his creditors."

And far away, faint but unmistakable, carried on that one-way inward breath of air, we heard it: horns. Many horns, from beyond the Ashfen, from the direction of the free cities.

Vane's armies were moving.

And somewhere in that grey host, riding toward the Doorway he'd spent a decade trying to find, came the debtor king—to collect his collateral, never dreaming it had already opened, and that inside, the whole country he thought he'd killed was waiting, patiently, with paperwork.
