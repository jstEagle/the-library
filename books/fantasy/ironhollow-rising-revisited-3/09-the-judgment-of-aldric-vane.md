## 9. The Judgment of Aldric Vane

The letter went out at dawn, carried by a boy on a borrowed horse, and by noon the whole Ashfen knew that Ines Iversen had summoned the King.

*Come to the Doorway,* it said. *Bring your crown. I will forgive your debt.*

It was, he reflected as he waited among the standing stones, either the bravest or the stupidest entry he would ever make in a ledger. Forgiveness. Every other currency had failed—gold Vane could mint, legitimacy Vane could manufacture, fear Vane could command. But a debt forgiven could only be given by the one who held it, and the guild's oldest ledger named Aldric Vane as its final debtor. The old country's law did not recognize his crown. It recognized what he owed.

Wren crouched at the base of the Doorway, checking the ropes she'd rigged through its frame—not to hold it open, he understood now, but to hold it *shut* if things went wrong. Brannoc stood on the ridge with an unstrung bow across his knees, watching the plain below where Vane's army had made camp three days ago, close enough to march, far enough to pretend they hadn't come for murder.

"He'll bring a hundred men," Brannoc said without turning.

"He'll bring ten," Ines said. "A king doesn't come to a surrender with witnesses."

"And if he comes to make one?"

Ines touched the ledger under his coat, the oldest book, the one whose pages smelled of dust and candle smoke and something older than both. "Then we learn whether the law behind this door still has teeth."

* * *

Vane came at dusk, and he came with ten.

He was not what the broadsheets promised. No tyrant's bulk, no usurper's swagger. He was a lean man in a plain black coat, gray at the temples, with the tired eyes of someone who had been awake for ten years straight. He dismounted himself, waved off his guards, and walked up the fen path alone until he stood before the Doorway—the great stone arch, half-swallowed by ash-gray moss, humming faintly like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

"You're younger than I expected," Vane said.

"You're older." Ines stayed where he was, the ledger open in his hands. Behind him, Wren had drifted left along the arch; Brannoc had drifted right. He didn't look at either of them. Looking would tell Vane where to aim.

"Your letter said forgiveness." Vane's voice was level, almost gentle. "I've had priests offer me absolution. Chancellors offer me erasure. You're the first to offer me arithmetic. So let's do the sum, Master Iversen. What does the ledger say I owe?"

"Everything you have," Ines said. "You bought your crown on credit from a country that officially doesn't exist. The collateral was the Doorway. The interest has compounded for ten years. By the guild's reckoning, you owe the old country its whole self back—including the part of you that signed."

Something flickered in Vane's face. Not anger. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own name spoken correctly for the first time in a decade.

"I was twenty-three," Vane said quietly. "The free cities were eating each other. Someone had to sign. Someone always has to sign." He stepped closer. "And now you're telling me there's a court behind that stone that can take it all back."

"I'm telling you the debt is real. What happens next is between you and the judges."

"The judges are dead."

"The judges are *waiting.*"

For a long moment the only sound was the wind moving through the fen grass, and beneath it, the hum of the Doorway, patient as interest accruing.

Then Vane smiled, and it was the smile of a man who had bought everything once and believed he could buy everything twice. "Or," he said, "there is another settlement. You hold the ledger. I hold the armies. Marry the two and we don't need judges at all. Be my Royal Ledgerkeeper—I offered once before. Now I offer more. Co-rule. Half of everything. A country rebuilt on honest accounts, yours and mine. You've seen what the free cities are—councils that arrest truth-tellers, magistrates who'd rather surrender than be embarrassed. They don't deserve the law you're carrying. We could *give* it to them."

Behind him, Ines heard Wren's breath catch. Not fear. Something worse—the sound of a woman calculating whether the man she loved might actually say yes.

And the terrible thing, the thing Ines would carry for the rest of his life, was how much he wanted to. Half of everything. A world where no account went unread. Where the dying courier at the counting-house door would have had somewhere to go.

"No," Ines said.

"Because of them?" Vane glanced at Wren, at Brannoc, dismissive. "Sentiment is a bad collateral, Master Iversen."

"Because of the courier." Ines turned the ledger toward him, to the newest page, written in his own cramped hand. "A man bled out on my doorstep holding a map so this debt could be collected. He didn't die for co-rule. He died because some debts have to be paid in full, publicly, or they're never really paid at all. You want forgiveness? It's here. All you have to do is stand trial for it."

Vane looked at the ledger for a long time. Then he laughed—a short, exhausted sound, almost admiring. "You know what I was told, when I signed? That the old country was a story. That ledgers were paper and paper burns." He drew a breath. "Fine. Let's see if the story collects."

He raised his voice. "I, Aldric Vane, submit myself to judgment."

The Doorway rang.

Not hummed—*rang*, a deep bronze bell-note that rolled across the fen and flattened the grass and sent Vane's ten guards stumbling backward down the path. The moss on the arch burned away like fog in sunlight, and through the stone came light the color of candleflame in a sealed room, and then figures—robed, faceless, their features worn smooth as river stones, seven of them, seated in air where no benches stood.

The court of the old country convened over the battlefield, and every soldier in Vane's army, three thousand strong across the plain, felt the note pass through their chests and stopped pretending they hadn't marched here for murder.

* * *

The proceedings took no time at all, because the evidence had already been entered.

That was the thing about ledgers, Ines realized as he stood before the smooth-faced judges: they never argue. They simply state. He read aloud—the purchase of the crown, the terms of credit, the collateral, the compounding years—and the judges' silence absorbed each line like sand absorbing water. Vane stood through it with his head high, and Ines gave him that: he did not flinch, did not interrupt, did not send his guards forward when Brannoc's hand went to his bow. Whatever else he was, the man had the courage of his signature.

When Ines finished, the middle judge spoke, in a voice like pages turning.

*"The claim of Aldric Vane upon the crown of the old country is found to be purchased, not earned, and defaulted in full. The crown is void. The debt stands collected."*

Across the plain, three thousand soldiers heard it too. Not the words—they were too far for words—but the *fact*, the way you feel a verdict in your spine. Ines watched it move through the army like wind through wheat. Men who had crossed the Ashfen for a king's gold looked at each other. Officers looked at their banners. Somewhere a standard-bearer lowered his pole without being ordered to, and no one raised it again.

Legitimacy, Ines understood, watching, was just an account everyone agreed to keep. And the agreement had just been closed.

Vane stood very still in front of the Doorway. When he finally spoke, his voice was almost a whisper. "And me? What does your court do with the debtor?"

The middle judge turned its smooth face toward Ines.

*"The creditor holds the debt. The creditor determines payment."*

Every eye—mortal and otherwise—came to Ines. Wren's hand found the small of his back. Brannoc shifted his weight, ready for anything.

Ines looked at Aldric Vane, at the tired eyes and the plain black coat and the twenty-three-year-old who had signed because someone always has to, and he said:

"The debt is forgiven. Paid instead by truth: your crown is void, your claim is ended, and you will live the rest of your life as an ordinary man among the people you ruled, and let them decide what to do with you. That is the sentence. That is also the mercy."

The judges' silence stretched. Then, one by one, the seven smooth faces inclined—an acknowledgment, a closing of books.

*"So entered,"* said the middle judge. *"The court remains in session. The office of Keeper remains open. Come inside, Ledgerkeeper, and be sealed into the law forever—"*

"No," Ines said, and reached for the ledger's final blank page.

But that, and the pen, and the price of the last line—that belongs to the next entry.
