## 2. What the Bones Owe

The apprentice's name was Sefa, and it cost the guildmaster nothing to give it away.

August learned this the way she learned most things that mattered — not from anyone's mouth, but from the small sounds the world made when it was lying. She was at her workbench on the morning it happened, three floors below the guildmaster's office, balancing the autumn ledger against a tray of bone tokens that had come in overnight from a widow in Copperlane. The tokens were her mother's design: hollowed knucklebones drilled with a single hole each, so a voice could pass through them like wind through a flute. August held one to her ear and listened to the dead woman inside it complain about the price of candles, and while she listened, she heard footsteps cross the office above her — the guildmaster's tread, heavy and deliberate, and beneath it the lighter, quicker step of someone wearing government boots.

She heard the guildmaster say a name.

She heard him say *Sefa*.

The token went cold in August's hand. Not metaphorically — the bone actually cooled, the way it always did when the dead within it flinched away from something living. The widow's voice thinned to a thread and stopped complaining about candles. Above her, the government boots shifted their weight, and there was the scratch of a pen, and then the lighter step descended the stairs, unhurried, satisfied, and passed out through the guildhouse door into the street where the morning market was setting up its stalls as though the world were still an ordinary place.

August sat with the ledger open in front of her and did not move for a long time. The entry she had been writing — *four tokens, one damaged, credited* — sat unfinished under her nib, the ink drying into a small brown scar on the page.

Sefa was fourteen. Sefa could hear iron rusting, which sounded to her like screaming, and so she wore wool gloves even in summer and had once cried for an hour after being made to hold a nail. Sefa had been with the guild two years. Sefa swept the token room on Thursdays and hummed while she worked, badly, cheerfully, a tuneless little song about a fisherman's daughter that she'd invented herself because she couldn't carry anyone else's.

And now her name was written in a crown clerk's hand, and the scratch of that pen was still ringing in August's ears like a bell struck once and never allowed to stop.

*You could go up there,* said the widow's voice, faintly, from the token in August's fist. The dead always knew when the living were arguing with themselves; they found it tedious. *You could ask him why.*

"And what would he say?" August murmured. "That we have forty members to feed. That the auditor comes back in a month. That one girl is cheaper than all of us." She set the token down gently, the way you set down something sleeping. "He wouldn't even be lying."

That was the worst of it. That was the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone for the rest of the day: the arithmetic was sound. August Zhou had kept the guild solvent for six years by understanding exactly this kind of arithmetic — the cost of wax, the cost of bread, the going rate for grief. She had never once let sentiment unbalance a column. And here was the guildmaster doing the same work she did, at a larger scale, with a larger ledger, and the entry read: *one apprentice, gifted, expendable — against forty lives, continued.*

The books balanced. They always balanced. That was the horror of books.

*

They came for Sefa at dusk, when the lamps were lit but the streets still held a little grey light, the hour when the city looked softest and behaved worst. August heard the cart before she saw it — wheels with iron rims, and iron, these days, sang to everyone whether they were gifted or not. It came up Token Street too fast for a delivery, and it stopped in front of the guildhouse, and two men in the queen's grey got down without speaking to anyone.

August was at the window of the workroom. Below, Sefa came out onto the steps with her sweeping broom still in her hands, because Thursday was nearly over and she hadn't finished the token room, and she stood there blinking at the grey men the way you blink at weather that has turned without warning.

"Sefa Aldous?" one of them said, reading from a paper.

"That's me," Sefa said. Then, helpfully, because she was fourteen and honest and had no idea yet what honesty cost: "Is it about my hearing? Because I've been better about the gloves—"

The men took her arms. There was no violence in it. That was somehow worse than violence would have been — the practiced, bored efficiency of it, the way they folded her into the cart like clerks filing a document. Sefa didn't struggle. She looked back at the guildhouse once, at the lit windows, at the faces gathered behind the glass of the workroom where August stood, and her expression wasn't fear yet. It was confusion. It was the look of someone waiting for the adults to explain the rules, certain that any moment now someone would say *stop, there's been a mistake*, because surely the world ran on the same careful bookkeeping she'd been taught, surely every debt was recorded, surely—

The cart rolled away south, toward the river, toward the prison barges the crown had moored there since the hunt began. August watched until the sound of the iron wheels faded below the noise of the market packing up for the night.

Nobody in the workroom spoke. Old Merrin poured himself tea he didn't drink. The twins, who catalogued grave-goods, stood shoulder to shoulder by the shelf of memorial plaques as if hoping the dead would offer advice. Finally the guildmaster came down the stairs, and he looked older than he had that morning, and he said, in the voice he used for closing accounts:

"It was necessary."

August turned from the window. She found, to her surprise, that she was not angry in the way she expected to be angry — hot and loud and useless. What she felt was colder and more precise, the feeling she got when an account refused to reconcile no matter how many times she checked it: the certainty that somewhere, a figure had been entered wrongly, and everything built on top of it would have to come down.

"You sold her," August said.

"I saved thirty-nine people."

"You sold her."

"Zhou." He rubbed his eyes. "The auditor had names already. Half of them wrong. I gave him one true name so the other half would stay wrong. You think I slept last night? You think I'll sleep tomorrow? But if the guild goes down, every gifted soul in this house goes into a cart, and not one of them is as young or as—" he searched for the word and settled, horribly, on it— "*as cheap* as she was."

Cheap. August held the word in her mouth and tasted its weight, and understood that he was right, and hated him, and hated more that she understood.

"The ledgers," she said quietly. "All of them. Mine included. You understand what happens next month, don't you? When the auditor comes back and asks whose handwriting balances the token accounts. Whose records show payments only a listener could verify." She saw it land in him, the flicker behind his eyes. "My books are evidence, guildmaster. Yours are confessions. We're not saving thirty-nine people. We're deciding the order in which we're taken."

He had no answer for that. He looked at her for a long moment — and August realized, with a strange detachment, that he was memorizing her face, the way you memorize a figure before closing a page — and then he climbed back up the stairs, and the door shut above her, and the lock turned.

The lock turned. She noted that too.

*

The ghost arrived at midnight.

August knew it was coming before it came; the whole guildhouse had gone quiet in the wrong way, the way a room goes quiet when a door opens somewhere far off. The tokens on their rack were restless, clicking softly against each other like teeth. She sat up in her narrow bed in the room behind the workroom, and she waited, and at half past the hour the air at the foot of her bed grew cold and dense, and Sefa stepped out of it.

Not a ghost, quite. Not yet. The girl was alive somewhere on a prison barge on the river — August was sure of it, sure with the deep certainty of her gift — but her voice had already left her. Fear will do that; the crown's cages were built to do exactly that. What stood at the foot of August's bed was Sefa's voice, wearing the shape of Sefa the way smoke wears the shape of a chimney, and it was furious, and it was terrified, and it was fourteen years old.

*"You knew."*

The voice didn't sound in the ears. It sounded in the bones, in the marrow, in the places August had spent her whole life learning to listen. She didn't get up. Getting up would have been a kindness to herself, something to do with her hands.

"You knew," the voice said again, and now it was crying, and the crying resonated through the floorboards and set every token on the rack in the next room ringing. "You balance the books. You know everything that comes through this house. You heard him say my name, didn't you? I can tell. Your face does a thing when you hear things. You do it right now."

"Yes," August said. "I heard."

*"And you let them take me."*

"Yes."

The silence that followed was enormous. Somewhere across the city a clock tower counted twelve, and the dead in the walls counted with it, and then stopped, and waited, because even the dead wanted to hear what August Zhou would say next.

"I'm going to tell you the truth," August said, "because everyone else in your life has decided you're cheap, and I won't be one of them. Yes, I heard. No, I didn't stop it. I told myself the same arithmetic he told himself — that one name buys forty safeties — and I let myself believe it because believing it meant I didn't have to burn anything down tonight. And then I watched the cart take you, and I understood that the arithmetic doesn't work. It has never worked. Every name they take makes the hunt stronger, and the stronger the hunt, the more names it needs. We are not buying time, Sefa. We are feeding it."

The ghost-shape flickered. Anger and fear fighting for the same small space.

*"Then what are you going to do?"*

August got up. She dressed in the dark, in travelling clothes, in the coat with the deep pockets, and while she dressed she talked, because talking was the only payment she had to offer and she intended to overpay.

"There's a ledger in the guildmaster's strongbox," she said. "Not the trade ledger. The old one. The covenant ledger — the record of the pact between the living realm and Ironhollow, written before either kingdom had its name. My grandmother helped copy it. I've read it since I was a child, and I understood maybe a tenth of it, and I thought the rest was poetry." She pulled the coat on. "It isn't poetry. It's terms. And I think — I think the hunt isn't hunting us, Sefa. I think we're the loose threads. I think they're cutting the covenant apart strand by strand, and every gifted person in a cage is a strand cut, and if they finish, there won't be a land of the dead to hear anymore. There'll just be silence, harvested and owned."

*"How do you know?"* the voice whispered.

"Because the dead have been whispering about it for a month, and I've been too busy balancing books to listen properly." She took up her satchel, her picks, her small knife, the wax stylus she'd used since apprenticeship. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry isn't enough and I know it. Maybe someday you'll hate me slightly less. Maybe you won't, and that will be fair. But I'm going to get you back, and I'm going to make them return every voice they've stolen, and I'm going to need the covenant ledger to learn how."

The shape of Sefa wavered at the window, thinning already, stretched between the barge on the river and the girl it belonged to. Before it went, it said one more thing, softer than the rest, and it was the thing August would carry through every chapter that followed:

*"I hummed while I swept. Did you ever hear me? Nobody ever listened."*

"I heard you," August said. "Every Thursday. It was a good song."

The window emptied. The cold went out of the room like a tide withdrawing.

*

The guildmaster's study smelled of lamp oil and old decisions. The strongbox was behind the map of the northern graves, and the lock was good, but August had balanced this guild's accounts for six years and knew to the copper what her employer valued, and what he valued least was anything he couldn't spend. The covenant ledger lay at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than paper had any right to be, its cover stamped with a symbol she had traced absently a hundred times without asking questions: two interlocking rings, one of breath, one of bone.

She took it. She left, on the strongbox shelf, in her own meticulous hand, a single line of accounting — the first dishonest entry of her career, and the truest:

*Borrowed against a debt unpaid. Interest accruing. — A.Z.*

Then she went down the back stairs, past the token room where Sefa's broom still leaned in its corner, out through the kitchen garden where the night soil smelled of rosemary, over the wall, and into the dark streets running south toward the river, toward the marsh country beyond it, toward whatever waited where the maps went thin.

Behind her the guildhouse windows glowed, warm and doomed. Ahead of her the road was black, and somewhere along it, she would later learn, a disgraced cartographer was also running — but that was a debt the night had not yet introduced.

The covenant ledger pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and August Zhou walked into the dark owing everything, and intending, for the first time in her careful life, to pay it all.
