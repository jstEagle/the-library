## 4. The Toll Road

Wren had drawn a thousand roads in his life, and he had never once thought to ask what they wanted.

This one announced itself before he saw it: a straight pale seam through the marsh grass, running north-northwest toward the hills where the bells of Ironhollow were said to rust in their towers. No cart ruts. No milestones. Just a road that looked like it had been *decided* rather than built, the way a scar is decided by a wound.

"Stop," August said.

He stopped. Behind them, three days back now, the hunt's riders would be finding the burned page and the empty workbench and the trail he'd spent two nights carefully misdirecting — his last act of competence as a royal servant, spent on treason. His hands still shook when he thought about it, which was constantly.

"What is it?" he asked.

She was standing very still, her satchel clutched against her ribs like a child. The covenant ledger inside it made a faint sound when she moved, a papery whisper that Wren had begun to suspect was not entirely papery. "The road," she said. "Listen."

He listened. Wind in the reeds. Water moving somewhere beneath the peat. And underneath both, so faint he might have imagined it, a sound like coins being counted — slow, patient, endless.

"It's a toll road," she said. "My grandmother's stories called them the old king's roads. They run between here and the border country, and they don't take coin." She turned to look at him, and her face in the grey morning light was the face of someone doing arithmetic she hated. "They take memory."

* * *

They argued about it for an hour, there at the edge of the road, while the marsh steamed around them and the counting-sound went on beneath their feet like a heartbeat under floorboards.

"We go around," Wren said. "I have maps. I can find another way north."

"Your maps are why we're being hunted." August said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "And every other way north adds six days. The hunt adds none. We take the road."

"And pay with what? You don't know what it takes. It could take everything."

"It takes what you can spare," said a voice from the reeds.

Wren's hand went to his knife. August's hand went to his wrist and held it down.

The dead stood in the shallows where the water was too shallow to hide anything living. There were three of them, or perhaps one of them seen three ways — a woman in a coat rotted to lace, a man whose face was all weather, a child who was mostly suggestion. Their outlines shivered like reflections on disturbed water. The woman spoke again, her voice arriving not through the air but through some older channel, directly behind Wren's eyes.

"The road is honest. That's more than either of you can say."

"We're not liars," Wren began, and stopped, because the child laughed — a small bright sound like a dropped bell — and the laughter knew him. Knew about the maps he'd altered. Knew about the fortress at Coldharbour Row whose eastern wing he'd left off the survey at the minister's instruction, and the people who had gone into that wing and never come out, and how he'd told himself it was just ink, just lines, just following orders from men who owned his commission and his debts.

"Yes," the woman said gently. "You are. Both of you. That's why the road will let you pass."

August's grip on his wrist tightened. "What does it cost?" she asked the dead. "Exactly."

"Exactly?" The man's weather-face turned toward her. "It takes what you give it. A memory freely offered buys passage freely given. A memory hoarded is taken anyway, and taken badly — torn out with its roots, and the roots are always tangled in other things." He smiled, or the weather arranged itself into something like a smile. "Pay honestly, speaker's-daughter. Pay small, and often, and choose your losses before they choose you."

Then they were gone, or the light changed, or Wren blinked; however it happened, the reeds were only reeds, and the road lay pale and patient before them, waiting to be paid.

* * *

The first mile cost Wren the smell of his mother's kitchen.

He didn't decide to give it up. He walked onto the road, felt something lean against him like a customs officer's hand, and understood that payment was being requested, and reached into himself for something he could live without — and the kitchen was simply *gone*. Not the memory of the kitchen; the memory remained, but flat, a drawing of a room instead of a room. Bread and bay leaves and tallow smoke: nothing. He stood in the middle of the road grieving a smell and could not even remember what he was grieving for, only that there was a hole shaped like warmth where something used to be.

Beside him, August made a small sound.

"What did it take?" he asked.

"A song." Her voice was steady in the way of someone holding a door shut against wind. "My mother used to sing while she worked the ledgers. I don't—" She frowned, testing the absence. "I remember that she sang. I don't remember how it went."

They walked. The road unspooled ahead of them through country that grew stranger mile by mile — the grass silvering, the sky taking on the color of pewter, occasional shapes in the distance that might have been trees or might have been gallows or might have been bell-frames with no bells. And every mile, the road leaned against them again, patient as tide.

Wren learned to pay quickly and cheaply. The name of a tavern keeper in Lowmarsh who'd once cheated him. The taste of the wine he'd drunk the night he passed his cartographer's exams. The face of a girl he'd kissed behind the survey office and never thought of since — no, that one the road refused, pushed back at him like a coin too worn to spend, because apparently he *did* think of her, more than he'd admitted, and the road only took what was truly spare.

August paid differently. She paid in her mother, in installments, and each time she did it Wren watched her age slightly and then set her jaw and walk on.

"You could stop," he said, on the second day, after she'd given up the memory of her mother's hands — the specific way they'd folded cloth, he gathered, though he couldn't have said how he knew. "We could leave the road. Go around."

"There is no around. You know there's no around." She kept walking. "And besides—" She stopped herself.

"Besides what?"

"Nothing."

But later, at the fireless camp the road permitted them — fires were apparently a debt the road wouldn't cover — she sat with her knees drawn up and told him, staring into the dark where flames should have been.

"When my mother died, everyone told me the grief would fade. That's what grief does, they said. It fades. And it did, and I hated that more than anything. Because she was the only person who ever heard me — really heard me, before I knew I needed hearing — and the world just... let her get quieter in me. Year by year. Like a coin wearing smooth." She turned her head. In the dark her eyes caught light that wasn't there. "So I'm paying the road in her on purpose. While I still get to choose which parts go. Do you understand? It's the only way I've ever been able to keep anything of hers — by deciding how to lose it."

Wren thought about the kitchen smell, already gone, already unmournable. "No," he said quietly. "But I believe you."

* * *

On the third day the road began to climb, and the dead came out to walk beside them.

Not the three from the edge of the marsh. Others. Dozens. They kept pace just beyond the corner of vision, and when Wren turned his head fully they were never quite there, but their voices arrived anyway, layered like sediment:

*—the strand frays at Coldharbour—*

*—they think it's broken, fools, broken things don't scream—*

*—tell the speaker. Tell the speaker it isn't broken. Tell her it's being eaten—*

August stopped walking. "Eaten," she repeated. "What's eating it?"

The voices overlapped, urgent now, the way a crowd gets urgent:

*—the engine—*

*—the crown's engine—*

*—it drinks the gifted and chews the covenant and the queen's man calls it duty—*

*—strand by strand, speaker, strand by strand—*

"They're talking about the prison-fortresses," Wren said, and his own voice came out wrong, thin, because he knew exactly what they meant. He had drawn those fortresses. Not omitted this time — drawn, surveyed, rendered in beautiful precise ink: the walls, the wards, the deep rooms where the crown housed the gifted it collected. He'd been told they were sanctuaries. Protective custody, the minister's letters had said, for the safety of the realm and the gifted alike.

He had believed it, or had arranged his believing so carefully that belief and cowardice were indistinguishable, and he had never once asked what the deep rooms were *for*.

"Wren." August was looking at him. "You've gone white."

"I mapped them." The confession came out of him like a tooth coming loose — awful, and then suddenly, shockingly, a relief. "Coldharbour Row. Vell's Keep. The one under Harrowgate they don't put on any list. I drew every wall, every ward-line, every approach. I told myself it was sanctuary. I told myself—" He laughed, and it wasn't a laugh. "I'm a cartographer. I know what a cage looks like on paper. I've known since the first survey. I drew the bars and labeled them *protection*."

August said nothing for a long moment. The dead waited too, a held-breath silence along the whole silvered hillside.

Then she said, "The girl they took. Pell. My apprentice." Her voice cracked down the middle and she let it. "She'll be in one of your deep rooms. Won't she."

"Yes."

"Then you're going to draw me a way in."

The dead murmured assent, all of them at once, like wind through a forest of bells. Above them the road crested the hill, and beyond it, far off across a plain of rust-colored grass, Wren saw them: towers, many towers, and from the tallest hung bells green with age, motionless, silent — and yet he could feel them the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue.

The road leaned against him one last time, requesting payment.

Wren reached into himself and found, to his surprise, that he knew exactly what to give. He gave it gladly: the last thing the crown had ever given him, the memory of the day he'd sworn his commission, the queen's ring cold on his brow, the words *faithful service unto the map's end* — and the road accepted it, and let him pass, and somewhere behind him he heard the dead begin, softly, to sing.
