## 6. The Crown's Arithmetic

They took Wren at the ford, and they took August an hour later, and the order of those two facts would haunt him longer than the bruises.

He had heard the horses first — he always heard them first, a habit beaten into him by years of mapping roads for men who preferred their arrivals unannounced — but there had been nowhere to go. The marsh country narrowed to a causeway of packed clay, black water on either side, reeds whispering in a wind that carried no warmth. The hunt came out of the mist in grey livery, six riders with the queen's seal stitched at their shoulders: a closed eye above crossed keys. Wren knew that seal. He had drawn it himself, once, into the corner of a survey map, his pen steady because his hands had not yet learned to shake.

"Cartographer," said the officer, reading his face the way one reads a placard. "Wren Aldous. The crown has been looking for you."

"I've been looking for myself too," Wren said. "If you find me first, let me know."

The officer did not smile. They bound his wrists with cord that hummed faintly against his skin — warded cord, iron-threaded, the kind used on the gifted — and when Wren laughed at the absurdity of it, the officer only looked at him with something like pity.

"It doesn't matter what you are," the officer said. "It matters what you know."

* * *

The prison-fortress had been a monastery once. Wren recognized it from his own work — he had surveyed it three years ago, before his disgrace, when it was still called Saint Verity's and its bells rang for matins instead of silence. Now the bell tower stood empty, the bells themselves pried out and carted away, and the windows had been fitted with lattice screens of cold iron so that the light came through striped and grey, like light at the bottom of a well.

They were separated at the gate. That was the worst of it — watching August go one way down the corridor while he went another, her chin lifted, her ledger clutched to her chest like a shield she knew was useless. She did not look back. He admired that, and hated it, and understood it completely. Looking back was a luxury for people who believed someone might follow.

His cell was small and dry and had been a monk's study; a bracket for a candle still jutted from the wall, and someone had scratched a prayer into the doorframe in a dead language. Wren sat with his back against the stone and did what he did best: he made maps. In his head, he traced the fortress — corridors, stairwells, the drainage channel beneath the kitchens that his survey had flagged as unmapped, which meant unguarded, which meant possible. He built the whole structure in his mind, room by room, and then he built the ways out of it, and then he reminded himself that none of it mattered unless August was alive to walk out with him.

He was still counting doors behind his eyes when the minister came.

* * *

Minister Corvane did not look like a villain. That was the first horror, and Wren would carry it with him ever after: the man looked like a clerk. A tired, competent clerk with ink-stained cuffs and spectacles perched on a long nose, carrying a leather folio under one arm as though he had come to discuss grain tariffs. Two guards flanked him, but he barely seemed to notice them. He pulled the cell's single stool closer, sat down with a small grunt, and opened the folio across his knees.

"Wren Aldous," he said. "Formerly Royal Cartographer, Second Chair. Dismissed for — how did the writ phrase it? 'Deliberate falsification of crown surveys.' Omitting certain roads." He glanced up over the spectacles. "You'll forgive me if I don't pretend I don't know which roads."

"The roads to Ironhollow," Wren said.

"The roads to Ironhollow." Corvane nodded, as if confirming an entry in a ledger. "Do you know why your omission mattered so much? Not sentimentally. Practically."

Wren said nothing. In his experience, clerks filled silences the way water fills cracks.

Corvane obliged. "Because the covenant is a road," he said. "That is not a metaphor, Master Aldous, though I understand you people love metaphors. It is a structure. It has strands, load-bearing points, junctions where the power of the death-realm crosses into ours. For two hundred years we have drawn our borders around it and pretended it was weather. Something holy. Untouchable." He turned a page in the folio. "Her Majesty takes a more modern view. Everything can be measured. Everything measured can be managed. Everything managed can be harvested."

"Harvested," Wren repeated. The word tasted like pond water.

"The gifted are not criminals," Corvane said, with the mild patience of a man correcting a child's arithmetic. "They are conduits. Every person born with the hearing, the seeing, the touching-across — every one of them carries a strand of the covenant in their blood. When they are caged within iron and salt, that strand does not die. It *sings*. And we have learned to listen." He tapped the folio. "Seventy-one conduits currently in custody. Each one feeding a thread of power into the engine at Vellmark. Do you know what the engine does?"

"I imagine you're going to tell me."

"It unwinds the covenant," Corvane said simply. "Strand by strand. Carefully. We are not barbarians — we take only what the structure can spare, the way a forester takes only what the wood can spare. When the last strand is drawn out, the realms will be fully separate for the first time since the founding. All that power, all that *crossing*, held in one place. Under crown control. No more ghosts whispering in markets. No more bone-tokens, no more guilds trading in grief. No more fear of the dead." He smiled thinly. "A kinder world, Master Aldous. You should hear some of the letters. Mothers write to thank us."

Wren thought of August in a cell somewhere below him, her gift pressed flat under iron, and felt something cold walk up his spine. "And the conduits? After the last strand?"

Corvane adjusted his spectacles. "The strands regrow, given time. The vessels do not always survive the extraction. There is loss in any great undertaking. Her Majesty accepts this. I accept it. You will accept it too, eventually, because acceptance is simply arithmetic performed honestly." He closed the folio. "Which brings me to you. You mapped the prison-fortresses. You know their weaknesses, their sightlines, their drains. You also know the old roads — the ones you so conscientiously erased. Help us finish the survey of Ironhollow's approaches, and your sentence commutes to service. Refuse, and—" he gestured vaguely at the stone walls, at the striped grey light, "—well. The cells are not full yet, but they are filling."

"You want me to map the way in," Wren said slowly, "so your engine can be brought to the border."

"I want the kingdom to be safe," Corvane said, and the terrible thing, the thing Wren would turn over at three in the morning for the rest of his life, was that the man meant it. There was no cruelty in his voice. There was no cruelty anywhere in him. Only arithmetic, performed honestly, by a man who had never once questioned the sum.

* * *

They gave him bread and water and left him alone until evening, and then — this was the part Wren could never decide whether to be grateful for or sickened by — they made a mistake. Or perhaps it was not a mistake. Perhaps Corvane had ordered it, a final argument delivered without words.

They marched the prisoners past his cell.

It was a short procession, six figures in grey smocks, wrists warded, eyes down, escorted by four guards toward whatever chamber fed the engine's appetite. Wren pressed to the barred window slit as they passed, counting — five, six — and then stopped breathing.

The last figure was small. Thin in a way that was new, a way that hunger and iron had carved onto a frame that had once been round-cheeked and loud. But Wren knew her. He had seen her portrait sketched in the margins of August's papers, drawn quick and loving during a night on the toll road: Tamsin, the apprentice. The girl whose name the guildmaster had sold.

She was alive.

Barely. Her gait had the loose, dragged quality of someone being half-carried, and when she lifted her head — just for a moment, just enough — Wren saw that her lips moved constantly, silently, shaping words no sound came out of. Speaking to someone. Or for someone. Or perhaps her voice was already halfway extracted, already singing down a thread into the engine at Vellmark, and what walked in the procession was only the remainder.

Her eyes found his window. Found him. And in them was no recognition, no plea, nothing human reaching for anything human — only a vast, exhausted anger, the fury of a girl who had learned precisely what her name had cost and who had paid it.

Then the guards turned the corner, and she was gone, and Wren slid down the wall with his back against the prayer-scratched doorframe and put his face in his hands.

*Duty*, Corvane had said. *Arithmetic performed honestly.* Seventy-one conduits, each one a person, each one a thread, each one singing into a machine that ate the boundary between the living and the dead the way rust eats a bell. And the mothers writing thank-you letters. And the officers with pity in their eyes. And the whole apparatus of it — the ledgers, the folios, the seals — so clean, so orderly, so *reasonable*, that no single hand anywhere in it ever had to feel itself doing murder.

Wren had spent his career drawing lines. Borders, roads, coastlines — lines that told people where they were. He had thought his sin was erasing a line, omitting the roads to Ironhollow, keeping one truth off the crown's paper. He understood now that he had only ever been a minor clerk in a much larger accounting. The crown had been drawing lines for years: between the gifted and the loyal, between the living and the dead, between what could be taken and what could be spared. And every line it drew, it drew in blood and called it ink.

He thought of August. Of the way she balanced her books, meticulous, honest to a fault — until the day survival required her to burn a page. She had told him about it once, on the toll road, in the dark, her voice low with shame. *I lied for the first time in my career,* she'd said, *and the terrible part is how easy it was.* He understood that now too. He understood that honesty was a luxury of the unmeasured, and that Corvane's arithmetic only worked if you agreed to count people as numbers.

Tamsin was alive. Barely. Which meant there was still time — a little, a thread's-width of time — to unmake the sum.

Wren raised his head. The candle bracket was empty, the light dying, but his mind was already working, laying lines across the fortress the way he laid them across marshland: here the drain, here the guard rotation, here the weak joint in the world. Somewhere below him, August was listening to the dead even through iron — he was sure of it, sure the way he was sure of north. And somewhere south, an engine was pulling threads out of the fabric of everything, and a girl with silent moving lips was being spent to feed it.

In the morning, Corvane would come back for his answer.

Wren already knew what he would say. He would say yes. He would say yes, Minister, I'll draw your survey, I'll need my instruments, I'll need access to the lower levels to verify the sightlines — and he would smile the small, apologetic smile of a disgraced man grateful for a second chance, and every line he drew would be a lie, and every lie would be a key.

He had erased the roads to Ironhollow once to keep the crown out.

Now he would draw them again — straight, true, and leading exactly where he wanted the hunt to follow: to the border, to the bells, to August, and to the reckoning.

Outside the iron-latticed window, the first star came up over the monastery ruins, thin and white as a scratch on slate. Wren watched it and began, silently, to count doors.
