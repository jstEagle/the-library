## 7. Ledger Closed, Ledger Open

The guildhouse still smelled of tallow and ink when August came back to it, three weeks a fugitive, with rain in her boots and a dead man's name stitched into the lining of her coat.

She had told herself she would not come back. She had told Wren the same, and he had looked at her with that cartographer's patience — the look of a man who knew that maps lie mostly to themselves — and said nothing at all. But the prison carts were leaving for the capital on the morrow's first bell, and the apprentice was in them, and there was no road on any of Wren's charts that led away from that fact without passing through her own conscience.

So here she stood, in the doorway of the only home she had ever kept, listening to the house remember her.

The dead did that. They pooled in old places like water in low ground. The guildhall's rafters held the voices of every ledger-keeper who had ever bent over these desks, murmuring sums into the dark, and beneath them, fainter, the whisper of everyone whose names had passed through those ledgers — the paid and the unpaid, the found and the lost. August had spent years learning to keep that chorus at arm's length, to hear it as weather rather than as weeping.

Tonight it sounded like an accusation.

"You shouldn't have come by the door." Master Corvane's voice, from the shadows past the counting tables. He sat where he always sat, behind the great desk with its iron-bound corners, a candle burning down between his folded hands. He had not lit a second one. "There are ways in that don't leave footprints."

"I wanted you to see me coming," August said. "I wanted you to know I wasn't sneaking."

"Sentiment." He said it the way he said *arrears*. "Sit down, Zhou."

She didn't. She stood in the middle of the floor where the candlelight could find her face, and she set the covenant ledger on the desk between them with both hands, the way one sets down something breakable, or something that might bite.

Corvane looked at it a long time. The candle guttered. In its light his face was all planes and shadow, older than she remembered, or perhaps she had simply never let herself see how old. The guildmaster who had taught her that a ledger was a promise made visible. The guildmaster who had sold a girl's name for the price of the guild's continued breathing.

"The crown's men searched this room twice," he said finally. "They took everything with writing in it. Everything but what you're holding."

"They were looking for names," August said. "This isn't names. This is older than names. This is the pact itself — the covenant between the living realm and Ironhollow, written down before either kingdom had a seal to stamp it with. And I know what your new masters are doing with their engine, Master. I've seen the border. I've heard what's left of the seam. They aren't hunting the gifted. They're *harvesting* us, strand by strand, and when they've eaten through the last thread of the covenant there won't be a land of the dead anymore. There'll just be a hole, and everything in it, and everything after us."

"And you mean to stop it." Not a question. He turned the ledger toward himself but did not touch it, the way a man does not touch a snake he is negotiating with. "With what? You, a bookkeeper with a gift for eavesdropping on graves?"

"With my voice," August said. "If it comes to that."

Something moved behind his eyes — not softness; she had given up expecting softness — but recognition. The look of a man doing arithmetic he hated.

"You want the keys," he said.

"I want the keys to the prison carts. The ones leaving at first bell. There are eleven gifted in those cages, and one of them is fourteen years old and used to sleep in the loft above this room."

"Tamsin." Her name came out of him carefully, like a coin pried from a clenched fist. "You know what giving you those keys costs me. If the hunt traces them—"

"They won't trace them to you. They'll trace them to a break-in. I'll make sure of it. Broken hasp, forced lock, mud on the sill. A fugitive's work, not a guildmaster's treachery." She leaned forward. "And in exchange you get the one thing the crown wants more than any of us. The covenant ledger. Proof the pact exists. Proof it can be mended — or broken. Think what that buys you, Master. Think how long the guild survives on a secret like that."

He was quiet for so long that the candle burned a finger's width shorter. Outside, the city muttered its night noises: a dog, a drunk, the far-off groan of the harbor cranes. Somewhere under all of it, the dead in the walls were listening too. She felt them lean in.

"You were the best of them," Corvane said at last, and his voice had gone rough in a way she had never heard in eleven years of audits and closings. "The best I ever trained. You understood that a ledger isn't about numbers. It's about what we owe each other. Every line a debt. Every debt a thread." His hands unfolded at last, and from the chain at his belt he drew a ring of keys — iron, black with handling, each one stamped with the crown's warrant number. "I told myself selling Tamsin's name was arithmetic. That one girl against forty souls under this roof. I told myself the books balanced."

"They don't," August said quietly. "You know they don't. Some entries never close."

"No." He laid the keys beside the ledger, side by side, a trade made visible. "Some entries you carry until the account is settled by other hands." He pushed both across the desk. "Take them. Both. And Zhou—" For a moment he was not the guildmaster, only an old man in a cold room. "When you speak to whatever waits at the border of that place, tell it the guild remembers the old terms. Tell it some of us never agreed to what's being done."

"I'll tell them," August said. "They already know. They've been telling me since I walked in."

She took the keys. She took nothing else — not the coin in the strongbox, not the spare cloak on its peg, though her own was soaked through. At the door she paused, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in her chest since the marsh country, since Wren's fire, since the first night she had understood what she was going to do.

"I lied to the auditor," she said. "In this room. First lie of my career. I burned a page for it."

"I know," Corvane said. "I smelled the smoke."

"It was the right thing. That's what frightens me. I keep finding out the right things all feel like betrayals now." She pulled her hood up. "Goodbye, Master."

"Ledgers open," he said — the old closing, the blessing of the trade.

"Ledgers open," she answered, and went out into the dark.

* * *

The prison yard behind the assize court stank of wet straw and fear, and August stood in it at moonset with a crowbar she had taken from a fence line and eleven names written on her heart.

The carts were wagons, really — four of them, barred cages built onto flatbeds, wheel-locked and guarded by two soldiers who had, thank every patient ghost, gone to warm themselves at the brazier by the gate. The locks were crown-issue, good iron, but the keys turned smooth as butter, and August moved down the line of cages in the blue-black hush with her breath held, opening doors the way she had once opened ledgers: carefully, in order, leaving no mark of herself.

The first cage held a man who pressed himself into the corner when the door swung wide, blinking at her like something born underground. "It's real?" he whispered. "You're real?"

"Real enough. Go — the drainage culvert behind the tannery, follow it north, there are people at the old mill who'll hide you. Go now, go quiet."

They went. One by one, the gifted of the crown's harvest slipped out of their cages and into the dark: a woman with frost blooming from her fingertips even in summer; twin brothers who spoke in a language no living mouth had used in centuries; a boy no older than Tamsin who could make lamps burn green. August counted them out the way she counted everything, and the count was holy to her, each soul a line item entered into the only ledger that had ever mattered.

Tamsin was in the last cage.

The girl sat with her back against the bars, knees drawn up, and she did not move when the lock clicked open. In the thin moonlight her face was thinner than August remembered, sharpened, with a bruise going yellow along the jaw. But her eyes were the same eyes that had looked up from the loft ladder a hundred times, asking to be taught, asking to be trusted.

August knelt in the straw outside the cage, making herself small, making herself harmless. "Tamsin."

"I know why you're here." The girl's voice was flat as a closed account. "Wren told me. He got word to the mill people. The bridge. You're going to be the bridge." Her chin lifted. "You're going to do to yourself what they're doing to all of us, and you think that makes it better."

"It doesn't make anything better," August said. "It makes it *possible*. There's a difference."

"You sold me." No heat in it. That was the worst part — no heat at all, just the terrible bookkeeping calm August herself had taught her. "You stood in that room while Master Corvane read my name off the page, and you said nothing. I heard you say nothing. Do you know what that sounds like, from inside a cage? It sounds like a pen scratching. It sounds like someone balancing figures."

"Yes." August's throat ached around the word. "Yes, I know exactly what it sounds like. I hear it every time I close my eyes. I will hear it for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be." She reached through the bars, slowly, palm up. "I can't un-sell you. I know that. Debts like that don't close, they just get carried. But I'm carrying it, Tamsin. I'm carrying it to Ironhollow itself, and I'm going to pay it with the only thing I have left that they haven't taken."

Tamsin looked at the offered hand for a long moment. Then she rose, stepped over it, and walked out of the cage without touching her.

At the edge of the yard she stopped. She didn't turn around.

"I'm not forgiving you," she said. "Maybe someday. Maybe not. But I'm going to the border anyway. I'm going to stand there when you say the words, because somebody should witness it, and because—" her voice cracked, once, the fourteen-year-old showing through the bookkeeper's armor, "—because you were the only one who ever listened to me like I mattered, and I hate that I still love you for it."

Then she was gone, northward, toward the mill and the others, and August knelt alone in the empty cage's straw with her hand still outstretched, holding nothing, holding everything.

Far away, beyond the city walls, beyond the marshes, beyond the rusted bells at the edge of the world, August felt the dead begin to stir — felt them turn toward her like a field of flowers turning toward a sun that had not yet risen.

First bell would sound soon. And after it, the road to Ironhollow, and after that, the binding word.

She stood. She wiped her face with the back of her hand, businesslike, an entry closed if not resolved. Then she went to find Wren, and the others, and the end of the ledger.
