## 8. The Bells Remember

The border did not look like a border. It looked like a wound that had learned to be patient.

August stood at the edge of the bone orchard with the freed gifted arrayed behind her — eleven of them, gaunt from the prison carts, blinking in the grey light of a country where the sun came through like memory through fog. The apprentice, Sefa, stood farthest back, arms crossed, as if she had come not to fight but to witness August's failure personally.

The trees here grew in spirals, their branches hung with fruit of pale bone that chimed when the wind moved. Beyond them, the ground gave way to a plain of rusted bells half-buried in ash-grey grass, thousands of them, great cathedral bells and hand-bells and cracked fragments of bells, all silent, all waiting. The air tasted like iron filings and old hymns.

"They're listening," whispered Tam, a boy whose gift was knowing when anyone lied. He pressed his palm flat against his own sternum, as if holding something in. "I can feel them listening."

August could feel it too. The dead were close here — closer than she had ever known them, not whispering through tokens but *present*, a pressure like deep water against her skin. She had spent her whole life hearing the dead one at a time, one debt at a time. Here they were a congregation.

"Spread out along the orchard line," she said. "When the engine comes, don't run toward it. Whatever it shows you, whatever it promises — don't go to it."

"And what do we do?" asked Odile, an old woman who could mend broken things by singing to them, her voice still hoarse from the carts.

August looked out at the field of buried bells. "We hold the seam," she said. "And we hope the dead remember how to sing."

* * *

Wren saw the engine before he heard it, which frightened him more than anything else, because Wren had always trusted sound over sight. It came up the toll road like a cathedral being dragged sideways — a mass of black iron and brass ribs, crowned with funnels and harpoon-arms, each arm trailing a severed covenant-thread that glowed faintly gold at the fraying end. Behind it marched the hunt in their grey livery, and behind them, on a platform bolted to the engine's flank, stood the queen's minister, pale and neat as a ledger line.

Wren knew that machine. He had drawn its schematics two years ago, in a warm office that smelled of ink and approval, believing he was mapping roads. He had told himself then what he told himself every night since: *I didn't know what it was for.* The lie had worn thin as tracing paper.

He crouched beside August in the bone orchard, his map case open on his knees, though no map he owned covered this ground honestly. "The harpoons anchor into the covenant-strands," he said quietly. "Once all six are set, they can reel the whole seam taut and cut it free. They'll drag Ironhollow's power back to the capital like a net full of fish."

"And the gifted in the cages?"

"Fuel." He said it plainly because there was no kind way to say it. "Their voices keep the threads from snapping while they pull."

August's jaw tightened. He watched her do the arithmetic she was famous for — not coin this time, but lives, costs, debts. He had watched her burn a page rather than give a name; he had watched her trade the covenant ledger for prison keys. She was, he thought, the only honest bookkeeper he had ever met: she counted everything, including herself.

"There," she said suddenly, pointing.

Down the slope, half-hidden in the ash-grass, lay a bell — a small one, green with age, its clapper long rotted away. As the engine's first harpoon-arm swung up, the bell began to hum. No wind touched it. No hand struck it. It simply remembered, low and wavering, a note that might once have belonged to a funeral or a wedding; from this distance, Wren couldn't tell.

Then a second bell caught the note. Then ten. Then the whole drowned field of them, rising out of the grass like a congregation standing up.

* * *

August heard the song arrive the way she heard everything now — as a tide coming in.

It was not music, exactly. It was voices layered under voices, the dead borrowing the bells the way they had once borrowed her carved tokens, and beneath the surface note she could pick out individuals if she tried: a woman humming a lullaby in a language three kingdoms dead; a soldier counting under his breath, the way men count to stay calm; a child laughing, high and bright, and August's chest clenched around the sound because she knew that laugh, had heard it through a bone token four years ago, had balanced the account of a grieving father and never known until now that the girl's voice would find her again here, at the end of the world, singing.

*You hear us,* said the bells, in the plural voice she was beginning to understand. *You have always heard us.*

"I hear you," August murmured. Around her, the other gifted had gone still, heads tilted, each catching some thread of it — Tam wincing at truths, Odile swaying as if the song were something she could mend.

*The seam is thin,* the dead said. *The iron thing is hungry. Speaker, will you stand?*

"I'll stand," August said. Her voice sounded very small against the chorus. "But I'm only one voice."

*One is how every chorus begins.*

The first harpoon struck.

It came down through the air with a sound like a scream played backward and buried itself in the ground a hundred yards off, and where it struck, the world went *wrong* — the grass greyed, the light bent, and August felt the covenant-strand beneath it shudder like a plucked string about to snap. A second arm swung. A third.

"Hold!" August shouted to the gifted, though her heart said *run*. "Hold the line!"

* * *

Wren had made his decision somewhere on the toll road, paying memories to a road that charged fair, but decisions are cheap until the moment they cost. The moment was now.

The minister's voice carried across the field, amplified by brass horns, smooth as a signature on a warrant: "Citizens of the crown! You are harboring unregistered practitioners. Stand aside and be counted among the loyal. Remain, and be counted among the stolen."

*Counted.* Wren almost laughed. Even mercy, from the crown, was bookkeeping.

He reached into his coat and drew out the one thing he had kept clean all these years, wrapped in oilcloth against his heart: his commission, signed in the queen's own hand, naming him Royal Cartographer, Keeper of the True Roads. His last loyalty. His last excuse.

"You don't have to do this," August said beside him, not looking at him, watching the harpoons fall.

"That's why I can," Wren said.

He walked out of the orchard line, alone, into the open ground between the gifted and the engine, and the hunt's crossbows tracked him, and the minister leaned forward on his platform with interest. Wren held the commission up so all could see the seal.

"By this writ I mapped the true roads of this realm," he called, and his voice cracked only once. "And by the same authority I now declare the maps false. There are no roads to Ironhollow because Ironhollow is not a place you conquer. It is a promise you keep." He turned the commission over, produced his flint and steel — cartographers' kit, always carried — and set the parchment alight.

The queen's seal burned green, then black, then to ash that the wind took toward the bells. Somewhere in the chorus, Wren thought he heard a cheer — ragged, human, dead.

"Traitor," the minister said, without heat, the way a man reads a number. "Harpoon four. Aim past him."

* * *

The fourth harpoon fell, and the fifth, and the seam screamed.

August felt it in her teeth, in the bones of her hands, in the token-carving scars on her fingers. Five strands cut loose and reeling, gold light tearing free of the earth like roots ripped from soil, and the sixth arm swinging wide, and the dead's song faltering — not dying, but straining, a choir running out of breath.

*Speaker,* they said. *It must be now. It must be you.*

She looked back once. Sefa stood at the orchard line, and the girl's face was still hard, still unforgiving — but she stepped forward, one step, onto the ash-grass, and Tam followed, and Odile, and then all eleven of them, forming a ragged crescent behind August like notes arranging themselves into a chord.

"We're with you," Odile said. "Whatever it costs. That's what a guild is."

August turned to face the engine, and the burning field, and the minister's pale, patient eyes, and she opened her mouth to speak the first word of the binding —

— and found that the dead had already begun lending her their voices, filling her throat like floodwater filling a channel, hundreds of them, thousands, the lullaby-woman and the counting soldier and the laughing child, every soul the crown had caged and every soul the crown had forgotten, rising through her, through the bells, through the eleven gifted behind her, until the whole border rang with a single gathering note.

The minister stood up on his platform. For the first time, his neatness cracked.

"What is it doing?" he demanded of no one. "What is it *saying?*"

August drew the breath of the living and the dead together, and the sixth harpoon froze mid-fall, trembling, as every bell in Ironhollow turned toward her like faces.

She spoke.
