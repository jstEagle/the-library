## 2. A Name Written a Century Ago

I want it on the record, before anything else happens, that I did not believe in prophecy.

I believed in cataloguing systems. I believed in acid-free folders and the sanctity of marginalia and the moral superiority of anyone who has ever re-hinged a book spine correctly. I believed that a sealed volume in an archive is sealed for boring reasons — mold, mostly, or a donor's litigious heirs — and that the people who whisper otherwise are the same people who think the Dewey Decimal System is a government plot.

So when I tell you that I found my own name in a book dated 1897, understand that my first instinct was not wonder. My first instinct was to check for a forgery, then a coincidence, then a clerical error, in that order. It was only after all three failed me that I sat down on the floor of the restricted stacks of the Vellamont Archive, in the dark, with a lantern I wasn't supposed to have, and laughed until I cried.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Oscar hates when I get ahead of myself. She says it's because she's a cartographer and believes every story should be told at walking pace, with landmarks. So: landmarks.

The date was the fourteenth of Thawmonth. The place was Ironhollow, which I had come to on a research fellowship I had half-convinced myself was legitimate. The occasion was dinner with Oscar Marchetti, who I had met eleven days earlier when she marched into the archive's reading room, slapped a hand-drawn map of a country that does not exist onto my desk, and said, "You're the visiting archivist. Tell me if this reads as credible or if I've invented too many rivers."

It read as credible. That was the problem. Everything about Oscar read as credible — her ink-stained fingers, her habit of correcting other people's pronunciation of words she'd clearly learned from books, the way she looked at you like you were terrain she was deciding whether to survey. Eleven days later I was standing outside her cottage holding a bottle of plum wine and rehearsing sentences like a man rehearsing a confession, which, as it turned out, was exactly what I was doing.

She opened the door before I knocked. "You've been out there for four minutes," she said. "I could see your shadow pacing."

"Your window faces the wrong way for that."

"I moved the lamp." She took the wine, inspected the label, and nodded once, as though I'd passed some examination I hadn't known I was sitting. "Come in. Dinner's nearly ruined, which I'm told is traditional for first dates."

Her cottage smelled of woodsmoke and iron-gall ink. Maps covered every wall — not framed, pinned, layered over one another like sediment, so that the room itself was a kind of stratigraphy of her obsessions. There were maps of real places rendered with loving precision, and maps of places that existed nowhere but rumor: the Glass Steppes, the Drowned Choir, a coastline labeled only *here be weather*. I stood in front of one for too long and she caught me doing it.

"That one's from secondhand accounts," she said, handing me a bowl of something involving lentils and an alarming quantity of pepper. "Nine travelers' descriptions, averaged. The mountains are probably wrong. Mountains always are; everyone exaggerates them upward."

"You average testimony?" I asked. "Like a survey?"

"Like a court. Every account gets cross-examined." She sat across from me, tucked her feet under herself, and looked at me with an expression I would learn to call her working face — the one she wore when she was triangulating. "Your turn. Confess something. That's how these evenings go, I'm told. You trade confessions until the wine's gone and then you either kiss or never speak again. The literature is unclear."

"The literature," I said, "is frequently unclear. That's rather my whole profession."

"Then confess. Professionally."

And here is where I must admit to a failure of judgment that no archivist should ever commit: I had brought the page with me. Folded into my coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, against every rule of handling and every rule of sense. I had carried it around for three days like a stone in my shoe. And when she said *confess*, my mouth — which has never once consulted my brain before opening — said:

"I found my name in a book from 1897."

Oscar stopped chewing.

"It's a sealed volume," I went on, because apparently I was committed now, "in the Vellamont restricted collection. *Consilia et Vaticinia*, compiled by an order called the Sealed Page. Nobody's opened it since the sealing wax went on — I checked the wax, it's original, pre-1900, unbroken. I broke it anyway. Which is a firing offense, incidentally, so if you ever repeat this I'll deny everything and also cry."

"Wren." Her voice had gone very quiet and very careful, the voice of someone approaching unstable ground. "What do you mean, your name?"

"I mean my name. *Wren Halloway.* Spelled correctly. In a passage dated to the winter of 1897, describing — " I recited it, because I had read it so many times in three days that it had worn a groove in me, and things in grooves come out smooth: "*There shall come, on the eve of the thaw, an archivist who crosses, whose name is written here: Wren Halloway. She shall pass through the wound between worlds, and what follows her shall follow.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, Ironhollow did whatever Ironhollow does at night, which is nothing, magnificently.

"That's not possible," Oscar said.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. I've run through every explanation. Forgery — no, the ink predates the paper's binding, and the hand is consistent throughout the volume. Coincidence — statistically absurd, and besides, it's not just the name, it's the *eve of the thaw*, which is tonight, which is why I've been carrying the damn page around like a talisman instead of reporting it like a professional. Clerical error — someone named their daughter Wren Halloway in 1897 and the prophecy just happened to — " I gestured vaguely with my spoon. "No one was named Wren in 1897. Wrens are birds. I checked the parish records. I checked *all* the parish records."

"Why tonight?" Oscar asked. "Why is tonight the eve of the thaw?"

"Because tomorrow the passes open. Because the frost breaks tomorrow, according to every almanac I could find." I set down the spoon. My hands, I noticed, were not steady. "Because I came to Ironhollow eleven days ago on a fellowship I fabricated to get access to your valley's border records, because the Sealed Page has an entire subsection on this valley and its refusal of magic, and because I have been telling myself for two weeks that I am a serious scholar conducting serious research and not a woman following a century-old sentence like a trail of crumbs."

Silence. Oscar put down her bowl with great deliberation, the way you set down something fragile when the ground might move.

"And you waited until our first date to tell me this."

"I waited until our first date because I liked you," I said, "and I thought maybe if I said it out loud to someone with a good map-face, it would turn back into being ridiculous."

"Is it working?"

"Not especially."

To her eternal credit, she didn't laugh at me and she didn't laugh *near* me, which is worse. She got up, fetched the wine, poured two glasses, and sat back down. "Show me the page."

I unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that shook only a little. The paper was heavy rag stock, foxed at the edges, the ink brown-black and confident. The seal fragment still clung to one corner, deep red, stamped with a sigil like an eye folded shut. Oscar held it up to the lamplight, tilted it, and I watched her cartographer's face do its work — measuring, weighing, refusing to be impressed.

"The hand is period," she said finally. "Or a very expensive fake. Why bring it to *me*? I'm not an archivist."

"You're a woman who averages nine liars into a coastline," I said. "I wanted to know if you'd cross-examine it and find it lying."

"And did I?"

"No. That's the horror of it. It's true, or it's true enough, and true-enough is the most dangerous thing an old document can be."

Oscar looked at the page a long moment. Then she looked at me, and something in her face shifted — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone finding a landmark they'd drawn from rumor and discovering it's real.

"My grandmother," she said slowly, "kept papers. Sealed ones. She made my mother promise never to open them, and my mother made me promise the same, and I have been dying of curiosity since I was nine years old." She set the page down between us like a card played face-up. "If there's an order writing prophecies with names in them, and my valley is in their files, and you crossed a border to get here on the exact night their book says you would — "

"Then I'm either the punchline of a very long joke," I said, "or the beginning of a very bad one."

We drank the wine. We talked until the fire burned low — about the Sealed Page, about her grandmother's sealed papers, about whether prophecy was prediction or instruction, which is the question that keeps archivists up at night and should keep everyone else up too. At some point she took my hand across the table, ink-stained fingers lacing through mine, and said, "Whatever happens tonight, you're staying till morning. Not for prophecy reasons. For ordinary human reasons."

"For ordinary human reasons," I agreed, and meant it so hard it frightened me.

That was the moment, if you want the precise moment — and I have had a hundred years' worth of reason to want precision — when the air above the table began to shine.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because descriptions are all I have left, and because somewhere in Vaelmoor there may be a record-house where my testimony matters. So: the air above the table began to shine.

Not light, exactly. Light falls on things. This *unfell* — it gathered, the way heat gathers off summer road, except cold, and silver, and moving in slow spirals like water finding a drain. The lamplight bent toward it. The steam off our wine glasses leaned toward it. And in the middle of it, a seam appeared, thin as a cut in silk, and through the seam I could see — I can still see — a sky the color of bruised plums, and ash falling upward, and a horizon that was very slightly the wrong shape, like a drawing of a horizon done by someone who had only ever had it described.

"Oscar," I said. Very calmly, I would like noted. "Oscar, don't move."

"What is — " She turned. Followed my eyes up. And I felt her hand tighten around mine with a force that had nothing calm in it at all.

The seam widened. It made no sound, which was somehow the worst part — a thing that size should roar, should tear, should announce itself. Instead it simply opened, patient as a page turning, and the air in the cottage changed. It tasted of iron and snow and something older than both, something like the smell of a book that has been closed for a hundred years.

And I understood, with the terrible clarity that comes too late to be useful, that the prophecy had not been predicting anything. It had been *scheduling*.

"Wren." Oscar was on her feet, dragging my arm, both of us half-risen from our chairs, and the pull had begun — gentle at first, a leaning, then a lean with intent behind it. Our joined hands stretched between us. "Wren, hold on — "

"I am holding on."

"Hold on to *me*, not the — " The wine glasses went first. They rose off the table without breaking, turning slowly, and dissolved into the silver like sugar into tea, and I remember thinking, with the part of the mind that never stops cataloguing even as it dies: *acid-free glassware, that's going to bother me.*

My chair scraped backward. My feet left the floor.

Here is what wonder feels like when it curdles: it doesn't taste different. It tastes exactly the same — the awe, the impossible beauty, the plum-colored sky and the falling-upward ash and the sheer *scale* of the opening above a dinner table in a magic-dead valley where nothing magical was ever supposed to happen. Wonder and terror share a body. The difference is only which one your hands obey.

My hands let go.

I want to swear they didn't. I have replayed it ten thousand times, and every time, my fingers open — not because I chose to release her, but because the rift pulled the choice out of me like a splinter. One instant her grip was the warmest truest thing in the world, and the next the silver had taken me to the waist and her hand was closing on air, and her face —

Her face. I owe her this much, in whatever record survives me: she did not scream. She threw herself across the table, scattering bowls, catching my wrist at the last possible inch, and she *held*, with a strength no surveyor's daughter should have, and for one full second I believed in her more than I believed in gravity.

For one full second.

"Wren!" she shouted, and my name in her mouth sounded nothing like the name in the book. In the book it had been a label, a filed fact, a specimen pin. From her it was a person, whole and specific and loved, and I thought with perfect absurd clarity: *she'll come after me. She's a cartographer. She'll draw the way in.*

"Don't follow the prophecy," I gasped. "Follow *me* — "

And then the seam closed like a mouth, and the cottage was a cottage again, and the lamplight fell the correct direction, and Oscar Marchetti was shouting at a ceiling that had already forgotten her, and I was falling through a sky the color of bruises toward a field of ash, alone, with a page in my pocket that had gotten every word right.

The last thing I saw of my world was her lamp through her window, small and gold and stubborn, refusing to go out.

The last thing I heard was my own voice, saying the thing I'd rehearsed on her doorstep and never got to say, swallowed by the space between worlds where no one could possibly hear it:

*I found my name. But I'd have stayed for yours.*
