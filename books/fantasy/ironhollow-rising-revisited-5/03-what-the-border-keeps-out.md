## 3. What the Border Keeps Out

The morning after Wren vanished, I did what any reasonable person would do: I made tea, sat down at my drafting table, and stared at the empty chair across from me until the tea went cold.

Then I made a second cup for her, out of habit, and hated myself for it.

The chair was still there. The dent her elbows had left in my cushion was still there. On the table lay the map I'd been working on — the realm of Serevan, assembled entirely from a trapper's secondhand account and a drunk ferryman's lies — and beside it, the little notebook where Wren had been sketching marginalia the night before. She'd drawn a lopsided star next to my note about the Serevan coastline and written *ask Oscar if she's ever seen the sea* in handwriting that looped like it was showing off.

I closed the notebook. I did not cry. I am a cartographer; I deal in fixed points and verifiable distances, and grief has neither. Grief is a blank space on the map with no legend to explain it.

So instead I did the thing I'm actually good at. I got angry, and then I got systematic.

Ironhollow is a small valley. It has one road in, one road out, a mill, a chapel that hasn't held a service in twenty years, and eleven elders who meet on Thursdays in the back of the granary to decide things they pretend are decided by consensus. I have sold maps to six of them. I have flirted my way past two more. And I have spent thirty years living inside a border that doesn't let magic through, which means I have spent thirty years noticing what everyone else has trained themselves not to notice.

Because here is the thing about Ironhollow that no one says out loud: you don't build a wall against nothing.

Charms don't work here. Rifts won't open here. A peddler once tried to sell me a luck-knot that glowed everywhere from here to the coast, and inside the valley it was just string. Everyone knows this. Everyone accepts this the way you accept weather. But walls are built by people, people have reasons, and reasons leave paperwork.

I put on my coat, tucked Wren's notebook into the inner pocket like a talisman — which, notably, it wasn't, because nothing in this valley could be — and walked down to the granary.

It was Thursday.

* * *

Elder Maren Voss was shelling peas when I came in, which I took as a good sign. Elders doing domestic work are elders who haven't decided yet whether to lie to you. Elder Coram was there too, and old Hesk, and three others whose names I know mostly from map orders. Seven of eleven. A quorum of secrets.

"Oscar." Maren didn't look up from the peas. "We heard about your guest."

"Heard about her," I repeated. "That's an interesting way to put it. Most people would say *heard she was taken*. Through a hole in the air. At my dinner table."

Silence, except for the soft rattle of peas hitting a bowl. Coram shifted his weight. Hesk examined the ceiling beams as though they might offer him an exit.

"You were there," Maren said finally. "You saw it happen."

"I saw it happen. Which raises a question I've been turning over since dawn." I set Wren's notebook on the table between us, deliberately casual, the way you'd set down a card you're bluffing with. "Rifts don't open in Ironhollow. That's the whole point of this place, isn't it? That's why we're all here. So either last night I hallucinated watching the woman I'd known for three weeks get pulled through the ceiling of my own kitchen, or something about this valley changed. Or—" I looked around the table, meeting each pair of eyes in turn, "—or the border was never what you told us it was."

Maren's hands went still over the peas.

That was my answer, really. But I've learned that when you corner powerful people, you should let them talk, because they will always tell you more than they mean to.

"Sit down, Oscar," she said.

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." She brushed the chaff off her apron. "You want the story? Fine. You've earned the story, maybe. But understand that we keep it because most people sleep better not knowing it."

"I haven't slept at all," I said. "I'm uniquely qualified."

Coram made a sound that might have been a laugh or a cough. Maren shot him a look, then folded her hands.

"The border isn't a natural thing," she said. "It was built. Deliberately. By the founders of this valley, four generations back. They wove it — a working so large and so quiet that after the first generation, nobody remembered it was a working at all. People just knew that charms failed here, that rifts wouldn't open, that the world outside felt... thinner, somehow, when you crossed back in."

"Built to keep what out?" I asked.

And here Maren did something I didn't expect. She laughed. Short, bitter, tired.

"That's the question, isn't it? We don't know. The founders left records — sealed ones, kept by whoever holds the elder seat. My grandmother passed them to me the way hers passed them down. And the records say the border was raised 'against the crossing,' full stop. Against *crossing*. Not against an army, not against a plague, not against magic itself. Against the act of passing between realms." She spread her hands. "Either they built it to keep something out there from coming in. Or they built it to keep someone in here from leaving. The records aren't clear. Maybe they weren't meant to be."

The room felt colder. Outside, I could hear the mill wheel turning, ordinary as bread.

"My grandmother," I said slowly, "was Ada Marchetti."

Something flickered across Maren's face — quick, quickly buried, but I've spent my life reading terrain, and faces are just terrain with better lighting.

"Yes," she said.

"She held the elder seat before you."

"Yes."

"And she kept the sealed records."

Maren exhaled through her nose. "Oscar—"

"When she died, everything she owned came to me. Every drawer, every box, every locked chest in that house. I catalogued all of it. I make maps for a living, Maren. Cataloguing is what I *do*. There were no sealed records among her things."

"No," Maren agreed quietly. "There wouldn't have been."

"What does that mean?"

"It means that Ada Marchetti, in the last year of her life, removed certain documents from the elder archive. We know because the seals were broken and re-set, badly, by someone working alone at night. We never confronted her. She was dying, and she was the best of us, and we told ourselves whatever she'd taken, she had reasons." Maren met my eyes at last, and there was something in her expression I hadn't seen before — not guilt exactly, but its exhausted cousin. "We assumed she destroyed them."

"But you don't think she did."

"I think Ada Marchetti never destroyed anything in her life. I think she *copied* things. I think somewhere in that house you inherited, there is a page — or pages — that four generations of elders would very much like to read. And I think, given what happened in your kitchen last night, you may already be looking for it."

I stood very still. My kitchen. My grandmother's house, every inch of which I knew the way I know my own hands. And somewhere in it, hidden well enough to fool a professional cataloguer, a page that explained why a rift had opened above my dinner table and swallowed an archivist whose name had been written down a hundred years ago.

"I'll want the rest of the records," I said. "The ones you still have. The founder's account of the border-working. All of it."

"They're sealed—"

"Maren. A woman fell through my ceiling into another realm. Whatever courtesy the seal protects, it doesn't protect this."

Hesk spoke for the first time, his voice dry as August grass. "Give her the key, Maren. The girl's grandmother out-stubborned the whole council from her deathbed. You think we'll out-stubborn her awake?"

* * *

The elder archive was a room behind the granary I had walked past ten thousand times without once wondering about, which I suppose is the entire art of hiding things. Maren unlocked it with a key she wore on a cord around her neck, under her clothes, like the world's least romantic locket.

Inside: shelves of ledgers going back to the founding. The founder's own hand — a woman named Ilse Vael, which I noted, because *Vael* is half of *Vaelmoor*, and I am not a subtle person but I am a thorough one. Ilse Vael had written the account of the border-working herself, forty pages of tight, furious script describing a covenant between realms, a pact that governed crossings, and a decision — her decision, her signature at the bottom — to raise a dead zone where no crossing could occur.

"To protect the valley," Maren said, standing in the doorway as though the threshold itself required permission. "That's what she wrote. To give one place in the world where the realms' war couldn't reach."

"There was a war?"

"There's always a war, somewhere, between those two sides. The covenant was supposed to end it. Then it broke — forty years ago now — and the crossings started going wrong. Rifts that take people. Rifts that take *places*." Maren's voice dropped. "Ilse Vael sealed the valley against all of it. And the prophecy book — the one your archivist found her name in — was part of the same archive, once. Your grandmother copied from these shelves, Oscar. Whatever she copied, she thought you'd need it more than we did."

I took the ledgers home. I read until my eyes burned. And then, near midnight, having found nothing, I did the thing I should have done first: I stopped reading the house like a scholar and started reading it like a cartographer.

My grandmother had taught me to draw. She'd taught me that every map is a lie agreed upon, and that the truth lives in what the mapmaker chose to leave out. She'd also taught me, when I was nine, a game: *find the false wall.* Old houses hide rooms; old maps hide intentions. You look for the space that doesn't match the space around it. The wall too thick. The shelf too shallow.

Her house had one wall too thick. The pantry wall, on the north side, measured a hand-span deeper than the foundation plan said it should — and yes, I had the foundation plans, because of course I did, because she gave them to me when I was nineteen and told me to keep them *for when it matters.*

Behind the plaster: a cavity. In the cavity: a oilcloth packet, tied with waxed cord.

Inside the packet, a single page, copied in a hand I knew better than my own. My grandmother's writing — the same loops that had shown me coastlines, the same firm downstrokes that had corrected my first attempts at lettering. She had copied it from the prophecy book, the whole passage, dated in the margin: *transcribed 1897, from the sealed text.*

And beneath the prophecy itself, in her own words, smaller, pressed hard enough into the paper to tear:

*I have read this through forty times. It names the archivist who crosses, and the cartographer who redraws. It does not say what the Order intended when they wrote it. I believe they wrote it as instruction, not foresight — a recipe dressed as fate. If I am right, then someone must be outside their design when the time comes. I am making sure that someone is my granddaughter. Forgive me, Oscar. I could not warn you and keep you safe. Safety was the border. Knowledge was the door.*

I sat on my pantry floor until the candle guttered, holding a page in my grandmother's handwriting that proved she had known — a hundred years' worth of knowing compressed into one generation of her — that this day would come, and had chosen to arm me for it in secret rather than tell me.

Betrayal, I thought, can look exactly like love from the wrong angle.

The candle died. In the dark, my left hand began, very faintly, to ache — deep in the bones, in a way I had no map for.

And on the windowsill, where no draft should have reached it, Wren's notebook opened itself to the page with the lopsided star.
