## 5. The Ash-Field Guide

The ash-fields don't forgive, and neither do I.

That's the rule I live by out here on the grey edge of Vaelmoor, where the covenant broke and the ground never quite healed. Forty years of falling cinders will teach you a philosophy: keep your hood up, your wards fresh, and your heart in a locked box with the key thrown into the Glass Ford. I'd managed it for most of three decades. Hedge-witching is not a profession that rewards sentiment. You learn to read weather like scripture and trust nothing that arrives uninvited — especially not half-dead strangers lying in the ash like dropped laundry.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

I was out checking my snares at first light, which is the only honest hour left in this realm, when I saw her. A figure crumpled in the grey expanse maybe two hundred paces off, dark against the pale fall, one arm flung out as if she'd been reaching for something. Or pointing. Hard to say with the dying; they gesture at all sorts of things.

Now, a sensible woman would have walked the other way. The ash-fields collect bodies the way other places collect debts, and every one of them comes with complications. Some are Order traps. Some are rift-spill — people spat out of torn space with their shadows missing or their memories rearranged. And some, worst of all, are exactly what they appear to be: travelers who crossed where no one should cross, carrying trouble stitched into their skin.

I stood there in the falling grey and told myself all of this very clearly.

Then I went to her anyway. Sentiment is a habit, like biting your nails. You think you've quit, and then there you are, kneeling in frozen ash beside a stranger whose lips have gone the color of old pewter, muttering diagnostic curses under your breath.

She was youngish. Human, mostly — though "mostly" is doing some work in that sentence, because there was something about her edges that didn't sit right, like a map drawn slightly off-scale. Frost had gotten into her hair, turning it white at the temples, and her left hand was wrong. Not injured-wrong. *Paid*-wrong. The fingers were stiff and colorless, and when I peeled back her glove — she wore gloves, which meant she'd come prepared for a world she'd never seen, which meant she hadn't stumbled here by accident — the skin beneath bore a mark I hadn't seen since I was a girl with ink still wet behind my ears.

A line, fine as a spider's thread, running from the base of her palm up across the lifeline, ending in a small closed circle. Like a road drawn on flesh. Like a border.

Covenant-bearer's mark.

I sat back on my heels and let the ash settle on my shoulders while I thought about what a fool I was about to become.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about me, since we're apparently going to be spending time together, you and I, reader-of-whatever-this-becomes. My name is Bramble, which is a name I chose, because the name I was given belongs to a girl who died the day the Spire fell. I was a hedge-witch of the second circle once — licensed, sworn, respectable. I kept charms working in three villages and delivered babies in a fourth, and I believed, the way you believe in sunrise, that the covenant between Vaelmoor and the far realm was a good thing. Two worlds bound by law instead of war. What could be nobler?

Then the Order decided the law served them better broken, and the Spire came down, and everyone who'd ever signed anything or witnessed anything or so much as *read* the old texts became either a servant or a problem. I chose problem. It pays worse but the hours are flexible.

The Unsworn — that's us, the exiles from both sides of every border — we gather where the maps go soft. Waystations, ruins, the folds between realms where the Order's writ runs thin. We don't swear oaths. That's rather the point of us. After you've watched an oath get used as a leash, you develop opinions about swearing.

All of which is context for the fact that I hauled a frostbitten stranger onto my sledge, covered her with my good blanket — the wool one, not the itchy one, mind, I want credit for that — and dragged her four miles through falling ash to my hut at the field's edge, all while telling myself this was triage, not kindness. Triage has rules. Kindness just gets you hurt.

Her hand didn't warm properly even by the fire. That mark stayed cold. I've seen payment-marks before — crossing a border costs, always, and the body decides its own currency — but I'd never seen one shaped like that. Shaped like a *line*. Like something written.

I put the kettle on and waited for her to wake up, and I did not think about the last person I'd pulled out of the ash, twenty-two years ago, who hadn't been so lucky. I am very good at not thinking about things. It's practically a discipline.

* * *

She woke at dusk, which is typical. The dying wake at dusk; it's when the light stops lying to them.

I was grinding yarrow by the window when I heard the blanket shift, and then a voice, cracked and hoarse: "Where —" and then a cough that sounded like it had gravel in it, and then, with the particular stubbornness I would come to know very well indeed: "*Where am I.*"

Not *what happened to me*. Not *am I dying*. Where. As if the answer might be plotted.

"Vaelmoor," I said, without turning around. "Ash-fields, eastern reach. My hut. Don't touch the blue bottles, they bite."

Silence. Then the creak of someone trying to sit up and discovering what frostbite does to your opinion of furniture. I turned in time to watch her look down at her left hand, flex the fingers, fail, and go very still in the way people do when they're counting their losses and deciding whether to scream.

"The feeling comes back," I said. "Mostly. Give it a season."

"What did it cost?" she asked. Not *why can't I feel my hand*. She asked what it *cost*, like a merchant checking a receipt.

I looked at her properly then. Grey-eyed, sharp-chinned, hair full of my hearth-smoke, wearing travel clothes cut for a gentler climate and an expression like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. There was ash ground into the creases of her knuckles and, tucked inside her coat — I'd found it when I searched her for weapons, which I did, because see above re: complications — a leather roll of maps. Hand-drawn. Beautiful, actually, and I don't praise lightly; I once set a rival's cartography on fire out of professional contempt.

"You crossed at a thin place," I said. "Deliberately. With a map." I held up the roll. "These yours?"

"They're mine," she said, in the tone of a woman whose maps have been taken before and who has opinions about it.

"Good ones," I admitted, because flattery loosens tongues faster than questions. "Who taught you?"

"I taught myself." She pushed herself upright against the wall, winced, continued anyway. Stubborn. "Where's *here*, exactly? You said Vaelmoor. That's —" and then she stopped, because saying the name of a realm you've only ever mapped from rumor is different from standing in it, and I watched her understand that difference in real time. Her eyes went to the window, to the grey light and the falling ash, and I saw her do what mapmakers do: measure. Distance to the horizon. Slope of the land. Exits.

"You're a long way from anywhere," I said. "And the ash falls for another month at least. So you'll want soup, and answers, in whichever order you can manage."

"Answers," she said immediately.

"Naturally. Soup first anyway. Answers go down easier with broth." I ladled some out — nettle and dried mushroom, nothing fancy, but hot — and pressed the bowl into her right hand, since the left wasn't taking appointments. She ate like someone who'd forgotten hunger was a thing until just now, and between spoonfuls she watched me with those measuring eyes, and I let her. Let her take my inventory: the hedge-witch knots in my apron string, the exile's habit of sitting facing the door, the ward-sigils chalked over every lintel, faded and renewed and faded again.

"You're sworn to someone," she said finally. Not a question. Wrong, but confident about it.

"No one." I took the empty bowl. "That's rather the point of me."

"That sigil over the door is Order work."

"It's Order work *inverted*, love, which is a different animal entirely." I sat down across from her, close enough that she'd have to look at me. "I'm going to ask you some things, and you're going to decide how much truth you can afford. Understand: I dragged you out of the ash. Whatever you say next decides whether I'm glad."

Something flickered behind her eyes — fear, yes, but underneath it a kind of tired honesty. People who've crossed borders illegally tend to arrive pre-exhausted.

"My name is Oscar Marchetti," she said. "I'm a cartographer. From Ironhollow."

I kept my face still. Ironhollow. The valley that refuses magic. Even here, even among the Unsworn, that name carried weight — the one place both realms agreed to leave alone, the dead spot on every chart, the wound that never spoke. People fled *to* Ironhollow when the breaking came. Nobody came *from* it. Nobody crossed *out*.

"Nobody crosses out of Ironhollow," I said.

"I noticed." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "It cost me a hand's worth of feeling and I'd do it again tomorrow. Someone I —" she stopped. Started over. "Someone was taken. Through a rift. Into Vaelmoor. The valley wouldn't open for me, so I opened it myself."

"And the mark?" I nodded at her left hand. "You knew the price going in?"

"I knew there'd be a price. I didn't know the currency." She flexed the dead fingers again, watching them like they belonged to a stranger. "What I want to know is why the mark looks like —" she hesitated, and I could see her deciding whether to say it, and then saying it anyway, because she was that kind of fool, the brave kind, the kind that gets people killed and occasionally saves worlds: "— like something *written*."

There it was. The sentence I'd been dreading since I knelt in the ash.

I got up. Paced to the window. The ash fell, patient as ever, covering everything equally — the living and the dead, the guilty and the grieving. Behind me I heard her waiting, and she waited well, without fidgeting, which told me she'd spent her life being underestimated and had learned to use the silence.

"The mark on your hand," I said finally, "is called a bearer's line. It appears on people who are named in the covenant. Not people who *break* it, not people who *guard* it — people written into it. Bound to it. When the Spire fell, forty years ago, every marked soul in Vaelmoor felt it snap like a cut rope. Most of them died within the year. The rest of us ran." I turned around. "The Order hunted the marks. Burned them out of skin where they could, burned the skin along with them where they couldn't. I haven't seen a living bearer in thirty years. I'd begun to hope the line had died out entirely."

"So I'm — what? A relic?"

"You're a *problem*," I said. "You're a walking piece of a treaty that powerful people murdered, and you've just announced yourself in the one realm where that announcement gets you collected. The Order keeps records, Oscar Marchetti. Records of names. And they have a building full of archivists who read those records, and I promise you they are better at finding bearers than you are at hiding."

Something changed in her face at the word *archivists* — a flinch, quick and buried, but I've spent thirty years reading faces across campfires and I caught it.

"Whoever was taken," I said slowly. "They were an archivist?"

"Wren." The name came out of her like an arrow leaving a bow. "Wren Vale. She found her own name in a prophecy book dated 1897, and that night a rift opened above our table and took her, and everyone in Ironhollow told me to grieve and move on because the valley doesn't permit magic and therefore doesn't permit *hope*, and I —" She stopped. Breathed. "I made a map of a place I'd never been, using a flaw I found in my grandmother's handwriting, and I walked through the border at a thin place, and now I'm in your hut eating your soup."

I'll give her this: she summarized beautifully.

"Your grandmother," I said, "wrote prophecies?"

"My grandmother copied one. In her own hand. A page about a cartographer who redraws." Her jaw tightened. "She never said a word to me. Not one. I learned my own fate from a dead woman's filing system."

Ah. There it was — the inheritance wound. I knew that wound intimately; mine was dealt by a mother who swore oaths she shouldn't have. Betrayal by blood is the kind that doesn't scab.

"Come with me," I said.

She blinked. "Where?"

"Not far. There's a waystation — call it a village, if villages could be disowned by two realms at once. We call ourselves the Unsworn. Exiles. Smugglers, deserters, disgraced knights, hedge-witches with poor judgment." I pulled my cloak off its peg. "You need shelter, and I need —" and here I paused, because the true answer was *I need to not be alone with what your hand means*, and I wasn't ready to say that aloud, so I said: "— I need to put you somewhere the Order can't pluck you like a loose thread. And there are people there who knew the old covenant. Who might know what a bearer's line does when it walks into Vaelmoor forty years after the Spire fell."

"Why help me?" she asked. Direct. No coyness. I was beginning to suspect she was constitutionally incapable of coyness, which was either refreshing or exhausting, possibly both.

I could have lied. Said it was duty, or debt, or the general principle of the thing. But she'd crossed a border that kills most people, alone, on faith and cartography, for a woman whose name she said like a vow. You owe honesty to fools of that caliber.

"Because twenty-two years ago I pulled someone else out of the ash," I said, "and I was too careful, and I asked too many questions, and by the time I decided to act she was gone. I've done my sums on that night every day since. I don't intend to add to them." I threw her the spare cloak — the itchy one; she hadn't earned the good one yet. "Also, you make excellent maps, and I'm petty enough to want a cartographer in my debt."

She caught the cloak. Stood. Swayed, steadied, refused my arm with a look that dared me to offer twice. Her dead hand hung at her side, the little circle at the end of the line catching the firelight.

"One condition," she said.

"Bold, for a woman who can't hold her own bowl."

"Whatever your people know about the prophecy — about *me* — you tell me straight. No softening, no saving-it-for-later. I've had enough of other people deciding what I can bear to hear."

I looked at her for a long moment. Half-frozen, half-crippled, wholly determined, standing in a stranger's hut at the edge of a hostile realm because love told her to walk.

"Oh, you'll fit right in with the Unsworn," I said. "We're all terrible at being saved. Come on, then. It's four miles, the ash doesn't stop for sore feet, and if we're lucky we'll beat the curfew patrols."

We stepped out into the grey together, and behind us my hut's wards dimmed and sealed, and ahead of us the ash-fields stretched toward the hidden waystation where a disgraced knight was surely burning the stew again and twins were surely cheating each other at cards.

She walked beside me, mapping everything. I could see her doing it — the tilt of her head, the flick of her eyes, cataloguing slope and distance and cover. And I thought, with a sinking feeling I would later recognize as the exact moment my locked-box heart developed a crack:

*A bearer's line, walking east. The Order will smell her before week's end.*

And I was right. Though not, as it turned out, in the way I feared.
