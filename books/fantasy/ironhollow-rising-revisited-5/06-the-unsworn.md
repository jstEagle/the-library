## 6. The Unsworn

I woke to the smell of stew and the sound of someone arguing about me in the third person, which is never a promising way to enter consciousness.

"She's a spy," said a voice like gravel rolled in honey. "Nobody crosses the ash-fields alone unless they're running *toward* something."

"Nobody crosses the ash-fields alone at all," said Bramble's voice, closer, "unless they're too stupid to know better or too stubborn to care. I've met both kinds. This one's the second."

"I could be both," I offered, without opening my eyes.

A pause. Then the gravel-honey voice again, reluctantly amused: "She talks. That's either good or very bad."

I opened my eyes to a ceiling of dark timber crossed with beams, hung with drying herbs, bundles of feathers, and one brass instrument I couldn't identify but suspected was illegal somewhere. The room was low and warm, lit by lamps that burned without flame — which should have been my first clue that I was a long way from Ironhollow, where the most magical thing a lamp ever did was flicker dramatically during storms.

Bramble sat on a stool beside the cot I'd been laid in, her hands wrapped around a clay mug. The hedge-witch had an air of permanent, comfortable disapproval, like a cat who had decided to tolerate you. Beside her stood a mountain of a man in a knight's surcoat that had been deliberately, carefully defaced — the sigil scraped off, though if you knew where to look, the ghost of it remained, a crest erased rather than worn away.

"Oscar Marchetti," Bramble said. "Cartographer. Allegedly."

"Professionally."

"Mm." She handed me the mug. The stew inside was brown, fragrant, and contained at least three things I couldn't name. "Drink. You've been out two days. Your hand—"

I looked down. My left hand lay on the blanket, pale as paper, and when I tried to flex the fingers they moved — slowly, grudgingly, like a bureaucracy processing a request. The numbness from the crossing hadn't fully lifted. Where the border had taken its toll, the skin carried a faint tracery of silver lines, fine as veins on a leaf, spiraling up from my palm.

"That's not frostbite," I said.

"No," Bramble agreed.

"It's a toll-mark," said the big man. He said it flatly, the way you'd say *that's a tumor* or *that's a warrant for your arrest*. "Covenant-bearer's mark. Haven't seen one in forty years."

"And you are?"

"Sef." He didn't offer a title, which told me there'd been one once, and that losing it had been the point of scraping the surcoat clean.

The door banged open before I could ask the obvious question — *bearer of what covenant?* — and two people tumbled in mid-argument, so alike they had to be twins and so different in bearing they might as well have been different species. One was lean and quick-moving, all elbows and grin, carrying a crate that clinked in a way crates shouldn't. The other walked like the floor owed him money, arms crossed, mouth set in a line of chronic grievance.

"—all I'm saying is if you'd let me handle the Vell ferryman—"

"You *broke* the Vell ferryman, Ryn."

"I *persuaded* him. There's a difference and it's called results."

They stopped dead when they saw I was awake. Four eyes, identical shade of storm-grey, assessed me with the synchronized suspicion of people who had been double-crossed enough times to make it a reflex.

"So she lives," said the grinning one. "Excellent. Ilo. Smuggler, entrepreneur, occasional hero." He dumped the clinking crate by the wall.

"Ryn," said the other, in a tone suggesting he resented having to say it. "Smuggler. Occasional realist."

"Twins?" I asked, because it seemed safer than any other question.

"Unfortunately," they said together, then glared at each other for agreeing.

Bramble made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Welcome to the Unsworn, cartographer. Mind the twins, don't touch Sef's whetstones, and finish your stew."

* * *

The waystation, I learned over the next day, was built into the bones of something much older. The walls were mortared stone beneath the timber — old work, deliberate work, with channels cut into the floors that no longer carried anything. When I ran my fingers along them, tracing their pattern the way I trace everything, Bramble caught my wrist.

"Careful where you put your curiosity here. Some of this place still remembers what it was."

"What was it?"

"A border station. Back when the border was a treaty instead of a wound." She said it lightly, but her grip stayed on my wrist a moment longer than the sentence required.

I spent that first evening doing what I always do: drawing. My satchel had survived the crossing — my maps, my pens, the little traveling kit of a woman who has never trusted a place to stay where she put it. I sat by the lamp-fire and sketched the room, the people, the channels in the floor, because mapping is how I think. A thing isn't real to me until it has edges.

Sef watched me do it with an expression I couldn't read. Ilo watched me do it with an expression I could read entirely too well, the look of a man pricing merchandise.

"How much for one of those?" he asked, nodding at a sketch of the room.

"Not for sale."

"Everything's for sale. That's not cynicism, that's physics."

"This one isn't." I kept drawing. "It's how I keep from screaming."

He blinked. To his credit, he didn't push. Ryn, from his corner, muttered something that sounded like *"artists"* in the tone other men reserve for *tax collectors*, and I decided I liked him marginally more for it.

It was Bramble who finally gave me the shape of things, that night, sitting across from me with her tea gone cold and forgotten.

"You came through a thin place," she said. "Deliberately. With a map you drew yourself."

"It was a bad map. Deliberately bad. I left out a river and doubled a ridge-line, and the wrongness made a seam I could pull open." I turned my marked palm up. "It cost me this, apparently."

"Tolls always do. The borders between realms aren't walls, girl. They're ledgers. Everything that crosses gets written down, and everything written down gets paid for." She tapped my silvered palm with one gnarled finger. "That mark means the ledger noticed you specifically. Means you're not cargo. You're a party to the contract."

"The contract being...?"

And so she told me. All of it, in the flat, careful voice of someone who has told it many times and never gotten used to it.

Forty years ago, there had been a covenant. Not a metaphor — a document, an actual binding text, sealed in a spire that stood at the meeting-point of the realms. It governed passage, trade, magic, sanctuary. Two realms, one law, shared power, checked and balanced like anything worth trusting. And then, one winter night four decades past, the Spire fell. The covenant shattered. And where its authority had been, the rifts began.

"They're not doors," Bramble said. "People think of them as doors, holes, passages. They're not. They're *hunger*. The covenant used to define where one realm ended and the other began — a line drawn in law. When the law broke, the line broke with it, and now the border doesn't know what it is. So it eats. Geography, mostly. Sometimes memory. Once, an entire village that had the poor judgment to be in two places at once."

"Two places at once?"

"Border villages. Under the covenant they belonged to both realms, held in common. Afterward—" She shrugged, and the shrug carried a graveyard in it. "The rifts took them. Both copies. As if to settle the question of ownership."

The lamp-flames guttered, though there was no draft. In the channels under the floor, I would have sworn I felt something shift, like a sleeper turning over.

"The Order," I said. "Wren mentioned—"

Her head came up sharply. "What did you just say?"

"Wren. She's why I'm here. She's an archivist — we were—" I heard myself hesitate on the word, which was absurd, given everything. "*Seeing each other.* Her name showed up in a prophecy written a century ago, and a rift opened above our dinner table and took her. Into Vaelmoor. The Order keeps records; I thought maybe they'd know where she was taken."

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Sef, who had entered silently sometime during Bramble's story and stood by the door like furniture that had learned to frown, said:

"The Order of the Sealed Page doesn't *keep* records, cartographer. It *is* records. And it doesn't help people whose names are in prophecies. It collects them."

* * *

They argued about me for another full day. I know because they did it badly, in whispers, in a house with walls thinner than their discretion.

I passed the time usefully. I mapped the waystation — every room, every channel, every beam — partly because it steadied me and partly because I'd noticed something in the floor-channels that itched at me: they weren't decorative. They were a diagram. Someone had built this place as a schematic of something larger, and I had a professional hunch about what.

On the second evening, Ilo found me bent over the great-room floor with charcoal, connecting channels to doorways to hearth-stones on a sheet of paper.

"You're drawing the house," he said.

"I'm drawing what the house is *of*."

"And what's that?"

I finished the last connection and sat back. The pattern resolved: a tower, ringed by seven radiating lines, each line terminating in a small square station. Seven stations around a spire.

"That," I said quietly, "depends on whether anyone wants to tell me about a spire with seven outposts."

Silence behind me. I turned. All five of them were there — Bramble, Sef, the twins — arranged in the doorway like a painting titled *We Were Going to Tell You Eventually*.

"The Covenant Spire," Sef said. "Seven warding stations. This was one of them." His jaw worked. "I was garrison commander of another."

"You said you were a knight."

"I said I was Sef." He came into the room and lowered himself onto the bench across from me, the wood creaking in protest. "Knight-Captain Sefrian Vale, Order of the Spire Wardens, before the order stopped existing because I—" He stopped. Started again. "The night the Spire fell, I held command of the north station. We had warning. We had hours. I gave the order to stand down."

"Why?"

"Because the covenant the Spire enforced was unjust, and I was young, and I thought letting it break would break the injustice with it." He met my eyes with the terrible steadiness of a man who has rehearsed this confession in the dark for forty years. "I was half right. The injustice broke. So did everything else."

The fire popped. Ryn shifted his weight. Ilo, uncharacteristically, said nothing at all.

"You let it fall," I said.

"I opened the gate."

I looked down at my own marked hand, silver-lined, ledger-noticed, and felt the strange vertigo of recognizing a pattern: a person who breaks a system believing the breaking will be merciful, and learns otherwise. Sef had done it with a gate. Whoever wrote the prophecy in 1897 had done it with ink. And I had done it with a deliberately flawed map, pulling myself through a seam in the world toward a woman I barely knew and could not stop wanting to find.

"We're all traitors to something," Bramble said into the quiet. "That's rather the point of the name. Unsworn. No realm holds our oath, so we hold each other's. Badly, usually. But we hold it."

"It's a terrible system," Ryn said.

"It's the only one that's never lied to us," Ilo said.

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

"No," Bramble agreed. "They're not."

* * *

Here is the thing I could not have explained to anyone in Ironhollow, least of all to myself: I had spent my whole life drawing rooms I'd never sit in, coastlines I'd never walk, cities that existed only in a drunk sailor's secondhand memories. I loved them precisely because they weren't mine. Belonging, in Ironhollow, was a settled thing — everyone in their valley, everyone in their place, the border holding us all gently prisoner and calling it safety. I had never fit inside it, and I had assumed the fault was mine, a flaw in my personal geography.

But sitting in a smuggler's den built into the skeleton of a broken treaty, eating stew containing unidentified meats, listening to a disgraced knight and a hedge-witch and two bickering twins argue about whose turn it was to check the rift-snares — I felt something I had only ever managed to *draw*: the sensation of a place accepting your outline. Of edges that fit.

They were exiles. I was an intruder. None of us belonged anywhere, and so, somehow, we all did here.

"Don't get sentimental," Bramble warned, catching my expression. "You've been here three days. The twins haven't even robbed you yet."

"That's coming," Ilo confirmed cheerfully.

"Tonight, probably," said Ryn.

"See? Family."

I laughed — actually laughed, the first time since Wren fell upward out of the world — and the sound surprised all of us, including me.

Then Sef cleared his throat, and the warmth in the room banked itself like a covered fire.

"There's a matter we should discuss," he said. "You said the Order took your archivist. If her name is in a century-old prophecy, she won't be in a common cell. The Order detains prophesied persons at the record-house in Corvane — the Vault of Living Texts, they call it, which tells you everything about how they see people." He spread a map on the table — a real one, military-issue, better than anything I owned, and I confess I coveted it immediately. "Three days' travel. Garrisoned. Warded against exactly the kind of crossing-magic your hand now carries."

"You're offering to raid a fortified archive," I said, "for a woman you've never met."

"I'm offering to raid a fortified archive," Sef said, "for a woman the Order has no right to hold. The fact that you love her is your business."

I didn't correct him. Partly because arguing felt like a luxury. Mostly because he wasn't wrong.

Bramble was already pulling supplies. The twins were already arguing about approach routes. And I was already bending over Sef's map, pen in hand, marking the roads — and noticing, with a cold prickle along my silvered palm, that where my marked hand touched the paper, the ink seemed to settle, to *hold*, as if the map were glad of me.

As if the map knew something about what I was for that I didn't yet.

"Cartographer," Bramble said quietly, watching my hand. "What did the ledger mark you *as*?"

I looked at the spiral lines winding up from my palm, and for the first time since the ash-fields, I answered honestly:

"I think we're going to find out."
