## 7. Where They Keep the Archivists

I have broken into three keeps, two fortresses, and one wedding — the last by accident, and I still maintain the bride started it — but I had never broken into a record-house before, and I want it noted that record-houses are the worst of the lot.

Keeps at least pretend to be honest about what they are. They put their walls where you can see them. A record-house in Vaelmoor is a different animal entirely. This one had been a granary once, back when the district fed people instead of filing them, and the Order had kept the grain-slate roof and the wide loading doors because they were convenient for moving crates. The crates just happened to contain people.

"They rotate the guards at dusk," Bramble said, crouched beside me on the ridge of a collapsed aqueduct that ran within thirty feet of the east wall. Below us, the ash-fields gave way to the grey outskirts of Corven's Rest, all soot-stained brick and chimneys breathing smoke that smelled faintly of copper. "Four on the doors, two walking the perimeter, one on the roof with a horn."

"Seven," I said.

"Eight." She nodded toward a narrow window near the roofline, glowing amber against the dusk. "The lamp-tender. He never leaves. Old man. Half-blind. I've watched this place two days and he hasn't moved except to eat."

"Then he's either the most loyal man in Vaelmoor or the most bored."

"He's the most frightened," Bramble said. "There's a difference you learn to see."

She would know. Bramble had been watching things her whole life — herbs, borders, the small tells of people deciding whether to be kind. She was wrapped in a patched grey cloak that made her look like exactly what she wasn't: an unremarkable woman waiting out the weather. Underneath it she had enough knives to open a modest shop.

Below us, Oscar Marchetti lay flat against the cold stone of the aqueduct with her ruined map spread under her hands, and she was doing the thing she did — the thing that had stopped making me uneasy around chapter four of knowing her and started making me grateful. She was reading the building.

Not the guards. Not the walls. The *building*. Her fingers moved over the old survey lines she'd drawn from secondhand accounts, and every so often she'd tap a spot and murmur: "Grain cellar there, must be. Loading floor above it. If they kept the granary chutes—" Tap. "—then there's a way down into the records that doesn't go through any door."

"You're certain?" Ilo asked. He was upside down, more or less, draped over the aqueduct's edge with his chin in his hands, watching the loading doors with the patience of a cat at a mousehole. His twin was somewhere along the south wall doing whatever Ryn did, which was being quiet in a way that made me trust him slightly less than I trusted his brother, though I'd have died for either of them and said so only when drunk.

"I'm certain it's a granary," Oscar said. "The rest is inference."

"Inference got a man I knew hanged," Ilo said cheerfully.

"Inference is all anyone has," Oscar said, not looking up. "Maps are just inference you can hold."

I liked her for that. I liked her less for how right she usually turned out to be.

* * *

We went in at full dark, through the grain chute.

It was Bramble's idea, refined by Oscar's inference, executed by my shoulders, and complained about by everyone. The chute was a slate-lined shaft barely wider than a barrel, angled steeply down from a gap in the east wall where the Order's masons had bricked it over lazily — mortar over brick without clearing the old grain dust, which meant the patch crumbled like stale bread under a pry-bar. I went first because I was biggest and because if the shaft ended in a crossbow bolt I preferred it hit me.

It didn't end in anything so dramatic. It ended in a cellar that smelled of dust and lamp oil and, underneath both, faintly and horribly, of ink — the sharp iron-and-vinegar bite of the Order's archival ink, the kind they use for documents meant to last centuries. Documents meant to outlive the people they describe.

That smell should have told me everything. It didn't, because I was busy counting.

Bramble dropped down beside me, then Oscar, then Ilo, who landed with the grace of a man who had been falling out of things professionally since childhood. Ryn came last, silent as spilled flour.

The cellar was long and low, ribbed with stone arches, stacked with crates. Real crates, wooden ones, some stamped with the Order's seal — a closed eye over an open book. We moved between them in single file, and it was Ilo, running his hand along a crate lid as we passed, who stopped first.

"Sef," he whispered. "Sef, come look at this."

The crate was half-open. Inside, packed in straw, were ledgers. Dozens of them. And on each spine, in the Order's careful hand, a name and a date.

*Alenna Voss, 1861. Tam Merric, 1874. Odile Brantome, 1893.*

"People," Ilo said. His voice had gone strange. "These are— these are *people*."

"Not anymore," Bramble said quietly. She had gone very still, the way she did around death. "Or not yet. Look at the dates. Some of these are older than the border of Ironhollow itself."

Oscar had lifted one ledger out with hands that weren't quite steady. She didn't open it. She held it like something that might be sleeping.

"Wren's name will be in one of these," she said. "Written down. Filed. Like weather records."

"Yes," I said, because lying to her seemed worse than the truth.

"Then let's go get her file," she said, "and burn the rest of the cabinet."

"There's a thought I can endorse," Ilo said.

But it was Ryn — quiet, watchable Ryn — who put a hand on the crate lid and said, "Not yet. Noise carries in stone. We get the archivist, we get out, we come back with fire when we have time to enjoy it." And the thing was, he was right, and I hated how smoothly he said it, how rehearsed it sounded, like a man reciting lines someone else had given him. I filed that away. I was getting good at filing things away. It's a knight's whole retirement plan.

* * *

The stairs up from the cellar came out behind a shelf of census rolls, into the long central hall of the record-house — the old granary floor, vaulted and vast, now lined with shelving that climbed three stories into the dark. Lamps burned in niches every twenty feet. Somewhere above us, paper whispered.

And there were desks. That was the detail that got under my skin and stayed there. Rows of reading desks, neat as a chapel, each with its lamp and its chair, arranged facing a raised dais at the far end where a single large desk sat beneath a framed copy of the Order's creed: *KNOWLEDGE IS THE ONLY JUST SUCCESSION.*

They hadn't built a prison. They'd built a library where the books could read themselves.

"The prophesied ones work here," Bramble breathed. "Look — the desks, the lamps. They keep them *reading*."

"Why?" Ilo whispered.

"Because a prophecy is a lever," Bramble said. "And the Order wants to know which way each person pushes."

We heard the footsteps then — soft slippers on stone, a guard's rounds — and we folded ourselves into the shelving like water into cracks. I pressed against a shelf of tax codices with my knife loose in my grip and my heart doing the slow heavy work it does when the body knows what the mind won't admit: that this was the part of any raid where I was least useful. Big men make noise. My whole craft is being loud at the correct moment, and the correct moment was not now.

The guard passed. A boy, really. Sixteen maybe, yawning, trailing a lamp that threw our shadows huge and wavering across the spines of a hundred years of filed lives.

When his footsteps faded, Oscar touched my arm and pointed up.

Third gallery. There was a light on in the far corner, past the last shelf, where the regular patrol didn't reach — a warm spill of lamplight on the floor, and beneath it, faintly, a voice. Reading aloud.

I knew the voice. I had heard it for exactly one evening through a rift that shouldn't have existed, in a valley where magic couldn't follow, while the woman it belonged to told Oscar Marchetti about a book with her own name in it. But I knew it anyway, the way you know a song from a room you weren't in. Grief makes eavesdroppers of us all.

Oscar was already moving.

* * *

She was at a desk in the corner of the third gallery, and the first thing I thought — the thought I'm ashamed of, the thought I've never said aloud — was that she looked *well*. Color in her face. Hair braided back practical and neat. A blanket over her shoulders that some lamp-tender or guard had clearly given her, because the Order dressed its prisoners in comfort when the prisoners were useful.

She sat surrounded by books. Not scattered — *arranged*, in the careful concentric order of someone who has been working a problem for weeks. Open volumes weighted with stones. Marginalia in three colors of ink. And in the center, on a reading stand, a book I recognized from Wren's own description that night in Ironhollow: black boards, silver clasps, older than the border.

The sealed prophecy. Unsealed. Read to tatters.

Wren looked up when we came around the shelf, and here is the thing I will carry to my grave: she looked at five armed strangers emerging from the stacks, one of them the lover she had been stolen from six weeks ago, and her first expression was not relief.

It was *apology*. The wince of a scholar interrupted.

Then recognition landed, and her eyes found Oscar's, and something in her face cracked open — but she didn't stand. She put one hand flat on the prophecy book, like a woman holding down a map in wind, and said:

"You shouldn't have come."

"Hello to you too," Ilo murmured.

"Wren." Oscar's voice came out stripped down to bare timber. She crossed the gallery like a woman crossing ice, arms half-raised, afraid the shape of her might be wrong. "Wren, I crossed a border that kills magic and lost the feeling in my left hand to do it. I walked through ash-fields. I am not leaving without you."

"I know." Wren's voice broke on it, just slightly, and reassembled itself. Scholars are like that; grief gets footnoted. "I know, and I'm sorry, and I need you to sit down, because I've read all of it, Oscar. All of it. Not the page your grandmother copied — the whole thing. And the page is a lie of omission, and if you walk out of here tonight, you'll walk into the second half blind."

"We'll walk out of here tonight," I said, "and you can read to us on the road. I'm told I have a lovely listening face."

"You don't," said Ilo.

"I have a knife, though."

"Everyone stop." Bramble hadn't moved from the edge of the shelf-shadow. She was watching the stairwell behind us with her whole body, the way she did when the air changed. "Something's coming. Not guards. Guards yawn."

And then, from the darkness at the center of the hall, from the dais beneath the creed, a lamp flared alight — no match, no taper, just flame arriving where it was wanted — and a voice said, dry as old vellum:

"She always says that. Every one of them says that, in the end. 'You shouldn't have come.' It's practically a citation format."

A figure rose from behind the great desk. Small. Elderly. The lamp-tender, half-blind, the man who never left — except he saw us perfectly, and the lamplight in the niches bent toward him like wheat toward wind.

He looked at Wren, and Wren looked at the book under her hand, and Oscar looked at the space between them, and I understood, with the particular dread of a man whose job used to be standing in doorways, that the rescue was over and something much more complicated had begun.

"Sit," said the lamp-tender kindly. "All of you. The archivist is right — you'll want to hear the second half before you run. Everyone always wishes they had."

Above us, in the rafters, something shifted its weight — and the shelves began, very softly, to hum.
