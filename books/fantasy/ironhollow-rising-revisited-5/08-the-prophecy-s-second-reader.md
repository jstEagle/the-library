## 8. The Prophecy's Second Reader

I had been a prisoner for eleven days, and in that time I had read everything.

This will not surprise anyone who has met me. My mother used to say I'd read the backs of cereal boxes at funerals. What she meant as an insult was, in fact, the truest description of my character ever committed to speech. Put me anywhere — a waiting room, a dungeon, a Vaelmoor record-house with bars on the windows and a guard who hummed off-key — and within the hour I would have located every written word in reach and begun cataloguing them.

So when Oscar Marchetti kicked open the door of my cell with a hedge-witch's fireball still smoking off her shoulder and said, "Wren. We're leaving," I did not immediately stand up.

"One moment," I said. "I'm almost done."

She stared at me. Behind her, a disgraced knight with a face like a closed ledger muttered something about *archivists* in the tone other people reserved for plagues.

"I've transcribed it," I explained, holding up forty-one pages of notes in my own cramped hand. "The full prophecy. The one they sealed away in 1897. They only let me see fragments at first, but the warden was lonely, and lonely men love to feel important, and it turns out if you ask a man to explain his own importance he'll hand you the keys to do it." I tucked the pages into my coat. "All right. Now we can leave."

Oscar looked at me the way she used to look at me across the table at Marlow's, on our first and only date, right before the sky opened above the soup course and swallowed me whole. Like I was a map of a place she'd never believed existed.

"You're not even surprised to see me," she said.

"Wren," said the knight — Sef, I'd learn his name later, though at the time he was mostly eyebrows and disapproval — "we have perhaps four minutes before the garrison—"

"She's coming," Oscar said, and took my hand, and her left hand was cold as river stone and missing something I couldn't name yet, and we ran.

* * *

The waystation smelled of woodsmoke, dried herbs, and the particular staleness of people who have been hiding together long enough to stop apologizing for it. There were five of them, counting me now: Bramble the hedge-witch, who fed me nettle soup and watched me like I might be poisonous; Sef the knight, who sharpened a sword that had clearly seen better centuries; Ilo and Ryn, twin smugglers who finished each other's sentences and each other's thefts; and Oscar, who sat across the fire from me and did not take her eyes off my notebook.

They wanted the story. I gave them the shape of it first — the dinner, the rift, the record-house, the eleven days — and then, because I am who I am, I gave them the rest.

"The prophecy is longer than the Order admits," I said. "What circulates publicly — what got copied into your grandmother's book, Oscar — is maybe a tenth of the text. The Order keeps the full version under seal. They call it the Veredict. I read all of it."

"Out loud?" Ryn asked. He was the one with the scar through his eyebrow; or possibly Ilo was. I hadn't learned to tell them apart yet, which would become relevant later, though I didn't know it then.

"Some of it. The parts that matter." I turned to a page near the end of my transcription. My hands were steady. I want that noted somewhere, by someone, because inside I felt like a kettle left too long on the flame. "There are two figures named. Not one. Two."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the ash-fields with that low moan the Vaelmoor wind makes, like the land itself clearing its throat before bad news.

"The first is the archivist who crosses on the eve of the thaw," I said. "That's me. That part you know. But the second figure — the one the covenant actually requires — is called *the cartographer who redraws*. 'She who maps the unmapped shall hold the pen, and what her hand writes, both realms must keep.'"

Everyone looked at Oscar.

Oscar looked at her own hands, the way she'd been doing since I arrived, flexing the left one like it ached. "That's not possible," she said. "I'm just — I draw places. Places that don't exist. It's a hobby."

"It's never been a hobby," I said gently. "You mapped Vaelmoor before you'd ever seen it. Your flawed little map got you through a border that kills magic. You are the second reader, Oscar. I'm just the footnote."

Bramble snorted into her soup. "Footnote that got herself prophesied. Humility looks strange on you, archivist."

"There's more," I said, and here is where I should have paused, should have thought, should have done anything other than what I actually did, which was to keep reading in the flat, careful voice I use for difficult citations, because precision felt safer than mercy and I chose safety.

"The text names three movements of the covenant: the crossing, the redrawing, and the keeping. And then it names—" I stopped. Restarted. "'One among the company shall carry the knife. The betrayer walks beside the bearers from the first mile, and their faithlessness is older than the journey.'"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"A betrayer," Sef said slowly. "In this company. Which currently consists of six people, four of whom are sitting around this fire."

"Five," said Ilo. "Plus the archivist. Six." He frowned. "Seven if you count the soup."

"Someone in this room," I said, "is named in a century-old prophecy as the one who breaks us."

And that — that was the moment the warmth went out of the room. Not all at once. In degrees, the way heat leaves a body. I watched it happen and hated myself for causing it and knew, with the terrible clarity of someone who has spent her life reading other people's words, that the words were not the problem. The words had always been there, waiting in a sealed book, patient as rot. I had merely carried them into the light.

* * *

Here is what suspicion does to a found family: it does not break it. Breaking would be clean. Suspicion *bends* it, slowly, so that every gesture acquires a second meaning. When Bramble handed Sef the salt, was she testing him? When Ilo volunteered for first watch, was he clearing space for something? When Ryn laughed at my joke about the humming guard — and it had been a good joke — was the laugh a fraction too quick?

I lay awake that night on a pallet between the twins' bedrolls and listened to six people breathe and tried to match each breath to a degree of guilt. This is the occupational hazard of archivists. We believe that everything can be understood if only we read enough of it, and so we read, and read, and sometimes what we find is that understanding is not the same as bearing it.

Oscar found me at the edge of the camp before dawn. She moved quietly for someone whose entire skill set was making marks on paper; I hadn't heard her approach until she settled beside me on the cold stone and pulled her coat around us both without asking.

"You couldn't sleep either," she said.

"I slept fine. I just also read for four hours in between."

A huff of laughter, quickly smothered. Below us, the ash-fields were turning the color of old pewter, and the first thin light caught the rift-scars on the horizon — those seams in the world where the borderlands had begun to eat themselves. From up here they looked almost beautiful. Almost intentional.

"When I came through the border," Oscar said, "it took feeling in my left hand. Payment, Bramble called it. A toll." She flexed the fingers. They moved, but stiffly, like a puppet remembering its strings. "Eleven days I told myself I was coming to save you. That's the story I liked. Damsel, rift, heroic cartographer. Very tidy." She looked at me. "Then you tell me the prophecy says I'm the one holding the pen, and suddenly I'm not the rescuer, I'm the instrument. Do you understand how that feels?"

"Better than you'd think," I said. "I spent eleven days in a cell being told my whole life was a citation. That I crossed over because a book said I would. That my choices were just the prophecy wearing a costume." I hugged my knees. "It makes you doubt everything. Whether you fell in love with Ironhollow or with the idea of escaping it. Whether you said yes to that date because you liked me or because 1897 said you would."

Oscar was quiet for a moment. Then, wryly: "For the record, I asked *you*. Nobody prophesied my taste in archivists."

"No," I admitted. "Your taste appears to be entirely your own fault."

"And yours?"

"My taste," I said, "runs toward women who kick down doors for me. Apparently. It's new information. I'm still cataloguing it."

She smiled, and for a moment the suspicion receded, and it was just the two of us on a cold ledge in a dying realm, and I thought: whatever else the prophecy takes from us, it doesn't get this. It doesn't get the joke.

Then she said, "Who do you think it is?"

And the moment curdled.

"I don't know," I said.

"You've read the whole text. You're the best reader any of us knows. You have a theory."

Of course I had a theory. I had three. I had spent the night assigning motive to every person in the camp the way I'd assign shelf-marks to books, and the exercise had made me sick, and I said none of it, because saying it would make it real, and because — this is the part I keep returning to — because the prophecy says the betrayer's faithlessness is *older than the journey*, and everyone in this company had an older story than the one they'd told me. Everyone. Including, if I was honest, me.

"I think," I said carefully, "that the prophecy wants us to ask that question. I think a text that fractures its own readers is doing half the betrayer's work already. Maybe the cruelest thing the Order ever wrote was that line."

"That's very elegant," Oscar said. "It's also a non-answer."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

She didn't push. That's one of the thousand things I love about her: she knows when a door is locked from the inside. She just put her arm through mine, and we watched the sun come up wrong over Vaelmoor — pale, diluted, like tea steeped twice — and neither of us said the obvious thing, which was that dawn was coming earlier now, and the thaw with it, and the solstice behind that, and the clock underneath everything, ticking.

* * *

We broke camp midmorning. It went badly, the way mornings go badly when six people no longer trust the air they share.

Sef announced the route — south along the ridge, then east through the Riftlands toward the Covenant Spire — and Ilo said, "Why east?" in a tone that was one degree too flat, and Ryn said, "Because the Spire's east, idiot," in a tone that was one degree too sharp, and suddenly the two of them were arguing about a compass point as though the compass point had insulted their mother, and Bramble had to shout over them that if they wasted daylight she'd turn them both into something with fewer opinions.

Sef stood apart through all of it, arms folded, watching the group watch him. I saw him notice being noticed. I saw something move behind his face — guilt, or its rehearsal — and I marked it, the way I mark everything, helplessly, usefully.

Oscar unrolled one of her maps on a flat stone to plan the day's march, and I watched the others gather around it, and I watched them gather *carefully* — positioning themselves where they could see each other, the way soldiers eat with their backs to walls. Even the map, her beautiful impossible map, had become evidence. If she drew the route, whoever memorized it could sell it. If she didn't, she was withholding. There was no innocent option anymore. The prophecy had seen to that.

"We'll need water before the Riftlands," Bramble said. "There's a spring past the ridge. Or there was, forty years ago. Nothing out here stays where you leave it."

"Nothing anywhere does," said Ryn, and laughed, alone, and nobody joined him, and I saw his face do something complicated and quick before it smoothed over.

I filed that away too. I filed everything away. That is my gift and my sickness: I am a vessel for other people's texts, and I could not stop reading the one unfolding in front of me, every glance a marginal note, every silence a redacted passage.

As we formed up to march, Oscar fell into step beside me and lowered her voice.

"The line about the betrayer," she murmured. "Read it to me again. Exactly."

"'One among the company shall carry the knife,'" I recited. "'The betrayer walks beside the bearers from the first mile, and their faithlessness is older than the journey.'"

"From the first mile," she repeated. Her eyes narrowed, working the phrase the way she worked terrain — looking for the pass through it. "Wren. Who was in the company from the first mile? Because you weren't. You were in a cell. I wasn't — I crossed after. By that line, it's one of the four who found me in the ash-fields."

"Or," I said, "the prophecy counts differently than we do. Prophecies usually do. It's practically their hobby."

Ahead of us, the ridge rose black against the pewter sky, and beyond it, faint as a vein under skin, shimmered the first of the Riftlands — territory that no longer agreed to exist. Sef took point. The twins flanked our supplies. Bramble brought up the rear, muttering wards under her breath, and I noticed — because I notice everything, God help me — that her wards were drawn facing inward as well as out.

Six people walking toward a spire, arranged like a diagram of distrust.

And beneath my feet, faint but unmistakable, the ground gave a shudder — the deep, tectonic shiver of a rift opening somewhere ahead of us, hungry and unhurried, as if the land itself had read the same prophecy and decided to hurry us along.
