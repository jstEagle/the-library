## 9. Road Through the Riftlands

I've been a smuggler for eleven years, and in that time I've learned exactly three things worth teaching. One: never trust a road that's too convenient. Two: never trust a man who says "trust me." Three: if you're going to lie to your twin brother, at least be good at it, because he can smell a bad lie like a dog smells fear.

I'm telling you this up front so you understand that when I say the Riftlands were the worst stretch of country I have ever crossed, I am not exaggerating for effect. I don't do that. Ryn does that. I'm the honest one.

The Riftlands sat between the ash-fields and the Spire road like a wound somebody had tried to close with spit and prayer. From the ridge above them they looked almost ordinary — rolling hills, dead grass, a riverbed or two — except that here and there the hills simply stopped. Not ended. *Stopped*, the way a sentence stops when the writer forgets what he was saying. You'd see grass, then a seam of pale light hanging in the air like a scar, and beyond it more grass that might have been the same grass or might have been grass from somewhere else entirely, growing at a slightly wrong angle to the sun.

"Rifts," Sef said, stating the obvious with the flat authority of a man who'd once been paid to state obvious things for a living. "Unstable ones. They move."

"They move," Bramble agreed. She was walking beside me with her staff slung across her back and her satchel clinking with the particular music of a hedge-witch who hoards bottles the way other people hoard regrets. "Forty years ago you could cross this whole valley on the old roads. Now the roads are... suggestions."

"I love suggestions," I said. "Suggestions are how I make my living."

Nobody laughed. Tough crowd. Exiles are a tough crowd; humor doesn't keep you warm out here, but I've never found anything else that does, so I keep carrying it anyway.

Oscar stood at the edge of the ridge with her map case open, and I watched her face while she looked at the Riftlands, because I've made a study of faces — it's the second most useful skill a smuggler has, right after running — and hers was doing something complicated. She wasn't afraid. Or she was afraid, but underneath the fear was something hungrier. She looked at that broken country the way Ryn looks at an unlocked window.

"That one," she said finally, pointing at a seam maybe half a mile down the slope. "It's stable. Relatively. It pulses — opens wide, then narrows. If we cross at the narrow point, we lose maybe twenty feet of ground instead of twenty miles."

"How can you possibly know that?" Wren asked. She'd been quiet all morning, quieter than usual, and there were ink stains on her fingers that hadn't been there yesterday. She'd been reading something in the night. Nobody asked what. We were all very politely not asking each other things; it was becoming our group's chief form of intimacy.

"Because I drew it," Oscar said, and held up her map.

Now, I want to be clear about something. I've seen maps before. Good ones, bad ones, forged ones — I've sold forged ones, which is a sin I'll answer for eventually, though probably not soon, since I plan to live a long dishonest life. A map is supposed to be a picture of a place. This was not a picture of a place. The lines on Oscar's map were moving. Slowly, lazily, the way water moves under ice, but moving — and where the rifts crossed the paper, the ink had pooled into bright seams that pulsed, narrow and wide, narrow and wide, keeping time with something.

"Your maps aren't supposed to do that," Sef said.

"No," Oscar agreed. "They're really not."

* * *

Here is what crossing the Riftlands is actually like, for those of you fortunate enough never to have done it: imagine walking through your own house blindfolded, knowing that any door you open might open onto the sea. That's the Riftlands. The geography isn't just broken; it's *contested*. A hill will be a hill for an hour, and then a rift will drift over it like weather, and when the rift moves on, the hill is gone — not destroyed, just *elsewhere*, filed away in some corner of Vaelmoor or Ironhollow or wherever lost geography goes to wait. The locals — the few exiles who still risked the crossing — called it the land being "unwritten."

Bramble told me that on the way down the ridge, in her matter-of-fact way, while she handed out strips of dried meat that tasted like punishment.

"Unwritten," I repeated. "That's comforting."

"It's accurate. Comfort is not my department." She squinted at the nearest seam. "When a rift takes ground, it doesn't kill what's on it. Mostly. It just... removes it from agreement. A tree taken by a rift stops agreeing to be a tree. Sometimes you find them again, decades later, still standing, still alive, having missed nothing — because for them, nothing happened. No time passed inside the seam."

"And sometimes?"

"Sometimes they come back wrong." She said it the way you'd say *sometimes it rains*. "Don't stand near the seams, Ilo."

"I never stand anywhere, Bramble. Standing is for people with slower feet."

But I did stand near a seam, later that first day, because that's the kind of genius I am. We were picking our way along a shelf of rock above a dry riverbed, single file, and the shelf simply ended — no warning, no crumbling edge, just rock, then a seam of light two feet wide, then rock again on the far side, continuing as if nothing had interrupted it. Except the rock on the far side was a different color. Greyer. And the lichen on it grew in spirals instead of patches.

"Jump," Sef said from the front.

"It's two feet," I said. "I jump two feet for breakfast."

"Then jump."

I jumped. And mid-jump — I swear this on our mother's grave, may she rest and may she also forgive me for the headstone debt — mid-jump, the seam widened. Just a breath. Just enough. For half a heartbeat I was nowhere. Not falling, not flying, not anywhere. There was no ground under me and no sky over me and no *me*, either, if you want the truth of it, no Ilo, no hands, no name, just a kind of enormous attentionless dark, like being a word that hasn't been thought yet.

Then I hit grey rock on hands and knees, and I was Ilo again, and my heart was trying to exit through my teeth.

Ryn hauled me up by the collar. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to; his face said everything, in the language only twins speak, the one that's mostly insults with a thin mortar of love between the bricks. *You idiot. You absolute idiot. Don't you ever.*

"Fine," I croaked. "Fine. Point taken. Seams: bad. Noted for future reference."

But here's the thing I didn't say out loud, the thing I kept folded up small behind my ribs where Ryn couldn't read it: for half a heartbeat, being unwritten hadn't been terrifying. It had been *quiet*. All the debts and the lies and the weight of my brother's safety, all of it gone, because there had been no me to carry it. And some traitorous cellar of my soul had found that restful.

I'm telling you this because it matters later. Everything matters later. That's the great joke of a life.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, and I took first watch, because I sleep badly in the Riftlands even by my standards. Around midnight — or what I guessed was midnight; the stars out here drifted, slowly rearranging themselves like bored courtiers — I heard movement and found Oscar awake, sitting cross-legged on a flat stone with her maps spread around her like a card sharp's table.

"Can't sleep either?" I asked.

"I slept in Ironhollow my whole life," she said. "Twenty-six years of a valley where nothing magical ever happens. I think I'm owed a little insomnia now, as compensation."

"Back pay. I respect it." I sat down at the edge of her map-circle, careful not to touch anything. Even exhausted, even half-asleep, I could tell the papers were *busy*. "Can I ask you something, cartographer? And remember, I'm a smuggler, so flattery is probably involved."

"Ask."

"When you look at these rifts — the seams, the unwritten places — what do you see? Honestly."

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then she put her finger on the map, on one of those pooled bright seams, and pressed gently, and — I saw it happen, I was three feet away, I'll swear to it in any court that will have me — the seam on the *map* narrowed, and out in the dark, half a mile away, a line of pale light in the grass dimmed like a lamp turned low.

"The same thing I've always seen," she said softly. "Shape. Distance. Relationship. A rift isn't a hole, Ilo. It's a disagreement about where things are. And disagreements have edges. Edges can be drawn." She lifted her finger. The distant seam breathed wide again. "I stabilized that one for about ten seconds. Maybe less. It costs me something — I don't know what yet, exactly, but it costs. Like bleeding from a place I can't see."

"Why would you tell me that?" I asked. "You've known me four days. Four days ago I would have sold that information for a warm meal and a boat ticket."

"I know," she said, and smiled at me, tired and unguarded. "But you came back for Ryn at the ford last spring — Bramble told us. And you gave up your cut of the Emberline run to buy his medicine. Smugglers don't do that. So whatever you call yourself, I don't think that's what you are."

Well. What do you do with that? I did what I always do. I made a joke — something about how she clearly needed more sleep if she was trusting hedge-witch gossip — and I went back to my watch, and I didn't look at the maps again.

But I thought about it. Because she was right, and because being seen accurately by someone you've known four days is either a gift or a threat, depending on the day, and I hadn't decided which.

And because — third thing, the thing I haven't taught anyone — when she pressed her finger down and the world obeyed her, even for ten seconds, I felt something I hadn't let myself feel since I was a boy watching Ryn get sick: hope. Ugly, inconvenient hope. Because if her maps could hold ground steady, then maybe there was a road through this mess after all. And roads mean cargo, and cargo means delivery, and I have spent my whole life delivering things to places they were promised.

Maybe what I was carrying, without meaning to, was five idiots and a prophecy.

* * *

The next day, the Riftlands tested us properly.

It started fine. Oscar led us along a route she'd plotted overnight — "the seams breathe in patterns," she said, "and patterns can be mapped" — and for six hours we moved through broken country like it was merely difficult instead of impossible. She'd stop at every rise, check her papers against the horizon, and call the turns: left along the dry channel, wide of the leaning stone, cross the seam *now*, while it's narrow, run, don't walk. Twice we crossed seams so narrow they were barely cracks, and both times I jumped them thinking hard about being a person, just in case.

Around noon we came to the place the locals call the Maw — a rift so old and so wide it had become a landmark, a canyon of pale light half a mile across, humming faintly, the sound of it like a wet finger on a glass rim. There was no crossing it. Every account agreed on that. You went around the Maw, adding three days to the journey, or you didn't go.

Oscar stood at its edge for a long time.

"There's a pattern in the pulse," she said. "It's not random. It swells and narrows like breathing, and the rhythm repeats every—" she counted under her lips, tapping the map— "every ninety-one seconds. When it narrows, the floor of it comes back. Briefly. Ground returns when the rift contracts. If someone held it narrow—"

"Held it how?" Sef asked.

"With a map." She said it plainly, no drama, which somehow made it worse. "If I draw the Maw as it is when contracted — draw the ground that should be there — and anchor the drawing, the rift may agree to it. Temporarily. Long enough to cross."

"Temporarily," Bramble repeated. "Define temporarily."

"Minutes. Perhaps. And it will cost me. I told you — it bleeds me somewhere I can't see. Doing it to a rift this size..." She flexed her left hand. I noticed the fingers were pale, bloodless at the tips, and had been since yesterday. She noticed me noticing and closed the hand into a fist. "It's the best option. The Order is behind us and the solstice is ahead of us. We don't have three days."

Nobody liked it. I watched them all not like it — Sef's jaw set like a drawn bow, Bramble already digging in her satchel for whatever witches dig in for, Wren's face gone white and stubborn at once. And underneath their not-liking, I watched something else, something I recognized because I'd felt it myself the night before: they believed she could do it. Four days, and they believed.

"Right," I said, because someone had to break the spell and it's traditionally my job. "New rule. Nobody crosses until the cartographer says. Nobody dawdles when she says go. And nobody—" I pointed at Ryn, who opened his mouth— "*nobody* jumps early. We clear?"

"We're clear," Ryn said quietly, and squeezed my shoulder once, hard, which in twin means *I'm frightened for you and I will deny it forever*.

Oscar knelt at the edge of the Maw with her papers weighted by stones, and she began to draw. I've watched forgers work, and I've watched Ryn pick a lock in the dark, and I have never seen anything like the concentration on her face. She wasn't copying the ground. She was *arguing* with it — pen moving fast, lines going down sure and clean, and with every stroke the hum of the Maw stuttered, caught, changed pitch.

"Thirty seconds," she said through her teeth. "When I say, you run. All of you. Don't stop, don't look back, don't help me. Sef first, then Bramble and Wren, then the twins. Go on my word."

"You come last," Sef said. It wasn't a question.

"I come last."

The Maw's hum climbed. The pale light of it shuddered — and narrowed. Where the canyon of nothing had been, ground flickered into being: grey rock, real rock, a bridge of it spanning the whole impossible width, wavering at the edges like a reflection in disturbed water.

"*Go,*" Oscar said.

Sef went, boots hammering the phantom stone. Bramble shoved Wren ahead of her. Ryn grabbed my arm and we ran together, twin hearts slamming in twin chests, and the bridge buzzed under our feet like a struck wire, and halfway across I made the mistake of looking back.

Oscar was on her knees at the edge, one hand flat on the map, the other braced against the ground, and her left hand — the one that had been pale since yesterday — was *grey*. Not pale. Grey, like the far-side rock, like unwritten things, the color draining up her wrist in slow veins. Her mouth was open in a soundless count. The bridge was fraying behind us, edges dissolving into light.

We made the far side. Sef caught Wren as she stumbled; Bramble turned immediately, staff raised, watching the seam where Oscar still knelt alone.

"Ninety-one seconds," Oscar whispered, to herself, to the map, to the Maw. "Hold. Hold. *Hold.*"

And then she was running too, across her own drawing, and the bridge died thirty feet behind her, and she came up the bank grey-handed and shaking and grinning like a woman who had just pickpocketed a god.

"It worked," she said. "Mostly."

"Your hand," Wren said sharply, catching it, turning it over. The grey was receding, slow as a tide going out, but reluctantly, leaving the fingertips bloodless.

"It comes back. It came back last time." Oscar flexed her fingers. "It's the price. Maps cost. I knew that going in — every cartographer knows you trade something for accuracy. I just never expected to pay in *body*."

"Then we ration you," Sef said flatly. "No more stabilizations unless I approve them. That's not a request."

"It's a bit rich, coming from the man who guarded the Spire when it fell," I said, mostly to lighten the air, and instantly regretted it, because Sef went white and Bramble put a hand on his arm, and Wren looked at him with an expression I couldn't read and didn't like.

"Later," Sef said, to all of us, to none of us. "There's a confession I owe this company, and you'll have it tonight. But not on an empty stomach with the Order at our backs. Walk first."

So we walked. And I fell into step beside Oscar, because someone had to, and because I wanted to ask her something I'd been chewing on since midnight.

"That price," I said, keeping my voice low. "The grey. Does it spread? Does it ever *stay*?"

She was quiet for three steps. Four. Five.

"I don't know," she said finally. "But I do know this, Ilo — the Spire is bigger than the Maw. Whatever reforging the covenant costs, it'll cost more than a hand." She looked down at her own fingers, flexing them, counting them like coins. "And the terrible thing is, I'd pay it. I'd pay it gladly. That's what frightens me. Not the price. How easily I'd pay it."

Ahead of us, the Riftlands rolled on toward the Spire road, seamed and scarred and breathing. Behind us, far off, a horn sounded — one note, then another, deliberate, spaced. Not a hunting horn. A *summons*.

Bramble stopped dead. "That's an Order signal," she said. "Three notes means they've found the trail. Four means they've found *us*."

The horn sounded a fourth time.

I looked at Ryn. Ryn looked at the sky, whistling, casual as Sunday — and I want it on the record, here, now, before whatever comes next: my brother cannot whistle. He has never been able to whistle. And he was doing it badly, deliberately, the way a man does something wrong on purpose so his twin will notice.

I noticed.

I just didn't yet understand what I was noticing.
