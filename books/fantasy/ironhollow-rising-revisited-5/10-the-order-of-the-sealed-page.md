## 10. The Order of the Sealed Page

There are three things a smuggler learns before he learns anything else: how to read a road, how to read a mark, and how to lie with his whole face while his stomach files a formal complaint.

I was doing all three at once when the Order found us.

The road out of the riftlands ran along a ridge of black stone that Ilo and I had used twice before, back when our cargo was silk and stolen relics instead of a cartographer, an archivist, a disgraced knight, a hedge-witch, and whatever it is I am now, which I prefer not to examine in daylight. The ridge was good ground. High enough to see trouble coming, narrow enough that trouble could only come from two directions.

I'd told Sef as much that morning. "Two directions," I said. "We post watchers at both ends and we're untouchable."

He'd grunted, which from Sef is a signed affidavit.

What I hadn't said — what I will never say, not here, not in this telling, maybe not ever — is that I knew the ridge was good ground because I'd described it to the Order's outriders four nights ago, in exchange for a promise about my brother's heartbeat.

So when the white-and-grey cloaks appeared at the western end of the ridge at dusk, moving in the neat, patient lines of people who already know where you'll run, nobody was more surprised than my face.

* * *

Let me back up, because a man should at least be honest on paper, even if he can't manage it anywhere else.

The Order of the Sealed Page wears grey over white, carries no weapons you can see, and smiles like accountants. They were founded, according to every record Ilo and I ever fenced or stole, to *preserve* the covenant between Vaelmoor and the realm beyond the veil — the one Oscar comes from, the one she calls home with a capital H. Guardians of the pact. Keepers of the law. Very fond of seals, hence the name, and very fond of pages, which they keep locked in record-houses like the one we broke Wren out of.

For forty years, since the Spire fell and the covenant shattered, they've been the closest thing either realm has to a government. And for forty years, the rifts have been eating the borders like moths in a wool warehouse, and the Order has been very busy *managing* the problem. Studying it. Documenting it. Sealing the worst tears and letting the rest spread, slowly, the way you let a fire burn in a neighbor's field if your own granary is full.

Bramble figured it out first. Of course she did. Hedge-witches spend their whole lives being underestimated by institutions, so they get very good at reading them.

"It doesn't add up," she said, three nights before the ridge, hunched over her little cookfire with her bandaged hands wrapped around a tin cup. We were camped in a hollow beneath a dead watchtower, the kind the old covenant used to staff with wardens on both sides. "If the Order wanted the rifts closed, they've had forty years and every archive in Vaelmoor. The Spire's repair rites are written down. I've *seen* fragments. They're not lost knowledge. They're *filed* knowledge."

"Filed where?" Sef asked.

"The deep vaults. Under seal." She looked up, and there was something in her face I didn't like, because Bramble's face usually holds exactly two expressions — dry amusement and dry exhaustion — and this was neither. "Under their seal."

Oscar had been sketching by firelight, the way she does, her ruined left hand braced against her knee while the right one moved. She'd stopped drawing. "You're saying they're not failing to fix it."

"I'm saying," Bramble said, "that a fire this convenient doesn't stay lit by accident."

* * *

Here's what we pieced together over the next two days, from Bramble's fragments, from Wren's memory of everything she'd read in the record-house, and from documents Ilo lifted off a dead courier whose horse we did not steal, mostly because it bit me.

Before the covenant broke, magic flowed between the realms like water between connected cisterns. Both sides drew from the shared level. Ironhollow — Oscar's valley — was built as a valve, a place deliberately kept dry, though I don't think anyone alive remembered why until Oscar started asking questions nobody wanted answered.

When the Spire fell, the flow didn't just stop. It *diverted*. Every thread of power that used to weave between realms got pulled through channels only the Order controlled. Their record-houses aren't archives. They're reservoirs. That cold hum you feel walking past one, the way charms work a little too well within a mile of their walls — that's not preservation magic. That's *storage*.

"They didn't fail to stop the flood," Wren said quietly, the night before the ridge. She has a way of saying quiet things that makes everyone lean in, which I suspect she learned in a library. "They dug the canal."

"And the prophecy?" Oscar asked. Her voice was careful. Everything about Oscar is careful, except her maps, which are reckless in a way I've come to admire. "The one that named Wren a century ago. Was that theirs too?"

Wren and Bramble exchanged a look, the kind married couples exchange, or twins, or people who've read the same terrible book.

"Sealed Page doctrine says the prophecy was a warning," Wren said. "That it foretold the covenant's breaking so the Order could prepare."

"But?" Sef said.

"But prophecies don't name specific archivists a hundred years out unless someone wrote the name down *after* meeting her," Wren said. "And the book I found myself in was dated 1897. My grandmother wasn't born yet. Somebody backdated it. Somebody wrote a script and then spent a century casting for the role."

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a rift sighed — they make a sound like a slow breath through teeth, if you've never heard one, and I don't recommend acquiring the experience.

It was Oscar who finally said it, in a voice flat as fresh parchment:

"They didn't write a prophecy about the future. They wrote a job posting. And then they waited a hundred years to hire."

* * *

Which brings us back to the ridge, and to me, standing in the last light with white-and-grey cloaks advancing from the west and my brother's life folded up small in my chest like a letter I keep rereading.

There were twelve of them visible. With the Order, visible is a courtesy, not a count. They advanced without hurry, which frightened me more than a charge would have. Hurry means uncertainty. Patience means they'd already done the arithmetic.

"Sef," I called. "West end. Twelve, maybe more behind the rise."

He was already moving. I'll give him this — the man is fifty years old and moves like a rumor of himself, all economy, no waste. He posted Bramble at the east approach with Ilo, put Wren and Oscar in the lee of a boulder with strict instructions involving the words "*do not*" and "*under any circumstances*", and took the west slope alone, sword drawn, looking like a painting of a better age.

"You could surrender," the lead cloak called up. He had a pleasant voice. Accountant's voice. "The Order has no quarrel with the smugglers. Only with the bearer."

Meaning Oscar. Meaning the woman who could redraw the covenant and drain their canal.

"We're a package deal," I shouted back, because apparently my mouth had decided to commit to honesty for once. "No refunds."

The lead cloak sighed, genuinely regretful, and raised one hand.

The air above the ridge *opened*.

Not a rift — those are wild things, ragged-edged, hungry. This was a cut. Clean, surgical, deliberate, held open by six of the cloaks chanting in unison, and through it came the smell of stored magic, ozone and ink and something older, and out of it stepped things the Order keeps the way other institutions keep guard dogs.

I'm not going to describe them properly. Some things lose power when you describe them, and some things gain it, and these were the second kind. I'll say only that they were wrong in the specific way a room is wrong when someone has died in it, and that they moved like they were being *read aloud*.

"Bramble!" Sef roared. "East side too!"

Of course there were cloaks on the east side. There had always been going to be cloaks on the east side. I had told them the east side was watched lightly, because Ilo was there, and I had needed the Order to believe my brother would be somewhere survivable.

The fight lasted four minutes and cost us everything we owned.

* * *

You want the details? Fine. A smuggler's honest ledger, since I'm apparently keeping one now.

Sef killed two of the wrongnesses outright, which should not be possible and was magnificent to watch; he fights like a man settling a very old debt. Bramble burned a hole in the world east of us big enough to drive a cart through, using a spell I know cost her something permanent, because afterward her hands shook for hours and she hid it by holding her cup with both of them, which she'd never done before. Ilo fought beside her like they shared one body, which, in fairness, they sort of do.

Wren dragged Oscar behind the boulder and held her there, and Oscar screamed at her to let go, and Wren didn't, and later I understood that Wren had seen what Oscar hadn't: that the moment Oscar reached for her map-magic on that ridge, the Order would stop trying to capture her and start trying to erase her, and erasure, per their own records, is a procedure they're very good at.

Me? I fought. I want that in the ledger. Whatever else is true about me, I fought, badly, with a knife I've carried since I was fifteen, and I took a cut across the forearm from a cloak who apologized while doing it, which is the most Order thing I've ever experienced.

And then the lead accountant-cloak did the thing that turned my stomach from guilt into something harder.

They burned the camp.

Not attacked it. *Burned* it, methodically, with a cantrip that ate cloth and leather and paper and left stone untouched. Our supplies. Our maps — the spare ones, the trade goods, the food for nine days, the water skins, Ilo's medicine for the lung trouble he's had since we were children, the letters from our mother that I still carried and never read because reading them felt like opening a door I'd nailed shut.

They burned it all while fighting us, unhurried, thorough, and the lead cloak called up the slope in that pleasant voice:

"You should understand what this is. This is not punishment. This is *logistics*. In nine days you will be too weak to travel, and we will collect the bearer at our leisure. Tell her the Order thanks her for making the prophecy so easy to follow."

Then they closed their clean little cut in the air and withdrew, because they'd gotten what they came for, and it wasn't blood. It was our winter.

* * *

We sat in the ashes for a long time.

Ilo went through the wreckage twice, then a third time, finding nothing, because there was nothing to find, and each pass made his breathing worse, and each rattle of it landed on me like a debt collector's knock. Bramble sat with her shaking hands in her lap and stared at the burned spot where her herb satchel had been, and I knew — everyone knew — that some of what burned there couldn't be replaced in any market in either realm.

Sef stood watch, because Sef always stands watch, especially when standing watch is pointless.

And Oscar Marchetti knelt down in the ash of her own pack and picked up, of all things, the charred corner of a map she'd drawn in Ironhollow, before any of this, a map of a realm that existed only in rumor. She held it the way you hold a photograph of someone gone.

"They weren't trying to kill us," she said. Her voice was very steady, which is how I've learned to tell when she's furious. "They were trying to *starve the timeline*. Nine days to the solstice. They just made sure we arrive desperate."

"Desperate people take bad deals," Wren said softly.

"Desperate people," Oscar said, standing, folding the burnt map-corner into her coat like a relic, "take shortcuts. And I am done letting the people who wrote this story decide what the shortcut costs."

She looked around at us — at the hedge-witch with the shaking hands, the knight with the old debt, the twins with one lung between them, the archivist with ash in her hair — and something in her face settled, the way ground settles under a foundation stone.

"They hoarded the magic. They broke the covenant to do it. They wrote a prophecy and cast it like a net and let two realms bleed for forty years to keep their reservoirs full." She said it plainly, no thunder, and somehow that was worse than thunder. "Fine. Then we don't restore their law. We don't reseal their Spire under their terms. When we reach it — and we will reach it, hungry if we have to — I'm not reforging the old pact."

"What then?" Bramble asked.

Oscar smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman looking at blank paper.

"A new one. In my own hand."

* * *

We walked through the night, because stopping meant starving sooner. Around midnight, Ilo fell in beside me, wheezing a little, and bumped my shoulder with his the way he's done since we were boys stealing apples.

"You went pale when they burned the letters," he said.

"I go pale at all fires. Occupational hazard."

"Mm." He looked straight ahead. My brother has always had the infuriating gift of seeing through me while pretending to look at scenery. "Whatever you're carrying, Ry. Whatever it is. You should put it down before it burns *us*."

I laughed, because laughing is the best lock I own.

Ahead of us, Sef suddenly stopped, one fist raised, and the whole column froze. He was staring down into the valley below, where a firelight glowed that shouldn't have been there — a single campfire, small and defiant, in country where the Order had just proven it could find us whenever it pleased.

Sef's face, when he turned back to us, was the face of a man who recognizes handwriting.

"That fire," he said slowly, "is laid the way the old Spire wardens used to lay them. Three stones. Green wood banked low." His jaw worked. "I know that fire. I taught that fire."

He started down toward it before any of us could stop him, and none of us did, because the look on his face made it clear that whatever he was walking toward, he'd been walking toward it for forty years.
