## 11. Sef's Confession

There is a particular kind of silence that falls around a fire when everyone is thinking the same thing and no one will say it. I have heard it on battlefields, in barracks after a court-martial, once at my own father's funeral when the priest said something generous about a man who had never once been generous in his life. It is the silence of people waiting for someone else to be brave.

We had been sitting in it for two days.

The waystation Bramble had led us to was an old toll-house, back when the rift-road between realms had been something you paid a copper to cross instead of something that ate your horse. The roof leaked in one corner with admirable consistency. Ryn had claimed the driest bedroll by pretending to be asleep whenever anyone came near it, which was, I will grant him, the most honest thing he'd done all journey.

Oscar sat across the fire from me, sketching. She always sketched now — her maps had begun doing things maps shouldn't do, steadying ground under our feet in the riftlands, and she'd taken to drawing constantly, as if the talent were a bird that might fly off if she stopped watching it. Wren sat beside her with the prophecy book open on her knees, though she wasn't reading it. She was watching me over the top of it, the way one watches a kettle one suspects of harboring opinions.

Bramble's wound was healing. Slowly, badly, the way hedge-witch wounds heal — she refused anything stronger than her own poultices, on the grounds that "clean magic is for people who plan to live somewhere afterward." Her arm was bound to her chest and she was using her good hand to stir a pot of something that smelled like pine tar and regret.

Ilo was gone. Taken at the ford's far bank, dragged through a rift by Order hands while we held the line at the water. Every hour that passed made him harder to get back, and every hour that passed also made the other question louder.

Because the prophecy named a betrayer. And because I had not told them what I did.

* * *

Let me tell you how a knight comes to betray a Spire.

You imagine treachery as a door opened in the night, a knife, a purse of coin. It isn't. Treachery is usually just arithmetic performed too early, or too late, or by the wrong person. Mine was performed over eleven years of standing guard at the Covenant Spire, watching what the old law *did*.

The covenant, as written, bound the realms together — shared magic, shared law, a council drawn from both sides of every border. That was the story they taught squires. What the covenant actually was, by the time I took my post, was a ledger. Vaelmoor's magic flowed where the law directed it, and the law directed it wherever the Order of the Sealed Page said, and the Order said it should flow to the same forty families it had always flowed to. Ironhollow got sealed off entirely — declared "magic-dead by design," a buffer, a sacrifice zone — and nobody in the Spire so much as adjusted their collar. I stood at my post above the great seal and watched petitioners turned away. Farmers whose wells had soured when their ley-line was rerouted. A woman from the City of Borrowed Names — before it had that name, when it was just a ditch full of people the law didn't recognize — begging for her son's name to be entered in the rolls so he could legally exist. Turned away. Turned away. Turned away.

For eleven years I wrote reports saying all was well. For eleven years I polished armor that had never seen an honest fight, guarding a door that only ever opened inward, for people who already had everything the door protected.

And then, forty years ago — no. Let me be exact, since exactness is the last virtue I have left. Thirty-eight years ago, on the eve of the solstice, the Order came to the Spire with a writ under the old law itself, ordering the covenant's "provisional suspension." They called it maintenance. They meant to break it and blame the breaking on rifts, on instability, on the realms' own unfitness for each other. And they needed one knight to look away for one hour.

They didn't need to bribe me. That's the part that shames me most, in the long dark watches when shame keeps its own calendar. They didn't bribe me. They showed me the writ, and I read it, and I thought: *finally.* Finally someone is going to do something about this rotten law. I let them in because I believed the old covenant deserved to fall. I told myself the Order would build something better on the rubble.

They built a hoard. They built forty families' worth of better. They built the rifts themselves, uncontrolled, devouring the borders, and then they built the prophecy — a century-old book, backdated, sealed — to control whoever came next.

So when I say I betrayed the Spire, understand: I did it with a clear conscience. That is the worst sentence I know how to write, and I have written a great many sentences trying to avoid it.

* * *

The fire cracked. Bramble's pot bubbled its pine-tar regret. Oscar's pencil whispered.

"I can hear you deciding," I said.

Everyone looked up. Wren closed the book on her finger like a woman marking a page she intended to return to.

"Deciding what?" Ryn asked, with the innocence of a man who had been deciding exactly that.

"Whether I'm your betrayer." I set down the whetstone I'd been pretending to use. My sword hadn't needed sharpening since the ford; I'd been stropping it out of nerves, which is a habit I recommend against, as it makes the nervousness visible from across a room. "You've all been polite about it for two days, and politeness is worse than accusation, so let's have it done."

Nobody spoke. Then Oscar, of course, because she was the one who asked questions the rest of us were avoiding: "Is there something to have done?"

I looked at the fire. Fire is easier to address than people; it doesn't flinch.

"My name," I said, "is Sefarian Voss. Knight-Commander of the Third Watch, Covenant Spire. Or I was." I pulled off my left gauntlet — I still wore gauntlets indoors, another tell I'd never managed to kill — and turned my palm up. The brand was there, faded to a pale scar: the Spire's sigil, a tower within a circle. Every knight bore it. "I held the Third Watch for eleven years. And thirty-eight years ago, on the night the covenant fell, I was the man on the wall. And I let them in."

The silence changed quality. Before, it had been suspicion. Now it was something colder and more careful, the silence of people recalculating distances to exits.

"You *let* them in," Wren repeated. Not a question. An archivist checking a citation.

"They had a writ. Under the old law. Ordering the seal's suspension for maintenance." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way my laughs always did around this subject. "Maintenance. Like the covenant was a roof."

"And you believed them." Oscar's voice was level. Pencil still. That frightened me more than anger would have.

"I believed the law deserved breaking. That part was true. What I didn't believe — what I refused to check, because checking would have cost me my comfortable treason — was what they intended to build after." I met her eyes. Hers were gray as survey ink. "I'm not going to insult you by claiming I was deceived. I was *willing*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and I have spent thirty-eight years learning exactly how much."

Bramble stirred her pot. "Hm," she said.

"That's it? *Hm?*"

"What do you want, a verdict?" She didn't look up. "You confessed before anyone accused you. In my experience guilty men wait to be caught. This one's been sitting on his confession like a hen on a bad egg for two days because he couldn't stand the waiting more than he feared the judging." She pointed her ladle at me. "That's not innocence. But it's not treachery either. It's just a man who's tired."

"I held the line at the ford," I said. "I'd hold it again. But that doesn't—"

"No," Wren said quietly. "It doesn't."

She had opened the prophecy book again. She turned it toward the firelight, and I saw the page she'd marked — the passage she'd decoded in the record-house, the one naming the betrayer within the company. Her finger rested beneath the line. She didn't read it aloud. She didn't need to. We all knew its shape by now: *one among them who has already broken what he swore.*

"It fits you," she said. "I won't pretend it doesn't. You broke an oath. The prophecy says the betrayer breaks oaths."

"The prophecy," Bramble said, "was written by the same people who bribed him. Pardon me — the same people he *let in*." Her tone made it clear which version she preferred. "Do you trust their casting choices?"

"I trust," Wren said, with the terrible gentleness archivists deploy instead of knives, "that the words mean what they say. That's the whole discipline. Everything else is hope wearing scholarship's coat."

* * *

Ryn, of all people, broke the deadlock. He sat up in his stolen dry bedroll and said, "Ask him the real question, then."

"And what's that?" I asked.

"Whether you'd do it again." He shrugged. "Not whether you're sorry. Sorry's cheap, I sell sorry wholesale. Whether, standing on that wall tonight, knowing everything you know now — you open the gate."

I considered it honestly, because he deserved that much and because I had rehearsed the dishonest answer for decades and was sick of its taste.

"No," I said. "Not because the old law was good. It wasn't. It was a machine for grinding the many into the few's bread. But you don't burn a bridge while people are still crossing it. They broke the covenant with the realms mid-passage — farms, families, whole valleys like hers—" I nodded at Oscar, "—sealed off and abandoned. If I'd known the price would be paid by everyone *except* the men holding the writ, I'd have put them off that wall myself. I was angry at the law. I aimed my anger at the wrong target, and the recoil killed thousands."

Ryn chewed on that. "See," he said finally, to the others, "that's the answer of a man who wouldn't do it again. I'd know. I've given the other answer recently enough to recognize the shape." He said it lightly, and it landed heavily, because we all knew what he'd given the other answer *for*, even if only half of us knew the details yet. Ilo's absence sat in the middle of our camp like a stone none of us would step around aloud.

Sef, I told myself. Careful. You are not owed grace here. You are being *audited*.

Wren looked at me a long moment. Then she did the thing that undid me more thoroughly than any verdict could have: she turned the prophecy page past the betrayer-line, to the verses after it, and read them aloud.

"*When the oath-breaker stands in the ash of his keeping, / and names his fault before the company, / the road shall judge him not by the falling, / but by what he raises in the ruin.*"

Silence again. Different silence. The kind that comes after a bell stops ringing.

"That's not in the Order's copies," Wren said. "I checked, in the record-house. They cut these lines out of every edition. Four lines, cleanly excised, and the binding re-sewn so you'd never know pages were missing." She looked at me. "Someone didn't want the betrayer to have a road back. Which suggests they knew exactly who their betrayer would be, and wanted him condemned by the prophecy rather than forgiven by it."

"They recruited me," I said. "Of course they knew."

"They *counted on* you," Oscar said. She had started drawing again — I could hear the pencil — and when I glanced down, I saw she was sketching the Spire from my description, tower within circle, quick and sure. "Which means they'll expect you to break again. When we reach the Spire, they'll offer you the same bargain. Your old rank. Your honor restored. One hour of looking away." Her pencil stopped. "And they'll be right to offer it, because it nearly worked once."

"It will not work twice," I said.

"Good," she said simply. "Then we don't have a betrayer. We have a man the enemy thinks is theirs, walking into their house wearing their brand." Something moved behind her eyes that I'd learned to recognize as the cartographer's look — the moment terrain resolves into route. "That's not a liability, Sef. That's a key."

Bramble snorted into her pot. "Listen to her. Two weeks ago she couldn't cross her own valley's border without losing feeling in a hand, and now she's drafting people like fortifications."

"People are terrain," Oscar said, "if you map them honestly. And honesty is the whole job."

Wren reached over and touched the back of Oscar's hand — a small gesture, quickly done, the kind of thing neither of them seemed to notice anymore and everyone else did. Then she looked at me. "The company judges," she said formally, archivist to the bone, "by what you raise in the ruin. So raise something. Starting tomorrow, you teach us the Spire. Every corridor, every watch rotation, every seal. Everything you know."

"Everything," I said, "will take all night."

"We have all night," Bramble said, "and the pot's not getting any less regretful."

* * *

So that is how I spent the night before we marched on Ilo's trail: kneeling in the dirt of a leaking toll-house, drawing the Covenant Spire from memory in the firelight while four exiles and a mapmaker leaned over my shoulder and asked questions I'd spent thirty-eight years hoping no one would ask.

It should have felt like flaying. It mostly did. But underneath the flaying was something I hadn't felt since I was a squire taking his first oath — the strange lightness of a vow being made rather than kept. Because that's what the confession was, in the end. Not an ending. An enlistment.

Near dawn, Oscar finished her sketch and turned it toward me. The Spire, in her hand, looked different than it ever had in mine: not a fortress, not a tomb. A draft. Something with margins.

"You drew it smaller than it is," I said.

"No," she said. "I drew it the size it's going to be. Things shrink when you take them back from the people who stole them." She tapped the margin around the tower, the blank space she'd left deliberately on every side. "That's where the new law goes. We'll need a man who knows exactly where the old one failed."

I looked at the blank margins for a long time. Then I picked up the charcoal she offered, and began, for the first time in thirty-eight years, to draw something I intended to build.

We broke camp at first light. And it was Ryn — quiet, watchful Ryn, who had barely slept — who spotted it first: on the ridge above the toll-house, a single rider in Order gray, sitting motionless against the brightening sky. Watching us. Not attacking. Not fleeing.

Waiting, I realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the dawn, for us to see him.

He raised one hand — not a threat, not a salute. Something between. And turned his horse toward the road we would have to take.
