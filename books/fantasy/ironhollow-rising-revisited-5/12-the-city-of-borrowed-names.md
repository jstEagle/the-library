## 12. The City of Borrowed Names

The city announced itself the way a good liar does — by telling you the truth about everything except itself.

We crested a ridge of black slag an hour past dawn, and there it was below us: no walls, no gate, no banner. Just rooftops. Hundreds of them, tiled in every color that clay and desperation could produce, terracotta and slate and one audacious stretch of copper gone green with age, all of it crowded up against the banks of a river the color of hammered tin. Smoke rose from a thousand chimneys in a thousand thin gray threads, and not one of them matched its neighbor's angle, because the wind off the riftlands couldn't decide which way to push.

"What's it called?" I asked.

"Depends who you ask," Bramble said. She was walking better than she had any right to, her staff clicking against the slag like she was scolding the ground for existing. "The Vaelmoori call it Vetch. The Ironhollowers called it Tallow's Rest, back when Ironhollowers came this far. The Order calls it *an infestation of unregistered persons*, which is my personal favorite."

"And what do the people who live there call it?"

Bramble smiled with half her mouth. "Whatever name they brought with them."

Sef grunted from the rear of our little procession. He'd been quiet since the fire, since his confession, since we'd all decided — not unanimously, not comfortably, but decisively — that a man who let a spire fall out of principle might still be worth marching beside. "They'll search us at the bridge," he said. "Not for weapons. For names."

"For *names*?"

"You'll see."

I should explain something about myself, since apparently I'm the cartographer of the covenant and therefore obligated to have opinions about geography: I love cities the way some people love storms. From above, from a map, they're order. Grids and arteries, intention made solid. But walk into one and the order dissolves into pure argument — every street a negotiation between what was planned and what people actually do. A city is a map that keeps redrawing itself.

This one argued louder than most.

The bridge over the tin-colored river was old, older than anything else in sight, its stones worn smooth as soap. At its near end stood a booth, and in the booth sat a woman so old she seemed less like a person than a geological feature the city had grown around. She had a ledger open before her and a cup of something steaming, and she looked at each of us in turn with the flat patience of someone who has heard every possible answer to her question and been disappointed by all of them.

"Names," she said.

Bramble went first. "Maren Thistlefoot."

The woman wrote it down without looking up. Sef gave his — "Aldous Grey" — and got the same treatment. Wren said "Wren Halloway," which was, I gathered, only half true; the surname was borrowed, the first name stubbornly kept. The twins gave names that were so obviously invented — "Fenwick and Fenwick" — that the old woman's pen paused for the first time, just briefly, in what I can only assume was professional contempt.

Then she looked at me.

And here is the thing I did not expect: I hesitated. Not because I couldn't think of a false name. Because standing there on that bridge, with a ledger open and a stranger's pen waiting, I realized I didn't want to give one. Every name I'd ever owned belonged to Ironhollow. Oscar Marchetti, daughter of the valley, granddaughter of a woman whose handwriting I'd found copied into a century-old prophecy. If I lied about my name, I felt — absurdly, irrationally — like I'd be lying about the map of me.

"Oscar," I said finally. "Just Oscar."

The old woman looked up. Her eyes were the pale gray of river ice, and they stayed on me a moment longer than the others had gotten. Then she wrote *Oscar* in the ledger, alone, no family clinging to it, and waved us through with two fingers.

"Welcome to nowhere," she said. "Mind the third street. It moves."

* * *

It moved. The third street moved. I want that on the record, because when Ryn told me that morning I laughed, and I would like history to know that I was wrong and he was right, and that I have rarely been so pleased to be wrong about anything.

Here is how it worked — or rather, here is how it failed to work, which is more interesting. The city sat where three rifts had once torn through the land and then, instead of sealing, had softened. Scarred-over. The streets nearest those scars didn't hold their geography the way honest streets do. Walk down Copperlane at noon and you'd come out on Copperlane, same cobbles, same crooked lampposts. Walk it again at midnight and you might emerge two streets east, or into a courtyard that hadn't existed yesterday, or — if the rift-scar was feeling ambitious — into a version of your own front door with the paint a color you'd never chosen but somehow always meant to.

The locals had adapted the way water adapts: completely, and without complaint. Shopkeepers chalked their hours on doors that might migrate overnight. Children wore bells, so their parents could find them when the streets shuffled. There was a whole profession — the *wayfinders*, they were called — who did nothing but walk the shifting districts each dawn and redraw the chalk-marks, and when I learned this I had to sit down on a convenient barrel and breathe for a while, because it was the closest thing to home I had felt since leaving Ironhollow.

A city that required constant remapping. A city that *knew* it was provisional. I could have kissed it.

"It's not magic," Wren said. We were walking the third street together — Copperlane, currently — while the others arranged lodging with a woman Sef owed a favor to, or possibly a debt to, or possibly both; with Sef the categories had blurred. "That's what I keep coming back to. The rift-scars aren't casting spells. They're just... wounds that never learned to close. And look what grew around them anyway." She gestured at the street: a baker hauling his oven door open, a woman stringing laundry between buildings that had definitely not been neighbors last week, two old men playing dice on a doorstep with the serene confidence of people who had played on that exact doorstep for years and would simply follow it wherever it went.

"The Order would raze this place," Wren said quietly.

"The Order razes everywhere," I said. "It's their whole personality."

But she wasn't joking, and neither, really, was I. Because I was seeing it — the thing she was seeing. This city was what happened when the covenant broke and nobody important noticed. When the law between realms collapsed, the powerful retreated behind their borders and hoarded what magic they could grab, and the rest — the farmers whose fields straddled the old boundary lines, the families with cousins on both sides, the merchants, the lovers, the children born exactly on the seam — all of them fell through the cracks. Literally. Some of them fell through actual cracks.

And the ones who survived didn't rebuild the old law. They couldn't. So they built *this*: a city with no walls because walls require a side, no banner because banners require an enemy, no official name because a name requires someone with the authority to grant it. They built a place held together not by decree but by consent — by ten thousand small daily agreements to share the same shifting ground.

I stood in the middle of Copperlane with my sketchbook open and my pencil moving before I'd consciously decided to draw, and what came out wasn't a map. It was closer to a portrait. The bakery with its migrating address. The bell-wearing children. The wayfinder's chalk-marks, already fading, already being renewed. All of it provisional. All of it *chosen*.

"That's the covenant," Wren said, reading over my shoulder. Not asking. Saying it.

"That's the covenant," I agreed. "Or it could be. If anyone with power bothered to notice that it already exists."

* * *

We lodged that night in a boarding house run by a woman named Odile, who had one arm, a voice like a landslide, and a policy of never asking guests anything except whether they wanted the fish stew. The stew was excellent. The company was better.

Because the boarding house was full — full of the borrowed-named, the border-born, the twice-displaced. And after dinner, with the fire going and the fish stew settling warm in all of us, they talked. That's what the house was for, I think. Odile fed people and then the house did its real work, which was letting people say aloud, sometimes for the first time, where they'd come from.

There was a ferryman from the Ironhollow side whose village had been on the wrong side of the border when it hardened — literally the wrong side, one morning the valley's edge shifted a hundred yards and his farm, his family, his entire life was suddenly in Vaelmoor, and the new law said what crossed the border stayed crossed. He hadn't seen his mother in thirty years. He said it plainly, without drama, the way you talk about weather, and it was worse than if he'd wept.

There was a woman from Vaelmoor who had married a man from the valley lands, back when such things were merely difficult rather than forbidden. When the covenant shattered, their marriage became contraband. He'd gone home to Ironhollow thinking it was temporary. She'd raised their daughter alone in this city under a name that wasn't hers, and the daughter — a sharp-eyed girl of maybe fifteen who was mending a fishing net by the fire and listening to everything — had never met her father, and lived forty miles from him, and might as well have lived on the moon.

There was an old man who had been a covenant-warden, one of the keepers of the original pact, before the Order dissolved the wardenship and burned the rolls. He'd spent forty years memorizing what he could remember of the terms — reciting them to himself like prayers, he said, so that somewhere in the world the old promises would stay alive even if no one honored them. He recited a fragment for us, right there by the fire, in a language I didn't know but understood anyway, the way you understand a lullaby. Something about *shared bread* and *open roads* and *neither realm shall hold the other's breath*.

I looked around that room — the ferryman with his thirty lost years, the mother and her net-mending daughter, the warden reciting dead law to a fire — and I understood, finally and completely, what the prophecy had been asking of me. Not heroism. Cartography. Someone had drawn a line across the world forty years ago and called it justice, and everyone who didn't fit inside the line had been erased from the map. My job — the job I'd been stumbling toward since I forced myself through the border and paid for it with the feeling in my left hand — was to draw the map again. Properly this time. With all of them on it.

"I used to think the covenant was a wall," I said, later, to whoever was still awake — which turned out to be Wren, Bramble, and the net-mending girl, who was pretending to sleep and fooling no one. "Something that divided. But listen to the warden's words. *Neither realm shall hold the other's breath.* That's not a wall. That's a promise. The covenant was never the problem. What they replaced it with — the hoarding, the sealed borders, the Order — *that's* the problem. The covenant was a handshake. They turned it into a locked door and blamed the door."

"And you're going to redraw it," the girl said, from her corner, eyes still closed. Not a question. News traveled fast in a city with no secrets and moving streets.

"If I can."

"You can." She opened her eyes. They were the pale gray of the river outside, of the bridge-keeper's ledger, of every patient thing in this city that had learned to wait. "My grandmother says the world doesn't need new laws. It needs someone to read the old ones out loud, in a voice nobody's allowed to interrupt." She went back to her net. "Also she says the fish stew gives Odile's prices away, so don't pay more than four coppers."

* * *

I drew until the candle died.

Not maps, exactly — or not only maps. Portraits of the city in ink and graphite: the bridge and its geological gatekeeper, Copperlane mid-shuffle, the bell-children scattering like bright birds, Odile's boarding house with its chimney smoke bending against the wind. And threaded through all of it, almost without deciding to, I started drawing the *connections*. The ferryman's route to the farm he'd lost. The daughter's forty miles to her father. The warden's remembered covenant, rendered as a diagram, its clauses radiating outward like the spokes of a wheel — and at the center of the wheel, where a hub should be, I left a blank space and stared at it for a long time.

Because I knew what went there. I'd known since the bridge, since the ledger, since I'd given my name with nothing attached to it and felt *lighter* for it. The center of the covenant wasn't a king or a council or an Order. It was a blank space waiting for a hand. Waiting for consent. The old covenant had been written to be rewritten — I could feel it now in every line of the warden's recitation, in every clause that said *we agree* rather than *it is decreed*. It was a design, not a decree. Someone, a long time ago, had built a promise shaped like an invitation.

The problem was that the invitation had been answered, forty years ago, by people who preferred decrees. And those people were going to arrive at the Covenant Spire ahead of us, or behind us, or around us, and they were not going to be interested in blank spaces.

I woke before dawn with the sketchbook still open on my chest and found Wren sitting by the dead fire, the prophecy pages spread around her like fallen leaves, her lips moving through the final lines she wouldn't yet share with me. She looked up when I stirred.

"How long have you been awake?" I asked.

"Long enough to count the exits," she said, which was archivist for *I'm worried*, and also archivist for *I'm not ready to tell you why.*

Outside, the city was doing its morning argument with itself — carts arguing with cobblestones, gulls arguing with gulls, the third street quietly relocating. Somewhere a child's bell rang, bright and insistent, and a parent's voice followed it, and the sound was so ordinary and so impossible that I lay there a moment just listening, memorizing it, adding it to the map I was building behind my ribs.

Then Ryn burst through the door without knocking, white-faced, and the morning stopped being ordinary at all.

"Riders on the north road," he said. "Order colors. A dozen of them, maybe more — and they're not riding past." He swallowed. "They're asking at every door for a company of six. And Oscar—" His voice dropped, and I watched him choose, in real time, whether to say the next part. I would think about that choice later, much later, though I didn't know it yet. "They're asking for the cartographer. By description. They know you're here."
