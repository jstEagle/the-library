## 13. What Wren Read Last

I had been an archivist for eleven years before I learned that the most dangerous thing in any archive is not the locked case or the cursed folio. It is the page you've read a hundred times and think you know.

The prophecy had been my companion since the record-house — first as a prisoner's burden, then as a puzzle, and now, on the road through the Riftlands, as something closer to scripture I was afraid to finish. The company carried it folded into Bramble's satchel, wrapped in oilcloth against the ash-rain, and every night at the fire someone would glance at it the way you glance at a sleeping animal you're not entirely sure is tame.

The first nine lines were famous, or infamous, depending on which side of the border you'd been born on. *The archivist who crosses on the eve of the thaw.* That was me; I'd made peace with it the way you make peace with weather. *The cartographer who redraws.* Oscar. She'd taken it less gracefully, but she'd taken it.

It was the last three lines nobody could read. Not because they were lost — because they were written in a cipher keyed to something we hadn't been able to identify. Sef called it "the Order's little joke." Ryn called it "a waste of good vellum." Ilo didn't call it anything, because Ilo had been gone from our table for six days now, and the space where he sat was louder than any commentary would have been.

We were camped in the lee of a shattered waystone two days out from the Glass Ford, if Ryn's intelligence was to be believed, and one long night's ride from the Covenant Spire beyond it. The solstice was four days away. Everyone was counting. Nobody said so out loud, except by saying everything else out loud instead.

I sat apart with the page spread across my knees, my little archive lamp — a Vaelmoor make, brass and green glass, salvaged from the record-house — burning low over the cipher. And I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only professional virtue I have left: I wasn't decoding it that night. I was avoiding it. I'd been avoiding it for a week, finding excellent reasons to re-verify line seven, to cross-check the marginalia, to inventory our supplies twice.

Because I had noticed something. Three nights ago, by accident, tired enough to be careless, I'd held the page up to the light of my lamp through the back of the vellum — checking water damage, nothing more — and the cipher had shifted.

Not broken. Shifted. The way a lock shifts when you put the right key near it without turning.

The key was the lamp. Or rather, the light. Or rather — and this was the part that had kept me awake ever since — the light passing *through* the page, reading it from behind, the way you'd read a palimpsest. The Order hadn't written the final lines in a cipher at all. They'd written them on the other side of the parchment, mirrored, pressed through, legible only when you stopped trying to read what was in front of you.

An archivist's joke, if you liked. A test of humility. You cannot read the ending until you stop looking at the surface.

I turned the page over that night because I had run out of excuses, and because Oscar was watching me across the fire with that expression she got when she'd mapped a coastline and knew there was a harbor behind it.

"You've got it," she said. Not a question.

"I might have it."

"You've had it for three days." She came around the fire and sat down beside me, close enough that her shoulder touched mine, close enough that I could see the burn-scarred map-lines faintly silvering the skin of her right hand where the rift-magic had gotten into her in the Riftlands. "You do your avoiding face."

"I don't have an avoiding face."

"Everyone has an avoiding face. Yours involves cataloguing." She nodded at the page. "Read it. Or don't. But either way, stop performing scholarship at it and tell me what's wrong."

So I told her about the lamp. About the mirror-side. About the shift. She listened the way she did everything — completely, without interruption, as though the information were terrain she intended to walk every mile of.

"And you're afraid of what it says," she said.

"I'm an archivist. I'm professionally afraid of what things say. That's the whole job."

"Wren."

"The last time a text told me my role in it," I said, "a hole opened above a dinner table and ate me." I heard my own voice go thin and hated it. "I'm allowed a little professional caution."

Oscar said nothing to that. She just put her hand over mine — the scarred one, warm even through the cold coming off the waystone — and waited. Which was worse than arguing. Arguing I could have managed.

* * *

I read it aloud, because some things need a voice to be real, and because I am a coward who prefers to share the weight.

"*And the covenant shall be reforged only by the hand that belongs to neither realm,*" I read, "*for a hand of either realm will write its own realm's hunger into the law, and the law will be a hunger, and the hunger will be a war.*"

Bramble looked up from her herbs. Sef went still in the way he went still, all at once, like a door closing.

"*And the price of the ink is the bearer's anchor,*" I read, "*for no hand may hold the pen and the home both; the covenant drinks deep of what roots its writer, and what roots the writer it takes.*"

Nobody spoke. The ash-rain hissed on the waystone above us.

"*And the anchor shall be known to the bearer by its absence,*" I finished, "*and the bearer shall know it too late, and the knowing shall be the last kindness the old world does her.*"

I lowered the page. My hands were steady, which surprised me; archivists train for steady hands, and apparently eleven years of training holds even when the text is eating someone you love.

"Belongs to neither realm," Sef said slowly. "That's Oscar. Ironhollow's sealed. Magic-dead. By the terms of any covenant, it's neither realm. It's outside both."

"It's a valley," Bramble said sharply. "A farming valley full of people who can't light a charm-candle. It's not a *realm*, it's—"

"It's exactly the kind of place the Order would exclude from the accounting," Sef said. "That's how they wrote it. That's how they write everything. What they don't want to count, they define out of existence."

"That's the first part," I said quietly. "There's a second part."

"Of course there is," Ryn muttered from the edge of the firelight. He'd been quiet all week, quieter than grief alone explained, and I noted it the way I note everything — automatically, professionally, and filed under later. "There's always a second part. Prophecies are like debts."

"The price of the ink is the bearer's anchor." I looked at Oscar. I didn't want to look at Oscar. I looked at Oscar. "Anchor to home. Whatever roots the writer. It takes it."

Silence. The specific silence of five people doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

"For me," Oscar said at last, and her voice was level, cartographer-level, surveying-a-coastline level, "the answer's obvious. My maps. The magic in them. That's my anchor — the craft, the—" 

"No," I said.

She blinked at me. "No?"

"No. Listen to the line again. *What roots the writer it takes.* Not what empowers her. What *roots* her." I was talking fast now, archivist-fast, the way I talk when I'm trying to outrun a conclusion. "Your maps are the pen. The anchor is separate. The anchor is what you'd go back to. What makes you *you*. For most people it'd be family, or a house, or—"

"Or a valley," Oscar said.

The word landed in the middle of us like a stone dropped down a well. We all heard it hit bottom.

"Ironhollow," Sef said. "The border. If the covenant reforges itself through her, and the price is her anchor—"

"Then Ironhollow stops being sealed," Bramble whispered. "Or stops being. There's a difference, and I don't like either option."

"There's a third reading," I said, and hated myself for it, because there is always a third reading and the third reading is always worse. "*The bearer shall know it too late.* Too late means after the ink dries. After the choice is spent. Whoever writes the new covenant doesn't get to weigh the cost beforehand. They find out what it took afterward, when it's already paid."

"So she'd be signing blind," Ryn said.

"She'd be signing *exactly*," I corrected him, because precision was the only weapon I had left tonight. "She'd know the shape of the price. Just never the moment of payment. That's the cruelty of it. The Order designed it that way — I'd bet the archive on it. A clause written by people who wanted the covenant reforged on their terms, by someone desperate enough to pay anything, and too committed to stop at the threshold and ask questions."

"Why would they design it that way?" Bramble asked. "If they wanted the old law restored, why build a mechanism for a new one?"

"Insurance," Sef said grimly. "In case their plan failed. In case someone like us got this far. You build a trap into the remedy itself. Either the covenant stays broken and they keep their monopoly, or it gets reforged and costs the forger everything that made her capable of forging it. Either way, the Order wins."

"Cheerful," Ryn said. "He's cheerful. I missed this about him."

"Sef." Oscar's voice cut across the gallows humor, flat and clear. "The border of Ironhollow. When it falls — if it falls — what happens to the valley?"

Sef was quiet for a long moment. Firelight moved on his ruined-knight face, and for once he didn't look like he was choosing his words for a court. He looked like he was choosing them for a friend.

"Honestly? I don't know. Sealed borders are usually seals against something. Your valley's magic-dead — that's not natural, that's engineered, same as the Spire's corruption. Something's being held out. Or held in." He met her eyes. "When the seal breaks, whatever's on the other side of it comes through. Or gets out."

"And the people?"

"People survive seals breaking," Sef said. "Usually. Mostly. It depends what the seal was for."

"*Mostly*," Oscar repeated.

"I won't lie to you on this road. Lying to you on this road would be a waste of the little time we have."

I watched Oscar take that in. Watched her do the thing she does — turn inward, go still, become a person consulting an interior map. I knew what she was looking at. I'd seen her draw it often enough: the valley in her mind, every farmstead, every fence-line, the border stones she'd grown up touching without knowing what they were.

Her home. Her anchor. The thing the prophecy wanted as payment.

"Four days," she said finally. "Solstice is in four days. The Spire's two days past the Ford. So I have until then to decide whether I—"

"You don't decide tonight," I said.

"When, Wren? At the Spire? With the Order arriving and the rifts opening?" She laughed, short and unamused. "This is what I *do*. I map terrain. This is terrain. Show me the ground and I'll tell you the route. The route is: I write the covenant, I lose the anchor, and everyone I grew up with finds out what their valley was keeping out at the exact moment it stops being kept out."

"That's not a route, that's a cliff."

"Cliffs are terrain too." She stood, brushing ash off her knees, and looked down at me with an expression I couldn't fully read — part apology, part defiance, part something tender and terrible underneath. "I need to walk. Don't follow me yet."

I let her go. I watched her silhouette move out past the firelight toward the dark shoulder of the Riftlands, one hand trailing along the broken waystone like a woman taking bearings, and I felt the prophecy's last line settle over me like snowfall.

*The bearer shall know it too late.*

But I was not the bearer. I was the reader. And readers, I thought, rising and folding the page with hands that were finally, usefully unsteady — readers are not bound by the same clauses.

There was one more thing in the margins. Something I'd seen on the mirror-side and hadn't said aloud, because saying it would change everything, and I'd wanted one more night of the old everything.

The final lines weren't in the Order's hand. The cipher, the mirrored script, the whole hidden ending — the ink was different. Older. Fainter. Written by someone else, decades before the Order ever sealed the page, and left there like a letter tucked into a wall.

And beneath the last line, almost too small to see, a signature in that older hand:

*A. Underhill.*

My grandmother's name. The archivist whose notes had started all of this — whose handwriting Oscar had recognized in her grandmother's copy of the prophecy, back in Ironhollow, a lifetime ago.

The Order hadn't written the ending. Someone had written it *first* — and hidden it inside their seal, waiting for a reader stubborn enough to look from behind.

I stood at the edge of the firelight with the page in my hands and the cold coming down off the Riftlands, and I made the reader's choice, the only choice I have ever been any good at.

I went to find Oscar, and tell her that the woman who wrote the prophecy's ending had left more than a warning.

She'd left instructions.

* * *

I found her a quarter mile out, standing on a rise of slag-black rock where the Riftlands began their slow dissolve into geography that couldn't commit. Below us, a rift hung in the dark like a wound in a tent — a soft gray shimmer, breathing. Beyond it, visible through the tear, was a stretch of country that did not exist anywhere: a river running uphill through a forest of white trees, patient as a painting.

Oscar was sketching it. Of course she was. Lamplight, pencil, the quick sure strokes of a woman drawing the impossible because it was there and someone had to.

"You should be sleeping," she said, without turning.

"You should be grieving," I said. "Seems fair. We can trade."

That got a huff of almost-laughter out of her. She shaded the white trees, then stopped, pencil hovering. "Wren. If it's true — if the anchor is Ironhollow — then when I write the covenant, my mother wakes up in a valley that isn't sealed anymore. Whatever the border was holding back walks into her kitchen. And I'll have done it. With my own hand. On purpose."

"Yes."

"And I won't know until after. I won't get to choose with full knowledge. The prophecy makes sure of that."

"Yes."

She was quiet a while. The rift breathed below us. Somewhere in the white forest, something made a sound like a bell underwater.

"Then help me choose now," she said. "Before the ink. Before it's too late to matter. If I'm going to pay for this, I want to pay *informed*. That's not much. But it's mine."

"That's why I came." I held up the page. "There's more. On the back. Under the Order's seal. Someone wrote the true ending first — and signed it A. Underhill."

Oscar went very still. "Underhill. That's—"

"Your grandmother's name. The handwriting Oscar-the-elder used for her copy. The archivist who hid the truth inside the lie." I stepped closer, turning the vellum so the lamplight caught the faint older script beneath the Order's gloss. "She didn't just foresee the betrayal. She built a backdoor into the prophecy itself. Instructions. For whoever finally read it honestly."

Oscar took the page from me like it was a live coal. Her eyes moved across the hidden lines, and I watched her lips move, translating the old cipher the way only someone raised on Underhill marginalia could.

"What does it say?" I asked. "What did she leave?"

Oscar looked up at me, and the firelight caught the map-scar on her hand, and her expression was one I'd never seen on her before — hope and horror in equal measure, the face of a woman reading coordinates for a place she'd been told didn't exist.

"She says the anchor doesn't have to be *lost*," Oscar breathed. "Wren — she says it can be *moved*."
