## 14. Ambush at the Glass Ford

I have been ambushed eleven times in my life, and I will tell you what I tell every green exile who asks me how to survive one: the trick is not to be surprised. The trick is to be *annoyed*. Surprise makes you stupid. Annoyance makes you fast.

So when the birds went quiet over the Glass Ford that morning — all of them at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone says your name in it — I did not feel fear. I felt the particular irritation of a woman who had just gotten her boots dry for the first time in three days.

"Company," I said, and spat into the river.

The Glass Ford earns its name honestly. The water runs so clear over white stone that crossing it feels like walking on air, or on glass, or on some god's abandoned window. Beautiful country. Which is to say: terrible country, because beautiful places are where clever people set their traps, and I have never once met an ambush in ugly terrain. Nobody bothers to kill you in a bog. They let the bog do it.

We were strung out along the near bank — Sef ahead with his shield slung, Oscar and Wren in the middle arguing about contour lines like an old married couple of maps, the twins bringing up the rear pretending they weren't holding hands, which they always were, which was the only honest thing either of them ever did.

Oscar looked up from her sketching. "Bramble?"

"Birds stopped," I said.

Sef's hand went to his sword without any hurry at all, which is how you know a man has done this before. "How long?"

"Long enough."

Wren had gone pale in the way she does — not frightened exactly, but *braced*, like a woman reading the last page of a book she already regrets opening. She'd been quiet since the ford came into view. She'd been quiet since the prophecy's last lines, since the word *anchor* sat down at our fire and refused to leave. I didn't blame her. Some knowledge is a stone you carry in your mouth.

"Across or back?" Sef asked me.

I looked at the water, at the far bank with its sweet cover of alder and birch, at the high ground behind us where nothing moved, which was itself suspicious, because that ground should have had birds on it too.

"Across," I said. "They want us in the open or they'd have hit us already. Water breaks a charge. And I don't fancy being caught against a river with my trousers down."

"You're always caught with your trousers down," Ryn said.

"That's why I'm still alive. Nobody expects it twice."

We started across.

* * *

Let me tell you about the Order of the Sealed Page, since you may not have had the pleasure. They dress in grey, which they believe is humble, and they fight in formation, which they believe is righteous, and they kill in the name of a law they wrote themselves, which they believe is holy. I was married to one of them once, briefly, before I understood that a man who loves an institution more than a person will always, eventually, feed the person to the institution. He fed me to it. I crawled out the other side as something new. Something *unsworn*.

So I knew what we would meet at the ford. Not soldiers — clerks with swords. Men and women who could quote you the covenant line by line and could not tell you why a hedge-witch's garden mattered, or a smuggler's sister, or a map drawn by a woman who had never seen Vaelmoor until a month ago.

They waited until we were mid-river. Of course they did. Mid-river is where you are slowest and proudest, telling yourself the crossing is nearly done.

The first bolt took Sef's shield with a sound like a bell being murdered. The second took the water a hand's width from Wren's hip. Then the alders on the far bank stood up and became men, twenty of them, thirty, grey as the stones they'd lain against, and the ford that had been glass became a corridor of arrows.

"Shield wall!" Sef roared, and there he was, the disgraced knight, planting himself in knee-deep water between the arrows and the rest of us like a door swinging shut. "Offense! Get them moving!"

Ilo grabbed Ryn's collar and hauled him low. Oscar had Wren by the wrist, dragging her toward the far bank, and I brought up the rear the way I always do, because someone has to be the arse of the company, and I've got the widest one.

Here is what nobody tells you about holding a line: it is not brave. It is bookkeeping. You count bodies, you count bolts, you count the seconds between volleys, and you spend yourself like a miser spends gold — grudgingly, a coin at a time, hating every expenditure. I put up a warding sigil with my left hand and felt it shred after four heartbeats; the Order carries null-charms now, they've learned, the bastards. I threw hex-seed with my right and watched two grey men sit down hard in the shallows, coughing. I counted. Fourteen seconds between volleys. Twelve now. They were getting eager.

A bolt found the gap under my arm where the sigil had died.

I'll be honest with you, because I've never seen the point of lying about pain: it went in like winter. Cold first, then a heat that had no business being inside me, then my knees informing me they had resigned. The river took my weight kindly enough. The white stones were very beautiful from below. Everything is beautiful from below; that's the joke of it.

"Bramble!" That was Oscar's voice, cracked down the middle.

"Don't—" I got a mouthful of ford and finished anyway. "Don't stop moving. I'm fine. I'm *irritated*, which is better than fine—"

Hands under my arms. Sef, bleeding from the scalp, hauling me up out of the water like I weighed nothing, which flattered me until I remembered adrenaline lies for everyone. He dragged me onto the far bank and set me against an alder, and I pressed both palms to my side and felt the blood come through my fingers warm as tea.

"Arrow's barbed," I told him. "Don't pull it. Bind around it. And if I die, I want it noted that I said *across*, and I was *right*—"

"Shut up, Bramble." His voice broke on it, just slightly, and I decided to allow it. A man should be permitted one crack in the armor per ambush. It's traditional.

* * *

The Order came across the ford in ranks, shields up, boots finding the white stones with the horrible discipline of people who have rehearsed. Twenty paces of open water and they crossed it like a procession. That's the thing about believing you're righteous: you stop hurrying.

And Oscar Marchetti stood up on the bank with a map in her hands.

I want to describe this properly, because I was lying at the right angle to see all of it, and because I have thought about it every day since, and because some things deserve witnesses.

She had torn a page from her road-book — the one she draws everything in, the one with the riftlands stabilized in ink, the one that cost her already — and she knelt and pressed it flat against the earth of the bank, both palms down, the way you'd hold down a wounded bird.

"I need the river," she said. Not to us. To the map. To whatever listens to maps when a cartographer means it.

The ink woke.

I've seen hedge-magic all my life. I've seen knights' battle-wards and archivists' seal-craft and the Order's cold grey law. None of it looked like this. This looked like *drawing*. Lines lifted off the page and lay themselves along the ground — the river's course redrawn, the banks redefined, the ford rewritten as something steeper, deeper, faster. The Glass Ford stood up. There is no other way to say it. The water rose in a slow glassy wall along the line she'd drawn, and the first rank of the Order reached it and found their knees underwater, then their waists, then their doctrine, which had not accounted for geography changing its mind.

"Hold the line!" their captain shouted, and they held it, because they always do, and the river took their footing away, and Sef went down the bank with his shield and his grief and his terrible joy and met them in the churn.

I tried to get up. My body filed a formal complaint. So I stayed against my alder and I watched, and I will tell you the truth, which is that watching is its own wound.

Because Oscar was paying for it. I could see that from ten feet away. Every second the river stayed risen, the color drained further out of her left eye — not closing, not blinding, just *fading*, like a map left too long in sun, the iris going from brown to grey to a pale washed nothing. She swayed. Wren had her around the waist, anchoring her — that word again, *anchor*, doing its work whether anyone wanted it to or not — and Oscar kept her palms flat on the page and her jaw set and she *held the line*, our line, the real one, the one made of water and ink instead of shields and blood.

"Enough," Wren was saying into her hair. "Oscar. Enough. They're breaking. *Enough.*"

"They'll regroup—"

"They're *drowning*, love—"

Something in that word — *love*, tossed off in terror, the way you throw a rope — went through Oscar like the bolt went through me. Her hands trembled. The river shuddered. And then, deliberately, like a woman setting down something heavier than she can admit, she drew one last stroke with her finger through the wet ink and let go.

The Glass Ford settled back into itself. Gentle as sleep. The white stones gleamed up through the clear water, unbothered, beautiful, as if nothing had happened, which is rivers' whole philosophy.

The Order was in retreat — those who could retreat, hauling their drowned doctrine up the far bank. Sef stood waist-deep among the abandoned shields, breathing like a bellows, and did not pursue. Wise. Vengeful men die at fords. It's practically a rule.

Oscar turned around.

Her left eye was half gone. Not dark — *empty*, a pale winter-grey where the brown had been, the pupil a ghost of itself. She looked at me with one whole eye and one ruined one, and she smiled, and I have been on the wrong end of a great many things in my life but nothing has ever hurt quite like that smile.

"Well," she said. "That's going to make depth perception interesting."

"Sit down before you fall down," I managed, "or I'll haunt you personally."

She sat. Wren folded around her. And I lay back against my alder with an arrow in my side and watched the water run clean over the stones, and I thought: *this is what love looks like when it stops being a feeling and becomes a fact.* It looks like a woman trading half her sight so that strangers and exiles and a knight with a cracked heart can live another day. It looks like blood in the water that isn't yours, hurting worse than the blood that is.

Ryn crouched beside me with bandages and that face he makes when he's hiding something, which lately was always. He bound around the barb, gentle for a smuggler, rough for a brother.

"You knew," I said quietly, while the others saw to Oscar. "Before the birds stopped. You looked at the high ground first."

His hands didn't falter. That was the damning part. "Everyone looks somewhere first, Bramble."

"Aye." I closed my eyes. "We'll talk about where you looked. Later."

He tied off the bandage, stood, walked away, and did not look back — and a man who doesn't look back is either innocent or carrying the heaviest load on the road.

* * *

They got the arrow out at dusk, by firelight, Wren's steady hands and Sef's shoulder to bite. Barbed, as I'd said. I screamed in a manner I intend to deny to my grave, and then I slept, and then I woke in the small black hours with Oscar sitting watch beside me, her ruined eye catching the firelight like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"How much can you see?" I asked her. "Out of it. Honestly."

"Light. Shapes. Like looking through frosted glass at a world someone's still drawing." She turned toward me, and even half-blind she found my face without hunting for it. "It was worth it. Don't argue. I heard you start to argue."

"I was going to say thank you," I lied.

"No, you weren't. You were going to say it was reckless and that I've made myself a liability and that the Spire will eat me alive if I keep spending myself like this." She smiled again, softer. "You might be right about all three. But Bramble — when I had my hands on that page, I wasn't afraid. For the first time since Ironhollow, I wasn't afraid. I knew exactly what I was for."

I reached over and took her ink-stained hand, the one that had held the river up, and pressed it against my bandaged side.

"This is what you're for," I said. "Not the magic. This. The spending. Any fool can draw a line, girl. You drew one and stood behind it. That's rarer than sorcery, and it costs less, mostly."

She laughed, wet-eyed, and squeezed my hand, careful of the wound.

Then her head came up, sharp, listening — and mine followed, because past the fire, past the sleeping forms of Sef and Wren and the twins, Ryn was standing at the edge of the camp looking north, and in his hand, small and grey in the firelight, was a sealed page with the Order's mark upon it.

He hadn't sent it. He was reading it. And whatever it said, his face in that light was the face of a man watching a trap close — and trying to decide whose throat it would close around.
