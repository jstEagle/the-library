## 15. The Traitor Named

I have been robbed before. Professionally, you understand — there's a difference between a thief and a smuggler, and the difference is mostly paperwork and a better coat. I've had my pockets picked in three cities, my cargo seized in two more, and once, memorably, my boots stolen while I was wearing them. So believe me when I say I know the shape of a betrayal. I know how it walks. It walks like a friend.

But knowing the shape of a thing doesn't stop it from breaking your ribs when it finally arrives.

* * *

It started, as most of my worst days do, with a map.

Oscar's maps had gotten strange since the riftlands. Not bad — strange. She'd sit by the fire with her charcoal stub and draw lines that weren't roads so much as suggestions, and when she finished, the ground under us would hold still for a few hours instead of trying to eat itself. Useful. Uncanny. The kind of useful that makes people nervous, because if a map can hold the world together, someone will eventually ask what else it can do.

We were three days out from the Glass Ford, camped in the lee of a shattered waystone, when I noticed Ryn wasn't sleeping.

Now, my brother has never slept well. He sleeps like a cat in a house full of dogs — one ear up, always. But this was different. This was him sitting at the edge of the firelight with his back to all of us, doing something small and precise with his hands.

I'm his twin. I know what his hands look like when they're counting money, when they're picking a lock, when they're lying. And I knew what they looked like that night, folding something over and over until it was small enough to hide.

I didn't say anything. That's the part I keep coming back to. I saw it, and I filed it away like a good smuggler files everything — under *later*, next to *probably nothing* and *not my problem*. Because he's my brother, and because for thirty years the space between "Ryn is acting strange" and "the world is fine" has never once collapsed on me.

I should have asked. I want that written down somewhere, in ink, in a record-house that doesn't burn: *Ilo Marchetti-adjacent, professional criminal, saw it and said nothing.*

* * *

The Order found us at the Glass Ford two days later. You'll hear about that from Bramble, or from Oscar, who watched half her sight burn away holding the line. I'm not going to tell that part. I'm going to tell you what happened after, because after is where I learned that the ambush wasn't luck, wasn't cleverness, wasn't the Order finally getting competent.

After was where I did the arithmetic.

Here's the sum. Since the waystation — since chapter six of our little saga, if prophecies come with chapters — the Order had found us four times. Once in the riftlands, when we'd taken no road and left no trail. Once outside the record-house, though that one could be explained; sieges leave marks. Once at the crossing where Sef nearly drowned, and once at the Ford. Four times. And each time, the Order had known not just where we were but *when* — they'd arrived hours ahead of us, rested, provisioned, positioned.

You can't track people through land that erases geography. I know, because I've tried to sell routes through it, and the honest answer I gave every buyer was: don't. The riftlands don't hold a trail any more than water holds a footprint.

So either the Order had a diviner of world-shattering talent following us invisibly across terrain that eats diviners for breakfast —

— or someone was telling them.

I sat with that arithmetic for a full day, turning it over, looking for the error. There wasn't one. There never is, in the sums you least want to balance.

And then, on the morning after the Ford, while Bramble lay fevered under three blankets and Oscar sat apart with her ruined eye and her ruined certainty, I caught Ryn again. Firelight. Back turned. Hands folding.

This time I walked over.

* * *

"Whatcha got there, brother?"

He flinched. My brother, who once talked his way past a customs officer while standing on the very crates he was smuggling, flinched like a first-year cutpurse. He crumpled the thing in his fist fast, but I'd already seen the corner of it: heavy paper, official cream, a wax seal the color of dried blood.

The Order's seal.

"Ilo." His voice did its usual trick, easy and warm, the voice that had gotten us out of a hundred scrapes. "Couldn't sleep."

"No," I agreed. "Neither could you. For about nine days running."

"It's been a hard month."

"It's been a hard month," I said, "for everyone except whoever's been sleeping soundly in an Order camp, eating their rations, and drawing their pay."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind me, Sef turned over in his bedroll. Ryn's face went through three expressions in the space of a breath — the lie, the better lie, and then, horribly, the truth, which looked older than both.

"You should sit down," he said.

"I'm standing fine."

"Ilo. Sit down."

I sat down. That's love, right there. That's the whole ugly definition of it: your twin tells you to sit, and even as the floor drops out of your world, you sit, because some part of you is still five years old and believes he'd never steer you wrong.

He opened his fist. The paper was a message, unsent, meant for a dead drop we'd passed an hour ago. In his neat smuggler's hand — the hand I taught him, incidentally, the summer we were eleven — was our route. The Glass Ford. Our numbers. Bramble's injury. Oscar's eye. Everything.

"How long," I said. It came out flat. I'd meant it to come out flat.

"Since the waystation."

The waystation. The night we'd all sat around a fire and become, as far as I knew, a family. The night I'd told him — gods help me, I remembered telling him — *"Feels good, doesn't it? Having people."*

"The whole time."

"The whole time."

"Why."

Not a question, really. Questions have answers. This was more of a wound shaped like a word. But he answered anyway, because Ryn has never once in his life let me stay angry without giving me a reason, as if reasons were a bandage.

"They have Ilo," he said.

The words took a second to arrive. When they did, they arrived all at once, like cold water.

"What?"

"After the record-house raid. They grabbed me on the way back — I'd gone ahead to scout the drop, remember? They didn't take me. They showed me *her*." His voice cracked down the middle, and for a moment he wasn't a traitor, he was just my brother, twenty-six years old and coming apart. "They have her in a cell in Vaelmoor's capital. Have had her since the raid. They sent me a letter in her handwriting. They let me hear her voice, Ilo. Through a speaking-glass. She was — she was asking if I'd forgotten her birthday."

I stared at him. Behind us the fire burned, and Bramble breathed shallow and fevered, and Oscar sat alone in the dark with half her sight gone buying our escape, and none of it — none of it — was as loud as the sound of my own name in my brother's mouth.

"They have *me*," I said slowly. "I'm sitting right here. I'm the one they don't have."

"They have the version of you they need," he said. "The hostage. Don't you get it? If I run, if I warn you, if I stop — they kill her. And if I'd told you back at the waystation, you'd have done something stupid and brave, because you always do something stupid and brave, and then she'd be dead, and it would be my fault twice."

"So instead you fed them Oscar. You fed them *all of us*. Bramble took a blade through—" My voice climbed and I strangled it down to a whisper, because Bramble was sleeping ten feet away. "Sef nearly died at the crossing. Oscar is blind in one eye. Wren spent six weeks in a cage. And every single time, it was you handing them the map."

"I thought it would just be information," he whispered. "Routes. Timing. Nobody was supposed to get hurt. They kept telling me nobody would get hurt, and I kept believing them, because believing them was the only thing between me and—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "You want to know the worst part? The truly pathetic part? Some nights I told myself I was playing a long game. Feeding them just enough wrong to lead them astray. But I checked, Ilo. I went back over every message. I never once lied to them. Not once. I couldn't make myself do it, because if they caught me lying, she'd die, and then every message I ever sent would be a murder weapon."

There it was. The whole architecture of it, laid bare: a man building a bridge of betrayals across a canyon of fear, telling himself each plank was temporary.

I should have hit him. Some part of me, some old animal part, wanted to very badly. Instead I heard myself say, in a voice I barely recognized:

"She wouldn't have wanted it."

"Don't." 

"She'd have rather died than—"

"*Don't.*" It came out ragged. "You don't know that. I don't know that. That's the trap, Ilo, that's the whole beautiful trap — you can't gamble with someone else's life unless you already know the answer, and I didn't know, I *don't* know, and every day I didn't call their bluff was another day I chose her over all of you, and every day I hated myself more, and every day it got easier, and that last part is the part that scares me. Do you understand? It got *easier*."

I understood. Gods forgive me, I understood completely. That's the thing about villainy nobody puts in the ballads — it isn't a mask that drops. It's a slope. It's a series of mornings, each one only slightly worse than the last, until you look up and the person you were is a speck below you and the person you've become is holding a letter with a blood-colored seal.

I stood up. My legs felt borrowed.

"What happens now?" Ryn asked. Small. Like a kid.

"Now," I said, "you show them to me. All of them. Every letter, every drop, every instruction they gave you. Now you tell me exactly what they know and exactly what they think they know. And now you pray — to whatever gods survived the covenant — that there's enough left of your usefulness to trade."

"Trade for what?"

I looked at him for a long moment. At the face I'd seen every morning of my life, mirrored.

"For her," I said. "They're holding one twin. We're going to give them the other."

* * *

I woke the others at dawn. All of them, in a row, because I refused to let Ryn tell it twice and I refused to let anyone hear it secondhand. He confessed with his hands open and empty on his knees, and I stood beside him, which surprised everyone including me.

Sef listened without moving. When it was done, he said only: "I let the Spire fall. I don't get to throw stones." Which is Sef for *I forgive you*, wrapped in enough gravel to hide it.

Bramble, gray and propped against a log, laughed until she coughed, then said, "Boy, you are the *worst* double agent I have ever met. You didn't feed them a single falsehood? Amateur. Come here, I'll teach you."

Wren said nothing at all. She just looked at Ryn for a long time with those archivist's eyes, cataloguing him, and whatever she wrote in whatever ledger she keeps behind her face, she closed the book gently. That frightened him more than anger would have. It frightened me too.

Oscar was last. She stood at the edge of the circle with her patched eye and her charcoal-stained fingers, and she said, "The messages. Did they ever ask about the maps specifically? What I can do?"

Ryn nodded, miserable. "Every letter. The Spire's seal. What it costs you. Whether the price goes up."

Oscar exchanged a glance with Wren — one of those glances the two of them had been trading since the prophecy, whole paragraphs compressed into a flicker — and I watched her arrive at the same conclusion I had, one beat ahead of me:

They weren't tracking us to stop us.

They were measuring her.

"Well," I said, clapping my hands together because somebody had to break the silence and it might as well be the man whose entire family had just detonated. "New plan. We're going to march straight down the route my dear brother sold them, on the schedule they expect, and when the Order shows up to collect their informant —"

"— we collect Ilo instead," Wren finished softly.

"And Ryn," I said, "is going to write them one last letter. Telling them everything is going perfectly."

Ryn looked up at me, eyes red-rimmed, and for the first time in nine days — maybe longer, maybe since the waystation, maybe since childhood — my brother smiled like himself.

"One last lie," he said.

"One last lie," I agreed. "Make it a good one. It's the only inheritance I'm leaving you."

Behind us, the road bent toward the Order's waiting arms, and for once in my life, I couldn't wait to be ambushed.
