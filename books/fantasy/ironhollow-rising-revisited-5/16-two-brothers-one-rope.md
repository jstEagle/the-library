## 16. Two Brothers, One Rope

My brother has always been better at being caught than I am at catching him.

That's not self-pity. It's inventory. Ilo and I came out of the same womb eleven minutes apart, and in those eleven minutes he apparently signed some contract with the universe that says doors open for him, guards like him, ropes slip off him. I got the other half of the inheritance: the ability to count coins through a locked purse by sound alone, and a face that makes people assume I'm lying even when I'm telling the truth. Which, to be fair, is usually because I am.

So when Sef laid out the plan on the floor of Bramble's wagon — charcoal scratches on a board, the Order's forward camp drawn from memory — and said, "We go in quiet, we get the ledger, we burn the leverage," I knew two things.

One: it was a good plan.

Two: my brother was going to ruin it out of love, and I was going to let him, because that's what twins do.

* * *

Let me back up, because you deserve the whole ugly shape of it.

Three days after the Glass Ford — three days of Bramble sleeping propped upright because lying down pulled at the wound in her side, three days of Oscar learning to aim her one good eye like a woman relearning how to pour water — Wren found the pattern in the Order's dispatches. She'd been reading them upside down over dinner the way she reads everything, which is to say completely and without permission.

"They're not hunting us," she said. "They're herding us. Every ambush site has been on a route Ryn described."

The fire cracked. Sef's hand went to his sword hilt out of habit, then stopped, because the person he wanted to point it at was sitting right there eating stew.

I didn't deny it. Denial's for people with futures.

"Ilo," I said. "The Order took Ilo the night before we left the waystation. They've had him since chapter six, if chapters were a thing, which they're not, but you understand me. Every campsite, every ford, every shortcut I gave them, and every time I stalled long enough to buy us an hour, they took it out of his hide." I turned my hands over so they could see the burns. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you where the ledger is."

Oscar looked at me for a long time with her ruined eye and her working one, and I braced for the verdict.

What she said was: "Then we steal him back."

Not *then you hang*. Not *then you walk into the riftlands and keep walking*. *Then we steal him back*, in the flat practical voice of a woman who maps impossible places and has decided this is simply one more coastline to chart.

Bramble, gray-faced against her pillows, laughed until she had to stop. "Of course we do," she wheezed. "We're the Unsworn. It's practically our founding document."

Sef said nothing. He just started moving pieces on the charcoal map, and that was worse than any speech, because Sef only plans when he's already forgiven you, and I hadn't earned it yet, and he gave it anyway. That man's grace is a debt I intend to carry past my grave and argue about with whatever's on the other side.

Wren was the last. She set down her bowl and looked at me with those archivist's eyes that file everything for later sentencing.

"You fed them Oscar's route," she said quietly. "She lost half her sight at the Glass Ford because of information you sold."

"Yes," I said.

"And you're going to help us take their camp apart."

"Yes."

"Good," said Wren, and picked up her spoon, and that was the whole trial. I have been judged by magistrates, guildmasters, and one very disappointed fence in Corvane, and none of them ever frightened me or freed me the way a woman saying *good* over a half-eaten bowl of barley.

* * *

Here's what we knew about the Order's forward camp at Marrowgate: it sat in the shell of an old toll-fortress, three walls standing, the fourth opened to the sky where a rift had chewed through it years ago. They kept their records in the gatehouse — the Order keeps records the way dragons keep gold, obsessively and as a substitute for a soul — and among those records was the binding writ on Ilo. A prisoner transfer without the writ is just kidnapping; with it, they own him lawful as a deed to land. Burn the writ, and every guard holding him is committing a crime instead of performing a duty. Morale does funny things after that.

Oscar drew the fortress. Of course she did. She sat cross-legged on the wagon floor with a stub of charcoal and produced Marrowgate in a quarter hour — walls, towers, the collapsed fourth wall, the well, the guard rotations as best we knew them — and when she finished, the paper did something none of us expected. It steadied. The lines stopped smudging. The charcoal wouldn't rub off no matter how I tried.

"It holds," she said, staring at it. "While the drawing's accurate, it... anchors. Like the riftland maps." She looked up, and her good eye was bright and her bad one was doing something eerie, catching light it shouldn't. "I can't make a door. But I can make the ground remember where it is."

"Can you make a wall forget?" Sef asked.

She smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, and I thought: *gods help the Order.*

The plan was simple, which should have warned me. Simple plans are just complicated plans that haven't met my family yet.

I would go in as myself — the Order still believed I was theirs, bought and bleeding, and I intended to arrive looking exactly as guilty as I felt. Walk in, report a false route, ask to see my brother to prove my loyalty. Get eyes on Ilo. Signal. Then Sef and Oscar would come through the broken wall under cover of Oscar's map-work scrambling the guards' sense of distance, Wren would be in the records with a satchel of fire-oil, and Bramble —

"Bramble stays," Sef said.

"I can hold a line with one arm," Bramble said.

"You held a line with two arms and it cost you a rib and most of your blood. You stay. You watch the horses. If we don't come out by moonrise, you ride."

"I don't leave people," Bramble said, in the voice of a woman who had once, evidently, left someone, and had been paying for it across decades.

"You're not leaving anyone. You're keeping the road open. That's the job nobody thanks you for." Sef put his hand on her shoulder, gently, the way you touch old wood you're afraid might split. "Hold the road, Bramble."

She held the road. Remember that. Whatever else went wrong — and things went wrong in ways I'll be tasting for years — Bramble held the road.

* * *

Walking into Marrowgate was the strangest homecoming of my life, and I've been disowned twice.

The sentry at the gate knew my name. Knew my price. There's a particular shame in being recognized as a traitor by the people you betrayed for — they don't respect you, they *use* you, the way you use a tool you'd never lend out. The captain, a dry stick named Vessin with ink-stained fingers and a voice like a filing cabinet, received me in the gatehouse surrounded by shelves of ledgers, and I understood suddenly why Wren hated these people. They had catalogued my brother. Somewhere behind Vessin's shoulder, spine-out between two tax rolls, was a book with ILO MARCHETTI-VAEL in it, and inside that book was everything he was worth, which the Order had calculated to the copper.

"The route," Vessin said.

"Lie," I said. Well. I said a route. Same thing.

He wrote it down. He believed it, or pretended to, and either way he waved me toward the cells to "confirm the asset remains cooperative." Confirm the asset. My brother. Eleven minutes older than me and reduced to a word from a ledger.

The cells were under the gatehouse, five of them, four empty. In the fifth, a man sat on the floor with his back to the wall, and when he saw me he grinned with two teeth gone and said:

"Took you long enough. Did you bring food, or did you bring the rescue? Please say both."

"Ilo—"

"If it's just food, I want the spiced kind. If it's the rescue, I want to complain about your timing first and eat during."

"It's both," I said, and my voice broke on it, and he was up and at the bars with his hands wrapped around mine before I could be embarrassed about it. His fingers were cold. They'd been cold for weeks and I'd known it abstractly, in reports and routes, and now I knew it in my skin, and I would have burned Marrowgate down around my own ears right then if he'd asked.

"Listen," he whispered, fast, his forehead against mine through the bars. "Writ's in the captain's strongbox, second shelf, red clasp. I've watched them open it nine times. Nine, Ryn. I counted, because counting's your thing and somebody had to keep the family business going in here."

"You absolute idiot," I said, wetly. "You beautiful idiot."

"That's the spirit. Now here's the part where you tell me the plan, and here's the part where I tell you what's wrong with it."

And gods, he was right. He was always right about the wrongness of things. Because while I stood there memorizing the strongbox, the cell block door opened above, and boots came down the stairs, and Vessin's filing-cabinet voice said:

"Search him."

They'd searched me at the gate, casually. This was not casual. Two guards went through my coat with the thoroughness of people who had already been told what to find, and found it: the signal-mirror tucked in the lining, the little scrap of Oscar's anchored map-paper I'd carried as a talisman, stupid, sentimental, fatal.

Vessin looked at the mirror. Looked at me. And smiled the smile of a man whose ledger has just balanced.

"Thank you for coming home, Ryn," he said. "Your brother told us you would."

* * *

I need to say something about what happened next, and I need to say it honestly, because everyone in this company has been generous with me beyond what I deserve and the least I owe them is the truth even when the truth makes me look like a fool.

I fought. Badly. Two guards, a captain, iron cuffs — I got one elbow free and cost Vessin a nosebleed, and then I was face-down on the stairs with a knee between my shoulder blades, and the last thing I saw before they hauled me up was my brother's face at the bars.

And Ilo was smiling.

That's the part I couldn't understand for hours afterward. He was smiling like he'd won something. And when they dragged me past his cell he pressed his hand flat against the bars and said, clear and calm, the way he says things that are true:

"Hey. Little brother. Count to eleven for me."

Eleven minutes. Our whole lives measured in the gap between us, the head start he always had.

They put me in the cell next to his. Through the wall, in the dark, I heard him laughing softly to himself, and finally I asked him what in all the hells was funny, and he said:

"You came. That was the whole plan, Ryn. Not the mirror, not the signal — you coming. They think they caught a spy. What they actually caught is bait, and now every guard in this fortress is going to be watching the cells tonight instead of the wall." A pause. "Your friends are already inside. I heard the horses change. Someone's very good with animals."

Bramble. Holding the road, and everything on it.

"But—" I said, and stopped, because the objection assembled itself slowly and horribly: *but now you're still in the cell, and I'm in the cell beside you, and the writ is upstairs.* "Ilo. The plan was to get *you* out."

"I know," he said, gentle as a knife going in. "Plans are your thing too. But listen — they need one of us talking and one of us quiet, and you're the one who knows where the Spire company is camped, so they'll work on you, and you're terrible at resisting, Ryn, you always have been, you cry at other people's weddings. Whereas I—" and I could hear the grin in the dark, "—I am *very* good at being caught."

"Ilo—"

"When they move us tomorrow, on the transfer, there'll be a moment. There's always a moment. You take it. You run east, low along the water, and you don't look back, and you tell the cartographer she owes me a map with my name spelled right." His voice cracked, just once, just slightly, the only honest crack in the whole performance. "Eleven minutes, little brother. Let me have this one."

* * *

They came for us at moonrise, which was early, which meant the Order was nervous, which meant somewhere in the fortress above, Sef and Oscar and Wren were doing exactly what Ilo predicted: pulling the night apart at its seams.

I heard it before I saw it. A sound like tearing cloth, vast and slow — a rift opening, or Oscar's maps making the world doubt its own edges. Shouting. The bell in the tower ringing wrong, arrhythmic, panicked. Boots thundering overhead away from us, toward the broken wall, toward wherever the fighting was thickest.

Our cell door rattled. Stuck. Of course it stuck. Ilo had it open in forty seconds with a lockpick he'd been hiding — where, I will never know, and I've decided not to ask, because some mysteries are load-bearing.

We came up into chaos. Fire in the records wing — Wren's work, precise and total, the Order's century of catalogued souls going up in honest smoke. Sef in the courtyard like a storybook illustration of wrath, holding six guards off a doorway with a broken shield. And Oscar at the center of it all, kneeling with her palm flat on a sheet of pinned paper, and around her the fortress was *confused* — guards charging distances that weren't there, staircases that delivered them to walls, the whole architecture drunk on her redrawn geometry.

"There!" Ilo shouted, pointing at the strongbox room, and we went together, brothers, one rope, exactly as advertised.

The writ was there. Red clasp, second shelf, just like he'd counted. I snatched it and thrust it toward the brazier and Ilo grabbed my wrist and stopped me — because Vessin was in the doorway, nose crusted black, sword out, and between us and escape.

"Drop it," Vessin said, "or I take the twin instead of the spy."

And Ilo moved.

I want to be exact about this, because history — our history, the company's history, whatever gets written after — deserves precision. My brother stepped *between* me and the sword, knocked the writ from my hand into the fire himself, and said, cheerfully, terribly: "Take me, then. Again. You're getting slow, Captain — that's twice now."

"No," I said.

"Ryn. Go."

"*No—*"

"Eleven minutes," he said, and smiled, and Vessin's men took his arms, and he let them, went easily, the way he does everything, the way doors open for him even when the doors are prison doors — and the last thing he said as they dragged him back into the dark was not goodbye. It was instructions.

"East! Low along the water! Don't look back!"

I looked back. Obviously. I'm the one who lies.

* * *

Sef found me at the wall, or rather I fell into him, and he carried me out under one arm like contraband while Oscar's paper-world came apart behind us and the fortress remembered its own geography all at once, groaning, settling, furious. Wren ran beside us with singed eyebrows and an empty satchel and an expression I can only describe as *archival wrath*. We reached the tree line. We reached the horses. Bramble, one-armed and white-lipped, had the road held, every animal saddled, and she took one look at my face and didn't ask, which was the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me.

We rode east, low along the water.

I didn't count the minutes. I couldn't. For the first time in my life the numbers wouldn't come, and I understood that Ilo had taken them with him — the eleven minutes, the counting, the family arithmetic — and left me the rest: the guilt, the gratitude, and a debt shaped exactly like my brother.

Behind us, Marrowgate burned against the stars. Ahead, the road bent north toward the Covenant Spire, and the solstice, and the end of everything.

In my pocket, folded small, was a corner of scorched paper I'd grabbed from the strongbox floor without thinking. I unrolled it by moonlight.

It was a page from the Order's master registry. One entry, half-burned, the ink still legible:

*Subject: Marchetti-Vael, I. Status: transferred. Destination: the Spire. By order of the First Seal.*

They weren't taking him to another cell.

They were taking him to the place where covenants are rewritten — and the solstice was three days away.

"Horses," I said, turning my mare north, my voice steady for the first time all night. "We ride for the Spire. Tonight. All of it. Every mile."

Because the Order had made one mistake, the same mistake they always make, the mistake of men who keep ledgers instead of souls:

They thought my brother was an asset.

They were about to meet him as an argument.
