## 18. Solstice Eve

I have read a great many terrible documents in my life. I was, after all, an archivist before I was a prophecy, and archives are mostly terrible documents with better binding. I have read ledgers that priced children by the tooth. I have read edicts written in the cheerful hand of men who never once had to live under them. I thought I had developed a tolerance.

Then I watched First Reader Cassian Vael unroll the old covenant across the Spire's threshold, smooth it flat with both palms like a man laying out a picnic blanket, and say, "We're prepared to be reasonable," and I understood that I had never once known what terrible was.

The Spire's great doors had been sealed for forty years. Oscar had opened them two days ago with a map and a stubbornness I have since learned to find attractive rather than alarming, and now they stood wide to the evening air, which smelled of ash and cold iron and, faintly, of the river far below — the Glass Ford, where Bramble had bled for us and where I still sometimes heard, in quiet moments, the sound of the Order's crossbows loosing.

Now the Order had come up the mountain road in force. Not skirmishers this time, not the hunting parties that had harried us through the Riftlands. This was the whole apparatus: sixty soldiers in grey-and-silver, supply wagons, a field altar with the Sealed Page enthroned on it like a relic, and at the front of it all, walking unhurried as a man strolling to his own garden party, Cassian Vael.

And beside him, wrists bound in silver cord that made him flinch every time he moved, walked Ilo.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew of Ilo before this moment, because it matters. Ilo was the twin who talked. Ryn was the twin who watched. Between them they could smuggle anything through anything, and their method was simple: Ilo made everyone look at him while Ryn made off with the goods. Ilo's laughter was a tool, honed sharp, deployed precisely, and I had seen it disarm border guards, corrupt officials, and Sef, once, into laughing at his own confession.

There was no laughter in Ilo now. He walked beside Vael with his chin up and his eyes moving constantly — counting soldiers, marking wagons, cataloguing exits — and when his gaze found ours at the top of the Spire steps, he did something extraordinary. He winked.

"Still alive, then," he called up, conversational, as if we'd run into each other at a market. "Took you long enough. Do you know how boring Order prisons are? No contraband whatsoever. I've been forced to make conversation with *clergy*."

"Silence," said Vael, without heat.

"You see?" Ilo spread his bound hands. "This is what I mean. Sixty armed men and not one of them can take a joke."

Oscar's hand found mine. Her left hand — the one that had gone numb crossing the border, the one that held the covenant-bearer's mark — and I felt her fingers trembling, though her voice, when she spoke, was level as a drawn line.

"Let him go."

"Ah." Vael looked up at us. He was smaller than I expected, which is its own kind of menace; you spend your life imagining monsters large, and then one arrives at average height with kind eyes and a scholar's stoop. "The cartographer speaks. Good. I confess I hoped it would come to conversation rather than conclusion. Blood is so difficult to get out of stone."

He gestured, and two soldiers brought forward a folding table, and on the table they laid the old covenant — the original, or something claiming to be, its vellum dark with age, its edges burned where the breaking had begun forty years ago. Beside it, a fresh quill. An inkpot. A blank page.

"We are not unreasonable people," Vael said. "Whatever my predecessors were. The covenant broke. The realms have bled for it — rifts devouring borders, magic running wild in Vaelmoor, dead zones spreading in yours. We propose restoration. The old law, resealed, renewed. Order restored to both realms. And in exchange—" he touched Ilo's shoulder, lightly, the way you'd touch a piece of furniture "—the smuggler walks free. All of you walk free. Amnesty. The Unsworn pardoned, the exiles returned home. Even the knight."

Sef, standing at my left with his sword already drawn and resting point-down against the stone, said nothing. He had said very little since the ambush. He stood like a man holding a door shut from inside himself.

"And if we refuse?" Bramble asked. She sat propped against the doorframe, because she could no longer stand for long, her wound wrapped in poultices and charm-rags that Ryn — silent, gray-faced Ryn, who had not spoken to anyone but his brother since the truth came out — kept adjusting with hands that shook.

Vael smiled at her with genuine warmth, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "Then at dawn, when the rifts open widest, we reseal the Spire ourselves, with the blood of everyone standing on these steps. The prophecy will be fulfilled either way, Mistress Archivist. You of all people should appreciate that. It has only ever been a question of whose hand holds the pen."

* * *

Here is what I had read last, in the record-house, in the weeks I spent refusing rescue: the final lines of the 1897 prophecy, the ones the Order had cut from every copy they controlled. *The covenant shall be reforged by the one who belongs to neither realm, and the price shall be the bearer's anchor to home.* I had decoded those lines sitting in a cell with a stolen lamp, and I had understood them, and I had said nothing to Oscar for three days because saying it aloud would make it true.

She knew anyway. She always knows. That is the trouble with loving a cartographer: she reads terrain the rest of us don't even see, including the terrain of my silences.

So when Vael offered his reasonable bargain, I watched Oscar do the arithmetic behind her eyes. The old law resealed. Ironhollow sealed again — magic-dead forever, the valley that refuses, the border built to keep something out or in. And herself outside it, anchorless, having paid the price for a law she despised.

"No," she said.

"Cartographer—"

"No." She stepped forward, down one step, so she stood above him on the stairs like a verdict. "You want me to say it plainly? Your Order broke the covenant. On purpose. Forty years ago, because shared power frightened you, because a pact between realms meant neither realm could be ruled. You wrote a prophecy and then you imprisoned everyone it named. You hoarded magic until it rotted, and now the rot is eating both worlds, and you stand there offering me *amnesty* to help you nail the lid back down." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it. It got quieter, and more precise, like a blade being turned. "I've read your law, First Reader. I've mapped what it erased. My answer is no. My answer was always going to be no."

Something moved behind Vael's kind eyes. Not anger. Something sadder and more patient.

"I hoped you'd say that," he said quietly. "Forgive me. But I needed it witnessed." He turned to his soldiers. "At first light, then."

* * *

They withdrew down the slope to make camp, and the light went out of the sky, and we stood on the Spire steps in the blue hour doing the thing armies and lovers and doomed companies have always done on the night before battle: we prepared, and we pretended preparing was the same as being ready.

Ryn carried Ilo up the steps himself — the silver cord wouldn't break, but Oscar's maps could unmake what bound things, briefly, at cost — and the twins held each other in the doorway for a long time without speaking, which for them was a speech. Then Ilo pulled back, looked his brother in the face, and said, "You told them. About me. Before they made you."

"They figured it out."

"Good." Ilo grinned, and it was almost the real grin, the weapon-sharp one. "Hate that you're braver than me. Terrible for the brand."

Bramble made tea, because Bramble makes tea the way other people pray, and we drank it around a small fire in the Spire's hollow heart, beneath the corrupted seal that Oscar had spent two days studying like a patient surgeon studying a wound. Sef sharpened his sword. Ryn sat pressed against his brother's side as if the silver cord had left a mark on both of them. And Oscar sat apart, drawing.

Not mapping. Drawing. I watched her hand move over fresh paper — quick strokes, lines that weren't roads or rivers but something else, something with the grammar of a map and the soul of a contract — and I understood she was writing. In her own hand. The new covenant, or the bones of it, the thing she would attempt at dawn whether the world was ready or not.

I went and sat beside her.

"Tell me the price again," I said. "All of it. Don't soften it."

She didn't look up. "The bearer's anchor to home. For me that's Ironhollow. The valley, the border, everything I grew up inside. When I sign, it burns out of me. I'll never—" Her pencil stopped. Started again. "I won't feel it anymore. Home. Anywhere. That's the shape of it."

"And your maps?"

"Gone too. All of them. Every map I ever drew stops being true the moment the ink dries on this one." She finally looked at me, and her eyes in the firelight were dry and very steady. "That's fair. That's the trade. Maps of what is, for one map of what could be."

"Oscar—"

"Wren." She set down the pencil and took my hand, the right one, the ordinary one. "Tomorrow, whatever happens — I need you to do what you did in the record-house. Anchor the words. Read them back to me while I write, so I don't lose the thread. Can you do that?"

"You know I can."

"Even if it costs—"

"Yes," I said, before she could finish, because I had read enough prophecies to know that some sentences should never be allowed to end. "Yes. Whatever it costs. Yes."

We sat like that while the fire burned down, and above us the broken seal pulsed faintly, like a bruise, and far below on the mountainside the Order's campfires bloomed in the dark — dozens of them, orderly, patient, certain.

The last calm before the storm doesn't feel calm. That's the lie they tell you. It feels like tea going cold in your hands, and a woman drawing tomorrow in pencil, and your own heartbeat counting down hours you cannot map.

Near midnight, Ilo stood, stretched, and announced to the room: "Well. If we die at dawn, someone should know I was the funny one."

"You were," Ryn said.

"The funniest."

"The funniest," Ryn agreed, and his voice cracked on it, and Ilo put his arm around his twin, and nobody laughed, and everybody smiled.

And then, in the deepest hour of the night, when even Sef had closed his eyes, the great doors of the Spire — which Oscar had opened, which we had guarded, which stood watchable in the moonlight — swung slowly inward on their own.

No soldier stood there. No reader in grey-and-silver.

Only the wind, and beyond it, far down the mountain, every campfire of the Order going out at once, all together, like blown candles —

and rising from the direction of the Glass Ford, thin and cold and getting louder, a sound none of us had heard before and all of us, in our bones, recognized:

the rifts, opening early.
