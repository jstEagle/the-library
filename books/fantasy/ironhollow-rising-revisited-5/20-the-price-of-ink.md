## 20. The Price of Ink

I have drawn maps of places that do not exist. I have drawn maps of places that exist but refuse to admit it. I have once, in a moment of professional desperation, drawn a map of Ironhollow itself so beautiful that a merchant offered me his horse for it — and I want it noted that I refused, because the horse was ugly.

But I had never drawn anything that *obeyed* me before the Spire. And now, with the last stroke of the new covenant still cooling in the air like a brand pulled from a forge, I understood that everything obeys at a price, and that the price was coming due, and that it was coming due to me.

The ink left my hand first.

I don't mean metaphorically, though God knows I'll be mining this moment for metaphors for the rest of my life. I mean the ink — the vial of Spire-black Wren had ground from the covenant's original seal, the ink that had been waiting a hundred years for someone foolish enough to use it — lifted off the page as I finished the final line and hung in the air above the covenant like a constellation deciding where to fall. The words I'd written glowed. Not gold, not white. They glowed the color of my own handwriting, which is to say they looked like every map I'd ever loved, lit from underneath.

And then the light went looking for the rest of my work.

"Oscar." Wren's voice, very calm, the way she gets when she's terrified and has decided terror is inefficient. "Your satchel."

My satchel was smoking.

Every map I carried — the flawed crossing-map that had cost me feeling in my left hand, the riftland charts that had held the ground beneath our feet for nine days of impossible travel, the half-finished survey of Vaelmoor I'd started in Bramble's kitchen on a night I'd pretended was research and had actually been hope — all of it was glowing through the leather. All of it was being *read*.

"It's taking the magic," Sef said from the doorway, where he stood with his sword still drawn, watching the Order's retreating banners through the broken wall. He said it flatly, the way you report weather. "The old law required an anchor. She wrote consent instead of decree, so the covenant is choosing its own price."

"Can we refuse?" Bramble asked. She was sitting against the plinth, one hand pressed to the bandage on her side, gray-faced and stubborn about it. "Can she just — not pay?"

Wren looked at me. I looked at the covenant. The covenant looked back, in the way that very large truths look back at you: patiently, without malice, entirely indifferent to your feelings about them.

"No," I said. "It's not taking. It's *trading*. That's the whole point. That's what I wrote." My voice did something embarrassing on the last word. "Consent means you give something real. Otherwise it's just the old law wearing a nicer dress."

"And what are you giving?" Ryn asked quietly.

He was standing apart from us, near the shattered record-shelves, and he had been standing apart since the ambush at the Glass Ford, since Ilo had stepped into the Order's chains in his place, since none of us had quite figured out how to talk to him without the conversation turning into a knife. He looked terrible. He looked like a man who had been carrying a boulder for weeks and had finally set it down and discovered the boulder had been holding him upright.

"I think," I said, "you're about to watch."

* * *

Here is what it feels like to have the magic burned out of your life's work:

It feels like being read aloud.

Not painfully. That's the strange part. I braced for pain — after the border-crossing, after the ford, I considered myself something of an expert on magical pain, and I had a whole taxonomy worked out. But this wasn't pain. It was attention. Every map I had ever made with true craft — not the commission work, not the pretty things I sold to tourists, but the real ones, the ones I'd made by listening to rumors until the rumors confessed — every one of them opened inside me at once, and the covenant read them, and then it let them go.

I felt the ash-fields go first. Nine days of walking, of Bramble's hedge-lore, of Ilo singing to keep our spirits up and Ryn harmonizing before he remembered he wasn't supposed to be likable — gone, smoothed out of the parchment like breath off glass. Then the riftlands, the geography that erased itself, the routes I'd stabilized with sweat and swearing. Then, further back, deeper, the realms I'd mapped from secondhand accounts in a valley that refused magic, the ones I'd drawn out of pure longing because no one would ever let me see them —

Those didn't vanish. Those were *used*. I felt the covenant take the shape of every imagined place I'd ever invented and pour it into the seams between Vaelmoor and home, filling gaps I hadn't known existed, and I understood, dimly, through the roar of it, that this was the trade. Not destruction. Repurposing. Everything I had ever drawn to keep magic *out* of my hands was being spent to hold magic *in* its borders.

"You're laughing," Wren said. She had both my hands now. When had she taken my hands? "Oscar. You're laughing."

"I'm a cartographer," I said, "whose maps are finally accurate," and then I laughed again, or sobbed, the distinction having become academic.

And then the tether.

I knew it was there the way you know your own heartbeat — never thinking about it, until someone puts a hand on your chest and says *hold still*. The covenant found it easily. A thread running out of me, south and west, down through stone and root and the long patient geology of the world, all the way to a valley that kept magic out because keeping magic out was the only thing it knew how to do.

Ironhollow.

"Oh," I said.

"Don't," Wren said.

"It's fine. It's — Wren, listen, this is the good part—" Because it was, in a way. Because the prophecy's last lines had said the price was the bearer's anchor to home, and I had spent three chapters' worth of sleepless nights dreading that it meant I could never return, that the valley would spit me out, that I'd traded my whole world for two worlds and gotten neither. But that wasn't it. The thread didn't snap. It *unraveled*, gently, from my end, and I felt it go into the covenant too, and I felt what it became: not a chain from me to one hidden place, but a thousand small permissions, seeping outward, letting magic into Ironhollow for the first time in four generations like thaw-water finding cracks in a field.

The valley wouldn't lose me. The valley would lose its walls.

"That's better," I told Wren, whose face was doing several things at once. "That's strictly better. I'm getting the better end of—"

I fainted.

In my defense, I had earned it.

* * *

I woke to firelight and arguing, which is how I know I was among family.

"—not staying, that's not how this works, you don't get to trade yourself for me twice in one week—"

"Ilo—"

"*Twice,* Ryn."

I opened my eyes. We were in the Spire's undercroft, which had stopped being corrupted somewhere around the time my unconscious body hit the floor, and it was almost beautiful now — vaulted, clean, the old shelves standing straight again as if relieved of a great weight. Bramble was asleep propped against Sef, who was awake and watching the door with the vigilance of a man who expected the world to apologize to him personally. Wren sat beside me, her fingers laced through mine, reading — of course she was reading; the woman relaxed by studying — a slim volume she closed quickly when she saw me stir.

Across the fire, the twins were having their argument, and everyone else was pretending not to listen, which meant everyone was listening.

"You freed yourself," Ryn said. "When the covenant sealed, the Order's claims dissolved. Their leverage dissolved. Everyone the Order detained walked out of the record-houses at dawn. You were free before you knew you were captive."

"And you've been free the whole time, technically, if we're defining freedom as 'able to physically walk away while your brother rots,'" Ilo snapped. "Funny how definitions work."

"I'm not rotting. I'm *staying*. There's a difference."

"There is," Ilo agreed, deadly quiet, "and it's about forty years and a continent."

I sat up. My head protested. My left hand, I noticed distantly, had feeling in it again — the covenant had apparently decided that if it was going to take everything else, it could return the small debts. I flexed the fingers. They obeyed. Everything obeyed tonight except the people I loved.

"Ryn," I said, and the room turned. "Walk me through it."

He flinched. Good, some uncharitable corner of me said. Then that corner was overruled, because he lifted his chin and met my eyes and answered like a man testifying, which, in fairness, he was.

"The records are a ruin. A century of the Order's edits, forged prophecies, names added and struck to suit whoever held the seal. If the new covenant means anything — if consent actually replaces decree — then someone has to rebuild the archive honestly. Every realm. Both sides. No sealed pages." He swallowed. "I'm the best liar either world ever produced. Which makes me the only person alive qualified to catch one."

"He's right," Sef said, to visible surprise, including his own. "An honest archive needs a man who knows every way an archive can be dishonest."

"I hate that," Ilo announced.

"I know," Ryn said.

"I hate it *so much*."

"Ilo—"

"If you say 'it's for the twins' future' I will burn this undercroft down with both of us in it."

Ryn crossed the fire and knelt in front of his brother, and whatever he said next, he said too low for the rest of us, and Ilo's shoulders came down from around his ears by degrees, and at the end of it Ilo punched him in the arm — hard, the kind of punch that is a sentence — and then held onto him with both fists twisted in his coat like a much smaller and angrier barnacle.

Bramble, who had been awake for at least the last minute and feigning sleep out of politeness, cracked one eye. "Well," she rasped. "Covenant's sealed, traitor's redeemed, twins are crying. Somebody put the kettle on before the universe notices it forgot to charge us for something else."

The universe, as it happened, had already charged me. But I kept that to myself, and watched the fire, and let Wren's thumb move slowly across the back of my hand, and felt — for the first time since a rift opened above a dinner table and took the woman I was falling for — quiet.

* * *

We left the Spire three days later.

Three days, because Bramble needed them, and because Ryn needed them, and — though I'd have denied it under oath — because I needed them. Three days of Ryn showing us the archive he intended to build: empty shelves labeled in his precise smuggler's hand, *Vaelmoor, verified* and *Beyond-the-Valley, verified* and, on the lowest shelf, alone, a label that made my throat close up: *Things We Got Wrong.* He'd already placed one item there. The original 1897 prophecy, with the betrayer-clause intact, displayed without apology.

"Future generations should see how badly we read each other," he said, catching me looking.

"Future generations should see that you kept it," I said.

On the morning we left, Ilo hugged his brother for exactly four seconds — I counted; grief makes me precise — and then shoved him and stalked out the door, and that was the goodbye, and it was enough. Ryn stood in the Spire's doorway as we went down the mountain, getting smaller, becoming a figure, becoming a mark on the landscape. I caught myself reaching for charcoal to sketch him and found my satchel full of blank paper.

Blank paper. I turned a sheet over twice, checking. Blank. Clean. Innocent. The magic was gone — all of it, thoroughly, irreversibly. Whatever I drew now would stay exactly where I put it, obedient to nothing but geometry.

I should have felt robbed. Instead, walking down through the ash-fields that were already greening at the edges — the covenant's work, spreading like spring — I felt something closer to relief. For twenty years my maps had been a prayer to a door that wouldn't open. Now the door was open, and I could walk through it like anyone else, and if I wanted to draw what I found there, I'd have to do it the slow way: by looking.

"Empty-handed," Wren observed, falling into step beside me. Her shoulder found mine. Shoulders are underrated. Prophecies get all the press, but shoulders do the daily work.

"Not empty." I patted the satchel. "Just honest."

"Mm. And what does the famous cartographer draw, when her drawings can't hold the world together anymore?"

I thought about it the whole length of the ash-field, and most of the way through the pass, and I had an answer by the time we crested the ridge and saw, far below, spread out in the morning light like something newly forgiven: the City of Borrowed Names, its walls open, its banners flying in both realms' colors at once, refugees streaming in and out freely for the first time in living memory.

"Maps of what could be," I said.

Wren looked at me sidelong. "That's not a thing you can map."

"No," I agreed. "It's going to be extremely difficult. Possibly a lifetime's work. Possibly it can't be finished at all." I took her hand. Her fingers were ink-stained too, these days; she'd been transcribing the covenant for the city's archive, in her own careful archivist's script, and I found I liked the evidence of it. "Fortunately, I know an archivist who crosses on the eve of the thaw. And it's nearly spring."

Below us, the city bells began to ring — not alarm, not decree. Just ringing, because someone felt like it, and in a world remade by consent, that was reason enough.

Wren laughed, and squeezed my hand, and said, "Then we'd better start walking."

And we did.
