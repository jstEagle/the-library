## 21. Home, Unmapped

**Bramble**

The City of Borrowed Names stopped borrowing the day the covenant sealed. You could feel it in the streets — doors repainted in colors their owners had only ever dared at night, names spoken aloud like coins finally spent. The baker on Tallow Row turned out to be a Vaelmoor exile named Odessa who'd been calling herself Marta for eleven years, and she cried into her first batch of honest bread. I bought three loaves just to have something to do with my hands that wasn't shaking.

I don't convalesce well. Nobody tells you that about nearly dying — that afterward there's all this time, and time is the worst thing for a wound. Sef changes my bandages because I refuse to look at the mess the Glass Ford made of my side, and he does it with the grave tenderness of a man apologizing to every battle he's ever walked away from.

"You flinched," he said this morning.

"I did not flinch. I performed a tactical repositioning of my torso."

He almost smiled. Sef's smiles are still rationed, like everything else was during the war nobody declared. "Bramble. You held a line of Order knights off a rope bridge with a broken staff and one working arm."

"Somebody had to. Oscar needed her strokes." I looked out the window while he worked, because looking at Sef's face while he touched the wound felt like too much honesty for one morning. "You know what's strange? I keep waiting to feel like a hero and it hasn't come. It's just — sore. Everything's just sore."

"That's how you know it was real," he said. "Glory is what other people call it later. In the moment it's only ever sore."

I decided then and there to forgive him for the Spire. Not out loud — out loud would make it a ceremony, and ceremonies are how the old law used to eat people. Just quietly, internally, the way you forgive weather.

Downstairs, Ilo was teaching Ryn to cook. This is either reconciliation or revenge; with twins it's impossible to tell. Ryn came back from the Spire two weeks ago with ledgers under his arms and guilt worn smooth as river stone, and Ilo hadn't spoken to him for six days, and then one evening Ilo put a knife in his hand and said, *chop*, and that was that. Some forgiveness doesn't want words. Words would only get in its way.

The house we're sharing belongs to a woman who traded her name away twice and has decided, now that names are free, to go by nothing at all. She calls herself the Landlady and charges rent in stories. We're behind on payments already. I've been putting her off with hedge-remedies, but last night she heard me muttering over Bramble's Bitter Brew — my own recipe, my own name in the title, which still startles me when I say it — and declared us even through midwinter.

Midwinter. After the solstice. Time keeps happening, which I suppose is the whole point of what we built.

Oscar came by at dusk with a roll of paper under her arm and something nervous in her walk, and I pretended not to notice, because some things you let people carry to the door themselves.

* * *

**Sef**

I have kept watch over many things: a Spire, a lie, a company of misfits who fight like cats and hold like iron. Keeping watch over a healing city is easier work, though no less strange. The City of Borrowed Names has become, in the space of a season, simply a city. People argue about taxes. There is a petition to rename Tallow Row after someone everyone has already forgotten. Ordinary life is the most miraculous thing I have ever guarded, and it requires so little from me — mostly that I stop standing at windows like a sentry and sit down at tables like a man.

I am learning to sit at tables.

Today I sat at one across from Bramble while I changed her bandages, and she forgave me without saying so, the way she does everything — sideways, thorned, sincere underneath. I have decided to accept it without saying so back. Between the two of us we have constructed an entire conversation out of silence, and it is the most honest one I've had in forty years.

Ryn asked me yesterday if I thought he should stay at the Spire permanently. He's been rebuilding the record-house, cataloguing honestly this time, unsealing page by page what the Order sealed. He looked older than Ilo now, which shouldn't be possible for twins, and yet grief does its own arithmetic.

"The Order wrote prophecy as a cage," I told him. "You're writing records as a door. Someone has to stand in a doorway."

"And you?" he said. "What will you guard now?"

I didn't have an answer then. I have a partial one now, assembled from small evidence: Bramble's bandages, Ilo's laughter coming through the floorboards, the way Oscar stands in doorways herself lately, half-in, half-out, watching Wren read. I think perhaps I am done guarding walls. I am considering the radical vocation of guarding people, which cannot be done from a window.

This evening the whole household gathered — Bramble limping down with dignity, the twins bickering over seasoning, Wren with a book she wasn't reading, Oscar with a map she wasn't showing anyone. The Landlady demanded our week's story and Ilo told the tale of the Glass Ford with such embellishment that Bramble threw a bread roll at him, and I found myself laughing before I remembered I was a disgraced knight, and then remembering that the disgrace had been the honest part all along, and laughing harder.

Afterward, on the step, Oscar said to me: "You're staying, then?"

"For now," I said. "You?"

She looked east, toward the mountains, toward a valley that had refused magic for a hundred years and was learning otherwise. "I have to go back," she said. "Not because I'm needed. Because I want to see it."

There is a difference between those two reasons. I spent four decades not knowing it. I said, "Then go see it," and meant it as a blessing, and I believe she took it as one.

* * *

**Ilo**

Here's what nobody tells you about getting your brother back from being a traitor: he comes back wrong-shaped. Not bad-wrong. Just — there are new rooms in him where there used to be open hallways, and I keep walking into them.

We're cooking again, which is how we talk. Ryn chops like a man doing penance, precise and joyless, and I correct his knife work until he remembers how to be annoyed at me, and then we're twins again for a few minutes at a time. It's enough. It's going to be enough. I've decided.

"Tell me the truth," he said tonight, elbow-deep in onions. "Do you regret it? The exchange. Me for the rope."

"Every day," I said, and watched his face fall, and added: "Because you were gone and I was here eating terrible food. Not because I'd trade differently. I'd shove you down that corridor again tomorrow, and you'd let me, and that's the whole ugly beautiful contract of us."

He laughed wetly into the onions. Good. Onions hide everything.

The thing about this city is that everyone in it chose to be here twice — once when they fled, once when they stayed after the names came free. That's a different kind of belonging than being born somewhere. Born-belonging is an accident. Chosen-belonging is a craft. I'm a smuggler; I respect craft.

And I've been watching the cartographer and the archivist circle each other all week like two people trying to share the last blanket without admitting they're cold. Tonight, finally — finally — Oscar unrolled whatever she's been hiding on the table, and Wren leaned over it, and their heads came together, and the whole room politely developed urgent business elsewhere. Bramble studied her tea like it contained prophecies. Sef inspected the ceiling beams for structural integrity he has no qualifications to assess. I grabbed Ryn and announced loudly that we needed to check on the stew, which was not on any fire, and dragged him to the kitchen.

"They're going to be fine," Ryn whispered.

"They're going to be insufferable," I corrected. "Fine and insufferable. Those are different things."

Through the doorway I saw Oscar take Wren's hand and put it flat on the map, over some place neither of them is from, and Wren close her eyes like she was memorizing it. I turned around and gave my brother the privacy of my back.

Some things you smuggle. Some things you just leave alone to arrive.

**Wren**

I have read ten thousand documents in my life and understood perhaps nine thousand of them, and the one I understand least is myself, lately.

Here is the problem, stated plainly, as I would state anything: the prophecy is fulfilled. Every clause closed. The archivist crossed on the eve of the thaw — I did. The cartographer redrew — she did. The betrayer was named and forgiven, the spire was opened, the solstice passed, and the rifts sealed with a sound like a book shutting gently. There is no remaining line that requires me. My role concluded. And a woman whose role has concluded is a strange object: all the momentum gone out of her, left standing in a borrowed city holding a finished book.

I tried explaining this to Oscar on the fourth night, in the careful archival language I use when I am frightened.

"So you were only with me because of the prophecy?" she said, and her voice was very level, which in Oscar means the opposite of calm.

"No. Yes. No." I set down my cup. Precision, Underhill. "I mean: I no longer have a reason. Before, everything was necessity. Crossing, surviving, finding you, anchoring your words at the Spire — all of it was required, and requirement is a kind of permission. You never have to ask whether you're allowed to love someone when the universe has scheduled it." I heard the word leave my mouth and did not retrieve it. "Now the schedule is empty. Now if I stay, it isn't destiny. It's just a choice. A plain, ungoverned, unwritten choice, made by a person with no role at all."

Oscar was quiet for a long moment. Then she unrolled the map she'd been carrying all week — the one she wouldn't show anyone — and spread it on the table between us.

It was Ironhollow. But not as I'd seen it in her earlier work, all borders and boundaries, the valley drawn like a fortress against the world. This map was open at every edge. Rivers ran off the parchment and continued, faintly, onto blank paper beyond, as if the valley had finally agreed to be part of a larger country. And in the margin, in her small fierce hand, she had written a single line:

*No longer bound.*

"I spent my whole life drawing places I couldn't go," she said. "Mapping realms from rumor because the border wouldn't let magic in and wouldn't let me out — except it turns out it wasn't keeping me in, it was keeping me *small*. And then I lost the hand, and half my sight, and every drop of magic my maps ever carried, and do you know what I discovered?" Her eyes — one whole, one scarred pale at the edge — were bright. "I discovered none of it was ever mine anyway. The magic was the covenant's. The longing was mine. The wanting was mine. That's the only thing I actually own."

"What are you saying, Oscar?"

"I'm saying the prophecy never made me love you. It just gave us terrible timing." She put her hand over mine, flat on the map, the way she'd pressed it at the Spire when the words needed anchoring. "There's no schedule now. No role. No reason. So here's my reason: I choose you. Ungoverned. Unwritten. In my own hand."

And I — archivist, reader, woman of ten thousand documents — said the least archival thing I have ever said, which was nothing at all, because I was kissing her.

The map crinkled under our hands. Neither of us minded. It was, after all, a map of what could be, and something was.

* * *

**Oscar**

Morning found us on the roof of the Landlady's house, because Wren wanted to see the city wake and I wanted to draw it, and these turned out to be compatible desires.

Below us, the City of Borrowed Names went about its first fully honest century. Smoke rose from chimneys with real names attached. Somewhere a bell rang that used to be silent on principle. My scarred eye ached in the cold the way it always does now, a dull reminder priced into every sunrise, and I found I didn't begrudge it. Half a view of this was more than I'd ever expected to have of anywhere.

"Do you miss it?" Wren asked. "The magic in the maps?"

I considered honestly. "I miss the way a finished map used to hum. Like a struck bell that wouldn't quite stop." I tapped the blank margin of the new one drying on my knees. "But that hum was never really mine — it was the old law leaking through me. What I have now is harder. Ink that stays ink. Lines that mean exactly what they say and nothing more." I smiled. "Turns out I was always better at meaning things than at magic."

"And Ironhollow?" she said, gentler. "When you go back."

"When *we* go back," I said, and watched her pretend she hadn't already packed.

Because that's the hook of it, the thing I've been turning over all week like a stone with something glinting underneath: the new covenant unsealed the valley. Magic is seeping into Ironhollow for the first time in a hundred years — Bramble says the hedges there will be *ferocious* — and nobody knows what a magic-dead place looks like when it wakes up. No maps exist. No accounts, no rumors, nothing. An entire realm of first drafts.

It's the only unmapped thing left.

I rolled up the city, kissed my archivist on her ink-stained fingers, and started making lists.
