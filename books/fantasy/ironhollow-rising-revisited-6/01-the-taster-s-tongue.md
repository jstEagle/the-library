## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison in the king's honeyed porridge tasted of almonds and regret.

Quentin Lockridge let it sit on the tongue for the prescribed count—ten heartbeats, no more, no less—then swallowed with the smooth, unbothered motion of a person swallowing nothing at all. Around them, the morning hall went on breathing: pages scurrying with ewers of watered wine, a chamberlain arguing softly about tapestries, the king himself hunched over a dispatch from the salt coast, his grey beard trailing into his cup. No one watched Quentin. That was the point of them. A taster who drew the eye was a taster who had already failed at half their job.

Almonds and regret. Cyanide wore many faces—bitter peach kernels, burnt sugar, sometimes nothing at all—but this batch had been cut with something floral, something that bloomed at the back of the throat like a funeral lily. Quentin catalogued it without moving a muscle of their face. *Hemlock extract, perhaps, or foxglove steeped too long. Cut with almond oil to mask the bitterness, which means an amateur, which means someone local.* The dose was small. Small enough that a healthy body might shrug it off entirely; small enough that the intended victim was meant to sicken slowly, over weeks, while suspicion scattered like startled birds.

Small enough, certainly, not to trouble a taster whose blood had been pickled in every poison the Thornfell apothecaries could brew.

"Is it fit?" the steward asked, as he asked every morning, in the flat voice of a man asking about weather.

"It's fit," Quentin said, and meant: *someone tried to kill your king today, gently, the way you'd weed a garden.*

They did not say it aloud. They never said it aloud. Instead they took the silver stylus from their belt, scratched three small marks into the wax tablet they carried everywhere—the private shorthand of nine years' service—and moved on to the bread.

* * *

The bread came up from the castle kitchens on a covered tray, borne by a kitchen girl who couldn't have seen fifteen winters and who looked at Quentin the way people looked at executioners: politely, and slightly sideways, as though courtesy might be contagious where death was concerned. Quentin lifted the cloth. Steam rose. Underneath sat a loaf still warm from the oven, its crust split in the long diagonal slash that was the mark of Wren's knife, and beside it a pat of butter stamped with the crown's sigil.

Quentin broke the heel from the loaf, chewed, waited. Good wheat, a whisper of rye, salt from the eastern pans. No bitterness. No numbness spreading along the gums. No sweetness where sweetness had no business being. They let the crust dissolve on their tongue like a eulogy and gave the girl a nod.

She fled.

That was fine. That was normal. In nine years, Quentin had learned that the court divided all humanity into two categories: those who might be poisoned, and those who might do the poisoning. There was no third box on the ledger. A taster lived permanently in the second column, and even the servants who brought the food kept their distance, as if the profession were catching—as if, by tasting death daily, Quentin might exhale it onto the innocent.

Let them think so. Fear was a kind of armor, and Quentin had learned early that armor didn't need to fit well to hold.

They finished the inspection in the usual order: the butter (clean), the preserved plums (clean, though the syrup had begun to ferment—a note for the kitchens, not a threat), the smoked river-trout (clean, but *interesting*, because the smoke had been done with alderwood instead of the usual oak, and alderwood smoke carried trace compounds that could mask certain alkaloids, which meant either a new cook or a careless one, and both possibilities deserved a word). By the time the king finally lifted his spoon, Quentin had already eaten a portion of everything on the table and found it, on balance, only mildly murderous.

"You're looking pale, Taster," the king said, around his first mouthful. He said it every morning. It was practically a blessing.

"I'm always pale, Majesty."

"Mm." The king chewed, swallowed, returned to his dispatch. "Vessane tells me the mountain roads are restless again. Smugglers in the high passes. You'd know something of that, I imagine."

Quentin's pulse did not change. Nine years of practice had burned that reflex out of them entirely; their heartbeat was a servant now, trained to sit when told. "I taste food, Majesty. Roads are outside my jurisdiction."

"Of course they are." The king smiled without warmth, the smile of a man who owned the map and resented the territory. "Lady Vessane believes otherwise. She has... theories about the Thornfell passes. Old stones and older bargains." He waved a hand, dismissing his own words before they could become dangerous. "Eat something, Taster. You make the rest of us nervous, standing there hungry."

*I'm never hungry,* Quentin thought, and did not say. Hunger required appetite, and appetite required a future worth eating toward. What Quentin had instead was a schedule, a tongue calibrated finer than any assay-laboratory's scales, and a body so saturated with toxins that most poisons registered as flavor notes rather than threats. Bitter almond. Burnt sugar. The green bite of hemlock. The coppery ghost of arsenic, which lingered like a bad memory. Nine years of measured doses, taken on purpose, in ascending strengths, until Quentin's blood had become a fortress and their palate a map of every way a human being could be quietly murdered.

It was, the spymaster had once told them, the safest job in the kingdom. It was also, Quentin had privately concluded, the loneliest. You could not share a meal with anyone. You could not kiss anyone without tasting what they'd eaten and cataloguing it. You could not accept a gift of wine without running through the vintage, the seal, the dust pattern on the cork, the possibility of thallium. Trust, for a taster, was not a feeling. It was a procedure, performed so often it became invisible, like breathing.

Quentin had gotten very good at invisible.

* * *

The morning's work done, Quentin took the long way back toward the servants' quarters—not because it was shorter, but because it passed the kitchen corridor, and the kitchen corridor smelled of yeast and woodsmoke and, faintly, of rosemary, which was the smell of Wren's hands.

Wren ran the castle kitchen the way a good captain runs a ship: absolutely, cheerfully, and with a rolling pin within reach at all times. She had come to the castle as a child—nobody remembered exactly when, and Wren herself claimed her memory began the day she first tasted soup—and she had grown into the kind of cook whom guards lied to their sergeants for, just to get seconds. Orphans ate at her hearth whether they worked there or not. Homesick soldiers got extra dumplings and no questions asked. And once a day, without fail, a plate appeared in the alcove by the flour store, set aside under a clean cloth, holding whatever Wren had decided the castle's taster ought to eat.

Quentin had never asked her why. Asking would require conversation, and conversation would require proximity, and proximity would require Quentin to explain why they flinched when offered anything to drink.

Today the alcove held two fresh rolls, a dish of honey, and a sprig of rosemary laid across the plate like a signature.

Quentin stood in the doorway longer than was strictly professional.

"You're looming," Wren said, without turning from her cauldron. She was stirring something that smelled of onions and patience. "You loom every morning. Most people knock. Some people even say hello."

"Hello," Quentin said. Their voice came out rougher than intended, rusted from disuse. They cleared their throat. "The trout was smoked with alderwood."

"Was it? Brannoc's cousin sent up a new shipment. Said oak was dearer this season." Wren glanced back over her shoulder, flour to the elbows, a smudge of ash on one cheekbone like war paint. "Is it a problem?"

"No. It's—" Quentin hesitated. "It's fine. It's good, actually. Just... different."

"Different isn't bad." Wren turned fully then, wiping her hands on her apron, and looked at Quentin with the particular directness she reserved for dough that wasn't rising properly. "You look tired, Lockridge."

"I always look tired."

"Yes, but usually you pretend better." She nodded at the alcove. "Eat your rolls. The honey's from the beekeeper down in Millbrook; he waters it down sometimes, so I checked it myself. Well. I made Tam check it. I don't eat honey."

Something in Quentin's chest did a small, unauthorized maneuver. "You checked my honey."

"I check everyone's honey. It's called feeding people." But she was smiling as she said it, the crooked smile that pulled the ash-smudge cheek higher than the other, and Quentin felt the smile land somewhere beneath the ribs, in the fortified interior where they kept everything they couldn't afford to feel.

Because that was the trouble with Wren. Everyone else in the castle saw a taster: pale, precise, untouchable, a creature of procedure who sampled death before breakfast and reported it in wax and shorthand. Wren saw a person who liked rolls. Who lingered in doorways. Whose shoulders came down half an inch when the kitchen smelled of rosemary. She fed Quentin the way she fed everyone—generously, without arithmetic—and every morning Quentin accepted the plate knowing that the kindest thing they could ever do was keep walking, keep the wall up, keep the double life double.

Because Quentin Lockridge was not, in any way that mattered, a taster.

The taster was the mask. Beneath it—beneath the wax tablet and the calibrated tongue and the careful, forgettable face—was the last living heir of a warding bloodline that the histories recorded as extinct, hidden in plain sight inside the very court that believed it had buried the name. Every smuggler's path through the Thornfells lived in Quentin's memory: every goat-track, every collapsed mine-shaft repurposed for contraband, every warden's patrol rotation memorized and sold, carefully, anonymously, to the right criminals at the right times so the wrong cargo never reached the wrong hands. Quentin had spent nine years being nobody, guarding a secret that would get them killed in a dozen inventive ways if it surfaced, and the discipline of it had worn grooves so deep that some mornings Quentin wasn't sure there was a person left underneath the procedure.

But there was. And the person underneath liked rolls. And rosemary. And the sound of Wren humming in the mornings, tuneless and content, a melody Quentin had never heard anywhere else and had privately decided was Wren's own invention, hers alone, as particular to her as the ash-smudge and the crooked smile.

"Eat," Wren repeated, softer now. "You've got that look. The one where you're doing sums nobody asked you to do."

Quentin sat on the flour barrel, took a roll, and bit into it. Warm. Good wheat, a whisper of rye, salt from the eastern pans, and underneath it all the faintest thread of rosemary baked into the crust, which Wren denied adding and added anyway, every time.

No bitterness. No numbness. Nothing to catalogue except the fact that this—this barrel, this alcove, this woman's back turned trustingly while Quentin sat in her kitchen eating bread she'd checked for them specifically—was the single most dangerous thing Quentin had tasted in nine years.

Dangerous, because it was the one thing in the castle Quentin would grieve losing.

* * *

They stayed until the bells rang terce, longer than usual, trading the kind of talk that wasn't really talk: Wren complaining about the fishmonger, Quentin offering opinions on trout with the gravity of a state counselor, both of them circling, always, the same unspoken center and never once touching it. When the bells faded, Quentin rose, brushed crumbs from their coat, and performed the small ritual that had become, over nine years, the closest thing they had to prayer: they palmed the second roll.

Wren pretended not to see. This was also ritual. The roll was ostensibly for the road, for later, for hunger—except Quentin was never hungry, and they both knew it, and the roll would go, as it always went, to the orphan boy who slept in the woodstore behind the tannery and who thought the castle's terrifying taster was simply a strange, generous ghost who left bread.

"Same time tomorrow?" Wren asked, already turning back to her cauldron, casual as anything, as if the answer weren't the hinge of Quentin's entire day.

"Same time tomorrow," Quentin said.

In the corridor outside, the roll warm against their palm, Quentin paused at the narrow window that faced the mountains. The Thornfells stood sharp against the morning sky, blue-grey and patient, their high passes veiled in cloud. Somewhere up there, old stones stood in old circles, doing work no one at court remembered how to do, anchored by a bargain no one alive fully understood. Somewhere up there, the smugglers' paths wound like veins through the rock, and Quentin knew each one the way they knew the poisons: intimately, professionally, and with a low constant hum of unease that had never quite gone away.

Lately, the unease had been louder.

Three contacts in two months had mentioned the same thing, in the sideways manner of people reporting weather they don't believe: someone was buying up old ward-stones. Not stealing them—buying them, paying honest coin through intermediaries, which was stranger than theft. Stones that had guarded the border since before the kingdom had a name, pried from their circles and carted away by men who asked no questions and paid in advance.

Quentin had filed the reports. Had told themselves it was smuggling, ordinary greed, nothing that touched the buried name they carried.

But standing at the window now, roll in hand, watching cloud crawl across the high passes, Quentin felt the unease sharpen into something with edges. The king had mentioned Vessane's theories. Vessane, who had arrived at court two years ago from nowhere in particular, who smelled—when she leaned close, when the wind was right—of deep stone and cold air, of places the sun had never bothered to visit.

Below, in the kitchen, Wren had begun to hum.

Quentin listened for a moment—tuneless, content, hers—then turned from the window and walked out into the ordinary morning, carrying their roll, their secrets, and the growing, formless dread that the world beneath the mountain had begun, at last, to move.
