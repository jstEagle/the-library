## 2. Salt Roads Through Thornfell

The road out of Ironhollow smelled of woodsmoke and wet granite, and Quentin Lockridge walked it the way they walked everything: like a person with nowhere particular to be.

That was the trick of it. Nine years of tasting had taught them that the deadliest thing in any room was not the poison but the attention it drew. A taster who moved too quickly through the castle was a taster with something to hide. A taster who lingered over the king's cup, who hummed while he worked, who complained cheerfully about his feet—*that* taster was furniture. Invisible. And invisibility, Quentin had learned, was not merely useful. It was load-bearing. It held up everything.

So they ambled now, boots scuffing the old salt road as it climbed away from the city gates, past the last tanneries and the shrine where travelers spat for luck, wearing the expression of a servant sent on a dull errand. Which, technically, they were. The steward's list tucked inside their coat named three items: lamp oil, dried herring, and a bolt of grey wool for the winter livery. All three could be found in Thornhollow village, half a day's walk into the mountains. None of them required Quentin personally.

That was rather the point.

The mountains rose ahead of them in the last of the light, the Thornfell range shouldering up against the sky like sleeping animals—dark, massive, indifferent. Most people in Ironhollow looked at those peaks and saw only distance: the far edge of the kingdom, the place maps got vague and cartographers started guessing. Quentin looked at them and saw something closer to a face. Familiar. Scarred. Keeping its secrets close.

They knew these mountains the way other people knew their own hands.

Not from maps—the crown's surveyors had mapped perhaps a tenth of what wound through that stone—but from nine years of quiet study, of memorizing smugglers' routes the way a priest memorized psalms. There were forty-one ways through Thornfell that appeared on no official document. Quentin could name thirty-eight of them. The remaining three belonged to people who would kill to keep them unnamed, and Quentin had decided long ago that professional courtesy extended even to murderers.

The salt road itself was honest enough—a proper trade route, wide enough for carts, marked with waystones every mile. But a quarter mile past the luck-shrine, where the road bent around a shoulder of rock, there was a gap in the guardrail barely wider than a goat track. Quentin stepped through it without breaking stride, the way a person steps through their own front door.

The path beyond dropped steeply through pine shadow, switchbacking down toward a river gorge that most travelers never knew existed. The air changed at once—cooler, wetter, threaded with the smell of moss and cold stone and, faintly, something mineral and strange that Quentin had never been able to name. It lived only in the deep places of Thornfell. Old places.

Their grandmother would have known what it was.

Quentin shut that door firmly, the way one shuts a door on a room full of things that might come out.

* * *

They made good time in the failing light. Their body knew this path the way it knew the layout of the king's breakfast tray: left foot here, handhold there, duck under the leaning birch before it caught your collar. Nine years ago, walking this route for the first time, they had been terrified—of falling, of being seen, of what waited at the bottom. Now the fear had worn down to something smooth and manageable, a pebble carried so long it fit the palm perfectly.

That was the thing about fear, Quentin thought, hopping a stream on stones worn glassy by centuries of water. You didn't conquer it. You just broke it in, like a boot.

The meeting place was an abandoned quarry hut two miles down the gorge, its roof half-collapsed, its walls furred with lichen the color of old coins. Someone had built a fire pit in the corner years ago and someone else had used it since; the ash was fresh. Quentin did not approach directly. They circled wide, came at the hut from above, and stood for a long moment in the shadows of the pines, reading the scene the way they read a plate set before the king.

One person inside. Sitting, not crouching. Breathing slow. The smell of pipe smoke—bitterleaf, cheap cut—and beneath it, faintly, tallow and rope. No steel oiled recently; no fear-sweat. Either it was who Quentin expected, or it was a very patient assassin with excellent hygiene.

"Evening, Magpie," Quentin said, stepping into the doorway. "You're early."

A shape by the fire resolved into a broad woman wrapped in a shepherd's cloak, her grey-streaked hair braided tight to her skull. Dree Marsh. Smuggler, fence, occasional rescuer of orphans when the mood took her, though she'd deny all three charges under torture. She looked up with eyes the pale green of bottle glass and grinned around her pipe.

"And you're late, Taster. Thought maybe the crown finally fed you something interesting."

"The crown feeds me nothing interesting. That's the entire job." Quentin settled onto an overturned crate across the fire pit, close enough for warmth, far enough for dignity. "Your message said you had news."

Dree's grin faded by degrees, the way sunset fades—not all at once, but steadily, until you realized the light had gone. She knocked out her pipe against her boot heel and didn't refill it, which from Dree was the equivalent of a church bell tolling.

"Aye. News." She reached into her cloak and produced something wrapped in oilcloth, setting it on the flat stone between them with the care of a midwife laying down a newborn. "Unwrap it."

Quentin did. Inside lay a chunk of grey stone the size of a fist, roughly hexagonal, its faces carved with lines so fine and so regular they might have been natural—except nothing natural drew patterns in sevens.

Quentin's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, the way it did when they tasted something bitter beneath the honey.

"Ward-stone," they said. Kept their voice level. Admired themselves for it later.

"Fragment of one." Dree nodded at it. "Cracked clean through, but look at the break. Look at the *edges*, Taster."

Quentin turned it in the firelight. The fracture surfaces weren't rough, the way broken stone ought to be. They were smooth. Fused. As if the stone had been melted apart by something very precise and very hungry.

"This wasn't smashed," Quentin said quietly. "It was *unmade*."

"Now you're catching up to where I've been all week, and I'll tell you, I don't care for the neighborhood." Dree leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Three months back, my people start noticing strangers in the high passes. Not soldiers, not surveyors, not pilgrims—none of the usual fools. Quiet folk. Grey cloaks. They don't camp, they don't sing, they don't light fires you can see. And everywhere they go, they're asking after the same thing." She tapped the fragment with one blunt finger. "Old ward-stones. Boundary markers. The kind that've been sitting on mountaintops doing nothing visible since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother. There's a trade in 'em now, Taster. Somebody's *buying*. Paying stupid money, too. Gold enough that three separate crews I know have gone looking, and none of them asking why."

Quentin set the fragment down. Their hands wanted to shake and they forbade them to.

"Who's buying?"

"That's the interesting part. Or the part that kept me up nights, anyway." Dree's voice dropped, though there was no one within a mile to hear. "The gold they pay is old. Not old like grandad's coin—old like *minted-under-a-dead-dynasty* old. My assayer nearly wept over it. Coin that shouldn't exist anymore, in quantities nobody should have anymore. And the grey-cloaks—" she hesitated, and Quentin watched her decide whether to say the next thing, watched her decide to say it, "—one of my boys got close enough to hear them talk amongst themselves. Said they spoke the low tongue fine enough, but when one of them cursed—just stubbed a toe, mind—he cursed in a language my boy'd never heard. Said it sounded like rocks grinding underground. Like the mountain talking in its sleep."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the gorge, a night bird called once and thought better of it.

Quentin stared at the ward-stone fragment and felt the pebble of routine fear in their palm suddenly grow edges.

Ward-stones were not supposed to be unmade. Ward-stones were, if the histories could be believed—and Quentin had spent nine years quietly doubting every history the crown approved, which made them oddly protective of the ones it hadn't—the bones of the border itself. The great wards that ringed the kingdom, that kept certain things out and certain things in, had been anchored in stones like this one, set by bloodlines whose names had been scrubbed from every record with a thoroughness that had always struck Quentin as less like history and more like surgery.

Someone was collecting the bones. Someone with dead-dynasty gold and a language like grinding stone.

"You're wondering the same thing I'm wondering," Dree said, watching their face. "I can see the wheels going round behind your eyes, Taster. So let me ask it plain, since you're the only soul I know who reads more than he says. What happens to a border when you pull enough nails out of it?"

"I don't know," Quentin said. It was true, and it was also the most frightening sentence they'd spoken in years, because they were supposed to be the one who knew things. That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of their life: they gave the smugglers warning of patrols and routes, and the smugglers gave them eyes in the dark places the crown couldn't see, and between the two of them they kept a fragile peace that nobody upstairs knew existed. Quentin was the map. Quentin was the one who understood the terrain.

And the terrain had just started moving.

"There's more," Dree said. "Course there is. The grey-cloaks aren't just buying. They're *counting*. My boy says they carry ledgers—proper ones, bound in leather older than the coin. And every stone they find, cracked or whole or buried under six feet of scree, gets written down. Like inventory." Her pale eyes fixed on Quentin. "Like somebody's taking stock before a sale."

*Before a sale.* The words sat in Quentin's chest like swallowed ice.

Everything sold. Everything bought. Somewhere in the arithmetic of the world, someone had tallied up the old defenses of Thornfell and found them wanting—or found them *valuable*—and was preparing to move them, the way a merchant moves stock, the way a court moves debts.

Quentin thought, absurdly, of Wren. Of the kitchen at dusk, of the extra roll pressed warm into their hand that morning with a look that asked nothing and offered everything. Wren, who sang while she worked, songs she claimed not to remember learning. Wren, who fed orphans off the books and called it waste management. If the borders failed—if whatever the wards kept out came pouring through the passes—Ironhollow would burn, and the kitchens would burn first, because kitchens were always in the oldest parts of the castle, the parts built on foundations nobody remembered laying—

Stop. Quentin put a hand flat against the cold stone floor of the quarry hut and breathed. One breath. Two. The taster's discipline: assess the dose, assess the body, do not panic before the evidence requires it.

The evidence did not yet require panic. It required work.

"Dree. I need three things."

"Course you do." But she said it fondly, reaching for her pipe again. "Go on."

"First—whoever's selling to the grey-cloaks, I want names. Second—I want word sent to every crew you trust: nobody sells another stone until I say. Tell them the crown's cracking down, tell them plague lives in the stones, tell them anything. Third—" Quentin hesitated, then committed, because hesitation was a luxury the next few weeks wouldn't afford. "Third, I need to walk the high boundary myself. The old line, north of the Salt Spur. I want to see how many markers are missing."

Dree went very still. "The old line's not a smuggling route, Taster. Nobody walks it. Nobody's walked it in living memory, except—" She stopped herself, and Quentin watched her almost-words hang in the smoky air: *except whoever set the stones in the first place.*

"Except ghosts," Quentin finished smoothly. "Yes. Well. Ghosts are my specialty. I taste for a living; I'm very familiar with things that are present and officially denied."

Dree studied them for a long moment, pipe smoke curling between them like a question mark. Whatever she saw in Quentin's face made her sigh, heavy and theatrical, the sigh of a woman accepting a burden she'd already accepted.

"Fine. Fine. I'll get you to the Salt Spur by week's end, and after that you're on your own, because I'm sentimental, not suicidal." She jabbed the pipe stem at the ward-stone fragment. "Keep that. Show it to someone who knows more than both of us, if such a creature exists. And Taster—" her voice gentled, which was somehow worse than the threat of it, "—whatever family business you're not telling me about, whatever it is that makes you look at these mountains like they owe you money—you might want to settle accounts soon. Because if somebody's pulling nails out of the border, I'd wager my best mule they know exactly whose hands drove them in."

Quentin stood. Collected the oilcloth bundle. Made their voice into the pleasant, empty instrument it needed to be.

"I have no family, Dree. I'm a taster. I taste things."

"Aye," Dree said, to their back, as Quentin stepped out into the dark. "And I'm a shepherd."

* * *

The climb back was harder than the descent, as climbs always were, and darker, as nights always were when your head was full of unwelcome arithmetic.

Quentin counted ward-stones instead of sheep. The old boundary line ran along the spine of the western peaks—thirty-one major anchors, if the fragments of song and story Quentin had hoarded since childhood could be trusted, and Quentin had never dared decide whether to trust them. Thirty-one stones, set by thirty-one hands, or one pair of hands thirty-one times, or—

No. Stop. That door again, rattling on its hinges.

But the door had never rattled like this before. For nine years Quentin had kept it shut with discipline and distance, with the daily small treasons of a hidden life well-lived: taste the food, map the routes, slip the warnings, take no thanks, make no roots. Invisibility as a fortress. And it had *worked*. It had worked so well that some mornings Quentin woke and genuinely could not remember what was being protected, only that protection was required.

Now someone was buying up the bones of the border with the gold of a dead dynasty, speaking a language like grinding stone, keeping ledgers like a merchant before a sale. And Quentin had stood in a ruined hut holding a piece of the answer in their hands, and felt—not fear, exactly. Something older than fear. Something that lived below fear, in the cellar of the self, in the place where the locked door was.

Recognition.

The mineral smell of the deep gorge clung to their coat as they climbed, and Quentin found themselves breathing it deliberately, the way one presses a tongue against a sore tooth. Their grandmother had smelled of that. Their grandmother, whose name Quentin had not spoken aloud in nine years, whose grave was somewhere on the far side of these mountains, whose hands had been scarred in patterns Quentin had been told were burns and had never quite believed were burns—

The stars were coming out overhead as Quentin crested the ridge above the luck-shrine. They counted them out of habit, the way they counted everything: the constellations wheeling over Thornfell, bright and orderly and fixed.

All present. All accounted for.

For now.

Below, the lights of Ironhollow glimmered in the valley like embers in a grate, and somewhere among them a kitchen would still be warm, and someone would be humming a tune she couldn't remember learning, and tomorrow morning Quentin would stand at the king's elbow and taste his breakfast and smile his furniture-smile and tell no one anything, the same as every day for nine years.

But tonight, walking down through the dark toward the gates, Quentin carried a dead dynasty's worth of dread in their pocket alongside the steward's shopping list, and for the first time in nine years, the mask sat crooked on their face.

Something had begun counting the old stones.

And Quentin could not shake the feeling that somewhere in that ledger, in that inventory of everything bought and sold and taken stock of, there was a page with their family's name on it—and beside it, in careful, grinding-stone script, the word *paid*.
