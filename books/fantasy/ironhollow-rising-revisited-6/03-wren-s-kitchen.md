## 3. Wren's Kitchen

The kitchen woke before the castle did, the way it always did, and Wren woke with it—not because she had to, but because her body had long ago learned the rhythm of ovens the way other people learned the rhythm of their own hearts.

She came down the back stair in the dark with her sleeves already rolled, past the scullery where the night-boy, Tam, slept curled in the warmth of the banked bread oven like a cat who had claimed the best spot in the house and knew it. She didn't wake him. There would be time enough for work when the light came, and until then there was only her, the coals, and the particular holy silence of a kitchen at four in the morning, when anything could still be made of the day.

Wren struck flint to the main hearth and watched the fire take, orange blooming up through the black like dawn arriving early just for her. Twenty-two years she'd been doing this. First as a scullery girl with burned fingers and no last name anyone cared about, then as under-cook, then—when old Marta's knees finally gave out and the king's steward realized no one else could feed three hundred mouths without mutiny—as keeper of the kitchens entire. The castle above might belong to King Aldous and his banners and his taxes and his endless gray politics, but this room, this warm stone belly of the place, belonged to her. Everyone knew it. Even the king knew it, though he'd never say so. He'd once sent down a compliment via chamberlain about her honey-cakes, and Wren had sent back up a honey-cake and a note that read *I know. Tell no one.* The chamberlain had been scandalized. The king, according to rumor, had laughed until he coughed.

She filled the great copper with water from the cistern pump, hauled it to the hook over the fire with a grunt she refused to be embarrassed by, and began the morning the way mornings ought to begin: with porridge for sixty, oats tumbling into the pot in a slow ivory cascade, salt from the crock, a knob of butter the size of her fist because winter was coming and butter was cheaper than sickness.

And while the porridge swelled and the first loaves proofed under their cloth, Wren hummed.

It wasn't a song she knew the name of. It wasn't even, properly, a song she *knew*—it lived somewhere below knowing, in the same place her hands kept the memory of kneading and her nose kept the memory of what burning sugar smelled like two breaths before it burned. It was six or seven notes, rising and falling, simple as a rocking chair, and she'd caught herself humming it for as long as she could remember: over bread dough, over sick children, over the grave of the old hound Bristle who had slept by her hearth for eleven years. When she tried to grab hold of it—to find its beginning, its end, where she'd learned it—it slipped away like steam off a pudding. There was no beginning. It had simply always been in her, the way the scar on her left thumb had always been there, origin unknown.

*Hush now, low now,* the melody seemed to say, though she'd never put words to it and didn't dare try. Some things lost their power if you looked at them straight.

The kitchen door banged open at half past five, right on schedule.

"Right, you lot," Wren said, not turning from her dough. "Flour's in the bins, water's hot, and if any of you touches my brown sugar before the guards' puddings are done I will know, I always know, and there will be consequences of a creative nature."

"Morning to you too, sunshine," said Dossie, the oldest of her three kitchen girls, forty if she was a day and built like a dock crane, already tying on her apron. Behind her came little Fen, fourteen, all elbows and ambition, and behind Fen came Cook's-bane himself—the name the guards had given the cat, a vast orange tom with a torn ear who surveyed the kitchen from atop the flour bin like a small tyrant reviewing troops.

"It smells like heaven down here," Fen said, inhaling dramatically. "It always smells like heaven."

"That's because heaven is mostly onions and hard work." Wren handed her the rolling pin without looking. "Pastry. Six dozen. And don't give me that face, your pastry's better than mine was at your age, which I'll deny saying if you ever repeat it."

By six the kitchen was running full-tilt, and Wren moved through it the way a river moves through its own bed—knowing every stone, every eddy, every place where the current ran fast. This was the thing nobody outside these walls understood: a castle kitchen was not a room. It was a country. It had borders (the serving hatch, the great doors, the stair to the hall), it had laws (wash your hands, count your knives, nobody eats before the taster does), it had diplomacy (the quartermaster wanted her budget cut again; the captain of the guard wanted hot meals on the wall walk in all weather; the chamberlain wanted everything garnished because garnish looked expensive) and it had citizens, dozens of them, whose whole lives passed through her jurisdiction daily.

There were the guards, big hungry men and women who came down at shift-change smelling of rain and spear-oil, and whom Wren fed twice what the crown allotted because soldiers who felt forgotten got careless, and careless soldiers got killed, and she'd buried enough names in her heart already. There were the servants, chambermaids and grooms and laundry-girls, who came shyly to the kitchen door at odd hours because everyone in the castle somehow knew that Wren fed people who were hungry even when they couldn't say why they were hungry. And there were the orphans.

That had started by accident, the way most of the good things in her life had. A chimney-sweep's boy, eight years old, caught stealing bread off the cooling rack six winters back—and instead of hauling him to the steward for the whipping that was customary, Wren had sat him down, handed him the rest of the loaf, some cheese, and a job scraping pots. "You steal it again, I break your fingers," she'd told him. "You work for it, you eat every day. Choose." He'd chosen. His name was Pip, and he was seventeen now and ran the castle's coal deliveries, and he brought her news from every corner of the city the way other boys brought flowers.

After Pip came others. Not many—a handful at a time, the ones the city chewed up and spat out too small. Wren had no philosophy about it. She'd been one of them once, a child delivered to the castle gates with nothing but the dress she stood up in and no story anyone bothered to record, and someone—she'd never learned who—had fed her that first night. Warmth passed forward was the only scripture she kept.

"You're humming again," Dossie said, appearing at her elbow with a tray of risen loaves. "Same tune."

"Am I?" Wren said, though she knew perfectly well she was.

"You always do it when you're happy. Or worried. Which is it today?"

"Can't it be both?"

Dossie snorted and slid the loaves into the oven's maw. "Everything with you is both. You're a woman made entirely of both."

Wren laughed and flicked flour at her, and the morning rolled on.

* * *

Midmorning brought the first of her regulars, as it always did: the guards' relief, then the chambermaid with the toothache who needed clove oil and sympathy in equal measure, then the young priest who pretended he came for charity's sake but really came because Wren's barley soup was better than anything the temple served and God, in her opinion, would understand.

And then, a little after ten, when the rush had crested and broken and the kitchen settled into its midday rhythm, the door opened quietly—the way it always opened quietly, because its owner had spent nine years learning to move like a shadow—and Quentin Lockridge slipped inside.

Well. *Slipped.* That was the word everyone else would have used. The crown's food-taster moved through the castle like smoke through a keyhole, silent and unremarkable, present everywhere and noticed nowhere. But Wren had watched that smoke for nine years now, and she knew better. She knew that Quentin's silence wasn't emptiness. It was a held breath. Nine years long.

"There's my favorite ghost," she said, not turning from the stew pot. "Sit. There's the end of yesterday's loaf and there's fresh butter, and before you say a single word about how you shouldn't, remember that I decide who eats in this country and you're a citizen whether you like it or not."

"I brought you something," Quentin said, and set it on the table: a small paper twist, which when Wren opened it proved to hold dried apricots, the good kind, from the southern caravans, the kind that cost more per pound than a guard earned in a week.

"Quentin Lockridge." She fixed them with her best cook's stare, the one that had quelled mutinies among scullery boys. "Where did a crown servant get southern apricots?"

"A merchant owed me a favor."

"What kind of favor makes a man owe *apricots?*"

"The kind I'm not at liberty to discuss." They sat, and took the bread she pushed toward them, and ate the way they always ate—carefully, appreciatively, like someone who understood food better than almost anyone alive and enjoyed it less than almost anyone alive. That was the poison-taster's curse, Wren supposed. Nine years of cataloguing the world by its dangers. She'd seen them taste a plain apple once, at some feast, and watched their face do a small complicated dance: pleasure first, genuine pleasure, and then immediately beneath it the inventory—*bitterness at the core, wax on the skin, who handled it, how long since washing*—until the pleasure drowned. Eating, for Quentin, was an act of courage performed three times a day.

So she did what she always did. She fed them things that required no courage. Bread, butter, stew, plain apples from her own basket, things so honest they couldn't hide anything.

"Eat all of it," she said. "You've gone thin again. The court's chewing you up."

"The court doesn't chew me. The court doesn't notice me. That's rather the point of me."

"Well, *I* notice you, so sit still and eat." She said it lightly, the way she said everything important, wrapped in banter like medicine in honey. But she meant it down to the bone, and she thought—hoped—that Quentin knew it. Nine years of stolen breakfasts in this kitchen. Nine years of them appearing at her elbow whenever the weight of whatever-it-was-they-carried grew too heavy, saying nothing, being fed, saying nothing more, leaving lighter than they came. Other people might have demanded explanations. Wren had decided long ago that some people were like feral cats: you didn't grab them, you set out food and waited, and one day—if you were patient, if you were safe—they stayed.

She was patient. She was very good at waiting. Waiting was most of what a kitchen was.

"Tell me something true," Quentin said suddenly, around a mouthful of bread. It was an old game between them, older than either would admit. *Tell me something true.* One true thing, traded like coin.

Wren considered, stirring the stew. "True," she said. "True is... I found a grey hair this morning. Right at my temple. And I didn't pluck it."

Quentin's mouth curved—the rare real smile, the one that reached their eyes and made them look five years younger and infinitely less dangerous. "Vanity?"

"Time," Wren said. "I'm thirty-one, Q. I've earned every one of them, including the grey ones. Plucking them felt like lying to the mirror." She pointed the spoon at them. "Your turn."

Quentin looked down at the bread, and for a moment something moved behind their face—something old and tired and locked. "True," they said at last. "Sometimes, in this kitchen, I forget to be afraid. For a minute or two. And those are—" A pause. Recalculation. Wren watched them choose a smaller truth than the one they'd found. "Those are the best minutes I have."

And there it was again, that ache she'd carried for years now, the one shaped exactly like Quentin Lockridge and their locked rooms. *Someday*, she thought, ladling stew into a bowl and setting it before them with unnecessary force. *Someday you'll tell me what you're afraid of, and I'll feed you through it, because that's what I do.*

"Someday," she said aloud, "you're going to tell me your whole story. Start to finish. And I'm going to make a pie for the occasion. A big one."

"What kind of pie?"

"Haven't decided. Depends how bad the story is. Treason gets cherry. Betrayal gets rhubarb, unsweetened."

Quentin laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them—and for a moment the kitchen held nothing but warmth: the ovens ticking, the pot murmuring, Tam asleep in the corner with flour dusting his hair, the cat purring on the bin, and Quentin Lockridge eating stew like a person instead of an instrument.

Wren turned back to her dough and, without thinking, began to hum. *Hush now, low now.* The little melody rose and fell, rose and fell, and she kneaded to it, and shaped loaves to it, and didn't see Quentin go very still at the sound of it, staring at her back with an expression she would have given much to understand.

* * *

Evening came down over Ironhollow like a lid, and the kitchen emptied by degrees—Dossie home to her grandbabies, Fen off to the dormitory, Tam dispatched with a basket of ends-and-crusts for the widow Cally on Tanner's Row, because hunger didn't keep office hours. Wren banked the fires, wiped down the great tables, counted her knives, and stood a moment in the quiet with her hands flat on the warm wood, the way a captain stands on an empty deck.

It had been a good day. Ordinary. She treasured ordinary days the way misers treasured coins, counting them, hoarding them, knowing—without ever letting the thought fully form—how easily they could be spent.

She was banking the last coals when the strangeness started.

First, the cat. Cook's-bane, who feared nothing on this earth except the bath tub, rose abruptly from his spot, arched his back into a shape like a question mark, and stared at the high window over the sink—the little window that faced north, toward the mountains—with his pupils blown wide and black.

Then the bells. Not the alarm bells, not yet. Just the watch bell on the gatehouse ringing the hour, except it rang wrong—hesitant, the watchman losing count, starting over. Wren had heard that bell ten thousand evenings. It had never once stumbled.

And then the light changed.

Not dimmed—changed. The last red of sunset through the windows went thin and strange, like light through dirty glass, and the shadows in the corners deepened all at once, and the coals in the hearth seemed to shrink from themselves. Somewhere overhead, out in the courtyard, a servant cried out—a short, sharp sound, quickly smothered, the way people cry out when they've seen something their minds refuse.

Wren stood up slowly. Her arms prickled. The lullaby rose unbidden in her throat—*hush now, low now*—and for the first time in her life the melody felt less like comfort than like a hand reaching for a sword.

She stepped to the north window and looked up past the wall, past the roofs, to where the sky was going black over Thornfell.

The stars were going out. One by one, like candles pinched by an unseen hand, the stars were going out.

Down in the dark of her warm kingdom, Wren pressed her palm flat against the cold glass, and every instinct she owned—the instincts that had kept a nameless orphan alive, that had built a country out of flour and stubbornness—screamed at her with a single voice:

*Find Quentin.*
