## 4. The Night the Stars Went Out

Quentin had learned to read the sky the way other men read ledgers.

Nine years on the castle's high east wall had taught them which stars rose first over Thornfell in autumn, which ones the mountain mist swallowed by midnight, which ones the old wardens' almanacs called *anchor-lights*—the seven bright points that supposedly hung above the border stones like nails holding a painting to a wall. Quentin didn't believe in anchor-lights. They believed in aftertastes and ledger entries and the weight of a coin purse changing hands. The sky was just the sky.

But they still looked at it every night before bed. Habit. Or superstition wearing habit's coat.

On the last night the stars existed, Quentin was awake because of a bad almond.

The king's evening meal had included marchpane—honey and almond paste pressed into the shape of the royal stag—and Quentin had caught it on the third bite: something beneath the sweetness, faint as a whisper under a door. Bitter green. Not enough to kill. Enough to sicken, maybe, if the dose were larger; enough to tell Quentin that someone in the palace kitchens or someone paying the palace kitchens wanted the king weak rather than dead, and soon.

They had spat it into their handkerchief with practiced grace, declared the marchpane "heavily sugared but sound," and spent two hours afterward writing a coded note that would reach the captain of the gate by morning. That was the shape of their life: poison catalogued, threat deflected, no one the wiser. The crown's food-taster, diligent and dull. A man—or not quite a man, or not quite anything anyone could name—who tasted for a living and saw nothing else.

The note was sealed. The candle was guttering. Quentin sat in their narrow room above the tasting chamber with the window open to the autumn cold, doing what they always did before sleep: looking up.

The first star went out while they watched.

Not set. Not dimmed behind cloud. It simply ceased—a point of light snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, the way a candle dies when pinched between wet fingers. Quentin knew every kind of darkness. They knew the dark of a cellar, the dark of closed eyes, the dark behind the smoke-stain on a ceiling. This was none of those. This was a hole where light had been, clean-edged and absolute.

They told themselves it was cloud. Mist off the Thornfell passes could swallow stars whole; they'd seen it a hundred times.

Then a second star went out. Then a third and fourth together, and this time Quentin felt it—not saw, *felt*—a sensation like a tooth being drawn from the jaw of the world, a deep wrongness in the bones of the valley. The remaining stars began to fail in earnest then, winking out in no order Quentin could find, north and south and overhead, until the black above Ironhollow was not the black of night at all but the black of absence, of a page torn out of a book.

Quentin stood so fast the chair went over backward. They didn't hear it hit the floor.

Because they knew—some part of them had always known, some part they'd buried so deep even they couldn't dig it up without bleeding—what those stars were. The almanacs called them anchor-lights. The smugglers called them the Mountain's Teeth. And the oldest songs, the ones sung in kitchens by women who claimed they'd learned them from their mothers, called them something else entirely: *the eyes of the seal.*

Seven lights for seven founders. Seven bloodlines who had given something to the border wards when the wards were young, back when Ironhollow was two courts instead of one and the mountain had to be persuaded, bound, bargained with, to keep its teeth behind its lips.

Six of those lines had died out honestly—plague, war, time. The seventh had been murdered to the last child, or so every history in the royal library agreed, and Quentin knew better than anyone alive how thoroughly histories could be rewritten, because they had spent nine years making sure this one stayed rewritten.

*Extinct,* the records said. *Root and branch.*

And yet the stars were going out one by one like lamps in a house whose owner has stopped paying the lamplighter, and there was only one reason in all the world that should happen.

"No," Quentin said aloud, to the empty room, in a voice they barely recognized.

The bells began.

* * *

Ironhollow's alarm bells were meant for fire or invasion, and they rang accordingly—clashing, arrhythmic, each tower pulling its rope in panic without coordination. Quentin took the stairs three at a time, out into the inner ward, where torchlight and shouting had already replaced sleep.

Courtiers spilled from the great hall in dressing gowns and half-buckled armor, faces turned upward, mouths open. A woman Quentin recognized as a baroness from the river country was on her knees in the mud, praying to a sky that could no longer hear her. Guards ran the wall-walks with lanterns held high, as if light could be thrown upward like a stone.

"Quentin!" Someone seized their sleeve—the under-chamberlain, a soft man named Pell, his wig askew. "The stars! Is it poison? Has someone poisoned the— the air, the water, is it—" He was a fool, but he was a frightened fool, and frightened fools reached for the nearest explanation that involved someone to blame.

"The sky isn't poisoned," Quentin said, and heard how steady their voice came out, how the nine years of practice held even now. "Get everyone under cover. If the wards are down, the passes are open."

"The wards?" Pell blinked. "What do the wards have to do with—"

"Everything," Quentin said, and left him standing there.

They climbed. Their body knew the way to the east wall the way a tongue knows the roof of its own mouth, and they took it without deciding to, pushing past guards, up the stair that smelled of cold iron and bird droppings, out onto the parapet where the wind came down off Thornfell carrying pine and snowmelt and, tonight, something else underneath—something mineral and old, like the breath of a cellar that had been sealed for centuries.

From the wall, Quentin could see the whole sweep of the border country. The beacon towers along the Thornfell crest were dark. All of them. Those beacons weren't lit by men; they were fed by the wards themselves, pale blue flames that had burned without fuel since before the kingdom had a name, visible for forty miles on any clear night, the surest sign in the world that the borders held.

Every flame was gone.

And in the place where the strongest of them had burned—Warden's Rest, the old watchtower above the salt road—Quentin could see, with eyes sharpened by nine years of noticing what wasn't supposed to be noticed, a faint glow rising from the ground itself. Deep red. The color of coals under ash. The color, though they had no right to know this, of the light that came through the cracks of certain doors in the deepest parts of the mountain, doors that appeared on no map and were carved with crests that no living herald would recognize.

Doors like the one Quentin's grandmother had shown them once, in a dream or a memory or a lie, before teaching them the taste of every poison in the world and telling them: *You will never be safe, little one. But you can be invisible. Invisibility is a kind of safety, if you never stop paying for it.*

The last star went out.

It happened directly overhead, and Quentin felt it go the way one feels a bone break—not in the body, exactly, but in the truth of the body. Somewhere far below the castle, below the mines, below the roots of the mountain, something that had been held shut for three hundred years swung open with a sound no ear could catch but every soul in the valley flinched from. Horses screamed in the stable yard. Dogs in the town below began to howl, hundreds of them, a chorus rising from every hearth and kennel at once.

Quentin gripped the parapet stone and made themselves think, because thinking was the one discipline they had never allowed terror to interrupt.

The wards were anchored in blood. Everyone knew that much; it was in the public catechism, the pretty version taught to children. What the catechism did not say—what only four people in the kingdom knew, and Quentin had killed one of them—was *whose* blood, and *how*. The founders had not merely blessed the wards. They had been made into them. Bound into the anchor-stones, their souls set burning in the high places of the sky as living locks. When a founder's line ended honestly, the lock was supposed to pass to the next line, and the next, down through the generations of the seven houses. Six houses had managed their successions. The seventh—

The seventh had been sold. Every scrap of it. Sold to the court beneath the mountain in a bargain so old the ink had faded, and the payment had not been gold.

*Only one bloodline could have anchored those wards,* Quentin thought, watching the red glow strengthen in the notch of Warden's Rest. *And it was supposed to be extinct.*

Unless it hadn't been extinct. Unless somewhere, somehow, a thread of it had survived—had been hidden, or bought, or traded away in pieces too small for anyone to notice—and unless someone had just now found that thread and pulled it.

Someone who had been buying up old ward-stones. The thought arrived unbidden, cold as the wind: Brannoc's contact at the salt road crossing, three weeks ago, spitting into the fire. *"Someone's buying the old anchor-stones out of the passes, Lockridge. Paying silver for rocks. Nobody pays silver for rocks unless they know what's inside 'em."*

Quentin had filed it away as one more thread in the smuggling web. They had been so busy watching the roads for poison moving toward the king that they had never thought to ask what might be moving toward the *mountain*.

Below, in the courtyard, the panic was organizing itself into something worse than panic: orders. Riders were being saddled. The king's banner was coming out of the armory. And cutting through the crowd with a tray held level despite everything—threading between armored men like a boat through reeds—came a figure Quentin would have known by silhouette alone, by the set of her shoulders, by the way she moved through chaos the way she moved through her kitchen: unhurried, inevitable, feeding the world regardless.

Wren.

She was handing bread to the guards on the wall stair. Of course she was. The sky had fallen and Wren had opened her ovens, because that was what Wren did—she fed people, all people, the orphaned scullery boys and the disgraced captains and the strange quiet taster nobody invited anywhere, and she had done it for twenty years without ever asking why the castle's accounts paid her kitchen a stipend that predated her own arrival, why her name had been in the household rolls since before she could walk.

Quentin's stomach turned over—not from poison, for once, but from a thought forming at the bottom of it, in the dark where thoughts go that we refuse to finish.

*Paid.* The word surfaced from some ledger they'd glimpsed years ago and never let themselves reread. An old entry, an odd entry, in the founding accounts: a disbursement dated decades back, marked not in crowns but in a currency the clerks had crossed out and rewritten twice.

They had assumed it was an error. They had built nine years of invisibility on the assumption that the dangerous things were all outside the walls.

The red glow in the notch of Warden's Rest pulsed, slow and patient, like something breathing.

Quentin turned from the wall and went down into the crowd, toward the smell of fresh bread, walking as calmly as they had ever walked in their life while every instinct they owned screamed at them to run—to run down the salt road, over the passes, away from whatever had just woken beneath the mountain and away from the terrible arithmetic assembling itself in their mind.

But Wren was in the courtyard. And Quentin had spent nine years learning exactly one lesson perfectly: you can hide from anything except the thing you love.

"Wren," they said, and their voice cracked on the second syllable, and she turned, flour-dusted and frowning, holding out a roll as if bread could answer the end of the world.

"You look like you've seen a ghost," she said.

"Worse," Quentin said. "I've seen a debt come due."
