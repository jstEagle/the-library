## 5. What the King Knew

The summons came before the bells had finished their third round.

Quentin was still in the tasting chamber, a cup of the king's morning wine balanced on their tongue, cataloguing its finish—blackberry, iron, and beneath it the faint almond ghost of bitter-leaf, harmless in this dilution, a flavor they had learned to read the way other people read weather—when the knock came. Two knocks, a pause, one more. The palace guard's signal for *come now, quietly, without your coat*.

They swallowed. Set down the cup. Wiped their mouth with the linen cloth embroidered with the crown's stag, the cloth that had been theirs for nine years, worn soft as an old apology.

"His Majesty requests the taster," said the guard through the door, and then, because the night had been what it had been, added: "He requests you *now*, Lockridge."

Quentin looked at themselves in the polished silver mirror bolted to the wall—the mirror every taster was required to keep, so that the court could see, afterward, exactly how a poisoned mouth looked. Grey eyes. A face built to be forgotten, which was the point of it, which had been the work of nine years. They arranged that face into its usual expression: attentive, slightly dull, a servant who had seen nothing and understood less.

Then they went up through the waking castle, past corridors where servants ran with candles and courtiers clutched each other in doorways, all of them asking the same question in different registers of panic: *where did the stars go?*

Quentin knew where the stars had gone. Or rather, Quentin knew what the stars had been—not stars at all, not over Ironhollow, but the light of the border wards showing through from the far side, a thousand small anchors burning in the dark like lamps hung in windows by someone who loved you enough to leave them lit. Last night those lamps had gone out, one by one, west to east, like a house being shuttered room by room by someone moving through it in the dark.

Only one bloodline anchored those wards. And that bloodline was supposed to be ash, three generations cold, a name scrubbed from every ledger in the kingdom.

*So whose blood went out with the lights?*

The thought sat in Quentin's chest like a swallowed stone as they climbed the stair to the king's audience chamber, and they were careful, so careful, to keep their breathing even around it.

* * *

King Aldous IV received them not on his throne but at the long map table in the war room below it, which was worse. Thrones were theater; Quentin knew how to be invisible in front of theater. The map table was where Aldous did his real thinking, and he had cleared everything off it except a single object: a shard of grey stone the length of a forearm, veined with something that caught the candlelight and refused to give it back.

A ward-stone. Broken. Quentin had walked past a hundred of them in the Thornfell passes without ever touching one, because you didn't touch ward-stones, the way you didn't touch a sleeping dog or a debt.

"Lockridge." The king didn't look up. "Your tongue is the finest instrument in my kingdom. So use it. Tell me what you taste in this room."

Quentin let their eyes move across the chamber slowly, the way a dull man would—candles, guards, the window slit showing a sky still wrong, still starless, a black ceiling pressing down on the world. And then, because the king had asked and the king did not ask twice:

"Damp stone, Majesty. Old smoke. Fear."

"Fear." Aldous finally raised his head. He was not an old man, but last night had aged him the way a flood ages a bridge. "Whose?"

"Everyone's, sire. The sky went out. People fear what they can't explain."

"And you? What do *you* fear?"

Here it was, then. The first blade, offered flat side first, the way Aldous always began. Quentin let a beat pass—a servant's beat, the pause of a man assembling honest words—and said, "That whatever broke the wards will come down the mountain before I'm old enough to retire."

It was a good answer. It was true, even, in the way that mattered. But Aldous only studied them, and Quentin felt the study like a hand moving along shelves, checking for the book that wasn't where it should be.

"You've served me nine years," the king said. "You've taken enough poison into your body to kill a garrison. You know things about my borders—" a thin pause, "—that no taster should know. Smuggling routes. Which lords water their loyalty. Which roads stay open in winter." He turned the broken ward-stone over with one finger, gently, the way a man touches a wound to see if it still hurts. "You are the most informed insignificant person in my court, Lockridge. So when I tell you that three days ago, a quarryman in the eastern passes reported men buying old ward-stones out of field walls and grave markers—paying silver for *rocks*—and that two nights ago the wards died—I want you to taste this room again. Properly this time."

Quentin tasted it properly.

And there it was, under the damp and the smoke and the king's fear: a smell that did not belong in any room above ground. Cold mineral. Deep wet granite. The particular breath of places the sun had never reached—stone from under the mountain, carried up, still exhaling its long underground night.

It was coming from the woman standing in the shadow of the doorway behind the king's chair.

Quentin had noticed her, of course. Noticing was the job. Lady Vessane—new these four months, arrived with letters of introduction from a duchy Quentin couldn't find on any map worth trusting—stood with her hands folded and her face arranged into serene attention, grey silk, grey eyes, hair pinned in a style forty years out of fashion. She smelled of deep-mountain stone the way a fishmonger smells of fish: completely, unapologetically, as though she had never considered that anyone might mind.

No one else in the room seemed to notice. That was the frightening part. The guards stood beside her and breathed her and smelled nothing but a lady's perfume.

*She came up from below,* Quentin thought, and the thought arrived with the certainty of a poison identified. *Or she has never left.*

"I taste old stone, Majesty," Quentin said aloud, blandly. "Older than the castle."

"Do you." The king glanced back at Vessane, and here was the thing Quentin filed away and would carry for the rest of their life: Aldous did not look at her the way a man looks at his advisor. He looked at her the way a man looks at the thing sitting on his chest while he sleeps. "Lady Vessane assures me the wards' failure is a natural decay. She tells me the original binding was always meant to expire—that we have simply lived past our warranty." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "She is very persuasive. She has persuaded me of many things I did not wish to be persuaded of."

"Sire?"

"But she cannot tell me what the wards were *anchored* to. My grandfather's grandfather burned the records—or so the story goes. The bloodline that made them was extinguished, root and branch, for treasons too old to name." Aldous set the ward-stone down with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a lock turning. "Extinguished. And yet I find myself wondering, Lockridge, in the sleepless hours—what does a fire need, to burn again?"

*A spark,* Quentin thought. *A survivor. Someone who learned to make themselves small and flavorless and forgettable, who spent nine years swallowing poison so no one would ever look twice at their blood.*

What they said was: "I wouldn't know, Majesty. I'm a taster. Fire is the cook's department."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, faintly, the alarm bells rang their slow uneasy round, still not convinced the emergency was over. Vessane's grey eyes rested on Quentin with an expression of perfect, patient blankness—the blankness of a ledger page waiting for ink.

"The kitchens," she said, and her voice was pleasant and dry and slightly echoing, like wind in a corridor, like a voice accustomed to very large empty spaces. "Yes. I understand the kitchen staff are devoted to you. The cook especially. Wren, isn't it? Such a cheerful woman. She came to us as a child, I believe. An orphan. No family, no history, no one at all to inquire after her." A small smile. "How convenient the crown's foundlings are. So unencumbered."

Every hair on Quentin's body stood up and pointed at her.

There was no reason for it. Nothing she had said was a threat, on its face. It was small talk, advisor's talk, the sort of thing said to fill silence. But underneath the words Quentin's trained tongue tasted what it had tasted in the wine a hundred times before: the almond ghost. Bitter-leaf. Something sweet with death folded into the center of it, invisible unless you had spent nine years learning to find death in sweetness.

She knew about Wren. She had said Wren's name in front of the king as casually as a hand setting a piece on a board.

"She makes excellent bread," Quentin said. Their voice came out level. Nine years of practice, level. "Majesty, if there's nothing further—"

"One thing more." Aldous rose, and up close, in the candlelight, Quentin saw that his hands were shaking, just slightly, and that he was gripping the table's edge to hide it. "Last night, when the alarms sounded, you did not run to the windows with everyone else. The guard captain noted it. You went instead toward the kitchens." He let that sit. "Why, Lockridge?"

Because the person I love most in this world sleeps above the ovens, and if the sky is dying I will spend its last minutes standing between her and it. Because I have spent nine years being no one so that no one would ever learn what my blood can do, and last night I understood, all at once, that the hiding was never protection—it was just starvation with better manners.

"To check the ovens, sire," Quentin said. "Fires left banked overnight are how castles burn."

Another silence. Longer. Then Aldous laughed—one short, exhausted bark, without any humor in it whatsoever.

"Ovens. Yes." He waved a hand, suddenly old and tired and done. "Go. Taste things. Stay alive. It's the one duty you've never failed at."

Quentin bowed—the full bow, forehead nearly to the floor, the bow of a man with nothing to hide—and turned for the door.

"Master Lockridge." Vessane's voice stopped them at the threshold, light as dust. "Do give my regards to the kitchen. I do so love the smell of bread."

Quentin did not turn around. They inclined their head, the smallest acknowledgment a body could make and still be called courtesy, and walked out through the antechamber, down the stairs, past the guards who parted for them without seeing them, exactly as designed.

Their hands did not start shaking until they reached the bottom of the stairwell, in the dark, where no one could watch a taster come apart.

* * *

They stood in the cold passage below the war room and made themselves breathe, the way they'd been taught—four counts in, seven held, eight out—the way you breathe through a mild toxin while your body decides whether today is the day it stops forgiving you.

Nine years. Nine years of being furniture, of being a function, of cultivating a personality so bland that even the king, a man who noticed everything, had to strain to see anything behind it. And it had worked. It had worked right up until the moment a woman smelling of the mountain's roots had said *Wren* in that pleasant echoing voice, and Quentin had understood, with the sick lurch of a floor going out, that invisibility had a price tag after all, and the price was this: when the knives finally came out, there would be no one who knew to stand in front of you.

Worse. There would be no one who knew to stand in front of *her*.

Vessane had named Wren the way a collector names a piece she intends to acquire. Quentin turned the moment over and over, looking for another reading, and found none. Four months in the court. Letters from a duchy that didn't exist. Stone-smell that no one else could scent. Persuasions the king resented and submitted to anyway. And now the wards dead, and a broken anchor-stone on the map table, and questions about fires needing sparks—

The arithmetic finished itself, ugly and clean.

*She knows the wards fell because she knows what they were built on. And she is interested in the kitchen.*

Quentin pushed off the wall. There was no time to be afraid; fear was a luxury item and they'd never carried credit. There was a ledger in the kitchen steward's office—old ledgers, decades of them, because the castle kept accounts of everything, flour and salt and wages and, in the oldest books, in a hand no one living could identify, entries that Quentin had flipped past a hundred times without reading closely.

*Foundlings. Arrivals. Payments rendered.*

If Vessane knew Wren's history—if Wren's history was anything other than orphan-luck—then somewhere in those ledgers was a line that said so. Quentin had spent nine years reading poisons in aftertastes. They could read a lie in a ledger.

They took the servants' stairs two at a time, past the laundry, past the buttery, into the warm yeast-and-iron breath of the kitchen corridors, where the morning's baking was already underway and the world, for one more hour, still pretended to be ordinary.

Wren was at the big table with her sleeves rolled, flour to her elbows, laughing at something the scullery boy had said, and for a single suspended moment Quentin stood in the doorway and simply looked at her—the way you look at a lit window from a cold road—and understood that whatever was written in those ledgers, whatever bargain had been struck before either of them was born, they were already too late to prevent it.

They were only, possibly, in time to answer it.

"Wren," Quentin said, and heard their own voice come out wrong, stripped of all its careful blandness, and saw her smile fade as she read it. "I need to see the steward's ledgers. The old ones."

She wiped her hands. She didn't ask why. That was Wren all over—she fed people first and asked questions after, and she was already reaching for her shawl, already saying over her shoulder, "Mind the loaves, Tam, they're due at noon"—as if walking into whatever came next together were the most natural thing in the world.

Behind them, faint through the kitchen's high windows, the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

And somewhere beneath the mountain, Quentin was certain, something had begun to count.
