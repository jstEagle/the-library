## 6. A Soul on Ledger

The kitchen ledger lived in the bottom drawer of the spice cabinet, beneath the cinnamon that had gone to brick and the saffron Wren guarded like a dragon's hoard. It was a fat, cracked thing bound in grey leather, its spine stitched twice because the castle's bookkeeping had outlived three binders and one small fire. Every cook before her had kept it the same way: wages, deliveries, debts, dismissals. Forty years of names in a dozen different hands.

Wren went looking for it on a Tuesday, of all ordinary days. The stars had been gone for six nights, and the castle above her ovens had gone strange with it—quieter, jumpier, full of courtiers who flinched at their own shadows and lords who suddenly remembered piety. But kitchens were kitchens. Bread still needed proving, the morning porridge still needed stirring, and the stores still needed counting, because fear made people hungry and hungry people did not wait politely in lines.

She was reconciling the flour accounts when she found the discrepancy.

It was small. That was what caught her, later—how small it was. A single line in the winter entries from thirty-one years ago, written in a hand so fine and careful it looked like it had been engraved rather than inked:

*One soul, female, aged six. Received in good condition. Paid in full.*

Below it, in the same engraver's hand: *Wren.*

She stared at it for a long time. Long enough that the proving dough over by the warm wall began to slump and spread, long enough that Tam, the scullery boy, asked her twice if she wanted the second oven lit. She said yes to the second question and no answer at all to the first, because her mind had gone somewhere cold and quiet, down past the warmth of her kitchen into a place where words like *received in good condition* were written about children by people who signed their work.

*Paid in full.* Paid to whom? Paid by whom? She had arrived at Ironhollow Castle at six years old, carried up the mountain road in a grain cart by a woman who was not her mother and who never came back for her. She remembered that much—the smell of the cart, the woman's rough hands lifting her down, the way the gates had swallowed her whole. She had always assumed plague. Famine. Some village tragedy that left orphans scattered like seed. Half the castle staff had stories like hers; it was how the kitchens filled themselves, generation after generation, with children nobody would come looking for.

But nobody had ever *paid* for her. Nobody wrote *in good condition* about a famine orphan.

She turned the page backward. The entry before hers was another name, a boy, marked the same way. The page after, another. Eleven entries across nine years, each in that same meticulous script, each ending in *paid in full*. Then the entries stopped, and the ledger returned to flour and tallow and the price of eels, as if nothing had happened, as if eleven children had passed through some door in the middle of an accounting book and vanished into a column of figures.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Thirty-one years of feeding people had taught her hands to be steady when everything else shook, and they held the ledger open now the way they'd hold a knife—firmly, without drama, ready for whatever came next.

"Tam," she said, and her voice came out level. "Watch the bread."

* * *

She found Quentin where she knew she would find him, because Quentin Lockridge was the most predictable unpredictable man in the castle. Every evening after the king's supper was tasted and survived, he came down to the kitchen annex and sat on the same stool by the hearth, close enough to the warmth but never quite in it, and drank one cup of Wren's barley tea while the castle slept around them. Nine years of that stool. Nine years of him arriving with poison-ghosts on his tongue and leaving with something gentler there instead.

He was already sitting when she came through the archway, ledger under her arm like a shield or a weapon—she hadn't decided which. He looked up, and she watched his face do something complicated: the flicker of welcome, the quick cataloguing glance he gave everyone (was this safe, was this poisoned, was this a trap), and then—when he saw what she carried—a stillness so complete it was almost loud.

"You found it," he said quietly.

Not *found what*. Not *what's that*. He knew. Her heart did something painful and strange, half grief and half fury, because he had known this whole time. All those years of tea and extra rolls slipped from the kitchen tray, all those evenings of comfortable silence—he had known what was written in her name in a dead woman's careful hand, and he had let her go on humming lullabies over soup pots without ever telling her she had been bought.

"Sit down, Quentin." She set the ledger on the flour-dusted table between them, open to the page. "And you're going to talk until I tell you to stop."

He looked at the entry for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was stripped of all its usual dryness, laid bare down to something young and frightened underneath. "I didn't know what it meant. Not at first. I want you to understand that—I was nineteen, I'd just been made taster, and I was told to memorize certain ledgers as part of my training. Court records. I thought it was about supply chains." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "I thought everything was about supply chains, back then."

"And now?"

"Now I know exactly what it means." He turned the page toward himself, and his finger hovered over the word *soul* without touching it, the way he touched nothing in this castle without first considering whether it might kill him. "Six nights ago the stars went out. You were here. You heard the bells."

"I heard the bells."

"The border wards died with them. The wards that keep the Thornfell passes sealed against what lives on the other side of the mountains—and against what lives *under* them." His eyes came up to hers, and she saw that he was terrified, and that he had been terrified for years, and that he had gotten very good at cooking terror down into something that looked like competence. "Those wards are old, Wren. Older than the kingdom. And they were never anchored in stone or prayer or any of the things the priests claim. They were anchored in souls. Living ones. Bought and bound, one per generation, and the crown has been paying for them for centuries through channels nobody audits."

The kitchen was very warm and very quiet. Somewhere above them, a shutter banged in the wind.

"Say it," Wren said. "Say the rest."

"Wren." He said her name carefully, like a word in a language he was afraid of mispronouncing. "Your name is in that ledger because you are the anchor. You have been the anchor since you were six years old. Your soul—the part of you that hums, that remembers songs you never learned, that makes this kitchen feel like the only warm room in the world—that part doesn't entirely belong to you anymore. It belongs to the wards. It was sold the day you came up the mountain, and the payment cleared, and everyone involved signed their names in a book and went back to their suppers."

She had expected, in the walk down from the spice cabinet, to feel many things. Rage, certainly. Betrayal, certainly. What she felt instead, standing in her own kitchen with thirty-one years of her life laid open on a flour table, was a terrible clarifying calm—as if someone had finally opened a window in a room she'd been suffocating in without knowing it.

"That lullaby," she said. "The one I hum. The one I've never been able to remember learning."

Quentin went white. Actually white, the blood leaving his face in real time, and she understood that she had just guessed something true, something he knew, something worse than everything else he'd said.

"It's the contract," he whispered. "Isn't it. You sing it every day. Over the bread, over the pots, over anyone sick or sad who comes through your kitchen. And every time you sing it—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Every time, the seal holds a little tighter. They built it into you, Wren. They put the key inside your own mouth and let you use it your whole life without knowing what it unlocked."

Wren thought about all the bread she had blessed. All the orphans she had fed, the guards, the grieving, the homesick taster on his stool by the hearth. Thought about how the kitchen had always felt like hers—not assigned, not borrowed, but *hers*, down to the stones—and understood, with a lurch, that even that might have been designed. A contented anchor holds better than a miserable one. Of course they'd let her have a kitchen. Of course they'd let her love it.

"How long have you known?" she asked. "That it was me."

"Four years." He didn't look away, and she gave him that much credit—nine years of tasting poison had taught him to face ugly things straight on. "Four years ago I traced the ward payments back through the old chancery records. I found eleven names. I found yours. And I found out what happened to the other ten."

"Tell me."

"They faded." His voice cracked on the word. "Slowly. Over decades. They got quieter, and forgetful, and then one day they'd stop mid-sentence and look at you like they were trying to remember where they'd put something important, and then they'd smile and keep stirring the pot, and there'd be less and less behind their eyes every year until—" He stopped again. Pressed his knuckles to his mouth. "Cook Marta. Who taught me the difference between arsenic and hemlock by smell alone. She was number four in the ledger. I used to think she just got old."

Wren sat down. Not because her legs gave out—she refused to let them give out—but because she wanted to be level with him for what came next, and level meant seated, and seated meant her hands were free.

"So the crown has been eating us," she said. "A spoonful at a time, over thirty years, and calling it border security."

"Yes."

"And you've known for four years, and you stayed, and you tasted their poisons, and you drank my tea every night, and you never once said a word."

"Yes." His jaw tightened. "Because the moment I said a word, you'd have started asking questions, and questions get noticed in this castle, and noticed people get added to ledgers. Because the only thing keeping you alive and *you* was that nobody—including you—knew what you were worth. My silence was the only protection I had to give you. I know how that sounds. I've had four years to find a version of it that sounds better, and there isn't one."

"No," Wren agreed. "There isn't."

She looked at him across the table—at this thin, careful, impossible man who had spent nine years swallowing death in measured doses so that he could stay near the machinery of the court, and four of those years carrying her death warrant around behind his eyes. She was furious with him. She was furious with the fine-handed clerk and whoever had paid for her and the whole rotten edifice of a kingdom that balanced its borders on the souls of children. But underneath the fury, in the place where she kept the things that were actually true, she recognized something: Quentin had not betrayed her. Quentin had stood guard. Badly, silently, at enormous cost to himself—but he had stood.

"You should have told me," she said. "Even if it killed us both. You should have let me choose."

"I know." He said it simply, no defense left. "That's the worst of it. I know."

The wind rattled the shutters again, harder now, and beneath the wind—if you listened the way cooks listen, with your spine—there was something else. A low vibration coming up through the flagstones, faint and rhythmic, like a heartbeat in the bones of the mountain. Six days it had been there, ever since the stars went out. The other servants said it was settling stone. Wren had spent her life listening to buildings breathe, and she knew settling stone when she felt it, and this wasn't it.

Something under the mountain was waking up. Something had been waiting for the wards to weaken, and the wards were weakening because—

Because of her. Because the anchor was fraying, and the fraying thing was her, and every day she spent not knowing was a day the seal thinned.

She closed the ledger. Gently. As if it were a sleeping child.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "You're going to tell me everything else. All of it—who sold me, who signed, where the payments went, what happens when the anchor fails completely. You're going to tell me tonight, over tea, and tomorrow we start figuring out how to unmake a bargain that thirty-one years of kings thought was permanent." She leaned forward, and for the first time since she'd opened the drawer that morning, she smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "Because I'll tell you what I know, Quentin Lockridge, taster to the crown. I have fed this castle for thirty-one years. I have fed the guards who protect it and the servants who run it and the orphans who'll inherit whatever mess the nobles leave behind. And I am not going to sit in my kitchen and quietly fade like Cook Marta while some under-mountain accountant collects on me in installments."

Quentin stared at her. And then—slowly, like ice going out of a river—he began to grin, tired and fierce and a little bit awed.

"There's a problem with that plan," he said. "Several problems. Armed, well-funded, well-buried problems."

"Good," said Wren. "I've fed armed problems before. They eat the same as anyone."

She rose, tied her apron back on, and pulled down the big kettle—the one she used when the whole household needed feeding, the one that meant trouble or celebration, and usually both. Behind her, Quentin slid off his stool, and she heard him say, softly, almost to himself:

"We'll need the smugglers' roads. And there's a warden I have to find—one they disgraced for asking exactly the right questions."

The kettle began to heat. Above them, far above, the starless sky pressed down on the mountain like a lid, and beneath the flagstones the slow heartbeat went on—closer now, or maybe just more impatient.

Wren measured out the barley by feel, humming under her breath without thinking, and stopped herself mid-note.

For the first time in thirty-one years, she was afraid of her own song.
