## 7. Down the Smuggler's Road

The city gates had not yet opened for the morning carts when Quentin slipped through the fish-gate with Wren's hand locked in theirs, and by the time the first bell of dawn rang out over Ironhollow, they were already three miles into the foothills and running on fear.

Fear, Quentin had learned over nine years of tasting death in small measured sips, was a flavor like any other. It sat on the back of the tongue, mineral and cold, like water from a well dug too deep. It could be swallowed. It could even be catalogued. What it could not be was ignored, because the moment you stopped paying attention to fear was the moment it killed you.

So Quentin paid attention.

They paid attention to the way Wren's breathing had gone ragged an hour ago and hadn't steadied since—she was tough as a bread oven, but she'd spent her whole life in kitchens, not on mountain paths, and her boots were made for flagstones, not scree. They paid attention to the light behind them, where the city still glowed against the dark sky like a coal refusing to die, and above it, that terrible blankness where the stars should have been. They paid attention, most of all, to the road itself: the switchbacks half-swallowed by bracken, the cairns stacked by no shepherd's hand, the particular lean of a pine that meant someone had once tied a rope to it and hauled something heavy up the slope in the dark.

Smuggler's country. Quentin's country, though they had never been allowed to say so aloud.

"Tell me again," Wren said, between breaths, "why we're climbing *toward* the mountain instead of away from it."

"Because away is south, and south is the lowlands, and the lowlands are open ground." Quentin pulled her up over a shelf of rock, feeling the tremor in her arm. "Open ground means riders. Riders mean lanterns."

"You keep saying that like I'm supposed to know what it means."

Quentin hesitated. The truth was ugly and new and they were still learning its shape themselves. "Last night, when the wards fell—I saw them from the wall. Not just the sky going black. There were lights moving in the passes. Lanterns that gave off no light. They burned, but nothing around them brightened. Like holes cut in the dark, walking."

Wren stared at them. In the grey pre-dawn her face was pale, flour-and-fear pale, and there was a smudge of ash along her jaw she didn't know about. "That's not possible."

"No," Quentin agreed. "And yet."

* * *

Brannoc found them at midmorning, or perhaps Brannoc had always been there and simply decided to be visible.

He stepped out from behind a leaning slab of granite as if the mountain had exhaled him—a big man built like a dray horse, grey-streaked beard braided with bits of copper wire, wearing a coat that had been patched so many times the original cloth existed only as rumor. He carried no visible weapon, which in Thornfell meant he carried several.

"Lockridge." His voice was gravel rolled in honey. "You look like shit."

"I've been told I never look anything else."

"That was before." His eyes went past Quentin to Wren, and something in his weathered face shifted—not softening exactly, but recalibrating, the way a man recalibrates when he realizes the cargo is more fragile than advertised. "This her, then? The one from the ledger?"

Wren stiffened. "You know about the ledger?"

"Everyone who moves goods through these mountains knows about the ledgers." Brannoc spat, with ceremony. "We just didn't know what was being written in 'em till this week. Come on. Ilex's waiting at the Salt Gate, and we're burning daylight we can't afford."

"The Salt Gate's watched," Quentin said. "It's been watched since before I was born. It's the most obvious route through the whole range."

"It is." Brannoc grinned, and the grin showed teeth like old tombstones. "Which is why nobody watches it anymore. Obvious is a kind of armor, Lockridge. You of all people should know that. You wore it for nine years."

Quentin had no answer to that, so they walked.

* * *

The Salt Gate was not a gate at all. It was a seam in the mountain where two faces of limestone leaned together like conspirators, leaving a passage barely wide enough for a loaded mule, its walls streaked white with mineral bloom that glittered dully even under the starless sky. Someone had carved steps into the floor of it centuries ago, worn now to shallow suggestions. The air inside smelled of stone and old salt and, faintly, of something else—something sweetish and wrong, like flowers left too long in a closed room.

Ilex was waiting at the far end, and Quentin's first thought was that they had expected someone older.

She looked scarcely more than thirty, though the smugglers' grapevine insisted she'd been running the Thornfell routes since before the current king's coronation. She was small and sharp-featured, with close-cropped black hair going silver at the temples and eyes the flat grey of a blade that has been sharpened past polish into something almost gentle. She sat on a boulder mending a pack-strap, and she did not look up until both newcomers had fully emerged from the seam.

Then she looked, and Quentin felt the look go through them like a probe through a lock.

"So," Ilex said. "The taster."

"You know me?"

"I know everyone who matters to my roads." She tied off her knot and stood. "Nine years you've been feeding the crown's routes to us through dead drops and coded grain manifests, and never once asked for a coin. Do you know how much that unsettled my people? A gift with no hook in it. We spent two years convinced you were setting a trap."

"And now?"

"Now I think you were buying something." Her gaze flicked to Wren, then back. "I just didn't know what. Until last night, when every ward-stone from here to the coast went dark, and certain parties started offering very good money for guides who knew the deep passes." She shouldered her pack. "Certain parties being Lady Vessane's people. Lanterns and no light. You've seen them?"

"From a distance."

"You'll see them closer. They don't tire, they don't eat, and they don't stop at cliff edges—they walk straight down and keep coming. So we won't be taking any route a sane person would take." She smiled without warmth. "Fortunately, sanity was never our trade."

* * *

They climbed for the rest of the day.

Not walked—climbed, up goat-tracks that weren't tracks at all, through a hanging valley where the pines grew sideways out of bare rock, along a ridge so narrow that Quentin made Wren clip herself to the guide-rope with hands that shook only a little. Brannoc carried most of their supplies without appearing to notice the weight. Ilex moved ahead like a thought, reading the mountain the way Quentin read aftertastes—instantly, totally, with a lifetime of pattern beneath every judgment.

And Wren, to Quentin's quiet astonishment, kept up.

She cursed. She complained. At one point she sat down on a ledge and announced with great dignity that she was going to die here and wanted it noted that the last thing she'd eaten was a heel of stale bread, which was an insult to her life's work. But she kept up. When the path required trust, she trusted. When it required courage, she reached down somewhere and found it, the way she reached down for everything—extra rolls for orphans, second helpings for homesick guards, a warm kitchen conjured out of cold stone through sheer refusal to let the world be colder than she allowed.

By late afternoon, watching her haul herself over a final shoulder of rock with gritted teeth and muttered imprecations, Quentin understood something they should have understood years ago: that love was not the soft thing they'd been hoarding in silence all this time. Love was this. Love was keeping up. Love was following someone into a mountain because they asked, and asking someone into a mountain because you had to.

The thought terrified them more than the lanterns did.

* * *

They made the cliff road at dusk—or rather, the cliff road made itself known to them, because it had no business existing at all.

It ran along the eastern face of Mount Ceren itself, a shelf of dressed stone maybe eight feet wide, jutting from the sheer rock with nothing below it but four hundred feet of air and then a talus field of broken bones of the mountain. It had been built long ago—Quentin could see the tool-marks, fine and precise, in a style no living mason used—and it ran arrow-straight for nearly a mile before vanishing around the mountain's flank.

"The Old King's Road," Brannoc said, seeing Quentin's expression. "Aye. Everyone thinks it's a legend. Legends are useful that way."

"Why would anyone build a road here?"

"Same reason anyone builds anything under this mountain." Ilex had gone still, her head cocked, listening to something Quentin couldn't hear. "To reach what's underneath. Move. We cross before full dark, and we don't stop on the shelf for anything. Anything. Understood?"

They crossed.

The wind came up the face in long slow gusts that smelled of snow and that same sweet wrongness Quentin had caught at the Salt Gate, stronger now, and beneath their feet the road hummed—very faintly, more felt than heard, like the memory of a bell. Wren stumbled once, caught herself, and said, "Do you feel that? It's like—" She pressed her palm flat to the stone. "Like a heartbeat. Slow one."

"Don't touch it," Ilex said sharply.

But Quentin had already knelt, already laid their own hand beside Wren's, and what they felt made the hair rise along their arms. The stone wasn't humming. It was *listening*. The whole road, the whole mountain-face, was holding its breath around them the way a court holds its breath around a king—and Quentin knew that quality of attention intimately, had lived inside it for nine years, and had never once expected to find it in bedrock.

It knows bloodline, they thought, and did not say. It knows whose hand is on it.

They stood and said nothing, and the road let them pass.

* * *

The ambush came at the narrowest point, where the shelf pinched to six feet between the mountain-wall and the drop, and it came not with sound but with the absence of it—the wind dying all at once, the way it dies before an avalanche.

Ilex froze mid-stride. Brannoc's hand closed on Wren's collar and yanked her backward off the path's center line, and in the same instant the darkness ahead of them opened.

Three lanterns. Three holes in the night, each one a perfect circle of absolute black ringed by a thin corona of nothing, swinging gently as their bearers stepped onto the road. The bearers themselves Quentin could barely see—shapes in travel-cloaks, moving with a smoothness that was worse than any lurching horror, because it was almost human. Almost. Their feet made no sound on the stone. Where their faces should have caught the last grey light, there was only a suggestion of features, like a portrait painted by someone who had been told about faces but never seen one.

"Hollowed servants," Ilex breathed. "Out of the under-court. On the surface. That shouldn't—" She cut herself off, because the servants raised their lanterns, and the lanterns began to burn brighter, which is to say the circles of black began to widen, and the edges of the world near them began to fray.

What happened next, Quentin would spend the rest of their life failing to describe properly.

Brannoc roared—actually roared, like something out of the old stories—and charged, and from inside his shapeless coat came a hatchet and a length of chain and a violence so total it seemed to bend the air. Ilex shoved Wren and Quentin toward the pinch-point and turned to follow Brannoc, drawing a knife that gleamed with a greasy, deliberate unpolish. And Quentin, whose entire martial training consisted of knowing which poison worked fastest and how to fake a seizure convincingly, grabbed Wren's wrist and ran.

Behind them: the crack of the chain, a sound like ice shearing off a roof, Brannoc laughing in a way that had no joy anywhere in it.

At the pinch-point, the lead servant was simply *there*, having crossed forty feet of road in the time it took Quentin to blink, and its lantern swung up, and the circle of black reached for them—

—and Wren stepped in front of Quentin.

Later, Quentin would try to reconstruct it and fail. But in the moment, they saw clearly: Wren, cook's apron still on under her traveling cloak, planting herself between the hollowed thing and the person she loved, and raising one flour-scarred hand as if to ward off a hot pan lid—and the lantern-light, the devouring dark, *flinched*. Recoiled. The servant staggered as if struck, its blank face tilting, and for one heartbeat the sweet wrongness in the air curdled into something like confusion.

It knows her, Quentin thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. Whatever she is to the bargain—it knows her.

Then Brannoc's chain took the servant across the shoulders and knocked it off the shelf entirely, and it fell without screaming, its lantern shrinking away below them into the dark like a sinking star in reverse, and Ilex was shouting *move, move, MOVE*, and they moved.

They came off the Old King's Road at a place where a fallen slab formed a bridge to a side ravine, and Ilex kicked the bridge's keystone loose behind them with a precision that suggested she'd planned the retreat before she'd planned the advance. Stone thundered down into the ravine. The remaining two lanterns appeared at the edge of the broken gap and stopped there, swaying, patient as arithmetic.

"They'll go around," Ilex said. She was bleeding from a gash along her forearm and seemed to regard this as a personal affront. "Takes them the night. We have until morning, and not one hour more." She turned, and in the starless dark her grey eyes found Quentin's with terrible directness. "Now you tell me the rest of it, taster. All of it. Because those things looked at your cook like she was the prize in the vault, and I have run contraband through these mountains for twenty years, and I know contraband when I see it."

Wren laughed—a short, cracked, utterly fearless sound. "Contraband. That's one word for it."

"Wren—" Quentin started.

"No." She faced them both, ash-smudged, exhausted, magnificent. "No more half of it, Quentin. You heard what she said. You've had two days of telling me things in pieces, and every piece is worse than the last, and I am done being protected. If we're doing this—if we're really walking into whatever's under that mountain—then everyone on this road knows everything, starting now."

The wind moved through the ravine, carrying the sweet wrongness and, fainter, from far below and far within, that slow listening hum of stone.

Quentin looked at Wren—at the woman who had fed a castle for twenty years, who had just flinched a nightmare off a cliff-road with an empty hand, who had found her own name in a ledger marked *paid* and chosen fury over despair—and felt the last of the old life's silence come loose in their chest like a rotten tooth.

"All right," they said. "All of it. But we walk while I talk."

And somewhere behind them, patient as arithmetic, the lanterns began to move.
