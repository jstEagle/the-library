## 8. The Disgraced Warden

Ilex had carried a lot of things down the smuggler's roads in her time—salt, steel, secrets, once an entire bishop's worth of contraband relics sewn into the lining of a dead man's coat—but she had never carried anything that felt like this. The cook walked beside her with a soul like a struck bell inside her, and every step they took deeper into Thornfell made it ring louder.

She could feel it through the soles of her boots. That was the thing nobody who hadn't worked the high passes understood: the mountain talked. Old ward-stone hummed under the trail like a plucked string that never quite went still, and a good smuggler learned to read that hum the way a sailor read weather. For nine years the string had been steady. Dull, even. Tonight it thrummed, off-key and hungry, and every time Wren's bare feet touched bare rock—she'd lost her shoes somewhere on the cliff road and refused to complain about it—the thrum bent toward her like a plant toward light.

Ilex kept her hand near her knife and said nothing about any of it.

Brannoc ranged ahead, a big shape in the dark, his lantern hooded to a coin-slit of amber. Behind them came the taster—Lockridge, though Ilex had known better than to believe that name for years—with the particular silence of someone who had spent their whole life learning how not to be heard. Even exhausted, even bleeding from a graze along the jaw where the lantern-men's crossbow bolt had kissed them, Quentin Lockridge moved like poured water. It was unnerving. It was also, Ilex admitted privately, exactly what she'd have expected of the crown's poison-eater.

"Left fork," she murmured as the trail split around a fallen pine. "The right goes to a toll cave, and the toll is your teeth."

Wren made a small sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a prayer. "You're joking."

"I never joke about teeth." Ilex ducked under a low branch. "Keep to my heels. The stone gets talkative past here, and I'd rather it didn't learn your name."

*Too late for that,* she thought, feeling the hum lean again.

They climbed for another hour through switchbacks stitched with frost, and then the trees opened onto a shelf of granite above a black valley, and there it was: the warden's station at Coldmere, or what was left of it. Once it had been a fortress of pale stone with a beacon tower and a garrison of forty. Now it was a broken tooth against the sky, its tower snapped off halfway, its walls furred with moss, its gate hanging open like a mouth that had given up on words.

Ilex felt the usual knot tighten in her chest. She'd delivered Brannoc's message two nights ago—a single line scratched on slate: *The wards are down and I'm bringing you the reason why.* She'd half expected no answer. Sera Vail answered messages the way avalanches answered footsteps: rarely, and decisively.

Tonight there was a fire burning in the old signal room, small and disciplined, and a silhouette against it that sat the way soldiers sit—spine straight, hands visible, weight balanced for standing up fast.

"That's her," Brannoc rumbled, coming back to meet them. His voice dropped. "Fair warning. She's been in a mood since the stars went out."

"She's been in a mood since the crown stripped her," Ilex said. "The stars just gave her something to be right about."

They crossed the yard. Wren hesitated at the threshold, one hand pressed flat against the doorframe, and Ilex saw her flinch—not at cold, not at fear. At recognition. The way you flinch when a song you half-remember starts playing in a strange room.

Inside, the signal room had been swept clean and arranged with a soldier's economy: bedroll, whetstone, a wall of pinned maps so dense they overlapped like feathers. And at the center of it all, Sera Vail.

She was smaller than her reputation. Grey-shot hair cut short as a hedge, a face weathered down to its load-bearing structure, and eyes the color of the valley at dusk—grey going to dark, missing nothing. She wore no uniform. She wore a plain wool coat with the warden's clasp torn off, and Ilex noticed the threads where it had been ripped away rather than unpinned. Someone had tried to take it gently and Vail had declined the courtesy.

"You brought them," Vail said. Her voice was gravel over iron. "Through the passes. Past the lantern-men." Her gaze moved from Ilex to Brannoc to the taster and stopped, finally, on Wren—and something happened in her face. Something Ilex had never seen there in eleven years of running cargo together. Vail's composure cracked, just at one corner, like ice under a boot.

"What's your name?" Vail asked Wren, and her voice had gone quiet in a way that made the whole room quieter.

"Wren," the cook said. "Just Wren. Kitchen-born. I've cooked for the castle at Ironhollow since I was six."

"Since you were six," Vail repeated. She stood. She crossed the room slowly, the way you approach a spooked animal or a lit fuse, and stopped an arm's length away. "May I?"

Wren glanced at Quentin. Quentin gave a nod so small Ilex nearly missed it. Wren held out her hand.

Vail took it palm-up, turned it over, and pressed her thumb hard against the inside of Wren's wrist, over the pulse. Her lips moved—counting, Ilex realized. Counting heartbeats the way you count the rings of a tree. Then Vail closed her eyes, and her shoulders dropped half an inch, and when she spoke again her voice was rough as a file.

"Nine years," she said. "Nine years I told them. I stood in the great hall at Ironhollow with the ledgers open in front of the whole court, and I told them the border wards weren't fed by ley-line and goodwill. I told them the founding contract named a price, and the price was a person, sold before she could walk. And the Lord Chancellor called me a drunk, and the King's Justice called me a traitor, and they tore the clasp off my coat in front of forty men." She looked down at Wren's wrist, still holding it, gentle now. "And here you are. Alive. Cooking soup."

"I make very good soup," Wren said, and her voice wobbled only a little.

Vail laughed—one dry note, no joy in it—and let go of her hand. "Yes. I imagine you do." She turned to the taster, and the softness shut off like a lamp behind glass. "And you. The crown's tongue. Nine years tasting for the king while the king sat on a stolen anchor. Did you know?"

Quentin didn't flinch. Ilex gave them credit for that; most people did, under those grey eyes. "I knew the wards fell the same night everyone else did. I knew the court beneath the mountain exists, because my family helped build it. I did not know about the bargain until three days ago." A pause, precise as a measured dose. "And I've spent those three days getting her out."

"Getting her out," Vail echoed. "Not getting answers. Not getting leverage." She looked at Wren again. "You understand what you are? What's been done to you?"

"No," Wren said honestly. "That's rather why we climbed a mountain."

* * *

Vail unrolled the oldest of her maps across the trestle table and weighted the corners with a knife, a candlestick, a horseshoe, and a lump of raw ward-stone that made Ilex's teeth ache just to look at it.

"The border wards of Thornfell were raised four hundred years ago by the Lockridge line," Vail said, tapping the mountain range. "Blood-anchored. Living seals. Every generation, a Lockridge renewed the binding freely, and the wards held, and the things on the far side of the dark stayed on the far side of the dark." Her finger moved to the castle. "Two hundred years ago, the family died out—or the court was told they did. The wards began to fail. So the crown brokered a replacement. Not blood freely given. Blood *bought*. An infant, purchased from a mother who was promised her child would live a charmed life, and bound into the founding seal beneath the mountain as a living anchor."

The room was very still. The fire popped.

"They didn't tell the mother that part," Vail said. "They told her the child would be raised in comfort. Which, I suppose, in the way of liars, was true. Comfort. Soup. Orphans fed from your kitchen." Her eyes flicked to Wren. "You've spent your whole life feeding other people because some clerk in a basement decided you'd never be fed yourself. Your soul has been leaking into that seal since before you could talk, drip by drip, keeping the dark out. And last week, somebody started drawing on the account faster than it refills. That's why the stars went out. That's why the wards died. Somebody below is emptying you."

Wren's hand had gone to her own chest, fingers spread, as if checking whether she was still there. "The lullaby," she whispered. "I hum it when the kitchen's quiet. I've always known it. I don't know where from."

"Your mother," Vail said simply. "Contract-songs are taught to the seller. It's how the under-court binds consent that isn't really consent. They need the words spoken over the sale." She straightened. "It will matter later. Right now you need to know the shape of the trap you're standing in."

"And the shape is?" Quentin asked.

"The Steward of the under-court wants the account emptied," Vail said. "Or wants it renegotiated. Either way, the seal fails within a season unless a new anchor is set. Blood-kin of the founder, freely given—that's the only binding strong enough to replace a bought soul. Which is why the lantern-men aren't chasing the cook." Her gaze settled on Quentin, and Ilex watched the taster go very carefully still. "They're chasing the last Lockridge. You've been hiding in plain sight for nine years, Master Taster, and the moment you stepped onto this mountain, everything under it knew your name."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the ruined yard, wind worried at the broken gate.

"So that's it," Wren said quietly. She wasn't looking at Vail. She was looking at Quentin, and her expression was the one Ilex imagined she wore over a pot about to boil over—watchful, warm, and absolutely immovable. "All those years. All those little kindnesses. The extra roll. The seat by the oven. You weren't just being kind."

"I was being kind," Quentin said. "The rest was cowardice wearing kindness's coat. I should have told you."

"Yes," Wren said. "You should have." She let that stand between them for a long breath, then lifted her chin. "But you climbed a mountain instead of running. So we'll deal with the coat later."

Ilex exhaled slowly through her nose. Eleven years of hauling contraband, and she'd never heard anyone survive a conversation with that much honesty in it.

Vail rolled the map closed. "Then hear the rest, because it's the part that got me disgraced twice over. There's a third option. Not replacing the anchor. *Unbinding* the original contract—dissolving it root and branch, freeing every soul the under-court holds in glass. But it has to be done inside the seal chamber, by someone with standing, and it requires the founder's blood, freely given, spoken over by name." She met Quentin's eyes. "Your name. Your real one. Spoken aloud, on record, in front of the court below."

"That's a death sentence," Ilex said flatly. "The moment the under-court knows a living Lockridge exists—"

"It already knows," Vail said. "That ship sailed the night the stars went out. The only question left is whether the Lockridge dies hidden in a ditch, or stands in the deep place and says their name out loud." She picked up the torn warden's clasp from the table, turned it over in her scarred fingers, and set it down again. "I spent nine years being right with no one willing to listen. I'm not going to waste vindication being careful."

Brannoc shifted by the door, wood and iron groaning. "The lantern-men will have found the cliff road ambush site by dawn. They'll follow the stone-hum straight here."

"Then we don't wait for dawn," Vail said. She was already reaching for the map wall, pulling down pins, her hands moving with the crisp economy of a woman who had waited nine years for permission and intended to spend every second of it. "There's a mine-shaft below the old garrison cistern. It drops into the galleries, and the galleries run down to the sealed doors. We go tonight, we go quiet, and we go *down*."

Wren looked at the floor as if she could see through it—all that stone, all that dark, and somewhere at the bottom of it, a glass vessel with her name scratched on the side.

"Down," she agreed softly. Then, to Quentin, with the ghost of her kitchen smile: "Try to keep up. I've fed guards for twenty years. I know exactly how fast people move when they're scared."

Ilex caught Quentin's eye over the guttering fire—two professionals, briefly, acknowledging the same arithmetic. Whatever waited below had already taken one life from each of them. The only question was what they'd trade next.

Above the broken tower, through the rent in the clouds, a single star flickered back to life—then guttered, and died again, like a candle someone couldn't decide about.

Vail saw it too. Her jaw tightened.

"They're deciding," she said. "Move."
