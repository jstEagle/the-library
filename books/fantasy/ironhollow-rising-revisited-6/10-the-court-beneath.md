## 10. The Court Beneath

The last mine-shaft opened like a held breath.

Wren felt the change before she saw it—a warmth rising through the stone, soft as proofing dough, and a light that came from nowhere and everywhere at once. She had spent her whole life in kitchens, where light was always earned: tallow candles guttering in drafts, hearth-fires fed hour by hour, lamps trimmed at dawn. Light was labor. Light was something you made.

This light was not made. It simply *was*, pouring up out of the deep places of the mountain like water finding its level, and it had no smell of smoke, no flicker, no warmth she could lean into. It lit everything and touched nothing.

"Gods above," Brannoc breathed behind her. The big smuggler's voice, usually good for jokes about cheese and customs men, had gone small.

"Not above," Ilex said grimly. "That's rather the point."

They stood at the mouth of a gallery so vast that Wren's eyes refused to finish measuring it. Pillars of living rock rose from a floor of polished black stone, each one carved—she squinted, then wished she hadn't—with figures that seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at them. Dancers. Reapers. Mothers holding children. A procession of faces spiraling up toward a ceiling lost in soft radiance, and among those faces, here and there, a crest she now knew better than her own name: a tower with an open door.

*Lockridge.* Quentin's family had walked these halls once. Had carved them, maybe. Had been carved into them.

And between the pillars, on pedestals of glass and silver, burned the lights.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one a globe no bigger than a loaf of bread, each one holding a slow-turning brightness that pulsed—Wren pressed a hand to her sternum, because the rhythm was wrong, or rather it was horribly right—and pulsed again, and again, patient as sleeping hearts.

"They're souls," Sera Vail said quietly. The disgraced warden had not spoken since the last ladder. Her voice had the flatness of a woman describing a wound she'd stopped expecting anyone to believe. "I told you what you'd find. I'll say it once more, plainly, so no one can claim they weren't warned: every light in this room is a person. Or was."

Wren counted without meaning to. It was a cook's habit, counting—loaves, mouths, spoons, days until the harvest fair. She got past four hundred before her throat closed and she stopped.

One of the globes hung nearer than the rest, at the end of a colonnade to their left, and its light was different. Warmer. Golden where the others were white-silver, and it turned in its glass the way butter turns in a warm pan, lazy and rich, and when Wren looked at it something in her chest pulled sideways, like a thread snagged on a nail.

She took a step toward it before she knew she was moving.

Quentin caught her arm. Their fingers were cold—they were always cold, nine years of poison rations would do that—but their grip was sure. "Don't," they said softly. "Not yet. Not until we know the rules of this place."

"The rules," Wren repeated. Her voice sounded strange down here, flattened by all that stone. "You mean the manners."

"I mean exactly that." Quentin's face was pale in the soul-light, their sharp taster's features sharpened further by shadow. "This is a court, Wren. Whatever else it is, whatever it's done—it is a court. Courts have etiquette. Etiquette is armor. We're going to need every scrap we brought."

Behind them, Ilex made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Nine years tasting for the crown and she still talks like a diplomat. You picked the right monster to love, cook."

Wren didn't correct him. She'd stopped correcting people three days ago, somewhere on the cliff road, when Quentin had said *the only person I've ever loved* out loud in the dark with lanterns-that-burned-without-light closing in, and the world had rearranged itself around the words and never quite settled back.

She looked instead at the golden globe. At the thread pulling in her chest.

It pulled *back.*

* * *

They were expected. Of course they were expected. Wren understood this the moment the great doors at the far end of the gallery—doors she had not noticed, doors that were simply part of the wall until they weren't—swung inward without a hand upon them, and music spilled out.

Not instruments. Voices. A choir of something too smooth to be human, singing a melody that Wren knew.

Her feet stopped. Her breath stopped. Somewhere behind her, Sera said her name, but the sound arrived from very far away, because Wren was hearing the lullaby.

*Hush now, hush, the mountain keeps,*
*what the mountain takes, the mountain sleeps—*

She had hummed it her whole life. Over soup and over orphans, over wounded guards and grieving widows, over Quentin asleep in a kitchen chair with flour on their sleeve. She had never known where it came from. She had told herself it came from her mother, the way freckles come from mothers, the way stubbornness does.

But this was the source. This was the well the song had been drawn from, and it was singing itself back to her down here in the dark, and every note fit a groove worn into her before memory began.

"Wren." Quentin's voice, low and urgent. "Wren, look at me."

She looked. Quentin's eyes were the color of strong tea, ordinary and dear, and they anchored her the way nothing in this beautiful terrible place could.

"That song," Quentin said. "Don't sing it. Don't even hum it. Not until we understand what it opens."

"What if it doesn't open anything?" Wren heard herself ask. "What if it just... closes? What if it's the only thing down here that's actually mine?"

Quentin had no answer for that. Wren watched them search for one and find nothing, and loved them a little more for not pretending.

Then the Steward came through the doors, and there was no more time for questions.

* * *

Wren had imagined the ruler of the court beneath the mountain as a monster. She supposed they all had; it was why Sera had insisted on walking first, why Brannoc carried his axe openly despite Ilex's hissed warnings, why Quentin kept one hand inside their coat near the little vials they never explained.

The Steward was none of those things.

She was small. That was the first shock—that she was *small*, barely taller than Wren, a round-shouldered woman in robes the color of deep water, with silver hair braided in a crown and a face like every grandmother in every village Wren had ever cooked for: lined, kind, tired around the eyes in a way that spoke of decades of caring for people who did not thank her.

She looked at Wren, and her whole face changed.

"Oh," the Steward said. Just that. One syllable, cracked down the middle, and then she crossed the black floor with quick uneven steps and took Wren's hands in both of hers, and her palms were warm, warm as fresh bread, warm as the only thing in this lightless country that had any heat at all. "Oh, look at you. Look at you grown. They told me you'd be tall. Your mother was tall. Did you get her hands? Let me see—" She turned Wren's hands over, examining them with a professional's eye, calluses and burn-scars and the crooked left index finger broken by a mangle when Wren was eight. "Yes. Yes, those are baker's hands. Those are *her* hands."

"You knew my mother." Wren's voice came out steady, which surprised her. Inside, something was coming apart with terrifying ease, like a knot that had only been waiting for the right pull.

"I dressed her wounds when she came down the first time. I sat with her the night before the signing. I held her when your father—" The Steward stopped. Closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, and Wren felt the wetness answer in her own. "Child, I have waited thirty years to say I'm sorry, and I am going to say it properly, with witnesses and terms and restitution, because you deserve a real apology and not a grandmother's babbling. But first—"

She drew Wren's hands against her own chest, over her heart, and Wren felt it beating, quick and human and afraid.

"—first, you must be starving. You've come all this way through the cold passes, and I know what my cousin's kitchen serves on the road. Come. Sit. Eat something that isn't smoked and salted within an inch of its life. There is soup. There has *always* been soup waiting for you, every night for thirty years, did you know that? Every night I told the kitchen: keep a bowl warm. In case tonight is the night she comes home."

And God help her, God forgive her, Wren's stomach growled.

* * *

The hall beyond the doors was smaller than the gallery, which somehow made it worse. It was built to human scale deliberately, lovingly: a long table of dark wood, benches softened with cushions, a hearth that burned with a flame that cast no smoke and gave off that same sourceless warmth. The walls were hung with tapestries showing harvests and weddings and children playing, and the table was set for six, and the six settings were arranged around the head of the table with one chair left empty at the Steward's right hand.

Wren's chair. She knew it without being told. The cushion was newer than the others. Recently replaced. Kept ready.

Brannoc hung back by the door with his arms crossed, refusing the bench offered to him. Ilex sat but touched nothing, his warden's eyes cataloguing exits. Sera stood with her back to the wall and her jaw set like a woman attending an execution she'd predicted. And Quentin sat beside Wren, close enough that their knees touched under the table, and ate nothing, and watched the Steward with the expression Wren had seen them wear over the king's wine: total attention, total suspicion, and beneath both, a hunger to be wrong.

The soup was barley and root vegetables with a bone-marrow broth, finished with a thread of wild thyme.

It was the best thing Wren had ever tasted.

She knew food the way Quentin knew poison—by aftertaste, by signature, by the story the flavor told about the hands that made it. And this soup told a story of patience and skill and *care*, of stock simmered for two days, of vegetables cut in a cook's uniform dice, of seasoning adjusted by someone who tasted as they went, the way Wren did, the way her mother must have—

"It's her recipe," the Steward said gently, watching Wren eat. "Your mother's. I wrote it down myself, the last autumn she was here. The kitchen has made it every week since. The cooks who learned it taught the ones who came after, and those taught the next, and not one of them knows whose hands it came from. But yours do, don't they? Some part of you tastes it and says *home*."

"Yes," Wren whispered. Because it did. Because that was the horror of it. Every spoonful said home, home, home, and the home it named was a cell of glass hanging in a lightless gallery four hundred paces away.

Quentin's knee pressed harder against hers.

"Tell me how it works," Wren said. She set down the spoon. Her hands were shaking now; she let them. "The bargain. All of it. You greeted me like a daughter and you feed me my mother's cooking and I want to believe—I *want* to, do you understand? That's the cruelest thing anyone's ever done to me, making me want to believe. So tell me the truth, all of it, and let me decide what I am to you."

The Steward looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked at Quentin, and something older and sadder moved across her face.

"You travel with a Lockridge," she said. "I wondered if I'd live to see it. The tower-and-door, walking back into the mountain." She folded her hands on the table. "Very well. The truth, as you ask. The wards that guard your kingdom are old, child. Older than the crown that claims them. They were raised by blood—the bloodline seated in Ironhollow, the line whose crest your friend carries whether they admit it or not. Blood anchors magic; that is the oldest law there is. But bloodlines thin. Die out. Are hunted. So the founders built a second anchor into the design, a reserve, a well to draw from when the blood ran dry. They filled it with consent, at first. Volunteers. Souls given gladly, held in trust, released when their term ended. It was meant to be honorable."

"And then?" Sera Vail said from the wall, her voice like a blade being drawn.

"And then the terms ended," the Steward said, "and the souls asked to go free, and the crown above discovered that releasing them would mean raising new volunteers, and volunteers are *inconvenient*. So the crown's brokers found another way. A way to fill the well without asking. Children sold by desperate parents. Debtors. Prisoners. Anyone the ledgers could mark *paid* and no one would miss." Her eyes went to Wren, and they were full of a grief so worn it had gone smooth as river stone. "Your mother came to the mountain to beg them to take her instead of you. They refused. A child's soul holds longer. A child's soul is..." She stopped. Started again. "A child's soul is pliable. It shapes itself around the ward. Grows into it. After thirty years, the seal and the soul are the same thing. To pull yours free carelessly would crack the wards from Thornfell to the sea."

"So I'm not a prisoner," Wren said. "I'm a *foundation*."

"You are the keystone," the Steward said simply. "And here is what I have fought my own court to preserve, Wren, daughter of Maren: it does not have to hurt. The bargain stands—the law of it, the weight of it—but a bargain is a shape, and shapes can be lived in gently or cruelly. Stay. Stay willingly. Walk these halls, learn this court, take your mother's chair. Let us make your anchoring a *homecoming* instead of a haunting. You will feel no pain. You will dream sweetly. And when at last the wards are rebuilt on new foundations—work I have begged the crown above to fund for twenty years—you will be released whole, honored, sung. I swear it on the mountain's root."

The hearth crackled. The soup steamed. The empty chair with its new cushion waited at the Steward's right hand.

And Wren understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that every word of it might be true. That was the trap's genius. Not lies—*comfort*. A warm hall, a warm bowl, a warm old woman's hands, and all she had to surrender was the fighting. The wanting-out. The self that had clawed up out of poverty and grief to build a kitchen where nobody went unfed. All she had to give up was the part of her that said *mine*.

She thought of the golden globe in the gallery. Of the thread that pulled in her chest.

She thought of Quentin's cold fingers catching her arm.

"No," Wren said.

The word fell into the warm hall like a chip of ice.

The Steward's face did not harden—that was the worst of it, it *didn't harden*—it only grew infinitely weary, the weariness of a woman who had argued this same argument with mothers and fathers and children for three decades and lost every time the ledger said lose.

"Child—"

"I'm not your child." Wren stood. Her chair scraped. "My mother sold me because she was starving and lied to, and I have spent my life feeding people so that no one at my table ever gets desperate enough to sell anything. You kept soup warm for thirty years? Wonderful. Keep it warm for the four hundred souls in your gallery. Sing them the lullaby. Give *them* their mother's recipe. Because I know what I tasted in that soup, Steward. I tasted care. Real care. Which means somewhere down here is a heart that remembers what consent looks like—and I'm going to find it, and I'm going to put my mother's soul back in her daughter's chest, and then I'm going to come back for every single light in that room."

Silence. Even the hearth seemed to hold its flame still.

And then, softly, from the doorway behind them—from the dark of the corridor they had not come through—a voice Wren had never heard but recognized instantly, the way you recognize a name from a ledger:

"Well said. Though I confess I hoped you'd take the soup."

Lady Vessane stepped into the firelight, and her smile was kind, and her eyes smelled of deep-mountain stone, and beside Wren he heard Quentin stop breathing.
