## 11. Terms of Trade

The court beneath the mountain did not want them dead. That was the first thing Quentin understood, and it frightened them more than any blade could have.

Hollowed servants had escorted them from the mine-shafts' last gate—silent figures in livery the color of wet slate, their faces smooth as river stones, their eyes holding nothing but a faint reflected glow—and instead of killing them, the servants had *led* them. Down a stair carved in a single spiral from living rock. Through galleries where the walls were veined with something that pulsed faintly, slowly, like the heartbeat of a sleeping animal. Past alcoves where glass vessels stood in ranks of hundreds, each one lit from within by a soft gold light that moved when you looked away and stilled when you looked directly at it.

Souls. Wren's people. Perhaps, Quentin thought with a sickness that climbed their throat like cold water rising, souls from families whose names Quentin's own bloodline had once been sworn to protect.

The Steward received them in a chamber that was not a throne room, though it served. There was no throne—only a chair of black wood grown, not built, its roots running down into the floor and out of sight—and no guards beyond the hollowed servants who arranged themselves along the walls like furniture given patience. The Steward himself was small. That was the second shock. Quentin had imagined something vast, something chitinous and terrible from the deep places, but the figure who rose to greet them was an old man with a scholar's stoop and hands folded neatly before him, dressed in grey robes that might have been spun from the mountain's own stone. His face was kind the way a well-tended grave is tidy.

"Quentin Lockridge." He said the name gently, and Quentin's whole body went rigid, because that was not the name they had carried for nine years. "Or should I say—the taster. Forgive me. Names are difficult down here. We keep so many of them."

Behind Quentin, Brannoc shifted his weight, and Ilex's hand drifted toward the knife at her belt. Sera Vail stood very still, her jaw set in the expression of a woman watching a wound she'd predicted finally open.

"You know why we've come," Quentin said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. Nine years of tasting poison taught a person to keep their voice level while everything inside them screamed. "You're holding something that doesn't belong to you."

"I'm holding something that was *sold* to me," the Steward corrected, with the mild air of a merchant correcting a ledger entry. "There is a difference, and the difference matters in law older than your crown. But yes. I know why you've come. Sit, please. All of you. Negotiation is thirsty work, even when no one drinks."

They did not sit. The Steward did not seem offended. He settled back into his black chair and folded his hands again, and the light of the captive souls in the walls seemed to lean toward him, the way candle flames lean toward a draft.

"The wards above are dying," he said. "You felt it. The whole mountain felt it—the stars going out one by one like lamps snuffed by a housekeeper making her rounds. My court is old, taster. Older than the crown that pays your wages. When the founders built the border wards, they needed anchors. Living anchors. Blood that could hold a shape against the dark. And my predecessors made a bargain: souls, freely traded, to bind the stones. Your friend's soul among them." He tilted his head toward Wren, who stood at Quentin's shoulder with her arms crossed tight, her knuckles white. "She has been a most excellent anchor. Patient. Warm. Souls that resist their binding fray the wards; hers has never resisted. She didn't even know she was bound, and so she couldn't struggle. It's remarkable, really. Like a knot tied in silk."

"Then untie it," Quentin said.

"With what?" The Steward spread his hands, and there it was—the merchant's gesture, the opening of the empty scale. "A binding is a weight on one side of a balance. Remove the weight without adding another, and the balance does not forgive you. The wards fail. The things beyond the Thornfell passes come through. Your king's soldiers would learn, quite quickly, what the old wars were actually about. I do not want that. Contrary to what you may have been told, I am not a destroyer. I am a *keeper*. I keep accounts. I keep balances. I keep what I am paid to keep." His eyes, pale as quartz, found Wren's face. "And I keep my word. Bring me an equivalent anchor—a soul of equal warmth, freely offered, willingly given—and I will release her. Tonight. Before the last star dies. You have my oath on the mountain's root itself, which is the only oath worth anything below."

The chamber went quiet. In the quiet, Quentin heard the faint sound of the glass vessels in the outer gallery: a whispering, like wind through wheat, like a hundred voices breathing at the edge of hearing.

*Freely offered.*

Quentin had walked into this chamber knowing, in the way one knows the taste of almond in wine—that small bitter note that means the end of something. They had rehearsed this on the stair, in the dark, while Wren slept against Brannoc's shoulder. They had said the words to themselves until the words wore smooth.

"I offer myself," Quentin said.

The words came out clean. No tremor. Nine years of swallowing poison in measured doses, nine years of keeping their face pleasant while their tongue catalogued death—they had all been rehearsal for this single sentence, spoken in a room lit by stolen souls.

"I'm the last of the warding line. Lockridge blood anchored these mountains before your court ever held a ledger. My blood is worth more than hers—you said yourself the founders used it. Take me. Let her go."

For one heartbeat—one full, complete heartbeat—hope rose in Quentin's chest like bread dough warming by a fire. Because the Steward had gone still. Because his quartz-pale eyes had widened, just slightly, and his folded hands had loosened, and somewhere in the veins of the wall the light had flickered brighter, attentive, *interested*—

Because it was working. Because it was going to be that simple. Because Wren would walk out of this mountain tonight, whole, and Quentin would stay, and it would hurt, God, it would hurt like nothing had ever hurt, but Wren would be free and the wards would hold and the trade would be *clean*—

The Steward laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. That was the worst part. It was the laugh of a scholar who has found an elegant error in a student's proof—a laugh of genuine, almost fond amusement, and it went through Quentin worse than contempt could have. Contempt would at least have meant they'd been taken seriously.

"Oh," the Steward said, wiping at the corner of one eye. "Oh, you *poor* creature. You truly don't know, do you? No one told you. They let you swallow it all these years and never once told you what it was doing."

"Tell me," Quentin said, and hated how thin their voice had gone.

"Your immunity," the Steward said, "is not immunity. Did you think the crown's tasters were simply blessed with iron stomachs? Every dose they fed you, every poison you learned to catalogue by aftertaste—it wasn't training, child. It was *treatment*. Nine years of measured doses, building up in your blood, settling into your marrow, weaving themselves through you like roots through soil. You are the most thoroughly poisoned person in this kingdom. Your blood would kill a warden. Your blood would kill *me*." He leaned forward, and his kindness curdled into something almost pitying. "A vessel must be clean, taster. A soul must be able to pour itself into the binding without carrying ruin with it. Yours carries nothing *but* ruin. If I poured you into those wards, you wouldn't anchor them—you'd *crack* them. You'd be a flaw in the glass. You are, quite literally, worthless to me. Worthless as a vessel. Worthless as an anchor. Worthless as a trade."

The hope died the way a snuffed lamp dies—not gradually, but all at once, leaving an afterimage burned behind the eyes.

Quentin heard Wren make a small sound beside them. Heard Brannoc swear softly under his breath. Felt Sera Vail's gaze on the side of their face like a physical weight.

Nine years. Nine years of doses, swallowed dutifully, morning after morning, and they had thought it was armor. They had thought it was *competence*, their one mastery, the thing that made them valuable enough to keep alive. And all along it had been a cage built cell by cell inside their own veins. The crown hadn't just hidden them. It had ruined them for anything else—for any other life, any other purpose, any other trade. Even now, standing in the one place their bloodline mattered, offering the one thing they had left, they were worthless.

*Loneliness disguised as competence.* They had worn it so long they'd forgotten it was a disguise.

"No," Quentin said.

The Steward raised an eyebrow.

"No," Quentin said again, louder, and stepped forward, and the hollowed servants along the walls stirred like furniture deciding whether to become wolves. "I don't accept that. I've been poisoned, fine. I've been ruined, fine. But I am still Lockridge blood, and Lockridge blood built these wards, and I am *standing here*, and I am telling you that you will release her, because I am not leaving this mountain without her." Their voice cracked on the last word and they let it crack. Let him hear it. "Name your price. Name any price. I have spent nine years being worth exactly what the crown decided I was worth. I am done being appraised."

The Steward regarded them for a long moment. The light in the walls breathed. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the last stars were going out.

"It is not my appraisal that matters," the Steward said at last, gently. "It is the binding's. The wards do not negotiate, taster. They are not cruel and they are not kind. They are a lock, and a lock does not care how badly you wish the key were different." He sighed, and unfolded his hands, and for a moment he looked every year of his ancient, patient age. "But. You interest me. You interest me because you refuse the arithmetic, and refusals are rare down here. So I will tell you a thing I perhaps should not." His quartz eyes slid past Quentin—to Sera Vail, standing silent at the edge of the firelight-less gloom. "Ask your warden why she was disgraced. Ask her what she refused to feed us, all those years ago. She knows more about the terms of trade than any of you. She may even know a term I have not considered." A thin smile. "Though I doubt it. I have had a very long time to consider."

"Sera?" Quentin turned. "What is he talking about?"

Sera Vail's face had gone the color of the slate-grey livery on the hollowed servants. Her mouth opened. Closed. When she spoke, her voice was rough as uncut stone.

"He's talking about a child," she said. "He's talking about the night I chose my rank over a life, and chose wrong. And he's talking about—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "He's talking about a third option. One I found in the founder's records, back when I still had access to them. One I was too much of a coward to use."

The Steward settled back in his chair, hands folding once more, the picture of a patient merchant watching two customers discover a flaw in each other's goods.

"Take your time," he said pleasantly. "The stars aren't getting any brighter."

* * *

They conferred in the antechamber, out of the Steward's hearing—or what they hoped was out of his hearing, though Quentin suspected that down here, hearing was less a sense than a property of the walls themselves.

"A third option," Ilex said flatly. She'd taken up position facing the doorway, knife loose in her hand, watching the hollowed servants in the gallery beyond. "There's always a third option. There's also always a reason nobody takes it."

"Unbinding," Sera said. She sat on a bench of raw stone, elbows on her knees, looking ten years older than she had an hour ago. "Not replacing the anchor. Dissolving the contract entirely. The founders wrote a dissolution clause into the original binding—I read it, years ago, before Vessane had me stripped of rank. It requires the founder's blood, freely given, inside the seal chamber itself. Not poured into the wards. *Given.* Spoken over. Named." She looked up at Quentin, and her eyes were terrible with an old guilt finally given somewhere to go. "It was written for exactly this. For the day someone came to take back what was sold. I found it and I buried it, because using it meant admitting what the wards were, and admitting that meant losing everything I'd built. I let a child be sold rather than say the truth out loud." Her voice broke and reformed. "I won't do it twice."

"And the catch," Brannoc rumbled from the doorway. "There's always a catch."

"The catch," Sera said, "is that the seal chamber is at the heart of the founding vault, and the Steward will not simply wave us through it. And the catch is that 'freely given' means the blood-kin must speak their true name—claim the lineage, out loud, in front of the contract itself. No masks. No aliases. No nine years of being nobody." She met Quentin's eyes. "Can you do that?"

Quentin looked down at their own hands. The hands that had held the king's cup every morning for nine years. The hands that had slipped Wren an extra roll through the kitchen door, a hundred small thefts of tenderness, the only currency they'd ever dared spend.

Somewhere beyond the antechamber door, in a gallery of glass and gold light, a hundred souls whispered like wind through wheat. One of them, Quentin thought—had to believe—was humming. Faintly. A thread of melody that Wren had been singing her whole life without knowing where she'd learned it.

"Yes," Quentin said.

It was the first time in nine years the word had cost them nothing to say.

* * *

When they returned to the Steward's chamber, he was waiting exactly as they'd left him, hands folded, patient as geology. But his eyes went first not to Quentin, nor to Sera, nor to the smugglers flanking the door.

They went to Wren.

And the kindness in his face sharpened into something else—something hungry and old and terribly pleased, the look of a keeper whose finest piece has begun, at long last, to sing.

"Well then," said the Steward of the court beneath the mountain, rising from his black wooden chair as the light of a hundred captive souls bent toward him like wheat toward wind. "I believe we are ready to discuss *terms*."
