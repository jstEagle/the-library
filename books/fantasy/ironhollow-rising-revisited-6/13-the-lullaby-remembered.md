## 13. The Lullaby Remembered

The glass sang back at her.

Wren had noticed it first in the steward's hall, when the Steward's voice curled around her like warm bread and she'd almost—*almost*—said yes. The souls in their vessels had hummed then, a low note beneath hearing, and she'd felt it in her teeth. She felt it now, too, standing in a side gallery of the under-court while Quentin argued terms with Sera Vail in hushed, urgent voices. Row upon row of glass orbs lined the alcoves here, each one holding a small captive light, and every one of them was singing to her.

Not with sound. With *recognition*.

She pressed her palm flat against the nearest vessel. The light inside flared, dimmed, flared again—a heartbeat answering a heartbeat—and something old and cold uncurled in Wren's chest.

*You know me,* the light seemed to say. *You have always known me.*

"Wren." Quentin's hand on her shoulder. "We should keep moving. Sera thinks there's a service corridor behind the granary vaults that runs under the seal chamber—"

"It's the lullaby," Wren said.

Quentin went still. They had that particular stillness, the taster's stillness, the stillness of someone who has just tasted something unfamiliar in a dish they thought they knew by heart.

"Say that again."

"The lullaby. The one I hum in the kitchen. The one I've hummed since—" She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Since forever. Since before the castle. I could never remember where I learned it. I told you that once, didn't I? Over the ovens, years ago. You asked and I said I didn't remember."

"You said your mother sang it." Quentin's voice was very quiet. "You said it was the only thing you had left of her."

"And I couldn't remember her face. Isn't that strange? I can remember her hands—flour to the wrists, same as mine—but not her face. And I could never remember learning the song." Wren turned back to the glass. The lights were pulsing now, all of them, in rhythm, like a room full of held breath. "Because I didn't learn it. It was put into me. Or—it was left in me. Like a key hidden under a loose floorboard."

Sera Vail had come up silently behind them, as wardens did. "Say the first line," she said. Not a request. A warden's order, stripped of rank but not of habit.

Wren opened her mouth. And found, for the first time in her life, that she was afraid of the song.

It was such a small thing. Six notes rising, four falling, words in no language she'd ever been taught but which her mouth had always known, the way a mouth knows its own name. She'd sung it over bread dough and feverish orphans and dying guards, over twenty years of kitchen mornings, and it had never been anything but comfort. A warmth she carried without knowing its weight.

But here, in this candleless court where souls hung in glass like fruit preserved in syrup, the song had a different flavor entirely.

"If I'm wrong," Wren said, "I wake something we can't put back to sleep."

"If you're right," Sera said, "you've been carrying the weapon since the day they sold you. Sing it."

*They sold you.* Such an ordinary sentence now. Wren had read her own name in that ledger three days ago—or a lifetime ago; time ran strange below the mountain—and the word *paid* beside it had burned itself into her memory. Paid. Like flour. Like salt. Like a pig for slaughter, except pigs got to stay whole.

Her mother had sung her this lullaby. Her mother, who had signed—or thumbprinted, or wept over, Wren would never know which—the contract that traded a child's soul for a mountain's safety. Had she taught Wren the song *before*, as a gift? Or after, as an apology? Had she known what she was leaving buried in her daughter, the way you leave a coin sewn into a coat for a journey?

*Hush now, the mountain is sleeping.*

The first line rose in Wren's mind unbidden, and the glass around her shivered.

She looked at Quentin. Nine years of extra rolls slipped across kitchen counters, nine years of a friendship built in stolen moments between the king's breakfast and the evening soup, and through all of it Wren had never once seen Quentin look frightened. Concerned, yes. Calculating, always. But this—this wide-eyed, white-knuckled terror—was new.

"Don't," Quentin said. "Wren. Listen to me. If the song is the contract's key, then using it means the court knows it too. The moment you sing, they'll understand what you are. What you've been. Every soul in this place will feel it, and so will the Steward, and so will whatever Vessane is. We lose our only advantage the second you open your mouth."

"Our advantage?" Wren heard her own voice go flat and hard, a voice she used for staff who'd burned the stockpot twice in one week. "Quentin. Look at them." She swept her arm at the gallery of glass. "Look at how many there are. That's not just my soul keeping the wards up. That's generations. Whole families. People who went missing from villages along the border for two hundred years, and everyone said wolves, everyone said fever, and they were *here*, in jars, being drunk slowly by a court that wanted its mountain pretty." Her hand trembled against the nearest vessel, and the light inside leaned toward her palm like a plant toward sun. "I spent my whole life feeding people because it was the one thing I could do that mattered. You don't get to ask me to walk past a pantry full of starving souls because it's safer."

Quentin's jaw worked. When they finally spoke, their voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "I spent nine years swallowing poison so no one would find out what I am. Hiding is the only skill I have. You're asking me to stand in an open room and be *seen*."

"I know," Wren said, gentler now. "And I'm asking anyway. Because I hid too, love. I just did it in plain sight, behind a ladle."

Something moved through Quentin's face then—grief and relief braided together, the expression of a person setting down a weight so heavy they'd forgotten they were carrying it.

"Sera," Quentin said. "How fast can you get us to the seal chamber once this starts?"

"Once what starts?"

"Once the whole court wakes up angry."

Sera smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a woman who had waited fifteen years to be proven right and intended to enjoy every minute of the reckoning. "There's a warden's run behind the east alcoves. Built for emergencies. It'll hold against everything except the Steward himself, and he won't move quickly—he never does. Pride moves slow." She rolled her shoulders like a soldier preparing for a long march. "Sing, cook. I'll get us to the door."

Wren faced the gallery.

Two hundred years of stolen light looked back at her.

She thought of her mother's hands. Flour to the wrists. She thought of the ledger, and the word *paid*. She thought of every orphan she'd fed, every guard, every homesick taster who took an extra roll and pretended not to be grateful, and she understood, suddenly and completely, that memory was not a soft thing. Memory was a blade. It had simply been waiting, all these years, in the shape of a lullaby, for someone to pick it up.

She breathed in, the way she breathed in before a long day's baking—deep, from the belly, the breath that carries.

And she sang.

*Hush now, the mountain is sleeping,*
*hush now, the stone holds its breath,*
*mama has paid what was owing,*
*papa has nothing left.*

The first note left her mouth and the world *broke*.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. The glass didn't shatter like dropped crockery; it *cracked*, fine lines spidering out from the center of each vessel, and the lights inside flared so bright that shadows fled to the corners of the gallery and cowered there. Wren felt the song leave her body like something physical, like dough pulled from the bowl, like her own soul reaching out through her throat to touch every captive flame in the room.

The nearest orb split down the middle.

The light inside didn't escape so much as *unfold*—expanding outward, unfurling like a banner, like wings, like a person stretching after decades of sleep—and for one impossible moment Wren saw a face in the radiance. A woman. Old. Smiling. Looking at Wren with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of someone seeing home.

Then the light streamed upward, through the cavern ceiling, through solid rock, gone—free—and the sound it made as it went was the lullaby's second verse, sung in a voice like wind through wheat.

One after another, the vessels cracked. The gallery filled with unfolding radiance, with voices layered on voices, with the smell—Wren would swear this to her grave—of bread baking, of rain on dust, of every good kitchen smell in the world, because these were souls and souls remember what they loved. Some of them circled her once, brushing her skin like warm water, before streaming away toward whatever waits above the sky.

And the court woke.

It began as a vibration in the floor. Then the walls themselves seemed to lean inward, the beautiful candleless architecture suddenly less like a palace and more like the inside of a throat. Somewhere far off, a bell made of bone began to toll—not ringing, but *tolling*, each strike a slow wet pulse. Voices rose in corridors Wren couldn't see, high and thin and wrong, the voices of the hollowed servants Vessane commanded, and beneath them a deeper sound, tectonic, patient, furious: the Steward, waking, understanding, *remembering* what had been done to him two hundred years ago by a bloodline he thought extinct.

"They're coming," Sera said, already moving. "East run. Now. Move like the kitchen's on fire."

Quentin grabbed Wren's hand.

For one heartbeat neither of them moved. In the middle of the cracking gallery, amid the storm of freed light, Quentin Lockridge—who had spent nine years being no one, whose whole life was a recipe of silence and substitution—looked at Wren and said, "Your mother gave you a weapon, and you just swung it. Whatever happens next—I need you to know I'd have swallowed every poison in the world twice over before letting them keep you."

"I know," Wren said. "Now run, you idiot. I'll sing the rest on the way."

She did, too. Verse by verse, running through the dark with Sera's lantern-less lamp lighting nothing and everything, Quentin's grip crushing her fingers, the bone bell tolling faster behind them, Wren sang the lullaby her mother had left sewn into her like a coin in a coat. And everywhere the song touched, glass cracked, and light unfolded, and the court beneath the mountain learned what it felt like to be robbed.

Behind them, from the deep places, something enormous began to climb.

Ahead, at the end of the warden's run, a door of black stone stood waiting—and beside it, framed in the glow of a hundred escaping souls, stood Lady Vessane.

She was smiling.

"Well," she said pleasantly, as if greeting guests at a garden party rather than blocking the only exit from a waking prison. "Isn't this a lovely family reunion. Do come through—I insist. We have *so* much to discuss about renewal terms."

The bone bell tolled. The freed souls wheeled overhead like startled birds. And Wren, still gripping Quentin's hand, still humming the sixth note, felt the last verse rise in her throat—the one she'd never dared finish, the one that had always caught somewhere behind her breastbone like a fishbone—

—and realized, with perfect clarity, that Lady Vessane was afraid of it.
