## 17. Lockridge, Last of the Line

The contract hung in the air like a wound in the world.

Quentin had seen it only for a heartbeat before Sera Vail began her work—light written in a language older than the mountain, older than the crown that had bought and sold beneath it—but even that heartbeat had been enough to sear it into their memory. Lines of script coiling around one another like serpents, names knotted into the weave, and at the center, small and terrible as a child's footprint in snow: *Wren. Paid in full.*

Now Sera's hands moved through that light, and the light resisted.

"It won't come loose," she said through her teeth. Sweat cut pale tracks down the dust on her face. Behind her, Ilex held the seal chamber door with his whole body wedged against it, boots skidding on stone worn smooth by centuries of feet that should never have walked here. The door boomed. Once. Twice. The hollowed servants on the other side did not shout or pound like men—they struck the iron in perfect unison, patient as tide, and somehow that was worse than screaming would have been.

"Sera." Quentin's voice came out strange to them, thin and far away. "The unbinding. What does it need?"

"A name." She didn't look back. "Contracts are spoken things, under all the ink. It was sworn aloud once, by blood-kin of the founder, and it can only be unworn the same way. A voice carrying the founder's blood has to claim the lineage—openly, fully, before the seal. Not a whisper. Not a half-truth. The whole name, the whole inheritance, everything they've spent their life denying."

The words landed in Quentin's chest like stones dropped down a well.

Nine years. Nine years of *Lockridge the taster*, of being nobody, of swallowing poison in measured doses until their tongue could read a court's intentions in an aftertaste, of walking smuggler roads at dusk under a name that fit them like a borrowed coat. Nine years since they'd stood over their mother's grave in the high pass and sworn that no one—not the king, not Lady Vessane, not the hungry dark beneath the mountain—would ever again have a Lockridge to hunt. They had buried the name so deep they sometimes forgot it themselves. They had made invisibility into a craft, an art, a religion.

And now the whole thing came down to whether they could stand in a vault full of enemies and say it out loud.

Behind them, Wren made a sound. Quentin turned. She was kneeling where Sera had placed her, inside the ring of ward-stones at the chamber's heart, and she looked—*wrong*. That was the only word for it. Her skin had taken on the grey translucence of candle wax held too near a flame. Her eyes were open but unfocused, fixed on something very far away, and around her, faint as breath on cold glass, a second shape shimmered: the outline of a soul straining against a tether nine decades old. Her lips moved. No sound came out. But Quentin knew the shape her mouth made when she hummed. They had heard it a thousand mornings over the kitchen din—the lullaby she couldn't remember learning, rising out of her like water finding its old channel.

She was singing it now, soundlessly, somewhere on the other side of the bargain.

"Quentin." Ilex's voice, strained, from the door. "Whatever you're going to do, do it soon. This iron is older than I am, and it's losing."

Sera caught Quentin's eye across the chamber. She had told them, on the long road down, why she'd been disgraced—how she'd refused to feed a child to this court, how the rank had been stripped from her shoulders like a coat taken from a thief. There was no pity in her face now. There was something better: recognition. She knew exactly what she was asking.

"The name," Sera said quietly. "Or we bury her soul down here with the rest of them, and when the wards finish dying, both courts come down with it. Every soul in glass goes out like a snuffed candle. Everyone above us goes with them."

The door boomed again. A hinge shrieked—a high, dying note.

Quentin walked to the center of the chamber.

The ward-stones rose around Wren in a broken circle, each one carved with a crest Quentin had known their whole life without ever asking why it felt familiar: the tower and the rising sun, the mark of the line that had built the border wards out of love and then been hunted for it out of fear. Their family crest. Their mother's crest, stitched inside every collar she'd ever sewn. Their own crest, pressed into the silver spoon they'd been given at birth and carried, wrapped in cloth, through nine years of tasting other people's poisons.

They drew it out now. The cloth fell away. The spoon caught the light of the contract and threw it back in fragments.

"Mother," Quentin said, and their voice cracked on the word like river ice in spring. "You told me never to say it. You told me the name was a door and doors swing both ways. You were right. But you also taught me that a cook feeds people who can't pay, that a warden stands a post no one thanks them for, that love isn't love until it costs." They swallowed. Their mouth tasted of copper and of every poison they had ever swallowed for the crown—nine years of arsenic and nightshade and worse, mapped and measured and survived, all of it pooled in their blood like sediment in a riverbed. "So I'm going to say it. And whatever hears me—I want it heard."

They stepped over the ward-stone threshold, into the circle, beside Wren. Her shimmering soul-shape turned toward them. It had her face. Of course it had her face.

"I am Quentin Lockridge," they said.

The chamber went silent—even the hollowed servants beyond the door stopped striking the iron—and the silence had weight, had texture, had *attention*. Something vast and old turned its regard upon them like a lighthouse beam.

"Daughter of Maren Lockridge, warden of the high passes. Son of Aldric Lockridge, who kept the salt roads honest. Last of the line that raised these wards with their own hands and their own hearts and paid for it with their own blood, freely given—" their voice steadied, deepened, became someone they had never been allowed to be, "—and I am here to unmake what was stolen. Let the contract be unworn. Let her go."

The contract *screamed*.

There was no other word for it. Light that had hung serene and serpent-coiled suddenly thrashed, script unraveling strand by strand, names burning off the weave like frost off morning grass. Quentin felt it tear through the chamber, through their own bones, a soundless pressure that popped their ears and filled their mouth with the taste of lightning. Around Wren, the tether—that grey umbilical running down into the stone, nine decades thick with stolen years—frayed, snapped, dissolved.

Wren gasped.

It was the most ordinary sound in the world, a woman drawing breath after holding it too long, and Quentin had never heard anything so beautiful. Color flooded back into her face like dawn coming up over Thornfell. Her eyes found theirs, focused, alive, and her hand shot out and gripped their wrist hard enough to bruise.

"You said it," she whispered. "Your name. You *said* it."

"I said it."

"Quentin—"

But the chamber shuddered.

Quentin felt it before they understood it: a lurch in the floor, in the air, in the marrow itself, the sensation of a great weight settling onto a structure never meant to bear it alone. The contract was gone. Wren's soul was free. And the wards—the border wards, the mountain seal, the ancient lattice that held two courts apart and a hundred thousand lives safe between them—were anchorless.

"They're falling," Sera said. She stood at the chamber's heart with her hands still raised, and her face was ash-grey. "The unbinding worked, but the lattice needs an anchor *now*. Tonight. Within minutes, or the whole weave collapses inward and takes both courts with it. There's no time to find another vessel. There's no time for anything."

"There was always going to be a price," Quentin said.

Wren's grip tightened. "No. No, you don't—you don't get to do this, not after you finally said your own name, not after nine years of—"

"Wren." Quentin took her face in both hands. Her cheeks were warm. That was the miracle of it; her cheeks were warm and wet with tears and alive, and Quentin wanted to memorize the temperature of them, the way they had once memorized the aftertaste of hemlock. "I've spent my whole life keeping myself safe. Poison-proofed. Name-buried. Invisible. Do you know what I learned, all those years in the king's hall? That safety tastes like nothing. That's the secret no one tells you. Safety is the flavor of nothing at all."

"Quentin, please—"

"You fed me rolls I didn't earn. You saved me a seat at your table when I had no name worth saving seats for. You hummed a song you didn't remember learning, and it turns out the song was a key, and *you* were the lock, and I—" their voice broke, and they let it, because they were done hiding things, done forever, starting now, "—I have loved you since the first winter, and I never said so because saying things is dangerous, and I am so tired of being safe."

They picked up the silver spoon.

The founder's crest gleamed on its bowl—tower and rising sun—and Quentin thought of their mother sewing that same mark into collars by candlelight, thought of her saying *the name is a door*, thought of every meal they had ever tasted for a king who had known all along what Quentin was and used them anyway. Then they turned the spoon, set its edge against their left palm, and drew it across in one clean stroke, the way you'd score bread before baking.

Blood welled up—dark, slow, heavy with nine years of accumulated poison.

"What are you doing?" Sera's voice, sharp with alarm. "The lattice will take a willing anchor, yes, but your blood is *poisoned*, child, it will burn the weave, it will—"

"Then the weave and I will burn together," Quentin said, "because there is nothing else. And because I think—" they laughed, a strange, unsteady sound, "—I think maybe that's the point. A seal made of stolen souls was always going to fail. Maybe what holds isn't purity. Maybe what holds is *cost*. Freely paid."

They pressed their bleeding palm to the central ward-stone.

Agony.

Not the clean bright agony of a cut—that had already faded into a distant sting. This was the poison leaving. All of it, at once, every measured dose the crown had ever fed them, every toxin their body had catalogued and survived and stored, tearing loose from tissue and bone and flooding outward into the stone, into the lattice, into the great waking web of the wards. Quentin felt each one by name: the arsenic from their first week, sweet-almond ghost at the back of the throat. The nightshade from the third year, blurring the candles into halos. The unnamed thing in the ambassador's wine that had nearly stopped their heart while the court laughed at some joke they hadn't heard. All of it pouring out of them and into the wards, and the wards *taking* it, weaving it in, and where it went the lattice flared—not gold, not white, but a deep furious violet, the color of a bruise healing, the color of something wounded choosing to hold anyway.

Quentin screamed. They were distantly aware of that—aware of Wren's arms around them, of Sera shouting words of binding, of Ilex abandoning the door entirely to help hold them upright as their knees buckled. The stone drank and drank. The poison was a river and the river had no bottom, because nine years of survival leaves sediment, and Quentin had survived more than anyone guessed. They gave it all. They gave the headaches and the numb fingertips and the nights they'd lain awake mapping their own pulse, waiting to see if tonight was the dose that killed. They gave the loneliness. They gave the mask. They gave the name they'd just reclaimed, stamped into the lattice in letters of blood and venom, so that any power that ever came hunting a Lockridge again would find one woven into the walls of the world, holding the door shut.

*Let this be what I'm for,* they thought, as the violet light climbed past their elbows, their shoulders, their eyes. *Not tasting. Not hiding. Holding.*

The agony crested—white, total, a wave breaking over the last dry shore of them—

and then stillness.

Not emptiness. Stillness. The difference mattered, and Quentin knew it the way they knew flavors: emptiness was the taste of safety, of nothing, of the invisible life. This was something else. This was the moment after the last note of a song, when the kitchen is quiet and the bread is proofing and someone you love is humming in the next room. The poison was gone. All of it. Nine years of the crown's slow murder, poured out into stone, transmuted into a wall between two worlds. In its place, Quentin felt the wards themselves—felt them the way they'd once felt poison on their tongue, intimately, precisely, a new sense blooming where the old burden had been. The lattice ran through them now. Or they ran through it. The distinction had stopped meaning anything.

Somewhere very far away, stars were relighting.

Quentin knew this without seeing it, felt it as a softening in the great tension they had become—one star, then ten, then hundreds kindling back into the black sky above the mountain, each one a knot retied in the weave. The border wards breathed. The mountain seal settled into its new anchor with a groan like a house in winter wind, and then, gradually, like that same house warming, went quiet.

Through the silence, muffled by stone and iron, came another sound: the hollowed servants beyond the door, striking it no longer in unison but wildly, desperately, and then not at all. Somewhere in the dark corridors of the under-court, glass was cracking. Souls were waking.

"Quentin." Wren's face swam into focus above them. She was crying, laughing, both at once, her hands framing their face the way theirs had framed hers. "Stay with me. Stay *here*, do you hear me? You gave enough. Whatever else happens, you don't give another drop, you hear me, you stubborn, brave, impossible—"

"Wren." Quentin's voice was a thread, but it was their own, and it carried their name in it now, openly, the way a bell carries its note. "Did I ever tell you? Your soup. The first day. I tasted it for the king, and it was the first thing in nine years that tasted like anything at all."

"That's not romantic, that's just my cooking—"

"It was everything." Their eyes were closing. The stillness was warm and wide and terribly inviting. "Everything, Wren. Don't let them take the kitchen. Promise me. Whatever the court below asks, whatever the crown above demands—keep the long table. Keep the light in the window."

"Quentin—"

But the darkness took them gently, the way sleep takes a child who has finally, after a very long day, been carried home.

The last thing they felt was Wren's forehead pressed against theirs, and the wards humming through the stone beneath them like a hearth-fire banked for the night—and beneath the humming, faint but unmistakable, Wren beginning to sing.

* * *

Quentin dreamed of the high pass at dawn.

Their mother walked ahead of them on the trail, sewing basket on her hip, and the wind smelled of pine and rain and nothing poisonous at all. She didn't turn around. She didn't need to. "You said it," she said to the mountains, to the sky, to Quentin. "Doors swing both ways, little bird. I only ever hoped you'd open yours onto something worth walking through."

When Quentin woke—or began to wake, or drifted up toward waking through layers of violet light—they could not feel their left hand.

They could not feel their left hand, and Wren was gone from their side, and somewhere above them, faint through fathoms of stone, a voice that was not Wren's and not Sera's and not any voice that belonged to either court was reading out charges in a tone of silk over steel.

Lady Vessane had come down the mountain.

And she was standing in the seal chamber doorway, looking at the empty space where a contract used to hang, and smiling.
