## 18. What the Poison Bought

Wren felt her soul come home the way a house feels its door open in winter—cold rushing in, then warmth, then the impossible, ordinary fact of being *in* again.

One moment she was kneeling on the seal chamber's cold stone with the lullaby still dying in her throat, and the next she was *full*. Full in a way she had never known she wasn't. It was like tasting salt for the first time after nine years of eating bread that had been quietly, kindly unsalted. Something that had been missing since she was six years old slid back into the hollow of her chest and settled there, humming, and Wren gasped so hard she nearly fell forward onto her hands.

The glass above the seal had cracked. She had done that. Her voice had done that. And through the fracture, streaming like light through a broken window, came—

*herself.*

Not memories exactly. More than memories. A warmth that knew her mother's hands before her mind did. A grief for a woman whose face she'd never been able to hold onto. The taste of a mountain spring she'd drunk from at four years old and forgotten until this exact second. All of it pouring home into her, filling rooms in her heart she hadn't known were empty because she'd never seen them furnished.

She heard herself sob. It sounded like someone else.

Above them—far above, up through miles of stone and mine-shaft and the court beneath the mountain—the sky was coming back.

She couldn't see it. But she could *feel* it now, and that was stranger than seeing. Every star that relit struck her restored soul like a small bell being rung inside her ribs. One. Then another. Then three in quick succession, somewhere over the western passes. The wards were waking, drinking from the new anchor, and each one sang as it came online, and Wren sat on the floor of the founding vault with tears running down her face, counting bells.

"That's it," Ilex whispered behind her. The smuggler's voice, usually so dry it could strike sparks, had gone soft and unsteady. "That's the border wards. That's all of them. Stars are out over Thornfell—I can feel it in my teeth."

Sera Vail said nothing. Wren glanced over her shoulder and saw the disgraced warden standing with her hand pressed flat against the original contract—that great web of written light still hanging in the air where Quentin had spoken their name—and Sera's face was wet, though her expression didn't change. Bitter vindication, arriving twenty years late, looked like that: a woman crying without seeming to notice.

And Quentin lay on the stone beside the anchor-stone, grey as old ash.

The joy went out of Wren like a snuffed candle.

* * *

She didn't remember crossing the chamber. One moment she was counting bells in her ribs; the next she was on her knees at Quentin's side, her hands hovering over their face, afraid to touch, more afraid not to.

"Quentin." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "Quentin, wake up. You did it. You absolute fool, you *did* it, the stars are out, everyone's saying—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, the way you talk to someone sleeping. "Quentin. Come back and see."

Their eyes were closed. Their breathing was there but barely—a thread of breath, thin as wire, going in and going out with long silences between. Their skin had gone the color of tallow. Of candle-fat. Of things that have given everything they were made of to something else.

Wren had spent thirty years in kitchens. She knew what dying looked like. It looked like this: not dramatic, not bleeding, just a body quietly deciding whether it wanted to keep being a body.

"No," she said. "No, no, no. You don't get to do this. You don't get to trade yourself like a sack of flour and then just—lie there—"

"They're fighting it." Sera Vail had come to stand over them both, and her voice was gentle in a way Wren had never heard from her. "The poison. Nine years of measured doses—it's all still in their blood, every antidote and every toxin, layered like sediment. And now their blood is the seal. The wards are trying to drink from a well full of arsenic and nightshade and gods know what else. Every time the magic cycles through them, the poison fights it. They're burning up from the inside, slowly, and the wards are burning with them."

"Then get it out of them." Wren looked up. "Bleed them. Purge them. There has to be—"

"If we bleed them, we break the seal." Sera crouched down, and her eyes—tired, iron-grey, kind in the way of people who have stopped expecting kindness to be returned—met Wren's. "The contract is unbound. The old bargain is dead. But the wards need an anchor *tonight*, girl, or both courts fall—the under-court's prisoners with it, and every soul we just freed goes dark again, and the mountain comes down on all our heads. Quentin knew that. That's why they cut their palm over the stone knowing what their blood would do. They did the arithmetic."

"I don't care about their arithmetic."

"Wren—"

"*I don't care.*" Her voice came out louder than she meant, ringing off the vault walls, and somewhere in the dark beyond the doorway Ilex turned sharply. Wren lowered her voice to something low and shaking. "I spent my whole life in other people's ledgers. My name in a kitchen book marked *paid*. My soul in some steward's column marked *assets*. I am not going to sit here and watch the person I love get entered into *another* ledger, marked *spent*, and call it arithmetic. Numbers are how they got us. Numbers are how they sold me when I was six years old. I will not let numbers take Quentin too."

Sera Vail looked at her for a long moment.

Then the warden smiled—small, crooked, exhausted—and said, "Your soul just came home through a ward-network older than the kingdom. Do you understand what that means? You're not just *in* the wards anymore. You're woven through them. Every star that lit tonight lit through you first."

Wren blinked. "What?"

"You can feel them. I watched you counting them." Sera took Wren's hand and pressed it flat against Quentin's sternum, over the slow, failing drum of their heart. "So stop feeling the wards that are working. Feel the one that isn't. Feel *them*. And then tell me, cook—if you can pull heat out of an oven and bread out before it burns, why can't you pull poison out of a man?"

* * *

Later—much later, in the telling—Wren would never quite be able to describe what she did next, except by analogy to the only craft she'd ever mastered.

It was like clarifying butter.

That was the thought that came to her, absurdly, as she knelt over Quentin's body with both palms flat against their chest. When you clarified butter, you melted it slow and low, and the milk solids sank, and the golden clear thing rose, and you skimmed away everything that would burn. You didn't fight the butter. You didn't boil it. You gave the good part somewhere to go, and the bad part somewhere to settle, and you were patient, and you kept the heat gentle, and you *skimmed*.

Her soul was in the wards. The wards were in Quentin's blood. Therefore her soul was in Quentin's blood.

Therefore she could skim.

She closed her eyes and reached.

The first thing she found was the wards themselves—not as light or sound but as *flavor*, because of course it was flavor, because she was Wren and she had always understood the world through her tongue. The ward-network tasted like cold clean water from a high spring, like snowmelt, like the air off a glacier. It flowed through Quentin in a great slow cycle, in through the crown of their head and out through the soles of their feet, and wrapped around it, clinging to it, fouling it, was the poison.

Nine years of it. She could taste every dose separately, the way she could taste every spice in a stew if she paid attention. Arsenic from the third year, faintly sweet at the edges. Nightshade from the fifth, bitter as burnt greens. Something mineral and wrong from the deep-mountain court, something that tasted the way Lady Vessane smelled—stone and old candles and borrowed time. Dose layered on dose, sediment on sediment, all of it caught in the current of the seal, all of it turning clean water foul.

And underneath it all, faint and stubborn, Quentin. Tasting like black tea taken too strong, like iron filings, like the specific lonely flavor of someone who had spent nine years making themselves invisible. Like the extra roll she used to slip them across the kitchen counter, warm from the oven, when they thought no one saw.

"There you are," Wren whispered.

And she began to skim.

She pulled gently at the current, coaxing it the way she'd coax a sauce that was about to break—low heat, slow hands, patience. The poison resisted. Of course it did; it had been put there deliberately, professionally, by people who knew their work, and it had had nine years to root itself into Quentin's blood like ivy into mortar. When she pulled at the arsenic, the nightshade clenched tighter around it. When she pulled at the nightshade, the deep-mountain thing hissed like fat in a fire.

So she stopped pulling and started *making room*.

That was the trick. That was the thing no poisoner and no warden and no steward beneath the mountain had ever thought of, because none of them had ever worked a kitchen. You couldn't force the milk solids out of butter by squeezing. You gave the golden part somewhere to rise, and the rest sank on its own.

Wren opened herself.

She let the ward-current flow *through* her instead of around her—let her own restored soul become the settling-pan, the wide shallow bowl where the heavy things could sink away from the living current. The poison came reluctantly, then greedily, pouring out of Quentin's blood and into hers, and it *hurt*, gods, it hurt—it was like swallowing nine years of Quentin's job in a single sitting, every measured dose, every controlled poisoning, every morning they had knelt at the king's table and swallowed death so calmly that no one ever noticed. Her vision went white at the edges. Her stomach heaved against nothing. Somewhere very far away she heard Sera shouting something and Ilex's boots pounding closer, and she ignored all of it, because the current was clearing, layer by layer, the way broth clarifies when you finally find the right skimming angle.

Arsenic, gone. Nightshade, gone. The mineral wrongness of the deep court fought hardest, clung longest, and when it finally tore free Wren screamed aloud, once, a short sharp sound like a gull, and kept her hands where they were.

And then—

Stillness.

Clean water. Cold, clear, snowmelt-sweet, cycling through Quentin's body unobstructed, and underneath it the flavor of black tea and iron and loneliness, growing stronger, growing *warmer*, the way embers grow when you finally bank them right.

Under her palms, Quentin's heart stumbled.

Then beat.

Then beat again, harder, and again, and color came flooding back into their face like wine into water—not all at once but in a slow tide, grey to pale to pink, and their breathing deepened from wire-thread to something human, and Wren realized she was laughing and crying at the same time, ugly, hiccuping, graceless, kneeling on cold stone in a vault under a mountain with her hands still pressed to the chest of the person she loved.

"Quentin," she said. "Quentin, open your eyes. Open your eyes right now or I'll feed you my burned porridge for the rest of your life, I swear it on every star in the sky, and there are a *lot* of stars again—"

Their eyelashes fluttered.

Their eyes opened—brown, unfocused, bewildered—and found her face.

"...Wren?" Their voice was a ruin, scraped hollow. "Why do you look like you've been poisoned?"

"Because I have been, you enormous idiot, and it was *worth it*." She gathered them up against her, careful of the sealed palm, careful of everything, holding them the way you hold bread that's still too hot to touch but you can't bear to put down. "Don't you ever do arithmetic about yourself again. Don't you *ever*. You hear me? You're not a price. You're not a payment. You're not an anchor or an asset or a line in anyone's ledger. You're *mine*, and I don't trade what's mine."

Quentin stared at her. And then, slowly, with the wonder of someone watching dawn come up over a pass they'd walked a thousand times in the dark, they started to cry—silent, disbelieving, nine years of invisibility dissolving all at once.

"The stars," they whispered. "Did it work? Are the stars—"

"One by one," Wren said. "I counted every single one. You owe me a count of forty-one bells, by the way. I'm keeping a ledger of my own now, and yours is *very* long."

* * *

They stayed in the vault while Quentin's strength came back by degrees—Sera pressing water into their hands, Ilex standing watch at the door with the particular alertness of someone who had learned never to trust a quiet ending. The freed souls of the under-court were moving somewhere far below; Wren could feel them now, a vast murmuring warmth in the ward-web, like dough rising in the dark.

It was Ilex who broke the silence, from the doorway, in a voice gone carefully flat:

"We've got company coming down the shaft. Lots of it. And leading them—" the smuggler paused, listening to something Wren couldn't hear, "—leading them is someone wearing the king's colors. Someone who smells, if you ask me, like old stone and candles that never burn down."

Wren felt Quentin go rigid against her side.

"Lady Vessane," they breathed.

And far above, through miles of waking stone, Wren felt the wards shiver—as if something old and hungry had just begun to descend.
