## 19. Two Courts, One Mountain

### I.

The souls came up out of the mountain like smoke deciding to be people.

Sera Vail stood at the mouth of the seal chamber and watched them go, one by one, through the long gallery of glass that had held them—some of them for centuries. The shards lay empty now, catching the light of the relit wards and throwing it in colors no candle had ever managed. Blue like deep water. Gold like harvest. One shard, near the end of the row, still glowed faintly with a color Sera had no name for, though she suspected it belonged to whatever Wren had left behind when her soul went home to her body: something warm, and stubborn, and faintly amused.

"Steward."

The word still sat strangely on her. Brannoc's brother Torv said it, ducking his head as he passed, carrying a crate of the court's records on his shoulder. He was a smuggler by trade and had been for thirty years, and he handled the ledgers of the dead with more care than he'd ever shown a crate of salt brandy.

"Not yet," Sera said. "I haven't agreed to anything."

"You've been giving orders since dawn," Torv said. "That's agreeing."

He was right, which was irritating. She had been giving orders since dawn. Someone had to decide what to do with four hundred freed souls, an abandoned mine-city full of doorways carved with a dead family's crest, and a court whose entire governing class had either fled upward or dissolved into the shadows they'd always partly been. The Steward—the old Steward, the true one, the exiled one who had come down with them and wept at the threshold of his own banishment—had pressed the signet into her hand two hours ago with shaking fingers and said only, *You refused them once. That is the whole of the qualification.*

She had refused them once. She had refused to feed a child into the seal, and it had cost her rank, her name, eleven years of exile in the border towns where people spat at wardens who'd fallen. And now here she stood, in a vault that smelled of stone and old light, holding a ring too big for her finger.

The freed souls gathered in the great hall in ones and twos, blinking, asking questions in dialects three centuries out of use. Some of them remembered dying. Most remembered only cold, and glass, and a lullaby sung from very far away. A woman in a farmer's coat that had gone out of fashion before Sera's grandmother was born approached her, hands folded, face composed with the particular courage of someone about to ask for something enormous.

"What happens to us now?"

Sera looked at her. At the crowd behind her. At the empty shards lining the walls, each one a small grave that wasn't a grave anymore.

"That's yours to tell me," Sera said. "There's a road up through Thornfell. It comes out above the salt passes. Anyone who wants exile—true exile, free passage, no questions asked—can take it tonight. There are smugglers who owe this mountain debts, and they'll guide you, and they won't cheat you, because I'll be checking." A ripple went through the crowd; somewhere, someone laughed, a rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its work. "And anyone who wants to stay—"

She stopped. Looked around the hall: the vaulted dark, the carved crests, the wards humming overhead like a hive that had finally decided the beekeeper was family.

"—anyone who wants to stay can help me build something better than what was here before. It will be hard. It will be badly organized. I have never governed so much as a kitchen."

"We could ask the cook," someone said, and the laughter came again, warmer this time.

The farmer-woman considered for a long moment. Then she turned to the crowd and raised her voice—a voice made for calling across fields—and asked, "Who stays?"

Sera would remember the sound of the answer all her life. Not a cheer. Something quieter and heavier than cheering: the sound of four hundred people choosing, out loud, together, after centuries of having no choice at all.

### II.

Above, in the castle, the king's abdication took eleven minutes, and Quentin Lockridge spent most of them trying not to laugh.

It wasn't funny. They knew it wasn't funny. But there was something in the sight of Aldric the Third—Aldric the Well-Fed, Aldric whose breakfast Quentin had tasted every morning for nine years, whose soup Quentin had once saved by spitting into a napkin and lying about a stomach ailment—standing in the throne room in his nightclothes because no one had let him stop to dress, signing away a crown to a council that included a smuggler, a disgraced warden, and a cook.

Well. The cook hadn't signed anything. Wren stood beside Quentin with flour still on her knuckles—she'd been up since before dawn, feeding the castle, because the world could end twice over and people would still need bread—but her name was on the charter all the same. *Wren of the Undercourt,* the scribe had written, and then crossed it out when she said, "Just Wren. Of the kitchen."

The exposure had taken less time than anyone expected. Vessane's ledgers were thorough; that was the horror of her. Every payment, every soul, every year of borrowed life itemized in a hand so elegant it looked like devotion. The king's signature appeared on the oldest pages. He had known. He had known for forty years, had eaten breakfast off plates bought with a child's soul while the taster he never bothered to learn the name of swallowed poison to keep his secrets.

Now he set down the pen and looked at the room—at the council members arranged below the dais, at the guards who would not meet his eye, at Quentin, whom he did not recognize even now.

"I don't suppose," the king said, "there's any arrangement—"

"No," said Marta Cinderfell, the smuggler matriarch who now held a seat her grandmother would have died laughing at. Her voice was gravel and finality. "There isn't."

They led him out through a side door, toward the apartments where he would live out his days under a comfortable, permanent guard. As he passed Quentin, he paused. Looked at them properly for the first time in nine years.

"The taster," he said. "You always looked familiar. Have we met?"

Quentin held their tongue until the door closed. Then Wren's hand found theirs, and the laugh finally escaped—half hysteria, half grief, all exhaustion.

"He never knew," Quentin said. "Nine years. He never once knew my name."

"He knows it now," Wren said. "It's in the charter. Right under mine."

* * *

### III.

Vessane's borrowed time ran out at sunset, and she spent her last hour counting.

Quentin found her in the tower room where the brokers' ledgers lived, sitting amid her own immaculate handwriting, a lamp burning low. She did not look surprised to see them. She looked, if anything, relieved—an accountant at last permitted to close the books.

"You should know," she said, "that I was going to offer you everything again. Even now. Power, safety, a name in the succession. Old habits." She smiled, and for a moment the smile was young—decades younger than her face, the smile of whoever she had been before the mountain bought her. "Do you know how long I've been alive, taster? Neither do I. That's the trick of it. You sell a year here, a decade there, and you stop keeping count, because keeping count means knowing what you've become."

"You sold a child," Quentin said. "You sold Wren. Her mother trusted you."

"Yes." No flinch. No excuse. Just the word, laid down like a stone. "I've had a hundred years to craft a version of that story where I'm not the villain. None of them survive contact with the ledger. So." She folded her hands. "Ask your question. Everyone who comes to gloat has one."

Quentin had thought they'd want to gloat. Standing there, they found they only wanted to understand—one thing, one small terrible thing.

"Why her? Out of everyone. Why a kitchen child?"

Vessane was quiet for a moment. Outside, the sun touched the mountains, and the light in the room went the color of old honey.

"Because her mother sang," she said. "The contract needed a spoken key, and blood that carried the old resonance, and a family desperate enough. I told myself the singing made it gentle." She shook her head slowly. "It didn't. Nothing made it gentle. That's the lesson, taster, if you want it: there is no arrangement so elegant that it isn't still theft underneath. I built my life on elegant thefts. Look how well they kept."

The sun went down. And Vessane went with it—not dramatically, not with smoke or screaming. She simply grew quiet between one breath and the next, like a lamp running out of oil, and the woman who slumped in the chair was small, and old, and mortal, and wore the face of a stranger. Whatever she had originally been, decades ago, was beyond even the ledgers' power to recover.

Quentin covered her with a blanket from the window seat. It seemed the only honest rite available.

Then they went downstairs, to the kitchen, where the light was on and someone was waiting.

* * *

### IV.

Wren burned the ledger page at midnight.

Not the whole book—Marta Cinderfell had claimed the rest for the council's reckoning, every debt and payment to be answered, however long it took. But page one, the first entry, dated the day a six-year-old girl arrived at the castle gates with a song in her head and nothing else: *One soul, purchased. Paid in full.* Wren had carried that page against her chest for two days like a coal she couldn't put down.

Now she fed it to the kitchen hearth and watched it curl.

The fire didn't feel different. That was the strange part. She had expected—she didn't know what. Trumpets. Cold. Some sensation of the missing piece sliding home. Instead her soul had simply returned the way feeling returns to a numb hand: painfully, gradually, and then all at once, so that she woke the morning after the seal broke and cried over an onion, because onions had *layers*, and she had layers, and both things were suddenly, unbearably true.

She was still learning what the restored connection to the wards felt like. It hummed under everything now, the way the castle's own heartbeat must hum under its stones. When she kneaded dough, she could feel the mountain breathing. When she sang—when she dared sing—the wards along the border brightened like coals breathed upon. Sera had sent a message asking, very politely, whether she might *stop doing that during council sessions*, as it made the chandeliers flicker.

The kitchen filled the way it always did at the end of a long day: guards off shift, orphans who had never actually been orphans but runaways the kitchen had quietly adopted, Ilex nursing a bandaged arm and complaining about it with tremendous artistry. Quentin came in last, smelling of tower dust and sunset, and Wren poured them wine without being asked, because some things you learned in nine years even without a soul.

"They're saying the council wants you," Quentin said, settling onto their stool at the end of the counter. Their stool. It had been theirs for years; nobody had ever remarked on it, and now it seemed remarkable that neither of them had ever remarked on it. "For the food safety office. Tasting, but for policy."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I'd retired this morning. Loudly. In front of witnesses." Quentin turned the cup in their hands. "I tasted the council's tea today. First thing I've tasted in nine years that was just—tea. Bitter, over-steeped, probably Marta's revenge for something. It was wonderful."

Wren leaned her hip against the counter and studied them. Grey still shadowed Quentin's eyes, and would for weeks; drawing the poison out had nearly killed them both, and the healers said the last of it would take its time leaving, like a guest who didn't realize the party was over. But underneath the grey there was something new. Lightness, maybe. Or just space, where the mask used to sit.

"You could go back to being nobody," Wren said gently. "If you wanted. Nobody knows your face now except people who love you. That's a kind of invisibility too. The good kind."

Quentin was quiet a long moment. Around them the kitchen roared on—Ilex arguing with a guard about dice, a scullery boy stealing rolls with the subtlety of a landslide—and Wren watched her friend, her partner, her person, weigh the old life against the new one and find, visibly, mercifully, that the scales had already tipped.

"No," Quentin said at last. "I'm done disappearing. I spent nine years being a ghost with excellent taste. I think—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "I think I'd like to be someone specific. Someone who's here."

"Well," Wren said, and picked up a roll, and threw it at them, because the moment was getting dangerously sincere and bread was the traditional remedy. "Someone specific. Catch."

* * *

### V.

Far below, in the great hall of the under-court, Sera Vail lit the first lawful flame the mountain had seen in three hundred years.

It was a small thing—a tallow candle, stubby, smelling of the smugglers' stores—and her hands shook lighting it, because the old contract had forbidden open fire within the seal's reach, and contracts written in light did not always die cleanly. But the wards held. The candle burned. And around her, in the hall, the freed souls who had chosen to stay watched the little flame with expressions she recognized from her own mirror: hope, held very carefully, the way you hold something that has broken before.

Torv appeared at her elbow with a slate. "Census. Four hundred and six souls accounted for. Two hundred and nineteen staying. The rest take the salt road at dawn—Brannoc's people are guiding, and Brannoc says—" he squinted at the slate, "—quote, 'tell the warden the mountain owes her, and we pay our debts.'"

"Tell him the mountain doesn't owe me anything," Sera said. "I'm the one paying."

"Suit yourself. Also—" Torv hesitated, which was unlike him. "There's a girl at the south gate. Sixteen, maybe. Says her grandmother was of the warding line, distant branch, thought the line was extinct. Says she's been dreaming about the doorways. Wants to know if the stories are true."

Sera looked at the candle flame. At the empty glass shards, gleaming. At the crests carved over the doorways—Lockridge crests, a name that had been a lie and a shield and a grave, and was now, somehow, a beginning.

"Show her in," Sera said. "And send word up to the kitchen. I think there's someone above who will want to meet her."

The candle burned steady. Somewhere far overhead, faint as memory, she could almost swear she heard singing.
