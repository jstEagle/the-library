## 20. The Taster Retires

The first thing Quentin tasted on waking was the medicine.

Bitterroot and silverleaf, steeped too long—the healer had rushed it, worried, and the tannins had gone sharp as a reprimand. Beneath that, the ghost of poppy for the pain. Beneath *that*, something Quentin had never tasted before in nine years of professional vigilance: a low, humming sweetness, like honey left in sunlight, like warm stone after rain.

Magic. The wards, running through their blood now instead of Wren's soul, and their tongue—cursed, gifted, ruined, remade—could taste it plain as salt.

Quentin lay in the infirmary bed in the east wing of Ironhollow castle and wept quietly into the linen, not from pain, though there was plenty of that, but because the sweetness was everywhere. In the walls. In the water pitcher. In the thin winter light through the shutters. Nine years they had walked through a world of hidden flavors, cataloguing danger in the space between one swallow and the next, and now the whole world had opened its pantry to them at once.

"You're awake." Wren's voice, rough with sleeplessness. She sat in the chair by the bed, flour still dusting her forearms—she'd come straight from the ovens, then, or never left. "You've been grey for six days. I've been talking at you like an idiot."

"I heard some of it," Quentin rasped. Their throat felt scoured. "Something about the state of the cellar stocks. Something about Brannoc."

Her face did the thing it always did when his name came up—tightened, then deliberately loosened, grief given a job to do. "Ilex is having him buried up top, where the sun gets in. He'd have hated the under-court. Said so himself, often, and loudly." She leaned forward and pressed her palm flat against Quentin's forehead, checking for fever the way she checked a proving loaf, by instinct and touch. "You're cool. That's good. Sera says the seal has settled. Says your blood took to the stone like it had been waiting for you all along."

"It had," Quentin said. "That's rather the point of a bloodline."

"Don't be clever yet. You haven't earned clever back." But her thumb brushed their temple, brief and unbearably gentle, and Quentin closed their eyes and let themselves be touched. That was another thing the poison had bought them: permission. When you have spent nine years being untouchable—testing every cup, distrusting every hand—you forget what it is to simply be held by a moment.

"The king's men came asking for you yesterday," Wren said, quieter now. "Chamberlain Odell. He wanted to know when you'd return to duty."

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him you were dying, which was true at the time, and that if he didn't like the answer he could take it up with the woman whose soul just got pried out of a mountain." She smiled without much humor. "He went pale and left. I think he thought I was threatening him."

"Weren't you?"

"Only a little."

* * *

Recovery, it turned out, was its own kind of tasting.

Each day brought something new to the tongue. The infirmary's willow-bark tea carried a faint green shimmer of hedge-craft, old and half-forgotten, the kind village grandmothers worked without ever naming it. The chapel bell, rung at noon, rang *audibly* sweet across Quentin's palate—a bronze-and-frankincense note that lingered like candied peel. Even the castle itself had flavor now: cold iron and older stone, the deep-mountain hum of the rebuilt wards threading through everything like a bass note under a song.

Sera Vail visited on the fourth day, moving through the infirmary with the stiff gait of a woman still learning to walk without a warden's badge weighing her belt. She pulled the chair close and studied Quentin with those grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"Tasting it yet?" she asked.

"Everything. All the time. It's—" Quentin searched for the word and failed. "Loud."

"It'll settle. The gift runs deep in your line; it was only ever waiting for the wards to wake it." She folded her hands. "Your ancestors weren't just anchors, Lockridge. They were the realm's palates. They could taste a broken ward three valleys off, taste a lie in a treaty, taste rot in a granary before the first mouse found it. The court beneath used to call them the Sweettooths. It wasn't an insult."

"I spent nine years using this tongue to keep myself invisible."

"I know." And there it was, the thing Sera Vail did that almost no one else could: she said *I know* and made it sound like absolution rather than accusation. "And now you get to decide what it's for. That's not nothing. Most people never get the choice at all."

She stayed an hour, telling Quentin in her dry, unhurried way what was happening above and below. The freed souls choosing their futures, some climbing toward exile in the sun-country, others staying to help Sera rebuild the under-court into something that kept its bargains honestly or made none at all. The council forming in the king's absence—he had abdicated two days after Vessane's borrowed time ran out, collapsing in the throne room into a drift of grey ash and expensive perfume, and no one had mourned her except perhaps the mountain itself. Ilex held a seat now, smuggler-turned-alderman, complaining loudly about paperwork. Wren had been offered a seat too, and had declined with a laugh, saying she already ran a small country and it smelled better than the council chamber.

"And you?" Quentin asked. "What does the disgraced warden do when the disgrace turns to vindication?"

Sera stood, straightening a coat that no longer bore any insignia to straighten. "Steward work. Ledgers. Apologies owed to people who can no longer hear them." At the door she paused. "There's a letter on your bedside table. Came up from the chancery yesterday. You should read it before Odell comes asking again."

After she left, Quentin read it. It was short. *His Majesty's household requests the Taster's return to duty upon recovery. The office remains unfilled. The Crown notes with gratitude the Taster's recent services.* No mention of the wards. No mention of souls. No mention of the fact that the Crown's gratitude had, for nine years, consisted of letting Quentin live unnoticed in a corner while the court spent a child's soul like coin.

*Recent services.* As if Quentin had returned a borrowed umbrella.

They set the letter down and looked at their own hand—the scarred palm, the cut long healed but the line of it still pink and new, the line where they had opened themselves to the stone. The hand did not shake. It hadn't shaken since the seal chamber. Whatever else the poison and the pact had done, they had burned the tremor out of Quentin Lockridge forever.

* * *

On the eighth day, Quentin walked to the kitchens.

It was a slow walk. The healers disapproved; Wren pretended not to notice and hovered anyway, matching their pace with the elaborate casualness of someone carrying a full tray she could catch them with. Winter sun came slantwise through the high corridor windows, and Quentin tasted it—actual sunlight, faintly citrus, absurdly lovely—and had to stop twice just to stand in it.

The kitchen was loud and warm and smelled of bread proving near the great hearth, of onions in butter, of the particular scorched-sugar sweetness of the winter puddings Wren made every year whether anyone asked or not. Orphans sat along the far bench doing sums under the eye of a scullery girl. Two guards argued amiably over the last of the stew. A cat slept in the flour bin, as was tradition and against all regulation.

And at the worktable nearest the door, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with string, stood a boy of perhaps fourteen, chopping turnips with the fierce concentration of someone defusing a bomb.

"That's Merrin," Wren said. "Came up from the river quarter after the abdication. His mother worked the palace laundry until the cough took her. He's been sleeping in the wash-house and stealing bread from us for two years, and I finally caught him at it properly and put him to work, which is how everyone worth employing arrives." She raised her voice. "Merrin! This is the taster I told you about. The one who nearly died. Try to look impressed."

Merrin looked up. His eyes went wide, then narrowed with the particular suspicion of a child who has learned that adults who are famous are usually also dangerous. "You're the one who broke the mountain," he said.

"I helped," Quentin allowed.

"Sera Vail says you ate poison for nine years on purpose."

"Also true."

"Why?"

It was a fair question, delivered with the flat directness children reserve for the questions adults have spent decades avoiding. Quentin set a hand on the worktable's edge and considered it. Why indeed. For the crown? The crown had sold a child before Quentin was born and called it governance. For safety? Safety had turned out to be a room with no windows. For invisibility—and yes, that had been the honest answer once, the lonely answer, competence worn as armor over a heart that dared not hope to be seen—

"Because I was good at it," Quentin said at last, "and I mistook being useful for being loved. Don't do that. Chop your turnips and find people who like you for no reason at all. It's a much better bargain."

Merrin chewed on this along with his turnip tops. "Wren likes you for no reason," he observed.

"Merrin," Wren said, in the voice that ended conversations.

But Quentin laughed—an actual laugh, rusty, surprising them both—and the boy grinned like he'd won something, and went back to his turnips with better humor.

* * *

Chamberlain Odell came on the tenth day, in his grey silk and his grey manner, bearing the resignation form Quentin had requested in writing and the expression of a man attending the funeral of a convenience.

"The King's household hopes you understand the irregularity," Odell said, laying the parchment on the infirmary table with two fingers, as though it might bite. "A Taster resigns. It isn't done. The office exists precisely so that it *cannot* be vacated casually. Who would test the royal plate? Who knows the smugglers' roads as you do? Who—"

"Who indeed," Quentin said pleasantly, and watched him falter, because they both knew the answer now. The roads were known to Ilex, who sat in council. The plate needed no testing, because the court beneath no longer dealt in poisoned cups and the crown above had learned, briefly and painfully, what happened to kings who traded in souls. The office existed because offices outlive their reasons, the way scars outlive wounds.

Odell cleared his throat. "There is the matter of your oath."

"My oath was to protect the King's person from harm taken by mouth." Quentin dipped the quill. "I fulfilled it for nine years, swallowed things no one should swallow, and asked no reward. I am released from it by the simple fact that there is no longer a King to protect—only a council, and councils can hire their own tasters, and should, and pay them properly this time." They signed. *Quentin Ashgrove-Lockridge*—no. Just *Lockridge*. Last of the line, first of the name to sign anything freely. The ink gleamed wet in the window light, and beneath it Quentin tasted the faintest thread of ward-magic rising from the parchment's seal, the council's new honesty-writs, spelled so that lies dried brown and visible. Their signature stayed black and clean.

Odell gathered the paper, hesitated, and—for the first time in nine years of shared corridors—looked at Quentin as a person rather than a fixture. "For what it's worth," he said, "the kitchen staff petitioned the council when you were dying. Forty-one signatures. Cooks and scullions and guards. They stood in the rain outside the chancery until someone read it aloud." He tucked the form away. "I have served this household thirty years and I have never seen that done for a Taster."

Then he bowed, exactly as deep as protocol required and not a hair deeper, and was gone.

Quentin sat very still for a long moment. Forty-one signatures. Wren's would have been first.

* * *

The last evening of the old life, Quentin went alone to the tasting chamber.

It was a small room off the pantries, windowless, lit by one lamp: a table, a row of spoons, a cabinet of reference vials, a chair worn smooth by nine years of the same body. Everything here had a flavor Quentin knew by heart—the lamp oil, the beeswax on the table, the cold mineral patience of the stone walls. On the shelf sat the little ledger in which they had recorded every poison, every dose, every aftertaste, in a neat hand that had grown steadily neater over the years as feeling drained out of the writing and discipline filled the space behind.

Quentin took the ledger down and read a few entries. *Arsenic, 3rd of Frostmoon. Almond ghost, fading fast. Note: the king's new wine masks it well—flag the vintage.* *Hemlock, spring. Faint mousiness. Doubled the antidote dose; hands steady.* Page after page of survival written in the language of solitude.

They carried the ledger to the kitchen hearth, where Wren sat mending aprons with Merrin dozing against her shoulder, and fed it to the fire page by page. It burned greenish and bitter-smelling, and Quentin tasted even that—arsenic's ghost rising off the flames, the last of nine years going up the flue.

"You could have sold that," Wren said. "To the historians. To the council. It's worth something."

"So am I," Quentin said, "and I'm not selling myself either. Not anymore. Not to anyone, for any price." They watched the cover curl and blacken. "Besides, the important entries were never in the book."

"No?"

"No. The book never recorded the nights you saved me an extra roll. Never recorded the lullaby through the kitchen wall. Never recorded—" Quentin stopped, because the next part was large and true and deserved daylight rather than firelight, and daylight would come tomorrow, in the kitchen, with flour on their hands. "Tomorrow," they said instead.

Wren looked at them across the hearth with an expression that had waited nine patient years and could wait one night more. "Tomorrow," she agreed.

* * *

Morning came up gold over Ironhollow, and Quentin woke in their own narrow bed in the taster's quarters for the last time, dressed in plain wool instead of court grey, and walked down to surrender the keys.

The steward's clerk received the ring of them—pantry, spice vault, tasting chamber, the little drawer of antidotes—with the mild bewilderment of a man processing the end of an era between appointments. "You'll want to sign the inventory release," he said, and Quentin signed, and the pen scratched, and somewhere below their feet the mountain hummed its low honeyed note, and none of it hurt.

At the kitchen door, Merrin was waiting, scrubbed and solemn, holding a wooden spoon presented on a folded cloth like a sword of state.

"Wren says you're staying," he announced. "Properly. Not just visiting."

"I'm staying."

"She says you can taste poison better than anyone alive."

"Anyone dead, too, probably."

"So—" He thrust the spoon at them, ears going red. "So you should teach me. What things taste like. When food's gone wrong. When water's bad. Wren says the river quarter wells go sour in summer and nobody notices till folk are sick, and if I could *tell*—" He stopped, embarrassed by his own wanting, which was so familiar to Quentin that it ached.

Quentin took the spoon. It was cheap beechwood, bitten at the handle end, seasoned with a thousand soups. They tasted it out of habit: wood and use and care, and underneath, faint as dawn, the sweet hum of a world waking up.

"First lesson," Quentin said, following him into the warmth and noise and light of the kitchen, where Wren stood at the great table with her sleeves rolled and her whole face open like a door finally unlocked, "is that you don't taste with your tongue. You taste with your attention. So pay attention, Merrin. Pay attention to everything."

Outside, far above the rooftops, the morning stars were fading into blue, each one going out gently, the way stars should—because the sun was coming, and not because anything had died.

Quentin stepped inside, and laid the mask down for good.
