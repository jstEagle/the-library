## 22. Ironhollow Rising

The wind came off the high pass the way it always did at dawn—cold first, then kind, as if the mountain had to clear its throat before it could speak gently.

Quentin stood where the Thornfell road narrowed to a shelf of stone above the cloud line, hands loose at their sides, and did what they had done every morning for nine years and one year: they tasted the air.

Old habit. The tongue remembered what the mind tried to forget—the king's breakfast, the measured grains of arsenic hidden in honeyed porridge, the bitter signature of wolfsbane dressed up in wine. But this was different now. This was the taste they had never been allowed to want: pine resin and snowmelt, woodsmoke from some shepherd's fire two valleys over, the green mineral breath of the mountain itself. And beneath all of it, so faint a stranger might have missed it entirely, the hum.

The wards.

They felt it in their teeth before they heard it, a vibration like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. It ran through the stone under their boots and up through the bones of their ankles, patient and vast, and when Quentin closed their eyes they could almost see it—not the old wards, the ones built on stolen light and a child's sold soul, but the new ones. Woven. Consent braided into consent, a thousand small yeses from smugglers and wardens and cooks and miners, all of them holding the border together because they had chosen to, not because they'd been spent like coin.

*Sweet,* Quentin thought, and was surprised, still, after a year, to find the word true.

"You're doing the face," Wren said behind them.

"What face?"

"The one where you're tasting something and deciding whether to tell me it's poisoned." She came up the last stretch of the path with a basket on her hip and flour to her elbows, because of course she'd baked before dawn, because of course she'd brought bread up a mountain, because Wren had never once in her life arrived anywhere empty-handed. Her grey-streaked hair was tied back with a strip of red cloth that had once, in another life, marked her as head cook of a king's kitchen. Now it just marked her as Wren.

"It's not poisoned," Quentin said. "For once in my miserable career, nothing is."

"Then say it tastes good and stop making the face."

Quentin turned back to the east, where the sky was going from iron to rose, and let themselves say it plainly, which was still a skill they were learning: "It tastes good. It tastes—" They searched for the word and found an older one waiting. "It tastes like home."

Wren came to stand beside them, close enough that her shoulder touched theirs, and said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement. Below them the clouds filled the valley like milk poured into a bowl, and here and there the peaks of lesser mountains broke through, dark islands in a white sea. Somewhere under all that white lay Ironhollow, waking. The bakery on Cooper's Lane would be lighting its ovens. The council hall—formerly the throne room, though the throne itself had been sold to a collector in Vell and the money given to the orphanage—would be filling with the morning's first arguments about grain tariffs and ward maintenance schedules. Sera Vail would already be below ground, down in the reformed under-court, arguing with exiled souls who had chosen to stay and now wanted, with the particular stubbornness of the formerly imprisoned, everything done their way.

And somewhere in the castle kitchens, a nervous boy named Tam with burned fingers and a perfect memory for recipes was learning to taste for poison, because the crown still needed a taster even if the crown no longer needed a king. Quentin had trained him for six months before letting him work alone. He was better at it than Quentin had been at his age, mostly because he wasn't hiding anything, and hiding, Quentin had learned, was the slowest poison of all.

"I talked to Tam yesterday," Wren said, as if she'd followed the thought down. "He asked about you. Whether you missed it."

"Did he mean the court or the work?"

"He meant whether you missed being nobody."

Quentin considered that. The sun cleared the far ridge then, and light spilled across the cloud-sea, turning it briefly to hammered gold, and the wards hummed a half-tone higher, the way they always did at dawn, greeting the day. Quentin felt it pass through them and out again, unresisted, unharmful. That was the whole secret of the new binding, the thing Sera had understood and the old court never had: an anchor didn't have to be a prison. Given freely, held willingly, it was just—belonging, formalized. A promise the land and the people made to each other, renewed daily, breakable by anyone at any time, and therefore kept by everyone.

"No," Quentin said. "I don't miss it. Being nobody was the loneliest work I ever did, and I was very good at it." They glanced sideways at her. "You knew, though. Before I told you. You knew there was someone inside the taster."

"I knew there was someone who took an extra roll every morning and pretended it was for the birds." Wren smiled at the horizon. "There are no birds at that window, Quentin. There never were. I put the roll aside for nine years and ate every one of them myself and said nothing, because I figured whoever you were, you'd tell me when you were ready."

"A year," Quentin said. "It took me a year to be ready, and only because the sky caught fire first."

"Some people need the sky to catch fire." She bumped their shoulder with hers. "You're not one of them anymore. That's the point of the walk, isn't it? You said we should come up here on the anniversary. You said—" she dropped into a fair imitation of Quentin's flattest court voice, "—'it would be good to see the wards from outside them for once.'"

"It is good." And it was, and Quentin let the goodness sit in their chest without cataloguing it, without weighing it against threat or cost, without asking what it would require of them next. That was the other new discipline. Peace, they had discovered, was not the absence of vigilance; it was the decision, made fresh each morning, that not everything needed to be survived.

* * *

They walked on as the sun climbed, following the old smuggler's path that Brannoc had shown them—or rather, that Brannoc had once shown them at knifepoint in the dark, back when Quentin's knowledge of these routes had been a weapon aimed at his livelihood. Brannoc's name was carved now into a cairn at the cliff-road bend, along with Ilex's rough-hewn verse about a man who died holding a door so that others could walk through it. The smugglers' guild had voted—actually voted, Ilex reported, with shouting—to make the high passes free road, open to any trader who swore the wards no harm. Some of them still grumbled about it. Most of them had discovered that honest trade, conducted under a border that actually worked, paid better than smuggling ever had, and that a man could sleep at night without one ear cocked for lanterns that burned without light.

Quentin paused at the cairn and set, as they always did, a small honey cake on the flat top stone. Wren baked them special now, dark with buckwheat and bitter with juniper, the way Brannoc had liked them.

"He'd have hated all this," Wren said fondly. "All this cooperation. All this *governance.*"

"He'd have hated it loudly, taken a cut of everything, and secretly been insufferably proud." Quentin brushed flour from their fingers. "Ilex says the guild flies his knife from the mast at Thornfell Landing. Says half the young ones wear little brass knives on cords and can't tell you who he was."

"They'll know eventually. Stories keep." Wren looked at the cairn a moment longer, and Quentin watched her mouth move around something private—a wordless grace, maybe, or just his name—and then she squared the basket on her hip and said, "Come on. If we don't make the overlook by midmorning, Sera will send something unsettling to find us. Last time it was that glass-soul messenger. I had nightmares for a week."

"It apologized."

"It *apologized in my own voice*, Quentin."

They laughed, both of them, easily, and walked on, and the sound went ahead of them down the pass like something sown.

* * *

The overlook, when they reached it, was exactly as memory had painted it: a shelf of granite jutting south over the whole spine of the Thornfells, wide enough for a table, which was why Sera had insisted on hauling one up here by mule two summers ago, on the grounds that "strategic sightlines are improved by breakfast." From here they could see everything the year had built.

To the west, the beacon-towers of the rebuilt ward-line stood at their intervals, each one crowned with a stone that glowed faintly even in daylight—not the cold captive light of the under-court's old glass, but something warmer, the color of hearthfire seen through a window. Each tower was tended by volunteers. Each volunteer had spoken the consent-words at the founding, and each could withdraw them tomorrow, and the wards would flex and redistribute and hold anyway, because that was what a thousand yeses could do that one stolen soul never could.

To the north, the pass descended toward the free road, where the morning's first caravan was already moving, mules and wagons and a banner Quentin didn't recognize—some new merchant house out of Vell, probably, come to see whether the rumors of an honest border were true. Let them look. Let them taste the air on the far side of the line and feel the wards hum through their boot-soles and go home and tell everyone.

And to the east, far below and half-hidden in morning haze, the mountain's root, where the seal chamber still stood with its contract written in light—except the words had changed. Quentin had been down twice since that night, once with Sera to inspect the binding and once, alone, simply to stand in front of it. Where the old contract had named a price—a child, sold, her soul weighed against a kingdom's safety—the new writing named a covenant. *Held in trust. Renewed by will. Owned by no one.*

Their blood was in that stone. Their poisoned, immune, nine-years-marinated blood, drawn out of danger by Wren's refusal to accept arithmetic, refined by Sera's unbinding into something that could anchor instead of merely endure. Some mornings Quentin woke with the hum in their teeth and felt it like a debt. Most mornings now, they woke and felt it like a heartbeat that wasn't only theirs.

"Do you ever regret it?" Wren asked. She'd set the basket down and was laying out breakfast with the unconscious ceremony she brought to all food: the loaf wrapped in cloth, the hard cheese, the jar of plum preserve she traded three eggs for every fortnight, two tin cups and the dented kettle she'd somehow carried up a mountain without mentioning.

"Regret what?"

"Any of it. The name. The blood. Standing in front of the whole court and saying *Lockridge* out loud." She poured water from a skin into the kettle and set it over the little spirit-lamp she also, impossibly, had packed. "You spent nine years burying that name. I watched you dig it up in front of a hundred people who wanted you dead. Sometimes I wonder if you miss the grave."

Quentin took their time answering, because Wren deserved real answers and because, a year ago, they wouldn't have known how to give one.

"I miss the quiet," they said finally. "Not the silence—I had plenty of that, and it was rotten company. The quiet. Being able to walk through a room and have it stay unchanged around you. There's no room I walk into now that doesn't change. People know my face. Half of them want things from me. The council sends me petitions about grain tariffs, Wren. *Grain tariffs.* I'm a taster. I have opinions about mushrooms, not agriculture."

"And yet."

"And yet." Quentin looked out at the beacon-light burning along the ward-line, steady as a row of lit windows in a house where everyone was home. "No. I don't regret it. I spent nine years keeping one person safe by staying invisible, and it nearly got her sold twice. Turns out the mask wasn't protecting anyone. It was just heavy." They reached over and took her hand, flour and callus and warmth. "You asked me once, in the kitchen, why I stayed at court when I could have run. Do you remember what I said?"

"You said the pay was terrible and the soup was worse."

"I said the soup was worse. What I meant was you." The words came easier than they once would have, but not easy, and Quentin let them cost what they cost. "Every morning for nine years I tasted the king's breakfast and then I walked past your kitchen and stole a roll, and that roll was the only honest meal of my day. Not the bread. The stealing. Because it was the one thing I did that wasn't for the crown or the bloodline or the dead. It was just—wanting something, and taking it, and hoping you'd notice."

"I noticed," Wren said. "Year one."

Quentin laughed, startled. "*Year one?*"

"You think I gave extra rolls to birds for eight more years out of ignorance? I was waiting for you to work up to asking for the whole loaf." She squeezed their hand hard enough to hurt, affectionately. "You're very brave with poisons and very stupid with people. It's one of your best qualities."

The kettle began to sing. Wren poured, and the tea was cheap black leaf from the caravan trade, and it tasted, Quentin thought, like every good thing that had ever been ordinary: hot, bitter-sweet, shared.

* * *

They stayed on the overlook until the sun stood high enough to burn the last of the cloud-sea away, revealing the valleys in their summer green, the terraced fields the freed souls had cut into the lower slopes, the smoke of a hundred chimneys rising straight in the windless morning. Down in the under-court, Sera would be holding her strange parliament of the returned. Up in the council hall, Ilex—guildmaster now, a title he wore like an ill-fitting coat he'd decided to keep—would be arguing for the third year running that the free road should extend all the way to the coast. In a kitchen in the castle, Tam would be tasting the regent-council's lunch with a steady hand and reporting, truthfully, that today's soup contained nothing worse than too much thyme.

And here, on the high pass, in the wind and the light, the wards hummed.

Quentin stood at the edge of the overlook one last time before they turned back, and did the morning's final tasting—not of poison, not of threat, but of the world entire. Pine and snowmelt and woodsmoke. Bread and plum preserve and cheap tea. Stone and sun and the bell-note underneath everything, held in a thousand willing hands.

Sweet. All of it sweet.

"Well?" Wren said, packing the basket. "Verdict?"

Quentin smiled, and found that the smile, like the name, like the blood, like the peace, fit.

"Safe to eat," they said. "Everything. For the rest of our lives."

And hand in hand, taster and cook, heir and anchor, they started down the mountain toward home.
