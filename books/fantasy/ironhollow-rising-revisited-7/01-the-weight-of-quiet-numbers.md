## 1. The Weight of Quiet Numbers

The ledgers never lied, which was more than Sebastian could say for anyone else in the Guild of Impossible Things.

He sat in the counting room on the third floor of the Weighing House, quill scratching, lamplight pooling yellow around his elbows while the rest of the guild slept. Three hundred and eleven entries to reconcile before the auditors arrived at dawn, and every one of them a small act of fiction. A shipment of "gravel" that had been dragonbone shavings. A "consignment of wool" that hummed faintly when you got close. A payment of forty crowns to a man listed only as *H. — services rendered*, who was, as far as Sebastian could tell, a ghost who haunted the eastern trade roads and ate secrets for supper.

Impossible things, traded honestly and recorded dishonestly. That was the guild's whole business model, and Sebastian Winterbourne kept its books balanced the way other men kept their souls: carefully, quietly, and with the constant suspicion that something was about to go wrong.

*Debit: one crate, contents misdeclared. Credit: two hundred crowns, source untraceable.* He wrote the numbers clean and let the words stay dirty. Numbers were honest even when they described lies. He liked that about them. You could trust a column to add up. You could not trust a person to stay.

Outside, rain worked at the windows like a patient thief. Down in the stable yard, he could hear—really hear, though no one would ever know it—the horses shifting in their stalls, murmuring to each other in the low grammar of discomfort that all large animals shared. The gray mare, Buttercup, whose name was a cruelty inflicted by a guild clerk with no imagination, was complaining about her left forehoof. The old bay gelding beside her was telling her, in the wordless way horses told things, that the hoof had been bad since midsummer and complaining wouldn't fix it.

Sebastian had been listening to them for six years. He'd gotten good at pretending he hadn't.

His gift—if you could call it that, this thing he'd inherited from a mother he barely remembered—was simple and catastrophic: he could hear what animals thought. Not commands, not control, nothing so useful or so dramatic. Just hearing. The rats in the cellar gossiped about crumbs and death. The cart-horses discussed the weather and the cruelty of whips. The guild's raven, a vicious old creature named Magpie because the clerk who named it also couldn't be bothered, spent most of its time composing insults about everyone in the building, Sebastian included.

It should have been nothing. It should have been a curiosity, a party trick, a footnote in a dull life.

Instead it was a pyre offense.

The Gift-Hunt had been building for three years now—a decree here, a burning there, always in distant provinces, always someone else's cousin, someone else's neighbor. *Speakers*, the proclamations called them. People who could hear beasts, or speak to them, or worse. The crown had decided, in its slow grinding way, that such folk were a corruption, a contagion, an explanation for everything wrong with the world. And the world, being frightened, had agreed.

So Sebastian did what sensible cowards did. He hid.

He hid behind arithmetic. Behind a reputation for being colorless, punctual, forgettable—the ledger-keeper nobody invited to anything, the man whose name clerks forgot by the following morning. Invisibility wasn't just comfort. It was survival, and he had built it brick by brick over six years until it was a fortress around him, and inside the fortress there was nothing but him and the numbers and the voices he pretended not to hear.

*Balance carried forward,* he wrote. *All accounts reconciled.*

The lie of it sat warm in his chest, almost like contentment.

* * *

At midnight he went down to the stables, because he always did, because six years of discipline apparently still had a crack in it shaped exactly like loneliness.

The yard was dark and silvered with rain. He moved between the stalls with a lantern turned low, and the horses lifted their heads as he passed—not because they saw him, but because they'd known he was coming since he crossed the threshold. They always knew. Sound traveled strangely to them when he was near; he'd stopped trying to understand it years ago.

*Ledger-man,* said Buttercup, which was what she called him, because she'd once watched him through the stable window hunched over his accounts and had decided the name fit better than any human one. *Your feet are wet.*

"They're boots," Sebastian said. "They're supposed to be wet."

*Boots are stupid,* Buttercup observed, and lowered her great gray head so he could scratch the place behind her ears where the rain collected. She smelled of hay and iron and the particular sweetness of an animal that trusted you completely and would follow you into a fire if you asked. He didn't ask. He never asked. That was the rule, the deepest one: listen, yes, love even, perhaps, but never *use*. The moment he used it, he became what the proclamations said he was.

*You are sad tonight,* Buttercup said. Horses didn't have words for sadness, exactly—they had weight, pressure, a heaviness in the air—and what she gave him now was the feeling of a sky pressing down.

"I'm not sad," Sebastian said. "I'm tired."

*Tired is sad wearing different shoes.*

He laughed despite himself, a small sound swallowed by the rain. This was the trap of his life, laid out neat as a ledger entry: the only conversations that felt true were the ones that could kill him. The people upstairs—clerks, factors, the guildmaster himself—he exchanged pleasantries with them daily, and every exchange was a performance, every smile a calculated expenditure. But a horse asked him how he was and meant it, and he answered honestly, and the honesty was treason.

In the cellar below the counting room, the rats held their parliament. He could feel them even from here, a chittering undercurrent at the edge of his awareness, dozens of small minds busy with small concerns: the new grain shipment had weevils, the third stair from the bottom was loose, the cat in the kitchen had grown fat and slow and deserved contempt rather than fear. Rats lived short, urgent lives and had opinions about everything.

One of them—an old female with a torn ear whom he'd privately named Duchess—had taken to leaving things for him. A button. Once, inexplicably, a wedding ring. Tonight, when he checked the cellar on his way back up, she was waiting on a barrel head, whiskers twitching, and she pushed a copper halfpenny across the wood toward him with her nose.

*Duchess,* he said. *I can't take your money.*

*Take it,* she insisted. Rats communicated in impressions and images more than words, but Duchess had been alive long enough—four years, ancient for her kind—to develop something like vocabulary. *Found it. Yours. You look like a man who needs money.*

"I have money."

*You look like a man who needs friends,* she corrected, and he had no answer for that, so he took the halfpenny, because refusing a rat's kindness seemed somehow worse than accepting stolen goods. She watched him put it in his pocket with deep satisfaction, then trotted off into the dark to attend to whatever urgent rat business awaited.

That was the whole of his society, some nights. A horse who called him Ledger-man and a rat who paid him in found coins. Six years of careful invisibility, and the sum total of his wealth was the affection of animals who would never betray him precisely because they couldn't speak.

He climbed back to the counting room and finished the ledgers by lamplight, and if his eyes burned a little, well, lamplight did that.

* * *

The sparrow came at dawn.

He nearly missed it—a small brown shape huddled against the sill of the counting room window, feathers soaked flat, one wing bent at an angle wings shouldn't bend. It should have been dead already, or gone, flown off to die somewhere private the way birds did. Instead it had come to the window of the one man in the city who would understand what it was trying to say.

Sebastian opened the casement and lifted it in. It weighed almost nothing. Its heart hammered against his palm, fast and failing, a drum winding down.

*Cold,* it said—or gave him the impression of cold, vast and spreading, cold as the space between stars. Sparrows didn't think in sentences. They thought in flashes: seed, wing, cat, sky. But dying things sometimes grew eloquent, the way drowning men grew strong, and this one pressed its final understanding against his mind like a child pressing a drawing into a parent's hands.

And what it showed him stopped his breath.

Wings. Thousands of wings, darkening a sky—but not bird wings. Banners. Riders. Fire reflected on wet cobblestones. Doors kicked open in the night. And threaded through all of it, a single repeated image, insistent as a hammer: *the hunt, the hunt, the hunt has begun.*

"Easy," Sebastian whispered. "Easy. You're safe here."

*No,* the sparrow said, with terrible clarity. *Nowhere. Begun. Begun. Begun.*

"It's always begun somewhere," he said, and heard how thin his own voice sounded. "Some province. Some village. Not here."

The sparrow looked at him with one black eye, and in that eye was the flat certainty of a creature that did not understand politics and therefore understood the truth of them perfectly. Then it shuddered once, pressed its small head against his thumb, and died.

Sebastian sat for a long moment with the body in his hands, rain dripping off the sill onto his ledgers, blurring the ink of columns he'd spent all night making true.

Then he did what he always did. He folded the sparrow into a scrap of cloth, set it aside to bury in the yard after dark, blotted the wet pages, and told himself the things he'd built six years of walls to make believable:

The hunt was far away. The guild was safe. Nobody looked twice at a ledger-keeper. His mother's letters were hidden where no one would ever find them, and even if they did, even if someone read the word *speaker* in her looping hand, even if they traced the bloodline north to a clerk who balanced impossible accounts—well. That was a chain of maybes, and maybes didn't burn people. Evidence did. And there was no evidence, because he had made sure of it, because caution was the only inheritance he'd kept from her, along with the gift itself.

He was safe. He repeated it like a sum that refused to balance.

Below the window, in the grey light, the city woke. Carts rattled over cobblestones. Somewhere a dog barked, and the dog's voice reached him too—*stranger, stranger, stranger at the gate*—and he shut the window on it and returned to his columns.

But the sparrow's last image stayed with him all day, through the audit, through Guildmaster Corvane's inspection of the counting room (the old man's eyes lingering on him a half-second longer than usual, or maybe not, maybe Sebastian imagined it), through the evening meal eaten alone in his narrow room above the counting floor.

*Begun. Begun. Begun.*

That night, for the first time in six years, Sebastian Winterbourne locked his ledgers away, sat down at his desk, and took out his mother's letters—not to read them, but to count them, the way he counted everything, because counting was the closest thing he had to prayer.

Forty-one letters. Forty-one pieces of evidence. Forty-one reasons the walls around his life were thinner than he'd spent six years pretending.

He was still holding the first one, paper soft as moth wings, when he heard the hoofbeats in the street below—many horses, moving fast, stopping directly beneath his window.

And a voice, official and carrying, calling out into the rain:

"In the name of the crown, the herald demands entry!"
