## 2. A Proclamation in Red Wax

The herald came at dawn, and the horses told me before I saw him.

I was in the stable yard with the cart-team, checking hooves the way I did every morning—pressing each frog of the foot, running my thumb along the shoe's edge, listening to what the animals had to say about it. Not with words, exactly. Their talk came to me as pressure and image and smell: the sour note of a loose nail, the gray flicker of a bad dream from the night before. The bay mare, Bess, had dreamed of fire again. She'd been dreaming of fire for three days.

*Iron man comes,* she said, in the way she said things—a heavy shape moving through her mind like a barge through fog. *Red on his chest. All the little humans standing still.*

I'd have dismissed it as a nightmare if the gelding beside her hadn't shivered and pressed his nose into my shoulder, hard enough to leave a warm damp spot on my coat.

An hour later, when the horn sounded from the guild square, I understood.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a full block of Copperlane, its façade all soot-dark stone and brass fittings, its sign depicting an owl shaking hands with a fish. Inside, we traded in what sensible folk wouldn't touch: bottled dreams sold to insomniac lords, years of life pawned by desperate mothers, names bought back from moneylenders who'd won them at cards. The ledgers were my domain. Three hundred and eleven accounts, each one stranger than the last, and every column balanced to the copper because I was very good at being unremarkable.

By eight o'clock the whole staff stood in the square outside, packed between the weighing house and the fountain that hadn't run clean in a decade. I found my place near the back, among the junior clerks, shoulders rounded, eyes down. Invisibility was a craft. I'd been practicing it since I was nine years old, since the day my mother pulled me close in a market crowd and whispered, *Never let them see you listen, Sebby. Never let them see you answer.*

The herald rode a black destrier that kept its ears pinned flat—the poor creature was terrified, though not of the crowd. It kept looking at the rooftops. I made myself not look where it looked.

He wore the crown's colors, white and gold gone gray with road dust, and across his chest hung a scarlet sash with a wax seal the size of a dinner plate. When he unfurled the proclamation, the paper caught the morning light and threw it back red.

"Hear ye," he called, in the flat voice of a man who had said this too many times in too many towns. "By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third, and by counsel of Lord Chancellor Veyne: the Gift-Hunt is declared throughout the realm."

Nobody breathed.

"Be it known that beast-speakers, hedge-witches, charm-mongers, and all who traffic with the uncanny are hereby named enemies of the crown. Any soul possessing such gifts must present themselves at the nearest garrison within thirty days, there to be *assessed*." The word came out smooth as oil over something rotten. "Any person found harboring, concealing, or aiding a gifted individual shall be judged complicit. Complicity carries the pyre."

A woman near me made a small sound, quickly swallowed. Somewhere behind us, a baby started crying and was hushed with desperate efficiency.

"The crown offers clemency to those who report," the herald continued. "Informants will be compensated. Informants will be protected." He paused, and his eyes swept the crowd, and for one horrible moment they seemed to snag on mine—not seeing me, exactly, but counting me, the way you count coins without looking at their faces. "The age of hidden things is ended. So says the crown. So says the law."

He nailed the proclamation to the guild's own notice board with four strokes of a hammer, each one landing like a verdict. Then he wheeled the destrier around and clattered off toward the merchant quarter, and the crowd stayed frozen a moment longer, three hundred people pretending they hadn't heard, before breaking apart into clusters of lowered voices.

Bess's dream of fire sat in my stomach like a swallowed coal.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane convened the staff in the great hall within the hour.

I'd worked under Corvane for six years and knew him the way you know weather: patterns, pressures, the feel of a storm before it breaks. He was a big man going soft at the edges, silver-streaked beard trimmed with surgical precision, rings on six fingers. He had a voice built for reading funeral rites and a smile he deployed like a weapon he didn't need anymore. Everyone said he'd built the guild into what it was. Nobody asked what he'd traded to do it.

He stood at the head of the long table with the proclamation laid out before him, and he read it aloud again, slowly, letting each clause land. Then he folded his hands.

"You've heard the crown's terms," he said. "I'll not pretend this doesn't concern us. Our trade touches strange corners. Some of you handle goods that could be *misconstrued*." A dry pause. "The guild survives by being indispensable and unremarkable. We will remain both."

His gaze traveled down the table, clerk by clerk, factor by factor, and stopped—for half a heartbeat, no more—on me. Or perhaps it didn't. Perhaps I only felt it stop, the way you feel a draft from a door you can't see.

"Effective today," Corvane continued, "every member of this house will report anomalies. Anything unusual in your ledgers. Anything unusual in your colleagues. Unusual purchases. Unusual questions. Unusual *talents*." He smiled, thin as a blade's edge. "You will bring such observations to me directly. Not to the garrison. To *me*. The guild protects its own—but only those who protect the guild."

Murmurs ran the length of the table. Old Hesk, the senior factor, cleared his throat. "And if someone refuses to report, Master?"

Corvane's smile didn't waver. "Then they've chosen their side, haven't they? And the pyre has room for two."

That was all. We were dismissed to our work as though nothing had happened, as though the world hadn't tilted on its axis before lunch. That was Corvane's gift, I thought bitterly as I climbed the stairs to the ledger room—he could make catastrophe feel like administrative procedure.

But my hands were shaking as I opened the day's accounts, and no amount of arithmetic steadied them.

*Report anomalies.* I ran the phrase over and over while I entered figures for dream-tinctures and bottled sighs. Six years of careful invisibility, and now the walls themselves had ears. Every colleague a potential informant. Every kindness a debt that could be collected in blood. Someone would report something—that was certain. Fear made people generous with other people's secrets. The only question was whether what they reported would point at me.

I thought of the sparrow. Three days ago, dying on the cellar steps, it had looked up at me with one bright eye and pushed a picture into my mind: hawks, many hawks, circling a city, and beneath them a voice like grinding stone saying *the hunt has begun*. I'd told myself it was a bird's fever-dream. Birds dramatized. They saw cats and called them dragons.

It hadn't been a fever-dream.

* * *

My mother's letters lived in the false bottom of my ledger chest, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, tied with the same faded blue ribbon she'd used on everything.

There were eleven of them. She'd written them in the last year of her life, after the coughing started, when we both knew what the winter would take. I knew them by heart—I could recite them the way priests recite liturgy—but knowing wasn't the same as having, and some nights I needed to hold the paper itself, to press my thumb where her pen had pressed hardest.

*They took the Henderson boy today,* she wrote in the third letter. *Sixteen. They say he charmed rats out of granaries. They say it like it's a crime, and maybe it is now. Sebby, listen to me. Your father thought your gift was a blessing. Your grandmother thought it was a duty. I think it's neither. I think it's a candle in a high wind, and the wind is getting worse. Hide it. Hide it so deep that even you forget where you put it.*

*I love you past the end of words. Burn these when you're safe. You'll never be safe, so burn them anyway.*

I'd never burned them. For eleven years they'd been the one true thing in a life built almost entirely of concealment—the ledgers, the bowed shoulders, the mumbled good-mornings designed to make people underestimate me. Every night I spoke with horses and rats and sparrows, and every day I pretended to be a man who balanced numbers and wanted nothing else. The letters were proof that someone, once, had known the whole of me and loved me anyway.

Now the whole of me was worth thirty pieces of silver and a guarantee of protection.

I sat in my narrow room above the ledger office with the door barred and the shutter closed, and I laid the letters out on the bed, and I tried to think.

Running was the obvious answer. Ironhollow, the old stories said—a sanctuary in the eastern fells where the gifted had hidden since before the kingdom had a name. But the stories also said the roads east were watched now, that the crown had posted watchers at every bridge and tollgate, that hunters had begun using trained beasts to sniff out speakers. Trained beasts. The thought turned my stomach. Somewhere, dogs and hawks were being taught to betray their own kind, and I couldn't even warn them.

And even if I reached the fells—even if the stories were true—what then? I'd be a fugitive forever. My mother's letters would still name me. My face was known here; my habits were known here. A man who vanishes the week the Gift-Hunt begins might as well hang a sign around his neck.

No. Running was what they'd expect. Running was what fear did.

Fear, I decided, could go to hell.

I would do what I did best. I would become smaller. More precise. More boring. From tonight, the beasts got nothing from me—not a word, not a glance, not a moment in the dark stables. The rats would wonder where I'd gone. Let them wonder. If the guild watched me, they'd watch a dull clerk who worked late and drank alone and had no friends worth reporting. I would bury the gift so deep that even I would have to dig for it.

And I would find out who else in this guild had something to hide, because a house full of informants is also a house full of people desperate not to be informed upon. Anomalies, Corvane wanted. Fine. I kept the strangest ledgers in the realm. I would read them differently now—not for balance, but for leverage. Not to use, never to use, unless the alternative was the pyre.

Cold calculation. My mother would have hated it. My mother would also have survived it.

I struck the flint.

The first letter caught slowly, the ink darkening before the flame took it. I fed the fire one page at a time, watching her handwriting curl and blacken—*hide it so deep*, *burn these when you're safe*, *I love you past the end of words*—until the ribbon went too, a brief blue tongue of flame, and there was nothing left but ash in a washbasin and a hollow behind my ribs where eleven years of proof used to live.

Through the floorboards, faint as breath, I felt the cellar rats stirring. They'd noticed the smoke. One of them—an old doe rat with a torn ear who liked to sit near my feet while I worked—pushed a question up through the wood: *Small-voice? Why does the den smell of ending?*

I pressed my palm flat against the boards and gave her nothing. Silence. The first silence in eleven years, and it tasted like swallowing glass.

*Go,* I thought at her, hard, the way you'd throw a stone. *Forget me. Forget this room. Forget my smell.*

She lingered a moment—I felt her confusion, hurt, the small animal stubbornness of a friend who doesn't understand why the door has closed—and then she withdrew, whiskers dragging, back into the dark beneath the guild.

I cleaned the ash from the basin, washed my face, and went down to finish the evening's accounts. Balance, post, initial, file. The most unremarkable man in Copperlane.

It was nearly midnight when I found it. Account 214, a routine purchase order for stable feed, signed off by Corvane himself—except the quantity was triple what our stables held, and the delivery date predated the order by two weeks, and the supplier's mark was one I'd never seen in six years of balancing this guild's books: a coiled serpent biting its own tail, stamped in wax the color of dried blood.

Red wax. Like the seal on the proclamation.

I stared at the entry until the candle guttered, and somewhere below my feet, in the walls and the cellars and the dark, the beasts of the Guild of Impossible Things waited for a voice that had promised them it would never speak again.

I blew out the candle and sat alone in the dark, listening to the silence I had chosen, and understood that I had just found the first thread of something much larger than a misfiled order.

Whatever Corvane was feeding, it wasn't horses.
