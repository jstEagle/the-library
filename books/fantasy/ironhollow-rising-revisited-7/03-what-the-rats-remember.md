## 3. What the Rats Remember

The rats came to him at the third bell of the night, as they always did, pouring out of the wall behind the coal cellar like spilled ink given legs.

Sebastian sat cross-legged on a flour sack with his ledger balanced on his knee—habit more than necessity, since no one audited the cellar stores at midnight—and listened.

*Cold,* said the eldest, a scarred female they called Grey-Nose because her muzzle had gone silver years before any rat's should. *The walls are cold this year. Colder than cold. The stones remember being warm.*

"Stones don't remember," Sebastian said, and immediately felt foolish for arguing with a rat about metaphysics in an empty cellar at midnight.

*Stones remember everything,* Grey-Nose said primly. *That is what stones are for. You people only use them to build on top of forgetting.*

He smiled despite himself. This was the arithmetic no ledger held: that the world was crowded, that every shadow had a voice in it, that he had never once in thirty-one years been truly alone except when he chose to be. The horses in the stable yard murmured their slow dreams. The mice in the granary argued over grain futures. And here, in the dark under the Guild of Impossible Things, the rats kept their own records—older and stranger than anything Sebastian entered by day.

*There is a new smell in the master's rooms,* Grey-Nose said, changing the subject the way old creatures do, abruptly, because the new thing matters more. *Iron and paper. Paper that has been handled by many hands.*

"Herald's wax," Sebastian said quietly. "Everyone smells it."

*Not the wax.* She crept closer, whiskers trembling. *Under the wax. Under the iron. There is an old smell too, keeper. An old bargain-smell. We know it. We smelled it once before, long ago, the night the master screamed.*

Sebastian went still. "Corvane doesn't scream."

*Men do not scream,* Grey-Nose agreed. *But we were in his walls then, as we are in his walls now, and what lives inside a man sometimes screams loud enough to shake the mortar. He bargained, keeper. Before you were born. He sat where you sit and he spoke to us—to the beasts, the way you do—and he made a trade.*

The flour sack seemed to tilt beneath him. "You're mistaken. I'm the only one who—"

*We do not mistake smells.* Her voice flattened into something almost pitying. *He traded with something in the deep places. Something under the hill. And whatever he gave it, it was not enough, or it was not right, or it was not freely given—for the bargain turned on him. It took from him instead. Something he could never buy back.*

"What did it take?"

Grey-Nose was silent a long moment. Around them, the younger rats had gone quiet, pressed against the walls, ears flat.

*We do not know,* she said at last. *We know only that after that night, the master stopped hearing us. Whatever door he had, it closed. And men who have had a door closed on them, keeper—they spend the rest of their lives nailing shut everyone else's.*

* * *

He climbed back up through the trapdoor at dawn with the words lodged in his chest like a fishbone.

It explained nothing, he told himself, climbing the narrow stair to the counting room. It explained everything. Corvane had been guildmaster for twenty-two years; Sebastian had served him for eleven of those, balancing accounts of trades so strange they would have been called lies anywhere else—a widow's memory of her wedding day, sold for three winters of health; a soldier's name, pawned against a debt of honor; forty years of a miser's life, purchased at auction by his own son. The Guild of Impossible Things dealt in everything except impossibility itself, and Sebastian had always assumed the strangeness stopped at the ledger's edge.

Now the rats were telling him the strangeness sat at its head.

He found Corvane already in his office above the weighing floor, a small neat man with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of pond water—flat, greenish, giving nothing back. He looked up as Sebastian entered with the morning tallies.

"Winterbourne." Corvane took the pages without reading them, which was unlike him. He read everything. He read everything twice. "You've heard the herald's decree, of course."

"I heard it, sir."

"Then you'll understand why I'm asking every officer of this house to look closely at his own department." Corvane set the tallies down and laced his fingers. "Anomalies, Winterbourne. Oddities. Beasts behaving strangely around a member of staff. Staff behaving strangely around beasts. A clerk who lingers in stables. A porter who whistles and has crows land on his shoulder." His voice stayed mild, administrative, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "The crown has made harboring a speaker a capital matter. If such a person exists within these walls, it is an act of loyalty—not betrayal—to name them. To ourselves first. Then, if necessary, to the crown."

"And if there is no one?" Sebastian asked. His own voice sounded distant to him, steady, clerical.

"Then we report that there is no one, and the crown sends someone to check anyway." Corvane smiled thinly. "Better we find our own anomalies than let strangers dig through our ledgers. You of all people understand the value of keeping our accounts private."

"Yes, sir," Sebastian said, and carried the tallies out, and did not begin breathing normally again until the door had closed behind him.

*Nailing shut everyone else's.*

* * *

She arrived on the afternoon barge, and Sebastian knew something was wrong before he ever saw her face, because the horses told him first.

*New one,* rumbled Duchess, the oldest cart-horse, as Sebastian checked her harness in the stable that evening. *Came off the barge with a box of books and a coat too good for a clerk. Walked the whole length of the yard without looking at us. Not once.*

"People don't look at horses, Duchess."

*People look at horses,* she said, with the enormous patience of her kind. *Even people who don't like horses. Even people who are afraid of them. They look, because we are large and we are there. This one looked at the windows. At the doors. At the roof. She counted things, keeper. I don't know how I know she counted, but I know.*

He might have dismissed it—he wanted to dismiss it—but then he saw her himself.

Isolde Marr. The new junior auditor, assigned by the crown's Board of Trade to review the guild's exemption filings, or so the letter said. She was perhaps thirty, dark-haired, dressed with a precision that made the other clerks look like sacks of laundry. She stood in the counting room doorway while the guildmaster introduced her, and she did not fidget, did not smile the nervous smile newcomers smile, did not perform uncertainty. She simply watched.

And when Corvane gestured toward Sebastian—"our senior ledger-keeper, the backbone of this house"—she looked at Sebastian for exactly two seconds longer than courtesy required.

Two seconds. He counted them the way he counted everything. In those two seconds her eyes did the same thing her body didn't: they moved. Down to his hands. Back up. A flicker toward the window, where sparrows gathered on the sill. A flicker toward the stable yard beyond.

Then she smiled, said, "I look forward to learning your systems, Master Winterbourne," in a voice like a well-kept file, and turned away.

He told himself it meant nothing. He spent the whole evening telling himself it meant nothing, hunched over his desk in the counting room while lamplighters worked the street below, while the building settled into its nighttime creaks, while the rats ran their midnight roads inside the walls.

*She hasn't left,* Grey-Nose reported near midnight, appearing on the windowsill with the ease of a creature who owned every wall in the city. *She sleeps in the guest room on the third floor. She put a line of ash across her threshold.*

"Ash?"

*A warding. Old country craft. Against listening things.* The rat's whiskers twitched. *Against us, keeper. Or against whatever she thinks listens.*

Sebastian stared out at the dark yard. "Why would a tax auditor ward her door against rats?"

*Why indeed,* said Grey-Nose, and was gone.

* * *

The days that followed taught Sebastian a new kind of arithmetic: the mathematics of being watched.

Isolde Marr was everywhere, and nowhere, and always precisely where the work justified her being. She requested the beast-trade ledgers—the ones Sebastian kept personally, the ones recording purchases of animal memory and instinct for the guild's clients. She requested the stable expense accounts. She requested, with a small apologetic smile, "a full inventory of all living assets," and stood in the stable yard the next morning with her list while Sebastian walked her past forty horses, six oxen, and the ravens the guild kept for messages.

She watched the animals. No—that wasn't quite it. She watched *him watching* the animals.

"The gray mare favors you," she observed, as Duchess pushed her great head over the stall door toward Sebastian's shoulder.

"She's known me eleven years."

"Mm." Isolde made a note. "Eleven years. And before that?"

"Before that I was in the provinces. Apprenticed."

"To whom?"

"A ledger-keeper in Marrowford." True. All true. Every word true, and yet each one felt like a coin he was passing to a cutpurse, knowing she'd weigh it later for clipping.

"You have a gift for animals," she said. It was not a question, and it was not a compliment. It was a sentence laid on the table between them like a card turned face-up, and Sebastian's blood went cold, and then colder when she added, lightly, "Most handlers do. It's nothing mystical. Feed them, speak gently, they follow you anywhere." She smiled. "Isn't that so, Master Winterbourne?"

"That's so," he said.

He excused himself at the earliest decent moment and walked, very slowly and very calmly, to the privy, where he stood in the dark and shook for the better part of a minute.

* * *

That night he did something he had not done in eleven years. He went down to the cellar and asked the rats for help.

Not for gossip. For strategy.

"If she reports me," he said, "what happens? Think like a ledger. What's the entry?"

Grey-Nose sat on the flour sack beside him, tail curled around her feet, and considered the question with the gravity of an auditor herself.

*If she reports you, the hunters come. The hunters take you to the weighing house—there is a weighing house for people now, did you know? They weigh your gift. If it is heavy enough, the pyre. If it is light enough, the draining pens, where they keep the ones whose gifts are useful.* She paused. *Either way, keeper, you do not come back through that door.*

"And if I run tonight, before she reports?"

*Then you confirm everything. A man who runs is a confession with legs.*

Sebastian laughed—a short, ugly sound—and then pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. Above them, faintly, through four floors of stone and timber, he could hear the building's sounds rearrange themselves around the sleeping auditor: her heartbeat, slow and even, third floor east. The scratch of her quill—no. She wasn't asleep. She was awake at midnight, writing.

*She writes every night,* Grey-Nose confirmed. *Long letters. Sealed with grey wax. Sent out with the morning barge.*

Grey wax. Not crown red. Not guild brown. Some third color, some third party, some employer Isolde Marr answered to that was neither the Board of Trade nor the Guildmaster Corvane.

The trap, Sebastian realized, staring into the dark, was not closing around him from one direction. It was closing from several, and he could not see whether the hands that built it belonged to the crown, to Corvane, or to something else entirely—or whether the hands knew about each other.

"Grey-Nose," he said slowly. "The night Corvane bargained. The night he lost something. You said it happened down here. In the deep places under the hill."

*Under the guild,* she said. *The old foundations go further down than the new ones, keeper. Much further. There are rooms down there that are not on any ledger.*

"Have you been in them?"

A silence. The longest she had ever kept.

*Once,* she said finally. *When I was young and stupid. There is a door down there, and on the door there is a carving of a tree with roots on both sides of it—roots going up into the sky and roots going down into the earth. And the door is warm, keeper. Warm like a living thing. And when I put my nose against the crack beneath it, I heard—*

Her whiskers trembled.

*I heard a word. One word, very old, very tired. It said:* ***remember.***

The coal cellar seemed suddenly very small, and very deep, and very far from the surface of the world.

"And Corvane," Sebastian whispered. "Whatever he traded with—is it behind that door?"

*No,* said Grey-Nose. *Whatever he traded with, keeper, is what locked the door.*

Above them, muffled by floors and stone, a floorboard creaked on the third floor east. Footsteps. Crossing to the window. Stopping.

Standing there, Sebastian was certain, looking down into the dark yard, toward the cellar grate, toward him.

He snuffed his candle and sat in the blackness, heart hammering, listening to the woman above listen to the dark below, and understood with perfect clarity that the comfortable double life he had built—ledgers by day, whispers by night, invisible, safe—was already over.

He just didn't yet know who had ended it.
