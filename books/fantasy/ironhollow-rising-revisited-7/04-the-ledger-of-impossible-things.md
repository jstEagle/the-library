## 4. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its strangest accounts in a room with no windows, and Isolde Marr had been given the key on her first morning.

That was the first thing wrong. Auditors were supposed to fight for access. They were supposed to be tolerated like lice—necessary, resented, kept from the soft places. Instead, Guildmaster Corvane had handed her a brass key across his desk with a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes and said, "Everything must be in order before the crown's men arrive. You understand."

She understood. She just didn't believe it.

The archive room sat below the weighing house, down a stair that smelled of tallow and old paper and something else underneath—something animal and faintly sweet, like a den. The lock was good. The hinges were oiled. Someone wanted this room used.

Isolde lit the lamp, set her satchel on the long table, and began.

* * *

The ledgers themselves were beautiful. That was the second thing wrong.

Beautiful books are expensive books, and expensive books mean someone is proud of what's written in them. These were bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, ruled in iron-gall ink so fine it looked printed. Each entry was dated, witnessed, countersigned. Whoever kept these books took pride in them the way a priest takes pride in a psalter.

She started with the current year and worked backward.

*Item: one memory, first kiss of Marguerite Hale, sold to buyer withheld. Price: remission of debt, eleven crowns.*

*Item: two years of life, seller unnamed by request. Buyer: the same. Price: forty crowns and a letter of passage.*

*Item: one name, whole and entire, surrendered voluntarily. Seller: a cooper's son, age nineteen. Price: paid to a third party; ledger records only "satisfied."*

Isolde read that last entry three times. A man had sold his name—not given it up for a disguise, not traded it as an alias, but sold it entire, so that somewhere there was a person walking around whom no document would ever again agree existed. She wrote the page number in her audit book and made herself move on.

There were trades in shadows. Trades in dreams, priced by vividness. One entry recorded the purchase of a woman's grief—"the whole of it, regarding her son"—and the price column said only: *see Corvane.* Another recorded the sale of seven hours, extracted from a sleeping man who would never know they were gone.

By midmorning her neck ached and her tea had gone cold, and she had filled nine pages of notes, and she had not yet found what she was looking for. Because she wasn't really looking for irregularities. Irregularities were her cover story, the reason the crown could justify paying an auditor's fee to a guild it officially trusted.

She was looking for a boy.

* * *

Sebastian Winterbourne. Junior ledger-keeper. Three years in post. Quiet, punctual, unremarkable in every way the guild's own records described him—and that was precisely what had caught Lord Chancellor Veyne's attention, because Veyne did not believe in unremarkable people. He believed in camouflage.

The intelligence file was thin. A hedge-witch hanged in Ashford Ferry eleven years ago, name struck from the record but recoverable if you knew where to dig: Winterbourne, first name expunged, executed under the old statutes for beast-speaking. A son, entered into the Guild of Impossible Things within the month, sponsored by an application no one could later locate. And then nothing. Eleven years of nothing. A man who filed things correctly and spoke to no one and was mentioned in no gossip, which in a building full of clerks was its own kind of miracle.

Veyne had put it to her plainly, in a room where the candles burned without flickering. "The last speaker is a rumor I intend to make a corpse. Find him. Prove him. The hunt needs a face, Marr, or it needs nothing, and I have promised the crown results." He'd slid a purse across the table, heavy enough to buy a house. "You will report to me and no one else. Not the hunters. Not the guild. Me."

So she audited. It was good work, honest-seeming, and it put her in every room in the guild with a legitimate reason to be there. She had watched Sebastian Winterbourne for six days now. He arrived early. He left late. He balanced columns that other clerks got wrong, and he never once looked at the animals in the yard.

That last part she might have missed entirely, if she hadn't spent three evenings pretending to work late herself. Everyone else glanced at the horses when they passed—the big bay gelding, the two draught mares. People look at animals. It cost nothing. But Winterbourne walked past them the way a man walks past a door he has decided never to open again, eyes fixed forward, jaw set, and once—only once—when the bay whinnied behind him, Isolde had seen his shoulders flinch as though the sound had been spoken directly into his ear.

Guilt, she told herself. Or fear. Animals sense both. It proved nothing.

She wrote it down anyway.

* * *

On the fourth day she found the gap.

It was small. It was, in fact, beautiful—which was how she knew it was deliberate. In the year-before-last ledger, between the sale of a haunted mirror and the purchase of a blessing (from whom, the ledger did not say), there was a folio numbered 214 that was followed immediately by folio 216. No 215. No stub torn out, no note of removal, no auditor's mark. Just a clean absence, ruled around neatly, as if the binding itself had agreed to forget.

Every other correction in eleven years of ledgers was documented to the point of tedium. This one omission was the only untidy thing in the entire archive, and it was tidy in the way a lie is tidy.

She checked the index. Folio 215 was listed. *Folio 215: transfer, category withheld, parties withheld, see keeper.* The index pointed at the missing page like a finger pointing at a wall.

Someone had removed it recently. The paper around the gap was still bright where sunlight through the door had never faded it—no, that was wrong too, there was no sunlight down here. The paper was bright where the surrounding pages had aged dark. Fresh removal. Within the season.

Isolde sat back in her chair and listened to the building breathe above her—the groan of beams, the scuff of feet, the distant complaint of the scales in the weighing house. Somewhere overhead, junior ledger-keeper Sebastian Winterbourne was entering figures in his neat hand.

Who removes a page? Either the guildmaster, who owned everything here—or the keeper of the ledgers, who touched everything here. And if it was the keeper, then the quiet little clerk with the flinching shoulders had taken something out of the record of impossible things and hidden it, and there was exactly one category of secret worth hiding from a crown that was burning gifted folk alive.

She closed the ledger carefully, aligning its edges with its brothers on the shelf, and felt the unease settle into her stomach like swallowed stone. Because part of her—the part that had grown up poor in a river town watching magistrates sell verdicts—had read those entries about sold names and bought griefs and thought: *good.* Let people trade their burdens. Let them buy back pieces of themselves. The guild was a fence around a fire, and the fire warmed people the law would otherwise let freeze.

And she was going to hand one of them to the pyre to keep her own seat warm.

*Professional duty,* she wrote in her audit book, in the dry language of her trade. *Discrepancy noted. Further inquiry warranted.* Her pen paused over the word *warranted*. Below it, small enough that she could later tell herself it was an accident, she added: *He balances the books like someone making amends.*

Then she blotted it, hard, and packed her satchel.

* * *

The report went out that night, the way all her reports went out: folded into a duplicate customs manifest, carried by a carter who believed he was hauling paper samples to the capital and never asked why the paper weighed so little and cost so much. By dawn it would be on the Chancellor's desk.

She wrote it in cipher, of course. Plain words for the audit—Corvane's accounts were clean to the point of theater, one folio missing, recently, deliberately. Then the rest, in the personal cipher only Veyne could read:

*Subject continues consistent with profile. Avoids animals. Possible concealment behavior. One folio removed from impossible-things ledger within the season; index references a transfer of withheld category. Recommend pressure applied via guildmaster—subject's patron appears to be negotiating protection. If Corvane is selling, we should be the buyers.*

She hesitated over the last line. Then she sealed it, because hesitation was a luxury she had stopped affording herself years ago, and gave the packet to the carter, and watched the cart roll out the gate into the grey morning.

Behind her, in the yard, the bay gelding lifted its head and looked at her. Not past her. At her. It held the look for a long moment, ears forward, and then turned back to its hay as if dismissing her from some consideration she would never be party to.

Isolde stood in the cold with her hands empty and understood, with the clarity that comes too late to be useful, that she had just done something that could not be audited, corrected, or returned.

Somewhere inside the guild, a kettle began to whistle. Footsteps crossed the yard—Winterbourne, early as always, a ledger under his arm, nodding to her the way he nodded to everyone: politely, briefly, already elsewhere.

"Good morning," he said.

"Good morning," she said, and smiled, and hated how easily it came.

He passed within arm's reach of her, and the bay horse raised its head again and made a low sound—not a whinny, something quieter, almost conversational—and Isolde watched the young man's step falter, just half a beat, before he mastered himself and walked on into the guildhouse without looking back.

She stood in the frost until her fingers went numb, holding her satchel against her chest like a shield, and thought about folio 215, and about what a man might pay to remove one page from the record of impossible things.

Whatever it was, she intended to find it before the crown's hunters arrived.

Even if finding it meant she'd been right all along.
