## 5. Betrayal at the Weighing House

The inventory was scheduled for the first bell after dawn, which meant Sebastian had been awake since the third, checking figures by candlelight and pretending, as he always did on inventory days, that his hands did not shake.

They shook now. Not from the cold. The Weighing House was the warmest room in the guildhall—its great brass scales threw off heat like sleeping animals—but Sebastian's fingers trembled anyway, and he could not make them stop.

The sparrow's words had been circling him for three days. *The hunt has begun.* He'd told himself it was a dying bird's delirium, the last confused flicker of a small mind going out. But last night the cellar rats had come up through the floorboards in numbers he'd never seen, dozens of them, sitting in the dark of the wine cellar with their whiskers trembling, all of them saying the same thing in their small urgent voices: *iron comes, iron comes, iron comes.*

He had counted the ledgers twice. He had checked every seal. He had done everything a careful man does when he is trying very hard not to think about the one thing that matters.

"Ledger-keeper." Isolde Marr's voice came from the doorway, crisp as new paper. She stood there in her grey clerk's coat with a slate under her arm and her dark hair pinned back so severely it looked like discipline made visible. "You're wanted at the main scales. Guildmaster's orders. Full inventory, every account, witnessed."

"Witnessed," Sebastian repeated. "That's unusual."

"The decree is unusual." She crossed the room without waiting for an answer, her boots striking the flagstones in a rhythm too even to be casual. "Everything is witnessed now. You of all people should appreciate thoroughness."

He followed her because there was nothing else to do. That was the trap of being invisible—you couldn't vanish when someone finally decided to look.

* * *

The Weighing House proper was a cathedral of commerce: vaulted ceiling ribbed with black oak, windows of thick green glass that turned daylight the color of pond water, and the great scales themselves, each tall as two men, their pans wide enough to hold a lamb or a coffin. The guild staff were already assembled along the walls—forty clerks and factors and stable-hands, standing in the silence of people who have been told to stand somewhere and are afraid to ask why.

Guildmaster Corvane stood at the head of the room beside a man Sebastian had never seen before.

The stranger wore no livery, but he didn't need to. His boots were crown-issue leather. His cloak was cut in the hunter style, short in back for riding, long in front for concealment. And at his hip, where a factor would carry a weight-set, he carried a long iron rod tipped with a cage of fine wire mesh—and inside the mesh, something small and pale was moving.

Sebastian's stomach dropped through the floor.

It was a wren. A common wren, brown and ordinary, its wings bent wrong where the wire had crushed them. It was looking directly at Sebastian, and it was screaming—not aloud, but in that other voice, the voice only he could hear—a sound like a heart tearing down the middle.

*They caught me they caught me they caught me they're using me they're using me to find you—*

The gift-detector. Of course. The crown had learned what the old stories said: that a caged songbird's terror resonates against a speaker's gift like a plucked string against its twin. Bring a frightened bird into a room, and any hidden speaker would feel it scream.

Sebastian felt it. God help him, he felt it like a knife between his ribs.

And he could not stop his face from doing what faces do.

He saw the moment the hunter saw it. The man's eyes—pale, patient, the eyes of a man who fishes with live bait—moved across the assembled staff, settled on Sebastian, and stayed.

"There," the hunter said quietly. "The ledger-keeper."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, but Sebastian's mind recorded it in terrible slow detail, the way it recorded everything—the sums, the seals, the small betrayals of arithmetic.

Two more hunters stepped out from behind the pillars where they'd been waiting. Crossbows, loaded. The staff scattered back against the walls with a sound like wind through wheat. Isolde Marr moved to the hunter's side with her slate held against her chest, and she was not looking at Sebastian with triumph or with pity but with something worse: professional satisfaction, faintly troubled, like an auditor who has found the discrepancy she was hired to find.

"Sebastian Winterbourne," the lead hunter said, reading from a warrant. "By decree of the Crown, you are apprehended under the Gift-Hunt statute. You will be conveyed to the capital for assessment." He paused, and something almost gentle entered his voice, the gentleness men use for livestock. "Cooperation is noted favorably in the record."

They bound his wrists with cord. They took the keys from his belt. One of them began walking him toward the far door, the one that opened onto the delivery yard where the wagons waited.

And Sebastian, moving in that slow terrible clarity, looked out through the green glass at the yard beyond—at the six great cart-horses stamping in their traces, tossing their heads, their breath steaming in the cold morning air—

—and he stopped pretending.

He had spent thirty-one years pretending. Pretending the horses liked him for his apples. Pretending the rats spoke in coincidence. Pretending he was a man who merely tolerated beasts rather than a man who heard them, who knew their names, who had sat with dying animals in the dark and listened to their last thoughts the way other men listen to music.

He reached out with everything he had.

*Run.*

Not a word, really. Not even a thought shaped like a word. It was older than words—all of it at once, the whole desperate flood of him: *run, fire, wolves, run, they will hurt you, run, my friends, RUN—*

The yard exploded.

Later, the clerks would tell it differently—that a dog had slipped in, that a wagon backfired, that the horses simply went mad as horses do. But Sebastian knew the truth, and the truth was that six draft horses weighing half a ton each heard him, believed him, and obeyed.

They went forward together, all six teams at once, harness snapping taut, and the first wagon lurched clean over an axle. The gates—old oak, iron-banded—did not so much break as cease to exist. Hunters dove aside. Someone was shouting. A crossbow bolt buried itself in the doorframe a hand's width from Sebastian's ear, and then the crowd of panicking bodies closed around him like water, and he did the only thing left: he ran.

Someone grabbed his collar. He twisted out of his coat and kept running, shirt and all, down the delivery ramp, past the shattered gate, into the street, into the maze of the lower town where the guild's oldest cellars connected to drains that connected to the river, a geography he knew better than anyone alive because the rats had shown him, years ago, drawing him maps in the dark with their small patient voices: *this way, this way, follow us, this way.*

He ran. Behind him the hunters' shouts multiplied. Above him the sky was the color of tin. Ahead of him a grate stood open—open, he would remember later, impossibly, conveniently open—and the rats were pouring out of it like spilled ink, streaming past his feet, and their voices rose around him in a chorus:

*Down, down, they can't follow, down, quick, quick—*

He went down.

* * *

The sewer was exactly as bad as sewers are, and Sebastian discovered he did not care.

He waded through thigh-deep water in the dark, one hand on the wall, following the rats' voices as they flickered ahead of him like lanterns: *left here, left, the low tunnel floods at tide, take the high one.* His bare feet found broken glass and he barely registered it. Somewhere above, muffled by stone and earth, he could hear the hunt organizing—horses, horns, the rhythmic knocking of poles against cellar floors, searching.

They would search the guild first. Then the lower town. Then, methodically, relentlessly, everywhere.

He found a dry junction where three tunnels met, and he hauled himself onto the stone lip and sat with his ruined feet in the water, shaking so hard his teeth chattered, and he made himself think.

Think, Winterbourne. You're good at thinking. It's the only thing you've ever been allowed to be good at.

Fact one: they knew his name. Not suspicion—certainty. *Sebastian Winterbourne,* read from a warrant, sealed in red wax. Warrants required evidence. Evidence required testimony.

Fact two: the trap had been built for him specifically. The wren, the witnessed inventory, the hunters positioned behind pillars before the staff ever assembled. Someone had told them the exact right bait for the exact right man.

Fact three—and this was the one that hurt, the one he kept turning over like a coin hoping it would come up different—only three people could have known. Himself. Isolde Marr, who had watched him too closely for two months and whose audit questions had circled, always circled, back to the beast accounts, the feed ledgers, the hours he kept in the stables.

And Corvane.

His mind shied away from it. Tried again. Shied again. Finally he forced himself to look at it straight, the way he forced himself to look at a bad column of numbers, because a lie in a ledger doesn't get smaller if you don't read it.

Isolde was clever, but she was new. New clerks don't know which bait works. New clerks don't know that a ledger-keeper visits the stables at night. New clerks don't know about the cellar, about the rats, about the particular shape of Sebastian's loneliness.

Corvane knew all of it. Corvane had known for years—had perhaps always known, had let Sebastian keep his little secret the way a man lets a mouse live in his pantry, unbothered, until the day the mouse becomes useful.

And the decree had offered protection to any institution that surrendered a speaker. Exemption from seizure. Continuity of charter. The guild's survival, purchased with one clerk's life.

*He sold me,* Sebastian thought, and the thought arrived without shock, which was somehow worse than shock. *He sold me the way I sell anything else. At a fair price. Witnessed and sealed.*

He put his face in his hands and, for the first time since childhood, wept—not loudly, because the walls had ears of their own down here, but silently, thoroughly, the way water finds every crack.

The rats gathered around him. Dozens of them, sitting in the dark on the wet stone, their small bodies warm against his legs. They didn't try to comfort him—they weren't built for comfort—but they stayed, and their presence said what they could say: *here, here, you are here, we are here.*

When the tears were spent, he lifted his head. His eyes had adjusted to the dark, and far down the eastern tunnel, where the brickwork gave way to older stone—stone older than the city, older than the guild, older than anything with a name—he could see movement. Small shapes. Birds, somehow, underground: starlings, roosting in a seam of the ancient wall, watching him.

And beneath their ordinary bird-noise, beneath the rats and the drip of water and the distant horns of the hunt, he heard something else. A whisper rising out of the old stone itself, out of whatever the city was built on top of and had forgotten. It wasn't words, exactly. It was older than words. It was a direction.

*East. Out. Follow the beasts. East.*

The sparrow's warning. The rats' iron. Now this. Everything wild and small and hunted was pointing the same way, like compass needles finding north.

Sebastian stood. His feet bled. His coat was gone, his keys were gone, his life was gone—every careful wall of it, every quiet ledgered year, seized and signed for in red wax.

But he was still alive, and he could still hear them, and they were telling him where to go.

"East," he said aloud, just to hear a human voice say it. It echoed strangely off the ancient stone, as if the word had been spoken in this place many times before, by many mouths, all of them fleeing.

He turned east, and the rats poured ahead of him into the dark like an honor guard, and above him the hunt rode out in every direction except the right one.

For now.
