## 8. Three Keystones, Three Debts

Sebastian did not sleep that night. He sat in the small stone room Old Wren had given him, beneath a roof thatched with river-reed and something older—willow wands woven in patterns he almost recognized from his mother's letters—and he listened.

That was the trouble. Listening was all he had ever done, and now the listening had become a burden too heavy to set down.

Ironhollow sang at night. Not with music, but with voices: the low grumble of goats complaining about the cold, the drowsy murmur of bees in their skeps even in winter, the dream-talk of the ward itself—a vast slow hum like a hearth-fire thinking. And beneath it all, threaded through everything like wire through wax, the Covenant Stone's silence. A cracked bell of nothing. He could feel it the way you feel a missing tooth: not the absence itself but the shape of the gap, aching.

*You are Winterbourne,* Old Wren had said, as if that explained anything. As if blood were a map and he'd simply never learned to read it.

He pressed his palms against his eyes until sparks bloomed. Somewhere below, a dog turned over in its sleep and muttered about rabbits.

"You're supposed to be resting," said a voice at the door—the human kind, dry as autumn leaves. Old Wren stood there with a clay cup steaming in her hands. She was small and bent and her eyes missed nothing, which Sebastian found exhausting. "Drink this. It won't help. But drinking things is what people do when they can't sleep, and I find the ritual steadies them."

He took the cup. Bitterroot and honey. He drank because she was right about rituals.

"The Stone showed you," he said. "Showed me. Whatever." His voice came out rough. "It doesn't matter what my grandfather's grandfather did. The covenant is broken. The blight is eating the forests, the crown is burning people like me, and I'm one clerk who's good with numbers. I balance ledgers. That's my gift, if we're being honest. The other thing—the hearing—that's just... a curse I've spent thirty years pretending isn't there."

Old Wren lowered herself onto the stool by the door with the care of someone whose joints kept their own accounts. She watched him for a long moment.

"When the Stone cracked," she said, "it didn't die. A broken word still has weight. It fell into three pieces, and the pieces fell into three hands, and those hands closed around them and would not let go."

"Three pieces."

"Keystones. The covenant was built on three promises, boy. Three debts, if you like—the old tongue has no word for *promise* that isn't also a word for *debt*. One keystone for each." She held up fingers, knuckles like river stones. "The first went into the water. The drowned court beneath Lake Vess took it, and they have kept it for four hundred years, and they do not give gifts. They trade."

Sebastian stared into his cup. "And the second?"

"Into fire. The Cinder Reach. There is an ash-giant there who was old when the covenant was young. He counts graves. All the graves. He holds the second keystone among his dead, and he will want a toll."

"And the third?" He already knew. He'd known since the Stone had shown him its own breaking, since he'd seen a younger Corvane kneeling in a vault with something glowing caught between his teeth like a thief with a jewel. "Corvane."

"Your guildmaster swallowed the third keystone whole," Old Wren said, without softening it. "It sits behind his ribs even now. It is what made him rich and it is what is killing him, though he's too greedy to know the difference between power and poison. To reforge the covenant, you must take it out of him."

"He'll never give it up."

"No," she agreed. "He won't."

* * *

Dawn came gray through the reed-thatch, and Sebastian walked.

He told himself he was walking to clear his head, but his feet had their own opinions, and they carried him down through Ironhollow's winding lanes to the hollow's edge, where the ward stood. You couldn't see it exactly—you saw *through* it, the pine forest beyond, the frost-silvered bracken—but you felt it, a pressure like standing under deep water, and the beasts of the town gathered near it the way people gather near a warm wall in winter.

A goat regarded him sideways. *The new one,* it observed to no one in particular. *Smells of ink and fear.*

"I don't smell of fear," Sebastian muttered, then realized he'd answered aloud, then realized the goat hadn't spoken aloud either, and laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it.

An old mare came and stood beside him at the ward's edge. He knew her; she'd carried him half-dead up the beast-paths two nights ago, when the crown hunters' horns were still ringing in his ears and Isolde Marr's face was still ringing louder. Her name was Bracken, and she smelled of hay and liniment and long patience.

*You are loud today,* she said. *Inside, I mean. Your inside-voice is shouting.*

"My inside-voice has a lot to shout about." He leaned his forehead against her neck. Her warmth soaked into him like the bitterroot tea hadn't. "They're going to come here, Bracken. The hunters. Corvane sold me, and Veyne wants a Winterbourne, and this place is full of people who can do things the crown burns people for. I led them straight to the last free sanctuary in the realm. I'm a plague walking on two legs."

Bracken considered this. Horses consider slowly and thoroughly; it is one of their virtues.

*When the cart broke your father's leg—you remember?*

He flinched. "I was nine."

*You sat with him in the road and you asked me to walk carefully to fetch the surgeon, and I understood every word, and I walked carefully.* She blew a breath through her nostrils, warm against his hair. *You did not make the cart break. You did not make the men follow you. You only spoke, and the world answered. That is not guilt. That is just being heard.*

"That's very wise for a horse."

*I am forty-one. I am wiser than everyone in this town except the bees.*

Despite everything, something in his chest loosened. He scratched her forelock, and she leaned into it, and for a moment he was only a man scratching a horse, and the ledger of his life balanced, briefly, at zero.

Then the moment passed, and the arithmetic resumed. Three keystones. Three debts. A drowned court that traded in memories. A giant who counted graves. A corrupt old man with a stolen star lodged in his chest.

And somewhere south, hunters sharpening their knives, guided by a traitor's maps.

*You are deciding,* Bracken observed.

"Yes."

*Deciding badly takes longer than deciding well. Decide.*

He looked past her, through the shimmer of the ward, at the forest where the blight had begun to show—even here, even this far north, a stand of pines on the far ridge gone gray and glassy, calcified mid-sway, like a photograph of a forest instead of a forest. The covenant's collapse, poisoning root and crown alike. Poisoning both realms. Poisoning everything except, apparently, his ability to feel sorry for himself.

He thought of the sparrow dying on the guildhouse cobbles, months ago now, whispering *the hunt has begun* while he'd patted its feathers and told himself it was nothing. He thought of his mother's letters curling to ash in the grate. He thought of Isolde Marr's careful, watching eyes, and how the worst betrayals always wore a friendly face.

He thought of the rats in the cellar, who had told him once, chittering in the dark: *the big man upstairs bargained with us, long ago, and lost something he cannot name, and so he hates us.* Even Corvane had been a speaker once, or something like one. Even Corvane had paid a price. Everyone in this story was paying prices. The only question left was whose price would buy back the world.

"All right," Sebastian said quietly. "All right."

*All right what?* asked Bracken.

"I'm going to Lake Vess."

*Good,* said the goat, from across the lane. *The fish-people owe me a favor. Tell them Gertie sends regards.*

"Gertie, I'm not telling the otter-queen you send regards."

*Then I shall be insulted, and it will be your fault, and I will hold it over you for years. Go safely, ink-and-fear.*

*Walk carefully,* said Bracken, which was, he understood, the highest blessing her people could confer.

* * *

Old Wren was waiting for him at her door, as if she'd known the exact minute his decision would finish cooking. Perhaps she had. She pressed a bundle into his arms: a wool cloak, a knife with a bone handle, a pouch of dried fish and hardtack, and a strip of birch bark covered in cramped script.

"A letter of debt-recognition, in the old tongue," she said. "Show it to Nesharra of the drowned court and she will know you come speaking seriously. The ash-giant needs no letters; he needs only your honesty, and woe to you if you bring less. And the third keystone—" her mouth twisted, "—needs no introduction at all."

"Old Wren." He swallowed. "I'm a clerk. I've never fought anything bigger than a filing error. What makes you think I can do this?"

She looked at him for a long time, and her eyes were the color of the Covenant Stone before it cracked—he was sure of it now, sure in his bones.

"Because the covenant wasn't forged by a warrior," she said. "It was forged by a listener. Someone who sat down across from powers that could have eaten him and *heard* what they actually wanted, underneath the roaring. That's the whole secret, boy. Every bargain ever struck, every war ever avoided—it comes down to listening past the teeth." She tapped his chest with one crooked finger. "You've been doing it your whole life in cellars and stables. Now go do it in palaces and grave-mounds."

"And if I fail?"

"Then the blight finishes its work, the crown falls, the wild places fall, and whatever crawls out of the ruin inherits the ashes." She shrugged. "So I'd recommend not failing. It focuses the mind wonderfully."

He almost smiled. Almost.

"One more thing." Her voice dropped. "The hunters will come here whether you're found or not. Veyne wants Ironhollow broken. When they come, we will hold. But the longer you keep them chasing you, the more of our strength stays unspent. Run loudly, Winterbourne. Make yourself worth following."

"That," Sebastian said, shouldering his pack, "is the first instruction anyone's given me that I'm naturally gifted at."

* * *

He left through the beast-paths at first light, Bracken carrying him as far as the high pass, where the snow began and horses' hooves became a liability. She stood in the falling white and watched him go, and he did not look back, because looking back is how you turn resolve into regret.

By the third day he was alone in the foothills above Lake Vess, and the lake lay below him like a coin dropped in a bowl of mountains—gray-green, vast, patient. Wind moved across it and it did not ripple quite the way water should. Something beneath the surface was breathing.

He took out the birch-bark letter and read it again, though he had it memorized. Then he took out his ledger-book—the one habit of his old life he couldn't shed—and on a fresh page, in his neat clerk's hand, he wrote:

*Account of Sebastian Winterbourne, keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, debtor.*
*Owed: three keystones, to be recovered at any lawful price.*
*Assets: one forbidden gift, one letter of debt, one knife, dried fish (insufficient).*
*Liabilities: grief, fear, a traitor's face I cannot stop seeing, and the strong likelihood of drowning.*

He looked at the entry for a long moment. Then, beneath it, in smaller script, he added the line he'd been avoiding since Old Wren's fireside:

*Terms: the maker's heir accepts the maker's debts. Payment begins at once.*

Below him, the lake exhaled. Far out from shore, the water bulged—gently, deliberately, unmistakably—and sank again. And rising up through the wind came a sound that no ordinary ear could have caught, but that landed in Sebastian's skull like a hook finding flesh: a voice like wet silk drawn over glass, amused and ancient and hungry.

*Well, well,* said the drowned court. *The land-people have sent us a Winterbourne at last. Come down, little clerk. We have been keeping your family's collateral for four hundred years—and Nesharra is *so* eager to discuss the interest.*
