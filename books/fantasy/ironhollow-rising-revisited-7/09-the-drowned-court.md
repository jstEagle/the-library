## 9. The Drowned Court

The road to Lake Vess took four days, and Sebastian spent all of them listening.

That was the trouble with the beast-paths Old Wren had shown him: once you knew to listen for them, you could not stop. The world was a chorus now instead of a murmur. Crows argued over a dead hare in the hedgerow and he caught every syllable of their squabbling. A fox trotted alongside him for an hour at dusk, not speaking, just *being* beside him, its presence a warm amber note in the back of his skull like a hearth-fire heard through a wall. Even the worms in the rain-softened earth had opinions, low and patient as monks chanting.

He had never known how loud the world was. He had spent thirty years pressing his gift flat beneath ledgers and silence, and now that it was unrolled, now that he needed it, he understood what he'd been smothering all this time.

*You grieve too loud,* a doe told him on the second morning, from across a meadow, without lifting her head from the grass. *The dead do not need your grief. The living need your feet.*

He walked faster after that.

Lake Vess announced itself before he saw it — a hush in the air, a flattening of sound, as if the whole valley were holding its breath. Then the trees opened and there it was: a sheet of grey-green water so still it looked poured rather than gathered, ringed with reeds gone silver in the late light. Mist lay on it in long ropes. No birds crossed it. No fish rose. Even the wind gave it a wide berth.

Sebastian stood at the shore and felt his skin prickle.

*Down here,* something said, and the voice came up through the soles of his boots, through the wet ground itself, through the roots of the reeds. It was a voice like water over stones, layered, many-throated, old. *Down here, ledger-keeper. We have been waiting since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to fear us.*

"I'm not—" He stopped. Corrected himself. There was no point lying to things that could taste truth the way fish tasted current. "I'm not a keeper anymore."

*No,* the lake agreed, and he felt amusement move through it, vast and cold and not unkind. *You are a beggar. Beggars we know how to deal with.*

* * *

There was a way down. Of course there was; the beasts had whispered of it for three days, each telling slightly different, all of them true. A drowned bell-tower stood a quarter mile along the southern shore, only its crown breaking the surface, crusted white with droppings and lichen. Beside it, half-hidden by reeds, a seam of dark water moved against the current — a spring feeding up from below, or a throat swallowing down.

*The otter-road,* a heron had told him, preening, incurious. *Cold, long, and honest. The court does not abide liars, but it abides everything else.*

Sebastian stripped off his coat, his boots, his shirt, until he stood in his linen smallclothes with his satchel wrapped in oilcloth and bound tight to his back with cord. In the satchel: bread, a flint, Old Wren's map drawn on hide, and nothing else worth drowning for. He hesitated a moment over the thought of leaving everything on the shore and walking away. Four days ago he'd have called this madness. But four days ago he'd still believed the guild was home.

He waded in.

The cold was total. It did not bite so much as *claim*, closing over him like a fist, and then the current took him — the downward pull of the spring-throat, gentle and insistent as a hand on his wrist — and the grey light folded shut above his head.

He had expected terror. What he got was the singing.

The lake spoke underwater the way cathedrals spoke under stone: in resonance. Every bubble that left his lips rang like a struck bell. The reeds' roots hummed bass notes. Somewhere far below, voices threaded together in a music that had no words but carried meaning anyway — *come down, come down, come and be weighed* — and Sebastian, who had spent his whole life translating between species, found he didn't need to translate at all. The water did it for him. Water wanted things simply. Water wanted him lower.

His lungs began to burn. He kicked into the dark, following the pull, and just when the burning tipped over into panic, the current set him down — actually *set* him, laid him on smooth stone like a mother laying down a sleeping child — and pushed him upward into air.

He broke the surface gasping in a cavern lit by pale green-gold light that came from nowhere and everywhere. Stalactites dripped overhead. The chamber was the size of the guild's great hall, and its floor was shallow water over white sand, and rising out of the shallows on every side stood pillars of woven reed and bone and river-glass, hung with things that glinted: coins, keys, buckles, a knight's spur, a child's shoe, a wedding ring. Everything the lake had ever taken. Everything the lake kept.

And on a throne of stacked river-stones sat the queen.

She was an otter the size of a woman, sleek and brown and silver-whiskered, wearing a mantle of mussel shells that clicked softly when she moved. Her eyes were black and bright and utterly without mercy, and when she looked at Sebastian, he felt weighed — truly weighed, the way he weighed goods at the guild, except that she was weighing *him*, and finding the sum interesting.

*Nesharra,* the whole chamber breathed, in a hundred small voices. *Nesharra, Queen Below, Keeper of the Drowned Court, She Who Remembers What the Land Forgets.*

"Your Majesty." His voice cracked. He knelt in the cold shallows because standing felt insolent. "I've come to bargain."

*I know why you've come.* Her voice, alone now, was dry and quick, like pebbles rattling in a current. *Old Wren's whelp. The Winterbourne. Come crawling for a keystone.* She turned her head sideways, whiskers twitching. *Do you know what a keystone is, little clerk? Not a rock. A promise. Your ancestor made three promises to hold the covenant together, and each promise was given flesh so it could not be forgotten or forged. My court holds the first. The Promise of Waters — that the wild would always have a place in human hearts, and humans a place in wild memory.*

"And I need it back. To reforge—"

*To reforge what was broken. Yes. Everyone knows the story.* Her tail flicked water. *What no one asks is why the promises were made solid at all. Because words fade, clerk. Words are cheap. Flesh is dear. If you want the promise back, you must pay what flesh costs.*

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry despite the water everywhere. "I don't have gold."

*We have more gold than your crown has ever minted.* She gestured with one clawed hand at the glittering hoards on the pillars. *We have crowns. We have swords. We have the bones of kings who thought gold was the coin of last resort.* She leaned forward, and the mussel-shell mantle rippled. *The court trades in one currency only. Memory. True memory, freely given, fully surrendered. You will not remember it afterward. It will be ours, entire, and it will live here forever in the deep places where nothing is lost.*

The cold went through him deeper than the water ever had.

"What memory?" he asked, though he already knew. Some part of him had known since the heron. Since the shore.

*That is your choice,* Nesharra said. *Choose well. Choose poorly. The price is the same either way: it must be true, and it must be yours, and once given it is gone.*

* * *

He asked for time, and she granted it, because courts are patient. He sat on a shelf of dry stone at the chamber's edge while the green-gold light pulsed like a slow heartbeat, and he went through his life the way he went through a ledger, line by line, looking for the entry he could bear to lose.

His father teaching him to whistle to the plough-horses, before his father knew what the whistling meant to the horses — no. Too early. Too foundational. Take that and he took the boy he'd been.

The night he first heard the cellar rats and laughed instead of screaming — no. That was the night he learned his gift could be joy.

The sparrow dying in his hands, saying *the hunt has begun*, and him dismissing it — he flinched away from that one. Guilt was heavy but guilt was load-bearing. Remove it and what held his resolve up?

And then, inevitably, his mother.

He had burned her letters two weeks ago, in the guild yard, watching twenty years of her careful handwriting curl to ash because the herald's red wax decree had made even paper dangerous. He had thought, standing there, that the burning was the worst thing he would ever do to her memory.

But the letters were ash. Ash was already loss. What remained was the memory underneath, and the memory was this:

A kitchen in winter. He was seven. Snow at the windows and the fire low because coal cost money. His mother at the table with her sewing, and him on the floor with the family cat asleep across his legs, and he was telling her — whispering, because whispering was safe — what the cat dreamed. Fields, the cat dreamed of, and birds made of ribbon, and a door in a hedge that opened onto summer. And his mother had put down her sewing and looked at him for a long moment, and he had braced for fear, because he was seven and already knew that what he could do made people afraid.

Instead she had smiled. Not the tight smile adults gave children. A real one, tired and crooked and lit from inside, and she'd said, *"Then you'll always have somewhere to go, won't you? Even when the snow comes."*

And she had never told anyone. Never once, in all the years after, all the years of his hiding, had she betrayed him, though betrayal would have been safer for her. She had carried his secret the way she carried everything — quietly, and without complaint, and until it killed her.

That was the memory. That was the one. The single proof, held nowhere else in the world, that someone had known what he was and loved him anyway.

If he gave it up, he would still know the fact of her love the way he knew entries in a ledger — recorded, attested, but not *felt*. He would know she had smiled. He would not remember the smile.

*Well?* Nesharra's voice came soft across the water, and for the first time there was something in it that was almost gentleness. *The court does not rush its debtors. But neither does it wait forever.*

Sebastian looked at the queen, at her black bright eyes, and understood suddenly that she was not cruel. She was *honest*. The lake took things; that was what lakes did. Boats, children, rings, memories. It did not pretend otherwise. The crown pretended. The guild pretended. Corvane had stolen covenant power and called it stewardship. At least the drowned court wrote its terms plainly and let you read them before you signed.

"You'll keep it safe," he said. "Truly safe. Forever?"

*Nothing given to the deep is ever lost,* Nesharra said. *It will be sung in the dark places when your name is forgotten. On the day the covenant is reforged, perhaps — perhaps — such things may be returned to their givers. The old law provides for it. No one has ever tested it.* Her whiskers quivered. *Will you test it, Winterbourne? Or will you swim home empty?*

He thought about Ironhollow. About Old Wren's face when she'd touched the cracked Covenant Stone. About the calcified forest Isolde had described — no, he hadn't heard that yet; he thought about the blight creeping through the wild places, the hunt, the pyres, the menagerie cages he could imagine all too clearly. About a world going quiet, voice by voice, species by species, until nothing was left to hear.

Against that: one kitchen. One winter. One smile.

"There's no other way," he said quietly. "Is there."

*There is always another way,* Nesharra said. *It is just worse.*

He laughed — a short, broken sound that echoed strangely off the river-glass pillars — and stood, and waded out into the center of the chamber where the water reached his waist, and faced the queen.

"The kitchen," he said. "Winter. My seventh year. My mother and the cat and the dream of fields. That's my payment. Freely given."

Nesharra rose from her throne of stones, and the whole court rose with her — shapes in the dark water, dozens of them, sleek heads surfacing in a silent ring around him, black eyes reflecting the green-gold light. The queen swam to him through the shallows, unhurried, ceremonial, and stopped close enough that he could see the fine scars on her muzzle, the age in her, the weight of centuries of bargains kept.

*Then give.*

He closed his eyes and did the hardest thing he had ever done: he opened the memory instead of holding it. He let it rise — the snow-light, the sewing basket, the cat's slow purr, the ribbon-birds, the crooked tired smile, *then you'll always have somewhere to go* — and he let it flow out of him like warmth flowing out of bare skin into cold water, and the court drank it in, and it was agony, and it was relief, and it was over.

The silence where it had been was not empty. It was worse than empty. It was a room with furniture missing — he knew a memory had lived there, knew its shape, its weight, its importance, the way you know a missing tooth. He knew his mother had smiled at his gift. He could not remember the smile. He reached for it and found smooth walls, polished by the lake, and behind them, faintly, distantly, something singing in a voice like water over stones.

His knees hit the sand. He was crying and hadn't noticed starting.

*It is done,* Nesharra said, and her voice was quiet now, almost kind. *Rise, giver. Rise and receive.*

One of the ring of otters swam forward bearing something on a woven mat of reeds. Nesharra lifted it in both paws and pressed it into his shaking hands: a keystone the size of a loaf, warm despite the water, carved from some material that was not quite stone and not quite amber, shot through with veins of moving silver. When his fingers closed around it, he felt the promise humming inside it — *the wild shall have a place in human hearts* — felt it the way he felt the beasts' voices, and understood that he was holding a piece of the world's word given flesh.

*Three payments make a covenant,* Nesharra said. *You have made one. Two remain, and they will cost more than this. They always do. The second debt is owed in the Cinder Reach, to one who counts graves. Go to him with an open mouth and a closed wit — honesty is the only coin he honors.* She turned back toward her throne, then paused. *Clerk. Winterbourne. Whatever your name becomes.*

He looked up. His eyes were still wet.

*The memory is beautiful,* she said. *We will sing it well.*

* * *

The current carried him up. He barely remembered the ascent — the dark, the ringing bubbles, the grey light opening above him like a wound — and then he was on the southern shore, on his hands and knees in the reeds, coughing lake water, the keystone wrapped in oilcloth against his chest and beating there like a second heart.

Dusk had fallen while he was below. Mist lay on the water. Somewhere a nightjar called, and he heard it — of course he heard it, he would always hear everything now — and waited for the comfort the hearing usually brought.

It didn't come. The nightjar's voice was beautiful and he felt nothing but the hollow, the smooth-walled absence where his mother's smile used to live. He sat in the cold reeds and tried to reconstruct the kitchen from facts alone: winter, fire low, cat, sewing, snow. Seven years old. She said something kind. She said—

Nothing. Polished walls. Faint singing.

He had burned her letters to keep her safe. Now he had sold her smile to save the world, and the world did not feel saved. It felt like a debt ledger with two entries remaining, and he was learning, line by line, what the covenant had always cost the people who maintained it. His ancestor had paid something too. Old Wren had never said what. He was beginning to understand why she hadn't.

Sebastian dressed by touch, shouldered his satchel, and turned east, toward the Cinder Reach, where the mountains smoked on the horizon like banksed fires and something enormous was waiting to hear him confess.

Behind him, far below the still grey water, faint and sweet and utterly unreachable, something sang his mother's memory to the dark.
