## 10. Ash and Appetite

The road to Lake Vess smelled wrong before Isolde saw anything wrong with it.

She had been three days behind Sebastian Winterbourne—three days of cold campsites and half-eaten meals and the particular silence of a man who knew how to move through country that wanted to hide him. She'd tracked better fugitives. She'd tracked worse ones who were harder to find. But Winterbourne left no sign a normal tracker could read, and the signs he did leave were the kind she couldn't put in a report: grass bent toward his path like it was listening, crows sitting in rows along fence posts facing the direction he'd gone, a farm dog that had watched her pass without barking, which was its own kind of testimony.

Beasts liked him. That was the gift, or part of it. And beasts were telling her exactly where he was, because they didn't understand that she was the enemy.

She rode through the last village before the lake at midmorning and bought bread from a woman whose hands shook. Not fear-shaking. Something else.

"How long," Isolde asked, "has the forest been quiet?"

The woman wrapped the bread twice over, as if one layer weren't enough to keep something out. "Since spring. Birds went first. Then the deer stopped coming down to the fields." She wouldn't look east, toward the treeline. "My husband says the trees are dying. I say it's worse than dying."

"What's worse?"

The woman finally looked up, and Isolde saw that her eyes were red-rimmed in the way of someone who had cried recently and often. "Dying things rot. These don't. My son found a stag in the wood last month, stiff as a fence post, gray as ash, and not one worm in it. Not one fly. The crows wouldn't touch it." She pushed the bread across the counter like she wanted it gone. "You're crown, aren't you? I can tell by the boots."

"I'm an auditor," Isolde said, which was true, and let the woman draw whatever conclusions she liked.

"Then audit this," the woman said. "Whatever you're hunting out there—it isn't just him. Something's eating the world from the edges in, and your king's men ride past it every day like it's weather." She turned away. "Good luck to you. You'll need more than we've got."

Isolde rode east.

* * *

She caught her first real sight of the blight an hour past noon, where the King's Road crossed a stream called the Alder Water, and she stopped her horse so abruptly that the animal sidestepped and blew hard through its nostrils.

On the near bank, everything was green. Alders, willow herb, moss on the stones, the ordinary clutter of a living waterway. On the far bank, everything was gray.

Not dead-gray. Not winter-gray. Gray the way iron is gray, the way old statues are gray—a mineral color, a color that belonged to things that had never been alive at all. The trees on the far side stood in their summer shapes, leaves and all, but the leaves were stone. She could see the grain of them, the veins, preserved perfectly, the way a fossil keeps the shape of a fern without keeping any fern in it.

And between the trees, mid-stride, stood a doe.

It had been running when it changed. Its forelegs were lifted, its head turned back toward something behind it, mouth open—and the open mouth was what undid her. Because around the mouth, the stone had cracked. As if whatever had been inside the animal when it froze had been trying to get out at the very end, and the stone hadn't been able to contain the shape of the scream.

Her horse refused the ford. It planted its feet at the water's edge and would not cross, and when she urged it, it turned its head and looked at her—not a horse-look, not the dumb sideways glance of an animal avoiding a stick. It looked at her the way a person looks at someone about to walk into a burning house.

"I know," she said quietly. "I know."

She dismounted and led it upstream until she found a place where the blight's edge narrowed to a finger of gray reaching across the water, and she crossed there, on stones, leading the horse wide around the frozen doe because she could not make herself look at it again and could not make herself stop looking.

The gray country was silent in a way no forest is ever silent. Living woods have a floor of sound—insects, wind in leaves, the small industrious rustle of things going about their business. This had none. Her footsteps sounded like hammer blows. When her horse breathed, she heard it from six feet away.

Half a mile in, she found the fox.

It was small, a vixen by the slim skull, and it was not fully stone. That was the first thing she understood, crouching over it with her hand on her knife: the change wasn't instant. It moved through a body like frost moves through a windowpane, from the edges inward. The fox's hindquarters were gray and rigid, fused to the ground where it had collapsed, but its forequarters still moved—shallow, rapid breathing, ribs fluttering under fur that was going stiff patch by patch, silver creeping up its spine like spilled mercury running uphill.

One amber eye found her.

Isolde had killed a great many things in her career. She had been trained to it early and well, and she had never once lost sleep over it, because the things she killed were, by the terms of her employment, things that needed killing. A blighted animal was a mercy case. Every hunter knew the protocol: anything touched by the wasting was put down clean, burned after, reported in triplicate. The order came from the Chancellery itself, signed Veyne, and it was the only standing order she had never questioned, because obviously—you didn't leave suffering things to suffer.

Her knife was already out. The fox watched it come with the flat, uncomprehending patience of an animal that has never been given a reason to expect anything else from human hands.

And then it made a sound.

Not a yelp. Foxes scream, everyone knows that; it's a horrible sound, a sound like a child in trouble, and she'd heard it a hundred times on night hunts. This was different. This was low, and broken, and rhythmic—a sound she recognized because she had heard it from people, from the dying, from her mother, at the end, in a room that smelled of tallow and lavender. It was the sound of a body working very hard at staying alive and beginning to lose the argument.

The stone had reached its shoulders now. She could see it happening. There was nothing mystical about it up close—that was the horror of it. It looked like disease. It looked like the flesh simply forgetting how to be flesh, cell by cell, surrendering its wetness and warmth for something permanent and cold. The fox was being turned into a monument of itself while it was still inside to feel it.

"Protocol," Isolde said aloud, and her voice sounded obscene in that silence.

The eye held hers. Amber, ringed with black, and in it—not pain, exactly. Pain she could have answered with the knife. What she saw was worse. It was the look of an animal that had decided, somewhere in its small fierce life, that humans were the thing in the world that sometimes helped, and was checking, even now, whether this one might be one of those.

Winterbourne's face rose in her memory unbidden: the ledger-room, the moment before the trap closed, when he had looked at her across the inventory tables with an expression she had catalogued at the time as *guilt* and now understood had been something else entirely. He had known what she was. He had looked at her the way this fox was looking at her. And he had still, in the last second, chosen to plead with the horses instead of pleading with her—as if he'd thought, even then, that she might be reached.

She thought about Lord Chancellor Veyne's study. The map on his wall with the blight-zones marked in gray ink, spreading year over year, decade over decade, and Veyne's manicured finger tapping the largest of them: *This is why we hunt speakers, Mistress Marr. The wild magic is curdling. It poisons everything it touches. We are not persecutors; we are surgeons.* She had believed it. She had built four years of her life on it, brick by brick, report by report, and the structure had borne her weight without complaint until this morning, on a streambank, looking at a doe frozen mid-scream.

Because if the wild magic was curdling—if the blight was the sickness—then why did the blight start at the covenant-lines? Why did the oldest maps show it blooming outward from the exact places where the old pacts had been struck and, according to every archive she'd ever audited, broken? Sickness spreads randomly. This spread along roads. Roads of obligation. Like water finding channels that had been dug for it.

Like something draining through a broken vessel.

The fox shuddered. The gray had reached its neck.

"Oh, hells," Isolde said, and knelt, and did the stupidest thing of her professional life.

She didn't know why she did what she did next. Later she would tell herself it was field improvisation, that some instinct of the trade suggested it, but the truth was simpler and less defensible: she pressed both her bare hands against the fox's chest, over the heart, where the fur was still warm, and she thought—with the whole force of her considerable will—*stay.*

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. She was an auditor. Her gifts were arithmetic and a flawless memory and the ability to lie beautifully in six dialects. Magic was not among her assets, and she had never wanted it to be, because wanting magic in the crown's service was how you ended up on the other side of the hunt.

But she stayed. Kneeling in the gray grass, in the terrible silence, hands on a dying animal's chest, she stayed, and the fox stayed, and the two of them kept each other company while the frost of the world climbed its spine.

It took an hour. She counted her own heartbeats to mark it, the way she counted everything, and somewhere past four thousand the breathing slowed, and slowed, and the amber eye lost its focus, and the last thing that changed was the eye itself—going from amber to gray glass, holding her reflection in it, so that the final image the fox's body preserved was Isolde Marr's face, looking down, doing nothing.

She sat back on her heels.

Then she took out her knife and cut free the patch of fur above the heart—the last warm patch, though it was cooling even as she worked—and wrapped it in oilcloth, and put it in the inner pocket of her coat, over the pocket where she kept her commission papers. Evidence, she told herself. For the report. Tissue samples were standard procedure.

She knew a lie when she heard one, even her own.

* * *

She made camp that night in a hollow at the blight's edge, close enough to hear the silence, far enough to keep green things in sight of the fire. Her horse grazed nervously at the end of a long picket. She wrote her daily report by firelight, in cipher, the way she always did:

*Subject W. tracked to Alder Water crossing, thence north along the lake road. Progress slower than expected—he travels by night, rests by day, avoids all settlements. He is not fleeing blindly. He knows where he is going.*

*Blight observed firsthand at grid reference fourteen. Extent greater than Chancellery estimates. Advise revision of containment protocols. Note: subject W.'s route bends around blight-zones consistently. He can sense them. Possibly he can sense more than that.*

She stopped. Read it back. In the firelight the words looked like what they were: the report of a loyal officer doing her job well. Four years of reports exactly like it. She had been proud of them. She had kept copies.

She thought about Veyne reading this one, in his warm study, tapping the page with one finger. *Good, Mistress Marr. Very good. And the tissue sample?* And she would send him the fox fur, and he would have it examined by his own physicians, and they would confirm what every hunter already knew, and the file would grow thicker, and somewhere in the capital a clerk would copy out the summary line—*blighted fauna destroyed per protocol*—and no one, anywhere in that whole vast apparatus, would write down the truth, which was that a woman had held a dying fox for an hour and done nothing, because the order said kill and her hands had said otherwise, and she had obeyed neither, and had no category for what she had actually done.

*Note,* she wrote, and paused so long the candle guttered.

*Note: requesting access to pre-Chancellery archives. Covenant-era records. Unredacted.*

There. It was done. A single line, buried in cipher, deniable, professional—an auditor asking for source documents was the most natural request in the world. If Veyne read it, it meant nothing. If Veyne read it and grew careful, it would tell her something too.

She sealed the report and did not send it. Not yet. The courier bird came through every third day; there was time. Time to decide whether she was reporting to the Chancellery or merely reporting on it.

In the morning she would take up Winterbourne's trail again. She told herself it was because the assignment mattered, because the last speaker was the key to everything, because duty was duty.

But she lay awake a long time, listening to the silence off to the east, and what she was really thinking about was the eye. Amber, going gray. Holding her face. All the way down into stone, that animal had looked at her like she might be one of the ones who helped.

Somewhere north, a man who could hear what beasts thought was walking toward Lake Vess, carrying whatever it was that Veyne feared badly enough to burn kingdoms over.

She meant to catch him.

She was no longer entirely sure what she would do when she did.
