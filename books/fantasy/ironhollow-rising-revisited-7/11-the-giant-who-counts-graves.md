## 11. The Giant Who Counts Graves

The Cinder Reach announced itself long before Sebastian saw it.

He heard it first—a sound like a thousand small fires whispering in a language made of embers. Not the warm crackle of hearth-flames but something older and hungrier, the voice of a land that had burned so thoroughly it had forgotten how to be anything else. The beasts who had guided him from Ironhollow—a one-eyed crow named Sorrel and a lean grey wolf who refused to give her name—stopped at the edge of the black country and would go no farther.

*The ash-father counts there,* Sorrel told him, hopping along a dead branch. *He counts everything. Every grave. Every debt. Do not lie to him, ledger-keeper. He will know. He always knows.*

*What do I call him?*

Sorrel ruffled her feathers, which was how she shrugged. *Whatever he asks you to call him.*

Then she lifted off into the pale sky, and the wolf pressed her muzzle once against Sebastian's palm—warm breath, a heartbeat of contact—and trotted back toward the green world, leaving him alone at the border of the ash.

Sebastian stood a long moment with his hand still tingling. Nine days out from Ironhollow. One keystone heavy against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with river-reed cord: Nesharra's price, a true memory of his mother, traded away beneath the cold water of Lake Vess. He knew he'd had a mother. He knew her name—Margaret Winterbourne—and that she had smelled of ink and lavender, and that she had died when he was nine. But the memory itself, the living texture of her, the sound of her voice reading aloud on winter nights—that was gone. In its place was a hollow, smooth-sided and aching, like a tooth pulled from the jaw of his mind.

He had learned to stop probing the hollow. Probing it hurt worse than leaving it alone.

*One down,* he told himself, and stepped into the ash.

* * *

The Cinder Reach was what happened when the blight stopped pretending to be patient.

Everywhere else he had traveled since fleeing the guild, the blight worked slowly—trees calcifying over seasons, streams going silent one by one, animals born wrong or not at all. Here it had simply finished. The forest was stone. Not petrified in the gentle geological way, but frozen mid-death: trunks still bearing the grain of bark, leaves still spread on branches, all of it turned to grey mineral hard as iron. A deer stood among the trees, complete and perfect and heavier than a cart, its stone eyes fixed on some threat that had caught it decades ago. Grass lay flattened in rows where wind had bent it, and the wind's shape had been preserved forever in mineral.

And under everything, ash. Fine grey ash that rose around Sebastian's boots with each step and hung in the air and got into his teeth. He wrapped his face in cloth as Old Wren had taught him and walked on, following the crow's directions from memory: *Follow the riverbed until the riverbed forgets it was ever a river. Then climb.*

The dead riverbed led him upward through two days of silence. No birdsong here—there was nothing left for birds to sing about. Even his gift, that constant low murmur of the world's voices he had carried since childhood, came back to him thin and strange, like shouting down a well. The stone deer did not speak. The stone trees did not speak. For the first time since the sparrow died in his hands back at the guild, Sebastian heard almost nothing at all, and discovered that the quiet frightened him more than any voice could have.

On the evening of the second day, the riverbed ended at a cliff of black glass, and Sebastian began to climb.

* * *

He heard the counting before he saw the counter.

It came up through the rock itself, through the soles of his boots and the bones of his legs—a rhythm like a great slow bell being struck, except each strike was a number. *Four hundred and eleven thousand, six hundred and two. Four hundred and eleven thousand, six hundred and three.* The numbers were spoken in a voice like a landslide given patience, and between each number was a pause exactly as long as the number before it, so that the whole performance was a kind of terrible music, grief set to arithmetic.

Sebastian climbed the last of the glass cliff and came out onto a plateau at the top of the world, and there was the giant.

He sat cross-legged at the plateau's center, and he was vast—not the boulder-hurling vastness of stories, but something more unsettling: a figure of ash and cinder compacted into human shape, twenty feet tall if he was an inch, his skin the grey of burnt paper, his eyes two coals banked low. Around him, arranged in concentric rings that stretched to the plateau's edges, were stones. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each stone the size of a fist, each one placed with care, and Sebastian understood with a lurch what they were before the giant spoke.

Graves. A graveyard with no bodies in it, only markers—for every tree calcified in the Reach below, every animal frozen mid-flight, every stream gone dumb. The giant was counting them. He had been counting them for a very long time.

*Four hundred and eleven thousand, six hundred and four.*

The coal-eyes turned and found Sebastian standing at the plateau's rim.

"You are late," the giant said. His voice did not boom; it settled, like ash falling. "The last one who came to me arrived forty years ago. He wanted fire for his forge. I gave it to him. He is a smear on history now." A pause, precisely measured. "You want the stone. Everyone who climbs wants the stone."

"I—" Sebastian's throat was full of ash. "Yes. The second keystone. The covenant needs it. I need it."

"Everyone needs." The giant's head tilted, and cinders sifted from his shoulders. "Need is cheap. Need is what the crown said when it broke the word your family kept. Need is what the guildmaster said when he swallowed his piece of the pact. Need is the excuse the world makes while it burns." He raised one enormous hand, fingers like charred timber, and pointed at the ground before him. "Sit, small keeper of ledgers. I know what you are. The wind told me. The wind tells me everything; it has nothing better to do."

Sebastian sat. The ash was warm beneath him, faintly, like a bed recently vacated.

"You have paid once already," the giant said. "The otter-queen took a memory. I tasted the transaction on the water. A mother." Something moved in the banked coals of his eyes—pity, maybe, or its close cousin. "A fair price. She is honest, for a drowned thing. But I am not Nesharra, and water is soft. Fire is not soft. My toll is different."

"I have little left to trade," Sebastian said quietly. "I've thought about it the whole way here. Coin means nothing to you. Years of my life—you count graves; you know how few I have. I don't even know what you'd want."

"I want," the giant said, "the names."

"The names?"

"Every name," the giant said, "of everyone you have ever failed."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Sebastian to count his own heartbeats. Forty-one of them. He knew because counting was what he did when the world became unbearable—it was the whole architecture of his life, columns and sums, everything balanced, everything accounted for. And now this burning creature wanted him to open the ledger he kept in the place no audit had ever reached.

"That's not a price," he managed. "That's just cruelty."

"It is inventory," the giant said. "I am what I am. I count what is lost. You carry losses the way a mule carries ore—packed tight, never unloaded, growing heavier until the mule dies under it or throws it off into a ravine and runs. I am asking you to unload. Whether it breaks you is not my concern. The stone does not care either. Nothing about this will care about you, small keeper. That is the first honest thing anyone will tell you on this road."

Sebastian looked at the rings of grave-stones stretching away into the dusk. Four hundred eleven thousand. Six hundred and something. Counted one by one, aloud, by a creature who had nothing else left to do with eternity.

"Why?" he asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "Why does honesty buy the stone? Nesharra took a memory. Corvane stole power outright. Why does the fire-keeper want confession?"

The giant was quiet for a moment. When he answered, the bell-rhythm of his counting had stopped entirely, and the plateau felt strange without it, like a room where a clock has died.

"Because the covenant was a confession," he said. "Did Old Wren tell you that? Did she show you the words carved in the Stone? Your ancestor knelt before the wild places—the first of us, before we were diminished, when the forests still had their voices and the rivers remembered their names—and he confessed. He confessed what men had done. The clear-cutting. The slaughter. The poisoning of the deep springs. He named every crime of his people aloud, without ornament, without excuse, and he asked not for forgiveness but for terms." The coals of his eyes brightened. "The wild places granted terms, because he did not flinch. That is the secret at the bottom of the old magic, ledger-keeper. Power does not flow to the clever. It flows to the honest. Cleverness built your guild. Honesty built the covenant. And look which one is crumbling."

Sebastian's hands had closed into fists without his permission. "And if I refuse?"

"Then you climb back down," the giant said, "and you carry your ore until the mule dies. And the third keystone stays in the guildmaster's chest, eating him alive, and the fourth hundred thousand graves become five hundred thousand, and eventually someone else's descendant kneels where yours knelt and pays what you would not." He gestured, vast and unhurried, at the stone forest below. "I am patient. I have counted longer than your line has existed. Refuse, and sit with me awhile. Company is rare. I will teach you the counting. It passes the time."

* * *

Sebastian did not decide so much as stop resisting. There was a moment—he would remember it later as the true hinge of his life, more than the reforging, more than the throne-room—when he understood that he had been rehearsing this confession his entire adult life. Every night at the guild, balancing impossible accounts, he had been practicing. Every entry that wouldn't balance, every sum that came out wrong no matter how many times he checked, had been the ledger of his own guilt trying to surface through the columns. He had simply never let it.

He opened his mouth. What came out first was barely a whisper.

"Tomas."

The name fell into the ash between them.

"My brother. Younger by two years. When the fever came through our village I was twelve and he was ten, and Mother sent me to fetch the herb-woman from the next farm, and I—I stopped. On the way. There was a fox den behind Miller's hedge, and I'd found I could talk to the vixen, and she had kits, and she was telling me about the winter, and I stayed. I stayed an hour. Maybe more. When I got to the herb-woman's it was dark and she'd had supper already and by the time we got back—" His voice cracked and he pushed through it, because stopping now would be dying now. "Tomas was dead by morning. Mother never blamed me. That was the worst part. She never once blamed me, and I have spent twenty years wishing she had, because then at least the weight would have a shape."

The giant said nothing. The coals watched.

"Fenwick." The second name came easier, which was its own horror. "The carter at the guild. He suspected what I was—no, he didn't suspect, he *knew*, he saw me talking to his horses one night in the yard, and he never said a word, never sold me, never even hinted. And when the Gift-Hunt decree came, when Corvane ordered us all to report anomalies, Fenwick started drinking. Badly. Because he was afraid, and afraid for me, and carrying it alone. And I saw it. I saw him shaking apart and I said nothing, because saying something meant risking myself. They took him in the second sweep. I heard he burned in Vellmarket Square." Sebastian's jaw ached. "I could have gotten him out. I knew the beast-paths before I ever fled through them. I could have warned him. I chose my skin."

"Mara Chen," he went on, and now the names were coming like water through a broken dam, and he let them. "The kitchen girl who covered for me when I missed inventory. They docked her wages. She had a sick father. I let her take the loss rather than explain where I'd been. Aldous Prine, the old archivist—I let the guild believe his errors were his own when half of them were mine, and he was dismissed in disgrace and died within the year, poor and ashamed. The rats in the cellar wall—forty of them, at least, when Corvane ordered the cellars flushed with lime after they told me his secrets. I knew it was coming. I had one night's warning. I saved three. I didn't even try to save the rest, because I was already thinking about my own escape route, already planning, and forty small lives seemed like acceptable arithmetic." He laughed, and it came out as something broken. "Acceptable arithmetic. God. I balance everything. That's what I do. And the balance always comes out fine for me."

"Isolde Marr," he said, and faltered.

The coals brightened a fraction. "Go on."

"She hunted me. She sold me—or helped sell me—at the Weighing House, and I hated her for it, and then she freed me on the road and burned her own life down to do it, and I still don't trust her, and I keep waiting for the trick, because everyone I've trusted has cost someone else. But that's not the failure. The failure is that when she freed me, when she stood there having thrown away everything for a man she'd known a handful of weeks, I felt—" He stopped. Started again. "I felt *vindicated*. Like her sacrifice proved I'd been worth saving. I took her ruin and made it a compliment to myself. That's the failure. Not what I did to her. What I did with her."

The names kept coming. The sparrow—the first sparrow, the one that died warning him about the hunt, whose warning he dismissed because the warning was inconvenient. The horses at the Weighing House, who had stampeded for him and been whipped and culled afterward; he had heard their screams from the sewers and run faster instead of turning back. Old Wren herself, who had fallen holding Ironhollow's gate while he escaped with the refugees, and though she had chosen it, had ordered it, had *planned* it—still, some nights, in the dark of the beast-paths, he suspected he had been the reason the hunters found the door at all, that his passage left a scent, a trail, a warmth the ward couldn't fully mask. He named it anyway. He named it because the giant was right: unexamined, it would ride him forever.

And last, when the sky had gone fully dark and the coals of the giant's eyes were the only light on the plateau, Sebastian named the one he had been circling all along.

"Myself," he said hoarsely. "I failed myself. Twenty years hiding in ledgers. Twenty years pretending the gift was a burden instead of a birthright, because a burden can be concealed but a birthright demands something of you. I hid. I hid so well I nearly let the whole wild world die while I balanced books in a basement. There's the tally. That's everyone. That's the whole account."

He sagged forward, hands flat on the warm ash, breathing like a man who had swum a great distance. Something was running down his face. He was distantly surprised to find it was tears—the first since Ironhollow's gate, since Wren's hand pressing the wardstone into his arms, since everything.

* * *

The giant reached out—one finger, vast as a fallen log—and touched the ground beside Sebastian. Where it touched, the ash compressed into a smooth grey disc, and on the disc, scores appeared, cut by heat alone: a list of names, small and precise, in a script like cooling lava.

"There," the giant said. "Counted. Entered. Kept." He drew the finger back. "Do you feel lighter?"

Sebastian considered the question honestly, because lying to this creature was clearly impossible and he was done with impossible things tonight. "No. I feel *hollowed*. Like the memory-price. Like something was scooped out."

"Yes," the giant said. "That is what honesty is. Everyone thinks it will fill them. It empties them instead. But here is the difference, small keeper, and mark it well: the hollow where a memory was will never fill again. The hollow where guilt was—" the coals flared, briefly, like a smile— "*that* fills. With other things. Watch."

He closed his hand, and when he opened it again, something lay in his palm no larger than a hen's egg: a keystone of black glass shot through with veins of ember-red, warm-looking even at a distance, pulsing with a slow light like a sleeping heart.

"The second keystone," the giant said. "Take it."

Sebastian took it, and it was warm as a living thing, and it sank its heat into his scarred palms, and somewhere far away—under a lake, inside a cracked stone in Ironhollow—he felt something answer, a resonance like two bells finding the same pitch.

"You'll come to the reforging?" he asked. "When it's time? The covenant binds the wild places too. Your voice should be there."

The giant was quiet a long moment. Then he turned back to his rings of grave-stones, and the bell-rhythm resumed, patient as erosion.

*Four hundred and eleven thousand, six hundred and five,* he counted, and then, without pausing the count, without looking up: "I have been counting deaths, ledger-keeper. If the boy from your bloodline keeps his word, someday I will count something else. Go. Before I finish this ring. And tell the drowned queen her sister still owes me a song."

Sebastian stood, legs shaking, keystone against his chest beside the first, and turned toward the cliff and the long road south—to the capital, to the gilded city, to the third keystone beating inside the chest of the man who had sold him.

Behind him, the counting continued, steady as a heartbeat.

But as Sebastian reached the cliff's edge, the giant's voice followed him down one final time, and it was not a number:

"The chancellor knows you're coming. The wind carries everything—even to Veyne. Walk carefully, heir of Winterbourne. The last stone is guarded by the one enemy who cannot be confessed away."

The words dropped into Sebastian's stomach like cold iron, and he climbed down into the dark, counting his own footsteps, all the way to the bottom.
