## 12. Hunters at the Hollow Door

The hounds smelled the ward before they saw it.

Captain Dreech felt the change in them through the leads—a sudden tightening, a low whine rolling up the leashes like water finding a crack in a dam. Twelve dogs, all of them bought from the crown kennels where their grandmothers had been bred with something older than dog. They could taste magic the way other animals tasted fear.

"Steady," Dreech said.

They stood on a ridge above a valley that was not supposed to exist. The king's maps showed a marsh here, a dead marsh, blighted and gray. But below them lay green terraces, smoke rising from chimneys, goats on a hillside, and at the valley's mouth a gate of black iron set into two standing stones that hummed with a light you could almost hear.

Ironhollow. The last free sanctuary. The place every hunted speaker in three kingdoms whispered about when they thought no one was listening.

Dreech had been hunting gifted folk for eleven years. He had burned a woman in Aldermoor who could make bread rise with a touch. He had drowned a boy in Vess who spoke to fish. He told himself each time that it was law, that it was order, that the alternative was the world tearing itself apart along seams no one could mend. Most nights he even believed it.

Tonight he did not believe it, because tonight the dogs were crying, and the light on the gate was singing, and somewhere down in that valley children were going to die for a decree written in red wax by men who would never smell smoke.

"The speaker's mark is confirmed?" asked the man beside him. Serl, the guild's man—no, not the guild's anymore. Corvane's man. He wore no colors now, just gray wool and a face like a closed ledger.

"Your master's information has been accurate so far," Dreech said.

"It will be accurate now." Serl unrolled a strip of vellum covered in Corvane's cramped hand. "The ward is old. Older than the kingdom. It runs on a covenant, and covenants have terms. The terms were broken sixty years ago—the stone cracked, the power thinned. It holds, but barely. It holds like an old dam." He tapped the vellum. "And dams fail at the weakest point."

"And the weakest point?"

"The gate itself. The stones are the anchor. Break the anchor, break the ward." Serl smiled without warmth. "Corvane stood at this gate once, Captain. Forty years ago. He knows what it costs to breach it."

"What did it cost him?"

Serl folded the vellum away. "Everything he's spent his life trying to buy back."

* * *

Sebastian heard them coming an hour before the horns.

He was on the eastern wall with Old Wren, helping her carry baskets of late apples down to the cellar, when the birds changed. It came over him like weather—the starlings lifting off the terraces all at once, the jays going silent mid-scream, and then the mice in the wall beneath his feet pouring westward in a river of small desperate bodies.

"They're coming," he said. The basket nearly slipped from his hands. "From the ridge. Dogs, and—" He stopped, because what the rats in the root cellar were screaming up at him made no sense. "Wren, there are guildmen with them. Guild seals. Someone gave them the road."

Old Wren did not drop her basket. She was eighty years old and she had buried two husbands and a daughter and half a sanctuary's worth of friends, and her hands did not shake for anything less than death, and this was death, so they didn't shake at all. She simply set the apples down on the wall and looked east.

"How long?"

"Dawn. Maybe sooner. The dogs are tired but the men aren't."

"Then we wake the hollow." She turned and her voice carried the way it always did, low and certain, the voice of a woman who had held this valley together with stubbornness and prayer for fifty years. Bells began ringing before she finished the sentence—someone had already seen the birds too. "Sebastian."

He looked at her.

"You know what I'm going to say."

"No." His throat went tight. "Send me to the paths. Send me with the children. I can hear the beast-roads, Wren, I can get them out—"

"You can. And you will." She took his face in both hands, apple-bruised and strong. "But hear me first. That gate has held since my grandmother's grandmother. It will hold tonight if someone stands at it and *means* it. A ward is not stone, boy. It is attention. It is love, given shape and schedule. And I am the one who has kept its schedule for fifty years." She smiled, and it was the worst smile he had ever seen, because it was peaceful. "You are the heir. You are the reason any of this matters. Get them out. All of them. Promise me."

"I can't—"

"*Promise me.*"

The bells were ringing. Down in the lanes, doors were opening, lamplight spilling out, mothers pulling coats onto children who were still half-asleep and asking questions in small frightened voices. Sebastian thought of his mother's letters burning in a guild brazier, curling into black moths. He thought of a sparrow dying on a windowsill saying *the hunt has begun*, and how he had gone back to his ledgers.

"I promise," he said.

She kissed his forehead, turned him around by the shoulders, and pushed him toward the stairs. "Go. And Sebastian—" He looked back. "When you reforge it—and you will reforge it—make the terms kinder than ours were."

* * *

Isolde Marr rode at the rear of the column and hated every minute of it.

She had been chasing Sebastian Winterbourne across half a kingdom. She had watched a forest calcify mid-scream, trees turning to gray glass with the shapes of birds still inside them. She had knelt in the ash of the Cinder Reach road and fed a blighted fox from her own ration tin while it looked at her with eyes that still knew what gratitude was. Somewhere between the drowned lake and the burning forest, the clean architecture of her mission had developed cracks, and now, riding toward a valley full of families with a company of men carrying pitch and fire, the cracks had become chasms.

Lord Chancellor Veyne wanted the last Winterbourne alive. The decree said speakers burned. Isolde had read enough of Veyne's private correspondence—she'd been reading it for months, telling herself it was duty—to know that alive meant *harvested*. There were rooms under the palace. She had seen the requisition ledgers. Chains, basins, drains.

"Rider ahead!" someone shouted.

A girl on a shaggy pony burst from the tree line, saw the column, and wheeled so hard the pony screamed. Arrows went after her. Isolde watched one take the pony in the flank and felt something in her chest tear loose and refuse to go back where it belonged.

The girl escaped into the dark. The pony did not.

"Move!" Dreech roared. "Before the ward-mistress reinforces it!"

Isolde rode on, and quietly, in the privacy of her own skull, she began composing the report she would never send.

* * *

They hit the gate at moonset.

It should have been simple. That was what Serl promised: the anchor stones, the pitch, the fire, the collapse. But Old Wren stood between the standing stones with forty of Ironhollow's defenders behind her—farmers with billhooks, a smith with a hammer, a one-eyed archer named Tam who hadn't missed all night—and the ward rose off the stones like heat off summer rock, and the first twenty arrows fired at the gate simply stopped, hung, and fell.

"Again!" Dreech shouted. "Bring the ram! Bring the fire!"

The hounds refused. Every one of them sat down at the edge of the ward-light and howled, and nothing Dreech or his handlers did could move them, and later Isolde would understand why: the dogs could taste what the men could not. The ward wasn't a wall. It was a promise, and promises made by people who mean them are very hard to break.

So they broke it the way everything breaks—not with force but with patience and treachery.

Serl walked forward alone, hands raised, and called out in a voice trained by decades of guild negotiation. "Mistress Wren! We only want the Winterbourne boy! Give him to us and the crown's mercy extends to every soul in this valley—you have my word as a servant of the Guild of Impossible Things!"

"He has no word to give," Old Wren called back, clear as a bell across the field. "I've known Aldous Corvane since he was a clerk stealing candle-ends. Whatever he sold you, captain, he sold it cheap, because it was never his to sell."

"The decree says otherwise."

"The decree," said Old Wren, "is red wax and cowardice."

What happened next happened fast. Later, survivors would tell it differently, but the bones of it were these: Serl stepped back, and the men brought forward not a ram but a cage, and in the cage was a gift-drained wretch from the palace menagerie—a thin, gray-skinned thing that might once have been human, that still had one flicker of the gift left in it, and that the hunters had brought as a living key, because a stolen gift can pick the lock of a covenant the way a stolen key opens a stranger's house.

They pressed the creature against the standing stone.

The ward screamed.

Everyone in the valley heard it—not with ears but with teeth, with marrow, with the oldest part of the brain. The light on the gate flickered like a guttering lamp. And Old Wren, who had kept its schedule for fifty years, who had fed it her attention every morning of her long life, did the only thing left to do.

She put her hands on the stone and poured herself into it.

"GO!" she screamed, to the valley, to the dark, to the boy already running. "GO NOW!"

The ward flared white. The cage-thing crumbled to dust. Serl was thrown thirty feet and did not get up. Half the hunter line staggered back, blinded, and in that blindness Tam's arrows found four of them, and Dreech screamed for fire, and the pitch-arcs came arcing over the wall-light—

—and Old Wren held.

She held while the fire caught the western terrace. She held while the refugees poured out the hidden postern and into the dark of the beast-paths. She held while her valley burned behind her, and she held so completely, so ferociously, that when the ward finally failed—if it failed; some said it simply chose to stop, having been given permission at last—she was already gone, slumped against the scorched stone with her hands still raised, her face turned toward the mountain path, wearing that same terrible peaceful smile.

* * *

Sebastian heard her go.

He was a mile up the beast-road with two hundred refugees strung out behind him—children on the backs of the hollow's few horses, elders carried in litters, a goat herd that had refused to be left behind—when it happened. The night went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with sound. Every wild voice within his reach, every rat and crow and fox and moth, went silent at once, the way a room goes silent when someone dies in it.

He stopped walking. He stood in the dark with his fists clenched and he listened to the absence where Old Wren had been, and the rage that came up through him was not hot. It was cold and enormous and patient, the rage of a ledger discovering a debt.

"Keep moving," he said, and his voice frightened the people nearest him, because it did not sound like a clerk's voice at all.

The beasts guided them. That was the miracle he would remember afterward, when he had time to remember anything: the whole living dark bending around the fleeing column. Foxes ran ahead marking safe turns. Owls called warnings where hunters' pickets waited on the high trail. A family of badgers, unasked, unbidden, dug a shelter cut into a hillside and held two hundred exhausted people in it through the worst hour of the rain. The wild places, poisoned and blighted and betrayed for sixty years, hid the heir of the creature who had once bound them—and hid him *well*.

Because they knew what he was. And they knew what he might yet undo.

Near dawn, a crow landed on a branch above the head of the column and spoke with the flat clarity crows reserved for important things: *"The gate is fallen. The old one is ash. The gray men turn toward the capital with their cages empty and their masters angry."*

Empty cages. Sebastian's blood went cold. Veyne wanted him harvested, not burned—which meant the hunt would not end here. It would follow him anywhere. There was nowhere left to hide.

Good, said the cold enormous thing inside him. Let it follow. Let it come all the way to the capital, to the spire, to the throne of thorns. He had two keystones in the pack on his back—heavy as sins, warm as heartbeats. One more remained, lodged in the chest of the man who had sold him, sold Wren, sold everything.

"Where next, Speaker?" asked Tam the archer, coming up beside him, ash-streaked, one eye red with grief. "There's nothing left to go *to*."

Sebastian looked south, toward the gilded city on the horizon's smudge, where a palace menagerie full of drained and screaming captives waited in cages, and a corrupt guildmaster counted his blood-money, and a chancellor sat on stolen power pretending it was a throne.

"There's one debt left," he said. "And I intend to collect it in person."

Behind him, two hundred refugees lifted their heads. Ahead of him, the crow spread its wings and flew south, showing the way.
