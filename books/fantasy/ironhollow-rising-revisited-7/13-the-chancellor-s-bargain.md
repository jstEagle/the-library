## 13. The Chancellor's Bargain

Lord Chancellor Aldous Veyne had not slept a full night in thirty-one years, and he had stopped pretending it was an accident.

He rose before the palace bells, as always, in the dark of his apartments above the Chancellery, and performed the small rituals that kept the machinery of himself running. Cold water to the face. A shave so precise it left no evidence of the razor. The grey morning coat, brushed twice, its buttons polished until they threw back light like coins. He dressed the way other men prayed: methodically, without hope of transcendence, but with absolute faith in the procedure.

Then he went down to the vault beneath the Chancellery, where the crown kept its most valuable asset, and he checked the gauges.

* * *

The vault had been a wine cellar once, back when the palace's appetites were merely human. Now it held no wine at all. It held pipes—copper, silver, and one long vein of black iron that ran up through the foundations into the spire above—and it held the hum.

Veyne felt the hum before he heard it, the way you feel weather coming in an old wound. It lived in the iron vein, in the walls, in his own teeth if he stood too close. Thirty-one years ago it had been a roar. He remembered the night they'd tapped it: young Veyne, then only Second Secretary of Revenues, kneeling in the dark with three men who would all be dead within the decade, watching a drill of cold-iron bite through the last skin of stone between the cellar and something vast and living on the other side.

The covenant, the old texts called it. A word binding realm to realm, human to wild, sealed in a stone older than the kingdom and tended by a bloodline of speakers who could hear what the wild places said. Every treaty, every border, every harvest that came in when it should—the whole quiet architecture of two worlds resting on one stone and one family's stubborn vigilance.

And every year, a portion of that power flowing through like water through a millrace, wasted on beasts and trees and people who could *talk* to them.

Veyne had done the arithmetic. That was the thing no one understood about him, then or now. They expected monsters to be hungry, mad, ravening. But Veyne had simply looked at the covenant the way he looked at any account and seen inefficiency. Power beyond measure, sloshing through the world uncollected, untaxed, unmetered. A river running to sea with no wheel set in it.

So he had set a wheel in it.

The drill, the iron vein, the gauges. Power siphoned from the covenant's flow and piped upward into the crown—and from the crown, discreetly, into the hands of the few men who knew where the kingdom's real strength lived. It was not theft. Veyne had always resented the word. It was *collection*. Revenue, finally drawn from an estate that had gone untaxed since the founding of the realm.

The first years had been magnificent. The gauges sang. The blight was a rumor in far provinces, deniable, distant. And Veyne rose—Second Secretary to First Secretary to Chancellor—on the strength of a kingdom that grew strangely vigorous while its neighbors withered.

But power taken is never power owned. The covenant had been built to flow, not to be dammed, and a dammed river silts. The gauges now read lower each year. The wild places were calcifying at their edges, forests turning to grey glass, rivers forgetting how to run. The blight crept toward the capital like a stain spreading through cloth. And the crown's stolen vigor was thinning with it—a king who aged faster than his portrait, ministers who tired by noon, an army whose marches grew shorter every season.

The whole edifice was failing. Slowly, elegantly, inevitably—the way a ledger fails when the entries have been false for decades.

Which was why, this grey morning, Veyne stood in the vault reading the gauges with the particular attention he gave to nothing else, and saw that the needle on the primary dial had dropped another fraction since Tuesday.

He wrote the figure in his pocket book. Small, neat numerals. Then he climbed the stairs to begin the day's work, which was, as it had been for thirty-one years, the management of decline.

* * *

Corvane arrived at the fourth bell, shown into the Chancellor's study by servants who did not exist on any roster and who would deny ever seeing him.

Guildmaster Edran Corvane had grown old badly, Veyne observed. There were men who wore age like a scar earned honestly; Corvane wore it like a debt collector's summons. His hands trembled unless he gripped something, so he gripped the arms of the chair across the desk, and Veyne let him grip them and said nothing for a long, comfortable moment.

"Chancellor." Corvane's voice had the wet rasp of a man who talked too much to people he feared. "You received my letter."

"I received several letters this week, Guildmaster. One of them concerned you." Veyne opened a drawer without looking at it, closed it again. He enjoyed this part—the theater of infinite information. "It concerns the item we discussed. The speaker."

"You have him?"

"I have a report that he fled north with a horse's help and vanished into the fens. I have your assurance, given twice now, that the Guild of Impossible Things contains no other anomalies. And I have a growing sense, Guildmaster, that you sold me a man you barely understand, for reasons you have not fully confessed even to yourself."

Corvane's knuckles whitened on the chair arms. "I sold you exactly what you asked for. The last speaker. The Winterbourne line."

"The last *known* speaker." Veyne rose and crossed to the window. Below, the palace yard was being swept by men who moved like puppets with tired strings. Even here, at the heart of the siphon, the vigor leaked away year by year. "Tell me what you know about the covenant, Edran."

"I know it's broken. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows it is *cracked*. Broken is a technical condition." Veyne turned. "Do you know what happens when a cracked covenant finishes breaking? I do. I've read the accounts of the last time, before the founding. The wild does not simply die, Guildmaster. It *closes*. The forests seal like scars. The beasts go silent—not dead, silent—and the land forgets it was ever anything but stone. And everything the crown has drawn from the flow for thirty-one years turns to ash in our veins at once. Do you understand? Not gradually. At once. Every man who has tasted the siphon will feel his own years come due in a single afternoon."

The color left Corvane's face in stages, like a tide going out.

"That is why," Veyne continued pleasantly, "the situation requires precision rather than panic. The covenant can be reforged. The old texts are clear on the mechanism—it wants a maker's heir, a Winterbourne, to pour the gift itself into the stone. Which presents us with a problem of inventory." He smiled, thin as a paper cut. "If the boy reaches Ironhollow and completes the reforging, the flow resumes naturally, the siphon dies, and every advantage this crown has purchased in three decades evaporates. If the covenant breaks entirely, we all burn. But there is a third path."

"What third path?"

"A maker's blood can feed the stone instead of heal it. A Winterbourne, delivered to the spire alive, can be... drawn upon. Directly. Permanently. A living anchor, a wellspring in chains. The covenant stabilizes around him. The siphon continues. Nothing changes except that one young man spends the rest of his life as a battery for the realm." Veyne sat again, folding his hands. "You can see why I need him breathing."

"He's in Ironhollow." Corvane said it flatly, hating the words. "Behind the ward. You'll need to crack it, and cracking it will cost—"

"Everything costs. I budgeted for it." Veyne slid a folded paper across the desk. "Ward-finders. Three of them, licensed under the new Gift-Hunt statutes, which I wrote, which means they answer to me and no one else. They'll locate the seam in the ward. Hunters follow. Your contribution, Guildmaster, is the interior knowledge—you mapped that sanctuary's approaches for me years ago, don't look surprised, you were paid handsomely at the time."

"I never mapped—" Corvane began, and stopped, because they both remembered the same winter, the same payment, the same signature.

"There is also the matter of your chest," Veyne said softly.

The silence that followed had texture. Veyne watched Corvane's hand rise halfway toward his sternum and stop, caught between instinct and dignity.

"You think I don't know what you swallowed, all those years ago? A fragment of the Covenant Stone, chipped off in the first days after the tapping, when you thought you could steal a piece of the flow for yourself. Greedy little side-ledger. It's been eating you alive ever since, hasn't it? Burning through you like acid through cloth. How many physicians have you terrified into silence?" He leaned forward. "Here is what you may not know: the fragment is a keystone. One of three that can seat the covenant's reforging—or complete its ruin. The boy will come for it eventually. Beasts whisper, Edran. Roads open. He is already walking toward you, and he doesn't even know it yet."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I arrest you this afternoon for trafficking with gifted persons—which, given what lives inside your ribcage, would be a remarkably short trial—and the fragment comes out of you on a table, with or without the rest of you attached." Veyne opened the drawer he'd toyed with earlier and drew out a second document, already sealed in red wax, already signed. "Or."

Corvane stared at the paper. "What is it?"

"A commission. Governorship of the Western Provinces—timber, tolls, tithes, a palace of your own and a garrison that salutes you. Everything you hoarded power here in the guild to become, granted outright, with my seal and the king's." Veyne set it down between them. "Your price is simple. Deliver Sebastian Winterbourne to the capital, alive, before the ward falls or after—I care not which. And when the boy comes for the keystone in your chest, you bring him to me rather than letting him take it. Fail in either, and the arrest warrant issues within the hour. Succeed, and you die old, rich, and obeyed in a province far from here."

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. Veyne recognized the expression on his face—he had seen it in mirrors, decades ago, in the days just before the drilling. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic on his own soul and finding the sum acceptable.

"You'll hunt him regardless," Corvane said at last. "Whether I help or not."

"Naturally."

"Then I'm choosing the version where I live."

"Yes," Veyne said. "You always do. It's your single reliable virtue." He pushed the commission across the desk. "Sign."

Corvane signed. His hand shook, and the signature came out crooked, and Veyne noted it for correction later—everything got corrected later, that was the entire philosophy of his life—and then the guildmaster was escorted out by servants who did not exist, and the Chancellor of the Realm sat alone with his ledgers.

* * *

That evening he dined alone, as he preferred, on food he did not taste, and afterward he took the private stair down to the vault one last time.

The gauges glowed in the dark like patient eyes. He read them by lamplight, recording figures, and found the primary needle steady—not recovered, never recovered, but holding. Holding because somewhere in the past day, faint but unmistakable, the flow had quickened. Something upstream had stirred. A speaker moving toward the stone, perhaps. Or the stone moving toward the speaker.

Either way, the current was rising, and currents could be caught.

Veyne closed his pocket book and stood a moment in the humming dark, listening to the sound that had replaced sleep, ambition, conscience, and every other inconvenient appetite. Thirty-one years he had fed this machine. It had made him the second most powerful man in the realm, and it would make him the first, and it would keep him there, and when the history books were written—if anyone still wrote history books—they would call him the man who saved the kingdom from the wild.

They would not write about the forests turned to glass. They never did. History was a ledger too, and he intended to remain its keeper.

He pinched out the lamp and climbed the stairs, already composing tomorrow's orders: hunters north, ward-finders north, a convoy route planned to the hour.

In the vault below, the black iron vein hummed on in the dark—hungry, patient, and very slightly louder than it had been the day before.
