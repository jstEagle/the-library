## 14. Isolde's Choice

The convoy moved at the pace of grief.

Isolde Marr rode at its head, as her commission required, and did not look back at the wagon. She didn't need to. She could hear him—no, that was his gift, not hers—but she could *imagine* him: Sebastian Winterbourne, wrists bound in gift-damp iron, wedged between two hunters who smelled of tallow and cruelty, jostling with every rut in the road like a sack of grain that had once been a man.

Four days out of Ironhollow. Four days since Old Wren had died holding the gate, and the ward had come down like a struck bell, and Isolde had done her job. That was the sentence she kept returning to, turning it over, looking for another meaning inside it. *She had done her job.* The hunters had poured through the breach. The refugees had scattered into beast-paths no human eye could follow. And Isolde had walked through the smoke of a dying sanctuary with her ledger under her arm and her commission sealed in red wax, and she had found him at last—the last speaker, the quarry of half a year's work—and put irons on him herself.

*Done your job,* said the voice in her head that sounded like her father. *Well done.*

Ahead, the road bent toward the capital through country that was dying in ways the crown's maps refused to record. Fields gone grey to the root. A stream running the color of old milk. Trees standing upright and stone-hard, their leaves fixed mid-fall like insects in amber. The blight. Everyone spoke of it now in the low, careful voice people used for plague and debt. Lord Chancellor Veyne spoke of it too, in his letters—crisp, reasonable, always reasonable—but his instructions never mentioned it. Only the speaker. Only the delivery. Only the reward for a job well done.

"Slower," Sergeant Brack called from the rear. "Wagon axle's complaining."

Everything's complaining, Isolde thought. The road, the horses, the men. The world itself, quietly, all around them, and none of them listening.

That was the thought that had been growing in her for three days now, patient as rot: *none of them listening.* She had spent her career believing that attention was a form of virtue—that to audit something truly, to see it exactly as it was, was a kind of honesty rarer than courage. It was why Veyne had chosen her, she'd assumed. Because she saw clearly.

But she had watched the blighted forest calcify, and she had knelt in the ash beside a fox frozen mid-lunge, its eyes still wet, still *alive* somehow inside the stone, and she had not killed it as ordered. She had left it there. She had told herself it was sentiment. Sentiment was a professional failing. She wrote it down in her private ledger anyway, under losses.

And she had watched Sebastian Winterbourne, bound and beaten, turn his head—not toward his captors but toward the horses pulling his own prison wagon—and murmur something so soft it might have been breath. And the lead horse had stopped screaming.

It had been screaming since Ironhollow. Horses do that, near gifted folk; they smell what men can't. This one had screamed itself hoarse for four days, rolling its eyes white, and then Sebastian had breathed at it, and it had gone quiet, and it had walked the rest of that day with its ears turned backward toward the wagon like a dog toward a whistle.

Isolde had audited enough impossible things to know when an account refused to balance. This one refused. The decree said speakers were abominations who unmade the natural order. But she had seen the natural order lean toward him like a starving thing toward bread.

Tonight they would reach the ferry at Coldbridge. Tomorrow, the capital. Tomorrow, the pyre—or whatever Veyne actually wanted, because the more she read of his correspondence, the less the word *pyre* appeared and the more the word *asset* did. She had been ordered to deliver him alive and intact, and intact meant something specific in Veyne's grammar. Intact meant the gift still working. Intact meant a man kept as a tool is kept.

She could deliver that. She had delivered worse, or so she'd believed until she started keeping honest books.

At dusk they made Coldbridge: a ferry landing, a toll house, an inn with a sign depicting a fish that had given up halfway to being a fish. The hunters stabled the horses, chained the wagon to a post, and went inside to drink their advance pay. Brack posted two sentries and then, within the hour, joined them, because Brack believed rules were for other men's benefit.

Isolde sat in the inn's corner with a cup of small beer she didn't drink and her commission open before her, and she performed the last audit of her old life.

*Item:* one speaker, value to the crown, per Lord Chancellor Veyne: incalculable.
*Item:* one sanctuary burned, population dispersed, casualties including one matriarch, age unknown.
*Item:* one forest calcified, one river poisoned, one kingdom slowly turning to stone while its government hunted the only man who might stop it.
*Item:* one clerk, complicit throughout, salary paid quarterly.

The column would not close. She had tried every entry, every justification, every cold clean line of reasoning she owned, and the sum came out wrong every time. Somewhere in her ledgers there was a figure she had never entered—a debt incurred the day she'd taken Veyne's coin, accruing interest ever since—and tonight, in an inn that smelled of spilled ale and wet wool, she finally read it.

She closed the commission. She went upstairs, took out the letter she'd written reporting her success, and fed it to the candle flame. Then she took out everything else—the cipher sheets, the payment drafts, the little black book of instructions in Veyne's hand—and fed all of it after, watching six months of her life curl into ash in a tin basin.

It was astonishing how little smoke it made.

* * *

She did it with a lockpick and a lie, which was, she reflected, the only inheritance her profession had ever given her worth keeping.

The sentries were asleep against the wagon wheel—one genuinely, one performing it badly—and the night was thick with river fog off the Vess tributary, the kind of fog that swallowed lantern light at ten paces. Isolde crossed the yard in her clerk's grey, unremarkable as weather, and knelt by the wagon's door.

Through the bars, Sebastian Winterbourne looked up at her. His face was a ruin of bruise and exhaustion, but his eyes were clear, and they were not surprised. That unsettled her more than anything. He'd known. Perhaps the rats had told him. Perhaps the horses. Perhaps he'd simply audited her the way she audited everyone else, and reached the same sum she had, one column over.

"You're either here to kill me," he said, voice cracked from disuse, "or this is the most elaborate trap I've ever been caught in."

"I'm here to commit treason," Isolde said. "Hold still."

The lock was crown-work, good iron, but locks were just arithmetic and she had never lost an argument with arithmetic. It opened with a click that sounded, in the fog, like a shot. Neither sentry stirred. Down at the stables, though, the horses began, very softly, to stamp.

"They know," Sebastian whispered. "They've known since you walked into the guild with your false ledger and your true eyes. They've been waiting for you to catch up."

"That's sentimental nonsense."

"Yes," he agreed. "Most true things are."

She cut the irons off his wrists with Brack's own knife, taken from Brack's own belt while Brack drank, and the moment the gift-damp metal left his skin he shuddered like a man surfacing from deep water. Then he went still, head tilting, listening to something in the fog that she couldn't hear and never would again if they failed tonight.

"The stable," he murmured. "The bay mare. She says the ferryman owes the toll house a grudge and keeps a second boat. East bank, below the reeds." A ghost of a smile crossed the ruined face. "You're not the only one who collects debts."

They moved. Fog, reeds, black water, a flat-bottomed skiff that leaked with dignity rather than urgency. By the time the inn's door banged open behind them and Brack's roar went up across the water, they were fifty yards out, and the current had them, and the capital road was already a memory behind them.

Isolde rowed. Sebastian, hands still too raw for the oars, sat in the stern with his face lifted to the fog, and she realized he was weeping without any sound at all—as if even his grief had learned to be quiet, all these years, so no one would hear it.

She looked away and rowed harder.

* * *

They made the east bank by moonset and walked inland until dawn, following a route Sebastian chose by means he declined to explain—"the badger setts run along the ridge, the ridge runs dry, the crows hate the open moor"—until Isolde stopped asking and started simply trusting, which frightened her more than the hunters behind them.

On the second morning, in the shelter of a hollowed oak, they finally had the conversation.

"Veyne doesn't want you dead," she said. "I need you to understand that, because it changes everything. The Gift-Hunt is theater. He wants you alive and working. There are letters—I burned most of them, but I remember all of them—in his own hand. He calls you an asset. He talks about *sustained extraction.*"

Sebastian was quiet for a long time. "Extraction of what?"

"What do you think? The gift. Whatever it is you hear. He's been feeding it into something for years—I don't know what. But the blight got worse the longer I worked for him, and I don't think that's coincidence anymore. I think the covenant—" she caught herself, because she wasn't supposed to know that word, and watched his eyes sharpen.

"Old Wren told you?"

"She told me enough to haunt me. She said the realms were bound by a word, and someone broke the word, and the breaking is leaking out of the world like blood from a wound. She said a Winterbourne made the word." Isolde wrapped her arms around her knees. "I'm an auditor, Sebastian. I follow sums to their source. Every sum I've followed for six months ends at Veyne's desk. And every sum Veyne follows ends at you."

"Then we're both going to the same place." He said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse than fear. "The capital. The spire. Him."

"He'll have three hundred guards between us and his desk."

"I'll have everything else." He gestured—at the moor, the wind, a kestrel hanging motionless high above them, a line of ants processing along the oak bark like a column of clerks. "You count what men own. I count what they don't. Veyne's walls are very thorough about what's inside them." His voice dropped. "He's less thorough about what isn't."

It was not a plan. It was barely a direction. But she had burned her commission, her cipher, her name in Veyne's service, and there was a strange cleanliness in having nothing left to lose but the choice in front of her.

"They'll hang you for this," Sebastian said. "Helping me. You know that."

"I know the exact tariff for it," Isolde said. "I helped write the schedule. Treason by aid, second degree: hanging until dead, goods forfeit to the crown." She almost smiled. "I've decided to contest the valuation of my goods."

For the first time since she'd met him, Sebastian laughed—a real laugh, brief and rusty, startled out of him—and then pressed a hand to his ribs as if the sound cost something. Maybe it did. Everything cost something now. She was beginning to understand that was the covenant's whole grammar.

They shook hands on it anyway, formally, absurdly, two fugitives sealing an alliance in the hollow of a dying oak with nothing signed but intent. Her hand was an auditor's hand, ink-stained and steady. His was a clerk's hand, scarred where the irons had been.

"To the capital," she said.

"To the spire," he said. "And the man at the top of it."

Far overhead, the kestrel folded its wings and dropped like a thrown knife—and rose again with nothing, screaming once, a sound Isolde could not translate. But Sebastian tilted his head, listened, and his expression changed into something she had not seen on him before.

Not fear. Recognition.

"What?" she asked. "What did it say?"

"It didn't say anything." He was already moving, quick and quiet, up out of the hollow toward the ridgeline. "It *asked*. There's a rider on the north road, alone, wearing guild colors, and it's riding hard, and it's riding toward us." He glanced back at her, and the last of the laughter was gone from his face. "Isolde—who else knows you burned your letters?"

The answer arrived in her mind unbidden, complete, and terrible: *no one.* No one living knew—except the one man who had written them.

Lord Chancellor Veyne already knew she had turned.
