## 15. Into the Gilded City

The capital announced itself three days before we reached it.

We heard it first as a smell—woodsmoke and tanneries and ten thousand cookfires braided into one greasy ribbon on the wind—and then as a sound, a low continuous roar like a river that had learned to argue. Isolde called it the city's breathing. I called it something else, though not aloud: a chorus of misery so vast it had stopped being voices and become weather.

"You're doing the face," Isolde said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're listening to something I can't hear and you hate what it's saying."

I pulled my hood lower. "I'm listening to nothing. That's the point of the disguise."

That was only half true. Since Ironhollow, since Old Wren fell at the gate with her arms spread wide and her voice raised in a word older than any kingdom, I had been hearing more than ever—the gift swelling instead of shrinking under pressure, as if the wild places knew their heir was running and shouted directions after him. Beasts along the road had whispered us past three checkpoints. A murder of crows had described the chancellor's patrol rotations in detail no human spy could match. And now, growing louder with every mile, came the thing beneath the city's noise.

Caged things. Many caged things. Calling out.

"Say it anyway," Isolde said. "You'll feel better."

"I'm fine."

"You haven't slept in two days, you flinched when a cart went by an hour ago, and you've been chewing your thumbnail like it owes you money." She rode her mule closer. "In the guild you were famous for being unreadable. Whatever happened to that man?"

"He read his own ledger," I said, "and didn't like the totals."

She let that stand. It was the closest thing to honesty either of us traded these days, and we both knew the exchange rate was steep.

* * *

The plan was Isolde's, refined by me, which is how all our plans went now. The crown's tax convoys entered the Gilded City through the Weavers' Gate twice a week, escorted by bored soldiers who checked faces against lists they barely read. What they never checked was paperwork. Paperwork was sacred. Paperwork was the one language every official in Veyne's realm spoke fluently and feared absolutely.

So we became paperwork.

Isolde had taken two sets of clerks' robes from a convoy we'd ambushed outside Marrow Ford—not killed anyone; she'd been very clear about that, and I'd been surprised by how glad I was to hear it—and dyed them the correct shade of bureaucratic gray. I forged our travel warrants using the guild's own seal conventions, which I knew better than my mother's handwriting, because for eleven years the guild seal *had* been my handwriting. Tax assessors Winterbourne and Marr, seconded from the provincial office at Cinderford, returning to the capital to reconcile the year's collections.

The beautiful thing about forgery, I'd told her, is that the best defense is detail. So I gave us detail. Warrant numbers cross-referenced to real convoy manifests. A supervisor's name—Dunmore—who really existed and really hated being asked questions. Even a complaint filed against ourselves for excessive mileage claims, because no auditor alive suspects a man who has already accused himself of something small.

Isolde had listened to all this with an expression I was learning to read. Not admiration exactly. Something adjacent to it, wearing admiration's coat.

"You could have run the guild," she said.

"The guild ran me."

"And now?"

"Now I'm running toward a spire full of stolen power with a woman who spent four months hunting me." I shrugged. "The ledgers keep getting stranger."

* * *

The Weavers' Gate took us without a blink.

A sergeant with a waxed mustache glanced at our warrants, winced at the sight of the self-filed complaint, and waved us through with the gratitude of a man who wanted no part of whatever we were. Beyond him, the capital opened up like a ledger itself—streets as columns, rooftops as entries, everything tallied and taxed and owned. The Gilded City earned its name honestly: the crown had gilded everything it could reach. Door handles. Signposts. The beaks of the brass pigeons on the customs house, which were real pigeons' distant cousins and looked embarrassed about it.

Gold everywhere, and underneath it, if you knew how to listen, rot.

We lodged at the Inkwell, a clerk's boarding house where anonymity was the house specialty. Our room cost four coppers a night and came with a window overlooking a dye-yard, a bed each, and a landlady who asked fewer questions than the furniture. Isolde spread her maps across the floor while I sat at the sill and did what I'd been dreading since the gate.

I listened.

It came in layers, the way the city's sounds always would now. First the ordinary life—dogs barking their territorial poetry, cats conducting their nightly audits of the alleys, rats in the dye-yard walls arguing about a dropped cabbage. Ordinary voices, ordinary wants. I could have listened to them forever. They were the world as it was meant to sound.

Beneath them, the other thing.

It started as a wrongness in the chorus, a note held too long. Then it resolved, voice by voice, into distinct cries, and I understood what I was hearing, and I wished with my whole body that I couldn't.

The palace menagerie.

Everyone knew the crown kept beasts—had kept them for generations, first as trophies, then, in the last decades, as something else. Old Wren had told me what the gift-draining was before she died: Veyne's engineers had found a way to siphon a gifted creature's magic the way you'd tap a maple, slow and systematic, until what remained was a husk that ate and breathed and felt everything and could do nothing. The power fed the crown's wards, its scrying, its endless hunger for control. The beasts fed it with themselves.

I had known that. Knowing and hearing are different countries.

There was a wolf up there whose mind had been scraped so smooth she no longer remembered her pack's names but still howled them anyway, syllables worn blank, calling to ghosts. There was a marsh-dragon, old beyond old, who had been singing the same song of her drowned homeland for forty years into stone walls, and the song had gone from grief to habit to something worse than both—a lullaby she sang to herself because there was no one left to sing it to. There were hawks who dreamed in circles. A bear who had forgotten what forests smelled like and grieved the absence without knowing what he was missing, which is the cruelest arithmetic there is.

And under all of it, threading every separate sorrow together, a single sustained note I took a long time to identify because I had never heard it before:

Hope. Faded, threadbare, nearly dead—but present. They still hoped someone was coming.

My hands were shaking. I gripped the windowsill until the wood bit back.

"Sebastian." Isolde's voice, quiet, from behind me. "What is it?"

I hadn't heard her cross the room. I never heard her anymore; she moved like guilt moves, silent and total.

"The menagerie," I said. My voice came out flat, which frightened me more than shaking would have. Flat was what I sounded like right before I broke something. "Forty-some voices. Maybe more. They're—" I stopped. Started again. "Veyne drains them. Their gift, their memories, piece by piece. There's a wolf up there who can't remember her pack. She howls to them anyway."

Silence from the maps. When I turned, Isolde was standing very still, and her face had gone the color of the reports she used to write.

"I drafted the requisitions," she said.

I waited.

"For the menagerie. Sedatives, chain-grease, dietary supplements for creatures I was told were 'assets.' I signed them as routine. I never—" Her jaw worked. "I audited their suffering and called it logistics."

"Yes," I said. Not cruelly. Cruelty would have required effort, and all my effort was going somewhere else, down a dark corridor inside me where something was pacing and growing larger.

Because here was the truth I was holding at arm's length: I wasn't horrified the way a good man is horrified. I was horrified the way a weapon is horrified—cold, focused, taking measurements. Some part of me had already begun counting guards, mapping the palace's inner roads, noting which towers stood over the menagerie's wing. Some part of me had heard that wolf howling to her nameless dead and thought: *soon.*

Old Wren had died so I could reach this city. The refugees had walked into the mountains carrying nothing so I could walk in here carrying forged papers. Every voice I'd ever heard, every beast that had ever trusted a ledger-keeper with a secret, had spent itself getting me to this exact window, this exact night.

And the menagerie was singing to me like a choir that had rehearsed for years without knowing why.

"We should eat," Isolde said finally. "And sleep. Tomorrow we start on the archive access routes. If we're going to move against Veyne, we do it with precision, not—"

"They know," I said.

She frowned. "Who knows?"

"The beasts. In the menagerie." I turned back to the window, to the dark mass of the palace rising against the stars like a headstone for something that used to be a kingdom. "They don't know my name or my face. But they know the covenant's heir exists. Word travels in ways we don't have words for. And they're singing to me, Isolde. Every night. Asking." I swallowed. "Not asking to be freed, even. Asking to matter. Asking if anyone remembers what they were before the chains."

Behind me, very softly, Isolde said: "What are you going to tell them?"

The honest answer was *nothing yet*, because telling them anything was days away and reckless and might burn our whole infiltration. The wise answer was the one she wanted, about precision and patience and archives.

What I said was: "Soon."

The word left me quietly, but it left me the way a keystone leaves a hand—final, weighted, irrevocable. Isolde heard it. I saw her decide not to argue, and I saw what it cost her, and I understood that she was watching the man she'd betrayed become the man she'd chosen to follow, and that the distance between those two men was measured in exactly this kind of moment.

"Then we work fast," she said, and rolled out her maps.

* * *

We worked three days as clerks before the city tried to kill us, which for the Gilded City was practically hospitality.

Our cover held beautifully. I reconciled fictional accounts in the provincial annex with genuine pleasure—there is a deep, shameful comfort in tidy numbers when your actual life is an untidy catastrophe—and Isolde charmed the archive under-clerks with the effortless authority of a woman who had once terrorized them professionally. By the third evening we had the palace's delivery schedules, the guard rotation for the menagerie wing, and confirmation of the thing we'd come for: Corvane had arrived two weeks ago and was quartered in the guild's capital branch, three streets from the palace, under the chancellor's personal protection.

He was close. The third keystone beat inside his chest a few streets away, and he didn't know we were here.

On the fourth night, walking back to the Inkwell through the lamplighters' hour, I made a mistake. Or perhaps not a mistake—perhaps the oldest kind of listening, the kind that doesn't ask permission.

An alley cat sat on a wall above me, thin as a folded letter, watching the street with the flat patience of her kind. As I passed, she spoke—not to me, just to the night, the way strays do, a small commentary on the unfairness of the world. But under her words ran the city's other current, the menagerie's chorus, and tonight it had changed.

Tonight they weren't asking.

Tonight they were counting.

Guards. Gates. Hours between patrols. The marsh-dragon's ancient song had shifted key and become, unmistakably, a tally. Forty years of captivity distilled into arithmetic. They were measuring their prison the way I measured ledgers, and they had arrived at the same conclusion I had: the numbers were possible. The numbers were *good*.

The cat looked down at me, and her eyes caught the lamplight, and in them I saw reflected something I hadn't put there myself.

"Soon," I told her again, barely a breath.

And from the palace hill, faint but perfectly clear, forty voices answered—one note, held, like a drawn bow.

Isolde touched my elbow. "Walk," she murmured. "Don't look up. We're being followed."

I walked. Behind us, footsteps matched ours step for step, patient and professional, and ahead of us the palace glowed gold against the dark, and inside it the choir sang its counted song, and somewhere in the guild branch three streets away, a dying man with a stolen heart slept badly, dreaming, perhaps, of ledgers.

Tomorrow, Isolde and I would find out who was following us.

Tonight, for the first time since Ironhollow burned, I wasn't afraid of what came next.

I was afraid of how much I wanted it.
