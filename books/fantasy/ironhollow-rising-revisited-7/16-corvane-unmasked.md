## 16. Corvane Unmasked

The guild's capital branch smelled the way Sebastian remembered it—wax, ink, and old money—but wrong, somehow, like a song played a half-step flat. He stood in the shadow of the colonnade across Copperlane Square and let Isolde adjust the collar of his clerk's coat for the third time.

"Stop fidgeting," she murmured. "You look exactly like what you're pretending to be. That's the whole point."

"That's what frightens me." He watched clerks stream up the marble steps with their ledger-satchels, gray men in gray coats, each one carrying a piece of the crown's arithmetic into that building. For eleven years he had been one of them. He had been *good* at it. "I spent my whole life trying to be invisible, Isolde. Now I'm walking back into the cage on purpose."

"You're not invisible anymore." Her eyes flicked to his—quick, assessing, the auditor's habit of measuring everything against its true value. "You haven't been since Ironhollow. Invisible men don't have bounties."

*Two thousand crowns, alive or dead.* The proclamation had followed them down three roads. He'd read it by candlelight in a waystation stable while the horses pressed close around him—not because they could help it, but because even now, even muffled as his gift was behind wards and distance, animals leaned toward him like plants toward a window.

He'd stopped asking himself whether he was doing the right thing somewhere outside Vesswater. The blight had answered that question for him: a wheat field gone to gray glass, stalks frozen mid-shudder, and a farmer sitting at its edge with his hat in his hands, not weeping, just staring, as if grief had passed beyond tears into something geological.

Corvane had helped break the covenant. Corvane held the third keystone. Everything else was bookkeeping.

"Ledger office is on the second floor," Isolde said. "Corvane keeps private quarters above it. He arrived from the eastern road two days ago—he'll be expecting reports on the hunt's progress, which means he'll expect *me*." She touched the satchel at her hip where her old commission papers still lived, forged now, but convincing. "I walk in the front. You go through the records room the way you used to when you were checking figures you weren't supposed to check."

"And if he's there?"

"Then you finally get your answer." She said it lightly, but her hand lingered a half-second on his sleeve before she turned away. "Sebastian. Whatever you find up there—the man who sold you wasn't doing it because he hated you. Remember that. Hatred is simple. What Corvane has is worse."

She crossed the square without looking back, becoming a clerk among clerks, and Sebastian felt the strange vertigo of watching someone walk into danger on his behalf. It was becoming a habit he did not intend to keep.

* * *

The records room hadn't changed. Same iron cabinets, same smell of dust and sealing-wax, same narrow window with its view of nothing but the neighboring wall. Sebastian moved through it on muscle memory, past the cabinets where impossible things were filed under respectable names—*deferred payments*, *contingent assets*, *long-term holdings*—because the Guild of Impossible Things had always understood that the most powerful magic was making monstrous things sound mundane.

He heard the palace before he saw it.

It came through the window, faint, threaded under the city's noise: the menagerie. He'd been hearing it since they entered the capital two days ago, a chorus of misery so constant he'd begun to think of it as weather. Lions pacing the same twelve feet of stone until their minds wore smooth. A gryphon—*a gryphon*, here, caged in the heart of the city—screaming in frequencies only he could hear, a sound like a knife being sharpened on grief. And beneath it all, something older and quieter, beasts so drained of gift they could barely form thoughts anymore, just pulses of want: *out, out, out.*

His hands curled against the cabinet. *Soon,* he told them, knowing they couldn't hear him, or hoping they couldn't. *Hold on.*

The service stair to Corvane's quarters was unlocked. Of course it was. Corvane had never believed anyone would dare.

Sebastian climbed.

* * *

Guildmaster Aldous Corvane sat at his desk with his back to the door, and for a moment—just a moment—Sebastian saw him as he had at fourteen, when he'd first been taken into the guild's accounting house: a tall, precise man with silver at his temples and a voice like a well-kept ledger, terrifying and magnificent, the person who had once said *you have a good eye for numbers, boy* and thereby saved Sebastian from whatever uncertain fate waited for friendless orphans with odd talents.

Then Corvane turned, and the years fell away like ash.

He was dying. There was no other word for it. The skin over his cheekbones had gone translucent, stretched thin as vellum over something that didn't fit inside him anymore. His hands, resting on the desk, trembled with a fine continuous shiver, and when he breathed there was a wet rattle underneath, as though his lungs were slowly filling with something that wasn't air.

"Sebastian." No surprise. No alarm. He looked at the boy he'd sold to the crown as if he'd been expecting him for tea. "You got my message, then."

"You didn't send a message."

"I sent Isolde Marr to find you. Years ago. I paid for her commission myself." His mouth bent—not quite a smile. "Did you think the crown's best hunter found you by accident? I needed you found, boy. Just not by *them*. She was supposed to bring you to me. She took a detour." Something moved behind his eyes—too tired to be anger. "Loyalty. It's the one trade I never learned to price correctly."

Sebastian's blood went cold and then hot. "She works for you?"

"She worked for Veyne. I bought her contract six months ago, when I understood what he intended." Corvane rose, unsteady, one hand braced on the desk, and Sebastian saw him sway and correct, sway and correct, like a mast in bad weather. "Sit down, Sebastian. You came all this way. Let an old man tell you how he ruined the world."

"I came for the keystone."

"I know." Corvane laughed—a dry, cracking sound, like a page tearing. "Straight to the accounts. You were always my best student." He opened the top drawer of the desk and drew out a single sheet of paper, yellowed, sealed with black wax. "But you can't take what I have. Not the way you mean. So first, the history. Every debt needs its ledger entry."

Sebastian didn't sit. But he didn't leave either.

"Fifty years ago," Corvane said, "I was a junior factor in this very branch. Ambitious. Clever. The guild's business was declining—the old trades, memory and years and names, were drying up because the covenant made wild magic too expensive to touch. Do you understand? The covenant *protected* people like you. It set a price on power so high that most folk sensibly left it alone." He tapped the desk. "And I found the flaw. A clause. The covenant binds human realm to wild realm through three keystones and one speaker's line—and the keystones must remain *held*. Held by willing keepers. But nowhere—nowhere—did it say the keeper had to remain *outside* the power."

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. "You swallowed it."

"One fragment. The third keystone, broken into three pieces by the original maker precisely to prevent—" Corvane paused, and something ancient and bitter crossed his face. "Precisely to prevent what I did. I took it into my body, thinking I would become a conduit. A reservoir. I thought I'd hold the covenant's power the way a bank holds gold." His hand drifted to his chest, almost unconsciously, pressing against the sternum as if something in there knocked to get out. "Instead it became a loan I can never repay. The power doesn't flow through me, boy. It *eats*. Slowly. Fifty years of slow. And every year I've grown hungrier and weaker both, and every year the covenant has starved a little more, because its third keystone is locked inside a man who cannot give it freely."

"The keeper must be willing," Sebastian whispered. Old Wren's voice in his memory. *The stone knows a grasping heart.*

"And mine has been grasping since before you were born." Corvane sank back into his chair, and for a moment the mask of the guildmaster slipped entirely, and what remained was just an old man in a great deal of pain. "Veyne knew. He's known for thirty years. He's been feeding off the covenant's bleed-through the way a tick feeds off a wound I made. And now the wound is killing us both, and he needs a Winterbourne's blood to patch his throne before it all comes down." He looked up, and his eyes—gods, his eyes were *yellowing*, the whites going amber like old varnish. "So. Here is the entry, Sebastian. Written plainly, as you'd want it."

He slid the paper across the desk. Sebastian didn't take it.

"What do you want?" Sebastian asked. "Why tell me any of this? Why not simply hand me to Veyne and collect your governorship?"

"Because handing you over buys me five more years of *this*—" he gestured at his own failing body with contempt, "—and giving you the keystone might buy the world another century. Even a grasping man can do arithmetic." He smiled, and it was terrible, because part of it was genuine. "Besides. You want to know the worst of it? The thing I've never said aloud?" He leaned forward, and the rattle in his chest sounded like distant surf. "I've heard them too. All these years. The keystone gave me a splinter of the gift—enough to hear the beasts in the guildyard, the rats in the walls, the horses. Enough to know exactly what they thought of me." His voice dropped. "Do you know what fifty years of being *hated* by everything alive sounds like, boy? It sounds like this." He tapped his chest, over the thing that gnawed him. "They weren't wrong about me. That's the hell of it. They were never once wrong."

Sebastian had come up those stairs full of hatred—had rehearsed it on the road, cold and clean, a blade he could use. And now he stood in front of the man who had orphaned his childhood trust, sold his life, broken the world, and found... a dying animal in a trap of its own making, gnawing its own leg for fifty years.

He hated him. He pitied him. The two feelings occupied the same space in his chest and neither would yield, and it was the most uncomfortable thing he had ever felt.

"The horses," Sebastian said quietly. "In the guild stables. When I was a clerk. They told me things about you."

Corvane went very still.

"They said you came to the stalls some nights. Late. When you thought no one saw. You'd stand at the far end and just—listen. To them. For hours." He watched the color drain from what little color Corvane had left. "You couldn't help it. The keystone made you listen. And they said you wept, sometimes. An old man weeping in a horse stall at midnight, hearing what the beasts thought of him."

"*Enough*—" Corvane's voice cracked like green wood.

"It's the only honest thing you ever did," Sebastian said. "Standing there. Listening anyway."

For a long moment the guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things sat with his head bowed over his desk, trembling, and looked—for the first time since Sebastian had known him—exactly his age.

"The fragment," he said at last, hoarse. "It can't be cut out. Believe me, I've employed surgeons. It's not flesh. It's *word*—the maker's own speech, made solid. It comes out the way it went in, or not at all." He raised his eyes. "The way it went in was through my throat, boy. Swallowed whole, freely given—to myself. Greed's signature on greed's contract." A pause. "But words written can be unwritten. If the covenant's heir stands before the keeper and speaks the old revocation—the words your ancestor wrote to bind it—then the stone must answer its maker's blood. It will come out. Through the chest. And it will likely kill the keeper in the doing."

Silence. Somewhere below, a clerk laughed at something; the ordinary world went on with its ordinary arithmetic.

"So that's the price," Sebastian said. "Your life for the keystone."

"My life is already spent. This is just the interest coming due." Corvane pushed back his chair and stood, swaying, and began to unbutton his coat with shaking hands. "Ask me, Sebastian. Ask properly. Make me say it. I've earned the humiliation; don't rob me of it."

Every year of Sebastian's life argued against what he did next. Eleven years of hiding. The pyre decree. The hunt. Old Wren falling at the gate. The menagerie screaming through the walls of the gilded city. He let all of it stand in him for one long breath—and then he set it down, deliberately, the way you set down a weight you intend to carry again later.

"Aldous Corvane," he said, and his voice came out steady, "keeper of the third keystone. Will you release it?"

The old man closed his eyes. When he spoke, his voice was almost peaceful.

"Gladly. Gods forgive me—it's the first thing I've ever given away free."

He tore open his shirt.

The thing in his chest was visible now—had perhaps always been visible to anyone who knew how to look. A shape beneath the skin, angular, amber-dark, glowing faintly like a coal wrapped in paper-thin flesh. Veins of light crawled outward from it across his ribs, black-gold, spreading. It had been spreading for fifty years. It had nearly finished.

"Speak the words," Corvane gasped. "Quickly. It *knows* you're here—it's been fighting me since you entered the city—"

Sebastian didn't know the words. He had never been taught them. But Old Wren had pressed a scrap of vellum into his hand at the gates of Ironhollow, kissed his forehead, and said *when the hour comes, your blood will remember what your mind forgot*—and now, as he opened his mouth, something older than memory rose through him like water finding its level.

He spoke in a language he had never learned, and the room *heard* it. The candles bowed. The windows rattled in their frames. Down in the square, every pigeon took wing at once.

Corvane screamed.

The sound was not entirely human. The fragment fought—of course it fought; it had spent fifty years learning the taste of a life, and it did not intend to be evicted. Light burst from the guildmaster's chest in jagged veins; the desk split; ink bottles shattered; and Sebastian kept speaking, tears streaming down his face, hating and pitying and grieving all at once, pouring the revocation into the air like water onto a fire that did not want to die.

And then—with a sound like a word being unsaid—the keystone came free.

Corvane caught it as it fell. Or it caught him. For one instant the old man held the fragment of the covenant in both hands, and it blazed between his fingers, and his face was transfigured—young, suddenly, all the borrowed years draining out of him at once, leaving only what had always been underneath: a clever, lonely boy who had wanted too much.

"Sebastian." His voice was smoke now, barely there. "The menagerie. Under the palace. Veyne drains them to feed the spire. Free them and the spire starves. That's your diversion—that's your *door*—" Blood flecked his lips. "And Isolde. Her contract. Veyne holds her sister. In the tower cells. Tell her—I'm sorry—I traded in children too, in the end—"

"Corvane—"

"Don't waste my last words on my name, boy." The ghost of the old smile. "Waste them on the work." His hands, still cradling the keystone, pushed it toward Sebastian with the last of his strength. "Take it. Finish the ledger. Mark my account... *closed*."

And Aldous Corvane, guildmaster, traitor, thief, penitent, died with his eyes open and his hands empty, looking—for the first time in fifty years—at peace.

Sebastian knelt beside him for a long moment. Then he closed the old man's eyes, took up the keystone—warm, heavy, humming like a living thing—and folded Corvane's hands over his breast, the way you'd close a ledger at the end of a long day's reckoning.

When he stepped into the corridor, Isolde was standing at the top of the stairs, pale as paper, her forged commission crushed in her fist.

"You heard," Sebastian said.

"Every word." Her voice shook. "My sister. He said Veyne has my sister."

"Yes."

Isolde closed her eyes. When she opened them, the last of the crown's auditor was gone from them, burned away like fog in sun. "Then we're done sneaking," she said. "The menagerie. Tonight. We break it open, we free them all, and we take the fight up that spire before Veyne knows his kingdom is bleeding."

Below them, faint through the floors, the caged voices of the palace beasts rose in a chorus—and for the first time, Sebastian heard something new threaded through the grief and the *out, out, out*.

Hope.
