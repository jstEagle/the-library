## 17. The Menagerie Breaks

The palace menagerie had been screaming for eleven years.

Sebastian heard it the moment he crossed the threshold of the lower galleries, disguised in a tax-clerk's gray coat with Isolde's forged seal at his collar. It was not one voice but a chorus, layered like sediment: the lion's grief ground down to a low continuous moan, the snow leopard's silence so total it rang louder than sound, the wolf-kin pacing a cage too small for its memories of running. Gift-drained beasts, Veyne's private treasury. Their magic siphoned through copper conduits into the spire above, their voices left behind like coins dropped down a well.

He pressed his palm to the cold iron of the first cage and let them hear him.

*I'm here,* he said, in the old way, the wordless way that was never really wordless. *I'm here, and I'm sorry it took so long.*

The lion stopped mid-stride. Its head turned, yellow eyes finding his through the bars, and Sebastian felt the moment land in his chest like a struck bell — the moment a caged thing remembers it is not alone.

*Winterbourne,* the lion said, and its voice was dust and embers and an old, old patience. *We smelled your blood on the wind three days past. We have been waiting.*

*Not much longer.* He moved down the row, hand trailing across iron, touching each lock in turn. The locks were guild-work — Corvane's own design, he realized, warded against speakers, which meant they were warded against exactly one kind of key. *I need a diversion. I need chaos. Can you give me chaos?*

The snow leopard laughed. It was not a comforting sound. *Little clerk. We have been rehearsing chaos for eleven years.*

* * *

Isolde Marr stood alone in the Hall of Records with a lantern, a crowbar, and the particular calm of a woman who has already burned her life down once this month and found the ashes useful.

The archive occupied the citadel's eastern wing: three floors of ledgers, writs, and sealed testimony, including — somewhere in the K-section, she'd memorized the shelf — every document pertaining to the Gift-Hunt. Names of the taken. Names of the informants. Names of the paid. If tonight went wrong, those pages would burn with her; if it went right, they would burn anyway, because a hunt dissolved on paper could be resurrected from paper, and Isolde had learned what resurrection cost.

She pried open the first case and began feeding writs to her lantern.

It took four minutes for the guards to notice the smoke. She'd counted on four minutes. What she hadn't counted on was the sound that came rolling through the citadel at almost the same instant — a roar, then another, then a chorus of them, braided together, rising from below like water finding every crack in a dam.

The guards in the doorway looked at each other. One said, "What in the crown's name—"

And then the menagerie broke.

* * *

Later, people who survived that night would describe it differently. A cook swore she saw a bear walking upright through the buttery. A gate-captain insisted the wolves used the stairs, took them two at a time, in formation, like soldiers. The stable-master simply stopped speaking for a week.

They were all true enough. Eleven years of stolen magic had pooled in the beasts like floodwater behind a failing levee, and when Sebastian pulled the last bar loose — iron shrieking, wards unraveling under his gift like wet paper — the flood went everywhere at once.

The lion took the grand staircase. The wolf-kin took the walls, flowing along the parapets like smoke given teeth. The snow leopard went straight up, along cornices and gargoyles, and dropped onto the first squad of hunters who came running with nets and iron pikes, and did not kill them, though everyone agreed afterward it clearly wanted to. *No blood unless they draw first,* Sebastian had said, pouring the words into them as they poured out of their cages. *We are not them. We are not them.*

The peacocks, of all things, were the worst. Forty of them, gift-scarred and half-mad, erupted from the aviary in a shrieking white storm that sent the palace guard into blind, flailing panic. Somewhere a herald's trumpet was trampled. Somewhere Lord Chancellor Veyne's personal banner caught fire from a knocked brazier, and no one put it out.

Through it all, Sebastian ran — up service stairs, through kitchens thick with steam and screaming cooks he gently shoved aside, toward the guild's capital branch where Corvane waited. The beasts' voices streamed around him like a river around a stone, and for the first time since the sparrow died in his hands outside Ironhollow, he did not feel alone inside his own skull.

*East wing's burning,* the wolf-kin reported, breathless with joy. *A small human female started it. She fights well for something without claws.*

*That's my partner,* Sebastian said, and was surprised to find the word cost nothing.

* * *

Isolde held the archive.

She held it longer than any reasonable person should have been able to hold anything, because the guards kept coming in waves of six and eight, and she kept meeting them in the narrow doorway where numbers meant nothing and her crowbar meant everything. Smoke curled along the ceiling. Her lungs burned. Somewhere below, the citadel was coming apart at the seams, and she allowed herself one fierce, disbelieving grin before the next wave hit.

"You're the informant," the sergeant in front snarled. "Marr. Veyne's own auditor. They'll hang you for this."

"They'll have to catch me," she said, and swung.

The crowbar took his shield-edge and spun him into his fellows. She kicked the lantern-oil canister down the stairwell behind her — she'd positioned it an hour ago, everything positioned, Isolde Marr did not improvise anymore, improvisation was how you ended up auditing monsters for a living — and the flame followed the oil down the steps with a soft, satisfied whump.

The guards retreated. Isolde leaned against the burning shelves, coughing, and thought about the fox in the Cinder Reach, the calcified forest, the look on Chancellor Veyne's face when he'd spoken of Winterbourne blood the way merchants speak of stock. She had spent years telling herself she was only following orders, and orders were clean, and clean things couldn't rot.

The archive burned around her like a confession.

* * *

Corvane was waiting for him.

Sebastian should have expected it. The guildmaster had always been waiting; it was his single talent, the patience of a man who had swallowed a piece of godhood and spent thirty years digesting it. He stood in the guild hall's capital chamber beneath the great seal of the Guild of Impossible Things, and he was dying, and he had clearly decided to do it standing up.

"Sebastian." His voice was gravel over hollow ground. Up close, the corruption showed: veins dark as ink crawling up his throat, eyes gone the yellow-green of pond water in late summer. The keystone fragment in his chest glowed faintly through his shirt, pulsing out of rhythm with his heart, as if it were a second heart trying to evict the first. "You brought the whole menagerie down on the citadel. I taught you bookkeeping. Look what you've done with the margins."

"You sold me," Sebastian said. His gift reached without his permission — he could hear Corvane's body, hear the fragment grinding against bone, hear the slow terrible arithmetic of a man being eaten by his own ambition. It made his stomach turn. Not with hatred. With pity, which was worse. "You sold me to buy the hunt's protection. Ten years I balanced your ledgers. Ten years I hid in your cellars, and you priced me like inventory."

"I priced you correctly." Corvane smiled, and there was blood on his teeth. "That was always my sin, boy. Not cruelty. Accuracy."

Behind Sebastian, the chamber doors boomed — hunters trying them, finding them barred, moving on toward louder emergencies. The building shuddered. Dust sifted from the rafters like gray snow.

"The third keystone," Sebastian said. "Wren told me what you did. You didn't just break the covenant. You ate a piece of it."

"And it has been eating me back ever since." Corvane opened his arms, slow, theatrical even now, even dying — but Sebastian heard underneath the theater the truth, plain as a ledger line: *exhaustion*. Thirty years of pain worn smooth as a river stone. "Do you know what power feels like when it isn't yours? Like swallowing a coal that never cools. Veyne offered me a kingdom's worth of covenant-magic if I'd help him crack the stone. I took my share in the only form I could carry." He tapped his chest, once, and winced. "One swallow. That's all it took. One swallow, and I've been drowning from the inside ever since."

"You could have given it back."

"To whom?" For the first time, the guildmaster's voice cracked. "The stone was broken. The wild places were closing. There was nowhere to return it, so I carried it, and it grew, and I told myself I was preserving it — the last fragment, keeper of the shard — and thirty years later here we are." He swayed. Caught himself on the great table. Ink-stained fingers, Sebastian noticed. Even now. Even dying, the man's hands were stained like a clerk's. "Here we are."

* * *

Outside, the night belonged to the beasts.

Isolde fought her way free of the collapsing east wing and stood in a courtyard ankle-deep in torn banners, watching a snow leopard walk calmly along the top of the wall while guards below fired crossbows that might as well have been thrown feathers. A blighted fox — thin, gray-patched, one ear crumpled — trotted at her heel like it remembered her, and maybe it did, maybe kindness left a scent. She scratched its head with the hand still smelling of lamp oil and ash.

Above the citadel, the covenant-spire pulsed with sick light. Veyne's light. She could feel it from here, a wrongness like pressure before a storm, and she knew Sebastian was climbing toward it, and she knew — with the certainty of a woman who had audited enough accounts to know when the numbers finally balanced — that whatever happened at the top of that spire, someone was going to pay the full price.

She just prayed it wouldn't be all of him.

* * *

In the guild chamber, Corvane knelt.

It was not a graceful descent. The fragment had grown restless — Sebastian could hear it, a hungry whispering in a language older than speech, tasting the open air, wanting out. The guildmaster's hands shook as he fumbled at his collar, and when Sebastian stepped forward to help, Corvane flinched back with a snarl that was half beast himself.

"Don't touch it. It knows speakers. It will climb into you like a rat into grain." He laughed, wet and awful. "Thirty years, and it still frightens me. Isn't that something? All that power, and I die afraid of my own dinner."

"Let me—"

"No." He tore the shirt open himself. And there it was: the keystone fragment, grown into him, a wedge of pale stone the size of a fist sunk in flesh below the sternum, its edges veined with black. It glowed when it saw Sebastian. Actually glowed, warm gold, like a dog recognizing home, and Corvane looked down at it with an expression of infinite, tired disgust.

"It likes you," he said. "Of course it does. Winterbourne blood made it. Winterbourne blood could unmake me with a touch." He set both hands against it. "Hold still, boy. This is going to be loud."

"Corvane—"

"I sold you." The guildmaster's eyes came up, and for one unguarded second Sebastian saw the man he might have been — the young clerk who'd once bargained with beasts and lost, before the loss curdled into hunger. "I sold you to save my own skin, and I told myself it was strategy, and it was cowardice, and I have run out of time to pretend otherwise. So take this. Take it, reforge the stone, and when they write the history—" he grinned, bloody, "—spell my name wrong. Do me that one kindness."

Then he tore the keystone out of his own chest.

The sound Sebastian made was not a word. The sound the fragment made was a chord — a vast, resonant chord that cracked the windows and stopped, for three full seconds, every fight in the citadel, every beast and guard and burning banner freezing as the covenant's voice rang out over the gilded city. Black blood poured across the guildmaster's hands. He folded the stone — steaming, singing, slick with thirty years of his life — and pressed it into Sebastian's palms with the last of his strength.

"Forgive me," Corvane whispered. "Or don't. But finish it either way. Finish it, and make my theft mean something better than me."

He died the way a ledger closes: suddenly, quietly, all the columns coming level at once.

* * *

Sebastian knelt beside the body for a long moment, the keystone hot as a living heart in his hands, and found — to his horror — that he was crying.

Not for the man. For the boy Corvane had been, the one who'd bargained and lost and never told anyone what it cost. For ten years of shared ledgers and cellar conversations and the strange, stunted almost-friendship of two people hiding in the same building for different reasons. Grief, he was learning, did not check whether its object deserved it.

*Go,* said a voice at the door — the wolf-kin, gray muzzle flecked with soot, holding the corridor against three hunters with nothing but its presence and its teeth. *The spire, speaker. Go. We hold the doors. We hold everything.*

Sebastian rose. Three keystones: one traded in memory, one won in confession, one bought in blood and begged-for mercy. All of them his. All of them heavy as sins.

Above him, through the shattered roof, the covenant-spire burned green against the stars, and at its summit something ancient and patient was waiting to learn what a Winterbourne was willing to lose.

He climbed.
