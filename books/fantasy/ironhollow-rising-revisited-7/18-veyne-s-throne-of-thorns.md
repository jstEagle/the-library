## 18. Veyne's Throne of Thorns

The covenant-spire had no stairs.

Sebastian understood that now, standing at its base in the heart of the Gilded City, craning his neck toward a needle of black stone that punched through the palace roof and kept going, up into the low grey clouds. The spire was older than the palace around it—older than the city, maybe. The palace had been built *around* it the way a pearl forms around grit, layer upon gilded layer, and every architect for three hundred years had pretended not to notice what sat at the center of their masterpiece.

The beasts had noticed. The beasts had always noticed.

*Up,* the ravens had told him, all morning, from every rooftop between the archive and the inner court. *He waits up. He has waited up for years.*

Isolde stood beside him in the shadow of an archway, still in her hunter's leathers, her hair dark with soot from the burning convoy two nights past. Below them, the citadel rang with chaos—the menagerie's escape had scattered guards like spilled coins, and somewhere to the east a hippogriff was screaming on the battlements while handlers fired crossbow bolts that it dodged with contemptuous ease.

"They'll regroup," Isolde said. "Veyne will have called everything he has to that spire the moment your beasts hit the gates."

"Then we'd better not keep him waiting." Sebastian flexed his hands. In the satchel against his ribs, wrapped in Old Wren's burial cloth, three keystones pressed together like sleeping animals: the drowned pearl of Lake Vess, the ash-giant's grave-counting stone, and the black shard Corvane had torn from his own chest with fingers that shook and eyes that, at the very end, had looked almost grateful. "How do we get up? There's no door."

"There's a door." Isolde nodded at the spire's base, where the black stone met the marble floor. "There's always been a door. It just doesn't open for people the crown wants."

"And it opens for us?"

"It opened for me once. Years ago. I never told anyone." Her jaw tightened. "I was sent to map the palace's hidden ways—my first commission, before I understood who I was really working for. That door opened when I touched it, and I told myself it was a trick of the light. I've been telling myself that for six years."

Sebastian looked at her. She wasn't looking at him; she was looking at the door, at the seam in the stone that only became visible when you stopped trying to see it.

"Maybe it knew," he said quietly. "What you'd become."

"Or what I'd undo." She stepped forward before he could answer, laid her palm flat against the black stone—and the seam split down the middle with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Inside, the spire was hollow, and it was not hollow.

A single stair spiraled upward along the inner wall, carved from the same night-colored stone, lit by nothing Sebastian could name—a light that came from everywhere and belonged nowhere. And filling the center of the shaft, rising through the whole height of the tower like smoke frozen mid-scream, hung something that made his gift recoil: threads. Thousands of them, millions, fine as spider silk and glowing faintly gold, all converging upward toward the summit. They pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like something breathing.

"The covenant," Sebastian whispered. He could hear it now that he was this close—not voices, exactly, but the *space* where voices should be, a vast chord with notes missing. "These are the strands. Veyne's been pulling power off them for decades."

"Not decades." Isolde started up the stair, her voice flat. "Since before I was born. My grandmother used to say the wild places began dying the year the old king died. Everyone thought she meant it as poetry."

They climbed. The stair seemed longer than the spire was tall, which Sebastian decided was a problem for a different day. With every turn, the golden threads thickened, and with every turn, more of them were *severed*—cut clean, their broken ends drifting like cut hair, dimming as they fell. And wound among the living strands, drinking from them, ran channels of darker light that all flowed one direction: up.

Toward the throne.

* * *

There was no throne room at the top of the spire. There was barely a room at all—an open platform beneath the open sky, ringed by a balustrade of black stone, high above the smoking city. The clouds here moved strangely, circling rather than passing, as if the sky itself had been taught to orbit this place.

And at the platform's center stood Lord Chancellor Aldous Veyne.

Sebastian had seen portraits, of course—the lean face, the silver temples, the statesman's calm. None of them had prepared him for how *still* the man was. Veyne didn't stand the way people stood, shifting weight, breathing visibly. He stood the way the Covenant Stone stood. As if he had been placed here long ago and had simply never left.

Behind him, where a throne should have been, grew a chair of thorns.

Not iron thorns, not wooden ones—these were black and gleaming and unmistakably alive, rooted into the spire's stone, their tips crowned with tiny buds that opened and closed like slow mouths. The golden threads of the covenant flowed into the thorn-chair's base and did not come out. Whatever they carried went in, and stayed.

"You brought the keystones." Veyne's voice was mild. Conversational. A man discussing grain tariffs. "I confess I wondered whether you'd make it past Corvane. The guildmaster was always better at surviving than at thinking."

"He's dead."

"I know. I felt it." Veyne turned, and Sebastian saw his eyes for the first time—pale, almost colorless, with pupils that caught the strange light and gave none back. "The third keystone leaving his body was like a tooth pulled from my own jaw. Forty years I fed him covenant-power through that fragment, letting him believe he'd stolen it. Greed is such a reliable leash." He smiled faintly. "You have his shard. And the other two. Which means you've done the hardest part of my work for me, Master Winterbourne. Again, I find myself in your debt."

"You don't know my name."

"I've known your name since before you could walk." Veyne clasped his hands behind his back and began, unhurriedly, to circle the thorn-throne. "Do you imagine the Gift-Hunt was about fear? Fear is a tool, nothing more. No—I have spent forty years managing a slow catastrophe, and the hunt was simply the net I dragged through the kingdom to catch the one fish I needed. Every speaker burned, every gifted child taken to the menagerie and drained—it was wasteful, I'll grant you, but each one narrowed the field. Each death said: *not this one.* Until only a Winterbourne remained."

"You killed them to find me." The words came out of Sebastian very quietly, and very cold. Behind his ribs, the keystones seemed to warm, as if they'd heard too.

"I killed them because the alternative was worse." For the first time, something moved behind Veyne's stillness—something that might, in another man, have been weariness. "Look at the strands, boy. Count the severed ones. The covenant your ancestor forged was a treaty between realms, yes—but treaties require payment, and the payment was *flow*. Power moving from the wild into the human world, and meaning moving back. Balance. When the flow stops, both sides rot. You've seen the blight. The forests calcifying. The rivers forgetting how to run. But you haven't seen what's coming for *us*—the cities going quiet next, the minds going dull, the whole human realm calcifying from the inside out. I broke the covenant because I was young and ambitious and the crown wanted power, yes. I'll own that. But I have spent every year since trying to keep the wound I made from killing everyone."

"So you've been drinking from it." Sebastian looked at the thorn-throne, at the threads vanishing into its roots. "Siphoning what was left. Keeping yourself alive on it."

"Keeping *it* alive." Veyne's voice sharpened, just slightly. "A siphon works both ways, if you're careful. What I draw, I can push back—but less each year. Less each month, now. The stone is failing, Winterbourne. In ten years, perhaps five, the last strand severs and both realms die of the same wound. Unless someone reforges the covenant. Which requires the keystones. Which requires—" he gestured, elegant and terrible, at Sebastian—"a speaker of the maker's blood. To pour the gift into the stone. Permanently."

The wind at the top of the spire had gone very quiet.

"Permanently," Sebastian repeated.

"That is the price. It always was. Your ancestor knew it when he built the thing—he wrote the terms into the stone itself. The covenant is sealed by a voice given wholly to it. Not lent. Not shared. *Given.* The speaker's gift flows into the stone forever, becomes the engine of the new balance, and the speaker lives out their days as any ordinary man. Deaf to the wild until they die." Veyne studied him with those colorless eyes. "Old Wren told you, I expect. Or guessed. They always guess, in Ironhollow. Sentimental people."

Sebastian's hand found the satchel strap. He had known. Some part of him had known since the drowned court, since Nesharra's otter-eyes had watched him with something like pity as he traded away his mother's memory. Every step of this road had been teaching him the same lesson: *everything costs, and the big things cost everything.* But hearing it spoken plainly, in this cold clean voice, with the severed strands drifting down around them like snow—

"How do you know the terms?" he asked. "You broke the covenant. You said so."

"I read them first." Veyne smiled again, and this time the smile had teeth in it, though his voice stayed gentle. "That's the difference between Corvane and me. He swallowed power without reading the contract. I read every word."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Below, faintly, the sounds of the battle reached the summit—muffled, distant, belonging to another world. A bell was ringing somewhere in the citadel. The hippogriff had gone silent or escaped or died; Sebastian couldn't tell, and hated that he couldn't tell.

Then Veyne said, "But I'm not a monster, Master Winterbourne. I'm a chancellor. I deal in alternatives." He spread his hands, and the golden threads nearest him stirred, leaning toward him like plants toward sun. "Consider what you're about to do. You will pour forty years of accumulated gift—your mother's line, her mother's, all the way back to the forge itself—into a cracked rock on a hilltop in Ironhollow. And then you will go home to—what? Wren's town of mud and suspicion? A farm, perhaps. Ledgers. Silence. You'll spend the rest of your life straining to hear a birdsong and hearing only birdsong. Do you know what that silence does to a speaker? I've watched them, in the menagerie, after the draining. They stop eating. Not from grief—from *hunger*. The silence is a wound that never closes."

Sebastian said nothing. His throat was too tight.

"Now consider my offer." Veyne walked to the edge of the platform and looked out over the Gilded City, its smoking quarters, its fleeing crowds, its burning menagerie gates. "The covenant need not be reforged on the old terms. Terms can be *renegotiated*. By the right parties. A new pact—drawn here, at the source, between the heir of the maker and the man who understands the machinery. We seal it together. The gift flows into the stone, yes—but through *us*. Through two anchors instead of one dead rock. The realms stay joined, the blight stops, the hunt ends, the gifted walk free. And the two of us remain what we are. You keep your voice, Winterbourne. Every whisper of it. The horses, the rats, the ravens—all of it, forever, and more besides. More than any speaker has ever held. And in return, the realm gets stability. Continuity. A chancellor who has kept the apocalypse at bay for forty years, and a covenant-heir whose word the wild places trust." He turned back, and his pale eyes were almost kind. "Shared rule. Not a throne—I'm told you'd hate a throne. Stewardship. You would be the bridge, and I would be the bank, and between us we could hold both worlds open for a thousand years."

The words settled over Sebastian like warm water.

That was the trap of them—they weren't cruel words. They were *reasonable* words. He stood there at the top of the world and let them soak in, and he hated himself for how good they felt. Keep the gift. Keep the voices. Never wake to silence again, never strain toward a hedge and hear only wind, never lose the horses' low gossip or the rats' bitter comedy or the ravens' terrible news. And save the world *besides*. Have both. Be allowed, just this once, to have both.

"You've offered this before," he said at last.

"Twice." Veyne didn't flinch. "Your grandfather. Your mother. Both refused. Both died badly, and the covenant kept dying, and here we are."

"My mother came here?"

"Your mother stood where you're standing." Something flickered across the chancellor's face—there and gone, too fast to name. "She was braver than you, I think. Or stupider. History hasn't decided."

Sebastian looked down at his hands. They were steady, which surprised him. He thought of the sparrow in the guild cellar, dying, saying *the hunt has begun*, and how he had dismissed it because the truth was inconvenient. He thought of Nesharra taking his mother's laugh—that specific laugh, the one that came before her best jokes—and the hollow it had left, and how he had learned to live around the hollow the way water learns to live around a stone. He thought of the ash-giant's toll, the names spoken aloud, and how light he had felt afterward, and how ashamed he'd been of feeling light.

Everything cost. The big things cost everything.

And Veyne was offering him a discount.

"No," Sebastian said.

Veyne's expression didn't change. "Think carefully. This is the only—"

"I've thought about nothing else since Lake Vess." Sebastian lifted the satchel off his shoulder. His heart was hammering, but his voice, when it came, sounded like someone else's—someone older, someone with dirt under his nails and a ledger balanced to the last copper. "You want to renegotiate because you're the wound, Veyne. You and your thorns and your siphons and your forty years of *managing* the catastrophe you made. A covenant anchored through you isn't a covenant. It's a hostage situation that lasts a thousand years. My mother saw that. My grandfather saw that. And I'm the third Winterbourne you've asked, so let me save us all some time."

He drew out the keystones. Three stones in his cupped palms—the pearl gleaming wet-dark, the grave-stone rough as a knucklebone, Corvane's shard still faintly warm and faintly wrong.

"The gift goes into the stone," Sebastian said. "All of it. Forever. On the old terms. The ones my ancestor wrote."

For the first time, Lord Chancellor Aldous Veyne moved like a man instead of a monument—fast, and hard, and afraid.

The thorn-throne bloomed.

Every bud along every branch snapped open at once, and from them poured a sound Sebastian had heard only once before, in a calcifying forest: the blight's scream, the sound of the wild places dying, except here it was *directed*, here it had a master, and the master's hand was already rising, and the golden threads above them were whipping like a sea in storm, and Veyne's mild voice cracked wide open into something ancient and furious:

"Then you'll pour it into a *grave*—"

Sebastian closed his hands around the keystones and stepped toward the stone.
