## 1. Small Charms

The market of Ironhollow woke the way it always did: with bells.

Not temple bells—those had been pulled down and melted into warden's manacles twelve years ago—but the flat, civic clangor of the Assay House clock, struck seven times by a mechanism that had never once been wrong. Petra Okonkwo counted the strokes from her stall in the Tanners' Row arcade without opening her eyes. Seven. Time to be small again.

She opened her eyes to gray light and the smell of wet wool, tallow smoke, and the river. Ironhollow in autumn was a city of damp stone and held breath. The fog came off the Hollowwater and pooled in the streets like something poured, and through it the lamplighters moved with their long brass poles, snuffing the night flames one by one. Tam, she thought, not for the first time and not with any particular feeling—or so she told herself—the tall one on the east arc who never spoke to anyone. He snuffed his lamps like a man closing doors gently behind him.

She turned that thought over once, found nothing useful in it, and set it down.

Her stall was three planks of grey pine on two barrels, tucked into the shadow between a chandler's awning and the shuttered front of what had been a shrine before the Binding. She laid out her goods with the economy of long practice: combs of polished horn, buttons carved from pig bone, needle cases, dice, little animals for children—a hare, an owl, a fat-bellied fish—all of them plain, all of them dull, all of them exactly as interesting as any other carver's work in any other market in the city. Nothing here that a Warden's eye would catch. Nothing here that *sang*.

That was the whole art of it, the art no one had ever taught her because no one alive could teach it: to carve so that the carving said nothing. Twelve years ago, when the gods were bound and every working charm in Ironhollow had been gathered up in carts and burned in the Assay Square while their makers watched—and some of the makers burned too—Petra had learned that the only safe magic was magic too quiet to hear. A charm that did nothing was legal. A charm that did almost nothing, that only leaned against the world the way a tired traveler leans against a wall, was invisible. She had built her life inside that margin. She had built it carefully, plank by plank, the way you build anything you expect to live inside for a long time.

She was arranging the dice in their cloth square when the woman came.

Petra saw her first as movement at the edge of the arcade—a woman in a dyer's smock stained blue to the elbows, standing too still amid the morning shuffle of buyers. People in markets moved even when they weren't buying; they drifted, they touched things, they kept their bodies busy so their minds wouldn't have to work. This woman didn't drift. She stood at the mouth of the arcade looking at Petra's stall the way a drowning woman looks at a rope, and after a moment she made herself walk over, one deliberate step at a time, as if each cost her something.

"Which are yours?" she asked. Her voice was roughened, days of crying worn smooth into something flatter. "The carvings. Which ones did you make?"

"All of them." Petra kept her hands moving, straightening a comb that didn't need it. "The buttons too."

"I need—" The woman stopped. Started again, quieter. "My son died. Nine days ago. Fever." She said it the way people said such things in Ironhollow now, quickly and low, glancing sideways—not because grief was shameful but because grief, these days, was a door, and doors were dangerous. "He was six."

"I'm sorry," Petra said, and meant it, and hated how small the words were.

"They say—" The woman's hands twisted in her smock. "They say there used to be charms. Before. For the dead. So they could find their way, or so you could say goodbye properly, or—I don't know what I've heard. It's all stories now. My mother told me stories." Her eyes came up, red-rimmed and desperate, and fixed on Petra's face. "You carve bones. Real bones. I can see it in your work, the way you know them. Is there anything? Anything at all?"

Every answer Petra had was the safe one. *There's nothing. That's all stories. The gods are bound, the dead are gone, buy a comb.* She had said versions of it a hundred times, to a hundred faces, and every time she said it a little more of her died quietly behind her eyes, and that was the price, and she had agreed to pay it, and the arrangement had held for twelve years.

But the woman was still looking at her. And somewhere under Petra's breastbone, in the place where she kept the thing she never looked at directly, something shifted its weight.

"There's no magic in anything I sell," Petra said slowly. "I want to be clear about that. If a Warden asks you where you bought it, you tell them it's a toy, or a keepsake, and it is, that's all it is."

"Yes," the woman breathed. "Yes. Whatever it is."

Petra reached beneath the stall, into the canvas roll where she kept the pieces she hadn't decided about yet, and drew out the charm.

It was small enough to close in a fist: a sliver of sheep's femur, sanded smooth, carved with a spiral that uncoiled from a single point and then, near the end, doubled back on itself and returned home. She'd cut it months ago on a night she couldn't sleep, and she had never put it out for sale because it was the closest she had ever come to carving something true, and true things got people killed. It had sat in the dark of the roll ever since, patient as a seed.

"It's called a returning," Petra heard herself say. "It doesn't do anything. But if you hold it when you think of him—if you talk to it—it gives somewhere for the words to go. That's all. Somewhere for the words to go." She pressed it into the woman's blue-stained hand and closed the woman's fingers over it. "A quarter-mark. For the bone."

The woman paid without counting, which told Petra everything about how much coin mattered this morning. Then she stood there holding the little spiral, turning it, and her face did something complicated—hope and shame and terror all fighting for the same ground—and finally she lifted it to her lips, as naturally as if her grandmother had done it before her and hers before that, and whispered into it.

Petra caught only the shape of the words, not the substance. *Come back. Come back. Mama's sorry.*

And the charm answered.

Later—much later, in the dark, going over it the way you probe a bruise—Petra would try to name what it actually was. Not sound. Sound went into the ears. This bypassed the ears entirely and arrived already inside, the way your own heartbeat arrives, the way pain arrives: a thin, cold thread of presence rising out of the bone and through the woman's cupped hands and into the air of the market, faint as breath on a window. A whisper. A voice with no body behind it, saying—there was no other word for it—*here*. Saying *I hear her*. Saying, in a tone that cracked Petra open along a seam she'd spent twelve years mortaring shut:

*Mama.*

The dead boy was answering his mother through Petra's carving.

The market went on around it. A man haggled over tallow. Two porters argued about a dropped crate. The Assay House bell rang the half-hour, indifferent. No one else heard it—of course no one else heard it; hearing the dead was a dying art in a city that had executed the last of its death-speakers on the steps of the Assay House while the crowd was forced to watch—but Petra heard it, and the hearing went through her like a fishhook through soft flesh, and every instinct she owned screamed the same instruction: *be nothing, be nothing, be nothing.*

Instead she heard herself say, very calmly, from very far away: "Take it home. Don't show anyone. Don't tell anyone where you got it. Burn it if anyone comes asking—burn it and say it was nothing, because it *is* nothing, do you understand me?"

The woman nodded, clutching the charm to her chest with both hands, and for one moment her face was transformed—not happy, nothing so simple as happy, but *lit*, the way a room is lit when a lamp is carried into it—and she said, "Thank you. Oh, thank you. I can say goodbye now. I couldn't before. There was nowhere to put it." And she walked away into the fog with her dead son's whisper folded against her heart, and Petra watched her go and felt the crack run through the wall of her life with a sound only she could hear.

* * *

She should have destroyed the rest of the roll that afternoon. She knew it even as she knotted the canvas closed and shoved it back under the stall. Every piece in it was the same kind of true—the owl with the too-knowing eyes, the comb whose teeth she'd shaped along a sleeping rhythm, three more spirals like the first. If one had woken, the others were waking. Something had changed in the deep water of the world, and her careful little boats were lifting off the mud whether she wanted them to or not.

Burn them. Melt them. Feed them to the chandler's fire next door and spend tomorrow carving buttons, honest buttons, dumb as stones.

She packed the roll into her satchel instead. She told herself it was prudence—you didn't destroy stock in public, someone might ask why—and the excuse fit so neatly over the truth that she could almost pretend not to feel the truth underneath: that her hands, which had been empty of the dead for twelve years, had felt a whisper pass through them that morning, and some starved animal part of her would rather burn than be starved again.

She sold combs and dice until the light went copper. She ate a roll of black bread standing up. She let the market carry her, anonymous among the hundreds of other small lives, and she was almost steady again—almost—when the child stopped at her stall.

A girl, maybe eight, ragged at the cuffs, alone, which was itself unusual enough to notice. She didn't touch the goods. She stood looking at the little carved animals with an expression far too old for her face, and then she looked up at Petra and said, conversationally, the way children say the most devastating things:

"You heard him this morning. The boy in the blue lady's hands." She tilted her head. "You went white as flour. I saw."

The market noise seemed to fall away from Petra like water off glass. "I don't know what you mean."

"He says thank you," the girl said. "For the door." She scuffed her shoe against the cobbles. "He says the dark was very long, and now there's a door, and his mama's voice comes through it, and he isn't scared anymore. He wanted somebody to know." She shrugged, suddenly just a child again, awkward and dismissive. "I don't like telling people things. They look at me like you're looking at me. But he asked nice."

"What's your name?" Petra heard herself ask.

The girl was already backing away, into the crowd, into the fog. "Doesn't matter," she said. "Matter is, they're waking up. All of them. You'd best decide quick which side of the door you're standing on, bone-woman."

Then she was gone, swallowed by the current of bodies, and Petra stood behind her three planks and two barrels with her heart slamming and the satchel heavy against her hip, full of sleeping seeds, full of doors.

Around her the market roared on, buying and selling, blind and loud. Above the arcade the fog was thickening toward evening, and somewhere in it the lamplighters were beginning their rounds, carrying their small flames down through the gray. And in the canvas roll against Petra's side, faint as breath on a window, something whispered.
