## 3. A Priest in the Gutter

The lamps of Ironhollow were the only honest work left in the city, and Tam Voss had come to love them for it.

He lit them at dusk, working the long iron pole down the avenues of the carver's quarter, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the gutters ran amber with tallow and rain. Each lamp took a moment of his full attention: the wick trimmed, the flame caught, the glass hood wiped clean of soot. A small sacrament. The Lamplighters' Guild paid him four coppers a night and never asked why a man with scholar's hands and a burn scar shaped like a sunburst on his forearm would choose such tedious labor. They assumed he was hiding from something. They were right. They simply could not have guessed how large the something was.

Twelve years ago, Tam had been the youngest ordained priest of the Concordance in Ironhollow's history. He had sung the dawn rites in the Temple of Nine Voices with a voice that made old women weep. He had believed—genuinely, stupidly, at nineteen—that the gods loved him back.

Then came the Binding. The king's edict, read from every balcony in the city while the sky itself seemed to hold its breath. The wardens going door to door with their brass scales and their silence-hoods. The temples sealed with lead and law. The priests who did not run were taken to the Square of Quiet, and Tam had watched from a rooftop as they burned—not the men, only their vestments first, and then, when the men refused to stop singing, the men too.

He had run. He had told himself it was strategy, that the faith needed survivors, that a hidden seed was worth more than a harvested stalk. Twelve years later he still told himself that, most nights, and some nights he even believed it.

Tonight, though, he was not thinking about seeds or survival. Tonight he was thinking about a bone charm.

* * *

It had started three weeks ago, with a fishwife named Maren Cole who lit a candle at the sealed temple's outer wall—a crime, but a small one, the kind even wardens ignored—and whispered a prayer to no one for her son's fever to break. The boy lived. Coincidence, said everyone. Children survive fevers all the time.

Two weeks ago, a stonemason pinned a scrap of carved bone inside his coat before descending a scaffold his fellows swore was sound, and the scaffold held when it should not have. He showed the charm around the tavern like a talisman. Coincidence again, said the sensible people, and then stopped saying anything at all when two of them were seen speaking to wardens.

But Tam had spent twelve years learning to hear what the city refused to say. And last night, standing in the doorway of a dying woman's room while her daughter pressed a small carved bird of yellowed bone into the old woman's palm—the daughter bought it from a stall in the carver's market, she said, a woman with quiet hands—the dying woman had opened her eyes, looked past Tam's shoulder, and smiled.

"There you are," she'd said, to the empty air. "I knew you hadn't gone far."

Tam had felt it then, unmistakable after twelve years of nothing: the pressure of presence. Faint as warmth through a wall. Something old, listening through a piece of carved bone no bigger than a thumb.

The gods were not free. But they were not entirely bound either. Something had cracked somewhere in the great machinery of silence, and prayers were leaking through.

And every thread of it led to one stall in the carver's market. To a woman named Petra Okonkwo, whose charms were too small to notice, too plain to arrest, too humble to be magic—which meant either she was the luckiest fraud in Ironhollow, or she was doing deliberately what Tam had failed to do for twelve years: keeping the old ways alive by making them invisible.

He had watched her stall for six days now. Tonight, finally, he would speak to her.

* * *

The carver's market closed at dusk, but Petra Okonkwo stayed late. Tam noticed everything about her routine because noticing was the only prayer he had left. She packed her wares in a wooden box lined with wool—each charm wrapped separately, he'd observed, each one handled like something sleeping. She worked by touch more than sight, her fingers reading the grain of the bone before her knife ever touched it. She was perhaps thirty, dark-skinned, with close-cropped hair gone silver at the temples far too young, and she moved through the world the way water moves through cracks: without disturbing anything, without being disturbed.

She never spoke to anyone longer than courtesy required. She never met anyone's eyes for more than a heartbeat. She had built herself into a person the city could look straight through, and Tam recognized the architecture instantly, because he had built the same thing out of lamplight and anonymity.

He waited until the last of the other vendors had gone, until the market square held only shuttered stalls and the smell of cold fat and the first lamps coming alight along the colonnade. Then he set down his lighting pole, walked to her stall, and stood where she could see him clearly—hands open, empty, at his sides.

"Good evening," he said.

She didn't look up from the charm she was wrapping. "Market's closed."

"I'm not here to buy." He kept his voice low, level, the voice of a man discussing weather. "I'm here about the bird you sold to Sela Wren. For her mother."

The wrapping stopped. Just for a moment—a hitch in the motion, a stillness like a hare hearing a distant hound—then it resumed, smooth as ever. "I sell trinkets. Keepsakes. Nothing more."

"Yes," Tam said. "That's very well done. The plainness of them. The way they're just small enough to escape notice. I've been trying to understand how you manage it, and I think—I think you don't carve power into the bone at all. I think you carve doors, and leave them unlocked, and let whatever's on the other side decide whether to walk through."

Now she looked up. Her eyes were brown and very steady and very tired, and behind the tiredness was something that made Tam's chest ache with recognition: the particular wariness of someone who has been alone with a secret so long that the secret has become the whole shape of them.

"You should go," she said quietly. "Whatever you think you know, un-know it. That's advice, and it's free, which is more than I can say for my charms."

"My name is Tam Voss." He said it plainly, watching her face. "Before the Binding, I served at the Temple of Nine Voices. I was ordained under High Priestess Amara Dey, may the Nine keep her memory, if any of Them still can."

The change in her was subtle but total. Her hands went still on the table. Her breathing didn't alter, but something behind her eyes recalculated, the way a gambler recalculates when an opponent shows an unexpected card.

"That name will get you killed," she said.

"It nearly did, once. I got away." He glanced down the empty colonnade, then back. "May I sit? I've been standing in gutters for twelve years. My knees have opinions."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, with a small jerk of her chin toward the stool behind her stall—the smallest possible concession—she permitted him to exist in her space. He sat. She did not offer him tea. He hadn't expected her to.

"You've heard the stories," he said. "Maren Cole's son. The mason and the scaffold. Old Widow Anselm talking to the air." He leaned forward. "I traced them all. Every rumor of a charm that worked. Six weeks of listening in taverns and confessionals that don't exist anymore, and every thread ends at this stall. At you."

"You traced rumors." Her voice was flat. "That's what wardens do."

"Wardens trace rumors to destroy them. I traced them because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because three weeks ago, for the first time in twelve years, I felt something answer. Do you understand what that means? Do you understand what it's been like, praying into a hole in the world? We were told the gods were bound for our protection. Bound safely, bound permanently, bound out of reach so they couldn't hurt us or help us or ask anything of us. And I have spent twelve years performing the motions of a faith whose object was amputated, and telling myself that was enough, that remembering was enough—"

His voice cracked. He hated that. He mastered it.

"And then a dying woman smiles at the air over my shoulder," he finished quietly, "and I know. I know the binding is thinning. I know something is getting through. And I know it's getting through your carvings."

Petra sat back. In the lamplight her face was unreadable, but her hands—her hands gave her away. They had gone to the edge of the table and gripped it, knuckles pale, as though the conversation were a current trying to sweep her away.

"Say I believe you," she said at last. "Say I'm what you think I am. What exactly are you asking me for?"

"I'm asking you to help me open the door wider."

"No."

The word came fast and final, a door slamming. She began packing her box with sharp, deliberate movements, the audience over. "You want me to carve louder. Bigger. Charms that actually do things, that people notice, that wardens notice. You want me to hang a sign above my stall that says *heretic, please arrest*. I buried myself for twelve years, priest. I made myself dust. And you want me to stand up in the middle of the square and shine."

"I want—"

"I know what you want." She rounded on him, and for the first time he saw the fire under the ash, twelve years banked and burning. "You want your god back. You want your temple and your vestments and your beautiful singing voice praised in the streets again. Well, I want my mother back, and neither of us is getting what we want, and the difference between us is that I understood that and you apparently didn't."

The words landed like blows, precisely because they were half true. Tam sat very still and let them land.

"The dead," he said softly. "Your mother was taken in the Binding."

Her face closed like a fist. "Get out."

"She was a priestess. Or a practitioner. Someone the wardens came for, someone you lost—" 

"I said get *out*."

He rose slowly, palms still open, giving her nothing to fear and nothing to strike. "I'm sorry. That was cruel, and I knew it was cruel when I said it, and I did it anyway because I'm desperate, and desperation is just cowardice wearing armor." He stepped back from the stall. "One thing, and then I'll go, and I won't trouble you again unless you send for me. There's a place called the Undercroft—an old cistern beneath the tannery row. There are others like us. Not many. Forty, maybe fifty, scattered through the city like embers in ash. We've kept the rites alive in fragments. We've kept *names* alive. And in six weeks, on the solstice, we mean to attempt something at the sealed temple that has never been attempted."

She had stopped packing. She wasn't looking at him, but she was listening—he could tell by the absolute stillness of her hands.

"The Binding was a rite," Tam said. "A great working, performed by the crown, sustained by something. Rites can be unwritten. Doors that were locked can be unlocked. But I can't do it alone, and neither can forty lamplighters and dockworkers and midwives. I need someone who knows how to make the gods listen." He held her gaze. "Someone who's been doing it for years, quietly, right under their noses. Petra. The gods are answering your charms. Don't you want to know why? Don't you want to know what's waking?"

For a heartbeat—one long, suspended heartbeat—he thought she would answer. Her lips parted. Something moved behind her eyes like a fish turning in deep water.

Then the moment passed, and she turned away, and picked up her knife, and began scraping shavings from the worktable as though he had already gone.

"Send for me," he repeated. "Or don't. But know that the door you've been holding cracked open isn't going to stay cracked. Things that wake keep waking. And next time, it might not be a fever breaking or a scaffold holding. Next time it might be something with teeth."

He retrieved his lighting pole and walked away down the colonnade, and did not let himself look back, because looking back was begging, and he had already begged enough for one night.

* * *

He lit the rest of his rounds mechanically, his mind elsewhere. The ache in his chest wasn't rejection—or not only rejection. It was recognition. When she had looked up at him with those exhausted, vigilant eyes, he had seen himself: another survivor who had traded the whole bright world for the right to keep breathing unnoticed. Two people carrying the same wound in different places, meeting across a market stall, and finding that the wound matched perfectly.

*We remember the same lost world,* he thought, touching flame to wick, watching the lamp bloom gold against the gathering dark. *She remembers it too. That's why she's so afraid. It's easier to mourn something alone.*

He was three streets from the tannery row when he felt it.

Not heard—felt, the way one feels a change in pressure before a storm. The night went taut. The lamp flames along the street all leaned at once, though there was no wind. And from the direction of the carver's market, faint and impossible and utterly distinct, came a sound Tam had not heard in twelve years:

Singing.

A single voice, low and wordless, threading through the crooked streets like smoke. Not human. Older than human. It rose from the direction of Petra's stall—or rather, from the small wrapped bundle she carried home each night, the one charm she never sold, the one she wore against her skin.

The voice sang three notes and stopped.

And in the silence after, every lamp Tam had lit that evening flickered in unison, like a congregation bowing its heads.

He stood in the gutter with his pole in his hand and his heart hammering against his ribs, and he understood two things with perfect clarity. First: whatever slept in Petra Okonkwo's pocket was not a charm. It was a prisoner. Perhaps the oldest one in Ironhollow.

And second: she had lied to him tonight. Not about refusing—her refusal was real, and he would honor it, for now. She had lied about not caring. No one guards a thing that carefully out of indifference.

Tam turned toward the tannery row and the hidden cistern below it, and began to walk faster, because there was preparation to do, and solstice was coming, and somewhere in the dark a god was singing through a piece of carved bone—and the woman who carried it had no idea yet what she was really holding.

Or perhaps she did. And that, Tam thought grimly, was the question that would not let him sleep.
