## 4. What the Bones Say

The voice came to her at the stall, on the third morning after she burned the charm.

*Cold,* it said. *It's cold where I am. Please. It's cold.*

Petra's knife slipped and gouged a furrow through the rib bone she was carving, ruining an hour's work. She set the knife down carefully, the way you set down something that might bite.

Not again. Not here.

But the voice did not stop. It was small—a child's voice, thin as wire—and it did not come from anywhere in the market. It came from inside her skull, or behind it, in the place where twelve years of silence had built its walls. *Cold. Mama said someone would come. Someone with small hands. Are your hands small?*

"I don't hear you," Petra whispered, lips barely moving, smiling at a customer like a woman commenting on the weather. "I don't hear anything."

*Liar,* said the dead child, without malice. Children never learned to be cruel about truth; they simply handed it over like a stone they'd found.

She sold two charms that morning—plain ones, knuckle-bones polished smooth, nothing carved into them at all—and closed early for the first time in six years. The other carvers noticed. Marta from the dye stall watched her pack up with narrowed eyes, and Petra made herself laugh at something, wave, move slow. Invisibility was a discipline. You could not let them see the seams.

Then she walked out of the market with the child's voice pulling at her like a hook behind her sternum.

*East,* the voice said when she hesitated at the fountain square. *You're going the wrong way. East. Down.*

Down. There was only one thing east and down in Ironhollow, and the city had spent twelve years pretending it wasn't there.

---

They called it the Sump now, officially—the Eastern Reclamation District, slated for drainage and new construction that never came. Unofficially, everyone called it what it was: the pit where they'd put the bodies.

Twelve years ago, when the Binding came down on Ironhollow like a guillotine, the dead had not been the problem. The dying was the problem. Three nights of it—wardens with white armbands going door to door with the lists, and then the fires, and then, when the fires grew too many, the pits. The king's new law had been elegant in its cruelty: magic dies with its practitioners. So the practitioners died, and their children were taken for re-education, and their bones went into the ground east of the wall where the clay was soft and no one would build.

Petra had been fourteen. She had survived because her mother had put a bone charm in her hand and told her to run, and because Petra had done the one unforgivable thing—she had kept a piece of her mother instead of letting her go. She had not thought about the pit in years. She had gotten very good at not thinking about things.

Now a dead child was walking her into it by the hand.

The Sump had gone wild in twelve years—willow scrub and blackberry bramble swallowing the sunken ground, standing water breeding mosquitoes in the wheel ruts of the corpse-carts. The city had fenced it, eventually, more to forget it than to keep anyone out. The fence was rust and gaps. Petra went through one like she'd done it a hundred times, though she never had, and stood at the top of the slope looking down at a field of weeds that was not a field.

*Here,* said the child. *I'm under the crooked tree. But you won't find me. Nobody finds anybody here. That's the whole problem.*

"How many?" Petra asked aloud. Her voice sounded obscene in the quiet.

There was a pause, the way there is a pause when you ask someone how much something hurt.

*I stopped counting,* the child said. *Mama counted but she stopped too. The lady with the warm voice says we shouldn't count anymore. She says counting is just another way of holding on.*

Petra's breath went strange. "What lady?"

*The one who sings. She's right there. Can't you hear her?*

And Petra could.

It rose out of the ground like water rising in a cellar—not one voice but hundreds braided together, worn smooth as river stones by twelve years of singing in the dark. A lullaby. An old one, older than the Binding, older than the kingdom, the kind of song that gets passed mouth to mouth until nobody remembers learning it. Petra knew it because her mother had sung it to her, and her mother had learned it from her mother, and somewhere at the bottom of that chain of voices was the first woman who ever hushed a frightened child in the dark.

Her mother was in the ground. Her mother was in the ground, and she was singing.

Petra's legs gave out. She sat down hard in the wet grass at the edge of the pit and pressed both fists against her ears, which was stupid, which had always been stupid, because the dead didn't speak through ears. They spoke through whatever part of you had once been open. And hers, apparently, had never closed at all. All these years she'd thought herself deaf. She'd built her whole life on being deaf. And the whole time—

*Don't cry,* said the child, distressed. *Please don't cry. The lady says crying makes ripples and the wardens feel ripples.*

"The wardens can't—" Petra started, and stopped, because even as she said it she felt it: a faint pressure, like a change in weather, at the back of her awareness. Something in the city turning its attention east. Or maybe it was only fear. Fear felt like that lately. Everything felt like that lately.

*Sing with us,* the child begged. *It's better when someone outside sings. It comes through clearer. We can almost remember our names when someone outside sings.*

So Petra sang.

She hadn't sung since she was fourteen. Her voice came out cracked and rusty and wrong, and it broke twice in the first verse, and none of that mattered, because the moment the first note left her mouth the braid of voices beneath her surged toward it like plants toward light. The lullaby thickened. For one vertiginous moment Petra heard them all distinctly—not a chorus but a congregation, each soul with its own grain: a man humming bass, a girl harmonizing a third above, an old woman whose voice carried the melody the way a riverbed carries water. And threaded through all of it, unmistakable, the warm voice the child had spoken of, the voice that had taught them the song so they wouldn't have to be alone with the cold—

*"Hush now, little ember, the fire keeps its own—"*

"Mama," Petra said, and the word fell out of her like a tooth.

The song faltered. Stopped. The silence underneath it was worse than any sound.

*Petra?*

Her mother's voice alone now. Small. Astonished. The voice of a woman seeing a ghost, except inverted, except everything about this was inverted.

"Petra" — the name repeated, disbelieving, and then, in Yoruba-accented Ironhollow dialect, the private endearment, the one no one else had ever used — *"omo mi." My child. My child, what are you doing here, why are you here, you have to go—*

"You're here." Petra's hands dug into the wet earth as if she could reach through it. "You've been here. Twelve years. You've been here the whole time."

*Omo mi, listen to me.* The voice sharpened, and twelve years fell away, and Petra was fourteen again being told to run. *You should not be able to hear me. What did you do? What did you carve?*

"Nothing. I swear it, I carve nothing, I make charms with nothing in them, I've been careful, I've been so careful—"

*No.* Grief in the voice now, and something else under the grief, something that sounded horribly like recognition. *No, my love. You were careful. But careful isn't the same as closed. When I put myself in the bone for you—I didn't close the door behind me. I couldn't. I was too afraid of leaving you deaf in a world that kills the hearing. I left it open a crack. Just a crack. And cracks, my love—cracks let the light in. Cracks let everything in.*

"What do you mean, everything?"

Her mother didn't answer directly. Instead her voice turned, addressed outward, downward, to the hundreds around her: *She hears. Do you understand? She hears!*

The response moved through the ground like a tremor. Hope, Petra realized. Twelve years of silence and patience and singing in the dark, and what lived in the pit was hope, still, undrowned, and it was all rising toward her at once—

*Please,* said dozens of voices, then hundreds, overlapping, urgent, gentle, desperate. *Please. Please. Please.*

*Tell the priest,* her mother said, cutting through them, and her voice had gone strange and certain. *The lamplighter. He still carries his god in a lantern. Tell him the door is open. Tell him it's time.*

"How do you know about—"

*We feel him pass every night. Warmth, on the cold streets. The last warm thing in this city. Go to him, omo mi. And Petra—* the voice gentled, broke a little, gathered itself— *whatever happens, whatever you decide, know that keeping me was not a sin. Hear me? Whatever the priests of the old days would have said, whatever you've told yourself for twelve years—love is not a sin. Now go. Before the wardens feel the ripples.*

The voices began to fade, drawing back down into the earth like tide off a mudflat, and the last thing Petra heard, faint and clear, was the child: *Thank you for singing. I remembered my name. It's Olu. My name is Olu.*

Then silence. Real silence, the ordinary kind, the kind she'd spent twelve years mistaking for safety.

Petra knelt in the weeds of the mass grave with dirt under her fingernails and her mother's endearment ringing in her skull, and she understood that her life had just split into before and after, the way a bone splits—one clean blow, and everything on either side of it changed forever.

She got up. She walked out through the broken fence without looking back, because if she looked back she would climb down into the pit and start digging with her bare hands, and digging was not what the dead had asked for.

They'd asked for the priest.

---

* * *

She found him at dusk on Tanner's Row, lighting lamps.

She had meant to approach him differently. She had spent the walk back into the city composing versions—casual, oblique, deniable—but all of those versions died the moment she saw him. He was on his ladder with his taper raised to the lamp glass, and the light caught his face, and he looked exactly like what he was: a man performing a small holy act in a city that had outlawed holiness, doing it every night, knowing it meant nothing to anyone, doing it anyway.

"Lamplighter," she said.

He turned. Recognition moved across his face—not surprise, exactly. More like a man watching a door he'd knocked on finally open a crack.

"Carver," he said. "Petra Okonkwo." He climbed down slowly, setting the taper in its hood. Up close he smelled of lamp oil and, beneath it, faintly, of incense—frankish resin, the kind that hadn't been legally sold in Ironhollow for twelve years. "I wondered how long you'd take."

"You knew I'd come?"

"I hoped." He glanced up and down the row. Evening foot traffic, a fishwife hauling her barrow, two boys fighting with stick-swords. Nothing that looked like white armbands. He stepped closer anyway and lowered his voice. "Three nights ago a charm of yours answered a prayer in the Saltmarket. Two nights ago, a warden took a man from the carvers' quarter for a charm that healed his daughter's fever. Last night—" he hesitated, studying her face— "last night I felt something from the east. From the Sump. Something old waking up. And this evening you're standing in front of me looking like a woman who's seen a ghost."

"I've seen four hundred ghosts," Petra said. "Give or take."

Tam went very still.

"They're in the pit," she said. "Under the crooked willow, under all of it. They never left, Tam. They've been down there for twelve years, singing, because the Binding didn't kill them—it *kept* them. And they're awake now, all of them, and they know my name, and my mother—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through. "My mother is down there. She's been down there the whole time. Every year I lit a candle for her rest and she was lying in the mud singing lullabies to dead children so they wouldn't be scared."

The fishwife's barrow squeaked past. Tam waited until it was gone.

"Your mother was bound?" he asked quietly. "She was on the lists?"

"She was a hedge-priestess. Bone-singer, like her mother before her. She healed a warden-captain's son during the fever year and the fool thanked her in writing." Petra laughed, and it came out wrong. "Gratitude. That's what killed her. That's what put her in the pit."

"And the others. How many are bound there?"

"Hundreds. Maybe more. They've stopped counting." She wrapped her arms around herself. The dusk was cooling and she was shivering and she let it show, because hiding it suddenly seemed like one lie too many. "They asked me to find you. My mother did. She said—she said you carry your god in a lantern. She said you'd know what to do."

For a long moment Tam said nothing. He looked at her the way a starving man looks at bread—not greedily, but with a kind of frightened reverence, as if abundance itself had become unbelievable.

"Come with me," he said. "Not tonight—tonight go home, bar your door, touch nothing, carve nothing. Tomorrow night. There's a cellar under the old tannery. There are people you need to meet, and things I need to tell you that you will not believe, and I'd rather you hated me indoors than in the street." He paused. "And Petra. Thank you for coming. I know what it cost."

"You don't know anything about what it cost."

"No," he agreed softly. "But I know what it costs to survive something everyone else didn't. I've been paying that price for twelve years too. Different coin. Same mint."

She turned to go, and his voice stopped her.

"The child," he said. "The one who led you there. Did it give you a name?"

Petra's throat tightened. "Olu. It said its name was Olu."

Tam closed his eyes. When he opened them they were wet, and he did not wipe them, and somehow that undid her more than anything else—the lamplighter standing in the gathering dark letting himself be seen crying.

"Olu Adesanya," he said. "Six years old. Daughter of the dyer on Cordwain Street. She was on my congregation's rolls. I performed her naming." His jaw worked. "I said the words over her when she was nine days old. *A light kindled, a light returned.* And then the Binding came and I ran, and I let them dig the pit, and for twelve years I've told myself there was nothing—nothing—I could have done."

"There wasn't," Petra said. "There isn't. That's the whole point of a pit."

"Yes," said Tam. "That's what I've told myself. But the dead have stopped agreeing, haven't they? The dead have started asking questions." He picked up his taper and relit it with steady hands, and the flame threw his shadow long across the cobbles. "Tomorrow night, then. Bring your knives, carver. All of them."

"Why?"

"Because," Tam said, touching flame to the lamp glass, and the light bloomed between them, warm and defiant and utterly illegal, "if the door is truly open—if the bound are truly waking—then everything changes tonight. Not the war we planned. Not the rebellion we imagined. Something older. Something the crown has been feeding on for twelve years without anyone knowing." He met her eyes. "Tomorrow I'll tell you what's wearing the king. And then you'll understand why your mother sent you to me—and why neither of us will ever sleep properly again."
