## 5. The Hollow King

The lamplighter's coat itched. Tam had worn it for three years now and had never stopped noticing the itch, the way coarse wool ground against the collar of a man who had once been robed in white silk embroidered with the sigils of six gods. He told himself the itch was penance. Some mornings he believed it.

He stood at his post on Solder's Row with his brass pole in hand, one lamp already lit behind him, waiting for the sun to drop low enough that lighting the next would not look like madness. Below him, Ironhollow was dressing itself in bunting. Red and gold cloth hung from every balcony along the Processional Way, and children perched on their fathers' shoulders waving paper flags stamped with the crowned circle of the Solvent Throne. Somewhere a fife-and-drum band was tuning, the notes souring off the stone walls. The whole city smelled of oil and roasting meat and the particular nervous excitement of people who have been ordered to celebrate and intend to do it properly.

The solstice procession came twice a year: midsummer and midwinter, the king riding from the palace to the Grand Temple of the Binding to renew the vows of the city — *no gods above the crown, no magic below the law* — before a crowd of forty thousand. Tam had watched it every year since he'd come down in the world from priest to lamplighter. The first year, he had wept. The second, he had prayed, quietly, into his sleeve, for the soul of the man on the white horse. By the third year he had learned to watch the way a surgeon watches: coldly, cataloguing, looking for what was wrong.

This year he saw it before the king even entered the square.

* * *

It began with the birds.

Ironhollow's pigeons were fat, shameless things, bred on market scraps and bold as tax collectors. They mobbed any procession, swooping for dropped bread, landing on banners, fouling the shoulders of dignitaries who dared stand still too long. But as the vanguard of the royal guard turned onto Solder's Row — twenty lancers in black-lacquered plate, the crown's sigil blazoned on their shields — the pigeons went up all at once, a single grey explosion of wings, and did not come down. They circled high over the rooftops, higher than Tam had ever seen them fly, wheeling in a slow, tight spiral like water finding a drain.

Then the dogs began. Not barking — keening. A low, continuous sound rising from cellars and courtyards and the yards behind the tanneries, the noise dogs make at funerals, at earthquakes, at things their eyes cannot parse but their blood remembers. Tam set his hand flat against the warm brick of the wall beside him and felt, or imagined he felt, a tremor under the skin of the city, deep and rhythmic, like a heartbeat heard through a floor.

*They know,* he thought. *Every animal in this city knows what's coming down that road, and none of them will say it, because they can't.*

A woman near him hushed her child. "It's just the drums, love."

It was not the drums. The drums hadn't started yet.

* * *

Tam lit his lamp as the light failed, more to occupy his hands than anything, and took his place among the crowd pressing against the barriers. He had chosen this spot deliberately. From here, the Processional Way ran straight toward him for two hundred paces before bending past the fountain of the Nine Martyrs, which meant the king would pass within ten feet of him, close enough to see — if you knew how to look.

And Tam knew how to look. That had been his work, once. In the old days, before the Binding, priests of the Concordance had been trained in the reading of souls the way physicians were trained in the reading of fevers. You learned to see the light a living person carried — not metaphorically, not mystically, but plainly, the way you see color. Every soul shone. The strong shone steady; the sick flickered; the dying guttered like candles in wind. It was nothing anyone could teach anymore. The knowledge had died with the temples, burned or buried, and the few men alive who still possessed it kept it hidden the way other men kept forbidden charms sewn into their coats.

Tam kept his hidden behind his eyes, and he used it perhaps four times a year, because using it hurt, and because most of the time what he saw was only the ordinary dimness of a city afraid to shine.

King Aldric rode into Solder's Row at sunset.

* * *

The crowd roared. It always roared. Aldric was a good king by every measure the city had left — twelve years of peace, granaries full, the wardens disciplined and fair-ish, the borders quiet. He had been a young officer of the palace guard when the Binding happened, they said; a soldier who had stepped into the vacuum after the gods were chained and the old royal line had ended screaming, and who had held the city together with his bare hands while it tore itself apart. Forty years old now, broad-shouldered, gray coming in early at the temples, famous for working sixteen-hour days and remembering the names of lamplighters' daughters. The crowd loved him the way you love a wall between you and the sea.

Tam looked at him and felt the cold come up through the soles of his boots.

There was no light in the man.

Not dim light. Not flickering light. Not the guttering of a sick soul or the smothered coal-glow of a despairing one. *Nothing.* Where every other human being on that street burned — the guardsmen bright and hard as forge-fire, the children scattered sparks, even the grim-faced wardens banked coals — King Aldric was a hole in the shape of a man. Tam's sight slid off him the way an eye slides off a blind spot. He had to look *beside* the king to know where the king was, the way you find a missing star by the gap it leaves in its constellation.

Twelve years ago, Tam had seen the king at a distance, at the first solstice rite, and remembered thinking: *there is a man carrying something heavy.* A burdened soul, a grief-darkened one. He had pitied him.

Whatever had been inside Aldric then was gone now. Eaten. And something else wore the space where it had been.

* * *

The king drew rein ten feet away, precisely where Tam had calculated, and raised one hand, and the roar of the crowd swelled and broke against him like surf against a cliff that does not care.

Up close, the wrongness resolved into details.

The face was handsome in a weathered way, the face of a man who had been handsome and was now merely well-preserved, like furniture waxed past its prime. But the skin was too smooth across the cheekbones and too loose beneath the jaw, as though the flesh had been fitted to the skull by someone working from memory rather than measurement. The eyes were brown, ordinary, kind-looking — and utterly still. Not calm. Still. There was a difference, Tam thought, watching them pass over the crowd without pausing on anything, the way a lantern passes over a room without illuminating it. Calm eyes moved. Calm eyes chose what to look at. These eyes received the world the way a wall receives thrown stones.

And the mouth — the mouth smiled. It smiled at exactly the right moments, at exactly the right width, and Tam realized, with a lurch of nausea, that the smile was running slightly ahead of the crowd. When the people cheered louder, the smile widened a half-second *before* they cheered. As if it were not responding to them. As if it were conducting them.

Then the king's gaze crossed Tam, and stopped.

It was nothing. It was a fraction of a second, less — the natural pause of a monarch acknowledging a lamplighter standing at his post, lamp lit, pole shouldered, doing his duty on Processional Day. Any warden would have said so. Tam's own heart said so, hammering, *it's nothing, look away, you've looked away, keep your head bowed—*

But the still brown eyes rested on him for one full breath, and during that breath Tam felt the sensation he had felt only twice in his life: once at his ordination, when the high priest of endings had laid hands on his head; and once, twelve years ago, standing at the edge of the temple district as the sky went black and the screams started, feeling the Binding close over the world like a fist.

The sensation of being *measured*. Of something vast and patient taking his weight, his worth, his usefulness, and filing it away.

The eyes moved on. The horse walked on. The moment passed, and the crowd surged, cheering, and somewhere a fife struck up the Solvent March, and Tam stood very still among forty thousand happy people and sweated through his lamplighter's coat in the cooling evening air.

* * *

The crown.

He made himself look at it, though everything in him wanted to look anywhere else. It rode the king's brow like it belonged there — and that was the horror of it, that it *did* belong there, sat snugger than any crown sits, no gap, no tilt, no human imperfection of fit. It was an old thing, older than the Solvent dynasty, recovered — so the schoolbooks said — from the ruins of the Grand Temple after the Binding, offered to Aldric by the Council of Wardens when they begged him to take the throne. Iron and gold, unadorned but for seven settings around the band, empty sockets where seven stones had once been set.

Seven sockets. Six bound gods. Everyone knew the count was wrong; scholars had argued about the seventh setting for a decade. The god of endings had never been accounted for in the Binding. The official histories said he had been destroyed — the only god killed rather than chained, because a god of endings could not be permitted to persist even in chains. The official histories said a great many things.

Tam looked at the crown with his hidden sight, and the crown looked back.

Where the king was void, the crown was *full*. It blazed — there was no other word — a dense, churning radiance packed into that narrow iron band, brighter than any single soul Tam had ever seen, brighter than a hundred souls, and it was not one light but *many*, countless small lights pressed together like fish in a net, like faces in a crushed crowd, moving, turning, pressing against the walls of their prison—

And feeding. Drawn out of the blaze in a thin, steady stream, down through the iron, into the hollow skull beneath it. The crown poured itself into the king the way a wick draws oil, and the king burned with borrowed fire, and the fire was made of *people*.

Tam's knees nearly went. He locked them, gripped his lamp pole, and put his head down as if overcome by the heat of the crowd, and breathed through his nose, slowly, the way he had taught novices to breathe through terror during their first rites.

*The Binding never ended.*

The thought arrived whole, terrible, obvious, the way the truth always arrives — as something you realize you have known for years and refused to read.

The Binding had not ended twelve years ago. It had *moved*. The old wardens, the founders, whoever they had been — they had chained six gods in the temple district and called it peace, and either they had lied about the seventh, or the seventh had done this to itself, or — worse, far worse — the seventh had been *taken*, dragged from its shattered temple and hammered into a circlet of iron and gold, and its substance used as fuel. And the bound dead — the thousands upon thousands whose souls had been harvested in those first mad years to power the wards, the official number was eleven thousand and the real number no one would ever say aloud — they were in there. In the crown. In the blaze of trapped lights. Burning, year after year, solstice after solstice, to keep a dead man walking and a city quiet.

*Eleven thousand souls,* Tam thought, *and I have spent twelve years lighting lamps for the living while the dead burn to warm a chair.*

* * *

The procession moved past him. The bend at the fountain took the king, the guards, the banner-bearers, the gilded chariot of the High Warden, all of it, away toward the Grand Temple and its renewed vows. The crowd began to disperse in the loose, laughing way of crowds released from reverence, and the fifes played on, and the pigeons came down out of the sky at last, spiraling back to the cobbles to hunt dropped bread, and the dogs stopped keening one by one, embarrassed, as if waking from a shared dream.

Tam lit the rest of his lamps.

He did it mechanically, well, the way he did everything mechanical now, while the rest of his mind turned over and over the thing he had seen. At the corner of Bellfounder's Lane he paused to trim a wick and caught his own reflection in the lamp's glass chimney: a middle-aged man in a cheap coat, soot under the fingernails, nothing remarkable. Twelve years ago he had been Father Tamsin Oduya of the Concordance, custodian of the third-holiest reliquary in Ironhollow, and he had run — that was the truth he built his nights around, the splinter in every prayer — he had *run*, when the wardens came for the temples. He had taken off his ring and walked out through the kitchen garden and left his brothers and sisters to the fires, and he had survived, invisibly, carefully, lighting lamps, telling himself that survival was service, that a hidden priest was a seed, that someday—

Someday. The word tasted like ash.

Because the enemy had never been the wardens. The wardens were men, and men could be reasoned with, resisted, outlived. The enemy was a crown that ate souls, sitting on the head of a corpse that smiled half a second early, and it had been fed every day for twelve years on the strength of everyone who kept their head down. On the strength of lamplighters. On his strength.

He finished the last lamp on his route and stood a long time in the dark at the top of Carver's Stair, looking down at the little quarter below — the crooked lanes, the shuttered stalls, the smell of bone dust and lamp oil. He had traced the working charms here over three weeks of careful asking: charms that answered prayers, in a city where prayer was a hanging offense. He had meant to come tomorrow. To beg help from some carver he'd never met, a woman named Okonkwo, according to his sources, who sold small protections and asked no questions.

Tomorrow felt obscene now. Tomorrow was a luxury for men who still believed the danger was distant.

Below him, in a window on the second row, a candle burned late. Someone was awake down there, carving, or praying, or both.

"Eleven thousand," Tam said aloud to the dark, testing the weight of it. His voice cracked on the number. "Hold on. Hold on. I'm coming."

He shouldered his pole and went down the stair, and behind him the city settled into sleep around its smiling, hollow king, and high above the palace, unseen by any waking eye, the pigeons rose once more into the night and wheeled, and wheeled, and would not land.
