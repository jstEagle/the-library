## 6. Terms of the Living

### Petra

The smuggler's cellar smelled of tallow and wet stone, and of something older underneath—incense burned long ago by people who had not been afraid to burn it. Petra stood at the bottom of the stairs with her arms crossed tight over her chest, counting the exits out of habit. One stairway behind her. A low arch to the left, breathing cold air from some deeper tunnel. A grate near the ceiling, too narrow for a grown woman but wide enough for a child or a desperate cat.

Three exits. That was better than her stall in the market, which had exactly one.

"You came." Tam descended the last steps, lamplighter's coat traded for a plain gray one that made him look like any tired man in Ironhollow, which was precisely the point. His hair was damp from the fog outside. He held a lantern low, so its light pooled at their feet instead of climbing their faces.

"I came to hear what you have to say," Petra said. "Not to agree to it."

"Everyone who comes down here says that." He gestured toward a table shoved against the far wall—a plank on barrels, three stools, a candle stub burning in a dish of salt. "Sit. Or don't. But I'd rather you were sitting when I tell you how bad it is."

She sat. She kept her hands in her lap, away from the little knife she wore at her hip, because he would see the gesture for what it was: fear wearing armor.

Around them, the cellar held shadows that might have been crates, might have been people. She could feel eyes. Not dead ones—living ones, watchful, belonging to whatever network Tam had spent twelve years building in the dark while she spent those same years building small things no one would notice. She had thought herself careful. She had thought herself safe. Then a grieving mother's charm had whispered back at her in the market, and every careful year had begun to crack like river ice.

"The wardens took Marisol Vane yesterday," Tam said. "Third house off Cooper's Row. You knew her?"

Petra's throat tightened. "Everyone in the carver's quarter knew Marisol."

"She carved a blessing-cord for her son's fever. It worked. The fever broke in an hour." Tam's voice stayed level, but something moved beneath it, deep water under ice. "A neighbor reported the smell of incense. By morning there was nothing left of her but a chalk outline on the cobbles where they dragged her, and a notice nailed to her door: *Trafficker in Forbidden Arts.* Her son is eight years old."

"I know." Petra stared at the candle flame. "I heard him crying through the wall."

"Then you know why you're here." He leaned forward, elbows on the plank, and for the first time since the market—the first time since she'd thrown his pity back in his face and told him to go light his lamps—he looked at her without begging. "Your charms are working, Petra. All of them. Every scrap of bone you've sold in the past month has answered someone's prayer. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means I'm careless," she said. "It means I've gotten sloppy after twelve years of being careful, and I'll carve quieter ones, and—"

"No." He said it softly, which was worse than shouting. "It means the Binding is failing. It means the old gods are waking up under the city like sleepers turning over, and they've found your hands and decided to use them. You didn't get sloppy. You got *chosen*."

The word landed in her chest like a dropped stone. Chosen. In the old stories—the ones her mother had told her by firelight before firelight itself became contraband—to be chosen by a god was either glory or ruin, and usually both, and usually ending in a pyre.

"I don't want to be chosen," she said. "I want to be invisible. Invisible is alive."

"Invisible is what Marisol was, right up until she wasn't." Tam reached into his coat and set something on the table between them: a bone charm, small as a thumbnail, carved with the spiral-and-line that was her own mark. Her work. "This came from a dockworker whose daughter drowned in the harbor. He held it and asked the dead girl to forgive him for not watching closer. And the charm—" His voice caught, just once. "The charm wept. Actual tears, warm, on his palm. He came to us asking if he was cursed. He wasn't cursed. He was *heard*, for the first time in twelve years, by something that loved him back."

Petra looked at her own carving resting in another man's grief and felt sick. "You traced it to me."

"I trace everything. It's what keeps me alive." He turned the charm over; her maker's mark caught the candlelight. "Here's what I know that you don't. There are others waking too. A midwife in Lowbridge whose herbs started curing things herbs can't cure. A bell-ringer whose bells ring themselves at midnight. The gods are surfacing all over Ironhollow, but they're coming up thickest through *you*. Through your bones and your knives and your mother's blood in your hands."

"Don't say her name."

"I didn't say her name."

"You said her blood." Petra stood, because sitting still had become impossible. "You don't know anything about my mother."

"I know she died in the Binding," Tam said quietly. "Like mine did. Like everyone's did, one way or another."

The cellar seemed to tilt. Petra gripped the edge of the table. Twelve years she had carried that night alone, sealed up tighter than any charm, and this stranger in a gray coat spoke of it like weather, like something that happened to everyone, and the worst part was that he was right, and the second-worst part was that it helped, a little, to not be the only one drowning.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

"To meet them." He rose too, slowly, giving her space the way you'd give space to a frightened animal—or a frightened god. "The gods waking through your charms. I was a priest, Petra. Before. Of the Quiet Mother, of the Nine Who Listen. I remember the rites. I remember how to speak to them without going mad. If they're rising through your work, then you're standing on the fault line, and when this city breaks—and it will break—you need to be standing next to someone who knows which way the ground moves."

"And what do you give me?"

"Shelter." He said it simply. No flourish. "When they come for you—and they will come, because warden-captains aren't fools and your stall glows brighter every week—my people will hide you. Move you. Feed you. Keep you alive. That's the bargain. Your gift against our silence."

"My gift isn't for sale."

"Everything's for sale in Ironhollow. The king sold the whole sky." He almost smiled. It didn't reach his eyes. "But I'm not buying. I'm asking. There's a difference, and I'm hoping you can still taste it."

Petra looked at him across the candle stub—at the lamplighter's calluses on a priest's hands, at the gray coat over a man who had once worn white and gold and lost everything for it—and felt the ache she'd been refusing since the market. Recognition. Two people who remembered the same lost world: bells that rang for joy, incense that meant welcome, gods who answered because they wanted to and not because a carver begged them to. She had spent twelve years pretending that world was ash. He had spent twelve years keeping its embers in his fists until his palms burned.

"If I do this," she said, "I set terms. Mine."

"Name them."

"One: I unbind no one and nothing until I decide it's right. Not you, not your underground, not your precious cause. My knife, my choice."

"Agreed."

"Two: the child. Marisol's boy. When this is done—if it's ever done—I want him somewhere the wardens can't find him. Tonight, if possible."

"He's already gone. My people moved him at dawn." Something softened in Tam's face. "You're not the only one who hears children crying through walls."

"Three." She hesitated. This term had no logic to it, no strategy, and she hated herself a little for saying it anyway. "Don't lie to me. Not even kindly. I've had twelve years of kind lies from a city that burned my mother. I'd rather have ugly truth from you."

Tam was quiet a moment. "Then here's ugly truth," he said. "If the Binding is truly failing—if the gods are waking—then someone bound them, and that someone is still binding, still feeding the chains, or the chains would have rusted through on their own. I don't know who. I don't know how. But when we find out, it will be worse than we imagine. It always is. And you may wish, before the end, that you'd stayed invisible."

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, Ironhollow went about its lawful, silent evening, and beneath it, in a smuggler's cellar, a carver and a priest shook hands over a bargain neither fully believed in.

"Then we're agreed," Petra said.

His hand was warm. Hers was steady. Both of them pretended that was enough.

### Tam

He walked her out through the tunnels himself, because the alternative was letting one of the others do it, and Tam had learned long ago that trust was a thing you built with your own feet, one mile of dark corridor at a time.

The passage sloped down and then up, under the bones of the old temple district, and their lantern threw their shadows huge against the wet brick. Petra walked half a step ahead of him, always, even now—even agreeing to his bargain, she refused to walk behind anyone. He found that he liked that about her. He found that he liked a great many things about her, and that liking them was a complication his life could not afford, and that knowing this changed nothing whatsoever.

"You keep looking at me," she said, without turning around.

"You keep noticing."

"Twelve years of selling charms to liars. You learn to read faces." She stopped at a junction where three tunnels met, marked with smugglers' chalk in symbols he hadn't decoded yet, and turned to face him. The lantern light made her eyes very dark. "Ask what you're circling around, priest. You're bad at it."

He laughed despite himself—a short, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. "All right. Why now? You've had twelve years to find the underground. Half the quarter knows my face under the lamp-oil. Why come down tonight?"

"Because Marisol's son cried through the wall," she said. "And because—" She stopped. Started again, slower, like someone walking on ice. "Because when you talked about the gods waking, I wasn't afraid of them. I was afraid of how much I *wanted* it to be true. Does that make sense? Twelve years I've told myself the quiet was safety. And the moment you said 'chosen,' some starving thing in me sat up and said *finally*. That frightens me more than the wardens do."

Tam understood that hunger better than he'd ever admitted to anyone. "The quiet was never safety," he said. "It was just a slower way of dying. We got good at calling it living."

"Yes," she said. "That. Exactly that."

They stood in the junction, close enough that he could see the fine scars on her knuckles from twelve years of knife-work, and the distance between them—which had been a negotiation all evening, a bargaining chip, a wall—suddenly seemed like the only dishonest thing left in the cellar they'd left behind.

"Petra," he said.

"Don't," she said. But she didn't step back.

"I'm going to say it anyway, because you asked for ugly truth, and here it is: I've been half in love with you since you told me off in the market. Since you looked at me like I was a fool and you were right. And I know the timing is obscene. I know we're standing in a smuggling tunnel with wardens above us and gods waking under us. I know—"

She kissed him.

It was not gentle. It was the kiss of two people who had been rationing themselves for twelve years and had run out of rations, and it tasted of tallow smoke and defiance, and Tam's lantern hand shook so badly the shadows swung around them like startled birds. Her hand fisted in his collar. His free hand found the back of her neck, the small bones there, warm under his palm, and he thought distantly, absurdly, *this is what the world was like before*—this unguardedness, this spending of yourself without ledger or law—

The whistle cut through the dark above them.

One note, high and doubled—the warden signal for a sweep in progress, two patrols converging. Tam broke away so fast he nearly dropped the lantern. For one heartbeat they stood frozen, foreheads almost touching, breath ragged, listening.

Another whistle. Closer. And beneath it, boots on the street grate overhead, many boots, moving in the disciplined rhythm of a coordinated search.

"They're sweeping the temple district," Tam breathed. "Tonight? They never sweep at night unless—"

"Unless someone told them where to look." Petra was already moving, pulling him by the sleeve toward the left-hand tunnel, the one breathing cold air. "Who knew about tonight? Who knew about this cellar?"

"Four people. Four people I'd trust with my life."

"Trust smaller," she said grimly. "Move."

They ran. The tunnel narrowed, the ceiling dropping until they stooped, the cold air thickening with the smell of deep water. Behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the scrape of the cellar grate being wrenched open, and a voice—muffled, official, bored—saying *"Clear. Check the junctions."*

Tam's mind raced ahead of his feet, laying the map of the underground over the map of the tunnels, looking for the route that didn't exist yet, the exit that wasn't watched. Ahead of him Petra ran like a woman who had spent her whole life memorizing ways out of rooms, and he realized with a lurch that he had just gambled everything—his network, his people, the embers he'd cupped for twelve years—on a carver he'd known for six days.

And that the worst part, the part he'd never confess to anyone, was that even now, running blind through the dark with warden whistles singing above them, some traitorous corner of him was still warm where her mouth had been.

The tunnel forked. Petra stopped, pressed her ear to the left wall, then the right.

"This way," she whispered. "There's an old drainage shaft. It comes up behind the tannery."

"How do you know that?"

Her smile in the lantern light was fierce and fleeting. "Because I've been planning my escape from this city since I was nine years old," she said. "Come on, priest. Try to keep up."

Above them, fainter now, the whistles sounded again—and this time, Tam heard something new threaded through them. Not a search pattern.

A recall. All patrols, falling back.

Someone had called off the sweep. Someone who had the authority to do it. And in twelve years of listening to the sounds of Ironhollow's law, Tam had learned that a recalled sweep was never mercy.

It was a trap being reset.
