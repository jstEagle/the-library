## 7. The Warden's Mercy

The cells beneath the Wardenhouse smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Ilsa Varn had long ago stopped noticing. Tonight she noticed everything.

She stood at the observation slit above Interrogation Room Three, her hands flat against the cold sill, watching the man they'd pulled out of the tanner's row two nights past. He sat chained to the chair with the particular stillness of someone who had decided, in advance, exactly how much he was willing to endure. His name—his real name, not the one on the arrest ledger—was Doss Ferren. Forty years old, maybe. A lamplighter's build, a lamplighter's calluses. And a bone charm sewn into the lining of his coat with the kind of care that meant he hadn't bought it off some market stall. Someone had made it for him. Someone who loved him, or feared for him, or both.

"Name," said Warden-Captain Breck from inside the room, though everyone knew Ferren's name by now. It was ritual, not inquiry. The law required a question before it permitted a method.

"Doss Ferren," the man said, for the fourth time that night. "Lamplighter, third district."

"And the charm?"

"What charm?"

Breck sighed the sigh of a patient man doing an impatient job, and nodded to the warden by the brazier. The irons came off the table—the long ones, the ones Ilsa had never once had to use in eleven years of service—and Ferren looked at them the way a man looks at weather coming over the hills. Not surprised. Just measuring how much time he had left before it broke over him.

Ilsa turned away from the slit.

* * *

She'd joined the Wardens at nineteen because her father had died in the God Riots and the city needed order, and she had believed—she could still remember believing it with her whole chest—that order was the same thing as mercy. Twelve years ago the gods had been bound, the magic outlawed, the temples sealed, and Ironhollow had stopped burning. That was the arithmetic she'd carried through every raid, every arrest, every family she'd watched emptied out of a house with their belongings stacked on the curb: whatever this cost, it cost less than fire.

The arithmetic had been getting harder to do lately.

It had started, if she was honest, with the carver woman. Petra Okonkwo. No—Ilsa corrected herself—it had started before that, with the neighbor. Maren Kettle, the button-seller, taken in the raid on Carver's Row three weeks ago for a charm that *worked*. That was the phrase in the report, and Ilsa had written it herself, and only afterward, walking home through streets where no lamp burned brighter than the law allowed, had she understood what she'd actually written. A charm that worked. Which meant the dead had answered. Which meant somewhere under all that silence, something old had heard a prayer and leaned close—and the law's response was to drag a button-seller with arthritic hands into a cell like this one.

Maren Kettle was still here. Sixty-one years old, charged with Unlicensed Working, First Degree. The penalty schedule put First Degree alongside arson and murder. Nobody had executed anyone for a charm in nine years—the magistrates preferred the long sentences now, the slow erasure—but the schedule was the schedule, and schedules were how a city convinced itself it wasn't afraid.

Ilsa had signed Maren Kettle's transfer-to-trial paperwork herself. Her signature had come out steady. That was the part that kept her up at night—not what she'd done, but how easily her hand had done it.

* * *

Down in Room Three, Ferren had not broken, but he had stopped pretending there was nothing to find. They'd cut the lining open and the charm lay on the steel table between them, small as a knucklebone, carved with lines so fine Ilsa couldn't read them from the slit. Breck wouldn't touch it bare-handed. Protocol said evidence of workings went to the Assessors, and the Assessors wore gloves and never smiled, and everything they touched came back catalogued and cold.

"It's nothing," Ferren was saying. "A keepsake. My daughter carved it."

"Your daughter." Breck picked up the charge sheet. "You don't have a daughter on record."

"She's not on record because she's ten years old, Captain, and I didn't think the Wardens needed to know about her." A pause, and then—foolishly, bravely—"Does the law require children to be on record now? Is that next?"

Breck let the insolence pass, which told Ilsa more than any answer would have. Breck was tired too. Everyone was tired; nobody talked about it; the tiredness just accumulated in the Wardenhouse like damp.

"The charm worked," Breck said. "The Assessor's preliminary says residual resonance consistent with answered petition. You understand what that means, Ferren. Somebody prayed through that thing and something answered."

Ferren was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again his voice had changed—lower, rougher, the voice of a man who had run out of reasons to be careful.

"My wife's been sick two years. Lung-rot, from the dye works. The physicians took everything we had and then told me to prepare the children." He looked up, and even across the distance and the slit's narrow angle, Ilsa felt the look land on her like a hand on the throat. "So my girl asked me for a piece of bone and a knife, and she sat up three nights carving, because her grandmother taught her mother the old strokes before the Binding and her mother taught her, quiet, the way you teach a child to swim in deep water. And you know what? It worked. Whatever your Assessors want to call it—it *worked*. My wife slept a whole night without coughing blood. One night. Maybe it was the charm. Maybe it was sleep. But my daughter believes she saved her mother, Captain, and I will be hanged in the square before I tell that child otherwise."

The room went still. Even the brazier seemed to burn quieter.

Breck looked down at the table. "Where is the girl now?"

"With neighbors." Ferren's jaw tightened. "Who have already been questioned twice, so you can stop pretending you don't know that."

Ilsa pushed back from the slit. She walked the corridor to the stair, down, down, past the duty roster and the arms rack, and stood outside Room Three with her hand on the door, listening to her own heartbeat argue with itself.

Eleven years. Eleven years of raids and reports and the clean, cold language of the law, and it had taken a lamplighter with dye-rot lungs and a ten-year-old with a bone knife to show her what the law actually sounded like from underneath.

She went in.

* * *

Breck glanced up, unsurprised to see her. "Varn. You're off rotation."

"I heard we caught the charm-carver's supplier." She let the lie sit there, plain as bread. "Thought I'd take a look at the evidence."

Breck studied her a moment longer than was comfortable, then gestured at the table. "Assessors collect it at dawn."

"I'll log it tonight. Save them the trip." She picked up the charm.

It was warm. That was the first thing—the wrongness of it, a scrap of bone holding heat like a living thing in a room full of cold steel. The second thing was the carving itself. She'd seen hundreds of confiscated charms, the crude market trinkets, the smuggled temple relics with their formal liturgical script. This was none of those. The lines were fine and sure and utterly untrained, a child's hand guided by something older than training, and as Ilsa turned it in the lamplight she felt—not heard, nothing so dramatic—a pressure behind her eyes, like a word trying to be remembered.

*Sleep,* the bone seemed to say. *Rest. Be easy.*

Her mother had hummed something once, when Ilsa was small and feverish, that had felt exactly like this.

She closed her fist around it. "I'll take it to the Assessor's office myself," she heard herself say. "Chain of custody. There've been questions about the evidence room since the Tallow Street thefts."

"There haven't been thefts on Tallow Street in four years."

"Then there'll be no questions." She met Breck's eyes. Somewhere in the last three minutes she had stepped over a line, and the strange thing was how quiet it was on this side of it. "Captain. The man's a lamplighter. His wife is dying. His daughter is ten. What precisely does the city gain from a First Degree conviction here?"

Breck's face did something complicated and then settled into the expression Ilsa thought of as the Wardenhouse face—the one that meant a man had done this arithmetic before and chosen not to finish it. "What the city gains is order, Varn. You know that better than most."

"Do I?" She looked at Ferren, who was watching her with the careful blankness of a man who has learned that hope is expensive. "Order. Is that what Maren Kettle gave the city? Sixty-one years old. Arthritis in both hands. She sold buttons, Captain. She sold *buttons*, and she's in cell fourteen because something she carved answered somebody's grief."

"You should be careful," Breck said quietly, "where you say things like that."

"Yes," Ilsa agreed. "I should."

* * *

She logged the charm as destroyed on capture—damaged beyond evidentiary value during the arrest, subject disposed per protocol—and she wrote it in her own neat, upright hand, the hand that had never once been questioned in eleven years of service, and she watched the ink dry, and she understood that she had just become a different person than the one who had signed Maren Kettle's transfer papers. There was no going back across that line. There was only deciding which side of it to stand on while the world arranged itself around her choice.

That night she couldn't sleep, so she walked.

Ironhollow after curfew was a city holding its breath. The lamps Ferren lit each evening burned along the avenues—third district, his district—and Ilsa found herself following them without meaning to, street by street, deeper into the rows where the carvers and dyers and lamplighters lived pressed together like pages of a book nobody read anymore. Here and there, in high windows, she saw charms. Little ones. Bones hung on red thread, shells, knots of wire. Most were nothing—superstition, habit, the way people still touched doorposts where mezuzahs used to hang. But a few, when the wind moved them, seemed to catch the light wrong. Seemed to hold it.

*They're waking,* she thought, and the thought arrived with the weight of certainty rather than suspicion. *All of them. All these years we've been arresting sparks, and underneath the whole city something is catching.*

She stopped in a square she half-recognized and realized why: the notice board. Three weeks old, rain-warp curling the paper, the Wardenhouse seal still legible. *By order of the Magistracy: the practice, sale, or possession of unlicensed workings is prohibited...* Below it, someone had pasted a second notice, smaller, hand-lettered, and Ilsa read it twice because she couldn't believe it had survived even a day:

*Maren Kettle made my mother's death bearable. Who among us has not prayed?*

No signature. Of course no signature. But the paste was fresh at the edges, and there were other notices beneath it, older ones, scraped half away, and the ghost-marks of more beneath those. People had been nailing their grief to this board for weeks. The Wardens had been scraping it off for weeks. And it kept coming back, the way—she thought, and almost laughed at herself—the way prayers do.

* * *

Two days later they brought in the girl.

Not Ferren's daughter—that was the first mercy, and Ilsa clung to it like a rail in floodwater. The neighbors had hidden the Ferren girl well. This was another child entirely: Sela, eight years old, daughter of the Kettles' other neighbor, the cooper's widow, caught by a patrol on Tanner's Row carrying a basket of carved bone scraps to—so the patrol sergeant's report claimed—"*an unknown recipient in the carver's quarter.*"

Ilsa read the report three times. *Unknown recipient.* The sergeant hadn't wanted to write the name. Everyone in the third district knew which stall in the carver's quarter sold charms that worked; the whole city knew, the way a body knows a fever before the physician does. The Okonkwo woman. The quiet one. The one whose work had started showing up in confiscation baskets six months ago, always deniable, always small, until lately it wasn't small anymore and the pilgrims had begun to find her stall like water finding a crack in stone.

The girl sat in the holding room with her feet not reaching the floor, and she did not cry, and that frightened Ilsa more than crying would have. Eight years old and she already knew the rules: don't give them tears to report.

"Your name is Sela," Ilsa said. She sat across from her, deliberately, not behind the desk. "My name is Ilsa. I'm not going to hurt you."

Sela looked at her with the flat patience of the very young and the very poor. "Everyone says that."

Fair enough. Ilsa set the basket on the table between them. Bone scraps, a dozen of them, each no bigger than a thumbnail, each carved with a few sure strokes. Not finished charms. Blanks. Practice pieces—or offerings, or payment. "Who were you taking these to?"

Silence.

"Sela. Look at me." She waited until the dark eyes lifted. "The woman you were bringing these to—is she the one who makes the charms that work?"

The girl's chin came up, and for a second she looked twenty years older. "If I say yes, you'll take her. Like you took Auntie Maren."

The room was very quiet. Down the corridor, boots passed and faded. Ilsa became aware of the weight of her own uniform, the badge on her breast, the whole apparatus of law and fear she wore like a second skin, and how it must look from across this table—from below.

"Auntie Maren," Ilsa said slowly, "is alive. She's fed. She's—"

"She's in a hole," Sela said. "My ma says everybody knows it. You put her in a hole for selling buttons that helped people." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing; it stayed level as a carpenter's line. "My ma says the gods are waking up anyway. She says you can't arrest the whole city."

"No," Ilsa heard herself say. "We can't."

Something flickered in the child's face—the first crack in the armor, surprise. Children expected monsters to insist they weren't monsters. They didn't expect agreement.

Ilsa looked at the basket. At the little bones with their few sure strokes, and remembered the warmth of the Ferren charm in her fist, the word trying to surface: *rest, be easy.* Somewhere in this city a carver was teaching the dead to speak again, one whisper at a time, and the law's answer was a hole in the ground and a child in a holding room, and Ilsa Varn had spent eleven years being the hand that knocked on doors at midnight, and she was so tired, gods, she was so tired of being the knock.

She stood up. "Wait here. Don't touch anything."

In the corridor she found Sergeant Dulac, young, eager, the kind of warden Ilsa had been at twenty-two and could no longer remember how to be. "The basket's empty," she told him. "Scraps of butcher bone, nothing carved. Kid was running an errand for her mother, got confused, wandered off the approved route. I've spoken with her. She's not a party to anything."

Dulac frowned at the holding-room door. "The sergeant's report said—"

"The sergeant's report was written by a man who wanted credit for a smuggling ring and found a child with a laundry basket instead." She held his eyes until the frown collapsed. "File it as I've described. If anyone asks, the reviewing officer was me. I'll sign it."

He hesitated. She watched him weigh it—young men weighed everything quickly—and watched him decide that arguing with a senior warden over a basket of nothing was a bad trade. "Yes, Warden Varn."

She went back into the holding room. Sela hadn't moved. Ilsa crouched so they were eye to eye, and she did the thing she would think about for the rest of her life, in the years when there would be a rest of her life, in the years when the Wardenhouse would be something else entirely: she reached out and straightened the girl's collar, the way you straighten a collar for a child going home.

"Go home to your mother," she said. "Take the long way. Don't run—running looks guilty. And Sela." She waited until the girl looked at her. "Tell whoever carves those bones that the wardens are watching the carver's quarter now. Tell her to be careful. Tell her—" *tell her I'm sorry about Maren Kettle; tell her I'm on the board at the square reading the notices at midnight; tell her I held a dead woman's prayer in my fist and it was warmer than anything in this whole rotten lawful city* — "tell her some of us are still counting."

Sela studied her for a long moment, eight years old and ancient. Then she nodded once, solemn as a magistrate, and slid off the chair, and walked out of the Wardenhouse into the gray morning without looking back.

* * *

Ilsa filed the false report at the ninth bell. Her handwriting was steady. It was always steady. That was the horror of it, she thought, pressing the seal into the wax—the horror was never in the hand, it was in how long the hand could keep lying before the arm refused.

She walked home through the third district as the lamps came on, Ferren's district, Ferren's lamps, each flame a small defiance nobody had arrested yet. In the square, the notice board had been scraped clean again. By morning there would be new notices. She was certain of it the way she was suddenly certain of everything now: the charms were waking, the dead were leaning close, and somewhere in the carver's quarter a woman with quiet hands was listening to all of it.

Ilsa stopped at the board. She looked at the empty space where the grief kept reattaching itself, and then, glancing once up the avenue and once down, she took out her knife and cut a single mark into the wood where the notices would go—small, shallow, deliberate.

Three fine lines. The old strokes. The ones from before the Binding.

Anyone passing would see only a scratch. But whoever pasted the next notice would see it, and know the board was watched by a friend.

Six days later, the summons came down from the palace: all senior wardens to report for solstice security briefing, the king's renewal rite, full ceremonial detail. Ilsa read the order twice, and felt the line under her feet begin to move toward something vast and hungry, and understood that whatever she had crossed already, the true crossing was still ahead.
