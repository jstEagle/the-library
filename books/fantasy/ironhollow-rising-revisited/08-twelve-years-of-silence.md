## 8. Twelve Years of Silence

Sleep had never been kind to Petra Okonkwo, but on the nights it came at all, it came with the smell of burning tallow and her mother's voice saying *hold still, little bird*.

She woke in the dark above Tam's cellar with her hand already closed around the charm at her throat, as it had been every night for twelve years. The bone was warm. It was always warm now—since the market, since the whisper, since everything began to crack open like river ice in spring. She lay listening to the sound of Tam breathing one room away, to the drip of the cellar's single pipe, to the city settling around her like an old dog turning in its sleep.

And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse through a wall: her mother. Not words. Not yet. Just presence, the way a lamp behind a door makes a line of gold along the floor.

*Hold still, little bird.*

Petra closed her eyes and let the memory take her, because she had learned long ago that fighting it only meant drowning slower.

* * *

The night of the Binding, Petra was eleven years old, and the sky over Ironhollow turned the color of a bruise.

She remembered that first—the color. She had been on the roof of the carver's hall where she wasn't supposed to be, watching the sunset go wrong. The west should have been gold; instead it curdled violet, then gray, then a gray so deep it looked like a hole cut in the world. And from the direction of the Godsfane came a sound she had no name for then and has never found a name for since—not thunder, not bells, but something between a scream and a door closing, so vast that it wasn't heard so much as felt, in the teeth, in the ribs, in the wet dark of the belly.

Every bird in Ironhollow went up at once. Every dog began to howl. And every person who could hear the gods—and in those days, in the carver's quarter, that was most of them—staggered as if struck.

Her mother fell to her knees in the doorway below, flour still on her hands from the morning's bread, and said, in a voice Petra had never heard her use, "They're being taken."

Not killed. Taken. Petra would understand the difference later, would understand it with a precision that felt like flaying, but that night she only climbed down and pressed herself against her mother's side and asked the question children ask: *Are we going to die?*

And her mother—Adaeze Okonkwo, who could hear the dead the way other people hear rain, who carved blessings into whalebone and river-stone and once, memorably, into the tooth of a horse that had refused to plow—her mother looked at the bruised sky and said, "No. But we are going to be quiet. Come inside, little bird. Quickly now."

The soldiers came at moonrise.

They wore white sashes over black armor, and they did not shout, which was somehow worse than shouting. They moved through the carver's quarter in pairs, unhurried, methodical, knocking on doors with the flat of a gauntlet. When the doors opened they read from scrolls in voices like men reciting inventory: *By decree of the Regency Council, in the name of order and the preservation of the realm, all communion with the bound powers is hereby ended. All vessels, charms, relics, and instruments of the old worship are forfeit. All practitioners will submit to severance.*

Severance. Even the word had been designed to sound clean. Like trimming a wick.

Her mother did not argue. Arguing, Adaeze always said, was for people with time. Instead she banked the fire, kissed Petra's forehead, and whispered, "Go to the cold room. Take the awl and the blank bones from my kit. Whatever you hear—whatever you hear, Petra, do you understand me?—you stay there and you carve."

"Carve what?"

But her mother was already opening the door to the white-sashed men, and there was no time for anything else.

* * *

The cold room was a root cellar behind the house, dug into the hillside, where the family kept winter stores and, less officially, the bones that were too sacred or too dangerous to keep upstairs. Petra sat in the dark among turnips and salted pork with her mother's carving kit in her lap, and through the floorboards she listened to the world end.

She heard the soldiers' boots. She heard neighbors—Master Ivo who mended cart wheels, the twin sisters who sang at funerals—being walked out into the street. She heard a woman beg, once, briefly, and stop begging in a way that made Petra's stomach fold in on itself.

And she heard her mother talking to someone. Low, calm, unhurried. Words she couldn't make out. Then a pause. Then, clearly, rising through the earth like water through cloth:

*"I consent. Do you hear me? I consent freely. Write it down."*

Then the sound that Petra would spend twelve years trying not to hear: a note like a plucked string, deep and wrong, and after it a silence so total that her own heartbeat sounded like vandalism.

The dead, which had murmured under Ironhollow since before there was an Ironhollow—a low tide of grandmothers and soldiers and children, always there, always companionable—went silent. All of them. Everywhere. As if the whole city had been plunged underwater.

Petra sat in the dark with the awl shaking in her hand, and she did not cry, because crying made noise, and her mother had told her to be quiet.

It was hours later—she would learn it was near dawn—that the trapdoor opened and her mother came down the ladder, moving strangely, like a puppet whose strings had been half-cut. Her eyes were open but fixed on nothing. Her lips were gray. And when Petra threw herself at her, her mother's arms came up slowly, and held her, and her voice when she spoke was her own voice but thinned, stretched, as if heard through a wall.

"Little bird. Listen to me. There is very little time, and I am going to spend it badly, because I love you, and love is wasteful."

"What happened? Mama, what did they do?"

"They took us." Her mother's hand found Petra's face in the dark, warm, familiar, trembling slightly. "All of us who could hear. They didn't kill us—they couldn't afford to. Souls are useful, little bird. Souls can be spent. They've put us somewhere, and they've put the gods somewhere deeper, and they mean to keep us both." A breath that was almost a laugh. "Your father always said I'd end up working for the crown eventually."

Petra didn't understand. She understood nothing, and she said so, and her mother drew back and held her by the shoulders, and in the thin gray light from the vent slit her face was already changing—going smooth, going distant, like wax left near a flame.

"I am going to fade," her mother said simply. "Whatever they've done to us, it eats. Slowly, if we're lucky. And I will not be able to stay with you, and I will not be able to speak much longer, and so we are going to cheat them. Both of us. Right now. Are you listening?"

"Yes."

"Give me your hand."

What followed, Petra would remember in fragments for the rest of her life: the awl warming in her grip as if another hand had closed over hers. The blank piece of whalebone—her mother's best stock, saved for important work—seeming to soften under the blade. The designs coming through her hands without passing through her mind, lines and knots and spirals she had no skill to invent and no right to know, her eleven-year-old fingers suddenly steady as a master carver's, guided from somewhere just behind her shoulder.

And her mother's voice, growing fainter with every stroke, dictating: *"There. Now the knot of holding—no, tighter, tighter, it must hold against silence itself—now the name. My name, little bird. Say it into the bone as you cut it. Not out loud. In the place where names live."*

"Adaeze," Petra whispered into the bone. "Mama."

*"Again."*

"Adaeze. Adaeze. Adaeze."

The last spiral closed. The bone went cool. And her mother sighed—a long, releasing sigh, the sigh of a woman setting down a load carried too far—and said, in a voice already halfway to elsewhere:

"There. Now they can't have all of me. Hide it where no warden would ever look, and never wear it in daylight, and never—" a flicker, a struggle, the light guttering— "never blame yourself. You hear me? Survival is not a sin. It is only a debt. And debts are meant to be paid forward, not mourned over—"

And then she was gone. Not dead—Petra would learn the terrible distinction soon enough—but gone from the room, gone from reach, folded down into a piece of bone the size of a thumbnail, and the cold room was just a cold room, and Petra Okonkwo was alone in it, eleven years old, holding her entire remaining family in one fist.

* * *

She hid the charm inside the hollow handle of her father's old skinning knife, wrapped in wool, packed with tallow to deaden any resonance. Wardens could sense worked bone—it hummed, if you knew how to listen, and the new wardens had been taught to listen. So Petra taught herself the opposite: how to carve so quietly the bone barely whispered. How to make charms so small, so plain, so deliberately mediocre that they passed inspection as trinkets, buttons, toys. She became famous in the quarter, in the way that poor people become famous, for selling lucky bits of bone that never quite worked.

That was the joke of it. That was the bitter, bleeding joke she had lived inside for twelve years: the whole city believed Petra Okonkwo's charms were frauds, and she let them believe it, because a fraud was safe. A fraud got fined for vagrancy. A real charm-carver got severed—or worse, got *collected*, because the crown had discovered, in those first hungry years, that souls could be farmed.

So she stayed small. She stayed dull. She watched Master Ivo's daughter get taken for a blessing carved into a cradle, and she said nothing. She watched the twin funeral-singers dragged from their beds, and she said nothing. Every year the list of names she hadn't spoken grew longer, and every year she told herself the same story: *if I rise, I die, and then who carries Mama? Who remembers the knot of holding? Silence is survival. Silence is the debt.*

Her mother had said survival was not a sin. Her mother had not said what it would feel like—this slow accrual, this interest compounding in the dark. Twelve years of watching people she could have warned, could have armed, could have taught, walk into white-sash hands because Petra Okonkwo had decided her own neck was worth more than their names.

Last week, they took Marisol from two doors down. For a charm that worked.

A charm that worked. That was the thing that would not stop gnawing at her, the splinter under the nail. Charms weren't supposed to work anymore. The gods were bound; the dead were silent; the whole current of the world had been dammed upstream at the palace. And yet Marisol's cheap little luck-knot had answered a prayer, and Petra's own grief-charm had whispered back in the market, and the bone at her throat had been warm for six days straight, warm as a living hand.

Which meant the dam was cracking. Which meant the old gods were stirring, reaching, answering whoever called—through her work, through her hands, through the small cowardly channels she'd spent twelve years digging.

Or it meant something worse. It meant the crown was drinking deeper than ever, and the bound dead were being pulled thin, and her mother's soul—worn smooth as river glass by a decade of captivity—was pressing against the walls of its little bone cell, trying to reach her daughter before there was nothing left to reach with.

Either way, the age of hiding was ending. Either way, the debt had come due.

* * *

Petra sat up in the dark and unknotted the cord at her neck and held the charm to the gray line of dawn coming under the door. Small. Plain. Warm. Twelve years of silence pressed into a thumbnail of whalebone.

"You told me not to blame myself," she whispered. "You didn't tell me what to do when hiding stops working."

The bone gave no answer. But the warmth pulsed once, twice, like a hand squeezing hers—like a woman setting down a load and picking it back up again—and Petra closed her fist around it and made the decision she had been circling for six days the way a moth circles a flame it cannot afford to touch.

She was going to find the lamplighter priest. She was going to ask him what he knew about the Binding, about the palace, about where the taken ones were kept.

And whatever he asked of her in return—however high the price ran—she was going to pay it.

Because her mother had been right about one thing, at least: survival was a debt.

And Petra Okonkwo was done paying it in silence.
