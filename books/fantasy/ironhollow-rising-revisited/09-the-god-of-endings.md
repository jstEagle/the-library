## 9. The God of Endings

The temple had not been opened in twelve years, and the air inside tasted like that.

Tam stood at the threshold with his lamp raised and his heart hammering against the cage of his ribs, breathing in the dead years: dust and cold stone and something older beneath them both, a sweetness gone to rot, incense burned down to its resin and left to fossilize in the braziers along the walls. The doors of the Temple of the Quiet End had been sealed with lead and law on the night of the Binding—wax stamped with the king's own seal, chains wrapped thrice around the handles, wardens posted outside for a month until even they forgot why they were there. Tam had picked the lock an hour past midnight with tools he'd stolen from his own lamplighting kit, and now he stood where no living soul had stood since the world went quiet.

"Forgive me," he said aloud, though there was no one to hear it. "I'm late."

His voice fell into the dark and did not come back. No echo. The nave swallowed sound the way the crown swallowed souls, and Tam felt that symmetry settle over him like a hand on the back of his neck.

He walked the aisle slowly, touching nothing, letting the lamp show him what remained. The pews were still ranked in their rows, gray with dust, some of them still bearing the small worn marks of the faithful—the initials scratched by a bored child, the groove worn into one armrest by a woman who must have gripped it through every service for forty years. The altar stood bare at the far end, stripped of its silver, but the stone itself was intact, and carved into its face was the sigil he had been born under: the circle broken open, the line passing through, the sign of endings that are also passages. *An ending is a door,* his mother had told him once, before the Binding made liars of all priests. *We do not worship death, little one. We worship the going-through.*

Twelve years ago, the king's soldiers had come for every priest of the Quiet End first. Not because theirs was the loudest faith—it was the quietest, the most patient—but because their god was the one the Binding needed most. You cannot lock a door without the god of endings. You cannot bind ten thousand souls forever unless you have bound the very principle of release.

Tam had been twenty-two. He had watched from a cellar window as they dragged old Father Anselm into the square, and he had done nothing, because doing anything would have meant dying, and dying would have meant the rites died with him. He had told himself that was wisdom. He had spent twelve years telling himself that.

Now he set the lamp on the altar and knelt, and his knees cracked in the silence like gunshots.

* * *

The rites came back to him the way a language comes back—not thought, but muscle. He had performed them ten thousand times as an acolyte: dawn rites and dusk rites, the rites for the dying and the rites for the newly dead, the long midnight litany that walked the soul to the door and saw it through. His voice was rusty. It cracked on the high invocations, and the Old Tongue sat strangely in his mouth after so many years of market slang and warden's cant, but the words remembered themselves faster than he did, and soon he was moving through the sequence with his eyes half-closed, hands tracing the broken circle above the altar stone, and the temple began—

—to listen.

There was no other word for it. The silence changed texture. It stopped being empty and became attentive, the way a room changes when someone enters it behind your back. Tam's skin prickled. The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft, and the dust motes hung motionless in the air like a held breath.

He finished the invocation and spoke the name.

Not the true name—that was beyond him, had always been beyond any mortal throat—but the courtesy-name, the handle by which the faithful had addressed their god for a thousand years: *Quiet-End, Door-Warden, She-Who-Stands-At-The-Passage.* He said it three times, as the litany required, and on the third speaking he felt something answer.

It was not a voice. It was a pressure, faint and vast, like the change in your ears when you descend into deep water. The lamp flame bent flat. Somewhere in the walls, in the stones themselves, something turned over—a vast attention shifting its weight after twelve years of stillness—and Tam pressed his palms flat against the cold altar and wept without deciding to, the tears cutting clean lines through the dust on his face.

"You're still here," he whispered. "Gods forgive us. You're still *here.*"

The pressure pulsed. And then, threaded through it, thin as wire, came something that was almost speech—not words, not yet, but rhythm, cadence, the shape of meaning trying to force itself through a membrane far too dense. Tam knew this feeling. Every priest did. This was what contact felt like across the veil: the god pressing its face against the far side of the glass.

But the glass should not have been this thick. Even bound, even sealed, the Quiet End had always answered her priests swiftly; she was the closest of the gods to the mortal door. This was different. This was a voice shouting through fathoms of black water, and Tam understood, with a lurch, that whatever held her was not the lead seals on the temple doors.

It was something else. Something closer to the throne.

"I can't hear you," he said. "Lady, I can't—" He stopped himself, breathed, and did what the oldest texts prescribed when the veil grew thick: he offered blood. He drew his belt knife across the pad of his thumb and pressed it to the altar stone, into the shallow channel carved there for exactly this purpose, and spoke the opening words of the Deep Litany, the rite reserved for the dying hours, when the door between stood widest.

The pressure surged. The lamp guttered. And the temple filled with a sound that Tam would carry to his grave: a groan, enormous and agonized, rising out of the floor and the walls and the air itself, the sound of something immense trying to speak through a throat that was not its own.

Then the vision took him.

* * *

He was standing in the throne room of the palace.

He had never seen it—he was a lamplighter, a disgraced priest, a man whose face appeared on no list worth being on—but he knew it instantly, the way you know a place in a dream. The vaulted ceiling painted with the ascension of the unified crown. The banners of Ironhollow hanging motionless. The long carpet running up the dais steps, wine-dark, worn pale in the center by twelve years of ceremonial feet.

And on the throne, King Aldric.

But not King Aldric. Tam had seen the king twice from a distance, at processions, and had felt the wrongness without being able to name it. Now, inside the vision, he could see clearly, and what he saw made him stagger backward in a body that wasn't there.

The king was hollow.

Not empty—worse than empty. Hollow the way a bell is hollow, shaped precisely to ring. Tam could see the shape of the hollowness, could see how carefully it had been made: a man's soul scooped out with surgical patience, year by year, and the cavity lined and polished until it resonated at exactly the frequency required. And seated in that cavity, wearing the man like a garment, coiled around the throne and up through the dais and down into the foundations of the palace and out beneath the whole city—

The crown.

It sat on the king's brow, gold and plain, unremarkable, and it was *feeding*. Tam could see the threads. Hundreds of them, thousands, fine as spider silk and strong as anchor chain, running out from the circlet in every direction—down through the floor, out through the walls, into the city, into the mass graves, into the sealed temples, into the bones of the bound dead. Twelve years ago the Binding had torn ten thousand souls out of the world and pinned them in place, and Tam had always assumed they hung suspended somewhere, imprisoned, waiting.

They were not waiting. They were being *eaten*. Slowly, steadily, thread by thread, the crown drank them—their warmth, their memory, their selves—drawing everything up through the silk and into the hollow king, sustaining him, sustaining the Binding itself, a furnace that burned souls for fuel and called itself a monarchy.

And at the center of it all, tangled deepest in the web, bound tighter than any of the others, Tam saw her.

The Quiet End.

She was wound through the crown like a wire through a rope's core. Every soul-thread passed through her; every act of drinking, of binding, of holding ran along lines she anchored. She was not the prison's prisoner. She was the prison's *load-bearing wall*. They had taken the god of endings and made her the mechanism of endlessness—made the door itself hold the door shut.

She was screaming. Tam realized the groan in the temple had been her scream, filtered through twelve years of stone and seal, and that she had been screaming the entire time, and that no one had heard, because hearing was the first thing they had bound.

The king's head turned. In the vision, in the empty throne room, the hollow man looked directly at Tam with eyes that held nothing at all, and the mouth opened, and what spoke was two voices braided into one—the dry rustle of a used-up vessel, and beneath it, vast and strained and drowning, the god.

*"Priest."*

Tam tried to kneel. There was no knee to bend.

*"You came late,"* said the god of endings, and there was something in the vastness that might, in another life, in another god, have been humor. *"Twelve years. I counted."*

"Lady," Tam managed. "Lady, I didn't know. We didn't know what they'd done—we thought you were imprisoned, we thought—"

*"Imprisoned."* The word moved through the vision like a tremor. *"A prison has walls. A prisoner has a cell. I am neither. I am a *pipe*, priest. I am the channel through which they drink the dead. Do you understand? They did not lock me away from my work. They made my work into their feeding."*

The threads blazed in Tam's sight, all of them at once, and he cried out at the sheer scale of it—the hunger of the thing, patient and bottomless, growing a little each year as the crown learned to draw deeper.

"How do I free you?" he asked. "Tell me how to free you."

The god was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, the braid of voices had shifted—the vessel's rasp fading, the divine depth surfacing, as if she were spending precious strength to be heard plainly.

*"You cannot cut the threads from below. They run through me. To sever them you must reach the crown itself, at the moment of its renewal, when theBinding is re-tied and the knots stand loose."*

"The solstice," Tam breathed. "The Rite of Continuance."

*"Six days hence. The vessel will sit upon the throne and the crown will drink a year's strength, and for the space of that rite the bindings pass through open hands."* A pause, and when she resumed, the vastness had thinned. He could hear her fraying. *"There is a carver in the market quarter. Bone-charms too small to notice. She has already begun to hear the dead. The threads respond to her craft—they were always meant to. Find her, priest. She is the knife. You are only the hand that holds it."*

Petra. Of course it was Petra. Tam's chest tightened with something that was not entirely duty.

"And if we fail?" he asked. "If the crown keeps feeding—how long does it last?"

The god's answer came slowly, each word costing her.

*"The dead are nearly spent. Ten thousand souls, drunk down to their last warm inch. When they are gone, the crown will reach for the living. It has already begun to practice."* The vision flickered, and Tam saw, for one terrible instant, the throne room as it truly was: the courtiers arrayed below the dais, each of them faintly dimmed, each of them carrying a hairline thread from the crown's rim to their breastbone, sipped at, sampled, kept fresh like a cellar of wine. *"Your king is not your tyrant, priest. Your king is the longest-suffering soul in Ironhollow. He has been aware inside that hollow for twelve years. He watches what is done with his hands. He screams where no one can hear, as I scream. Free me, and you may yet free him. Let the solstice pass, and there will be nothing left of either of us to save."*

The vision buckled. The throne room tore away like smoke in wind, and Tam was falling back through darkness toward his body kneeling at the altar, and the last thing the god gave him was not words but a sensation: the feeling of a door, ancient and patient, standing open at the end of a long road, and behind it—not nothing. Never nothing. A going-through, bright and kind, that the crown had spent twelve years teaching the world to fear.

Then the temple was only a temple. Dust and dark and the guttering lamp, and Tam on his knees with blood drying on the altar stone and his whole body shaking.

* * *

He did not remember lighting his way out. He found himself in the alley behind the temple with the lead-sealed doors closed behind him and the false dawn graying the rooftops, his thumb throbbing, his mind ringing with the god's voice. Six days. The solstice. A carver with bone charms too small to notice.

He thought of Petra Okonkwo's face in the smuggler's cellar—the wariness, the grief banked down to coals, the way she had kissed him like a woman making a withdrawal from an account she expected to close. He had promised her shelter in exchange for her craft. He had thought he was asking her to risk her life.

He had not known he was asking her to walk into the mouth of the thing that ate the dead.

Tam leaned against the cold brick and laughed, once, a broken sound, because the alternative was screaming, and the city did not need another screamer no one could hear. Above him the streetlamps he tended stood dark and dutiful, waiting for dusk. Somewhere across the sleeping city, in the palace on the hill, a hollow man sat awake on a throne, watching through eyes that were not his own, screaming alongside the god who wore his crown.

Six days.

Tam pushed off the wall and started walking toward the carver's quarter, and behind him, faint beneath the creak of the temple's sealed doors, he could almost believe he heard it still: the sound of a god counting down.
