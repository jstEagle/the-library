## 10. Charm Against Charm

The line began before dawn.

Petra knew it first as a sound—a shuffling, breath-warm murmur outside her shutters that she had not heard in Ironhollow for twelve years. She lay still on her cot and listened the way she had once listened to the dead: carefully, fearfully, cataloguing. Feet on cobblestone. A child's cough. The low, urgent voice of someone saying *she's real, I told you she's real.*

She had been real all along. That was never the problem.

By the time she unbarred her door and carried her folding table out into the gray light of Carver's Row, there were forty people waiting, and by midmorning there were two hundred, and the wardens' whistles sounded three streets over like small sharp birds announcing something no one wanted named.

*They're coming to buy,* she told herself, setting out her tray of charms with hands that would not quite steady. *People buying bone is an old thing. An innocent thing.*

But nobody bought bone anymore unless they believed it could do something. And hers did.

The first customer was a ferryman whose hands shook so badly he could barely hold the coin. His daughter's lungs filled with water every winter; the physicians bled her and burned rosemary and charged him a month's wages for it, and still she turned blue at night, gasping like a landed fish. He set a copper on the table—only a copper, all he had—and looked at Petra with the particular nakedness of a man who has run out of everything except hope.

"My neighbor says you helped his wife's hip. Says she danced at the wedding after."

"I carve bone," Petra said. "Decorative pieces. Amulets of—"

"Please." The word cracked down the middle. "I know what they say happens if you're caught. I know. But she's seven."

Petra looked at the ferryman's hands. She looked past them, at the crowd behind him—the miller's widow, the tanner's boy with the burn-scarred arm, a warden's own housekeeper standing with her head bowed as though praying to be invisible. They had all heard the same rumor, passed mouth to mouth through the city's thousand hidden channels: *the carver on Carver's Row, the one whose charms answer.*

Twelve years of careful nothing. Twelve years of carving small enough to ignore, dull enough to dismiss, dead enough to pass inspection. And now—

She picked up a sliver of riverbone, already shaped, already waiting in her pocket since before sunrise because some part of her had known. Her knife found the lines without her eyes. Three strokes for breath. One for the water to remember it was only water. And beneath them, faint as a fingerprint, the mark she had never dared carve before: the old sign of Nethys-of-the-Shallows, who had drowned with the other gods twelve years ago and apparently had not finished drowning yet.

The charm warmed against her palm. Not much. Just enough. Like a hand held under a blanket.

"For your daughter," she said quietly. "Wear it under her shirt. Tell no one."

The ferryman pressed the copper into her hand and gripped her fingers around it, hard, as if sealing a covenant, and walked away holding the little bone like it might hatch.

Behind him, the line surged forward.

* * *

By noon she had sold out twice and begun carving from raw stock just to keep pace, and this was where the trouble lived, because carving fast meant carving true, and carving true meant the gods came through like water through a broken dam.

A grandmother asked for peace for her husband's ghost, which had paced their bedroom for nine years, and when Petra handed her the charm the woman's face went slack and wet and she whispered, "He's stopped. Oh—oh, he's *stopped*, he's gone to rest," right there in front of thirty witnesses, loud enough that the murmur that ran through the crowd afterward was less a murmur than a bell.

A soldier with a ruined knee wept openly when he took his first painless steps away from her stall.

A mother held up her infant and said, "Bless her," and Petra, who had not blessed anything in twelve years, heard herself say words in a language she had learned at her mother's knee and buried at her mother's grave, and the baby laughed at nothing—or at something—and half the crowd made the old sign against evil and the other half made the old sign of blessing, and both signs were illegal, and everyone used them anyway, and Petra stood in the middle of it feeling like a woman standing in a field during the first thunderstorm after a long drought: exalted, and aware of exactly how flammable everything was.

*You are visible now.* Her mother's voice, or the memory of it, worn smooth as sea glass inside the little charm that rode against Petra's sternum, warm and silent these past weeks but present, always present. *Everything visible gets weighed.*

"I know," Petra murmured, to the memory, to the crowd, to herself.

"What was that?" asked the next customer, a nervous clerk.

"Nothing. What do you need?"

And then, cutting through the market noise, thin and silver and unmistakable: a warden's whistle. Close. Closer than the ones that morning.

The crowd did not scatter—Ironhollow crowds had learned that scattering was itself suspicious—but it rippled, compressed, went quiet in the way of animals sensing weather. And through the parting bodies came Warden Ilsa Vane, in her slate-gray coat with its iron buttons, her baton riding her hip like a second spine, her face doing what warden faces did, which was nothing at all.

Petra's hands went still on the table. Around her, customers became bystanders became strangers, the whole crowd performing innocence in perfect unison.

"Warden," Petra said. "Can I help you?"

"You can show me your license." Ilsa's voice was flat, official, bored. "Carver's license, stall permit, tax stamp."

Petra produced them. Her fingers were steady. She had practiced steadiness for twelve years and it finally paid its dividend. Ilsa examined each paper with excruciating thoroughness, turning them over, holding the tax stamp up to the light as though checking for forgery by sunbeam.

"These are in order," Ilsa said.

"Yes."

"Your prices are very low."

"Bone is cheap."

"Your work is very good." Ilsa's eyes lifted from the papers and met Petra's, and for one unguarded instant there was something in them that was not boredom, not officiousness—something quick and searching, like a bird checking a window for a way out. "Very good indeed. Some might say improbably good."

"Some might say I've had twelve years of practice."

Something flickered across the warden's mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile. It was gone too fast to arrest.

Ilsa set the papers down, squared their edges precisely, and then—deliberately, in full view of the crowd—took out a piece of chalk and drew a single vertical stroke on the corner post of Petra's stall. A tally mark. A brand. Every soul watching understood exactly what it meant: *marked. Watched. Next visit will not be a visit.*

"Regulations require periodic review of commercial stalls," Ilsa announced, loudly, for the record and the audience. "This stall has been reviewed."

Then, quieter, pitched for Petra alone, almost lost under the market noise: "Burn your ledgers tonight. All of them. Names, addresses, orders—everything that tells me who comes here. If I have to come back with a squad, I'll find those ledgers, and everyone in them goes to the Quiet House. Do you understand me?"

Petra's blood went cold and hot at once. "Warden, I don't keep—"

"*Do you understand me?*" And there it was again, under the iron: not menace. Urgency. Fear, even, wearing a warden's coat.

"I understand," Petra said.

Ilsa nodded once, turned, and walked back into the crowd, which parted for her the way water parts for a keel, and closed behind her, and Petra stood alone at her marked stall with two hundred people pretending they hadn't seen anything, holding a tray of charms that hummed faintly against her palms like caged birds.

That night she burned her ledgers in the hearth, page by page, and watched the names of the desperate curl and go black, and hated herself a little for every name, and understood, finally, what Tam had tried to tell her in the gutter months ago: that invisibility was not safety. It was just a slower way of being devoured.

* * *

Three days later, the warden came back. Alone, off-shift, in plain clothes that fooled no one—wardens walked a certain way even barefoot, a certain planted, listening walk—and Petra saw her coming from across the square and made the decision before she'd finished making it.

"Stall's closed," Petra said, when Ilsa reached her. "Come back during business hours."

"I'm not here for bone." Ilsa glanced around the square—at the shuttered stalls, the idling loiterers, the pair of children playing a hopping game that was definitely a lookout rotation, because children in Carver's Quarter learned early. "Walk with me. Ten minutes. Then I was never here."

They walked. Down Carver's Row, past the tanneries, toward the canal where the noise of the water covered conversation the way snow covers footprints.

"You warned me," Petra said. "Why?"

"Because I took a woman from the house next to yours. Maren Ostwick. Charm-maker. Her daughter is eleven."

Petra's throat tightened. "I know Maren."

"Everyone knows Maren. That's the problem." Ilsa kept her eyes forward, her voice level, a recitation. "She was processed standard. Interrogation, confession, sentence. Quiet House. Standard." The word *standard* came out of her mouth like a stone she'd been carrying too long. "Her daughter stood in the intake hall and didn't cry. Eleven years old, and she'd already learned not to cry where wardens can see. And I thought—" Ilsa stopped walking. Stopped herself, forcibly, like reining a horse. "I've done two hundred and six intakes in eleven years. I could tell you every face. I've never once thought about what happened to any of them after the door closed. Until her daughter looked at me like I was weather. Like I was something that just *happens* to people."

The canal slid by, green and slow. Somewhere upstream a barge horn moaned.

"So you let my neighbor's daughter go," Petra said carefully. "In March. The child with the fever charm."

"I filed a report saying no contraband was found." Ilsa's jaw worked. "First false report in eleven years. It sits in my chest like a swallowed coal. I keep waiting for it to burn through me and destroy my life, and instead it just keeps me warm, which is worse somehow." She turned and faced Petra fully, and up close, without the uniform's armor of blankness, her face was older than her years and tired in a way sleep doesn't fix. "Your charms work. Don't insult us both by denying it. I held one last week—confiscated, off the books, from a raid I wasn't supposed to be soft on—and it *answered*. I asked it a question I've never said out loud to another living person, and it answered, and I have not slept properly since."

Petra said nothing. The silence between them filled with canal-water sound and the distant bells of the palace quarter, marking the hour with their fat, blind, gilded voices.

"The king's procession is in nine days," Ilsa said. "Solstice. Whole city turns out, whole warden corps on parade route. And I am telling you this for reasons I do not fully understand myself: whatever you and the lamplighter-priest are planning—and don't bother denying that either, I've watched you meet at the fountain twice—you should know the parade route runs past the temple district, and the temple district wards have been doubled, and there are new men in the palace guard who don't have service records. Men whose files begin the day they arrived. Men who don't eat."

Petra felt the ground tilt slightly, the way it did when the dead spoke through her. "Men who don't eat."

"Hollow men. We call them that in the barracks. Everyone knows, no one says." Ilsa pulled her plain coat tighter, though the day was mild. "I'm not one of you. Understand that. I believe in order. I believe most laws exist because the alternative is fire. But I have started believing that the law I serve is a load-bearing wall built over a pit, and every year I prop it up, and every year the pit gets deeper, and last week I held a piece of bone that told me the truth about my sister's death, and I—" She cut herself off. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was warden-flat once more, and somehow that was more damning than tears. "There's a dead drop behind the chandler's on Tanner's Lane. Blue chalk mark means a message. I'll never admit to this conversation. If you repeat it, I'll arrest you so fast your feet won't touch the ground, and I'll mean it."

"And if I need you?"

Ilsa was already walking away. Over her shoulder, without turning:

"Chalk the drop. I read it every morning. God help us both."

* * *

That evening, Petra sat in the back room of her shop with the shutters barred and her mother's charm cupped in both hands, and for the first time in weeks, the charm warmed back.

Not words. Her mother had grown quieter as the season turned, as though the bound souls were sinking deeper into whatever dark sea the crown kept them in. But warmth. Presence. The sense of a hand resting on a shoulder.

"He's going to feed soon," Petra said aloud. "The king. At solstice. Ilsa's seen the preparations." She turned the charm over, its worn surface catching candlelight. "Mama, I'm going to try to reach you. All of you. I don't know how yet. But Tam thinks the rites at the Binding left seams, and I think—I think my charms have been finding them. Little seams. Little cracks. And cracks widen."

The warmth pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat counting along.

Or like a warning.

Outside, boots rang on cobblestone—not one pair, six, moving in formation—and a whistle blew, and another answered, and the sound rolled down Carver's Row like a stone down a well. Petra pinched out the candle and moved to the gap in the shutters.

Torches. Six wardens, torches, stopping at the mouth of the street. And at their center, a man in palace livery she had never seen before, pale as paper, standing too still, his shadow falling wrong in the torchlight—falling toward the flames instead of away.

He raised his head. Across the distance, across the shutters, across the wards of ordinary darkness, Petra felt his gaze find her shop with the cold precision of a compass needle finding north.

The crown knew where she was.

The chalk mark on her stall had not been a warning after all. It had been a claim.
