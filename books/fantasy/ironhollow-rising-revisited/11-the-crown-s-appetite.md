## 11. The Crown's Appetite

The summons came at dusk, written on vellum so fine it felt like skin.

*Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane. The King's Presence. Tonight.*

No reason given. No reason was ever given for the inner court's business; you went because the crown asked, and you were grateful to be asked, and you did not speak of what you saw inside unless spoken to first. Twelve years of law had worn that courtesy into Ilsa like a groove into stone. She had walked the palace corridors a hundred times, escorting dignitaries, standing honor at the solstice gates, once carrying a sealed writ she had never been permitted to read. She knew the smell of the place—wax and lilies and something underneath, faint as a memory, like a room where a fire had died long ago.

She dressed in her formal grays, pinned the warden's badge of crossed keys over her heart, and told herself the summons meant nothing. A promotion, perhaps. The carver's quarter had been quiet since the burnings—too quiet, the way a wound is quiet before it festers—and perhaps the crown wished to commend its captain for the cleansing.

She did not let herself think about the girl. The neighbor's daughter. The one who had run back into the smoke for a charm her mother had bought from Petra Okonkwo's stall. Ilsa had filed the report herself: *death by misadventure, fire spread from an unlicensed kiln.* Clean words. Words that fit in boxes.

She had stopped sleeping well since the burnings. She told no one that either.

* * *

The palace at night was a different animal than the palace by day. By day it performed itself—banners, guards, the choreography of power. By night it simply *was*, vast and half-lit, and Ilsa felt its age in her teeth as she climbed the eastern stair. The stones here were older than the kingdom. Older, some said, than the Binding itself, though saying such things aloud was a crime with a name now: *nostalgic sedition*. She had arrested a man for less. She had arrested a woman for carving bones too small to notice.

Her boots echoed wrong on the stair. That was the first thing. Each footfall came back to her doubled, delayed by half a heartbeat, as though the stair were repeating her steps to itself, learning them. She stopped on the landing and listened. Nothing. Stone did not echo late. Stone did not listen.

*You are tired,* she told herself. *You have not slept in nine days. Tired minds invent.*

A page in palace silver waited at the top, face smooth as an egg. "Warden-Captain. You're expected." He led her through galleries hung with portraits of kings who had never existed—Aldric the First, Aldric the Second, Aldric the Uniter—all of them the same face painted over and over across two centuries, which the histories explained as dynastic resemblance and which Ilsa, walking beneath them with her neck prickling, suddenly could not explain at all.

The same face. Not similar. *The same.* The same heavy jaw, the same deep-set eyes, the same small scar on the left cheekbone rendered in oil and gilt by painters dead three generations. She counted seven portraits before the page turned down a side corridor and she lost her count.

"Where are we going?" she asked.

"The Presence Chamber," the page said. "His Majesty holds evening court tonight. Small court. You are to observe."

"Observe what?"

The page did not answer. Pages never answered. But as they walked, Ilsa noticed the corridor emptying—not just of people but of light. The lamps here burned low and blue-tinted, and their flames did not flicker when they passed. They leaned. All of them, every flame in the corridor, leaning gently toward the doors at its end, the way grass leans toward a window. Toward light.

Toward the opposite of light, she thought, and could not say why the thought arrived dressed that way.

* * *

The Presence Chamber doors were black ironwood, twice a man's height, carved with the sigil of the Binding: a circle broken by a straight line, the old gods' wheel nailed shut. Two honor wardens flanked it, and both looked through Ilsa as if she were glass. The page knocked once. From within came a voice—not the king's, though it would wear the king's timbre—and said, "Send in the captain."

The chamber was round, domed, lit by a single ring of candles suspended from the ceiling on chains. In the center stood the throne: black stone, high-backed, ugly with purpose, and upon it sat King Aldric.

Ilsa had seen him a dozen times from parade distances. Up close, in this light, he was wrong in ways her body understood before her mind consented to. He sat too still. Not dignified-still, not portrait-still, but *held*-still, the way a marionette hangs between performances, strings taut, waiting. His skin had the gray undertone of meat kept too long. And his eyes—

His eyes were open, and they were looking at her, and there was nothing behind them looking back. She had seen dead men's eyes on battlefields. These were worse. Dead men's eyes at least had finished. These were *occupied*.

"Warden-Captain Vane," the king said. His voice was warm. It was a good voice, a voice built for coronations, and hearing it come out of that stillness was like hearing music from an empty house. "You have served the Quiet faithfully."

"Twelve years, Majesty."

"And before that? What did you serve?"

The question had teeth. She felt them. "The law, Majesty. Before and after. There is only the law."

Something moved in his face—not a smile, not quite. An adjustment. "Yes," he said. "There is only the law. Stay. Watch. Learn what your law feeds."

He turned from her then, dismissive as weather, and Ilsa understood she had not been summoned for promotion or commendation. She had been summoned the way one summons a witness.

The chamber doors opened again, and the court came in.

Small court, the page had said. There were perhaps thirty of them: ministers, high wardens, the Keeper of Seals, a bishop of the Church of the Quiet in white and gray. They arranged themselves in a crescent before the throne with the practiced grace of people who had done this many times, and none of them looked at the king, and none of them looked at each other. Their eyes stayed fixed on the middle distance, on the floor, on the candle ring above. Ilsa took her place at the crescent's end and did the same, and hated herself for how easily her body learned the posture.

"Bring the offering," the king said.

A side door opened. Two attendants entered, supporting between them a man Ilsa recognized: Lord Chancellor Edmun Vael, third rank of the kingdom, a soft-voiced administrator who had signed, among ten thousand documents, the warrant for the carver's quarter. He was seventy years old and had been robust at the autumn review. He walked now like a man wading upstream through deep water, and his attendants were less supporting him than steering him.

"Majesty," Vael said, and his voice cracked on the second syllable. "Majesty, please. I have served—"

"You have," the king agreed. He rose from the throne.

Ilsa had expected the movement to be slow, puppet-clumsy. It was not. The king descended the dais steps with terrible fluidity, robes whispering, and the whole court bowed as one body, and Ilsa bowed too, because twelve years of law lived in her spine and made the motion for her. Through her lashes she watched him cross to Vael and take the old man's face in both hands, tenderly, the way a father cups a child's head.

"The crown is hungry," the king said gently. "It is always hungry. This is not cruelty, Chancellor. It is arithmetic."

"Majesty—" Vael whispered. "Please. My daughters—"

"Will be provided for. The Quiet provides."

And the crown drank him.

There was no other word for what Ilsa saw, though her mind scrambled for others—siphon, leech, flame eating a wick—but *drink* was the truest. The crown of Ironhollow, iron worked with a broken circle, seemed to lean off the king's brow toward Vael's face, and Vael's face *answered* it. Light left him. Not metaphorically; Ilsa saw it, a pale luminescence rising out of the chancellor's open mouth and eyes like breath fogging winter air, streaming upward into the crown, and where the light passed out of him, he aged. Ten years in a breath. Twenty. His hair went from gray to white to nothing. His hands, still held in the king's gentle grip, shrank against the bones inside them. He made a sound—Ilsa would hear it for the rest of her life—a sound like a long sigh that wanted very much to be a scream and had been taught better.

Then it was done. The king released him, and Lord Chancellor Edmun Vael collapsed into his attendants' arms—or into what his arms had become. Dust in the shape of a man. The attendants bore the shape away with the same practiced grace as before, and a servant swept the few crumbs that fell, and the court rose from its bow, and no one spoke, and the candle flames all leaned together toward the throne and slowly righted themselves.

Ilsa's legs held her up. She would always be faintly astonished by that.

"You see," the king said, turning to her, and his face was kind, and his eyes were a room with the furniture removed. "This is what the Binding buys. Order has a cost, Captain. Someone must pay it. For twelve years, the crown has paid it *for* you—for all of you—so that no god walks your streets, so that no charm whispers in your markets, so that your daughter grows up in a city where the dead stay dead and the living stay free." He smiled. It reached his mouth and stopped there, at the border, like a traveler refused entry. "You burned a quarter full of little traitors last week. Good work. But the fire will keep needing fuel until the wheel is nailed down forever. At solstice, we nail it down forever."

"Solstice," Ilsa heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away, borrowed.

"The Rite of Perpetual Quiet. The Binding renewed not for a reign but for an age. You will stand honor at the gates. You should feel honored." He resumed the throne, settling into stillness like a thing poured into a mold. "That is all, Captain. Go home. Sleep. Forget nothing."

*I forget nothing,* Ilsa thought, bowing. *That is my curse and you have just made it my treason.*

* * *

She did not go home.

She walked out of the palace with her face arranged into obedience and her mind screaming, and she kept walking past the warden barracks, past her own street, through districts where the lamps burned honest yellow, until she found herself in the smuggler's warren below the Tanner's Bridge, following a rumor she had no right to know and every intention of using.

The cellar door was exactly where the rumor placed it, behind a dyer's shop that smelled of urine and mordant salt. She knocked the pattern—she'd extracted it from a boy she'd chosen not to arrest, another debt stacking onto the pile—and the door opened on a chain, and Tam's face appeared in the gap, lamplight behind him, and his expression when he saw her badge was the expression of a man watching a knife arrive.

"Warden."

"Ilsa Vane," she said. "Captain. And as of one hour ago, a traitor. May I come in before I finish becoming one?"

He studied her for a long moment. Behind him, at a worktable scattered with bone and knives, Petra Okonkwo had gone still, a half-carved charm under her fingers, watching Ilsa with eyes that had seen her neighbor dragged away and had not forgiven it and never would.

"I watched the king eat a man tonight," Ilsa said quietly. "I watched the crown drink the Chancellor of Ironhollow down to dust while thirty courtiers bowed. I can prove where the dust was swept. I can prove the portraits—the same face, two hundred years of kings, the same scar. And I can tell you that in six days, at the solstice rite, he means to nail the wheel down for an age." She met Tam's eyes. Then Petra's. "So either arrest me, or move, because I'm not standing in a doorway with this in my mouth a moment longer than I have to."

The chain came off.

Inside, the cellar was warm and close and smelled of tallow and bone dust. Petra hadn't moved. Ilsa set her hands flat on the table, deliberately, away from her weapon, and gave the carver the only honesty she had left.

"Your neighbor's daughter," she said. "The report says misadventure. The report is a lie I wrote. I chose the fire over the girl, because choosing otherwise would have cost me everything and changed nothing, and I have hated myself with great efficiency ever since. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you what I am, so you know what my proof is worth."

Petra looked at her for a long, unreadable moment. Then she slid a stool across the table with her foot.

"Sit," Petra said. "Start with the dust."

And Ilsa sat, and began—with the doubled echoes on the stair, the leaning flames, the marionette stillness, the light leaving Vael's mouth like breath, the swept crumbs, the smiling hollow eyes, and above all the words, the king's own words, spoken in that warm dead voice: *At solstice, we nail it down forever.*

When she finished, the silence in the cellar had weight. Tam stood with his knuckles pressed to the table, head bowed, and when he lifted his face Ilsa saw that the disgraced priest was gone and something older and harder stood in his place.

"Six days," he said. "Petra. Get your knives. We're going to need every charm you've ever been afraid of."
