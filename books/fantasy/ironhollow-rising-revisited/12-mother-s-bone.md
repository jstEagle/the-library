## 12. Mother's Bone

The charm had never been warm before.

Petra noticed it in the grey hour before dawn, when the forge-smoke settled low over the carver's quarter and even the rats went quiet. She kept her mother's bone on a cord around her neck, against her skin, where it had lived for twelve years—small as a knuckle, smooth as a river stone, carved with a spiral so fine you'd need a jeweler's loupe to find it. It had always been cool. Dead things were cool. That was the whole architecture of her survival: dead things stayed quiet, and quiet things stayed alive in Ironhollow.

But this morning it pressed against her sternum like a held breath. Warm. Faintly pulsing, the way a sleeping bird pulses in a cupped hand.

She sat up in the loft above Tam's cellar hideout, straw whispering beneath her, and closed her fist around it.

*Petra.*

Not a whisper anymore. Not the half-heard static of the bound dead that had been leaking into her dreams since the mass grave, since she'd stood among ten thousand nameless bones and heard her mother's voice rise out of them like smoke. This was clear. Close. The voice of a woman who used to sing while grinding grain, who used to say *again, from the top* when Petra botched a carving, who had pressed her own fingerbone into her daughter's palm and said *keep me small, keep me safe, live.*

"I'm here," Petra breathed. "Mama. I'm here."

*You've been talking to priests.* No accusation in it. Just weariness, deep as well-water. *Rebels. You smell of their candle smoke.*

"You've been listening."

*I listen to your heart. It's the only clock I have left.* A pause, and when the voice came again it was smaller. *You're going to do it. The unbinding. I felt you decide it three days ago, at the smuggler's table, when you put your hand flat on the wood and swore.*

Petra looked down at her hand as if it belonged to someone else. Three days of planning with Tam and Ilsa, six days until the solstice, and she had thought her resolve was a private thing, a blade sheathed under her ribs. But of course. Of course her mother heard everything. Her mother had been hearing everything for twelve years—the warden raids through the walls, the nights Petra wept into her pallet, the prayers strangers whispered over the charms she sold, all of it filtered through an inch of carved bone.

"Mama—"

*Don't.* The word cracked like ice on a pond. *Don't use that voice. Not yet. Let me say my piece first, because I've had twelve years to compose it and if you interrupt me with that voice I will lose every word.*

Petra's throat closed. She nodded, though there was no one to see it, and pulled her knees up, and waited.

*When the wardens came,* her mother began, *the night of the Binding—you remember, you were eleven, you hid in the flour chest—I had maybe a minute. One minute to choose what to do with the last of myself. And I want you to understand that I chose. They didn't take me whole. I gave. Do you hear the difference?*

"I hear it." She had heard it a hundred times since the grave, but she said it again anyway, because some things need saying out loud to be true.

*I gave the part of me that could be bound. The soul is not one thing, little bird—it's a handful of birds, and most of them flew free the moment the crown's shadow touched our street. What they caught, what the Binding netted out of the city—that was the part that chooses to stay. The part that loves a place. And I took that part, the last of it, and I folded it small, and I asked you to keep it.* The warmth against Petra's sternum pulsed once, hard. *I did not ask you to keep it forever. I asked you to keep it until it was safe to let go.*

"Let go." Petra's voice came out flat. "You mean dissolve."

*I mean finish.* Gentle now. Terribly gentle. *Everything finishes, Petra. Grain becomes bread becomes body becomes bone becomes dust becomes the wheat in someone else's field. That isn't death. Death is what the king does—holding, holding, holding, a fist that never opens. What I'm asking for is the opposite of death. I'm asking for an ending.*

"And the god gives endings." Petra heard her own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "That's what Tam says. The god of endings, freed, would release the bound dead—all of them, ten thousand souls the crown has been eating for twelve years. You'd be free, Mama. Free to—"

*To scatter.* The word fell between them like a dropped knife. *Free to come apart. Child, sit in it. Don't flinch. Sit in what you're promising me.*

Petra sat in it.

The loft was cold. Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear Tam turning in his sleep, muttering fragments of liturgy in a language the law had outlawed. Somewhere beyond the quarter's burned edge—Ilsa's report lay open on the crate by the door, the ritual calendar copied in a warden's careful hand—a king who was not a man sat in a room full of candles and drank the dead.

*Here is what freedom means for me,* her mother said. *The moment the god unbinds, everything the crown holds lets go at once. Ten thousand souls, released together, like a dam breaking. And I am worn thin, Petra. Twelve years in an inch of bone. I am a coin rubbed smooth—I was a woman once, with a face and a temper and a way with songs, and now I am mostly love and mostly memory, and when the dam breaks, love and memory don't hold shape. We scatter into the ground, into the wind, into whatever comes next. I won't suffer. I want that understood. But I also won't be me. There will be no one left to know your name. There will be no one left to have been your mother. Do you see? It isn't dying I fear. It's being unwritten.*

Petra found she was gripping the charm so hard its edges bit her palm. She made herself loosen her fingers, one by one.

"Then I won't do it," she said. "We'll find another way. Tam's people wanted to kill the king anyway—assassination, Ilsa said half the cell still wants it. If the vessel dies, maybe the crown—"

*The crown moves on.* Her mother's voice was patient, the patience of someone who had watched twelve years of dawns from inside a pocket of bone. *It moved onto Aldric when the last vessel failed. It will move onto whoever wears it next. Killing the man feeds it; freeing the god starves it. There is no third door, little bird. There is only the choice you already made, and the reason you haven't told the priest about it.*

Petra's breath caught. "What reason?"

*Because you already know what I'm going to ask. You've known since the grave. You lie awake composing arguments against me, and every argument ends the same place, and you can't bear to hear me say it, so you've said nothing.* A pause. When the voice came again it was almost amused, and the amusement broke Petra's heart worse than grief would have. *Stubborn. You get it from me. Your father was soft as spring clay.*

"Don't," Petra whispered. "Don't talk about him. Not tonight."

*Then let me talk about you.* The warmth shifted against her skin, and Petra had the sudden vertiginous sense of her mother leaning close, closer than she had leaned in twelve years. *You survived, child. You survived by making yourself small—small charms, small words, small life—and I have watched you do it and hated it and blessed it, both, every day, because smallness kept you breathing. But you are done being small. In six days you are going to stand in front of a god and carve the largest working anyone has attempted since the Binding, and you will pour yourself into it, and either you succeed or the crown eats you the way it eats everything. So hear me. If you are going to spend yourself, spend yourself on something true. Don't spend me instead.*

"That's not—" Petra's voice splintered. "That's not how it works. The rite needs an anchor. Tam worked it out with what's left of the temple records. Someone has to hold the pattern of the unbinding steady while the wards fight back, and the anchor has to be a bound soul, willing, close enough to the carver to hold. Mama, there is no version of this where I reach the crown and you aren't there. You're not the cost. You're the *tool*. The only one I have."

Silence. Long enough that Petra wondered if the connection had frayed, if her mother had withdrawn somewhere deeper into the bone.

Then, quietly: *Say the rest of it.*

"There's nothing else."

*There is. Say it or I'll say it for you, and you know I'll say it plainer than you can stomach.*

Petra stared at the dark rafters. Her eyes were burning. "If the unbinding works—if the god scatters the dead—then the anchor goes first. The anchor dissolves before the rest. That's the order of it. The pattern burns brightest at its center." She swallowed. "So it's not just that you end. It's that you end *sooner*. Days sooner. Maybe hours. I'd be trading whatever time you have left—years, maybe, if I never do the rite—for a city that doesn't know your name either."

*There.* Soft. Almost tender. *Was that so hard?*

"It was the hardest sentence of my life."

*And now we're finally honest with each other, after twelve years, which is its own kind of miracle.* The warmth pulsed again, and Petra realized her mother was laughing—not bitterly, but with the tired, real amusement of a woman looking at a truth she'd walked around for days. *Oh, my love. You think I don't know the arithmetic? I've had twelve years to count. Every hour in this bone, I have counted. Do you know what I counted most?*

"What?"

*The sound of you living. Your heartbeat through the cord. Your breath when you slept. The little hum you make when you're carving well—you still do it, you know, you've done it since you were six, and every time, for twelve years, I have thought: there. There she is. Whatever else I am or was or will be, I got to hear that.* The voice wavered, steadied. *A few more years of that, bought by leaving the god in chains and the king eating the dead and ten thousand souls screaming in a crown—is that what you're offering me? More listening? At that price?*

"Yes," Petra said fiercely. "Yes. That's exactly what I'm offering. Because you're my mother, and I am *allowed* to be selfish about you. Let the city save itself. Let Tam find another anchor. Let the gods stay bound another twelve years, another fifty—I don't care. I found you. After twelve years of silence I finally found you, and you want me to give you up for a city that burned our quarter and took Mrs. Adeyemi and shot a girl in the ash market—"

*Petra.*

"—a city that taught me to carve so small the gods themselves couldn't see me—"

*Petra Okonkwo.* Full name. The voice of a mother standing in a doorway. *Look at what you just said. Say it again, slowly, and hear yourself.*

Petra's mouth was trembling too hard to speak.

*You said: a city that taught me to hide. And you're right. It did. And hiding is how you survived, and I will never regret the minute I spent choosing it for you. But hiding is also why Mrs. Adeyemi is ash. Hiding is why Sela's daughter died in the fire while wardens stood in the street and watched. Hiding is why ten thousand people screamed for twelve years and the only ones who heard them were a disgraced priest and a girl selling knuckle-bone trinkets.* The warmth burned now, not with anger—with something older and cleaner. *I did not fold myself into this bone so my daughter could keep me like a talisman against doing right. I gave you the part of me that loves a place. So love the place, child. Love it loudly. Love it the way I loved it—enough to die for it, enough to *end* for it.*

"I can't." The words tore out of her raw. "Mama, I can't be the one. I was eleven years old. I didn't ask for any of this. I just wanted to sell charms and be invisible and survive, and now there's a god in the palace and a rebellion in the cellar and a man I—" She stopped. Breathed. Started smaller. "There's a man I love waiting downstairs, and a warden who betrayed her uniform for us, and six days, and you're asking me to choose the world over the one voice I have left, and I am telling you, mother to daughter, daughter to mother: I don't have it in me. Find someone else. Be angry with me. Haunt me. I don't care. I'm not giving you up."

For a long moment, silence. The forge-smoke grey outside the window lightened by one shade. Downstairs, Tam's muttering had gone still—he was awake, then, listening to the ceiling, saying nothing, because he was Tam and he knew which silences were doors.

Then her mother laughed again, and this time it undid her completely.

*Oh, little bird. You think this is me asking?* The warmth spread through Petra's palm, up her wrist, gentle as bathwater. *This is me telling you what I want, yes. But I have known since the grave that you'd refuse me. I raised you. You have your father's softness and my stubbornness and between the two of them you couldn't abandon a spider, let alone a city. I begged because I owed you the begging—I owed you the truth of what dissolution is, so that when you chose, you chose with open eyes. But Petra. Listen to me now, and believe me, because I have never lied to you and I will not start with my last hours of being anyone.*

Petra wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Tell me."

*If you refused—if you kept me, and hid, and let the Binding stand—I would stay in this bone, and I would listen to your heartbeat, and I would be grateful for every hour. And I would also watch you become smaller every year. I would watch the guilt calcify. I would watch you become the thing this city made you, forever, and I would know that my love was the chain that kept you there. That is not a gift, child. That is a haunting. Mothers haunt their daughters in worse ways than ghosts.*

"So what are you saying?" Petra whispered. "That you forgive me either way?"

*I'm saying there is no way to lose.* The voice was fading now, gently, the way a lamp gutters down rather than out. *If you keep me, I lose years and you lose yourself. If you free me, I lose myself and you gain the world. Either way, we lose something, because that is what the king's crown has made of every choice in this city—there is no clean hand anywhere in Ironhollow. But you asked me once, when you were seven, whether it was better to be a bird in a cage that lives long, or a bird in the sky that might be caught. Do you remember what I told you?*

Petra remembered. She remembered the kitchen, the smell of millet, her mother's hands dusted with flour. *"The sky,"* she whispered. *"You said the sky, always the sky, even for the bird that gets caught."*

*Even for the bird that gets caught.* A pause, and when her mother spoke again, each word came slow and deliberate, laid down like stones across a river. *Six days. Come to me before the solstice. Not for a argument—for a blessing. Bring the priest, if he makes you brave. Bring the warden, if she makes you angry. But come, and let me put my last shape around you, and then go and carve the sky open. And Petra—*

"Yes?"

*When it's done, when I'm scattered, don't you dare listen for me. There'll be nothing there, and you'll only hurt yourself. Grieve me once, properly, at the grave with the ten thousand, and then turn around and live. Marry the lamplighter. Sing off-key. Carve big, ugly, visible things. Be everything they tried to starve out of you.* The warmth gathered itself, one final pulse, fierce as a grip. *That is my price. Not safety. Not sparing. Life. Promise me life, and I promise you I'll go into the sky glad.*

Petra pressed the bone to her lips. It was warm as a living cheek.

"I promise," she said, and her voice broke on it, and she said it again until it held. "Life. I promise life."

*Good girl.* A sigh, long and slow, twelve years long. *Now go downstairs. The priest is awake and pretending not to be, and he makes terrible tea, and you should drink it while it's hot, because in six days neither of you may get to be ordinary again.*

The warmth dimmed—not gone, never gone, banked like a coal—but the voice withdrew, and Petra sat alone in the grey light with her mother's bone against her heart and her face wet and her chest strangely, terribly light.

Below, a floorboard creaked. Tam's footsteps, careful on the ladder. His head appeared through the hatch, hair sleep-mussed, eyes finding her immediately in the dark.

"You heard," he said softly. Not a question.

"She knows the plan. All of it. She's known for days." Petra climbed down past him, not trusting her legs on the ladder, and stood in the cellar's candle-glow looking up at this man who had lost a whole world and kept faith with its ashes anyway. "Tam. There's something I have to tell you about the rite. About the anchor. Something I should have said the night we bargained in this cellar, and didn't, because I was a coward."

He went very still. "Petra—"

"The anchor dissolves first." She made herself say it plainly, the way her mother would have. "And the anchor is her. And last night she begged me not to do it—and this morning she gave me her blessing instead—and I need you to understand that when we walk into that throne room, I am carrying my mother's ending in my pocket, and I am choosing it, and if you have a kinder plan, a third door, *any* door I haven't seen—"

"Petra." He crossed the cellar in two steps and took her hands, and his were shaking. "There's no third door. There never was. But you're wrong about one thing." His voice roughened. "You're not carrying her ending. She is. And she's carrying yours. That's what mothers do."

Outside, far off toward the palace, a bell tolled the dawn hour. Five days now. Somewhere above them, behind the crown of a hollow king, ten thousand souls turned in their sleep—and one of them, small and smooth and warm, curled closer to her daughter's heart and waited for the sky.
