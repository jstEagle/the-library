## 13. The Rebel's Bargain

The cellar beneath the tannery smelled of lye and old blood, and Tam had learned to find both comforting. Lye meant work was still being done above. Blood meant the city still bled, and a city that bled could be healed.

Tonight the cellar held thirty-one people, and it felt like a knife balanced on its point.

He stood at the head of the long table—actually two doors laid across barrels—and watched the schism move through the room like weather. On his left, the older ones: Marisol with her burn-scarred hands, Odo the ferryman, the widow Cett who had lost three sons to the Wardens' quiet disappearances. They had come to him over twelve years, one by one, carrying grief like contraband. They wanted the Binding undone. They wanted their dead given back to the dark, honestly and finally. They wanted the world they remembered.

On his right sat the others—the ones who had come in the last two years, since word spread that charms were working again, since Petra Okonkwo's small bones had begun to answer prayers. Younger faces. Harder ones. Bren of the river docks, who still wore the Warden's whip scar across his throat as a necklace he touched when he spoke. The twins, Asha and Rell, orphaned in the Ash Purges. And at their center, leaning forward with his elbows on the door-table like a man already halfway to violence, Corvus Hane.

Corvus had been a palace guard once. That was the whole of his argument, and he made it now for the third time tonight, his voice low and reasonable in the way that frightened Tam most.

"Six days," Corvus said. "That's what your warden brought us. Six days until the solstice rite, until the king renews the Binding and swallows another year of our dead. You're telling me we have a god-eating thing wearing our king's face, and your plan is to *carve* at it. To send a bone-carver into the throne room with a knife and hope."

"Not hope," Tam said. "A working. The unbinding rites are real, Corvus. I performed them before the Binding. I know the words."

"You were a priest." Corvus smiled without warmth. "You were a priest, and where did that get the gods? Bound. Where did it get you? Lighting lamps."

The cellar murmured. Tam let it murmur. He had learned that silence after an insult was worth more than any answer, because it left the insult hanging in the air, showing its own ugliness.

"The king is not a thing," Tam said finally. "I've seen him. I've stood close enough to touch the litter at the procession, and I'm telling you there is a man in there. A hollowed man, a used-up man, but a man. Aldric Veyra was crowned twelve years ago and something climbed into him through the crown, and every year since, the crown has eaten more of him to keep itself fed. If you put a blade through that body, you don't kill the crown. You free it. It finds another vessel—or it doesn't need one. It's been drinking souls for twelve years. What do you think it becomes if you cut it loose from its cup?"

"Then it starves," Bren said. "Good."

"It doesn't starve." Tam heard his own voice flatten, and hated how much it sounded like the lectern voice, the pulpit voice, the voice of a man who had once told three hundred worshippers that suffering was a door. "It has eaten thousands. It is not a beast, it is a *god*—the god of endings, the last door, the one who closes every eye. You cannot starve an ending. You can only choose what it ends."

"And your carver chooses to end it gently?" Asha laughed, short and bitter. "With a charm? While it sits on a throne behind a hundred wardens?"

"She won't be alone."

"No," Corvus said softly. "She'll have you holding wards open with prayers to gods who haven't answered anyone in twelve years. She'll have a turncoat warden opening gates. And she'll have us—" he spread his hands, taking in the cellar, the tallow light, the smell of lye— "doing what? Standing in the square singing hymns?"

"We get the people out. We open the ways. We hold the streets so the wardens can't seal the district while the working happens—"

"That's not a plan, priest. That's a funeral with extra steps." Corvus stood. He was a big man gone lean, muscle melted off a soldier's frame by years of hiding, and when he stood the room stood with him—half the room, anyway. "Here's my plan. Six days. We know the ritual calendar. We know the king will be in the throne room, still, open, mid-rite—that's when the crown drinks deepest and the vessel is weakest. We put steel in that weakness. Not a carving knife. Steel. Kill the vessel, take the crown, bury it under the mountain in a lead box, and Ironhollow wakes up free without needing a miracle."

"And the bound dead?" Marisol's voice cracked across the room like a whip. She was on her feet too, though it cost her; Tam saw her knuckles white on the table edge. "My sons are in that thing, Corvus. In the crown. In whatever passes for its belly. Your lead box buries them alive forever. Is that your freedom?"

"They're dead, woman."

"They are *held*." Marisol's voice broke entirely, and she didn't care, and Tam loved her for not caring. "There's a difference. There has always been a difference, and men like you learn it too late."

* * *

They argued until the candles drowned in their own wax. Tam counted the room the way he counted everything now, in losses. Seventeen for the unbinding. Fourteen for the knife. It wasn't even the numbers that frightened him—it was the shape of them. The young wanted the knife. The young always wanted the knife, because the young had never yet seen what a knife does to the hand that holds it after.

When the shouting burned down to embers, Corvus came to him in the corner by the salt barrels, where Tam kept the few relics of his old life wrapped in oilcloth: a broken censer, a psalter with half its pages torn out by a Warden's knife, a copper ring he had not worn in twelve years.

"Priest." Corvus kept his voice down. Around them the factions were leaving in twos and threes, not looking at each other, the way families leave rooms after bad news. "I don't think you're a fool. That's what makes this hard. I think you believe every word you say. So believe this one: if we do it your way and it fails, there is no second chance. The crown will know our faces, our names, our holes in the ground. It will eat this cellarm and everyone in it, and it will start with the carver girl, because she's the only threat it's ever had. You'll have traded the city for a prayer."

"And if we do it yours," Tam said, "we become the thing we're fighting. A power that decides whose soul gets spent for the greater quiet. I've lived under that law for twelve years, Corvus. I've lit lamps under it. I'm not going to carry it into the new world myself."

"Then you'd better pray harder than you ever have." Corvus shouldered past him toward the stairs, then stopped, hand on the rail, not turning around. "One more thing, priest. If the vote goes against you tomorrow—if the cellarm chooses the knife—you'll stand aside? Or will your faith only run one direction?"

Tam looked at the oilcloth bundle, at the ring inside it, warm as a coal even through the cloth, or so he imagined.

"I'll stand aside," he lied.

* * *

He walked home the long way, along the canal, because the long way passed the lampposts he tended and lighting them gave his hands something honest to do while his mind tore itself apart.

Twelve years ago he had been Father Tamasin Vael, third voice of the Choir of the Open Door, and he had believed—as cleanly as Corvus believed in steel—that the gods would protect the faithful. Then came the night the sky over the temple hill turned the color of a closed eye, and the Binding fell, and every prayer in Ironhollow hit a ceiling of silence. He had survived by being small. He had survived by learning to trim wicks instead of souls. Every year since, he had told himself the smallness was strategy, was patience, was the long road back. Tonight, walking the canal with a taper in his fist, he admitted what he had never admitted to anyone, not even himself in the dark hours:

Some of the smallness had been cowardice. And some of it had been relief. Because if no one expected anything of a lamplighter, then no one could be damned by his failures.

And now a bone-carver with tired eyes and a dead mother's soul worn smooth in her pocket had looked at him across a smuggler's cellar and said, *I'll do it, but you have to promise me the living will be sheltered*, and he had promised, and the promise had gone through him like a nail through the palm. Because he could not keep it. No one could keep it. Six days from now, some of these people—Marisol, maybe, or the twins, or Petra herself—were going to die, and he would be the one who had led them there with a psalter full of torn pages.

He lit the lamp outside the tannery. He lit the next one, and the next. Small flames against a large dark. It was all he had ever actually done, he realized. All those years of priesthood, all those sermons about the Open Door—in the end his whole theology fit inside a lamplighter's brass hood.

But a lamp is not nothing, he told the dark. A lamp is a choice about where the dark doesn't get to win.

* * *

The vote came at dawn, in the same cellar, by the old method—river stones in a clay jar, white for the unbinding, black for the knife. Tam had not slept. He suspected no one had. The stones clicked into the jar one by one, each rebel passing the vessel down the line with the solemnity of communicants, and Tam watched the jar fill and thought: *whatever it says, I will obey it, whatever it says, I will obey it,* knowing even as he thought it that obedience was a country he might not be able to reach.

Seventeen white. Fourteen black.

Close enough to break a movement in half. Close enough that Corvus's people would walk out and start their own war, a war within the war, and the crown would pick up both halves like crumbs.

Unless someone made the split impossible to survive.

Tam stood. The room went quiet the way water goes still before ice.

"Fourteen of you want the knife," he said. "I understand why. I've spent the night understanding it, and I'm not going to insult you with sermons. So here is what I offer instead." His heart was slamming against his ribs, and he made his voice come out level anyway, the old pulpit trick, the body obedient to the voice and not the reverse. "If the unbinding fails—if Petra reaches the crown and the working breaks—I give you my oath, sworn on what's left of my order, that I will put the knife in myself. My hand, my steel, my sin. You won't need to lift a finger. If the gentle way fails, I will do the terrible thing, and I will carry it alone."

"And if you die first?" Bren said. "Priests die easy. It's practically the job."

"Then Corvus leads, and I'll swear him in as my successor right now, before witnesses, so there's no argument afterward." Tam turned to Corvus and saw the surprise in the man's face—genuine, unguarded, the first honest expression he'd shown all night. "Do you accept the terms? Unbinding first. Knife second. One attempt at mercy, then no mercy at all."

Corvus studied him for a long moment. Somewhere above them the tannery vats began to churn, the day's work starting, indifferent.

"You're promising us both outcomes," Corvus said slowly. "Mercy and murder, whichever works. You know what that makes you?"

"It makes me the one who holds the whole weight," Tam said, "so no one else has to hold half of it and fight the other half. Yes. I know exactly what it makes me."

Corvus put out his hand. Tam took it. The grip was hard enough to hurt, and meant to.

"It's done, then," Corvus said to the room. "We do it his way. Six days. Everyone back to work—quietly, separately, the usual routes. And pray to whatever's left out there that the carver girl is as good as the priest thinks she is."

They filed out. Marisol squeezed Tam's arm as she passed, and said nothing, which was the kindest thing anyone had said to him in years.

Last of all came the twins, Asha and Rell, and Asha paused at the foot of the stairs and looked back at him with her parents' eyes, the eyes of people he had failed to save, and said, "You swore it, priest. On your order. We heard it."

"I swore it."

"Then I hope your god was listening." She climbed the stairs after her brother, and the trapdoor closed, and Tam stood alone in the empty cellar with the clay jar of voting stones and the candle burning down to nothing.

He picked up the jar. White stones, black stones, all mixed together now, indistinguishable by weight. He carried it to the corner and set it beside the oilcloth bundle, beside the broken censer and the torn psalter and the ring he still could not bring himself to wear.

Then he took out the psalter, opened it to the rite of unbinding, and began, by candlelight, to copy out the words in his own hand—one copy for Petra, one for Ilsa, one for himself—because in six days there would be no time to teach, only to trust.

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a bell rang the hour. Five bells until dawn's end. Five days and change.

Somewhere in the dark above, he knew, the crown was feeding. And somewhere in the carver's quarter, a woman with a dead mother's whisper in her pocket was sharpening bone into a key, and did not yet know what price the lock would ask.

Tam dipped his pen, and wrote, and did not stop writing until his hand cramped—because the promise he had made was impossible, and impossible promises were the only kind he had ever kept.
