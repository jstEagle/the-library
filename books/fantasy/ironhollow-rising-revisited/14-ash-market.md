## 14. Ash Market

The smoke came before the bells.

Petra smelled it at dawn—a greasy, resinous stink rolling down from the Warden's Gate, wrong for cooking fires, wrong for forge-smoke. It was the smell of pitch and lamp-oil, of things meant to burn fast and hot. She stood in her doorway with a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands and watched the sky over the carver's quarter turn the color of an old bruise.

Twelve years she had kept this shop small. Twelve years of carving charms too plain to notice, of selling bone buttons and hair combs to hide the working pieces among them like needles in a haystack of needles. She had learned the arithmetic of invisibility: never more than three charms on display, never the same prayer carved twice, never let them see you hope.

And now the wardens were coming with fire, because invisibility had never been safety. It had only been patience—*their* patience—and it had run out.

"Petra." Tam's voice, low, from the alley mouth. He still wore his lamplighter's coat, but he'd come without his pole, which meant he'd come running. His face was gray under the soot he never quite washed off. "They're clearing the whole quarter. Ilsa sent word an hour ago—she couldn't get more than that."

"I got nothing." Which was a lie; Ilsa's note was folded against Petra's breastbone, where her mother's charm used to hang. *Burn day. Come early or not at all.* "How many?"

"All of them. Every warden on the roster, plus the king's own guard. They're not making arrests today, Petra." He swallowed. "They're making an example."

Down the street, old Maren was dragging a handcart piled with her whole life—spindles, dyepots, a loom too wide for the cart bed that scraped sparks off the cobbles as she hauled it. Her granddaughter ran beside her clutching a cat. Nobody was screaming yet. That was the worst part, Petra thought. The quiet. Ironhollow had gotten so good at quiet that even now, with fire coming, they moved like people who believed that if they made no sound, no one would see them.

She had believed that once. She had built her entire life inside that belief, mortar and timber, and now she could feel it burning around her before the first torch was even lit.

"We take what we can carry," Tam said. "The cellar tunnel comes up behind the tannery—"

"I'm not leaving through any tunnel." Petra set down her tea. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Under the steadiness something vast and black was rising, twelve years deep, and she let it rise because fighting it had gotten Maren's son taken and the Ferro girl's father hanged and a hundred small kindnesses erased. "I'm leaving through my front door, in daylight, carrying my mother's bones. Let them write *that* in their reports."

* * *

The shop was one room and a loft, and it took ten minutes to empty of everything that mattered.

Her tools went into a satchel: the burins, the rasps, the little hooked knife her mother had used for gutting fish before it ever carved bone. The ledger of debts nobody could collect anymore. A jar of tallow. And then the box—ironwood, no bigger than a loaf, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord—and inside the box, wrapped again in wool, the charm.

It looked like nothing. That had always been its genius. A disc of bone the size of a thumbnail, worn smooth as a river stone by twelve years against her skin, carved with a pattern so fine you'd need a lens to read it: her mother's name, her mother's death-day, and beneath them a spiral with no beginning and no end, the oldest sign there was, older than the gods' own names. Inside it, folded small as a moth in winter, her mother's soul.

Petra had not heard her speak since the night at the mass grave. She did not know if the silence was sleep, or grief, or the slow wearing-away of a soul kept too long in too small a house. She only knew that when she held the charm, the hollow place behind her sternum—the one every survivor of the Binding carried—went quiet, and that was as close to her mother as she would ever get again.

She wrapped the box against her chest with a strip of linen, tied it tight, and put her coat on over it.

The knock came while she was banking the hearth. Three blows, official cadence. She didn't answer. The door came open anyway, and Warden Ilsa Voss filled the frame in full kit, visor down, looking less like a woman Petra half-trusted and more like the law given boots.

"You're still here." Flat voice, flat eyes. But her gaze flicked—to the satchel, to the cold hearth, to the bare walls where a careful eye might notice the pale rectangles where charms had hung until last night. "Good. You have maybe two hundred heartbeats. The pyre detail is at the fountain square. When they reach this street, everything on it burns, papers first."

"Papers first," Petra repeated. "You mean people first. You just say it in the order that lets you sleep."

Something moved behind the visor. "I sleep fine," Ilsa said, in the voice of someone who did not. "Back door. Now."

* * *

They went out through the yard behind the row of shops, past the shared privy and the leaning fence Maren's husband had mended every spring until they took him. Tam was waiting with a handcart disguised under sacking, and Petra almost laughed—two carts in one morning, the whole quarter fleeing on wheels because their legs had finally understood what their minds refused.

Maren was in the street ahead of them, still wrestling the loom. And behind Maren, small and quick in a patched red dress, came Sefa Ferro—the neighbor's daughter, eleven years old, all elbows and opinions, who had spent the winter sitting on Petra's counter asking questions no child should have to ask. *Why do the wardens burn charms if magic isn't real? If it isn't real, what are they afraid of?*

"Sefa!" Maren's voice cracked across the street. "Inside! Get inside!"

But the girl had seen the smoke and done what children do—run toward it, toward her grandmother, toward the thing she didn't understand. She was halfway across the lane when the first torchbearers rounded the fountain square.

There were thirty of them, maybe forty, in a wedge formation Petra recognized from the processions: shields forward, torches high, a warden-captain walking backward before them calling the streets. Behind the wedge came the wagons. Not arrest wagons. Oil wagons.

"Clear the way!" the captain called. "By decree of the Crown, the carver's quarter is condemned for sedition. Clear the way or be counted with it."

Maren froze in the middle of the street with her cart, an old woman between a wall of shields and her granddaughter, and Petra saw the exact moment the captain saw her and decided she was furniture. "Move her," he said, without heat, the way you'd say *move the barrel*, and two wardens peeled off to do it.

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do—all its parts already in motion, needing only gravity.

Sefa darted out from behind a rain barrel to reach her grandmother. One of the wardens moving Maren swung his shield arm wide to block the girl, a casual backhand, the kind of blow you give to clear a stray dog from a formation. It caught Sefa across the temple. She went down on the cobbles—not hard, not far, just down, the way children fall a hundred times a year—

—but her head found the iron corner of Maren's cart bed on the way, and she did not get up the way children usually do.

The sound Maren made was not a scream. Petra would remember that. It was smaller than a scream, a single punched-out syllable, and it was worse than any scream she had ever heard, because a scream asks the world for help and this sound already knew none was coming.

Everything after that, Petra saw in pieces.

The wardens resuming formation, because a dead child was not a stoppage of work. The captain's voice, unchanged: *Clear the way.* Torches going into doorways, into window boxes, into the dry eaves of shops whose owners had spent twelve years being careful. The oil wagons sloshing their cargo down the lane in long glistening ropes. Fire taking hold with a sound like applause.

Tam hauling Maren away from the loom, away from Sefa, while the old woman fought him with the strength of the dying. Petra kneeling in the street with her hands pressed to a small cooling face, doing the thing she knew how to do, the only thing—listening.

Nothing listened back.

That was the horror of it. In all of Ironhollow, with the bound dead pressed flat under the Binding like flowers in a book, there was no one home behind the silence. Sefa was simply gone, whole and entire, in a way the bound souls were not even permitted to be gone. The Binding at least preserved. This was erasure, clean and total, administered by an iron cart corner and a bored man's arm.

Petra knelt there until Tam's hand closed on her shoulder, and then she stood, and the black thing that had been rising in her all morning finally broke the surface, and she stopped being afraid.

* * *

Later, she could not have said what she intended. Only what she did.

She walked to the edge of the formation, into the smoke, and planted herself in front of the warden-captain with her mother's bones against her chest and her voice pitched to carry.

"Her name was Sefa Ferro," Petra said. "She was eleven years old. Write it down. Write down that the Crown of Ironhollow killed a child today for standing in a street, and then burned four hundred homes so no one would have time to bury her properly. Write it in your report. Go on. You have such tidy reports."

The captain turned. Through the smoke his face was ordinary, tired, a man doing a job—and that, more than anything, was what made her shake. Not monsters. Men.

"Woman, step back—"

"A man died in this quarter last month for owning a charm that worked." She was louder now. Somewhere behind her, Tam was saying her name in a warning tone, and somewhere beyond him the fire was eating the roofline of the shop where she had lived small and safe for twelve years, and she found she did not care about any of it. "A woman was taken in the night for a charm that healed her son's fever. You call that sedition. I'll tell you what sedition is. Sedition is a crown that eats its people and calls the eating law. Sedition is—" 

The backhand caught her across the mouth.

She tasted blood and went to one knee on the hot cobbles, and through the ringing in her skull she heard the captain say, wearily, "Take her name. Don't take her. We don't have time for a body today." And then, to the line: "Forward. Street by street. Nothing stands."

A boot prodded her shoulder. She looked up into a young warden's visor, and behind the slit of it, a face she knew—Ilsa's second, the boy with the freckles, the one who bought combs for his sister. He wouldn't meet her eyes.

"Go," he whispered. "North lane. *Go.*"

She went. It felt like retreat. It felt like the whole twelve years, compressed into one long walk north while everything she had failed to protect burned behind her.

* * *

Ilsa found them at dusk, in the tannery yard where the reek hid the smell of smoke.

She came alone, out of uniform, in a laborer's coat, and she walked past Tam without a glance and stopped in front of Petra like a woman arriving at a debt collector's table.

"The Ferro girl," Ilsa said.

"You know her name."

"I know all of it. I've read the reports for six years." Her jaw worked. "The captain's account says the child fell. It says she struck her head in the confusion caused by seditious elements obstructing a lawful action. There will be no inquiry. There will be a stipend to the grandmother, contingent on silence."

"Maren won't be silent."

"Maren is seventy years old and has nowhere to go. She'll be silent." Ilsa said it without cruelty, the way a surgeon says *it will hurt*. Then she reached into the laborer's coat and set something on the barrel between them: a leather dispatch case, palace-stamped, sealed with the crown's black wax. "Rota schedules. Ward assignments for the solstice. And this." A second object, smaller: a page torn from a chapel ledger, old paper, browned at the folds. "My grandfather's. He was a lay brother at the Temple of the Nine, before. I've kept it since I was sixteen because I couldn't make myself burn it. Tonight I understood why."

Tam stepped forward slowly, the way you approach a deer. "Warden Voss—"

"Don't thank me." Her voice cracked on the word *warden*, and she steadied it. "I took a man from his family in this quarter and told myself it was law. I filed false reports for years and told myself it was mercy. Today a child died in the street and men I've served with for a decade stepped over her body to light their torches, and I stood in formation and did nothing." She looked at Petra, and for the first time Petra saw what was under the visor: not hardness. Exhaustion. The specific exhaustion of someone who has been holding a door shut for years and has just realized what's on the other side of it. "I am done telling myself stories. So. Ask me what I brought you, carver, and then ask me what I need."

Petra touched her split lip. Behind them, above the tannery roofs, the glow of the carver's quarter lit the underside of the clouds like a wound seen through skin. Somewhere in that light was her shop, her bench, the hooks where her mother's charm had hung for twelve years—empty now, saved, but the walls around the saving gone.

"What you need," Petra said slowly, "is for this to mean something. For the girl's name to go somewhere other than a stipend ledger."

"Yes."

"Then you should know what's in the case you brought us." Petra untied the cord on the ironwood box, unwrapped the wool, and held the charm up between two fingers so the tannery's lantern light caught the fine-cut spiral. "Because in six days your king is going to renew the Binding at the solstice rite. He's going to feed it, the way he's fed it for twelve years. And I am going to carve it open."

Ilsa stared at the little disc of bone. "That's—"

"My mother. What's left of her." Petra closed her fist around it, gentle, the way you hold a bird. "Your king ate her, Warden. Along with thousands more. And today his wardens killed a child who asked why they were afraid of things that aren't real." She looked up, and let Ilsa see all of it—the rage, the grief, the terrible calm underneath. "So ask yourself what you need. And then decide how much of it you're willing to burn to get it."

Above the roofs, the fire roared on, patient as law. And in Petra's closed fist, faint as a heartbeat, for the first time in weeks, her mother stirred—and whispered one word:

*Six.*
