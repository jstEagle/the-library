## 15. What Ilsa Owes

The summons came at the fourth bell of the morning watch, and Ilsa Vane knew before she read it that she was already dead.

It was a small thing, the summons. A slip of grey vellum, sealed with the crown's black wax, delivered by a courier who would not meet her eyes. *Warden-Captain Ilsa Vane to report to the Lord Inquisitor's office, ninth bell, regarding irregularities in the Ash Market action.* Irregularities. That was the word they used now, instead of *treason*, because treason required proof and proof took time, and the Inquisitor liked to let his suspects simmer in the interval—let them run, or burn their papers, or hang themselves in some quiet stairwell where the shame could be hushed up.

She had known this was coming for three days, ever since the fire. She had stood in the smoking ruin of the carver's quarter with ash sifting down like grey snow, watching a warden named Doss kick through the charred bones of a workshop, and she had felt nothing at all—which was itself a kind of confession. Twelve years of feeling nothing had made her very good at her job. It had also, she understood now, been slowly hollowing her out the same way the crown hollowed the king, until there was nothing left inside the uniform but the uniform.

And then there was the girl. The neighbor's daughter—the carver's daughter, Petra Okonkwo's young friend, whose name Ilsa still did not know because no one had survived to speak it. They had pulled her body from under a collapsed lintel an hour after the fires were out, and she had been clutching something in her fist: a bone charm, plain as a button, carved with a spiral so small you'd miss it if you weren't looking. Ilsa had pried it from the dead girl's fingers herself, because that was the job, and she had put it in her evidence pouch, and she had carried it back to the garrison, and she had not entered it into the ledger.

That was the irregularity. That, and the false report. That, and the child she'd released on the night of the raid, three weeks ago now—a boy of maybe nine, sobbing over a charm he'd bought for his sick mother, whom Ilsa had walked out the back of the interrogation block and told never to come near the carver's quarter again. That, and every small mercy she had extended since the doubt first took root in her, each one a thread, and someone above her had spent the last week quietly pulling threads.

She sat at her desk in the warden barracks and drank cold tea and thought about running.

The thought was not new. It had been arriving more often lately, patient as weather. But running meant leaving, and leaving meant admitting what she was, and she had spent twelve years being nothing at all rather than admit what she was. Her father had been a priest of the Bound—of Maren, the tide-mother, whose temple stood in the Salt Quarter before the Binding cracked its bells and melted its icons. He had died in the first purges, or vanished into them; she'd been fourteen, and she had learned the lesson of the age quickly and well: survive by being small, by being gray, by being exactly what they wanted. She had become a warden because wardens were never suspected. The wolf is never asked if it eats sheep.

And it had worked. It had worked for twelve years. She had taken Petra Okonkwo's neighbor away in irons and filed the paperwork without her hand shaking. She had stood in burning streets and done arithmetic with other people's lives. And then a courtier had turned to dust in a palace corridor under the king's fingers, and Ilsa had seen the crown drink, and the arithmetic had stopped balancing forever.

Ninth bell came. She went.

* * *

The Lord Inquisitor's office occupied the top floor of the Hall of Judgment, and it smelled of lilies. That was the detail everyone remembered and no one understood—fresh lilies, changed daily, in a room where men were broken. Ilsa had decided years ago that the flowers were for him, not for the room. Corvel Hask liked beautiful things dying slowly.

He was standing at the window when she entered, hands clasped behind his back, watching the city with the expression of a man reading a ledger only he could see. He did not turn around.

"Warden-Captain." His voice was soft, almost kind. "Do you know why the old faith endured as long as it did? Before the Binding?"

"No, my lord."

"Because it was *small*." He turned then, and smiled, and the smile reached his eyes the way frost reaches a window. "A grandmother whispering over a soup pot. A fisherman scratching a sign on his hull. Nothing you could arrest without arresting everyone. So we didn't try to arrest it. We starved it. We made belief expensive." He crossed to his desk, unhurried, and laid one pale hand flat on a folder that sat in the center of it. "And then, twelve quiet years—and now charms are working again. Charms are *answering*. Do you understand what that means, Captain? It means somewhere, something has started paying the price again. Belief always costs. Someone is subsidizing."

Ilsa kept her face still. Below the window, Ironhollow sprawled in the morning haze, rooftops and smokestacks and the distant glint of the river, all of it ordinary, all of it doomed or saved depending on what happened in the next six days—though only she knew about the six days, and she intended to keep it that way.

"A carver named Petra Okonkwo," Hask continued. "Her stall drew crowds before the Ash Market action. Crowds are evidence. Yet your report lists her as *deceased in the fire*. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"Mm. And yet two witnesses place a woman matching her description leaving the quarter before the cordon closed. Carrying a bundle." He opened the folder with one finger, delicately, as though it might bite. "And here is a requisition log. Three weeks ago, a cell in the interrogation block was opened between bells, and a juvenile detainee released. The duty sergeant remembers nothing. But the lock was opened with a captain's key."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past, and the lilies breathed their sweet rotting smell.

"My lord," Ilsa said, "if you believe I've committed treason, charge me."

"Oh, I don't believe anything." Hask settled behind his desk at last, folding his hands. "Belief is for priests. I deal in probabilities, and the probability that you are useful to me just improved considerably. You see, Captain, I have a problem. In six days, His Majesty performs the Rite of Continuance, and the palace will be sealed, and every soul in this city will be either inside those walls or outside them, and I must ensure that nothing—*nothing*—disturbs the ceremony. Which means I need to know where the rebels will strike. And rebels, Captain, talk to sympathetic wardens." He tilted his head. "So here is what happens next. You will go about your duties. You will be watched—you have been watched for some time, so the sensation should be familiar. And when the traitors contact you, because they will, you will bring their plans to me. Do this, and the folder stays shut. Fail, and it opens, and everything you are goes into the ground with your father's name."

He knew about her father. Of course he knew. They always knew.

"You're asking me to betray people I may not even know," Ilsa said.

"I'm asking you to do what you've always done." Hask picked up a lily from the vase beside him and began, absently, to strip its petals, letting them fall on the desk one by one. "Survive by being what they want. You were simply better at it than most. Dismissed, Captain. Ninth bell tomorrow, and every day until the solstice. Bring me something worth hearing."

Ilsa walked out of the Hall of Judgment with her spine straight and her mind already moving, fast and cold, along paths she had refused to walk for twelve years. Because Hask had made one mistake, the same mistake all hunters make eventually: he had assumed she had something left to lose.

He had given her six days. He thought he was setting a trap.

He was setting a clock.

* * *

Finding the rebels was easier than it should have been, which told her how badly they needed her—or how desperate they'd become. She went to the lamplighter first, because lamplighters saw everything and belonged to no one, and because she had once seen Tam, the disgraced priest, close a shutter against a warden patrol with a timing so perfect it could only be practice. She found him trimming wicks on Candlemas Row at dusk, and she stood in his light and said, "I need to speak to Petra Okonkwo," and watched every muscle in his face decide whether to kill her.

"She doesn't see wardens," Tam said.

"She'll see this one." Ilsa reached into her coat and set the bone charm on the windowsill between them—the one from the dead girl's fist, the spiral worn nearly smooth. "Tell her it belonged to Sefa. Tell her I carried it out of the fire when everything else burned. And tell her—" She stopped. Swallowed. Twelve years of silence, and it came out anyway, rough as gravel. "Tell her I owe her a life, and I pay my debts."

Tam looked at the charm for a long moment. Then at her. Whatever he saw in her face, it was enough.

"The Tannery Row cellar," he said. "Midnight. Come alone, and come having decided, because we won't ask twice."

* * *

The cellar beneath the tannery stank of lime and old blood, and it was full of people who wanted her dead. Ilsa counted them as she descended the stairs: eleven faces, lit by a single lamp, and not one of them friendly. Petra Okonkwo stood at the front, arms crossed, and up close she was thinner than the market rumors suggested, with burn blisters on her knuckles and eyes that had the particular flat shine of someone who had recently cried everything she had and found something harder underneath.

"Sefa's daughter," Petra said. Not a question. "You pulled her body out yourself. You signed the report that called her a rioter."

"Yes."

"And now you're here."

"Now I'm here." Ilsa set her satchel on the trestle table and opened it, and let them see what was inside: folded papers, a wax impression, a guard rotation written in her own hand. "Before you decide what I am, look at what I brought."

Petra didn't move. Tam did—he stepped forward and lifted out the topmost page, and Ilsa watched him read it, watched his lips move around the words, watched the color leave his face.

"This is the palace ritual calendar," he said quietly. "This is the *inner* calendar. No one outside the priesthood has seen this in twelve years."

"Then you'll understand it faster than I did." Ilsa kept her voice level, though her heart was going hard against her ribs. "Page three. The solstice. There's a rite marked *Continuance*—it recurs annually, but this year it's different. This year the annotations change. Older entries say 'renewal.' This year it says 'deepening.' And there's a requisition attached—candles, blood-salt, a list of names." She met Petra's eyes. "Forty names. All of them taken in the last month. All of them people who were reported for charms that worked. Your neighbors. Your customers. They're not prisoners, Okonkwo. They're *stores*."

The lamp flame bent in a draft no one had made. Around the table, the rebels had gone very still, the way animals go still.

"Six days," Tam said. His voice had dropped into something older, resonant, the voice of a man who had once led congregations. "The solstice is six days off. That's when the Binding renews. That's when the crown—" He stopped, pressed his knuckles to his mouth.

"That's when whatever the king is gets fed enough to keep going another year," Ilsa said. "I've seen it feed. In the east corridor, three weeks ago. A courtier named Master Ellery touched the king's sleeve in greeting, and by evening he was dust in his boots. They wrote it down as plague. I wrote it down as plague. I'm done writing it down."

"Why?" It was Petra who spoke, sharp as a blade drawn. "Why now? Why any of it? You wore the gray for twelve years, warden. What broke?"

Isla looked at her—at the burn scars, the stubborn jaw, the grief worn like armor—and told the truth because there was no time left for anything else.

"A child with a charm for his sick mother. I let him go and lied about it, and it was the first honest thing I'd done since I was fourteen years old. And then I saw the king eat a man, and I understood that the law I enforce isn't law. It's a feeding trough." She spread her hands flat on the table, palms up, the way her father used to when he gave the blessing—she hadn't realized she remembered that until the motion was already made. "I can't give you back Sefa's daughter. I can't give anyone back twelve years. But I can give you this: the palace seals at dusk on solstice eve. After that, nothing gets in or out except through the ritual gates, and the ritual gates answer to the warden command structure. To *me*, until Hask decides otherwise. Six days. If you mean to do something, you do it in six days, and I open the way."

Silence. Then one of the rebels in the back—an older woman with a butcher's forearms—said what they were all thinking: "It's a trap. It has to be. Hask suspects her; she said so herself. He's using her to find us."

"He is," Ilsa said. "I'm going to feed him false plans, carefully, starting tomorrow. Every piece of misinformation I give him buys you hours. But understand the cost." She looked around the room, meeting each face in turn, refusing to flinch. "When this ends—however it ends—I am finished. There is no going back to the gray for me. Either we win, or I hang. Those are my terms."

"And ours?" Petra said.

"Shelter the ones I couldn't save. The names on that requisition—if they're still alive in six days, they're in the cells beneath the chapel wing. Get them out. That's my debt. That's what I *owe*." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, and she let it crack anyway. "I took a woman in irons three weeks ago for a charm that worked. I've been trying not to think about her name since. It's Marisol. She had a daughter."

Petra stared at her for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but recognition. One survivor to another, weighing the arithmetic.

"Marisol's daughter is ten," Petra said finally. "She's been sleeping in my shop since the fire. So yes, Warden. We shelter her. We shelter all of them." She reached across the table and pulled the ritual calendar toward her, and her finger came down on the solstice entry like a hammer coming down on a nail. "Six days. Then we end it."

Tam blew out the lamp. In the sudden dark, Ilsa heard the sound of eleven people breathing, and underneath it, faint as a memory, something else—a hum in the walls, in the lime and the old stone, like the city itself drawing breath before speech.

The clock had started.
