## 16. Six Days

The smuggler's cellar had a map.

It was not much of a map—charcoal on sailcloth, the lines smudged where hands had worried at them—but Ilsa unrolled it across the barrel-head with the crisp authority of someone who had spent twelve years memorizing the city she was paid to police, and Petra felt something in her chest loosen for the first time since the ash market. Here was Ironhollow laid bare: the palace crown rising above the terraces like a fist, the processional way snaking up through the wards, the temple district sealed behind its iron gates. Every street they would have to bleed along to reach the throne room, drawn in a dead man's careful hand.

"Six days," Ilsa said. "Solstice falls on the longest night. The rite begins at dusk and runs until the king renews the Binding at midnight. That's our window."

"And if we miss it?" Tam asked.

Ilsa looked at him the way one looks at a man who has asked whether drowning is wet. "Then the Binding holds another year. Another year of the crown eating souls. Another year of wardens burning carvers' quarters." Her jaw tightened. "My report is already filed. By tomorrow noon they'll know I took the ritual calendar. They'll know what I am. There is no missing it."

Petra traced the processional way with one finger. The charcoal came off on her skin, gray as grave-dust. "How do I get in?"

"You don't sneak in," Ilsa said. "You walk in."

* * *

They argued about it for three hours.

Tam wanted tunnels—the old priest-tunnels beneath the temple district that his order had used before the Binding, half of them collapsed, all of them watched since the rebellion began. Ilsa wanted a forged writ; she still had friends in the records office who owed her favors, though each favor now cost her a piece of herself she could not get back. Petra sat on an overturned crate with her mother's charm warm against her sternum and listened to them plan her death in increasingly elaborate ways, and finally she said:

"I'm a charm-seller. The whole city knows it now. Half of Ironhollow has bought bone from my stall." She looked up. "Let me be what I am."

The cellar went quiet. Even the gutter-rats along the wall seemed to pause.

"The solstice rite is public," Petra went on, and her voice steadied as she spoke, the way it always did when her hands were busy, so she picked up a scrap of bone from the floor and turned it over while she talked. "The people line the processional way. They always have—it's the one day the law lets them look at the crown without flinching. And this year, after the fires, after everything—" She met Ilsa's eyes. "This year they'll be looking for a miracle. So give them one. A carver walks the processional way with the crowd, carrying charms to sell, the way hundreds of peddlers do every festival. Nobody stops a peddler on solstice. It's tradition."

"It's suicide," Ilsa said flatly.

"It's invisible," Petra said. "Which is the only armor I've ever had."

Tam had gone very still. She knew that stillness—he did it when he was praying, or when he was afraid, and she had learned in the last weeks that for Tam those were often the same act. "Once you're inside," he said slowly, working it through, "the gates close for the rite. You'd be locked in with the court, the wardens, and the king."

"With the god," Petra corrected softly. "The king's just the bowl it's poured into."

She felt her mother stir against her chest—not words, not yet, but a pressure, a cold spreading under her ribs like river water. *Don't.* The soul worn smooth by twelve years in a finger of bone had been begging her not to do this since the night Ilsa brought the calendar, and Petra had learned to hold that cold the way she held grief: close, and quietly, and without letting it steer her hands.

* * *

They built the plan the way Petra carved: small pieces first, fitted together, nothing ornamental.

Ilsa drew the palace interior from memory—the antechambers, the warden posts, the rotation of the guard on high holy days. "The rite itself happens in the throne room. The king sits, the crown does its work, the High Ordainer reads the Litany of Quiet. The doors are barred from within by two honor guards. Everyone else kneels with their faces down—that's protocol, heads bowed during the renewal, no one watches the king work." Her mouth twisted. "Twelve years of protocol, and no one has ever once looked up."

"So I look up," Petra said.

"So you look up." Ilsa tapped the map. "You'll have perhaps four minutes between the start of the Litany and the renewal. Four minutes to reach the dais, reach the crown, and carve."

"Carve what?" Tam asked. "An unbinding isn't a charm. It took the combined rites of nine temples to bind the gods in the first place. You're telling me one woman with a knife—"

"One woman with a knife who can hear them." Petra set the scrap of bone down. "That's the part none of you understand yet, because you've never heard it. The binding isn't stone or chain. It's a knot. A great ugly knot tied around the god of endings, and every soul in it is a strand—and strands can be cut loose. My mother taught me knots before she taught me letters. I've been cutting souls free one at a time for twelve years, too small for anyone to notice." She spread her hands. "Give me four minutes at the knot itself, and I'll show you what small work adds up to."

"But the wards," Tam said. "The palace is warded against exactly this. Old wards, temple-work, the kind that were meant to keep priests out. The moment you touch the crown with intent, they'll know. They'll feel it the way a spider feels the web shake."

"That's why you'll be outside them," Petra said, "holding them open."

Silence again. Tam looked at her, and she watched him understand what she was asking, and she watched him decide to say yes anyway, because he was Tam, because he had been saying yes to impossible things since the night the temples fell.

"The wards anchor to the old temple stones," he said at last, his voice rough. "There are seven anchor-points in the processional way itself—lampposts, cornerstones, things nobody thinks about anymore. If I stand at the first anchor when the rite begins and burn what's left of my ordination, I can hold a channel open. Not break the wards—just keep one thread of them slack, long enough for your working to pass through." He laughed, short and bitter. "It will take everything. Whatever's left of the priesthood in me, whatever the gods left behind when they were bound. I'll have nothing after. No rites, no hearing, maybe—" He stopped.

"Maybe what?" Ilsa asked.

"Maybe nothing," Tam said simply. "Priests who burn their ordination don't always survive the burning. It's in the old texts. We didn't dwell on it."

"No," Petra said.

"Petra—"

"*No.*" The word cracked out of her harder than she intended, and the cellar rang with it, and both of them stared. She pressed her palms flat against her thighs and made her voice level. "You don't get to trade yourself for me. That's not the plan. That's not ever the plan. Find another way to hold the wards."

"There isn't one," Tam said gently. "You know there isn't. I've spent six days learning everything Ilsa stole, and there isn't one. It's me or it's no one."

"Then it's no one, and we find a third thing—"

"Petra." He crossed the cellar and took her hands, and her mother's charm went cold as ice between them, and Tam flinched—he could feel it too, now, the dead woman's disapproval radiating off the bone like frost. "Six days ago you told me you'd unbind the god even if it cost your gift, your mother, and your life. All three. Your words. I'm only being asked to pay one."

"That was before," she whispered.

"Before what?"

Before the kiss in this same cellar that a whistle interrupted. Before the nights planning side by side, shoulders touching, his lamplighter's callus catching on hers. Before she had let herself imagine a city on the other side of this with him in it. She said none of it. She said, "Before I had something to lose besides myself."

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Ilsa cleared her throat with the delicacy of a landslide, and they stepped apart, and the work went on, because the work always went on.

* * *

On the fourth day, Ilsa walked the processional way at dawn, alone, counting steps.

She did it openly—a warden inspecting the route before a state occasion was nothing, less than nothing, the most boring duty in the city—and so she walked the full mile from the Ash Gate to the palace stairs with her hands clasped behind her back and her heart hammering out a traitor's rhythm. Seven anchors. She found them all. Lampposts the lamplighters' guild had tended for a century without knowing what slept in their bases. A cornerstone before the customs house. The threshold-stone of a shuttered chapel that had been a tavern for twelve years, its god ground into the foundation like salt into bread.

At each one she paused, straightened her cloak, and scratched a small mark into the grime with her thumbnail—a warden's maintenance mark, meaningless, deniable. Tam would know them. Tam had spent the last three nights learning to read her shorthand by candlelight while Petra slept badly in the next room, and the memory of that—three conspirators bent over stolen paper, passing bread and ink between them like communion—was the closest thing to hope Ilsa had felt since the night she'd taken Petra's neighbor away in irons.

She owed that neighbor a debt she could never repay. The daughter was ash now, burned in the quarter with everyone else, and Ilsa carried the girl's name written inside her cuff the way other wardens carried prayer tokens. *Maren. Nine years old.* She touched her cuff at each anchor-point, seven times, a rosary of guilt.

At the palace stairs she stopped. Above her, the crown of the palace caught the early light, and even at this distance, even through stone and season and the dulling fog of twelve years of obedience, Ilsa could feel it watching. She had stood post in that building for a decade. She had never once felt it the way she felt it now—hungry, patient, aware.

*Six days,* she thought. *Hold on, whatever you are. Hold on, your majesty. Help is coming, God forgive us, help is finally coming.*

Then she turned, and walked back down the way, and reported the route secure.

* * *

On the fifth day, Petra carved.

Not charms—there was no time, and her stall was ash anyway. She sat in the cellar by lamplight and practiced the unbinding on scraps: chicken bones, a sheep's knuckle, the rib of a dog some rebel had buried with more love than theology. Each time she closed her eyes and reached for the knot-feeling, the sense of wound thread, and each time she cut a little cleaner, a little faster, until she could loose a dead creature's remnant in under a minute. The remnants rose around her like steam and scattered toward the ceiling, and Tam watched from the doorway with tears running silently down his face, because he hadn't seen a soul freed since he was nineteen years old, and Petra pretended not to notice because if she noticed she would stop, and she could not stop.

Her mother watched too. That was the difference. Her mother watched from the charm at Petra's throat, silent now for two full days, withdrawn into the bone like a hand pulling back from a flame, and the silence was worse than the begging. Begging meant arguing. Silence meant her mother had accepted something, and Petra could not bear to learn what.

On the fifth night, unable to sleep, she finally spoke to her.

"Mama."

The cold stirred. *Little one.*

"When it's done. When the god is free and the dead go—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "Where do you go?"

A long quiet. Then, gently, the way her mother had explained death to her at six years old, kneeling by the garden: *Everywhere. Nowhere. Into the ending, where all rivers run. It isn't punishment, Petra. It's just the last door, and I have been standing in a hallway for twelve years.*

"I could keep you." The words tore coming out. "I could leave one strand uncut. Keep you in the charm. Keep you with me."

*And watch you grow old carrying a ghost in your pocket?* The cold warmed, almost laughing, almost weeping. *You survived by making me small, my love. Don't make me small forever. Cut me loose with the rest. Let me be finished. Let me be weather. Let me be done.*

Petra pressed the charm to her lips, and it was only bone, only her mother's finger-bone, smooth as a river stone, and she cried the way she had not cried since the Binding—ugly, doubled-over, alone in the dark while two hundred feet above her the city that had outlawed grief slept on.

In the morning she got up and sharpened her knife.

* * *

On the sixth day, they rehearsed.

Ilsa played the High Ordainer, droning the Litany of Quiet from memory—she had heard it a thousand times, and reciting it now made her voice go strange and hollow, like a puppet speaking. Tam played the honor guards, the courtiers, the kneeling crowd. Petra walked it: the door, the forty steps to the dais, the angle of approach that kept her below the sightline of the throne, the moment she would rise from her knees among the crowd and simply—walk forward, the way no one in twelve years had dared to walk forward, because everyone knew the king was not to be approached, because everyone had forgotten that kings were only men, or had never been allowed to remember it.

"Again," Ilsa said, for the ninth time.

"Again," Petra agreed, and walked it again, and this time when she reached the imaginary dais she raised her knife-hand and made the first stroke of the unbinding in empty air, and the cellar lamps guttered, all of them at once, though there was no wind.

They froze. Tam's eyes went wide. "Did you feel—"

"I felt it," Petra breathed. Somewhere far above them, beyond stone and law and twelve years of silence, something vast had shifted in its sleep. Turned toward her. *Noticed.*

Her mother's charm went cold as January.

"They know we're coming," Tam said quietly. "Or they will. The crown will feel her the moment she enters the wards. It may already suspect. Six days of preparation, and we've been shouting."

"Then we stop preparing loudly," Ilsa said. She rolled up the map with steady hands, though her knuckles were white. "Tomorrow there's nothing left to plan. Tomorrow we eat, we sleep, we sharpen knives, and we say the things people say the night before."

Petra looked at the map, at the smudged processional way, at the small charcoal crown at the top of it. One mile. Forty steps. Four minutes. One knot.

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, the palace bells began to toll the hour—midnight, the turning of the day, six becoming five—and beneath their bronze voices, faint as a heartbeat under a floorboard, Petra heard the bound dead of Ironhollow turn in their sleep and whisper, all together, in a thousand ruined voices:

*Soon.*
