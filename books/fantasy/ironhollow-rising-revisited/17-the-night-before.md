## 17. The Night Before

The safehouse smelled of tallow smoke and old stone, and Petra could not stop counting.

Six days had become one. She counted everything now—the candles Ilsa had left burning in their iron sconces, the stairs between the cellar and the street, the breaths Tam took across the room from her as he sharpened a knife he would never use. Counting was what she did instead of praying. Praying was dangerous; it woke things. But numbers were small and dead and obedient, and she needed something obedient tonight.

*One candle. Two knives on the table. Three vials of Ilsa's warding ink. Four days since the fire took the carver's quarter.*

She sat at the worktable with her mother's charm laid out before her on a square of clean linen, and she tried to carve.

It was no use. Her hands knew the motions—she'd been carving since she was seven years old, since before she understood what she was carving—but tonight the bone resisted her like a stranger. Every stroke came out wrong. Too deep. Too shallow. The little prayer-grooves that should have flowed like water stuttered like a stammer.

"You're holding your breath," Tam said from across the room.

"I'm concentrating."

"You're drowning." He set down the knife and crossed the cellar, and she watched him come the way she always watched him come—as if watching might make it less real, less inevitable, less like standing at the edge of a high place with the wind already pulling. "Petra. Put the bone down."

"The wards need—"

"The wards are carved. They've been carved for two days. You've recarved them four times because you can't sit still." He pulled out the chair beside her, turned it around, and straddled it backward, arms folded over the top rail, close enough that she could see the lamplight caught in the gray of his eyes. Up close, the brand on his throat—the warden's mark they'd given him when they stripped his priesthood, the scar he hid under a lamplighter's collar—looked pale as old wax. "Tomorrow you walk into the throne room and unmake a god. Tonight you're allowed to be afraid."

"I'm not afraid."

He said nothing. Just looked at her, patient as stone, the way he'd looked at her in the market all those months ago when he'd first traced the working charms back to her stall and begged, *help me, please, help me*, and she had said no and meant it and then come to find him anyway three nights later because a dead child's voice had led her to a mass grave and her mother's soul had been singing in the dark.

That was the trouble with Tam. He never argued. He just waited for the truth to arrive on its own, and it always did, because he made silence feel like a place worth telling the truth in.

"I'm not afraid of dying," she said finally. It came out smaller than she intended. "I'm afraid of failing. There's a difference. If I die in there, fine. I've been dead since the Binding anyway—I just kept moving. But if I fail—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she pressed on anyway. "If I fail, the crown keeps eating. My mother stays bound. Everyone we buried in the ash market stays bound. And you—" She stopped.

"Say it," Tam said quietly.

"Don't make me say it."

"Petra." His hand covered hers on the table, warm and calloused, a lamplighter's hands that had once been a priest's. "We promised each other plain speech tonight. No more carving our words so small nobody notices them. That was the whole point of this evening, remember? You said it yourself. *Tomorrow we may not get another chance to be honest.*"

She looked down at their hands. His thumb moved once across her knuckles, deliberate, like a man tracing a line he'd already memorized.

"I love you," she said.

There. It was out. Twelve years of smallness, twelve years of keeping every feeling carved down to the size of a thumbnail so no warden would ever hear it, and it came out of her in two words like water through a broken dam. She wanted to laugh or cry or both, and instead she just sat there, trembling slightly, watching his face.

Tam closed his eyes. When he opened them again they were wet, and he didn't try to hide it, and somehow that undid her more than anything.

"I know," he said. "I've known since the smuggler's cellar, since you bargained with me like a merchant and kissed me like a prayer and then ran when the whistle blew. I've known since you came to me with your mother's soul in your fist and asked me to help you do the cruelest kind thing anyone has ever done." He turned her hand over, palm up, and pressed his lips to the center of it—the carver's callus, the ridge of scar where she'd cut herself the night of the Binding, twelve years ago, hiding her mother's remnant inside a charm too small to notice. "I love you too. I loved you when loving you was the most dangerous thing I could do, and I love you now that it's only fatal."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No." He smiled, and it was the crooked smile, the real one, the one he never wore for the underground or the lamplighting rounds or anyone but her. "It's supposed to be true. We're past comfort. Tomorrow is for courage. Tonight is for truth."

Outside, Ironhollow lay quiet under a sky bruised purple with the last light of the shortest day's eve. Somewhere up on the palace mount, King Aldric—or the thing wearing Aldric, the hollow vessel crowned with the god of endings' hunger—was preparing for the solstice rite. Somewhere in the cells beneath the warden barracks, Ilsa was memorizing the ritual calendar one final time. Somewhere in the mass graves beyond the city wall, ten thousand bound souls were waiting without knowing they were waiting.

And here, in a cellar that smelled of tallow and stone, two people who remembered the same lost world sat with their hands folded together, counting the hours.

* * *

They ate supper like it mattered, because it did.

Tam had traded half his winter coat for a chicken from the widow Marat two streets over, and he roasted it over the cellar's little brazier with rosemary he'd pinched from the temple garden—the old temple, the sealed one, where tomorrow night he would stand and burn whatever remained of his priesthood's power to hold the wards open while Petra carved. The meat was tough and the bread was stale and Petra thought she had never tasted anything so good in her life.

"This is what I remember," she said, around a mouthful. "Before. Meals like this. My mother used to make pepper stew on festival nights, and the whole building would smell it, and everyone would end up in our kitchen because my mother believed a table wasn't full until there was someone at it who hadn't been invited."

"She sounds like Tam-the-elder," Tam said, using the name the underground called him, mock-solemn. "I once fed forty people off two goats and a stolen cart of yams. Father Anselm said it was a miracle. I said it was arithmetic."

"What happened to Anselm?"

The question slipped out before she could catch it, and she saw it land—saw the flicker behind his eyes, the place where the grief lived that he carried the way she carried hers: folded small, worn smooth, always touched and never opened.

"He was taken in the second year," Tam said. "After the Binding. They burned the temples and they took the priests, and most of them went to the pits outside the walls." He turned the chicken leg over the coals. "I survived because I was twenty-two and good at being invisible. Same as you."

"Same as everyone left in this city," Petra said. "We're all just the ones who were good at it."

"And tomorrow we stop being good at it." He set the food aside and looked at her, and the lamplight made his face into something older than his years—a priest's face, a man who had spent twelve years lighting lamps against a dark he wasn't allowed to name. "Petra. If it goes wrong tomorrow—if the crown takes you, if the wards fail, if Ilsa doesn't open the gates—there's something I need you to carry out of that throne room, whether it's your body walking or just your soul flying loose. Can I give you that?"

Her chest went tight. "You can give me anything. That's the problem."

"Then take this." He reached up and unclasped the thin iron chain at his neck, the one she'd seen a thousand times and never asked about, and in his palm lay a small pendant—a lamp, worked in pewter, its flame a sliver of polished amber. "My mother's. She was a lamplighter before she was anything else. She lit the shrine lamps on the Street of Ash before the Binding, and when they outlawed the gods she kept lighting them anyway, one by one, until they took her too." He held it out. "I've carried it for twelve years thinking it was all I had left of the world before. But it isn't. The world before is sitting right in front of me eating my chicken. So I want it to go with you tomorrow. Not as a talisman—we've had enough of magic to last several lifetimes—but as a promise. Whatever happens in that throne room, someone will relight the lamps after. Someone will rebuild. It won't be ash forever."

Petra took the pendant. It was warm from his skin, and lighter than any of her charms, and heavier than anything she'd ever carried.

"You realize," she said, when she could speak, "that if you keep giving me things, I'll have to survive just to give them back."

"That," said Tam, "is exactly the plan."

* * *

Later, when the candles had burned low and the brazier had gone to embers, her mother spoke.

Petra felt it before she heard it—that faint warmth at her throat where the charm hung on its cord, the smooth worn bit of bone that held twelve years of her mother's soul, rubbed featureless by time and touch. Then the whisper came, soft as breath on a winter window.

*Little star.*

"Mama." Petra's eyes stung. Even now, even after everything—even after the grave, even after the begging, even after the terrible argument in the dark of the ash market's ruins where her mother had pleaded with her not to unbind the god, not to dissolve the last of her—*even now*, the sound of that voice went through her like a blade going through bread. "I'm here."

*I know where you are. I'm around your neck.* A pause, and then, dry as only her mother could be dry: *Though I still maintain this is an undignified place for a woman of my talents.*

Petra laughed, wet and sudden. Across the room, Tam looked up from the wards he was checking, and she shook her head—*it's all right*—and he nodded and turned away to give her privacy, because he was Tam, because he understood that some conversations belonged to the dead and their daughters alone.

"I'm scared," Petra whispered. "Not of dying. Of doing it wrong. Mama, if I carve the unbinding wrong—"

*You won't.*

"You don't know that."

*I know my daughter.* The voice grew softer, closer, the way it did when her mother gathered herself fully into the charm, all that remained of her pressing near. *You were seven years old when you first put knife to bone, and even then you carved true. When the wardens came and the screaming started and I told you to run, you didn't run empty-handed—you took me with you. Twelve years, little star. Twelve years you kept me safe when every law in this city said I should be dust. Do you think a woman like that raises a daughter who carves wrong?*

"But tomorrow—" Petra's voice broke. "Tomorrow I lose you. That's the price. The god goes free and the dead scatter and you—"

*Yes.*

"How can you bless that? How can you sit there, in my charm, and tell me it's all right?"

The silence stretched long enough that Petra thought her mother had withdrawn. Then:

*Because I have been a prisoner for twelve years, and I remember what freedom cost, and I would pay it again.* Each word came slow and clear, like stones laid one upon another. *Because I watched you grow up in hiding, small and silent and sharp as a knife's edge, and I swore that if I ever got the chance, I would buy you a better world even if the coin was me. Because your father is out there somewhere in the bound dark, and your grandmother, and the Marats' boy who drowned, and ten thousand others, and I will not spend eternity in a king's belly while my daughter blames herself for sparing me.* A pause, gentler now. *And because of the lamplighter.*

Petra's breath caught. "Mama—"

*I have been hanging at your heart for twelve years, child. You think I haven't noticed how your pulse changes when he walks into a room?* The whisper warmed, almost laughing. *Bring him here. I have something to say to him, and I am running out of tomorrows to say it in.*

So Petra called him over, and Tam came and knelt beside her chair, awkward as a boy, and she held the charm out between them so her mother's voice could reach them both.

*Tam of the temple,* her mother said.

"Lady Okonkwo." His voice was rough. "Ma'am."

*Twelve years ago, men like you held the doors of the temples open so people like me could run. I never learned your name. I never thanked you. So I'm thanking you now, late, the way mothers do everything.* A breath. *Tomorrow you hold the doors again. And I want you to hear this from me, because my daughter will never say it plainly enough even for the gods: she loves you. She has loved you since the cellar. She loves you the way I loved her father—stubbornly, secretly, and past all sense—and if either of you survives tomorrow, you had better not waste a single day of it being careful. Do you hear me?*

"Yes, ma'am," Tam said, and his eyes were shining, and he was smiling through it. "Loud and clear."

*Good.* And then her mother's voice turned back to Petra alone, and it was soft as the last ember of a fire, warm as a kitchen on a festival night. *Little star. Come here. Let me look at you—one last time, properly, not through bone.*

Petra lifted the charm in both hands and held it before her face, and she poured everything she had into looking—every year, every hidden morning, every carved night, the whole shape of the woman who had taught her that a table wasn't full until someone uninvited sat at it.

*There you are,* her mother whispered. *Oh, there you are. You grew up beautiful. You grew up brave. You grew up mine, all the way through, and nothing they did, nothing the crown does tomorrow, can ever unmake that. Freedom for me means goodbye. But listen to me, Petra Okonkwo: goodbye is not the opposite of love. Goodbye is what love says when it has finished its work and done it well.* The voice began to thin, fraying at the edges like light at dawn. *Now kiss your lamplighter. Sleep if you can. And tomorrow, when you stand in front of that hungry crown—carve true.*

"Mama—"

*Carve true, little star. I'll be listening.*

The warmth faded to embers. The charm hung quiet, not gone—never gone until tomorrow—but resting, the way a singer rests between songs.

Petra stood there a long moment with the bone in her hands. Then Tam rose and took her gently by the shoulders, and turned her toward him, and she went into his arms like a door finally opening after twelve years locked.

* * *

They did not sleep, not really. They lay together on the narrow cot in the dark of the cellar, wrapped in one blanket against the cold, and they talked in the low voices people use in hospitals and shrines, and they memorized each other the way scholars memorize books about to be burned.

He told her about his mother, the lamplighter, and the way she'd whistled while she worked, off-key on purpose because she said the gods liked humility. She told him about her father, whom she barely remembered except that he'd smelled of cedar shavings and had once balanced a whole orange on his nose to make her stop crying. He told her he'd been afraid every single day for twelve years and had gotten so used to it that fear felt like a coat he'd forget he was wearing. She told him she'd stopped dreaming after the Binding and had dreamed three times since meeting him, all of them about water.

Somewhere in the smallest hours, she said, "If I live, I want to carve again. Plain things. Buttons and combs and toys for children. No prayers. Just—things that make life softer."

"If I live," Tam said into her hair, "I want to reopen the temple. Not as a temple. As a school. Teach children to read, to count, to cook. Let the gods come back whenever they're ready. We'll leave a lamp lit for them."

"And if neither of us lives?"

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Then Ilsa burns us decently, and the underground tells our story badly, with all the details wrong, and in fifty years there'll be a festival about it where everyone eats too much and nobody remembers why." She felt him smile against her hair. "Honestly? That sounds nice."

"It sounds terrible."

"It sounds alive," he said. "That's the whole point of tomorrow, Petra. Not glory. Not even freedom, not really. Just a city where people get to be boringly, stupidly alive. Where a woman can sell a charm that doesn't work and a man can light a lamp for no god at all and nobody comes for either of them." His arm tightened around her. "That's what we're buying. That's worth any price the crown asks."

She turned in his arms and found his face in the dark, and kissed him—slowly, thoroughly, with none of the desperation of the smuggler's cellar interrupted by whistles, with all the time in the world and none of it, both at once. When they broke apart, her forehead rested against his, and their breath mingled in the cold air, and above them, through the grate to the street, the first gray hint of solstice dawn was seeping into the sky.

"One more hour," Tam murmured.

"We don't have an hour."

"We have this one." He pulled her closer. "This one's ours. I checked the calendar myself."

And so they took it—the last ordinary hour of the old world, spent in a cellar, in the dark, in each other's arms, while overhead the sun crawled toward the shortest day of the year and the palace on the mount began, very quietly, to wake.

At dawn, a knock came at the door. Three short, one long—Ilsa's signal.

Petra rose, and dressed, and hung her mother's charm around her neck, and tucked Tam's pewter lamp into her shirt over her heart. On the worktable she left the bone she'd failed to carve the night before, its unfinished grooves catching the new light.

She picked up her knife instead—the good one, the true one, the one her mother had given her at seven years old—and followed Tam up the stairs into the cold.

The solstice had begun.
