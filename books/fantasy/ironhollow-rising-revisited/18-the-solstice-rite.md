## 18. The Solstice Rite

The palace at midnight was a throat, and we were walking down it.

Ilsa had gotten us through the servants' gate with a forged writ and a dead man's seal—she'd said the seal belonged to a warden who'd asked too many questions and been reassigned to the coast, which meant he hadn't been reassigned at all. Tam walked beside me in his lamplighter's coat, his priest's robes folded beneath it like a second skin, like a wound. Ilsa went ahead, her warden's stride eating the marble, and no one stopped us because no one stops what looks inevitable.

The throne room doors were taller than my shopfront. Taller than my whole street. They were carved with the god of endings' sigil—a circle with its bottom cut away, an ending that never quite finished—and twelve years of polish had worn the grooves shallow, as if the palace itself wanted to forget what it was built around.

"You feel that?" Tam murmured.

I did. It came up through the soles of my boots like water through stone: a hum, vast and slow, the heartbeat of something enormous sleeping badly. Every bone charm I'd ever carved had whispered when it woke. This was not a whisper. This was a choir held underwater.

"The crown," I said. "It's already feeding."

Ilsa checked the guards' rotation against the ritual calendar one last time—the solstice rite began at moonset, and moonset was two hours off, and the throne room would empty for the priests' preparation, leaving only the king and his hollow honor guard. "Six minutes once they clear," she said. "Not five. Not seven. Six." She looked at me then, and behind all that iron discipline there was something raw. "Petra Okonkwo. I took your neighbor. I don't ask you to forgive that tonight. I ask you to remember it if you're tempted to die in there. Dying would be the easy thing, and easy things are how this city got here."

"I'm not planning to die."

"Nobody plans it. That's what makes it so popular." She turned back to the corridor. "Six minutes."

* * *

We waited in an alcove thick with the smell of tallow and old incense, and I did the thing I had done every night of my life since the Binding: I listened for my mother.

She was there. Of course she was there—she was everywhere now, or rather *I* was everywhere she was, because over these six days I had stopped wearing her charm and started wearing her soul against my sternum, tucked in a locket of ironwood that Ilsa's smugglers swore could pass any search. Twelve years in a sliver of bone had worn her smooth. She was less voice than weather now, less words than pressure, but she pressed against me the way she used to press her palm to my forehead when I had fever: *here, here, here.*

*You don't have to do this,* she said, in the wordless way she said everything now. *There are other carvers. There will be other chances.*

"There won't," I breathed. "And there aren't."

Tam's hand found mine in the dark. His fingers were cold; his hands were always cold, lamplighter's hands, wax-burned and wind-chapped, and I had learned their geography by heart over six days of planning that was mostly touching hands across maps. "Say it again," he said quietly. "The order of it. Say it until it's boring."

"Boring," I said, and he almost smiled. "I reach the dais during the offering. The king kneels. The crown drinks from him—that's when it's busiest, when it's looking down instead of out. I carve the unbinding into the crown's own rim, where the binding-sigils run. Circle closed becomes circle opened. One stroke wrong and—" I swallowed. "One stroke wrong and it takes me instead. Or takes everyone. Or nothing happens and we hang tomorrow."

"And your part?"

"I hold the wards open from the gallery. When the souls come loose—and they'll come loose all at once, twelve years of them—they'll want somewhere to go. The wards keep the panic contained long enough for them to find the door." He recited it like liturgy, because it was. "Then I burn what's left of my priesthood to keep them open. All of it. Everything the temple hid in me when I was ordained."

"That's the part I hate."

"I know." His thumb moved over my knuckles. "I've watched you hate it for six days. You're very good at it."

"Tam—"

"Don't make promises you can't keep and I won't either. That was our bargain." But his voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough, and in the dark of the alcove he leaned in and touched his forehead to mine, and for three heartbeats we were not a carver and a priest with a suicide's errand. We were just two people who remembered the same lost world, holding onto each other before the world tried to take the rest.

From down the corridor: the shuffling retreat of the honor guard, the low voices of priests withdrawing to prepare. Ilsa appeared at the alcove mouth, silhouetted against the distant glow of the throne room.

"Clear," she said. "Go."

* * *

The throne room was worse than I had imagined, and I had imagined it every night for six days.

It was built to make you small. The ceiling vanished upward into painted dark—clouds, I thought, or smoke, or the memory of sky from before the Binding, when gods still walked openly and the heavens weren't a crime scene. Banners hung dead-still in air that didn't move. And at the far end, on a dais of black stone veined with something that caught the light like oil on water, stood the throne, and above the throne—

The crown.

It floated. That was the first obscenity. No head bore it yet; it hung a foot above the throne's seat, turning slowly, and it was made of a metal I had no name for, dark and bright at once, and along its rim ran sigils that hurt to look at directly—not because they were beautiful but because they were *hungry*. I had spent my life carving small things, things too minor to notice, and I knew hunger in metal when I saw it. This crown had been eating for twelve years.

And below it, kneeling in the posture of prayer, was King Aldric.

He was not what I expected. I had pictured a monster, or a husk, or something crowned and grinning. Instead he was—ordinary. A man of perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, shoulders bowed under a ceremonial robe worth more than my street. He knelt with his hands open on his knees and his head slightly bowed, and if you didn't know, you'd think him devout.

But I knew. I heard the dead for a living, or I had, and even muffled inside the locket I could hear what poured through that man like water through a broken pipe. Souls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Bound into the crown's circuit, cycled through the king's body and back again, each pass wearing him thinner, each pass feeding the crown. He wasn't a tyrant. He was a *cup*, and the cup was cracked, and the crown kept pouring anyway.

"Oh," Tam whispered beside me. His face had gone the color of candle wax. "Oh, it's worse than the rites showed me. He's still in there. Petra—he's still *in there*, underneath. He's been aware the whole time."

That was the moment I understood why Ilsa had said what she said about dying being easy. Because killing this man would be mercy, and freeing him was going to be agony, and the crown did not care which we intended. To the crown we were both just more to drink.

"Moonset's close," I managed. "Get to the gallery."

Tam gripped my shoulder once—hard, deliberate, a promise shaped like pain—and slipped away up the side stair toward the wardens' gallery, where the old temple wards lay dormant in the stonework, waiting for a priest with nothing left to lose.

I walked toward the throne alone.

My legs carried me the way they'd carried me through every raid, every market day, every year of careful invisibility: steadily, without permission. The hum grew as I approached. It got into my teeth. My mother's soul pressed hard against my sternum—not *stop*, not anymore. She had given me her blessing last night, in the dark, in the wordless way she gave everything: *here, here, here.* Go. Do it. And whatever comes after, I am with you.

The dais steps were nine. I counted them because counting was what my hands knew how to do when my mind wanted to scream.

At the top, ten feet from the kneeling king, I drew my knife and my carving burin and knelt as if in worship, as a penitent might, close enough to see the pores of his skin, the fine tremor in his open hands. Up close his eyes were open too, fixed on nothing, and they were the worst thing in the room: brown, human, wet, and utterly vacant, like windows into a house where every lamp has been carried out one by one.

"I'm sorry," I told him. I don't know if he heard. I don't know if anyone in twelve years had spoken to him as a person. "This is going to hurt. Then it's going to stop."

Moonset came.

I felt it before I saw it—a shift in the room's pressure, a deepening of the hum, the crown turning faster above the throne. The Binding renewed itself at moonset; Ilsa's stolen calendar had been precise about that much. The crown descended. It settled onto the king's head with a sound like a kiss, tender and obscene, and the sigils along its rim flared, and the king's body arched, and the drinking began.

Now.

I set my burin against the crown's rim while it fed—while its attention was turned inward, down the channel of the king's spine, gulping at the bound souls cycling through him. The metal was warm. Warm as skin. It should have been cold; crowns are cold; this one had been sitting on a living furnace for twelve years and it had learned temperature the way a leech learns blood.

I began to carve.

The unbinding was the hardest work of my life, and I had once carved my mother's soul into a sliver of femur while soldiers kicked in our door. The binding-sigils fought me. Not passively—*actively*. As my blade traced the opening stroke, the groove ahead of me sealed itself, healed like flesh, and I had to cut it again, deeper, and the crown *noticed*.

The hum became a roar.

Every soul in the circuit felt me. I knew it the way you know a hand on the back of your neck. Twelve years of the bound dead—thousands of them, the ones taken in the raids, the ones worked to death building the palace, the ones who had simply prayed at the wrong hour—all of them turned their attention toward the point of my burin, and their attention came through the metal and into my hand and up my arm like a flood through a burst dam.

I heard them all at once.

That was the part no plan survived. We had planned for noise, for resistance, for the crown's counter-carving. We had not planned for *this*: every bound soul speaking through my bones simultaneously, grief and rage and pleading layered so thick it stopped being language and became pure weight. A child crying for a mother who'd been taken the same week. An old man reciting a name over and over—his own, maybe, making sure someone remembered it. A woman singing half a lullaby, the rest forgotten. My mother's soul in the locket screamed with them, or for them, I couldn't tell, couldn't separate her from the flood, and terror rose in me like black water: I was losing myself, I was becoming a conduit instead of a person, I was—

*Carve,* said a voice that might have been hers, might have been mine. *Carve, Petra.*

I carved.

Second stroke. Third. The crown fought back in earnest now—the metal heated under my fingers, hot enough to blister, and the sigils flared white and began to reach. I felt them reaching, tendrils of attention sliding toward my mind, offering things. They offered rest. They offered my mother whole, unbound, alive in some invented morning. They offered an end to fear, an end to guilt, twelve years of survival-guilt dissolved in one sweet swallow of surrender. *Stop carving. Open your hands. Be drunk instead of thirsty.*

"No," I said aloud, through gritted teeth, and cut the fourth stroke.

Somewhere above me, Tam's voice rose—chanted words in the old tongue, the liturgy of the open door—and light bloomed along the gallery rail as the dormant wards woke. I felt them snap into place like ribs closing around the room, a cage of sanctified intent, and the flood of souls slammed against them and *held*. For now. The roar became a trapped thunder, circling, seeking gaps.

The king screamed.

It was the first sound he'd made, and it was terrible—not the scream of a monster, not the scream of a victim even, but the scream of a man whose body is being used as a road by ten thousand fleeing travelers. The crown blazed. Blood ran freely from his nose, his ears, his eyes, and still the sigils drank, still the crown refused to let its harvest scatter, and I understood with sick clarity that it would burn the vessel to ash before it released a single soul.

Fifth stroke. Sixth. My hands were burned and shaking. The flood inside my skull had names now—I knew them, all of them, I would carry them forever—and among them, suddenly, close, *her*:

*Petra. The seventh stroke goes against the grain. Against the grain, love. Trust the old hands.*

My mother. Not from the locket—from the circuit. From inside the crown. She had been in the Binding's machinery all along, these six days, riding the current, learning its shape, and now she guided my blade the way she'd once guided my first knife through scrap bone in a kitchen full of lamplight.

Against the grain. Seventh stroke.

The crown shrieked—a real sound, metal in agony, a note that cracked the banners' rods and made dust fall from the painted sky overhead. The eighth stroke. The ninth. The circle that never finished was finishing now, closing not into a binding but into a *release*, and the sigils along the rim guttered like lamps starved of oil.

In the gallery, Tam's chant faltered.

I glanced up—one heartbeat of divided attention, the kind of mistake that kills—and saw him on his knees at the ward-rail, both hands pressed flat to the stone, light pouring off him like heat off a forge. Burning his priesthood. Keeping his promise. But the wards were flickering, and the flood of souls was pressing harder, and through the roar I heard him cry out—not liturgy anymore, just a man in pain, holding a door open with his body.

"Hold!" I shouted, uselessly, and bent back to the final strokes.

Tenth. Eleventh.

The crown's counterattack turned desperate. It stopped offering and started taking—it reached down my arms into my chest and found my gift, the hearing, the thing that made me *me*, and began to pull, and I felt it pulling, felt my whole life draining toward that hungry rim—

Twelfth stroke.

Thirteenth.

One left. One stroke between twelve years of silence and whatever came after, and the crown was burning my hands to the bone, and the king had stopped screaming, which was worse than screaming, and the wards above were failing, flickering, Tam's voice gone ragged and thin—

I set the burin against the final groove.

The crown spoke. Not in the flood-voice of the souls, not in the seductive murmur of before, but in a single vast pressure that was almost words: *I AM THE ENDING. YOU CANNOT CARVE AN ENDING INTO ITSELF.*

"You can," I said. My voice sounded very small and very certain. "You just have to mean it."

And I thought of my mother's palm on my forehead. Of my neighbor dragged away for a charm that worked. Of the child in the mass grave who had led me here. Of Tam's forehead against mine in the dark. Of a city of the living, waiting.

I carved the last stroke.

The crown broke.

Light—white, total, silent—swallowed the throne room, and every soul in Ironhollow came loose at once, and the wards above shattered like ice, and the last thing I heard before the flood took me under was Tam screaming my name, and my mother's voice, everywhere, nowhere, *here, here, here*—

Then darkness, and falling, and the beginning of the end.
