## 19. Unbinding

The wards were singing.

Tam had not heard them sing in twelve years—not since the night the Binding went up across Ironhollow like a lid closing on a pot, and every priest who still had a voice screamed into it and was silenced. Now the wards sang because they were breaking, and breaking things always made their truest sound.

He stood in the shadow of the throne room's western arch with his palms pressed flat against the stone, feeding himself into the old lattice line by line, thread by thread, the way his teachers had once fed themselves into the great rites of harvest and mourning. The stone drank him greedily. He could feel where he ended and the ward began growing less certain by the minute, his edges dissolving into geometry, and he welcomed it, because a man who was half ward could hold longer than a man who was only a man.

Across the hall, Petra knelt before the dais with her knife against the crown.

King Aldric sat upon the throne as he had sat through every solstice of Tam's hidden life—upright, serene, hands open on the armrests like a host welcoming guests to a table they did not know they were dying at. The crown sat on his head the way a leech sits on flesh. It was gold, but it moved. Tam had seen it clearly now, from this close, with the wards thin enough to let the truth through: it breathed. Slowly. Patiently. The way a thing breathes that has been eating for a very long time and expects to eat for a very long more.

And behind the king's eyes—nothing. Not emptiness, which would have been mercy. A *crowding*. Souls packed shoulder to shoulder in the small dark house of a man's skull, thousands of them, pressed there by the crown's appetite, and among them, faint as a lamp behind fogged glass, one light that flickered like it was trying to remember how to be a person.

Aldric. What was left of Aldric.

"Hold," Tam whispered, though no one needed telling. "Petra. Hold."

"I'm holding." Her voice came out ground flat, like stone dragged over stone. She was carving. He could hear the knife's small song under the wards' groaning—the same sound she'd made in the smuggler's cellar when she showed him the unbinding sigil scratched into a sheep's knucklebone, laughing at herself for shaking. She wasn't laughing now. The sigil she cut into the air above the crown, guided by bone charms floating in a slow orbit around her hands, was the largest working Tam had ever seen a single mortal attempt, and it was beautiful the way a flood is beautiful.

The crown noticed her.

It happened all at once. The breathing stopped. Every torch in the throne room guttered sideways, flames bending toward the dais as if pulled by an inhale, and the king's head turned—not the way a man turns his head, but the way a door swings—and the eyes fixed on Petra with the full weight of everything the crown had ever swallowed.

"*Little carver.*"

The voice did not come from the king's mouth. It came from everywhere—from the walls, from the floor, from inside Tam's own teeth. It was the voice of the god of endings, or what the god of endings had become: a vastness compressed into a crown until vastness had curdled into hunger.

"You bring a knife to a feast," said the crown. "How fitting. We have missed knives. We have missed *hands*."

"Petra—" Tam pushed more of himself into the wards. He felt his heartbeat stutter, felt the cold climb his arms. "Don't listen. Keep cutting."

"I am not talking to you, lamplighter." The crown's attention slid across the hall and found him, and it was like being found by winter. "Ah. A priest. They left one of you alive after all. Sloppy of us." Something like amusement moved through the vastness, and it was worse than anger. "Come closer, priest. We remember your order. We ate your high father's voice on the first night. He sang so beautifully while we took it."

Tam held. He thought of Ilsa at the gates with her stolen keys and her false reports, buying them minutes with her career and probably her life. He thought of the neighbor's daughter dead in the ash market, of the mass grave under the tannery hill where Petra had first heard her mother, of every name in the underground's little book of the taken. He put all of it into the lattice like coal into a furnace.

The wards flared blue-white along the walls, and the crown recoiled a fraction—an inch of gold lifting off the king's brow—and in that inch, Petra cut deeper.

* * *

Time went strange after that. Tam learned later that the whole unbinding took less than a quarter hour; inside it, it lasted years.

The crown fought the way a drowning man fights—without dignity, without strategy, with everything. It reached into the wards and pulled, and Tam felt his own memories being read like pages: his mother's kitchen, the smell of incense in the novitiate, the first time he'd seen Petra across a market crowd and known her instantly as another survivor of the same drowned world. The crown pried at these, looking for hinges, looking for fear to widen into surrender.

It found plenty. Tam was afraid. He was afraid down to the marrow, and he let the fear be fuel rather than a door.

"You cannot hold," the crown told him, reasonable now, almost gentle. "You are one man. You are *less* than one man—you are giving yourself away thread by thread, priest, we can taste you leaking through the lattice. When you fail, we will take her gift and wear it. We will take her bones and add them to our collection. And you will watch, because we will keep just enough of you watching."

"Then I'll fail slowly," Tam said.

His lips were barely moving. Most of him was in the walls now. He could feel the whole palace's wardwork as if it were his own circulatory system—the solstice rite-channels running under the flagstones, fat with twelve years of harvested souls, all of them flowing toward the dais, toward the crown, a river of the dead pouring uphill into a mouth. That was the Binding's true shape. Not a prison. A *funnel*. The bound gods had never been locked away; they had been farmed.

And the god of endings—the oldest of them, the one whose office was to close doors gently, to sit with the dying, to make an ending an ending instead of a theft—that god had been strapped to the funnel's mouth and made to drink forever, and a god forced to swallow endings without end becomes only appetite. Tam understood it fully now, standing in the lattice, feeling the wrongness of it in his own dissolving nerves. They hadn't imprisoned a monster. They had *manufactured* one, and crowned it, and called the crowning peace.

"Petra," he said. "It knows what it is. Tell it—it can stop. Make sure it knows it can *stop*."

She heard him. Of course she heard him; she was deaf to everything else, sunk in the carving, sweat sheeting down her face, blood on her fingers where the knife had slipped twice without her noticing. But something in her posture changed. She lifted her head, and she looked at the crown—not at the king, at the crown—and she spoke to it in the voice she used for the grieving mothers in the market, low and level and unbearably kind.

"I know what they did to you," she said. "I know you didn't choose this. I'm not here to kill you. I'm here to open the door."

The vastness went still.

For one suspended moment the whole throne room hung balanced, torches upright, wards humming, the king's chest rising and falling with a human rhythm that seemed almost surprised to be its own.

Then the crown screamed.

Not with the god's voice—with ten thousand voices at once, the harvested dead crying out all together as the unbinding sigil finally closed around the crown's base and began, link by link, to unmake the Binding's oldest knot. The sound hit the wards like a wave hitting a seawall. Tam braced, gave the lattice everything he had left, felt himself go gray at the edges—

—and the wall of souls broke *through*.

They poured past him. He felt them as cold and as pressure and as grief, an ocean of the taken flooding through the failing wards, and each one touched him in passing—a hand on the shoulder, a whisper, a name he almost caught—and none of them stayed, because they were free, they were *free*, they were scattering upward and outward through the shattered ceiling of the Binding like sparks from a bonfire kicked apart by wind.

But the crown held on to what it held closest.

The king convulsed on the throne. His back arched. His mouth opened and the scream came out of him now, human-voiced, ragged, and Tam saw the truth with the last clarity the lattice would give him: the crown was losing its harvest and it knew it, and it was doing what any starving thing does at the end. It clamped down on the vessel beneath it and began to drink *deep*—not sipping anymore, not farming, but devouring, pulling the last soul in the room toward its own ruin.

It was pulling Aldric apart. And when Aldric was gone it would pull Petra, and then Tam, and then it would tear a hole in the world wide enough to crawl out of, because a god of endings denied every ending will make its own.

"No," Tam said.

He stopped holding the wards.

No—he did something worse. He *inverted* them. Twelve years of hoarded priesthood, every scrap of consecrated power his order had smuggled and buried and sewn into his lamplighter's coat hem, every prayer he had ever whispered into the dark and never had answered—he reversed the flow. Instead of holding the walls against the flood, he opened himself as a channel, a second door beside Petra's, and he poured his entire remaining self into the crown's grip on the king, prying at it the way Petra's knife pried at the sigil.

The crown noticed immediately. Of course it did. It laughed, and the laugh shook dust from the rafters.

"Priest. You are offering us *yourself*?"

"I'm offering you a choice," Tam said. His voice sounded far away to him, coming from a mouth he was mostly no longer using. "That's the one thing you haven't been given in twelve years. So here are your terms, god of endings. You can devour this man—this hollow, broken, half-eaten man who never asked to be your cup—and stay what they made you. A hunger wearing a crown. Or—"

"Or?" The vastness leaned in. Even starving, even screaming, it wanted to hear terms. That was the saddest thing Tam had ever learned about gods: they could be bargained with, because they remembered being bound by bargain.

"Or you let him go," Tam said. "All of him. Whatever's left of Aldric, you release it whole, and you take the ending you were always owed. A real one. Clean. Your own."

"And my harvest?"

"Gone. Scattered. Free." Tam felt his heart laboring now, felt the cold reach his center. "You don't get them back. No one gets anything back. That's what an ending *is*. You of all of them should remember."

Silence. The longest silence of Tam's life, stretched across a throne room full of drifting sparks and one dying king and one kneeling woman with a bloody knife, all of them waiting on the will of a creature that had forgotten it had a will.

Then Petra moved.

She rose from her knees—slowly, like a woman surfacing from deep water—and she climbed the three steps of the dais, and she stood beside the throne with her knife still in her hand and her mother's charm glinting at her throat, and she looked into the crown's attention with no fear left in her at all, which frightened Tam more than anything the crown had done.

"There's a third term," Petra said.

"Petra, no—" But his voice had almost nothing left. It came out as smoke.

"The god needs a price to let go cleanly. An ending has to cost, or it isn't an ending." Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "So here it is. My gift. The hearing—the thing that lets me touch the dead, carve them, carry them. Take it. Take it as the price, and let the king go human, and go free yourself, and never feed again."

The crown's breathing resumed. Slow. Considering.

"That gift is your mother's last road home," the god said, and its voice had changed—smaller now, wearier, almost the voice of the office it had once held. "Give it, and when she scatters, you will not hear her goodbye. You will be deaf to her forever. Is that the price you name?"

Tam watched Petra's hand rise to the charm at her throat. Watched her close her fingers around it. Watched her mouth form the word—

"Yes."

* * *

The crown let go.

Tam felt it happen through every nerve he had left in the lattice: the great hunger opening its grip all at once, twelve years of enforced drinking ending in a single exhalation, and King Aldric's soul—what remained of him, a thin gray flame, a man-shaped candle-end—sliding free of the vessel and standing, for one impossible moment, visible above his own body like a reflection in still water.

He looked around the throne room. He looked at Tam. His expression was not gratitude exactly, and not terror. It was the look of a man waking from a long illness into a room full of strangers, trying to work out whether he had been rescued or captured.

"It's over," Tam told him, or tried to. His voice failed. He got the shape of the words out anyway, and perhaps the dead man read his lips, or perhaps the freed god carried the message, because Aldric nodded—once, slowly, like a man accepting a sentence he had long expected—and then the flame bent toward the ceiling, toward the scattering lights of all the others, and followed them out.

The body on the throne slumped. Just a body now. Old, worn, lighter than it had ever been in life. The crown rolled from its brow and rang against the marble steps, and lay still, and was only gold.

The god of endings rose out of it like heat-shimmer off summer stone—vast, dim, formless, and for the first time in twelve years, *unfed and unfed-upon*, simply present. Tam felt it pause beside him. Felt something ancient and exhausted regard what was left of a priest who had spent everything.

"You held," it said. Almost wonderingly. "Small creature. You held longer than the wards deserved."

"So did she," Tam managed.

"Yes." The presence turned toward Petra, who stood swaying on the dais with her hand still closed around her mother's charm, her face already changing—Tam could see it, the way a room changes when a lamp goes out—as the price began to come due. "Now comes the part," the god said quietly, "that even I do not govern. The paying."

It began to gather itself, to rise, to scatter upward after the others—and the last thing Tam felt of it, through the ruins of the lattice, through the gray static swallowing his senses, was not menace or majesty but relief, enormous and terrible, like a door finally allowed to close.

Then the throne room tilted. Then the wards' song cut off mid-note. Then Tam was falling, and the last thing he saw before the floor came up to meet him was Petra reaching for him with both hands, her mouth moving around words he suddenly, horribly, could not hear.

Her eyes went wide.

She couldn't hear them either.
