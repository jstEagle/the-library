## 20. The Price of Survival

The god of endings looked at her with the king's eyes.

That was the horror of it—Petra had braced herself for a monster, for the crown's hunger wearing Aldric's face like a mask, but what she saw instead was *tiredness*. A weariness so old it had worn grooves into the world. The god knelt in the ruin of the throne room, half-free, the unbinding sigil she had carved into the air still bleeding light around its wrists, and it regarded her the way a drowning man regards the surface.

*You offer your gift,* it said, and its voice was not sound at all but pressure, a tide moving through the marrow of her bones. *The hearing that is not hearing. The small door you have kept open all these years.*

"Yes." Her voice cracked on the word. Blood ran from her nose, from her ears, from under her fingernails where she had gripped the carving knife too hard. Every soul in Ironhollow's bound dead was pressing against the failing wards Tam held open behind her, and they were not gentle about it. They wanted out. They wanted through *her*. "Take it. Take my gift and let them go."

*And the woman whose bone you carry.* The god's gaze dropped to the charm at her throat—her mother, worn smooth as river stone by twelve years against Petra's skin. *She will scatter too. You know this.*

"I know."

*You choose anyway.*

"I choose anyway."

Behind her, Tam made a sound—not words, just a raw note of protest strangled before it could become a plea. He knew better. They had said everything there was to say last night, in the dark, with their foreheads pressed together: whatever the price, whatever it cost, the dead went free. She had made him promise not to argue today. He was keeping the promise the way he kept all his promises—with visible pain and perfect fidelity.

The god rose. It should not have been able to fit inside the throne room; it did not fit inside the throne room. Its outline exceeded the walls, exceeded the light, and yet the marble did not crack, because endings do not break things—they simply arrive at the place where things stop.

*Then witness,* it said. *I accept.*

---

It began with quiet.

Not silence—Petra had known silence her whole life, the enforced silence of the Law of Quiet, wardens' boots and shuttered windows and prayers swallowed before they left the mouth. This was different. This was the quiet at the bottom of a well, the quiet that comes after the last echo has finished dying.

The flood of souls that had been battering the wards—the thousands upon thousands of the bound dead, twelve years of taken names, pressing toward her like water toward a drain—all of it stopped. Hung. Waited.

And then the god opened its hands.

Petra felt every soul pass through her at once.

There was no other way to describe it, no gentler word. They came through her like light through glass, like winter through a wall, and each one touched her—each one *knew* her—for the length of a heartbeat. A fishwife who had died in the first purge. A warden who had turned his coat too late. Children. So many children. An old man who had carved bells before bell-making was outlawed, and who brushed past her spirit with something like gratitude. They poured through the door she had spent her whole life keeping barely, carefully, invisibly open, and the door did not break—it simply finished being a door, fulfilled itself, and ceased.

She heard her mother last.

Of course she did. Her mother had always been last, waiting at the back of every crowd, letting others go first, holding Petra's coat, mending Petra's hems, standing at the market stall's edge while Petra sold charms cut from her bones. Twelve years of that patience. Twelve years of being carried against her daughter's heart.

*Mimi.*

The name hit her harder than the god's voice had. No one had called her that since the Binding. No one alive had ever called her that.

"Mama." She was crying now, she realized; her face was wet, her hands shaking so badly the knife nearly slipped. "Mama, I'm sorry—I'm sorry I hid you, I'm sorry I kept you, I thought—I thought if I kept enough of you—"

*Hush.* The voice was warm. It was so warm. It was the voice from the kitchen, from fever-nights, from the last morning before the wardens came, when her mother had pressed the bone blank into her nine-year-old hands and whispered *carve me small, little bird, carve me where they won't look.* *You kept me. That was love, not theft. There is nothing to forgive that I have not already forgiven twice.*

"I don't want you to go."

*I know.* And then, gently, with the terrible tenderness only the dead can afford: *But look at me, Mimi. Look at what twelve years in a charm has left. A whisper. A warmth. You deserve a mother, not an amulet. Let me be done. Let me be free. Let me be weather, if I must be anything—let me be rain on your roof someday and not bone in your pocket.*

"I can't—"

*You can. You are the strongest carver this city ever made, and you learned it from me, and I will not have my last lesson be cowardice.* A pause. When the voice came again it was softer, almost amused. *Besides. The lamplighter boy is crying behind you, and he loves you, and you are going to need both your hands free for that.*

"Mama—"

*Goodbye, little bird. Carve plain. Live loud.*

And her mother's soul kissed her forehead—or the memory of a kiss, the shape of one, the way warmth leaves a cheek—and scattered.

---

The silence that followed was total.

Petra stood in the center of the throne room and listened for the dead, because listening for the dead was the oldest habit she had, older than speech, older than fear. She reached for the hum of the mass grave beneath the city. She reached for the murmur that lived in every charm, every grave-marker, every bone in every gutter of Ironhollow.

Nothing.

She reached harder. She clawed inward, down the channels the gift had worn through her, looking for even a thread, a whisper, an echo—

Nothing. Not emptiness, exactly. Emptiness would have been something. This was absence, clean and absolute, the way a healed wound stops hurting: not pain anymore, just the memory of where pain used to live.

"Oh," she said aloud, and heard her own voice as if from far away. "Oh, it's gone. It's really gone."

Then her knees gave out.

Tam caught her before she hit the marble. She knew it was him without opening her eyes—knew it by the grip, by the smell of lamp-oil and smoke and blood, by the way he gathered her in against his chest with one arm while his other hand still splayed against the failing ward-circle, holding it by fingertips alone. Even falling, even half-conscious, she registered that he had held the wards open through all of it, burning his priesthood's last reserves, and that his hands were blackened to the wrist like a man who had grabbed a stove.

"Petra." His voice broke apart on her name. "Petra, stay with me, look at me—"

"It worked," she whispered. "Tam. It worked. Can't you feel it? The wards—they're not fighting anymore—"

He went still. Then, slowly, he lifted his ruined hand from the circle, and the circle did not collapse, because there was nothing left to hold back. The binding was over. The wards were just lines on the floor.

"They're gone," he breathed. "All of them. The whole Binding—twelve years of it—" He laughed, or sobbed, or both; she felt his chest shake against her cheek. "It's over. You did it. You impossible, stubborn, brilliant woman, you actually—"

"The king," she managed. "The king—"

They turned together.

Aldric lay at the foot of his own throne, and he was small. That was the thing Petra would remember longest—not the death, which was quiet, but the smallness. The crown had rolled away from him across the marble, dark and inert as a cold coal, and without it the man was just a man: gray, gaunt, hollow-cheeked, dressed in robes too heavy for the body inside them. Twelve years a vessel. Twelve years eaten from the inside.

But his eyes—his eyes were his own.

They were open, and they were wet, and they found Petra's face with an expression she had never once seen on the king of Ironhollow in all the processions and proclamations of her life: peace. Plain, human, exhausted peace.

"Thank you," Aldric said. His voice was rust and dust, barely a whisper, and it was the first time in twelve years—perhaps the first time since the Binding—that the words came from the man and not the crown. "I remember... I remember my daughter's name. I couldn't—there was a girl, before, I had a daughter, and I couldn't—" A shudder went through him. "I remember everything they took. All of it. All at once." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, and a true one. "It doesn't hurt. Do you understand? After all this time, it doesn't hurt."

"No," Petra said softly. "I understand exactly."

His hand moved—an inch, no more, toward the fallen crown, and stopped. He looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread he has finally been permitted to refuse.

"Don't let them put it on anyone else," he said. "Promise me. Burn it. Bury it deep. Let it be the last thing that ever eats a king."

"We promise," Tam said.

Aldric nodded, slow, satisfied, and his eyes drifted up to the high windows of the throne room, where dawn was coming in gray and gold. Whatever he saw there, it was not the ceiling. His breathing slowed. Spaced out. Stopped between one breath and the next, without drama, without struggle—a man setting down a weight he had carried longer than anyone should carry anything.

The King of Ironhollow died human, in a room full of enemies, smiling at the sunrise.

* * *

Afterward there was chaos, but Petra remembered very little of it.

She remembered Tam carrying her down the palace steps because her legs would not hold. She remembered Ilsa meeting them in the courtyard with her helmet off and her eyes wild, shouting orders at rebels and wardens alike—and the strange, suspended moment when the two forces faced each other across the stones, waiting to see if the killing would continue now that the crown was cold. She remembered Ilsa saying, in a voice that carried across the whole courtyard: *"The king is dead. The Binding is broken. Stand down, all of you, or answer to me"*—and the wardens, after a long terrible silence, standing down.

She remembered the gates of the palace opening onto a city that had come out into the streets without being summoned. Thousands of people, standing in the gray morning light, looking up at the palace as if seeing it for the first time—as if seeing *each other* for the first time. Someone started singing. It was an old hymn, a forbidden one, one of the songs of the bound gods, and Petra knew it because her mother had sung it, and within a verse the whole street was singing it, off-key and ragged and weeping, and no warden moved to stop them because there was no law left to stop them with.

She did not sing. She sat in Tam's arms on the palace steps with her mother's empty charm in her fist and cried until she had nothing left.

Because here was the truth she could not say out loud, the truth that sat in her chest like a stone wrapped in silk: she had won. Everything she had fought for had happened. The dead were free. The god was loose in the world again, unchained, un-eaten. The city was singing in the streets.

And she could not hear her mother anymore.

Not a whisper. Not an echo. Not the faintest warmth at the edge of sleep. For twelve years, even in the worst of it—even in the raids, even hiding in cellars while wardens kicked doors in above her head—she had never been truly alone, because the dead had always been there, murmuring at the margins, and her mother had always been closest of all, a heartbeat of warmth against her sternum.

Now the silence was total, and it was hers forever, and it was the price, and she had paid it gladly, and she grieved it anyway. Both things were true. Both things would always be true.

"She's gone," Petra said into Tam's shoulder. "Really gone. Free. And I can't hear her, and I'm glad, and I'm not glad, and I don't know how to be both at once."

"You've been both at once your whole life," Tam said quietly. His hand moved over her hair, careful, steady. "You survived the Binding by being both at once. Grieving and carving. Hiding and hoping. This is just the last of it." He tilted her chin up so she had to meet his eyes. "You're allowed to cry for the hearing, Petra. You're allowed to miss her voice. Freedom isn't the same as having them back. Anyone who tells you otherwise has never lost anyone."

"And you?" she whispered. "Your gods are loose and distant now. Your priesthood burned its last power holding those wards. What's left for you?"

Tam smiled—that crooked, tired smile she had first seen across a gutter-lit street, when he'd begged her help and she'd refused him. "A city," he said. "Lamps to relight. People to feed. You." He hesitated, and then, plainly, the way he had learned to say everything in the last six days: "Mostly you."

* * *

She woke in Tam's arms.

Not dramatically—no sickbed, no fever, no long darkness. Just exhaustion claiming her on the palace steps and releasing her hours later, in a rebel safehouse that smelled of candle-smoke and someone's lentil stew, with a blanket over her and Tam sitting against the wall beneath the window, her head pillowed on his chest, his breathing slow and even. Awake. He had been awake the whole time. She knew it without asking; she could feel it in the tension of the arm around her, the vigilance of a man who had decided nothing would touch her while he drew breath.

Around them, the safehouse murmured with ordinary life—rebels sleeping in shifts, water boiling, someone laughing low and disbelieving in the next room, the laugh of people who had expected to die today and hadn't. Through the window, Ironhollow's bells were ringing. All of them. Every tower, unsanctioned, unregistered, ringing wildly and out of rhythm, because for the first time in twelve years there was no law against bells.

Petra lay still and listened.

Not for the dead. She knew better than to reach for that door again; it was closed, and it would stay closed, and some mornings that would feel like freedom and some mornings like an amputation, and she would learn to carry it the way she had learned to carry everything else. No—she listened to the living. The bells. The stew. Tam's heart under her ear, steady as a metronome, counting out a future.

For the first time in her life, the silence in her skull was just silence. Empty, human, hers.

"Tam," she said.

His arms tightened. "I'm here."

"The crown. Did we—"

"Burned at dawn. Ilsa supervised it personally. Ashes went into the foundry slag." A pause. "Aldric's lying in state in the temple hall. Ilsa says the city wants to bury him properly. As a man." Another pause, longer. "There's going to be a council. Rebels, wardens, guilds—everyone arguing about what comes next. Ilsa wants us there. Wants *you* there. The woman who ended the Binding." He said it lightly, but she felt the weight underneath: the world was already reaching for her, the way the world reaches for anyone who does something large. Visibility. The thing she had spent twelve years avoiding at any cost.

Petra looked at the window, at the gray-gold light of the first free morning, and at the empty charm hanging around her neck—her mother's bone, smooth and silent, a relic now instead of a prison.

"Not today," she said. "Today I want breakfast. And to walk through the market without hiding. And—" her voice caught, steadied— "and to figure out what a carver is, when the carving is just carving."

Tam laughed softly, and pressed his lips to her hair. "That," he said, "sounds like the beginning of everything."

Outside, the bells rang on, and somewhere over the rooftops of Ironhollow—she could not hear it, but she knew it the way she knew rain was coming—her mother was weather.
