## 21. A City of the Living

The Warden's Hall smelled different now.

Ilsa Varn noticed it her first morning back, standing in the doorway with her resignation letter folded in her pocket like a knife she'd decided not to use. For twelve years the hall had smelled of tallow, ink, and fear—the fear of the people dragged through its doors, the fear of the wardens themselves, who learned early that mercy was a disease and kindness a symptom. Now it smelled of bread. Someone had set up ovens in the courtyard behind the barracks, and the smell drifted in through the broken windows nobody had replaced yet, because glass was scarce and there were more important things to buy with what coin the city had left.

"Bread," she said aloud, to no one, and almost laughed.

"You'll get used to it." Doss came down the stairs two at a time, his arm still in a sling from the fire at Ash Market, his other hand full of ledgers. "Baker's wife whose daughter you pulled out of the burning quarter. She insists on feeding every shift. If you try to refuse she'll feed you anyway, and if you try to arrest her for obstruction she'll feed you twice."

"I'm not arresting anyone for baking."

"No." Doss set the ledgers on the table that had once been the duty desk, where Ilsa had signed a hundred warrants for people whose only crime was a carved bone or a whispered prayer. "You're not arresting anyone at all, apparently. That's the rumor. That Warden-Captain Varn has gone soft."

Ilsa looked at him. He was grinning. Under the grin he was watching her carefully, the way he'd watched her since the night she'd walked into this hall and told them all what she'd seen in the palace—the courtier who had withered to dust under the king's touch, the crown drinking light out of a man like marrow out of bone. Some of them had believed her immediately. Some had needed to see the empty throne, the king's body laid out in state with a human face at last, peaceful and small and dead. And some—four wardens, to be exact—had walked out that night and not come back, and Ilsa did not blame them. She had almost been one of them.

"Soft," she said. "Say it louder. I want the whole quarter to hear."

"They already have." He tapped the top ledger. "Forty-one applications this week. People wanting to *join*. Fishwives, a stonemason, two of the ash-market survivors, a boy of sixteen who can read and wants to 'keep the peace proper this time.' We've never had applications before. We've had conscripts and cowards and men who liked the uniform because it let them hit things."

"And now?"

"And now we have forty-one people who think the wardens might be worth joining." He pushed the ledger toward her. "Which means we have forty-one people who are going to find out very quickly whether they're right."

Ilsa sat down. The chair was the same chair. That was the strange part—that the furniture hadn't changed, that the stones of the floor were the same stones she'd stood on while signing orders she'd told herself were lawful, were necessary, were the price of quiet. The Binding had ended six weeks ago. The god of endings had scattered free with all the others, the bound dead had gone wherever freed souls go, and Ironhollow had woken up on the far side of it like a man waking from a fever, weak and astonished and unsure which parts of what he remembered were real.

But the hall was the same hall. The laws were still written in the same books. And the books, Ilsa had decided, were the next battlefield.

* * *

They met in the afternoon, in what had been the interrogation room—the one with the drain in the floor, the one Ilsa had never been able to look at without seeing her neighbor's face, the carver's apprentice taken for a charm that worked. They'd talked about tearing the room out entirely. In the end they'd kept it and changed what happened inside it: a long table, chairs on both sides, a window cut into the wall so that daylight came in. The drain remained. Some things you didn't paper over. You looked at them until looking stopped being unbearable, or you admitted it never would.

Around the table: Doss. Sergeant Maren, gray-haired, who had spent twelve years enforcing the Law of Quiet and had wept openly, twice, during the first week. A lean woman named Oyel who had been a rebel runner for Tam's underground and now sat in a warden's chair with the wary posture of someone waiting for the trap. And at the head, because no one else would take it and because she had stopped pretending she didn't deserve it, Ilsa.

"The old codes," Maren said, and put her hand flat on the stack of books between them as if holding down something that might fly up and bite. "All of it. The Law of Quiet, the charm statutes, the licensing of speech in public assembly. It's still law. Nothing has repealed it. The Council is arguing about whether the Council even exists anymore, since half of them fed the crown their own years and the other half ran. Until someone repeals it, technically—"

"Technically," Oyel said, "we're all criminals. Every person in this city who's said a prayer since the solstice."

"Technically," Ilsa agreed.

"So we burn the books," Oyel said. "Symbolic. Public. Big fire, everyone invited, the baker brings the bread."

"No," said Ilsa, and heard how her voice came out—not loud, but final, the voice she'd used for twelve years to end arguments, and she made herself soften it. "No. Burning them is easy. Burning them is what a guilty conscience does—it makes a big bright noise so it doesn't have to sit quietly with what it did." She pulled the top book toward her and opened it. The pages were dense, dry, precise: definitions of contraband, schedules of penalties, the careful legal language by which a city had strangled its own soul and called it order. "We don't burn them. We read them. All of them, line by line, in public, in the market square, and after each section we say what it did—to whom—and then we strike it. Ink, not fire. There should be a record. There should be a record of everything, forever, including us. Especially us."

Silence around the table. Maren's hand was still flat on the book, and Ilsa saw her knuckles whiten, saw the old sergeant wrestling with something.

"I took people," Maren said finally. "Under these sections. Forty-some over the years. I can name most of them. I told myself—" She stopped. Started again. "I told myself the alternative was worse. That without the Law there'd be panic, plague-gods, blood sacrifice in the gutters. That quiet was mercy."

"Was it?" Oyel asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely asking.

Maren was quiet a long time. Through the new window, Ilsa could hear the city: hammers, cart wheels, children, someone singing—a real song, an old one, sung at full volume in broad daylight, which six weeks ago would have earned the singer a cell.

"No," Maren said. "It wasn't mercy. It was anesthesia. And I was the needle." She picked up the pen. "Read me the first section, Captain. Let's get started striking."

* * *

The work swallowed the days whole.

Ilsa had imagined, in the fevered weeks before the solstice, that freedom would arrive like a flood—all at once, everything changed, the world washed clean. What arrived instead was paperwork. Disputes about property seized under the old law, and who got it back when the original owner was dead. Wardens who had to be vetted, some dismissed, some forgiven, and the endless bitter argument about which was which. Food shortages, because the palace granaries had been hoarding against a siege no one was coming to make. The temple district, sealed for twelve years, opened like a wound and had to be cleaned, aired, made safe for people to walk into without weeping, which turned out to be impossible, so they let people weep and posted wardens at the doors whose only duty was to stand there and be kind.

And the gods. Everyone wanted to talk about the gods.

They were gone—or not gone, but *distant*, the way weather is distant, the way the sea is distant from a city built inland. Tam's priests—he had gathered a handful, ragged and astonished to be alive—held rites in the reopened temples and reported the same thing every week: presence, vast and quiet, like something enormous sleeping just past the edge of hearing. No voices. No miracles. No answers. The god of endings, Tam said, had gone furthest of all, out beyond the sky's rim where endings live, and had left behind only a feeling in certain places—in doorways, at bedsides, at the moment a hard thing finishes—that the world was being gently, attentively closed and opened again, like a book.

People prayed anyway. Ilsa saw it everywhere, in the market, at the graves, in the temple queues: lips moving, hands open, faces lifted toward a silence that did not reply. And instead of despairing, most of them smiled afterward, and went back to their work, and Ilsa slowly understood why. For twelve years the gods had answered Ironhollow's prayers with horror—bound, devoured, twisted into the machinery of a hollow king. Silence, now, was not abandonment. Silence was a god resting. Silence meant nothing was eating anything.

She caught herself praying too, sometimes. Not to ask. Just to say thank you into the quiet, the way you'd speak over a sleeping friend.

* * *

On a grey morning three weeks into the codework, a girl was brought to the Hall, and Ilsa's stomach dropped before she even heard the charge, because the girl was maybe ten and the charge was charms.

"She was selling them," said the warden who'd brought her in—young, one of the forty-one, a former dockhand named Pell with honest eyes and a face that kept trying to apologize. "Bone charms. Little ones. Down by the fish stalls. Half the market bought one before anyone thought to—well. Before anyone thought."

Ilsa looked at the girl. Thin, sharp-eyed, hair braided tight, sitting in the chair across the table with her chin up and her fists in her lap, and Ilsa recognized the posture instantly because she'd seen it a hundred times across this same table: defiance bolted over terror, the look of someone who has already decided the world is against her and is determined to make it earn the victory.

"What's your name?" Ilsa asked.

The girl said nothing.

"We're not going to hurt you. Nobody here is going to hurt you. That's—I know how that sounds. From in that chair, from where you're sitting, I know exactly how that sounds." Ilsa turned her own chair sideways, unhurried, and set her hands flat on the table where the girl could see them, empty. "My name is Ilsa Varn. Six weeks ago I was the kind of warden you're afraid of. I arrested a woman from my own street for carving. I stood there and watched her be taken and I filed the report and I slept that night, which is the part I can't forgive myself for—not the arrest, the sleeping." The girl's chin dipped a fraction. Her fists loosened by a degree. "So I'm not going to tell you the wardens are good now. I'm going to tell you we're trying, in public, where everyone can watch us fail. And right now, trying means you eat breakfast, and then I take you home, and then I go find whoever taught you to carve, because if your charms are working, I want to shake their hand."

The girl stared at her. Then, slowly, suspiciously: "The charms aren't mine. They're my mum's designs. She's dead. I do them exact."

"Do they work?"

A pause. The girl's eyes flicked away, and Ilsa saw it—the flicker of someone deciding whether to spend a secret.

"One of them did," the girl said. "The little round one. Old woman bought it for her hip. Came back next day walking straight and laughing. Everyone saw." She lifted her chin again, braced for the blow. "So."

So. Ilsa thought of a stall in the carver's quarter, of a woman with steady hands and a guarded face, of bone dust and small knives and the terrible arithmetic of staying invisible. She thought of a fire, and a neighbor's daughter who hadn't come out.

"Pell," she said, "get breakfast. Then fetch the Okonkwo woman from the market—politely, Pell, *politely*—and tell her I'd count it a favor." She turned back to the girl. "Eat first. Then we'll talk about apprenticeships."

* * *

Petra Okonkwo arrived at midday, and Ilsa met her in the doorway of the Hall itself, because it mattered where you met people—she understood that now, understood how much of tyranny was architecture, who waited where, who was made to stand.

"You sent for me," Petra said. No greeting. Her eyes went past Ilsa's shoulder into the hall, cataloguing exits, counting wardens, the habit of a lifetime of hiding, though the hiding was over now—everyone knew her face, the woman who had unbound the god, the woman whose deafness to the dead the whole city grieved on her behalf as if it were a wound they shared. She wore it lightly, Ilsa thought. Or wore it the way soldiers wear scars: not lightly at all, but without apology.

"A girl was selling charms this morning. Your designs, she says."

Something moved in Petra's face—alarm, then grief, then a tired tenderness. "Nella. Her mother was taken in the second year. I taught her the basics because she kept hanging around the stall and because—" She stopped. "Because I remembered what it was to need the work."

"She's downstairs, eating. I'd like you to take her on. Properly. And I'd like the wardens to guard the stall while she learns—publicly, visibly, so the market sees whose side the law is on now." Ilsa paused. "That's the request. Here's the confession that comes with it: six weeks ago I'd have arrested her. A year ago I did arrest your neighbor, and I've read my own report since, and it's a clean report, tidy as a knife, and I hate it more than anything I've ever written. So I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to let me be useful, until trust catches up. If it ever does."

Petra studied her for a long moment. Behind her, in the street, the city went on being loud and alive—carts, arguments, the baker's bell, a child shrieking with laughter over nothing.

"Trust catches up faster than you'd think," Petra said at last. "It's forgiveness that takes its time. Come by the stall Thursday. Bring the girl's file—we'll strike it together, in front of the market. My neighbor would have wanted a crowd."

She turned to go, then hesitated, and when she spoke again her voice was lower, almost private. "You asked once, in another life, what the dead whisper. I can't hear them anymore. But I'll tell you what I know anyway, from before: they don't haunt the guilty, Warden Varn. They wait for the guilty to stand back up. You stood up. Somewhere, something noticed." A ghost of a smile. "Thursday."

Then she was gone into the noise, and Ilsa stood in the doorway of the hall she had helped make monstrous and was slowly helping make otherwise, and found that her eyes were wet, and let them be, because the corridor was empty and some mercies you granted yourself.

* * *

Evening. The codework session ran late; the candles burned down; Maren struck the last statute of the day with a hand that shook only a little. When the others had gone, Ilsa stayed, alone with the growing pile of struck pages and the one book she had not yet opened—the oldest one, bound in black, stamped with the seal of the first king's reign, the founding charter of the Law of Quiet itself.

Doss had flagged it weeks ago. "This is the root," he'd said. "Everything else grew out of this. When you're ready."

She was ready tonight. She broke the seal, which cracked like a small bone, and read.

The charter began with the usual language—order, safety, the perils of unlicensed divinity—and Ilsa read it with the numb fluency of long familiarity. Then, on the third page, in a clerk's careful hand, a marginal note beside the clause authorizing the Binding of the gods:

*Rite requires vessel of royal blood. Rite requires renewal each solstice. NOTE: the rite as performed binds the god to the vessel, not the vessel to the god. The crown may be worn by any who inherit. The god goes where the crown goes.*

Ilsa sat very still.

She had assumed—everyone had assumed—that the Binding was Ironhollow's sin alone, sealed in this city's stones, ended in this city's throne room. But the charter was older than that, and its margins were full of references: correspondence, precedent, citations of *the northern rite*, *the coastal rite*, *the sister-cities' compact*. Other thrones. Other crowns. Other vessels, wearing other kings, in other cities where the gods had gone silent twelve years ago—or longer, the margins suggested, much longer—and no Petra Okonkwo had ever risen among the carvers to carve them free.

She read the last citation twice. Then she closed the book, carried it home through the dark streets, and knocked on the door of the lamplighter's cottage by the old temple, where a light was still burning, because Tam had never yet let a lamp go dark.

He opened the door, saw her face, and reached for his coat.

"How many?" he asked.

Ilsa held up the charter. "The margins say seven," she said. "Seven crowns. And ours was the youngest."
