## 22. Ironhollow Rising

The bone was sheep's knuckle, small as a thumb joint, and it did nothing at all.

Petra turned it in the morning light that came through the new window—real glass, not oiled cloth, a luxury she still caught herself apologizing for—and carved the last groove of a running deer. The knife moved the way it always had, before. Before the whispers. Before the dead leaned against her ears like beggars at a market stall. Before she had learned that every line she cut could become a door.

This line was only a line. The deer ran nowhere but across the surface of the bone, and when she held it up and listened—and she still listened, out of habit, the way a man who has lived through a fire flinches at sparks—there was nothing. No murmur. No pressure behind the silence. Just the ordinary quiet of an ordinary morning in Ironhollow: cart wheels on wet cobbles, a fishmonger arguing about the price of eel, somewhere two streets over the hammering of the temple scaffolds.

She set the charm down among its brothers on the drying board. Forty of them now. Deer and herons and hounds and one lopsided bear she'd carved on a bad night and couldn't bring herself to discard. Plain bone, plain work, sold for plain coin to people who wore them because they were pretty, or lucky in the way anything handmade is lucky, or because they remembered—dimly, guiltily, the way you remember a dream from childhood—that their grandmothers had worn such things and the world had not ended.

The world had almost ended anyway. But not because of the charms.

Petra wiped her hands on her apron and looked up at the doorframe, where the empty charm hung.

It had been her mother's. Twelve years it had hung against her skin, warm with a soul worn smooth as river stone, whispering to her in the dark of every hiding place she'd ever crawled into. Now it was only bone and braided cord, and it swung gently in the draft from the door, tapping the wood with a sound like a small patient knock.

*Good morning,* Petra said to it, silently, the way she still did. *Quiet day ahead.*

The charm said nothing. It never would again. And for the first time since she was nine years old, silence did not feel like being buried alive.

* * *

The shop had been Ilsa's idea, though Ilsa would deny it if asked. *You can't sit in Tam's rooms carving trinkets forever,* she'd said, standing in the doorway with her warden's coat gone and her hair cut short and the look of a woman who had burned her own life down and found the ashes livable. *The quarter needs rebuilding. You need rebuilding. Same work, really.*

So they'd taken the ground floor of a house on Carver's Row—the third house from the corner, where the fire had stopped, where the scorch marks still climbed the brick like black ivy no one wanted to scrub away—and Petra had put her name on a scrap of paper and nailed it above the door. *P. Okonkwo, Bonecarver.* Her hands had shaken doing it. Not from fear. From the strangeness of writing her name in public, in daylight, where anyone could read it and know exactly where she was.

For twelve years her survival had depended on being unfindable. She had made herself small enough to slip between the law's fingers, quiet enough that even the dead sometimes missed her. And it had cost her everything it could reach: her neighbor Ferro, taken in the night for a charm that worked; his daughter Sela, who died in the ash market fire because invisibility is not protection, it is only a slower way of being alone; her mother, whose soul she had hidden so well that hiding became the whole shape of her life.

*You stayed invisible too long,* Ilsa had told her once, brutally, in the weeks after the solstice, when Petra woke screaming from dreams of smoke. *We all did. That's how it worked. That's how it always works.*

Now the Row was coming back. Half the houses were still gutted, their interiors open to the sky like dolls' houses after a child's tantrum, but there were roofs going on—she could hear the mallets from here—and the widow Ansa had reopened her dye shop two doors down, and every morning the smell of mordant and wool drifted past the window and made Petra think, absurdly, of color returning to a face.

Her first customer of the day came before she'd finished sweeping. A boy, maybe ten, gap-toothed, holding out a copper coin with the gravity of a man paying off a debt.

"My gran wants a bird," he said. "A flying one. For her window."

"A heron, then." Petra took the coin. "Or a swallow?"

"Swallow." He nodded decisively, as if this had been a negotiation of state. He watched her select a sliver of bone with the intense patience of children, and while she worked he chattered—about the temple, which was getting its roof back; about the wardens, who didn't take people anymore but walked the streets like everyone else, except taller; about his gran, who said the gods were gone but prayed anyway, just in case.

"They're not gone," Petra said. She blew bone dust from the swallow's wing. "They're just far away."

"That's what my gran says." The boy frowned. "How do you know?"

Because I was there, she thought. Because I held the door open while ten thousand souls poured through me like light through a cracked bell, and I heard them go—not dying, not scattering into nothing, but *leaving*, the way birds leave a city in autumn, all at once, without looking back. Because one voice stayed longest of all, pressed close against whatever remained of my hearing, and said, *my girl, my brave girl, live loud enough for both of us.*

"They went home," she said aloud, and gave him the swallow.

He held it up to the light, turning it, and Petra braced herself for the question she still got, once or twice a week: *does it work? Is it magic? Will it answer prayers?*

"It's just bone," she said. "But it's good bone."

He seemed satisfied. Children, she was learning, understood better than adults that things did not have to be magic to matter.

* * *

At midday she closed the shutters and walked up to the temple.

It was becoming a ritual of her own, though she'd never named it. The path wound up through streets that were half rubble and half resurrection, past the fountain where the god of endings had stood for one impossible hour at the solstice—tall as the palace spire, made of the same darkness as the space between stars, wearing the freed king's death like a crown of its own—past the wall where someone had chalked, in letters visible from three streets away, *THE QUIET IS OVER*, though whether that was celebration or warning depended on who read it.

The temple of the bound gods had been sealed for twelve years, its doors warded, its lamps dark. Now the doors stood open all day, and inside, every evening, Tam lit the lamps.

She found him on the scaffold by the eastern wall, sleeves rolled, plaster dust in his dark hair, arguing cheerfully with a mason half again his size about whether the relief of the goddess Vess could be restored or needed recarving entirely.

"—her left hand is original, look at the wrist—"

"The wrist's original, the fingers are somebody's idea of fingers. Let me carve new ones."

"You'll make her look like your wife."

"My wife has excellent hands."

Petra laughed, and both men looked down, and Tam's whole face changed—the way it still did, every time, as if her arrival were news too good to have gotten used to. He came down the ladder with more speed than dignity, kissed her with plaster-dusty lips, and got a cuff from the mason for abandoning the argument.

"He'll sulk all afternoon," Tam said, unrepentant.

"He'll win the argument instead. Vess will have his wife's hands and be better for it."

They walked the temple together, as they did most days. It was still mostly emptiness—the great hall stripped bare, the altars cracked, the murals whitewashed during the Binding and only partially scraped clean, ghost-images of gods surfacing under the plaster like bodies under ice. But the roof was whole now over the central nave, and the floor had been swept, and last week the first bench had arrived: one bench, oak, big enough for six people, sitting alone in the vast space like a boat on a dark sea.

"Seven came yesterday," Tam said. "To sit. Not to pray, exactly. To sit." He said it carefully, the way he said everything about the temple—as if speaking too loudly might frighten the fragile thing back into hiding. "Old woman, two brothers, a family with a baby. They just sat. I lit the lamps and they sat in the light."

"What did you do?"

"I sat with them." He shrugged, self-conscious. "I don't lead rites anymore. I don't think anyone should, not yet. Not until we know what we're... what's left." He stopped at the foot of the altar dais, where the god of endings had spoken through the king's mouth, where Petra had carved the unbinding into the crown itself while the world screamed around her. His hand found hers. "The old rites were built for bound gods. Bound worship. Everything chained to everything else. We'd just be building smaller chains."

"And if nothing comes back? If the gods stay far away and answer no one?" She asked it gently. She knew it kept him up at night—Tam, who had given his priesthood's last power to hold the wards open, who had burned his calling like kindling and called it fair trade. "Is an empty temple a failure?"

He was quiet a long moment. Above them, through the new skylight—a scandalous modern addition the masons had fought him over and lost—the afternoon light fell in a long pale column onto the dais.

"When I was a novice," he said finally, "they taught us that the gods were present in the lamps. That lighting them was a summons. Every flame called a god's attention down to you, and you had to be worthy of it, and if you weren't—" He smiled ruefully. "If you weren't, the silence was punishment. Exile. I spent twelve years feeling punished by a silence I couldn't even hear properly, and I wasn't even the one they'd bound." He turned to her. "And then the solstice came, and the god of endings stood in this room, and do you know what it said to me, at the end? When it was free and full and could have said anything?"

Petra shook her head. She had been unconscious by then, deaf already, held in his arms while the dead poured out of the world.

"It said *thank you*. Not for freeing it—for the lamps. All those years, the little lights in the dark. It said the silence was never punishment. It said silence is just..." He searched for it. "*Room.* Room for something to grow, or rest, or leave. It depends what you plant."

"So you light the lamps anyway."

"So I light the lamps anyway." He squeezed her hand. "Not as a summons. As a habit. As—" he laughed softly, "—as a charm. Plain flame, no magic. But it's good flame."

* * *

Evening came early that time of year, and they lit the lamps together.

There were nine of them now, iron stands salvaged from the wreck of the old temple, spaced down the nave. Tam went first with the taper, and Petra followed with the oil, and they worked in the easy wordless rhythm of people who have survived something together and no longer need to narrate it. Each lamp caught with a soft breath of gold, and the shadows retreated up the whitewashed walls, and the ghost-gods in the half-scraped murals surfaced a little further into visibility: a shoulder here, an eye there, hands raised in blessing or grief or simply greeting.

On the last lamp, near the doors, Tam paused.

"This one's yours tonight," he said.

It had become a small ceremony between them, though neither had proposed it. The ninth lamp stood closest to the entrance, where anyone leaving would pass it last, and Petra had claimed it the first week without explaining why. She took the taper now and touched it to the wick, and watched the flame stand up straight and steady in the still air.

Twelve years ago, on the night of the Binding, she had hidden in a cellar with her mother's soul against her heart and listened to the silence fall over Ironhollow like snowfall, like ash, like a lid closing on a box. Silence as exile. Silence as the sound of everything she loved being locked away where she could never follow. She had built her whole life inside that silence—small carvings, small movements, a self whittled down to fit through the eye of the law's needle—and she had told herself it was survival, and it was, and it had also been a kind of dying, slow and respectable, the death of a person who is careful enough to keep breathing and never anything more.

The flame flickered. Outside, the evening sounds of the living city rose up the hill: voices, wheels, a dog, someone singing badly and happily in a tavern on the Row. Inside, the lamps burned, and the gods were far away, and nothing answered, and nothing needed to.

*Live loud enough for both of us.*

"I'm trying," Petra whispered to the flame. "Look at me. Look at the shop. Look at the name on the door." She smiled, and her eyes stung, and she let them. "You'd laugh at the bear. It's terrible. I'm keeping it anyway."

The flame bent toward her, the way flames do in a room's faint breathing, and held, and did not speak, because it was only fire, and that was enough.

Tam's arm came around her shoulders. Together they stood in the doorway a moment, looking back at the nine small lights in the vast dim hall—one bench beneath them, empty, waiting for tomorrow's seven, or ten, or thirty; ghost-gods watching from the walls with their half-recovered faces; the whole space humming with that particular quality of silence that is not absence but attention, a held breath, a room that has decided to listen rather than speak.

"Walk you home?" Tam said.

"It's four streets."

"Long streets."

They went down together into the lamplit evening, and behind them the temple doors stood open, as they would stand open tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that.

Above the door of the bonecarver's shop, when they reached it, the empty charm turned slowly in the night wind, tapping its small patient knock against the wood—bone against timber, nothing against something, a mother's last gift hanging in the doorway of her daughter's loud, unfinished, miraculous life.

Petra touched it once, lightly, the way you greet someone sleeping.

Then she went inside, and lit her own lamp, and began to carve.
