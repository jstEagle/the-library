## 1. The Date That Waited a Century

The rain came down the mountain like it had a grudge.

Tamsin Fairweather stood at the mouth of the pass with forty pounds of uncut moonstone strapped to his back and a mule named Duchess who hated him on principle, and he watched the storm roll up the valley of Thornfell the way a man watches a debt collector cross the street toward him. There was no outrunning it. The clouds had eaten the peaks whole, and what light remained was the color of old pewter, flat and joyless, the kind of sky that made even the crows go quiet.

"Well," he said to Duchess, because talking to animals was cheaper than talking to people and neither of them listened anyway. "Looks like we're finding religion."

Duchess flicked one ear in a manner that suggested she found this less funny than he did.

He'd been running cargo through these mountains for eleven years—salt, spirits, moonstone, occasionally a person who paid well enough not to ask about—and in eleven years he'd learned three things worth knowing. One: never take a contract from a man who smiles too easily. Two: the Thornfell weather lies like a customs officer. Three: when the storm comes, you get under stone or you get buried under it, and there's no third option no matter how good your boots are.

There was stone ahead. He could just make out the shape of it through the sheeting gray—a shoulder of ruin jutting from the hillside above the old pilgrim road, half-swallowed by pine and ivy. A shrine, maybe. The mountains were littered with them, little way-stations of worship abandoned when the roads moved or the gods went quiet or the coin ran dry. Tamsin didn't pray in them. He sheltered in them. There was a distinction there that he felt was important, though he'd have been hard-pressed to explain it to anyone, least of all himself.

He climbed. The rain turned from assault to drowning somewhere around the switchbacks, and by the time he hauled Duchess under the broken archway his cloak weighed as much as she did and his hands had gone the color of fish bellies.

* * *

Inside, the shrine was small and mostly dead. A single vaulted chamber, its ceiling cracked open to the sky like an egg, letting the storm howl down into it. Moss had taken the walls in green continents. There had been statues once—three of them, flanking a dais—and two had been toppled and shattered so long ago that they were more suggestion than sculpture, lumps of granite wearing lichen like mourning veils.

The third still stood.

It stood at the back, in the driest corner, and Tamsin stopped when he saw it because something about it snagged on him, the way a nail snags a sleeve. It was a figure robed and hooded, faceless beneath the hood, both hands pressed flat over where its mouth would be. Not carved in fear. Carved in *stillness*. The kind of stillness that has weight, that fills a room the way water fills a cup.

Veyra, he realized. The silent god. His god, if he was honest, which he tried not to be before breakfast.

He'd been sworn to Veyra since he was nine years old, which in the mercenary trade made him practically a monk. It wasn't a demanding faith. Veyra asked nothing—literally nothing, no tithes, no prayers, no burnt offerings, because Veyra said nothing and had said nothing for as long as anyone could remember. The priests of the silent god kept silence themselves, mostly, and tended shrines like this one, and the faithful—scattered, quiet folk, smugglers and soldiers and widows and other people whose lives ran on things better left unsaid—touched the hooded statue's hand and went about their business feeling, vaguely, watched over.

It was a comfortable arrangement. God keeps silent. Man keeps moving. Nobody's disappointed.

"Evening," Tamsin told the statue, shaking rain off his cloak. "Mind if we drip on your floor?"

The statue, naturally, said nothing. He liked that about it.

Duchess settled against the far wall with the groan of a creature who considered the entire enterprise beneath her. Tamsin built a small fire from the wreckage of an old votive rack—apologies to whoever had donated the wood, though given the state of the place he suspected the statute of limitations had run—and dug out his supper: hard bread, harder cheese, and a flask of plum brandy that he rationed like a miser because it was the last of the good stuff until Greymoss.

He was halfway through the brandy and all the way through the bread when the lightning lit the chamber white, and in that instant of terrible clarity he saw the dais.

There was something on it. Something that hadn't been visible in the gloom—a shape tucked behind the standing statue, wedged in the angle between granite and wall, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff and brown as autumn leaves.

Now, Tamsin Fairweather had a rule about ruins, and the rule was this: leave what's sleeping alone. Ruins are full of things that were put away for reasons, and the reasons are usually older and meaner than you. But the lightning had shown him the shape, and once a man has seen a thing he can't unsee it, and besides—the storm wasn't stopping, the brandy was doing its work, and curiosity is the one sin every smuggler carries like a birthmark.

He got up. He told himself it was to stretch his legs.

The oilcloth crackled when he touched it, flaking like burnt skin. Beneath it was a book. Not a scroll, not a bundle of letters—a book, bound in leather the color of dried blood, its cover stamped with a seal he didn't recognize: a circle, closed, with a line drawn straight through it. A mouth, shut. Or a road, ended. He turned it over twice and couldn't decide.

"No," he said aloud, to himself, to the shrine, to the general universe. "Absolutely not."

He opened it anyway. This is the sort of man he was, and he'd stopped apologizing for it years ago.

The pages were heavy stock, hand-lettered, the ink faded to the brown of old wounds. Most of it was in no alphabet he knew—dense columns of characters like bird tracks in snow—but here and there the text shifted into Trade Common, the tongue of contracts and border crossings, and those passages were annotated in a different hand, cramped and urgent, the ink blotted as though written by someone whose hands wouldn't stop shaking.

He skimmed. Prophecies, mostly. He'd read prophecy before; it was the favorite pastime of mad kings and expensive whores, and it always sounded the same, all shadow and throne and *when the rivers run backward*. He was already composing the joke he'd tell at Greymoss—*found the end of the world in a shrine, traded it for half a wheel of cheese*—when his eye caught a date.

Not a vague date. Not *in the days of ash* or *under the seventh moon*. A date. Written plain, in Trade Common, in that shaking hand:

*The seventeenth night of Deepfrost, when the storm rides down from the White Teeth.*

Tonight. Tonight was the seventeenth of Deepfrost. And the storm—

Tamsin looked up at the cracked ceiling, where the rain was falling through in silver threads, and felt the first cold finger touch the back of his neck.

Coincidence, he told himself. Storms ride down from the White Teeth all winter. Dates repeat every year. Any book that names enough dates gets one right eventually, same as a stopped clock.

He laughed. Out loud, alone, in a dead god's house—he laughed, a short ugly sound, and turned the page to see what other nonsense the mad annotator had left, because now it was funny, now it was material.

And there he was.

*A man of the low roads shall come up out of the rain,* the hand had written, pressing so hard the nib had torn the paper. *A carrier of stolen things. Grey-eyed, scarred at the left brow, sworn to the Silent One since childhood and faithless in his heart. He will carry the word that waits a century, and on him hangs the choice: the war wakes, or the war dies forever.*

Tamsin sat down. He didn't decide to sit down; his legs simply resigned without notice.

Slowly, with the detached calm of a man watching someone else's disaster, he reached up and touched the scar over his left eyebrow—the one he'd gotten at fourteen, falling off a loading dock in Harrowgate, hardly worth remembering.

Grey eyes. Low roads. Stolen things. Sworn at nine, in a little chapel in the marsh country, because his mother thought it would settle him.

"Ha," he said.

The fire popped. Rain drummed the broken ceiling. Duchess sighed in her sleep.

"Ha ha," he said, and it came out wrong, thinner than laughing ought to sound, and then it wasn't laughter at all anymore, just breath going in and out of a man sitting very still on a cold stone floor with a dead century open in his lap.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he couldn't joke past, couldn't drink past, couldn't smuggle past the border of his own skull: the handwriting. The annotations weren't a hundred years old. The ink hadn't faded the way a century fades things. Whoever had written this had written it *recently*—within his lifetime, maybe within the decade—which meant somewhere out there in the grey world was a person who knew his face, his history, his faith, and had bothered to write it all down in a sealed book and hide it in a ruined shrine on a route he happened to run.

Which meant it wasn't prophecy.

Which meant it was a *trap*, or a summons, or bait—and Tamsin Fairweather had spent eleven years surviving exactly this kind of package. He knew what happened to couriers who carried things meant for other people. They got opened first.

He should burn it. That was the sensible move. Feed it to the fire, finish the brandy, sleep four hours, and be over the pass by dawn with his moonstone and his miserable life intact, none the wiser.

His hands didn't move.

The statue of Veyra stood over him, hooded, hands pressed to its mouth, keeping its ancient silence. Twenty years of touching that stone hand and feeling nothing come back—no warmth, no whisper, no sign. Twenty years of telling himself that was the point, that silence *was* the answer, that a god who asked nothing gave nothing and owed nothing.

And now a book, waiting in the dark behind the god's back, saying: *he will carry the word that waits.*

"What word?" Tamsin asked the empty shrine. His voice cracked on the second word, and he hated it. "What word? Who wrote you? What am I supposed to be carrying?"

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. Nothing ever answered.

But as he sat there, the storm screaming down through the broken roof, Tamsin became aware of a sensation he had no name for and would spend the rest of his life trying to find one for: the feeling of being *read*. Of the silence around him not being empty, but held—like a breath. Like a door, closed a very long time ago, with something patient standing on the other side of it.

He looked down at the book. At the seal stamped in its cover: the circle, closed. The line through it.

Deep in the mountain, so deep he might have imagined it—might have, would tell himself later he had—something shifted. Not a sound. The absence of one. A pressure change, like the moment before lightning.

Duchess woke, ears flat, and stared hard at the back wall of the shrine, at nothing Tamsin could see.

"Right," Tamsin said quietly, and closed the book, and did not burn it. "Mother Cassia's going to tell me this is a forgery."

He wasn't sure he believed it. He was less sure he wanted to.

Outside, the storm rode down from the White Teeth, right on schedule, as if it had been sent.
