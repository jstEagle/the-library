## 2. What the Silent God Owes

The storm caught him two miles below the shrine, as storms in Thornfell always did—suddenly, and with the personal malice of a thing that had been waiting all day for exactly this. Tamsin pulled Duchess's hood tighter over her ears and took the switchback trail down toward Greyhollow at a pace that was less walking than controlled falling, mud sucking at his boots, the book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heartbeat.

He'd considered leaving it. He'd stood in the ruined shrine with the rain hammering the broken roof and told himself, very reasonably, that a man could simply walk away from a book. Books burned. Books drowned. Books could be sold to a collector in Lowmark who'd never read past the first page of archaic verse.

But the seal had been real. That was the problem. He knew seals the way a smuggler knows locks—the circle closed, the line struck through it, Veyra's mark pressed deep enough into the leather to have been made by a stamp older than any living craftsman's hands. Forgeries were cheap. That particular weariness in the wax was not.

So he carried it down through the storm to Greyhollow, where Mother Cassia kept her chapel in the shell of an old granary, because if anyone in these mountains could tell him he was a fool, it was her.

* * *

The chapel smelled of tallow smoke and dried yarrow, and it was warm in the way that only poor places manage to be—heat hoarded carefully, spent grudgingly. Cassia was at her workbench grinding something that smelled bitter and green, her back curved like a question mark, her white braid thinning to silver thread at the end. She didn't turn when the door opened. She never did. She said she could tell Tamsin's footsteps from every other sinner's in the valley by the way they hesitated on the threshold.

"You're dripping on my floor," she said.

"Your floor has survived worse."

"My floor has survived you, which is worse." She set down her pestle and turned, and her eyes went straight past his face to the bundle in his arms, the way they always went to the wound before the man. "What have you got there?"

"A job gone strange." He unwrapped the oilcloth by degrees, stalling, and hated himself for stalling. "Smuggler's cargo out of Lowmark. I was supposed to deliver it to a buyer in the pass. Buyer didn't show. Storm did instead, so I sheltered in the old shrine up on Kestrel Ridge, and—" He stopped. Started again, drier: "And there was a book."

Cassia wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, the way she did when she wanted time to think without anyone noticing she wanted it. "A book," she repeated.

"A prophecy book." He laid it on her workbench between the mortar and a chipped icon of Veyra—a plain wooden disc, faceless, the circle-and-line carved where a mouth would be. "It names tonight's date. It names the shrine. And it describes—" His voice did something he didn't authorize. He cleared his throat. "It describes a man. Fair-haired. Scar through the left brow. Sworn to the silent god. Alone but never alone, it says, which is nonsense, because I've been alone my whole life except for the cat."

Duchess, offended, leapt onto the bench and sat on the book.

Cassia looked at the cat. At the book. At Tamsin. And Tamsin watched twenty years of knowing this woman rearrange itself behind her eyes, and understood, with a cold settling in his stomach, that she was not about to laugh.

"Cassia." He made himself say it lightly. "Tell me it's a forgery. Tell me some Lowmark charlatan stamped a fake seal on a fake prophecy to spook superstitious smugglers, and I'll sell it to a collector and drink the proceeds, and we'll both laugh about how frightened I was."

She didn't touch the book. She leaned over it instead, close, reading the cover without opening it, and her breath went shallow in a way he recognized from sickrooms. From deathbeds.

"This is not a forgery," she said.

"It could still be wrong—"

"Tamsin." Her hand came up and gripped his wrist, and her fingers were stronger than they had any right to be, knotted old rope that had held fast to faith through forty years of silence. "This seal. Look at it properly. Not the mark—the *press* of it. The depth of the strike." She tilted the book toward the lamplight. "Do you see? The wax isn't stamped. It's *poured*. This seal was made in a mold cut for one purpose only, and that purpose was the Rites of Closing. The imprisonment rites."

The word landed in the chapel like a dropped stone.

"The what?" he said, though he'd heard her say it, or half-said it, once, years ago, drunk on festival mead and talking around a thing she wouldn't name. *The old closing*, she'd called it, and then refused to explain, and he'd let it go because that was their arrangement—he didn't ask about her mysteries, and she didn't ask about his sins.

"The Rites of Closing." She said it now plainly, deliberately, like a woman setting down a weight she'd carried too long. "When Veyra was—when the god was sealed away. Before your grandmother's grandmother was born. The books that carry this seal were written inside the prison-shrine. They are not prophecies that were *made*, child. They are prophecies that were *left*. Instructions. Warnings." Her thumb traced the circle-and-line without quite touching it. "A letter from someone who knew they would not live to see it opened."

Tamsin sat down on the granary stool because his legs had decided the matter without consulting him.

"That's not possible," he said. "Veyra isn't sealed away. Veyra is—" He gestured at the faceless icon, at the whole quiet architecture of his life. "Veyra listens. That's the whole faith. The god hears everything and says nothing, and you make your peace with the nothing, and the nothing becomes a kind of answer. That's what you taught me. That's what I *built* on that."

"I know what I taught you."

"Then what are you telling me? That the god I've prayed to since I was six years old has been—" He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "That the silence wasn't silence? That it was a *wall*? That every prayer I ever gave went slamming up against stone while the god sat on the other side listening and couldn't—" His voice failed entirely this time.

Cassia let him fail. That was another thing she'd taught him: that silence was not emptiness, that a space held open for someone was itself a kind of speech. She put her hand flat on the back of his neck, the way she had when he was a boy and feverish, and waited.

"The silence was the prison," she said quietly. "Or the prison was the silence. I don't know which, and neither does anyone alive. But I know this." She turned the book over, finally touching it, and opened the cover with the care of a woman handling a relic or a blade. "I know why the god never answered you. Not because it didn't hear. Because it *couldn't speak*—and more than couldn't. Because its voice is the war, Tamsin. The old war. The hollow-born rose when Veyra last spoke freely, and the embers of that war have stayed cold for three hundred years for one reason only." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "Because our god chose to go mute rather than watch us burn each other again. The silence isn't absence. It's sacrifice. It has been holding the door shut with its own throat for three centuries."

The lamp guttered. Rain filled the pause against the roof, patient as a congregation.

Tamsin thought about every prayer he'd ever whispered into that silence. On his brother's grave. In the holds of smuggling ships. In the shrine on Kestrel Ridge, an hour ago, asking nothing, just saying *I'm here, same as always.* Twenty years of speaking into a well and hearing nothing, and telling himself that was holy, that was the discipline, that was the faith—

—and it had been a chained god pressing its ear to the other side of the wall.

"I used to think," he said, and had to stop, and start again, "that maybe the god ignored me specifically. Like the silence was personal. A punishment for something." He shook his head. "That's stupid."

"It's not stupid." Cassia's voice was fierce now, low and burning. "It's the most human thing there is. We build our whole lives around a silence and then we decide we deserved it." She closed the book and pushed it across the bench toward him. "Read it. All of it. Tonight. And then tell me what it says about the date."

"You already know what it says about the date."

"I want to hear you say it."

He looked at the book under Duchess's paws. He looked at the icon, the faceless circle, the line struck through where a mouth should be, and for the first time in his life the symbol looked less like serenity and more like a gag.

"It says," Tamsin said, "that on the night of the storm after the autumn equinox, a sworn man of Veyra, fair-haired, scarred, alone, will find the book. And that from that finding, everything follows. The breaking or the rekindling. The end of the silence or the end of the peace." He swallowed. "It doesn't say which. It gives both, like a ledger with two columns, and it doesn't tell you which column you're standing in."

"And the Inquisition," Cassia said. "You know they're in the mountains. You've seen their riders."

"Three days ago. White cloaks. Asking questions at the toll-house about shrines and relics." He'd thought nothing of it then. Smugglers thought nothing of anything until it stepped on them. Now the memory turned over in his chest like a body. "They're looking for something."

"They're looking for *you*. Or they will be. The Radiant Throne has spent three hundred years pretending Veyra died quietly, and the moment they learn otherwise—the moment they learn the god is not dead but *imprisoned*, and that its power can be seized—" She stopped herself, jaw tight. "They won't come to worship, Tamsin. They'll come with fire and ledgers. They'll burn every shrine in Thornfell to flush out the named one, and they will call it cleansing."

Outside, the storm was easing, the way storms do when they've delivered what they came for. Duchess jumped down from the bench and wound once around Tamsin's ankles, and he picked her up because his hands needed something to hold that wasn't shaking.

"So what do I do?" he asked. It came out smaller than he meant it to. "There's no column marked 'do nothing.' There's no page in this book for a man who wants to keep hauling brandy through the passes and stay invisible."

Cassia reached up and touched his scarred brow, gently, tracing the line of it the way she might trace the seal.

"You were never invisible," she said. "You were *unseen*. There's a difference, and you're about to learn it the hard way." Her hand dropped. "Go home. Sleep if you can. Read the book. And Tamsin—if riders in white cloaks come to Greyhollow, you come to me first. Whatever else happens, whatever the book says, you don't face them alone. Promise me."

He promised. It was the easiest lie he'd ever told her, because even then—even sitting in the warm yellow light of the only sanctuary he'd ever known—he could feel it coming, the way you feel weather in an old wound.

Three days later, the white cloaks crossed the pass.

Two weeks later, they burned the toll-house, and asked their questions louder.

And Tamsin Fairweather, who had spent his whole life believing the silence above him was peace, lay awake in the dark with the prophecy book open on his chest, listening to the mountain breathe, and understood at last that the silence had never been empty.

Something was on the other side of it. Something that knew his name.

And far below Greyhollow, deeper than any smuggler's tunnel ran, the pressure in the stone shifted again—that absence-of-sound, that held breath—and this time Duchess didn't wake, because she was already staring at the wall.
