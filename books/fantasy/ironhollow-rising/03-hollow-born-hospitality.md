## 3. Hollow-Born Hospitality

They took him at the ford, which was humiliating, because Tamsin had crossed that ford a hundred times and knew it was the one place in the Greywater where a man couldn't run.

The hollow-born came out of the willows the way smoke comes out of a banked fire—no sound, no warning, just a sudden thickening of the dark. One moment he was picking his way across the slick stones, the book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart; the next, three shapes stood on the banks on either side of him, tall and wrong-angled, their skin the grey-green of river stones, their eyes catching the moonlight like coins at the bottom of a well.

Tamsin did the arithmetic quickly. Three of them. One of him. A river at his back moving fast enough to drown a horse. He put his hand on his axe anyway, because a mercenary's pride is the last thing to freeze, and said, "Evening."

The tallest of them tilted its head. Up close he could see the seams of them—the faint ridges where bark had grown into flesh, or flesh into bark; he'd never been close enough before to wonder which. Hollow-born. The word his mother had used to frighten him, the word the miners spat, the word that meant everything under the mountain that hated the light.

"You carry something," the tall one said. Its voice was gravel rolled in honey. "Something old."

"Just cheese," Tamsin said. "Aged. Very old cheese."

It didn't laugh. None of them laughed. They simply closed the distance with that terrible unhurried grace, and when he swung the axe—one good honest swing, the kind that had settled a dozen bar fights and two contracts—it passed through the lead raider like a thought through a drunk man, meeting nothing but a swirl of shadow that smelled of loam and cold iron. Then hands like tree roots closed on his arms, and the world turned over.

* * *

He woke bound to a chair in a hall lit by lanterns of living moss, green-gold light pooling on floors of polished rootwood. His head throbbed. His axe was gone. The book, he noted with distant professional relief, was still against his chest—they hadn't taken it, which either meant they hadn't found it or they wanted him to keep it, and both possibilities were bad in interesting ways.

The hall was long and low, dug into the hillside rather than built upon it, and it was full of hollow-born. Dozens of them. They sat at trestle tables eating what looked like dark bread and roasted tubers, talking in low voices, and here and there among the grey-green adults moved children—small, quick, round-faced, laughing at some game played with knucklebones and a cup. Children.

Tamsin stared. He couldn't help it. In twenty years of soldiering and smuggling through Thornfell, he had heard a hundred stories about the hollow-born and not one of them had contained a child. The stories were all teeth and shadow and the taking of livestock, of travelers, occasionally of whole villages that had grown too proud. Nobody ever mentioned that the monsters might have families. That they might eat bread.

"Your face," said a voice beside him, dry as autumn leaves. "I have seen that face before, on men seeing snow for the first time."

She was old—not old the way humans were old, bent and shrunken, but old the way an oak is old: massive, weathered, patient beyond argument. Her hair was white as birch bark and fell in a heavy braid over one shoulder, threaded with small copper rings. She wore no crown, but every hollow-born in the hall angled toward her the way grass angles toward sun, and Tamsin understood without being told that he was looking at Seraq, matriarch of whatever this place was.

"I've seen snow," Tamsin said. "I grew up in Thornfell. It's overrated."

"So I have heard." She pulled up a stool and sat, folding her long hands, studying him with those coin-bright eyes. "You are the one who laughs at things that should frighten him. Mother Cassia speaks of you. 'My stubborn lamb,' she calls you. She says you fix her roof every autumn and refuse payment every spring."

"She owes me for the chimney," Tamsin said. "Two years running."

"And yet you go back." Something moved behind her eyes—not quite a smile, but the shape of one worn smooth by centuries. "Tell me, Tamsin Fairweather. What did you find in the shrine at Wolfjaw Pass?"

His blood went cold and quiet. "You know about that."

"We know about everything that happens above the stone. We have watched your kind for a very long time, the way you watch a fire you do not dare approach." She leaned forward. "We know about the book."

He could lie. He'd lied to magistrates, to guild factors, to an Inquisitor once, God rest his nerve. But there was something about the moss-light and the bread-smell and the sound of children laughing that made lying feel like tracking mud through a temple.

"It named me," he said finally. "By date. By deed. By—" He stopped.

"By description," Seraq finished softly. "'A man of the valleys, silent-sworn, who walks between.'" She recited it the way other people recited scripture—which, he supposed, was exactly what it was. "Yes. We have waited a hundred years for that verse to come true. Our grandmothers memorized it. Our mothers taught it to us as lullabies." Her voice roughened, just slightly, like a wheel finding a rut. "Do you understand what that means, human? Every hollow-born child in this hall grew up knowing that someday, someone would come walking down from the mountains carrying the end of our chains. Or the end of us. We did not know which. We only knew we would know him when we saw him."

"And you think it's me."

"I think the book thinks so. And the book has never been wrong." She rose, and the whole hall seemed to rise with her by degrees, attention flowing upward like water finding its level. "You expected death at the ford. I saw it in your shoulders when you reached for the axe. You thought we would kill you, or worse."

"The thought occurred."

"What would your people have done," she asked, "if they caught one of mine alone at a crossing?"

Tamsin didn't answer. He didn't need to. Everyone in Thornfell knew what happened to hollow-born caught above ground: rope, or fire, or the Inquisition's white wagons if you were lucky enough to be worth a bounty. He thought of the miners' songs, the ones with the cheerful choruses. He thought of the time he'd been fourteen and watched a crowd in Lowmarket throw stones at a hollow-born trader until what was left wasn't recognizable as anything at all, and how he'd stood in that crowd and done nothing, because doing nothing was what kept a man invisible, and invisibility was what kept a man alive.

"There it is," Seraq said gently, reading his silence the way Cassia read weather. "That look. Guilt is a strange thing on your faces—you wear it like a coat that doesn't fit."

* * *

They fed him. That was the part that undid him, later, when he lay sleepless in the guest alcove they'd given him—a real bed, woven of moss and willow, softer than any he'd paid for in years. They fed him first, before questions, before terms, before anything. A bowl of stew rich with mushrooms and some dark meat he decided not to ask about, and the bread he'd smelled earlier, dense and faintly sweet, and tea brewed from pine needles and honey.

An old hollow-born served him, one whose bark-skin had gone silver-grey with age, and when Tamsin took the bowl the old one pressed his hand briefly, two fingers to the back of Tamsin's wrist, and murmured something in a language like wind through a hollow reed. When Tamsin looked up, confused, the old one's eyes were wet.

"He says you have her hands," Seraq translated from across the table. "The hands of a maker, not a breaker. He has been waiting a long time to serve that kind of hand."

"I'm a smuggler," Tamsin said. "I break locks. Mostly."

"Mm." She sat across from him while he ate, and little by little, between mouthfuls, the story came out of her the way sap comes out of a tapped tree—slowly, then all at once.

How the hollow-born had not always lived under the mountain. How there had been a war, long ago, before the human kingdoms had names—war between the deep folk and the surface folk, war so old that both sides remembered it differently, except everyone remembered it ending badly. How Veyra, the silent god, the god of the quiet places and the held tongue, had ended it—not by victory, but by going still. By taking the war's fury into herself and holding it, the way a dam holds a river, and letting her own voice be the price. Silence as sacrifice. Silence as seal.

"Your priests think she died," Seraq said. "That the silence is a grave. We know better. The silence is a prison—and she is inside it, holding the door shut from within, and she has held it for a thousand years." Her hands tightened around her cup until the wood creaked. "And now your Radiant Throne has found the door. Their Inquisition digs like maggots toward Ironhollow. If they reach her—if they kill her instead of freeing her, or free her as a weapon—"

"The war starts again," Tamsin said quietly. "Either way."

"Either way," Seraq agreed. "Unless the prophecy is true. Unless there is a third road, and a man of the valleys walking it." She looked at him across the moss-light, and her ancient eyes were not hard at all; they were hopeful, and hope on a face that old was more frightening than any threat. "We will not force you, Tamsin Fairweather. Understand that. No chains, no knives. Stay as our guest. Eat our bread. Watch our children play. And when you are ready, tell us what the book told you in the storm."

* * *

Later, unable to sleep, he wandered the edge of the great hall and came upon the children again—or two of them, awake past bedtime, huddled by a brazier playing that knucklebone game. The smaller one looked up as he passed. Round face, big moonlit eyes, a smudge of ash on one cheek like any miner's kid in Lowmarket.

"You're the human," the child said. Not afraid. Curious, the way kids were curious about anything new.

"Last I checked."

"My gran says you're the one from the song." The child held up a knucklebone carved with a spiral. "Do you want to see? I can roll a six almost every time."

And Tamsin Fairweather, who had spent thirty years perfecting the art of belonging nowhere, sat down on the warm stones beside a hollow-born child and held out his hand for the bones.

Somewhere in the dark beneath them, a god held a door shut. Somewhere above, an Inquisition sharpened itself. But in the green-gold light of the brazier, a six was rolled, and a cheat's trick learned, and laughter shared between species that had spent a thousand years teaching their children to fear each other—and Tamsin thought, with a dread that felt almost like joy: *Oh no. I love them already.*

In the morning, Seraq came to him with a map of tunnels older than any kingdom, and one question:

"How soon can you take us home to Ironhollow?"
