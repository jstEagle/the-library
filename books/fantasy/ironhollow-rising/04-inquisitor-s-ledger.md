## 4. Inquisitor's Ledger

The cipher broke at dawn, as all wicked things eventually must.

Inquisitor Dain Marrow sat in the scriptorium of the Radiant Throne's garrison at Greywatch, his hands stained to the second knuckle with iron-gall ink, and watched the last glyph of the smuggler's book surrender. It had taken him eleven days. Eleven days of lamplight and vinegar and the slow, surgical peeling back of a substitution scheme so old it predated the Throne itself—a merchant's cipher, of all things, the kind used to hide shipping manifests and debts. The heretic who had written this had hidden a prophecy in the language of ledgers, and Marrow found that fitting. Everything, in the end, was accounting.

He turned the page with tweezers, though the vellum was sound enough. Carelessness was how relics got ruined, and ruined relics told no truths.

*On the night the storm-wolf circles Thornfell's crown,* the decoded line read, *a man of ash-grey eyes shall carry the book that names him. He walks unseen among you. He is the hinge.*

Marrow read it three times. Then he dipped his pen and copied it out in his own fair hand into the ledger he kept for such findings—leather-bound, brass-cornered, every page ruled and dated. On the facing page he wrote the operational note: *Storm over Thornfell peaks, four nights past. Cross-reference shrine registries for sightings of grey-eyed males, age thirty to forty-five, unattached, itinerant occupation.*

Itinerant occupation. Smugglers, peddlers, mercenaries. Men who walked unseen. The phrase pleased Marrow the way a well-cut key pleases a locksmith.

He did not smile. Smiling was for men who were surprised by their work.

* * *

"You've been down here three days," said Brother Ostell from the doorway, holding two cups of chicory that steamed like small dying animals in the cold. "The Prior asks after you."

"The Prior can ask the mountain," Marrow said, without looking up. "It answers more readily."

Ostell laughed—Ostell always laughed, a young man still charmed by his own company—and set one cup on the corner of the desk, where it would go cold and be poured out later, as they always were. He leaned over Marrow's shoulder, and Marrow allowed it. There was value in letting subordinates see the machinery working. Awe kept them obedient longer than fear did, and fear kept them longer than love. He filed that observation too. He filed everything.

"That's the Fairweather book?" Ostell asked. "The one the toll-wardens pulled off the dead courier?"

"The same." Marrow touched the cover without reverence. Calfskin, salt-stained, cheaply tooled. A book like ten thousand books. That was the genius of it—the named one had been carrying his own damnation in a satchel for who knew how long, indistinguishable from cargo. "Tell me what you see, Brother."

Ostell squinted at the decoded lines. "A prophecy. Heretical, obviously. The old war, the hollow-born, some chosen figure—" He stopped. His throat moved. "Grey eyes. Inquisitor, there are perhaps two hundred men in Thornfell with grey eyes."

"Fewer than that who walk unseen," Marrow said. "Fewer still who'd be carrying this. And fewer again who survived the storm-wolf night on the high passes and came down alive." He closed the ledger. "We are not looking for two hundred men, Brother. We are looking for one, and he has already told us everything we need to know about himself: he hides well, he moves through places built for hiding, and he prays."

"Prays?"

"He sheltered somewhere on that night. Everyone does, in a storm like that. And in Thornfell, the only roofs that don't charge are shrines." Marrow rose, joints cracking with the particular music of a body that knelt too often on stone floors. "He went to ground in one of Veyra's silent houses. Which means the shrines will remember him. Shrines always remember."

"And if they don't?"

Marrow took his coat from its hook—grey wool, cut plain, the only ornament the silver sunburst at the collar, polished dull so it wouldn't catch light or attention. "Then we remind them what remembering costs."

* * *

They burned the shrine at Hollowmere on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were market days and crowds made doctrine legible.

Marrow stood in the square while his acolytes stacked the pews, the prayer-benches, the little carved owls the faithful left as offerings to their wordless god—Veyra's sigil, the owl with no voice, the bird that sees and never calls. The villagers watched from doorways with the specific silence of people doing arithmetic: the cost of protest weighed against the cost of grief. Marrow respected arithmetic. It was the closest most souls came to honesty.

"Brothers and sisters of the valley," he said, and his voice carried the way voices do when they have never once doubted themselves. "You have kept a god here who does not speak. Ask yourselves why. A god who cannot answer is either absent or imprisoned, and either way, your devotion has fed an empty house while the Radiant Throne fed you nothing at all—not bread, not protection, not truth." He let that settle. "Today we correct the account."

The fire caught the dry pine with a sound like applause.

He watched the faces as the smoke went up. Most were blank—that stunned blankness of the suddenly dispossessed. But three faces, scattered through the crowd, showed something else. Not rage. Recognition. The look of people who knew exactly why the owl carvings mattered, and who now understood that someone else knew it too.

Marrow marked those faces. He did not approach them. Approaching was interrogation, and interrogation taught people to lie better. He simply wrote their descriptions in his ledger that evening—*woman, miller's widow, left hand missing two fingers; boy, fourteen, red hair; old man, coat mended with fishing net*—and set watchers on their doors. Guilt, given time and privacy, always walked toward the thing it was guilty about.

By Friday, the widow had sent a message up-valley by a tinker. By Sunday, the tinker was in Marrow's custody, and by Monday he had confessed to carrying letters between three villages and a priestess called Cassia, who kept the mother-shrine at Ashenford and who, the tinker swore through split lips, knew nothing except everything.

"A priestess," Marrow said. He wrote the name down and underlined it once, cleanly. "Of course. The named one would need a confessor. Someone whose silence is professional."

He gave the order for Ashenford that night, but he did not lead it himself. Not yet. First there was one more entry to make, and it was the entry that had kept him awake—not with fear, Marrow did not fear, but with the exquisite itch of a pattern almost complete.

He returned to the cipher book and read the marginalia again. The smuggler who had owned it had annotated the prophecy in a shaking hand: *the hinge, the hinge, gods keep us, it's under Ironhollow, it was always under Ironhollow.* And beneath that, older, in different ink entirely—a fragment Marrow had nearly dismissed as stain:

*The silence is not absence. The silence is the lock.*

Marrow sat very still in the lamplight.

Every inquisitor learned the orthodox history: Veyra the Silent had died in the Sundering, voiceless and unmourned, and good riddance, for a god of silence was a god of nothing, and nothing could not be worshipped by right-thinking people. Marrow had recited that history at his ordination. He had believed it the way one believes the floor will hold.

But he had spent twenty years reading heretical documents, and heresy had taught him the one lesson orthodoxy never could: that the Church lies best when it is bored. Nobody invents elaborate prison rites for a dead god. Dead gods get graves and forgetfulness. Only living things get chains—and chains, real chains, divine ones, require wardens, and wardens require shrines, and shrines require congregations who pray without knowing what they are feeding.

The whole faith of the silent god, laid end to end across Thornfell, was not a religion.

It was a lock's mechanism. Every worshipper a pin. Every priestess a spring. And somewhere beneath Ironhollow, behind all of it, something that had once been a god was holding the old war's embers cold with nothing but its own swallowed voice.

Marrow felt no horror. He wanted to be clear about that, later, if there ever was a later and anyone to tell. He felt the clean bright satisfaction of a man who has finally seen the true shape of a room he'd been cleaning for years. If the silence was a lock, then the lock could be opened. And if the lock could be opened, then whatever power had been spent keeping it shut—all that divinity, banked and hoarded and dripping interest for a century—was sitting in the dark under a mountain, waiting for an heir.

The Throne would want it. The Throne always wanted. But Marrow had long ago stopped pretending his ambitions ended at the Throne's altar rail.

He blew out the lamp, because dawn was coming and dawn was for action, and wrote one final line in the ledger before the light failed him entirely:

*Ride for Ashenford at first bell. Take the priestess alive. She is the bait; the named one loves his god, and love is a debt that always comes due.*

Then, after a moment, in smaller script, almost tender:

*And after the priestess—Ironhollow. I will go down myself. Some doors should only be opened by someone who understands what they've been holding shut.*

Outside, the first snow of the season was beginning to fall on Greywatch, softening the garrison walls, erasing the road south, laying a white page over all of Thornfell.

Marrow watched it come down and thought, with something adjacent to peace: *Let it fall. Ash writes more legibly anyway.*
