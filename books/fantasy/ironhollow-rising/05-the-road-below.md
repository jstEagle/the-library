## 5. The Road Below

The tunnels beneath Thornfell had no name, which was the first thing you needed to know about them. Named places got found. Named places got taxed, raided, mapped by the Inquisition's cartographers and burned off their maps a season later. The smugglers who'd cut these passages three generations back had understood that anonymity was a kind of armor, so they'd left the stone to speak for itself, and the stone said nothing at all.

Tamsin walked at the front with a lantern turned down to a coal's whisper and forty hollow-born behind him in the dark.

Forty. He still hadn't gotten used to the number. When Seraq had said *my people*, he'd pictured a war band—hard-faced raiders with iron teeth and old grudges. What he'd gotten was a village. Elders with canes. A woman carrying an infant wrapped against her chest, its small gray face scrunched in sleep. Children, half a dozen of them, moving through the black like they'd been born to it, because they had.

"Your map," Seraq said beside him, her voice pitched low out of habit rather than need. Hollow-born eyes didn't need light. She could have walked this tunnel blindfolded and backward. "How much farther?"

"To the deep junction? Half a day, if the collapses haven't shifted things." He tapped the oilskin sheet rolled under his arm without unrolling it. "After that, I'm honest with you: I don't know. Nobody's been past the sealed doors since before I was born."

"And yet the book says there is a road."

"The book says a lot of things." He kept his voice dry. It was easier than letting it shake. "That's the trouble with prophecy. It never has to carry its own pack."

Something that might have been amusement moved across Seraq's face—a shifting of the planes of it, the bone-ridges above her eyes tilting. Hollow-born laughter, he'd learned, lived in the face more than the throat.

They walked on. The dark pressed close and warm, smelling of wet limestone and old torch-smoke and, faintly, of the people behind him—woodsmoke and mineral and something green, like moss after rain. His own kind smelled of sweat and fear mostly. He tried not to draw conclusions from that. He failed.

* * *

They made camp at the third hour in a vaulted chamber where an underground stream ran shallow over pale stones. Tamsin got a fire going in a ring of old smuggler bricks—ventilation was good here, the air pulling upward through some unseen shaft—and the hollow-born arranged themselves around it with the ease of long practice. They didn't huddle toward the light the way humans did. They sat just outside it, comfortable in the rim of shadow, and watched the flames like it was a curiosity someone had brought along for the journey.

Tamsin was rationing out dried plums and hardtack when he noticed the boy.

He'd seen him earlier—small even for his age, whatever his age was, with the fine gray skin and faintly luminous eyes all the young ones had, eyes like coins at the bottom of a well. The boy had stationed himself near Tamsin's pack with the studied casualness of every hungry child in history, pretending tremendous interest in a crack in the floor.

"You want one," Tamsin said. Not a question.

"I'm not allowed to beg." The boy's voice was solemn as a funeral. "Seraq says begging is for people who've given up on being useful."

"That's grim advice from a matriarch."

"It's excellent advice." The boy held out both hands anyway, cupped, ready.

Tamsin put three plums in them. "Name?"

"Pip." The boy bit into a plum with sudden ferocity, as though it might be revoked. "Pip of the Understone line. That's my mother's line. My father was human."

Tamsin went very still, the way you do when a door opens somewhere inside you that you'd bricked over years ago.

"Yeah?" he managed.

"He left before I hatched. Mother says he was tall and lied beautifully." Pip shrugged, a small sharp motion. "I don't remember him. You're not supposed to miss what you never had, but I do anyway. Is that stupid?"

"No," Tamsin said. His own voice came out rougher than he intended. "No, it's not stupid."

He thought of Wren. Of course he did. Wren, who'd been four years younger and two heads taller by the end, who'd followed Tamsin into the Thornfell passes at fourteen because anywhere Tamsin went was automatically the center of the world. Wren, who'd died in a rockslide on a job so minor Tamsin couldn't even remember what they'd been paid, holding his brother's hand while the light went out of him, saying—this was the part Tamsin never told anyone—saying *at least it's dark, Tam. You always said the dark doesn't lie.*

He looked at Pip's small gray hands around the plum pit and felt something crack open in his chest that he'd have sworn was scar tissue.

"Eat," he said. "And tomorrow you walk up front with me. If we hit trouble, I want someone useful where I can see him."

Pip's whole face changed—the bone-ridges lifting, the coin-bright eyes going wide. For a moment he looked less like a child waiting to be sent away and more like a child who'd been invited to stay.

"Seraq says you're the named one," Pip said carefully. "From the book. That you're going to save us."

Tamsin laughed, short and ugly. "Seraq says a lot of things."

"Do you believe her?"

He should have given the boy something polished, something mercenary and deflecting. Instead he heard himself say: "I believe the book knew my birthday. That's not faith. That's just bad luck."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Bad luck can turn," he decided. "Mother says luck is just a road you haven't finished walking."

"Your mother sounds smarter than me."

"She is," Pip said, with devastating sincerity, and Tamsin laughed again—really laughed, this time, and heard the sound come back off the stone like it belonged down here, like the tunnels had been waiting years for someone to make a noise worth keeping.

* * *

The second day, the tunnel narrowed.

It happened gradually and then all at once—the wide smuggler passage shrinking to a crawl where the ceiling came down to meet the floor, where the walls sweated cold and the air went thin and mineral-tasting. Tamsin went through first, lantern between his teeth, testing each handhold, and when he emerged into the low chamber beyond he lay on his back breathing hard and listening to the sound of forty people squeezing through a crack in the world.

Pip came through last, wriggling like an eel, and popped up beside him grinning.

"You're bleeding," the boy observed.

"Just my knuckles." Tamsin flexed his hand. "Stone takes its toll."

"My mother bled for three days getting our line out of Kell's Reach. She says blood is how you pay the road. Everything alive charges toll; you just have to decide what currency." Pip peered at him. "You talk to yourself when you think nobody's listening. Did you know that?"

Tamsin stared at him. "I do not."

"You do. Last night you said, 'Wren would've hated this,' and then you said, 'no he wouldn't, he'd have loved it.'" Pip's voice was gentle in the way children are gentle when they repeat your secrets without knowing they're knives. "Who's Wren?"

For a long moment Tamsin looked at the lantern flame instead of the boy. Down here, in the honest dark, lying seemed pointless. Lying seemed almost blasphemous.

"My brother," he said finally. "Dead six years. He was—" He stopped. Started again. "He was the best of the two of us, and I spent so long keeping my head down after he died that I forgot there was any point in standing up. That's why I took smuggling jobs. Invisible work for an invisible man."

"But you're not invisible." Pip frowned, puzzled. "We see you. We've been seeing you since the shrine. You glow a little, you know. Just at the edges. Like banked coals."

Tamsin went cold all over. "I what?"

"Glow." Pip said it like it was obvious. "Since the night of the storm. Seraq noticed first. She says the god's silence sits differently on you than on other people. She says it sits on you like—" he screwed up his face, hunting for the word, "—like a hand on a shoulder. Like recognition."

Tamsin thought about twenty years of prayers thrown into silence. Twenty years of lighting candles at shrines where no answer ever came, and doing it anyway, because Veyra's quiet had always felt less like absence and more like attention. He'd told himself that was a fool's comfort. A smuggler's superstition.

But if the god had been imprisoned—if the silence was chains and not death—then maybe every prayer had landed somewhere. Maybe the silence had been listening the whole time, straining toward the one voice that kept talking.

"Tam?" Pip said. "Did I say something wrong?"

"No." Tamsin cleared his throat. "No, kid. You said something right. That's worse."

* * *

On the third day they reached the deep junction, and the map ended.

Tamsin stood before the wall where his oilskin chart simply stopped—a blank space beyond the last surveyed gallery, marked only with a question mark in his own handwriting and, beneath it, in faded charcoal from some dead smuggler's hand, a single word: *LISTEN.*

Behind him, forty hollow-born waited in the dark. Ahead, the stone was silent.

Except it wasn't. Now that he stood still—now that he let himself actually listen—he caught it. Faint as a heartbeat through a wall. A rhythm in the rock, deep and slow, too regular to be water, too patient to be anything alive.

Or too patient to be anything mortal.

"There's something down there," he said quietly. Seraq moved up beside him, and even she, with her dark-sighted eyes, tilted her head as if straining.

"I feel it too," she murmured. "Like a drum played under the world."

Pip slipped his small gray hand into Tamsin's bleeding one, entirely without ceremony, the way children do when they've decided a thing is settled.

"Is it the god?" the boy whispered.

Tamsin looked down at their joined hands—his human fingers, cracked and scarred, wrapped around something that glowed faintly at the edges, something that had chosen to walk beside him into the dark. Found family taking root in the dark, he thought. Cassia would have liked that phrasing. Cassia would have called it grace.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But it knows we're here."

He raised the lantern, and far down the unmapped black, something answered—not in words, not yet, but in a slow pulse of warmth through the soles of his boots, like a door unlocking somewhere very far below.

The road below had begun to listen back.
