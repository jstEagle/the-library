## 6. Where Gods Go to Sleep

The mountain did not want them.

Tamsin felt it in his teeth before he felt it anywhere else—a low, grinding reluctance in the stone itself, as though Ironhollow were a sleeping animal being asked to swallow something it had no appetite for. The smugglers' tunnels ended a mile back, collapsed in some old quake or older war, and from there Seraq's people climbed. They climbed through scree fields that sang when the wind crossed them, through a forest of black pines bent like old men listening to a confession no one would repeat, and up a stair cut into the living rock so long ago that the steps had worn into shallow bowls, each one holding a little cup of snowmelt that caught the dying light.

Pip walked ahead of him, small feet finding the worn hollows exactly, as if the mountain remembered her kind and made room.

"Your god lives here?" she asked, for the fourth time since the tunnels.

"Sleeps here," Tamsin said. "There's a difference."

"What difference?"

He considered the question honestly, because Pip deserved honesty even when the honest answer was ugly. "A sleeper can wake up glad to see you," he said. "Or not."

She frowned at that, chewing on it the way she chewed on everything—her dried meat, her questions, the world entire—and then scrambled up three more steps without an answer, which was its own kind of answer. Behind them, Seraq moved with the unhurried grace of someone who had walked battlefields and nurseries both, her grey-skinned hands steady on the rock, her four eyes half-lidded against the wind. Twenty-three hollow-born had come out of Thornfell's deep places to follow a human mercenary up a dead god's staircase. Tamsin still didn't understand it. He suspected he never fully would.

The last stretch of stair passed beneath an arch of stone that had been carved and then deliberately defaced—faces hammered smooth, inscriptions chiseled into senseless scars. Vandalism, Cassia would have called it. Erasure, Seraq called it. Tamsin thought it looked like grief. Someone had loved what stood here once, and someone else had hated it enough to spend years unmaking it, and neither of them had been able to bring themselves to pull the arch down entirely.

Beyond the arch: the shrine.

It opened without warning, the way the best secrets do—one moment cliff face, the next a cavern vast enough to hold weather. Stalactites hung like the ribs of some enormous patient beast, and between them, down in the dark, light moved. Not torchlight. Not the cold blue of witchfire. Something slower, warmer, the color of embers deciding whether to become ash or flame, breathing in and out along the cavern walls in long tides.

"Veyra's blood," Seraq murmured, and knelt.

"It's not blood." Tamsin heard his own voice come out flat, defensive, the voice of a man protecting something by refusing to name it. "Cassia always said it was just the mountain. Old heat trapped in the stone."

"And you believed her?"

"I wanted to believe *something* was true about my whole life," he said, "so yes."

Seraq rose and touched his shoulder—lightly, the way you touch a thing you're afraid might break or bite. "Then go down and ask it yourself."

* * *

The descent into the shrine proper took an hour, down a spiral gallery where the walls were carved floor to ceiling with figures. Tamsin knew these carvings. He'd grown up on their copies in Mother Cassia's chapel—smaller, cruder, safe behind candle smoke. Here they were naked and enormous. The silent god, faceless, hands open. Humans kneeling. Hollow-born kneeling. Both peoples reaching toward those open hands, and between the two crowds, carved smaller than all the rest, a single figure standing upright—not kneeling, not reaching, just *standing*, looking back over its shoulder at the viewer.

Tamsin had never noticed that figure in Cassia's chapel copies. He stopped in front of it now, snowmelt dripping off his cloak, and felt the strange vertigo of recognizing himself in stone older than any nation he could name.

"That's not me," he said aloud, because saying things aloud kept the fear at a manageable size. "I'm not in your book. I'm a smuggler. I'm a footnote. I'm the guy who carries other people's stories across borders and doesn't get one of his own."

The ember-light breathed. In. Out. It did not answer, and the silence was so familiar it was almost a comfort—the same silence that had answered twenty years of prayers, ten thousand nights of *are you there, are you there, are you there*. Tamsin had built his whole faith on that silence, the way you build a house on bedrock. You told yourself the silence meant steadiness. You told yourself a god who spoke would be a god who lied.

He kept walking.

At the bottom of the gallery the cavern floor opened into a bowl of black glass, smooth as a frozen lake, and at its center stood the prison.

He'd imagined it a hundred ways on the road below—a cage, a tomb, a throne of chains. It was none of those. It was a door. A simple door, freestanding, framed in silvered wood gone grey with age, standing alone on the glass with nothing behind it but more darkness. Chains ran from its frame into the stone at eight points, and every chain was thick as Tamsin's waist and furred with frost, and every chain hummed. Not rang. Hummed. A note too low to hear properly, felt instead in the sternum, in the soft parts behind the eyes.

The ember-light gathered thickest around that door, pooling at its base like worshippers who had forgotten, over centuries, how to leave.

Tamsin walked out onto the glass. His boots made no sound. Of course they didn't. This was Veyra's house, and Veyra's house was quiet.

He stopped ten feet from the door, close enough to see that the frost on the chains wasn't frost at all, up close—it was fine white feathers, millions of them, grown over the metal like moss over a fallen tree. He put his hand in his pocket and found the prophecy book there, where it had lived against his hip for weeks now, warm as a second heartbeat. *On the night the mountain breathes fireless flame,* it said, in ink that had waited a century for him, *the named one stands before the sealed door, and the god within shall know its own.*

"You know me, then," Tamsin said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "That's the joke, right? A hundred years of prophecy and it picks the one man who spent his whole life talking to a wall."

Silence. The light breathed. Somewhere far above, wind moved through the stalactites and made a sound like a congregation shifting in their seats.

Tamsin sat down. Right there on the black glass, cross-legged, the way he'd sat in Cassia's chapel as a boy, the way he'd sat in ditches and taverns and jail cells, talking to a god who never talked back. It seemed suddenly important to do this properly. To give it the respect of routine.

"I don't know if you can hear me," he said. "I don't know if you could ever hear me, or if I've been talking to myself for twenty years, which—honestly, wouldn't surprise anyone who knows me. But I'm here. I brought them." He jerked his chin upward, toward the gallery, toward the twenty-three hollow-born waiting in the dark with their hope like drawn knives. "Seraq thinks you're going to free them. Marrow thinks you're a weapon somebody forgot to pick up. Cassia—" His voice caught. He let it. There was no one here to see, except possibly a god. "Cassia is begging you with her whole broken body to stay asleep. And me."

He looked at his hands. Smuggler's hands. Scarred knuckles, a missing fingernail, ink stains from forging bills of lading. Hands that had held a dying brother in a pass above Greywater and promised him, stupidly, uselessly, *it's going to be all right.*

"I don't know what I think," he said. "That's the truth. Everyone keeps telling me what I'm for. The book says I'll break the war or rekindle it, and nobody—*nobody*—asked what I want. So I'm asking. First time in twenty years, I'm actually asking instead of just talking at you." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and stared at the feathered chains until his eyes burned. "Are you there? Are you *in* there? Is there anything left of you in there, or am I about to lead twenty-three people and a dying priestess up a mountain for a locked door and a bad feeling?"

The humming of the chains changed.

It was subtle—a shift in pitch, the way a held breath shifts before a word—and Tamsin felt it before he heard it, a warmth blooming behind his breastbone like a swallowed coal. The ember-light around the door drew inward, gathering, tightening, rising off the glass in slow ribbons that wound toward him and stopped, hovering, waiting.

And then, inside his own skull, in a voice that was not a voice—more like remembering, more like the moment after crying when the ache finally breaks and lets you breathe—he heard:

***Choose.***

One word. One word after twenty years of silence, and it wasn't *yes*, it wasn't *free me*, it wasn't *kill me* or *save them* or any of the answers everyone else had already decided on his behalf.

It was *choose.* As if the god had been listening all along. As if the silence had never been absence at all, but patience—the patience of a parent who knows the child has to walk to them on his own legs or the arriving means nothing.

Tamsin laughed. It came out wet and cracked and it turned halfway through into something that wasn't laughing at all, and he sat on the black glass in the house of his silent god and wept like the boy he'd been in Cassia's chapel, like the man he'd been over his brother's body, like everything in between, and the ribbons of light settled over his shoulders like a blanket, like wings, like arms.

"Oh," he managed, finally, wiping his face with a sleeve that was already soaked. "Oh, you absolute bastard. Twenty *years.*"

The warmth pulsed once—amusement, unmistakably amusement—and Tamsin laughed again, really laughed, and the sound went rolling up through the galleries past the carved figure standing apart from the kneeling crowds, and for one impossible moment the whole mountain felt less like a prison and more like a home someone had been keeping warm for him.

He got to his feet. His legs shook. He didn't care.

"All right," he said. "All right. I choose. Just—give me a minute to decide what."

He turned back toward the stairs to tell Seraq, to tell Pip, to start the work of figuring out what a man does with a god's permission—

And stopped.

Far above, at the mouth of the shrine, where the defaced arch stood against the darkening sky, lights were moving. Not ember-light. Torchlight. Many torches, descending the worn stair in disciplined order, and thin across the distance came a sound Tamsin knew from a hundred bad nights: the creak of armor, and voices singing hymns in unison.

At their head, a lantern burned white and merciless, and beneath it Tamsin could just make out the silhouette of a tall man consulting a ledger by its light, checking entries, ticking boxes, as though the mountain itself were a list of errors soon to be corrected.

Inquisitor Dain Marrow had found the door.

And Tamsin had maybe an hour to decide what he was willing to burn to keep it shut—or open it himself, and let everything out.
