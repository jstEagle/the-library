## 7. Ash Over Thornfell

The smoke reached Tamsin before the news did.

He was two miles down the smugglers' road, Pip chattering at his elbow about the way hollow-born eyes caught lantern light like a cat's, when the wind turned and brought it to him—burnt cedar, burnt thatch, and beneath both something sweeter and fouler: the smell of a shrine burning. Incense rendered into poison.

Pip stopped mid-sentence. "What is that?"

Tamsin didn't answer. He was already running.

* * *

Inquisitor Dain Marrow stood in the courtyard of the valley shrine and watched his men work with the satisfaction of a man watching a wound being cleaned.

The shrine of Veyra was a modest thing—a stone hall sunk into the hillside, its door carved with the god's sign: an open hand, palm outward, saying nothing. Marrow had knelt before such doors as a boy, in another life, in another name. He remembered the silence they taught there. He had mistaken it, then, for peace. He knew better now. Silence was not peace. Silence was rot given room to spread.

"Bring her out," he said.

Brother Halven dragged Mother Cassia from the smoking doorway by the arm, and Marrow felt a flicker of distaste—not at the roughness, but at the necessity of it. He would have preferred she walk. Dignity in cleansing mattered. But the old woman had refused to leave her altar, had wrapped her arms around the base of it like a child around a mother's leg, and so Halven had pried her loose, and now here she was on her knees in the ash-drifted snow, her white braid coming undone, her breath steaming in ragged pulls.

"You are Cassia," Marrow said. "Mother of this house."

"I am." Her voice was steady. He gave her credit for that. "And you are the man who burns libraries because he is afraid of what's written in them."

Marrow crouched to her level. Up close, her eyes were the pale grey of river ice, and they did not flinch. Good. He had no use for cowards, even broken ones.

"I burn nothing out of fear," he said. "I burn to make things plain. A ledger with two entries cannot balance. A world with two truths cannot stand. Your silent god has been a second truth in these mountains for three hundred years, Mother, and I have come to strike it from the book."

"You don't understand what you're striking." For the first time her voice cracked—not with fear, he thought, but with something worse. Pity. "You've read the prophecy. You know what sleeps below Ironhollow. You think you're snuffing a candle. You're standing on a powder keg with a torch, and you're calling the dark your enemy."

"The Radiant Throne does not deal in riddles." Marrow straightened. "It deals in light. And light, Mother, is very simple. It shows things as they are. So let us be plain: somewhere in these mountains is a man named in your forbidden book. A mercenary. Fair-haired, they say. Sworn to your silent god since childhood. The named one." He turned to Halven. "How many shrines remain on the western slope?"

"Four, Inquisitor."

"Then we visit four. And at each one, we ask this question—" he looked back down at Cassia, and smiled the thin smile he saved for sermons, "—until someone answers it honestly."

* * *

Tamsin came over the ridge above the valley as the sun went down, and the sight below stopped him like a hand around his throat.

The shrine was still standing—but only just. Its roof was half gone, black ribs open to the sky, and snow fell through the hole onto embers and hissed. His brothers of the order were lined up against the far wall, kneeling in the ash, guarded by Throne soldiers in their lacquered white. And in the center of the courtyard, on a chair carried up from the refectory, sat a man in grey-and-gold, reading a small book as calmly as a merchant reading accounts.

Tamsin knew that book. He had slept with it under his head for a month. He had left it hidden in a hollow wall in Seraq's camp, wrapped in oilcloth, three days ago.

His stomach dropped through the mountain.

*No.* Not possible. Unless—unless one of Seraq's people had talked, or been taken, or—

"He has it," Pip whispered beside him. The boy had followed him against every order, and Tamsin hadn't the heart or the time to send him back. "How does he have it?"

"I don't know." Tamsin's voice came out flat and strange. Below, the man in grey closed the book, rose, and said something Tamsin couldn't hear. One of the soldiers stepped forward and took Mother Cassia by the hair.

Everything in Tamsin went very quiet and very hot, the way it did before a fight—the old mercenary calm, the calm that had kept him alive through twelve years of contracts. But underneath the calm, for the first time he could remember, there was something else. Something with teeth.

That was Cassia down there. That was the woman who had fed him when he was nine years old and feral with grief, who had taught him the hand-signs of Veyra because spoken prayers felt too loud after his brother died, who had never once asked him to be anything other than what he was. Twenty years of her quiet kindness, and it had never once occurred to him that it could be taken away. That was the trick of safety, he understood now, watching a soldier press a knife to a priestess's throat: you only ever learned how much you loved a thing when you saw it about to be destroyed.

He had thought himself invisible. Small, careful, unremarkable—a man who moved through the world like smoke through a keyhole. He saw now that invisibility had always been a lie he told himself so he wouldn't have to admit he cared. You could only hide from a war you refused to claim.

Below, the Inquisitor raised his hand, and the knife pressed harder, and a thread of red ran down Cassia's white collar.

Tamsin stood up out of the rocks.

* * *

Marrow heard the footsteps before he saw the man—which was itself remarkable, because his soldiers, six of them, ringed and armed, had heard nothing at all.

The named one walked into the courtyard out of the falling dusk like a man walking into his own parlor. Fair-haired, yes. Younger than Marrow had pictured. Sword at his hip, unremarkable leathers, the callused hands of a working soldier. Ordinary in every particular except the eyes, which were fixed on Cassia with a nakedness Marrow found almost embarrassing. Almost useful.

"Tamsin Fairweather," Marrow said pleasantly. "You've saved me four shrines' worth of marching. I do appreciate efficiency."

"Let her go."

"Of course." Marrow gestured, and the soldier withdrew the knife, though he kept his grip on the old woman's arm. "I am not a butcher, Master Fairweather. I am a bookkeeper. Every entry must be paid for, but I never charge twice." He held up the prophecy book between two fingers. "This is contraband of the highest order. Sedition bound in calfskin. By law it burns, and everyone who has touched it burns with it. That would be you. That would be her—" a nod at Cassia, "—and her whole household, and, I suspect, a number of creatures in the high passes whose names I am still collecting."

The mercenary's jaw tightened. "Name your price."

"Direct. Good. The Throne rewards directness." Marrow set the book on the refectory chair and rested his hand flat upon it. "Here is my ledger. You will surrender yourself—willingly, publicly, before witnesses—and you will confirm, before these good brothers of yours, everything this book contains. The date. The deed. Your part in it. In exchange, the old woman lives, the household lives, and I burn this text instead of its readers." He tilted his head. "One soul entered into evidence, and the account closes clean. It is, I promise you, generous."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take her instead, and her brothers, and I keep taking until the mountains give you up anyway. They always do, Master Fairweather. Hiding is a debt, and debt comes due." Marrow watched the man's hands, not his face. Hands told the truth first. These hands were trembling—slightly, barely, the tremor of a man holding something enormous shut behind a door. "You should know," Marrow added, almost gently, "that I have read the final page. The choice it names. Break the war, or rekindle it. Either way, blood to the knees. There is no version of you, Master Fairweather, that I can permit to exist. The only question is how much ash we wade through to reach you."

For a long moment the courtyard held only the sound of snow falling on embers.

Then Tamsin Fairweather laughed—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it at all.

"You want me to confess in front of them." He nodded at the kneeling brothers. "So they'll know what I am. So they'll stop praying for me." His voice dropped low, and Marrow, leaning in to catch it, heard something beneath the anger that unsettled him more than any blade: grief. Old grief, freshly opened. "Clever. You burn the shrine, and then you make me burn myself."

"I prefer to think of it as honesty," Marrow said. "The last mercy the Throne offers."

The mercenary looked at Cassia. She looked back at him, and shook her head—one small motion, fierce as a struck bell. *Don't,* her ice-grey eyes said. *Don't you dare.*

He dared. Tamsin Fairweather pulled the sword from his hip, reversed it, and laid it in the snow at Marrow's feet.

"I'll come with you," he said. "But the book stays with me until Ironhollow. You don't read another word of it, and neither does the Throne. Those are my terms. Take them or start killing."

Marrow studied him for a moment. There was something here he did not have the shape of yet—some entry in the man's ledger he could not read. Men did not bargain for the privilege of carrying their own confession unless the confession was worth more than the rope.

But it did not matter. Nothing mattered except getting the named one off the mountain and into a cell where the choice named in that cursed book could die of starvation and neglect.

"Agreed," Marrow said, and smiled his sermon-smile. "Bind his hands. Gently. We are guests of the Throne, after all."

As the soldiers moved forward, Cassia began, softly, to pray—not aloud, never aloud, but with her fingers, the old hand-signs of Veyra, the silent language of the silent god. Tamsin watched her hands and answered, once, with his bound ones: the sign that meant *wait for me.*

And high above the courtyard, unseen in the gathering dark, a pair of hollow-born eyes caught the firelight like a cat's, and turned, and vanished toward the passes—to carry word to Seraq that the Inquisition had taken the liberator alive.

Which meant, Seraq would say, when she heard it, that the war had already begun. It simply didn't know it yet.
