## 8. The Choice of Chains

The dying light came through the crack in the prison-shrine's ceiling the way it had come for a hundred years before Tamsin was born and would come for a hundred years after, if he let it: thin, gray, indifferent. It fell across the black stone of Veyra's chains and made them gleam like oiled silk.

Tamsin sat on the cold floor with his back against the altar and did not move. He had not moved in what felt like hours. The word *choose* was still lodged in him like a splinter too deep to dig out.

Behind him, at the mouth of the stair, someone breathed wrong.

He knew the sound. He'd heard it on battlefields, in ditches, in the back rooms of chirurgeons who charged by the scream. Wet breathing. Breathing that cost something.

"Cassia." He was up before he finished saying her name.

She came down the last steps leaning on Seraq's arm, and the sight of it stopped him colder than the mountain air—the matriarch of the hollow-born, tall as a door and gray as iron, bent under the weight of a small human woman in a scorched robe. Cassia's left side was wrapped in bandages gone brown at the edges. Her face was the color of candle tallow. But her eyes found his, and they were still hers: patient, unimpressed, faintly amused at whatever she saw in his expression.

"Don't look at me like that," she said. "I've been worse."

"You haven't."

"I was younger then. It balances out." She let Seraq lower her onto the stone bench near the altar, and Tamsin saw the way her hand pressed her ribs when she settled—saw her breath catch and hold, the way a person does when they're rationing pain instead of air.

Marrow's fire had taken the valley shrine. Marrow's soldiers had taken Cassia, and given her back broken, as a message written in a language everyone understood. The trade had worked. The book was ash now, burned page by page in front of the Inquisitor while Cassia walked free—or limped free—and Marrow had smiled the whole time, because he'd already read it. Because burning it changed nothing. Because the date was coming regardless, and they both knew it.

"How long do I have you?" Tamsin asked quietly.

"Long enough." She said it too fast, which meant not long enough at all.

* * *

Seraq did not waste grief on ceremony. She stood over the chained dark where Veyra slept, arms folded, her hollow eyes reflecting the gray light like coins at the bottom of a well.

"You've heard it speak," she said. "One word. Twenty years of silence, and it spent its first word on you."

"It told me to choose. That's not much of a conversation."

"It's everything." She turned to face him, and up close her features were the beautiful terrible geometry of her kind—planes and angles, skin like slate shot through with veins of dim silver. "Do you understand what those chains are, mercenary? Not metal. Not stone. They are a *decision*, made by cowards a thousand years dead. Your god could have ended the war. Could have burned both armies down to their boots and started the world over clean. Instead your ancestors bound it, because they were afraid of it, and called their fear piety."

"They called it mercy," Tamsin said.

"I know what they called it. I've read the same ruins you have." Her voice dropped, and there was something underneath it he'd never heard from her—not command, not calculation. Grief. "I buried three children in the last war's last winter. My kind remembers every grave, Tamsin Fairweather. We remember because no one else does. And now the Radiant Throne sends its clean-handed butchers to finish what your ancestors started—to kill the god and drink its power, and then to burn us so thoroughly that even the graves forget us."

She stepped closer. This close, he could feel the heat that lived in hollow-born blood, faint as a banked forge.

"Shatter the chains," she said. "Free it. Let it rise with its full strength, and it will break the Inquisition like ice under a boot heel. It will protect its people—all its people, yours and mine. You heard it. You are the one it spoke to. You are the key it has been waiting a century to turn."

"And if it rises angry?"

Seraq's jaw tightened. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Then it rises angry. War is already here. Marrow is at the mountain's gates with two hundred men and a siege engine blessed by the Radiant Throne itself. There is no path out of this where nobody bleeds. There is only a choice about whose blood buys tomorrow." She laid one long hand against the black chain, and it hummed—a low note Tamsin felt in his teeth. "My people will follow you into the dark either way. But I am asking you, as one parent to whatever you were to your brother: don't make us bury anyone else."

* * *

They moved Cassia nearer the altar, propped her with bedrolls, and gave her watered wine she barely touched. Pip refused to leave—sat cross-legged at her feet with a whetstone and a knife he didn't know how to use yet, pretending to be a guard. Tamsin let him pretend. Some fictions were load-bearing.

When Seraq withdrew to the upper galleries to set her watchers, Cassia beckoned Tamsin close with two fingers, the same gesture she'd used a thousand times to summon him for chores, for counsel, for silence.

"You're going to listen to her," Cassia said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to listen to everyone. It's what I do. I'm very good at hearing things and doing nothing."

"No," she said. "You're going to listen to her, and part of you is going to want it, because part of you has always wanted a father with a voice. And I need you to hear me before that part wins." She caught his wrist. Her grip was shockingly strong for a dying woman; her hands had always been stronger than they looked, hands that had rung bells and kneaded bread and once, he suspected, done things no priestess should admit to. "Leave him chained."

Tamsin went still. "Cassia—"

"Listen to me. Twenty years I've served a god who never answered, and I never once doubted why. Do you know what that silence was? It wasn't absence. It was *restraint*. Every prayer I ever whispered, every widow, every soldier, every frightened child kneeling in my shrine—Veyra heard all of it, and held still, because moving would have cost the world a war." Her breath caught; she rode through it. "A god in chains is a god choosing patience. A god freed is a god choosing. And gods choose like weather, Tamsin. They don't choose like people."

"He spoke to me."

"One word." Her eyes were fierce and wet. "And you've built a cathedral on it. Child, I love you like the son I buried with my own name, so hear me: if you break those chains, you may save Thornfell today and drown it in fire by spring. Or you may save nothing at all. A thousand years ago, wiser people than either of us looked at that power and decided no one should hold it. Not the Throne. Not the hollow-born. No one. They weren't cowards, whatever she tells you. They were the only adults in the room."

"And Marrow?" His voice cracked on the name. "Marrow takes it anyway. The Throne cracks the prison, drinks the god, and comes for your people with divine fire. Doing nothing is also a choice, Cassia. It's just the one where we lose."

"Then lose," she whispered.

The word fell between them like a dropped bell.

"Lose beautifully. Lose as a man, not as a weapon. There is no version of this where you hold a god inside a war and stay innocent. There is no version where you get everyone home." Her hand slid from his wrist to his cheek, cool and trembling. "But there is a version where you're still *you* on the other side of it. Fight me on it if you must. Just don't decide tonight. Promise me you won't decide tonight."

Above them, faint through the mountain's bones, came a sound like a drum wrapped in wool: the first stone of Marrow's siege engine striking the outer gate.

Pip's head came up. "That's early," the boy said. "Seraq said dawn."

Tamsin looked at the chains gleaming in the gray light, at the dark between them where twenty years of silence had learned, at last, to say one word. He looked at Cassia's face, and at Pip's, and thought about his brother, and about how every terrible thing he'd ever done had started with someone he loved asking him to be brave.

The gate boomed again. Closer. Louder.

"Dawn was a lie," Tamsin said, rising, drawing steel. "He's not waiting for morning. Neither can we."
