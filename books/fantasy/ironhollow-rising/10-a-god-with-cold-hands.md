## 10. A God With Cold Hands

The morning after the end of the world, Tamsin Fairweather made tea.

It seemed a small thing, given everything. Given that his hands still glowed faintly in the dark, like banked coals wrapped in skin. Given that the mountain beneath him had groaned all night as the prison-shrine emptied itself into his bones. Given that somewhere below, Inquisitor Dain Marrow's soldiers were picking through the wreckage of their ambition and finding nothing left to seize.

But the kettle was copper, and the fire was real, and Pip had never tasted tea before. So Tamsin made tea.

"You're supposed to let it steep," he said. "Not stare at it. Staring doesn't make it stronger."

"Everything about you is strange," Pip said solemnly. The young hollow-born sat cross-legged on the shrine's broken steps, cradling the chipped cup in both hands like something rescued from a fire—which, Tamsin supposed, it was. "You bleed. You complain. You make tea from leaves." Pip took a sip and grimaced with theatrical betrayal. "And this is terrible."

"That's because you let it steep too long."

"You said to let it steep!"

"I said *steep*, not *drown*." Tamsin settled beside him on the cold stone. Below them, Ironhollow's great gates stood open for the first time in living memory—not breached, but opened, by choice, by Seraq's order. Hollow-born and humans moved through the courtyard together in wary streams, like two rivers learning they shared a bed. Some of them talked. Most of them stared. A few spat. But no one had drawn steel since dawn, and Tamsin had decided, privately, to count that as a miracle.

His hands ached. That was the thing no one warned you about divinity: it didn't make you less of a body. It made you more one. Every ache he'd ever carried—every scar, every old break, every winter spent sleeping in snow with nothing between him and the sky—had come back to him at once when Veyra's power poured into mortal flesh. The god's silence lived in him now, vast and patient, and around it, stubbornly, gloriously, his own small human noise: hunger, cold, the bruise on his ribs where Marrow's mace had caught him before the light took the man's conviction apart.

He was still bleeding from that. Slowly. Stubbornly. He'd checked twice last night, pressing his fingers to his side and watching red well up against them, and both times he'd laughed until Cassia told him to stop being an idiot and hold still while she stitched him.

A god who bled. A god with cold hands. He flexed his fingers now and watched the ember-light gutter under his skin.

*Choose,* Veyra had said, twenty years of silence ending in a single word. And then, when Tamsin had stepped into the prison instead of shattering or sparing it: *Oh.* Just that. *Oh,* like a parent seeing a child walk for the first time. Like grief and pride sharing one breath.

Tamsin hadn't heard the god since. But he felt Him—the way you feel a house around you, walls and roof and warmth. Not gone. Not free either. Carried.

"You're doing the face," Pip said.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're listening to something very far away and it's saying something very rude."

Tamsin snorted. "Close enough."

Footsteps on the steps behind them—Seraq's, unmistakable, the long deliberate stride of a matriarch who had outlived three wars and intended to outlive whatever came next. She lowered herself onto the stone with a grace that mocked her age, her grey-streaked crest catching the morning light.

"The human delegation is here," she said. "The ones who aren't spitting."

"And?"

"And they want to know what you are." She said it without malice, which was Seraq's way—truth delivered like a blade laid flat on a table rather than driven in. "So do I, some mornings. So, I suspect, do you."

Tamsin watched the courtyard. A hollow-born child—no older than Pip had been when the raids started—was showing a human soldier how to fold a paper bird, their hands clumsy together over the same small thing. His chest did something complicated.

"I'm the man who was supposed to pick a side," he said. "Break the war or rekindle it. The book was very clear. Date, deed, name." He turned his cup in his hands. "Nobody wrote down what happens if you refuse the question."

"Prophecies hate that," Pip said wisely. "It's like asking a riddle and getting an answer to a different riddle."

Seraq's laugh was low and dry as gravel. "Then we build the new riddle ourselves." She looked at Tamsin, and her eyes—old gold, unblinking—held none of the reverence he dreaded and all of the respect he'd never learned to accept. "They'll come today. Both sides. They'll ask you to be their god, Tamsin Fairweather. The Throne will call you a heresy. My people will call you a herald. And every one of them will be wrong, because what you are is *ours*. Do you understand? Not theirs. Ours. The tea-maker. The tunnel-walker. The man who carried my people through the dark under Thornfell when every other hand would have sold them."

"I carried you because Pip would have sulked forever otherwise."

"He would have," Seraq agreed gravely. "Unbearable."

"Extremely," Pip said.

Tamsin laughed, and the sound came out of him rough and startled, like something he'd forgotten he owned.

* * *

Mother Cassia received them in the shrine's inner chamber, propped upright on a cot among the ruins of the prison machinery, because Mother Cassia had never in her life received anyone lying down. Her burns had healed to pale seams across her forearms; Marrow's fire had nearly taken her, and the god-power Tamsin had pushed into her wounds—carefully, so carefully, like pouring water into a cracked cup—had done the rest. She called it cheating. She called most things cheating.

She looked up at him now with the expression she'd worn since he was nineteen and first stumbled into her valley shrine half-frozen and full of smart answers.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. Seraq, you too, don't loom, it's unbecoming."

"I am a matriarch of the deep roads," Seraq said, sitting.

"Then sit like one who minds her manners in a holy place."

"This stopped being a holy place when your god moved into that man." Seraq nodded at Tamsin. "Now it's just a room with bad acoustics."

Cassia smiled—thin, tired, real—and then looked at Tamsin alone, and the smile gentled into something older. "Show me your hands."

He held them out. The ember-glow pulsed once, twice, and steadied. Cassia turned them over in her own scarred grip, thumbs tracing the lines of his palms like she was reading a book she'd written herself.

"Cold," she murmured. "Still cold."

"Still me, though."

"Still you." She released him. "That's the whole miracle, boy. Not the power. That after everything, there's still a *you* in there to hold it." Her voice roughened. "I prayed for twenty years to a silent god and told myself the silence was faithfulness. It was cowardice. I knew something was wrong beneath us—I felt it in the stones—and I kept my head down same as you did. We were the same sin, you and I. Hiding in plain sight." She touched his cheek, brief and fierce. "Never again. Whatever they ask of you in that courtyard today—don't hide. Promise me."

"I've never been good at promises."

"No," Cassia said. "But you're good at keeping the ones that matter. I've watched you do it since you were nineteen years old and too stupid to know you were doing it."

Pip made a strangled noise that was definitely laughter. Tamsin elected, with divine restraint, to ignore it.

* * *

The parley convened at noon in Ironhollow's great gate-court, beneath the mountain's carved brow, where the founders of both peoples had once set their sigils side by side before the first war gouged them apart. Tamsin stood between Seraq and Cassia—hollow-born and human, matriarch and priestess, flanking him like the two halves of a door—and faced the assembled ranks.

The Throne's delegation was smaller than he'd expected. Three magistrates, a score of soldiers, and at their center a woman in Radiant white with a ledger chained to her wrist: Magistrate Elowen Vasse, sent to replace what Marrow had become. She looked at Tamsin the way an accountant looks at a sum that refuses to balance.

"Tamsin Fairweather," she said. "You hold the power of a god unlawfully detained by sacred rite."

"I hold it lawfully," Tamsin said. "I'm detaining myself. You're welcome to inspect the prisoner." He spread his cold hands. "Conditions are cramped, but the company's excellent."

Someone in the Throne's ranks almost laughed and converted it into a cough at the last moment. Vasse did not smile.

"You understand what you've done. Divinity in mortal flesh is the heresy the Throne has burned kingdoms to prevent."

"I understand what I've done," Tamsin said, and here he let a little of the silence rise in his voice, the vast patient quiet of Veyra, and watched the nearest soldiers sway like grass in wind. "I ended a war before it started. I freed a god by carrying Him. And I'm offering you the only terms on the table, magistrate, so listen close, because I'll only say this once, and I say it with the voice of the thing your ancestors buried under this mountain."

Silence took the court. Real silence—the kind that has weight.

"Hollow-born and humankind share these mountains, these valleys, this future. There will be no victor. There will be no throne above the other. Trade through the deep roads, open gates, shared courts. And the Radiant Throne will take its Inquisition—" he glanced at the scorch-marks Marrow's fires had left on the far wall, and his voice went quiet and terrible, "—and go home. Those are the terms. The alternative is me, and neither of us wants to find out what that means."

Vasse studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she unchained her ledger, tore out a page, and held it up—a writ, already drafted, awaiting only his description. The prophecy's page, repurposed. Names, dates, deeds.

"And if we agree," she said, "what do we write here? What are you, Tamsin Fairweather?"

He thought of a copper kettle. Of a paper bird folded by four clumsy hands. Of a stitch-worked scar and a matriarch's gravel laugh and a word spoken after twenty years of silence: *choose.*

He picked up the pen she offered, and wrote, in his own smuggler's hand, plain and unadorned:

*Tamsin Fairweather. Tea-maker. Tunnel-walker. Kin to both peoples. Seen.*

He signed it, and handed it back, and the mountain—old Ironhollow, patient as its buried god—seemed to exhale around them.

Above the gate, in the high cold light, the first snow of winter began to fall, settling equally on hollow-born crests and human shoulders alike.

And far beneath them all, in the marrow of the world, something ancient and silent curled contentedly around a mortal heart, and slept—no longer imprisoned, only home.
