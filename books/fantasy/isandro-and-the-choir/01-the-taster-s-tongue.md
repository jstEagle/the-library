## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The soup arrived at the king's table in a silver tureen chased with vine patterns, and Felix Reyes did what he had done every evening for eleven years: he loved it first.

He lifted the ladle with both hands — ceremony demanded it, though his arms were strong enough for one — and filled the shallow silver spoon the chamberlain held out to him. Steam rose off it in a pale ribbon. Around him the great hall murmured with the small politics of supper: a baron's joke landing badly, two cousins pretending not to quarrel, the king himself leaning back in his high chair with the patient hunger of a man who has never once wondered whether his food might betray him. That was Felix's job. To wonder on his behalf.

He tasted.

Barley first, soft as a confession. Then leek, sweet where it had caramelized against the pot's bottom, and behind that the dark mineral note of good stock made from bones roasted until they sang. Thyme — no, thyme and something wilder, a green thread of woodruff that the cook must have gathered from the shaded bank below the mill. A whisper of salt. Not too much. The cook, old Berthe, salted like a woman who trusted her eaters to bring their own longing to the table.

Felix stood very still, eyes half-closed, and let the flavor move through him the way a priest lets a psalm move through him: not for pleasure, though there was pleasure, but for truth. Every dish told a story if you listened with your whole tongue. This one said: *no harm here. No nightshade bitter under the sweetness, no arsenic's faint almond ghost, no jealousy simmered into the fat.* It said: *Berthe woke before dawn. Berthe is thinking of her daughter in the lowlands. Berthe made this soup the way she makes all her soups, which is to say honestly.*

"Clean," he said, and the word went down the table like a bell being rung, and the king picked up his spoon, and forty people began to eat.

That was the whole of Felix's art, and he was not modest about it because there was nothing in it to be modest about. He had been born with a tongue like a ledger. His mother used to say he could taste the difference between rain collected in a wooden bucket and rain collected in clay, and she had meant it as a joke, and then when he was nine the palace steward had come through the valley testing children with drops of bitter tincture, and Felix had named every herb in the mixture blindfolded, including the one that wasn't an herb at all but a poison the steward kept for exactly this purpose. After that his life had been decided. There were worse fates than being decided early. He got a room in the taster's quarters, warm and windowed; he got three meals a day and the right to refuse none of them; he got, most precious of all, safety, which in the valley of Vaelmoor was not a thing you earned but a thing you simply had, the way you had weather.

Because that was the other fact of Felix's life, the one so constant he had stopped seeing it, the way a fish stops seeing water: nothing strange survived in Vaelmoor. Nothing strange even *arrived*. The valley sat cupped in the mountains like soup in a bowl — Felix appreciated the comparison — and whatever the world beyond the passes contained, its wonders could not cross the ridgeline. Pilgrims came sometimes, peddlers more often, and they brought silks and spices and stories, but their stories died at the border like cut flowers. A man from the coast had once sworn to Felix that in his homeland rivers ran uphill during certain moons, and Felix had believed him the way you believe a story about somewhere else: completely, and with no consequence whatsoever. Magic was a rumor. Sorcery was a market stall in a city he'd never visit. The valley kept its own counsel, grew its barley, salted its honest soups, and asked nothing of the world except to be left alone.

Eleven years of tasting, and the strangest thing Felix had ever encountered was the year Berthe experimented with saffron.

He thought about this sometimes, standing beside the king's chair while the court ate, watching candlelight swim in the wine cups. He thought: *I am the man whose job is to be afraid, in the one place where there is nothing to fear.* It was a comfortable paradox, like a warm coat with one loose thread. He never pulled the thread. Why would he? The coat fit.

* * *

The knock came at midnight.

Felix was awake anyway — he often was, after a heavy service, his tongue still cataloguing ghosts of flavors against the roof of his mouth — sitting up in his narrow bed with a rushlight guttering on the sill. Snow had been falling since dusk, the fat silent flakes of a valley winter, and the world beyond his window was the color of wool.

Three knocks. Soft. Spaced wrong — not the rhythm of a fist or a knuckle, but something almost deliberate, almost *musical*, tap, tap-tap, tap.

He took the poker from the hearth and opened the door on a breath of cold so clean it hurt.

A child stood in the snow.

She was perhaps eight years old, small enough that the drifts came to her knees, and she was barefoot. Barefoot in snow past her ankles, in a thin shift the color of undyed flax, and she was not shivering. That was the first wrongness, and Felix felt it land in his stomach before his mind caught up: the cold should have been killing her, and instead she stood in it the way other children stood in summer grass. Her hair was white — not old-person white, not gray, but white as the snowfield, white as frost ferns on glass — and her skin beneath it had the faint blue-gray cast of moonlit ice. Her eyes were open and fixed on him, and they were dark, dark as the space between stars, and they did not blink often enough.

Felix's grip tightened on the poker. "Child," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, trained by years of announcing poison to kings. "Where is your mother? Where did you come from?"

She tilted her head. And then she hummed.

It was a melody. He would spend the rest of his life failing to explain what happened next, but in the moment it was simple: the sound entered his ears as humming, three notes rising and falling, and it arrived in his mind as *language*. Not translated — nothing so mechanical as translation. It arrived already understood, the way warmth arrives understood, the way your own name arrives when someone says it across a crowded room.

*Cold,* she said, without saying it. *Long cold. The door is broken and the cold walks.*

"What?" Felix whispered. The poker sagged in his hand.

She hummed again, and this time the notes carried something with edges, something urgent, and again understanding bloomed in him whole and unasked-for: *They broke it. They broke the singing and now the winter does not end, and I am the last voice, and I have walked a long way to find the one who cannot be unwritten.*

"I don't understand," Felix said, which was a lie, which was the most frightening part, because he understood every word, and there were no words, only a child's hummed melody hanging in the frozen air between them, and his teeth had begun to ache — a deep, resonant ache, as though the song were finding harmonics in his jaw, in the bones of his skull, in some hollow place he had never known was hollow until now.

The child swayed slightly on her bare feet. Up close he could see that the snow around her had not melted. It had *frosted over*, spreading outward from her in delicate feathering patterns, lacework climbing the doorframe, silvering the threshold stones. Frost, indoors, in a valley where nothing strange survived. In a valley where nothing strange had ever survived, in eleven years of tasting every danger out of a king's food, in thirty-one years of a life so safe it had been nearly featureless —

The thread. He pulled the thread, and the whole warm coat came apart in his hands.

*The one who cannot be unwritten.* She was looking at him. She had walked — how far? Through what? Past the ridgeline, past the border that no wonder had ever crossed — and she had come to his door, at midnight, barefoot, and sung at him in a language older than any kingdom, and she had been looking for *him*.

"You're freezing," Felix heard himself say, absurdly, uselessly, the taster in him reaching for the only remedy he knew. "Come inside. Now, before you — come inside."

She stepped over the threshold, and where her feet touched his floorboards the frost followed, blooming in slow white flowers across the wood, and she looked up at him with those starless eyes and hummed one more time, softly, almost tenderly, and the melody settled into his aching teeth like a key settling into a lock:

*You will do,* she said. *You are the tongue that remembers.*

Outside, the snow went on falling, patient as ever, blanketing the safe little valley — and for the first time in the history of Vaelmoor, something strange had come inside.
