## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

Felix had hidden contraband before — a wedge of smuggled cheese for the scullery boy's birthday, a love letter passed between a laundress and a guard — but he had never hidden a person.

The child sat on his narrow bed with her knees drawn up, watching him the way he watched an unfamiliar dish: patient, cataloguing, waiting to see what he was made of. She had not spoken since the night in the snow. She did not seem mute so much as *elsewhere* — as though speech were a country she'd left behind and humming was the road she traveled now.

He had given her his spare shirt, which hung off her like a sail, and a bowl of barley porridge, which she ate with a strange gravity, as if each spoonful were being counted somewhere. When she finished, she looked at him and hummed three low notes.

And Felix, standing there with the empty bowl in his hands, understood her perfectly.

*Thank you. You are kind. I am afraid.*

He nearly dropped the bowl.

It wasn't understanding the way one understands a foreign merchant's gestures — the shrug that means *I don't know*, the open palm that means *please*. It was understanding the way one understands one's own mother tongue, words arriving whole in his mind wearing meanings like familiar coats. Three notes, and he knew her gratitude, her kindness toward him, her fear. He stood very still, the way he stood when he tasted something he couldn't name, and let it happen again.

She hummed once more, a rising phrase, almost playful.

*Your heart is loud.*

"My heart," Felix said aloud, absurdly, "is perfectly quiet."

Her eyes went wide — not startled, but *delighted*, the way a child looks when a puppet first moves. She hummed again, faster, and this time the meaning came with texture, with color: *You hear me. You hear me. No one has heard me since the snow began.*

* * *

By morning he had convinced himself it was exhaustion. By noon he had convinced himself it was some trick of hers, a beggar's cleverness. By evening, when she hummed *the water in the east pipe is bad, do not drink it* and he checked and found the pipe fouled with dead leaves, he stopped convincing himself of anything at all.

He brought her breakfast himself, carrying it through the servants' corridors under a cloth as though it were a dish for the king. The taster's quarters sat behind the royal kitchens, a stone room with a pallet, a shelf of reference books on poisons, and a window too narrow to climb through — which was precisely why it was safe. Nobody came here. Tasters were tolerated the way drains were tolerated: necessary, unloved, unseen.

"You can't stay forever," he told her, setting down the tray. "Someone will ask questions."

She hummed a long, descending line, and grief poured into him like cold water into a boot. *Everyone says that. Everyone always says that.*

"Who's everyone?" he asked — then caught himself, because she hadn't said *everyone*; she'd said something in a language made of melody, and he had translated it without deciding to, the way a tongue finds its own blood when cut.

She tilted her head. Then, slowly, deliberately, she sang him a name.

It was not a word. It was a chord held in a single throat, two tones at once, and when it entered him it arrived with a face attached — a woman, tall, silver-streaked hair braided with river-glass, laughing in a doorway full of morning light. The name meant *she who kept the door*. The woman's face was worn smooth with age in the vision, the way stones wear in streams, and behind her Felix glimpsed others — dozens, hundreds of faces, each one a different song, a choir of names stretching back further than any kingdom he could imagine.

Then the vision folded shut, and he was kneeling on the floor of his room with porridge cooling on the tray and tears on his cheeks he had no memory of starting.

"That's your mother," he said hoarsely.

The child shook her head. She touched her own chest, then pointed at the space where the vision had been, and hummed something small and terrible:

*That is who I was singing to when they stopped us.*

* * *

The palace scholars were summoned the next day, because a child found barefoot in the king's snow could not simply be absorbed into the kitchens without someone asking whose she was, and Felix needed the question answered by someone other than himself.

Master Aldous of the Chancery arrived first, a dry man shaped like a question mark, who spent an hour with the child in the records room while Felix watched through the door. She hummed for him obligingly. Aldous transcribed what he heard using musical notation, then consulted his lexicons of the hill dialects, the marsh tongues, the old imperial speech that lingered in legal formulas. He emerged frowning.

"It resembles nothing," he told the chamberlain. "The intervals are wrong. Every human language I know builds meaning from discrete sounds — this child slides between pitches like water finding cracks. If it is language at all."

"If it isn't?" asked the chamberlain.

Aldous sniffed. "Then it is birdsong, and we are wasting the crown's time."

The second scholar was younger, a linguist named Vess who had catalogued seventeen dying tongues along the frontier. She listened to the child for half an hour and came out pale.

"There's a structure," she insisted. "Repetition with variation — call and answer, theme and return. That's not noise. That's *grammar*." She rubbed her arms as though chilled. "But the root forms are older than anything in my catalogs. Older than Old Imperial. It's as if—" She laughed, embarrassed at herself. "As if the language weren't descended from anything. As if everything else descended from *it*."

"And what does it say?" the chamberlain pressed.

Vess hesitated. "I can't translate it. But there's one element that recurs. A word — or what I think is a word. She returns to it the way a bell returns to its note." The young scholar's voice dropped. "Whatever it means, she's saying it constantly. Like a warning she doesn't know how else to give."

Felix knew what the word was. He had known since the second night, when the child had fallen asleep mid-hum and the melody had continued in her dreams, circling back again and again to a single falling phrase that filled his sleep with images: white horizons, trees standing like candles burned down to the wick, a sun the color of a coin at the bottom of a well.

*Winter. Winter. Winter.*

He said nothing. Saying it aloud would make it real, and besides — who would believe him? Who would believe that Felix Reyes, whose entire gift was a tongue that detected arsenic in wine, could understand a language that predated language? He barely believed it himself. Understanding her should have been impossible. Understanding was a thing built between people over years, a shared architecture of sound and meaning. What he had with the child had been assembled in a single night, complete, load-bearing, ancient — as though the knowledge hadn't come *to* him but had been *in* him all along, sleeping, waiting for the right voice to wake it.

That thought frightened him more than the winter did.

* * *

On the fourth day, the frost followed her indoors.

He noticed it first on the water jug beside his pallet — a lacework of ice ferns blooming across the clay despite the kitchen fires two rooms away. Then on the window glass, then along the mortar lines of the wall, delicate white feathers of frost spreading outward from wherever the child slept, as though the cold in her were leaking, or calling to its kin.

She saw him looking and hummed an apology so heavy with shame that he had to sit down.

*It is not me,* the song said. *It is what follows me. I am the last note of a song that used to be sung by many, and an unfinished note pulls. It pulls the silence after it.*

"The winter," Felix said quietly. "The endless winter past the pass. That's the silence."

She nodded, and the nod itself seemed to cost her warmth; more frost feathered out across the floorboards from beneath her bare feet. Felix fetched his woolen socks without letting himself think about it, knelt, and pulled them onto her small cold feet. She watched him with an expression he recognized from the mirror — the careful blankness of someone who has learned that hope is expensive.

"Why me?" he asked. "Why can I understand you? I'm nobody. I taste soup. My whole talent is that poison doesn't agree with me." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "My mother used to say I was born under a dull star. Safe and flavorless."

The child reached out and took his hand. Her fingers were freezing, and where they touched, he felt — not cold, exactly. Recognition. The same feeling he'd had the first night, of a key sliding into a lock he hadn't known his body contained.

She sang softly, and the meaning rose in him like groundwater:

*Nothing about you is dull. You are a door that was locked so well everyone forgot there was ever a doorway. I am the key that remembers.*

"A door to where?"

She looked at him with those old, old eyes, and outside the narrow window the snow fell straight down, silent as held breath, and somewhere far beyond the valley's rim the winter waited like a wolf at a door that was already opening.

Before she could answer — before the song could shape whatever unbearable truth came next — boots sounded in the corridor. Heavy ones. Official ones. And a voice Felix knew well, smooth as oiled steel, speaking to someone just out of sight:

"No, don't knock. Lord Cassian prefers his surprises unwarned."
