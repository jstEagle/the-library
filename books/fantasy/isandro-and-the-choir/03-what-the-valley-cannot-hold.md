## 3. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The archive beneath the palace was older than the palace above it, which Felix had always taken for one of those small jokes the world plays on the ambitious. The king's records lived in marble halls with gilded spines and clerks in clean linen; the valley's records lived three flights down, past a door that stuck, in rooms where the stone sweated in summer and the candles guttered for no reason anyone had ever bothered to name. Felix liked it there. Nothing in the under-archive had been touched by an important hand in two hundred years, and things untouched by important hands tended to remain true.

He came down at dawn, before his duties, carrying bread and a stub of tallow candle, and he told himself he was only curious. That was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and he went anyway. Curiosity was the polite word for what had moved into him the night the child arrived — something with teeth, that paced.

The child slept now in the taster's quarters, in the narrow cot that had been meant for an apprentice who never came. She slept curled like a comma, breathing slow, and when she dreamed she hummed, and the humming had changed. He had noticed it on the second night. It had been shapeless before, a lullaby without edges. Now it had structure. Now it sounded less like a song and more like a *sentence*, repeated patiently, as if to someone slow of understanding.

*Winter,* it said. *Winter. Winter.* And then other words, harder ones, words that sat in his chest like swallowed stones: *broken. broken. they broke it.*

Felix had spent his whole life cataloguing flavors, and he knew poison when he tasted it, even in a melody.

So: the archive. He set his candle on a scarred table and began, as any sensible man would, with the indexes. There were few. The valley's clerks had been men who believed in ink but not in order, and the earliest volumes were bound in leather gone soft as skin, their titles faded to rumors. He pulled them down one by one and read by tallow-light while the palace overhead woke into its ordinary noises — boots on stairs, a cart in the yard, the king's hounds being loosed into the garden.

What he found, in the third hour, in a volume titled only *Valley Ordinances, Being the First Hundred Years*, made him sit down without meaning to.

> *Let it be entered that the border is not a natural thing. The border was drawn. It was drawn by agreement, and paid for in blood freely given, and its purpose is exclusion. What lies beyond may not enter; what lies within may not leave and remain itself. Let no man call this cruelty. We were given a choice between walls and extinction, and we chose walls.*

Felix read it twice. Then a third time, moving his lips, the way he did with difficult ingredients.

*The border was drawn.*

He had grown up with the valley's strangeness the way one grows up with a limp or a family nose — as fact, unexamined. Nothing strange survived here. Everyone knew it. A hedge-witch from over the mountains could curse you six ways to Sunday in her own country and find, crossing into the valley, that her curses slid off like grease off wet slate. Charms went dull. Prophets went quiet. Once, when Felix was nine, a traveling conjurer had come to the harvest fair and stood on his stage before three hundred people and produced, from his sleeve, nothing at all, again and again, until the crowd laughed and the conjurer's face went gray and he packed up and left before dusk. Felix had laughed too. He remembered laughing.

Nobody had ever told him *why*. Nobody had told him because nobody knew, or because everyone knew so long ago that knowing had worn smooth and invisible, like the stone steps of a church.

He kept reading.

The ordinances gave way to ledgers, the ledgers to appointment rolls, and it was in the appointment rolls that he found the word that stopped his breath: *warden*.

> *Appointed this day, Aldous Vane, Warden of the Salt Line, Keeper of the Tasting, in succession to his father, with all rights and burdens pertaining.*

Warden of the Salt Line. Keeper of the Tasting.

Felix looked at his own hands. They were steady — they were always steady; it was the first requirement of his trade — but something behind them wanted to shake.

*Taster* was a humble word. Everyone knew what a taster was: a servant who ate the king's food and died if he was unlucky, a human sieve, a man valued exactly as much as his willingness to be poisoned. Felix had held the office eleven years. His father had held it before him, and his grandfather, back and back into the gray of the valley's memory, and the family joke — told at every funeral, groaned at by every mourner — was that the Reyeses were hard to kill. Bad luck for assassins. Good luck for kings.

But the roll did not say *taster*. It said *Keeper of the Tasting*, and it paired the title with *Warden of the Salt Line*, generation after generation after generation, unbroken, all the way down to a final entry in a newer hand:

> *Appointed this day, Felix Reyes, Warden of the Salt Line, Keeper of the Tasting, in succession to his father. The old forms are retained for continuity. The meaning of the forms is lost. May God forgive us the losing.*

Felix read that last line perhaps twenty times. *The meaning of the forms is lost.*

Someone had known. Someone, within living institutional memory — within the span of a single clerk's career, maybe — had watched the last understanding drain out of the office and had written down, in the dry language of appointments, something very close to grief.

He worked until his candle drowned in its own wax. He found more. He found a fragment of a treaty, copied by some diligent dead hand, speaking of *the Choir* and *the Two Realms* and *the Balance of the Door* — phrases that meant nothing and everything, phrases that rang against each other inside him like struck bells. He found a list of names called *the Given*, hundreds of names, each followed by the same notation: *voice surrendered, freely*. He found, folded into the back of the oldest ledger, a map of the valley drawn with obsessive care, and along the valley's rim, where Felix had always understood there to be simply — mountains, the world's ordinary edge — a line drawn in salt-white ink, and beside the line, in letters small as insect tracks:

*Here the singing ends. Here the silence begins. Keep it.*

Keep it. Not *keep them out*. Not *keep us safe*. Just: keep it. As though the border were not a wall but a wound, and the wardens were not guards but dressers of bandages.

Felix climbed the three flights with the map folded against his chest like a stolen thing, and the door stuck behind him, and he stood in the dusty stairwell and felt the vertigo take him properly at last.

His whole life was a wall. That was the truth of it, arriving all at once, the way cold water arrives. The valley was not a place where magic failed. The valley was a place where magic was *refused* — held out by a line someone had drawn and paid for, maintained by men who had forgotten they were maintaining anything, who had dwindled from wardens into food-tasters the way a cathedral dwindles, stone by sold stone, into a barn. Every flavor he had ever catalogued, every prayer he had tasted in the king's soup, every safe and ordinary morning of his safe and ordinary life — all of it sat on top of a sentence he had never heard spoken: *this far, and no farther, and we do not remember why.*

And the child had walked across the line. Barefoot. Through snow. Through the thing that could not be crossed.

Unless the line was failing. Unless the wall he had just learned he lived inside was already breached, and the humming in his quarters was the sound of the breach widening.

* * *

She was awake when he returned. She was sitting on the floor with her knees drawn up, and she was humming, and the frost was on the window.

Not on the outside of the glass. On the inside. In patterns — and Felix's trade had trained him to see patterns the way other men saw faces — in patterns that repeated, ferning outward from a center, each frond edged in fine white crystal, beautiful the way a fever chart is beautiful. The room was cold enough to see his breath. The brazier had not gone out; it burned cheerfully, uselessly, its heat stopping an arm's length from her as though the air around her had been taught better manners.

She looked up at him. Her eyes were pale, winter-pale, and they were not afraid, and that frightened him more than anything else that day.

"You crossed," he said, and heard his own voice crack on the ordinariness of it. "The line. You crossed the salt line, and nothing crosses."

She tilted her head. And then she hummed — deliberately now, watching him, testing — and the hum resolved into words the way fog resolves into shapes when you finally stand in the right place:

*The line knows a warden. The line let me pass to find the warden. The line is thin, keeper. The line is thin where the tasting stopped.*

Felix put his back against the door. The frost crept another finger's width across the glass, patient as ink spreading through water, and in its crystalline fronds he could almost read writing — or he was going mad, or the valley was finally letting its secrets through the wall, and he could no longer tell which possibility he feared least.

"The tasting never stopped," he said. "I taste every day. I—"

*You taste for poison,* the humming said, gentle, terrible, patient. *Your fathers tasted for the Door. Find the difference, keeper. Before the ones who broke it find you first.*

Outside, far off across the frozen gardens, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the alarm. The small bell over the servants' gate, rung once, softly, by someone standing in the snow who did not wish to be announced.

Felix had been a taster for eleven years. He knew, in his bones, in the tongue that had never once lied to him, the exact flavor of being watched.

Someone had found her.
