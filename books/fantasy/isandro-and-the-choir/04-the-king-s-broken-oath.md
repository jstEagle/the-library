## 4. The King's Broken Oath

Felix had tasted the king's soup eleven times before he understood that he was no longer tasting it at all.

The spoon rose to his lips out of habit, and his tongue performed its liturgy — marrow first, then leek, the ghost of saffron bought dear from southern caravans, a whisper of salt from the eastern pans — but beneath the cataloguing there was another sense entirely, one he had no name for. It was the child. She was three rooms away, folded into the narrow cot in his quarters among the sacks of flour and the tallow candles, and he could feel her humming the way a man feels weather coming in an old wound.

*Winter,* she sang, silent behind her lips. *Winter, winter, winter.* And underneath it, threaded through like gold wire through grey cloth: *the king's house remembers what the king has forgotten.*

He set down the spoon. The under-steward gave him a look — Felix Reyes was never slow at table, never — and Felix bowed and murmured something about a sour note in the broth, and the kitchen fell into its usual fury of correction while he stood very still and let the dread settle into him like sediment.

Three days she had been his. Three days of scholars with their lexicons spread open like wounded birds, none of them able to name her language; three days of Felix lying awake listening to frost bloom across the inside of the window glass in patterns like written script; three days of learning to hear her.

That was the impossible part. Not that she spoke — children speak, even strange ones, even barefoot ones who arrive out of a snowfall with no footprints behind them. But that when she hummed, the sound arrived in Felix's mind already translated, as if someone had pressed meaning into him the way a seal presses into wax. He had tested himself savagely. He had told no one. He had begun, quietly and without ceremony, to be afraid of the crown he served — because the child's song said the crown had done something, long ago, and the winter on the other side of the valley's border was not weather. It was a bill come due.

"Master Reyes." A page, breathless, at the kitchen door. "Lord Cassian asks for you. In the small gallery. Alone."

The soup went cold behind him. Felix wiped his hands on his apron, and followed.

* * *

Cassian kept the small gallery dim. He liked shadows the way other men liked wine — daily, and in quantities that would ruin a lesser constitution. He stood by the tall window where the light came through leaded glass in grey coins, and he did not turn when Felix entered.

"You've been distracted at table," Cassian said. "Twice this week the king waited on your tongue."

"The broth was off. I said so."

"The broth was perfect. I tasted it myself." Now he turned, and his face was pleasant in the way of drawn blades. "You've been sleeping badly. There are circles under your eyes like bruises. And your quarters — " he paused delicately — "your quarters have been cold. Unseasonably cold. Frost indoors, they tell me, though we are weeks from any frost worth the name."

Felix kept his breathing level. "Old windows. Bad mortar."

"Mm." Cassian crossed the gallery slowly, his boots silent on the stone. That was the thing about the spymaster: a big man who made no sound, as though the palace itself had agreed to keep his secrets. "Do you know what I admire about you, Reyes? Your tongue. Not the talent — plenty can taste arsenic in wine. Your *discretion*. Twenty years, and you have never once gossiped about what passes over the king's plate. You understand that some knowledge is a poison all its own, and the wise man declines the cup." He stopped an arm's length away. "So decline it now. Whatever you have found in your cold rooms, whatever you think you understand — carry it to your grave unopened."

"And if I don't know what you mean?"

"Then you're a fool," Cassian said softly, "and fools die of curiosity in this palace every year." He smiled, and it reached his eyes without warming them. "There is a child in the taster's wing. Barefoot. Mute. Arrived out of the snows nine days ago, though only three of those were under your roof — you hid her well, I'll grant you. The scholars failed because they were meant to fail. Her tongue is older than their books. Older than the kingdom. Older than the *border*, Master Reyes, which is a sentence I will deny ever speaking, as you will never repeat it."

Felix's mouth had gone dry. "What is she?"

"A debt," Cassian said. "Come due. And debts of that age are paid in blood or silence, and I have been authorized to choose silence." He let the word sit between them. "Tonight. Before the wrong ears hear her singing. You will bring her to me after the last bell, and tomorrow you'll taste soup again, and in a year this will be a strange dream you half-remember. That is the kind offer. Do not make me find the other kind."

* * *

In his office above the armory, Lord Cassian poured himself a measure of plum brandy he did not drink, and looked at the map of the valley pinned to his wall, and hated it.

Hated it cleanly, the way he hated few things anymore. The valley sat in the middle of the map like a held breath — a green oval ringed in mountains, marked on no pilgrim's route, taxed by no crown officer, guarded by nothing anyone could name. His own birth-valley. He had left it at six years old, sold with four other Choir-children to the old king, carried across the salt line in a closed cart while the singers' voices faded behind him like a tide going out. He remembered the exact moment the song stopped reaching him. It felt like going deaf in the soul.

They called it protection. The covenant's shield, drawn around the valley so magic could not enter and corrupt it. Cassian had spent forty years learning what it actually was: a wall around a crime scene. Inside lived the descendants of the wardens who had looked away, the farmers fed on soil the Choir's blood had watered, the tasters whose tongues had once sworn oaths to two realms and now swore them to one fat man's dinner. And outside — outside, past the salt line, the other realm froze. Had frozen for a generation now, since the last echo of the murdered Choir finally died, since the balance broke, since the winter came over the border like a creditor and found the door barred.

And now the last voice of the Choir had walked out of that winter, through snow that should have killed her, into *his* palace, singing names.

He knew the names. That was the worst of it. He'd heard them as a child, sung in rounds by women with frost-white braids, before the crown's men came with ropes and fire and the old king's warrant. *The king's house remembers what the king has forgotten.* Oh, it remembered. His grandfather's masters had seen to that — the ledgers, the sealed confessions, the warrant with its royal seal still legible under the wax. If the child lived to sing those names in front of the wrong audience, the winter would stop being a rumor at the borders and become a question asked in the great hall: *what did the crown do?* And the answer would unmake the throne, and the realms with it, or so the men who signed his orders believed.

Perhaps they were right. Perhaps they were simply old men protecting their graves. Cassian had stopped being able to tell the difference years ago, and that inability was its own kind of wound.

He drank the brandy after all. It tasted of nothing, the way everything tasted of nothing lately, except —

Except that for three nights running he had dreamed of the round-song. The children's part, the high line that wove over the women's voices. His part. He had sung it once, at six, barefoot on cold stone, and the dream always ended at the same place: the cart, the canvas, the song thinning behind him until it snapped.

He set down the cup. Below, the palace bells began the last peal, and somewhere in the taster's wing, a boy who should have brought him a child was making his choice.

Cassian put on his cloak and went down into the dark to see which way the choice had gone.

* * *

Felix moved through the kitchens like a man walking a rope over a gorge.

The last bell had rung. The scullery fires banked low. He had dressed the child in a scullery boy's castoffs, hood up, ash rubbed on her cheeks, her small hand locked in his. She had understood — of course she had understood; she understood everything he thought near her, which was a terror he'd examine later, if there was a later — and she had not wept or clung. She had only hummed, very softly, a phrase he now knew the shape of the way he knew his own heartbeat:

*The kitchens remember. The kitchens were always ours.*

"What does that mean?" he whispered, easing open the door to the buttery passage. "Nothing here is yours. Nothing here is mine, really. We belong to the crown, body and spoon—"

She looked up at him, and her eyes in the dark were the color of deep ice over deep water, and she hummed one word.

*Before.*

The passage took them down past the salt stores, past the ale casks sweating in the cold, past the great hearth where the night-baker slept in his chair. Twice they stopped — boots overhead, a door somewhere — and twice the child pressed her palm flat against Felix's chest, and the fear drained out of him like heat from a stone, replaced by something steadier and stranger, a note held low in his ribs. He was beginning to suspect that was also her doing. He was beginning to suspect a great many things, all of them arranged around one fact: Lord Cassian had offered him a choice between betraying the crown and burying a child, and Felix had discovered, standing in that dim gallery with his heart hammering, that the choice had never truly been offered at all. Some part of him had decided the moment the child knocked on his door in the snow.

At the bottom of the herb stair, the passage forked — left to the postern gate, right to the old cold-cellars that ran, according to plans no one consulted anymore, beneath the wall itself.

The child pulled him right.

"There's a grate," Felix breathed. "It's rusted shut, it's been rusted shut for—"

She hummed, and the frost on the cellar walls bloomed outward in feathers, and somewhere ahead iron groaned and gave with a sound like a sigh.

Felix stared at her. She stared back, and for the first time since the snowfall, she smiled — a small, grave, ancient smile on a face seven years old.

"Oh," Felix said, to no one, to the dark, to the whole impossible architecture of his life. "Oh, you've been waiting for me."

Behind them, far above, a door opened onto the herb stair, and lamplight spilled down the steps, and a voice that made no echo called out, quiet and terrible:

"Reyes. You're taking the stairs a servant shouldn't know exist. Think very carefully about the next thirty seconds."

Felix took the child's hand and ran.
