## 5. Crossing the Salt Line

They left before the third bell of morning, when even the palace cats had given up on being awake.

Felix carried a satchel of bread, hard cheese, and the small iron knife he'd used for ten years to pare fruit for the king's table. The child carried nothing. She walked beside him through the servants' gate with her bare feet making no prints in the snow — no prints at all, which Felix had stopped remarking on around the time he'd stopped sleeping properly — and her humming wound through the frozen dark like thread through a needle's eye.

*South,* she hummed. *Down where the river forgets to be a river. Down to the salt.*

He understood it the way he understood his own heartbeat: not as language, exactly, but as certainty. Three days ago that would have frightened him. Now it was simply the shape his life had taken, the way a man who has lived through a flood stops being surprised by mud.

The valley lay white and silent around them. Felix had walked these roads his whole life — to market, to the shrine, to the tannery where his mother had worked until her hands went soft with age — and he had never once thought of them as a cage. A valley was just a valley. The mountains ringing it were mountains. The strange quality of the light here, the way storms seemed to *thin* at the border and slide away like water off oiled cloth, was simply weather being kind.

He knew better now. He knew what the old ledgers in the under-croft had said, in handwriting gone brown with centuries: *The line is drawn. Let nothing cross. Let nothing remember crossing.* He knew what the word *taster* had meant before it meant a man who drank from the king's cup — *warden*, *watcher*, *keeper of the salt*. And he knew that the child beside him, whose name he still could not pronounce but whose grief he could taste like copper on his tongue, was the last living fragment of something his king's ancestors had murdered.

So they walked south, toward the salt line, and Felix found himself cataloguing everything the way he catalogued flavors. The frost on the fence posts: sweet-sharp, like sugared plums. The smoke from the last farmhouse chimney: bitter ash and someone's forgotten breakfast. The child's humming underneath it all: green things, growing things, a summer that had ended before any kingdom in this valley had a name.

He was memorizing. He did not know why. Some instinct older than his understanding told him to hold each ordinary thing up to the light and look at it fully, the way you look at a face you love when you suspect you will not see it again.

* * *

The salt line announced itself long before they reached it.

First the birds stopped. Not fled — stopped, mid-flight, wheeling back the way they'd come as though they'd struck glass. Then the snow changed, falling slower, hanging in the air like a held breath. And then Felix began to feel it in his teeth, in the roots of them, a low pressure like the moment before lightning — except this pressure had been there his whole life, so faint he'd never noticed it, and now that he was walking toward its source it swelled until his skull felt like a struck bell.

The child stopped humming.

The silence that followed was worse than any sound. Without her song, Felix could suddenly hear what the border actually was: a vast, grinding quiet, like the pause between two notes held too long, like a door that had been shut so long everyone had forgotten there was ever a room behind it.

"There," she said — not said, *hummed*, but the meaning arrived whole in his mind, and for the first time it came with an edge of fear that was not her own. It was his. She was showing him his own terror, reflected back, so he would know she saw it and did not despise him for it.

The salt line itself was almost nothing to look at. A seam in the snow, perhaps two paces wide, where the white took on a faint grey shimmer, like frost on the inside of a window. Beyond it the land continued — more snow, more pines climbing the far slopes — but the light beyond was wrong. Warmer-colored. Golden, almost, though the sun hadn't risen high enough to justify gold anywhere in this valley.

Felix stood at the edge of his entire life.

He thought about the ledger's warning. *Let nothing cross.* He thought about the scholars' faces when they'd failed to place the child's language, the way Lord Cassian's smile had gone thin and careful when he'd asked, so casually, whether the girl slept well. He thought about Maerlin — no. He didn't know that name yet. He thought about the stone singers he'd only seen in fevered fragments of the child's humming, mouths open mid-note, waiting three hundred years for someone to finish the phrase.

He thought about the soup he'd tasted yesterday, and the day before, and every day for fifteen years, and how each bowl had been the same, and how he had called that safety.

"I don't know what happens to me over there," he said aloud. His voice cracked the silence absurdly, small and human. "The records say men who crossed came back mad. Or didn't come back. The magic is supposed to—" He laughed, short and unhappy. "I'm explaining myself to a child who doesn't eat bread."

She looked up at him. Her eyes were the color of deep ice seen from above, blue shading into black, and in them was patience so old it made him feel like the child.

Then she took his hand.

Her fingers were cold, but not dead-cold — cold like river water in June, cold like something alive that had simply never learned warmth. She pressed his palm flat against her own throat, against the place where a voice lives, and began to hum.

It was the melody from the first night, the one that had made his teeth ache, but opened now, unfolded, given to him deliberately instead of leaking out of her in her sleep. And Felix understood what she was offering: a rope across a chasm. *Hold this. When you start to come apart, hold this.*

"Magic can't unmake me," he said slowly, testing the thought like a coin bitten for gold. "That's what you've been trying to tell me. Whatever erased the Choir, whatever the crown used — it slid off me. Like poison." Fifteen years of tasting the king's food, immune to every toxin in seven kingdoms, and he had thought it a small gift, a professional convenience. "Why? Why me?"

The child's humming turned enigmatic, almost amused. *Ask the salt,* it seemed to say. *Ask the line. You were made on this side of it for a reason, warden's-son. Walk through and find out what you are.*

He stepped forward.

* * *

Later — much later, in a realm where later was a flexible word — Felix would try to describe the moment of crossing and fail, because human language kept the descriptions of it the way a sieve keeps water.

His body expected death. Every nerve he owned braced for it, the way a man braces when the floor gives way. There was a tearing sensation, yes, but not of flesh — of *assumption*. Everything he believed about the world, about the valley, about the small safe shape of his own existence, was peeled back from him in a single bright instant, examined by something vast and curious, and then—

Held.

The child's song caught him the way a net catches a falling climber, and the something vast and curious looked at him through the mesh of that song, and Felix felt it *recognize* him.

Not see him. Not notice him. *Recognize* him — the way you recognize a scar, the way your tongue recognizes your own blood. Something in the fabric of the other side knew him, had known him, had perhaps been waiting for him with the terrible patience of a thing that measures time in centuries. Magic poured over him like water over stone and found, instead of resistance, a shape already cut to receive it. An old scar. A socket built for a keystone that had never been set.

He came out the other side on his hands and knees in snow that was not snow.

It crunched like sugar and sang like glass. That was the first thing: the singing. The whole world here was quietly, ceaselessly musical — the ground beneath him gave off a low sustained tone when his weight shifted, the air itself carried harmonics, and the trees — the trees were cathedrals.

There was no other word for them. They rose in ranks up the frozen hillsides, vast spires of ice grown into arches and buttresses and rose-windows, blue-white and translucent, and inside each one Felix could see — or thought he could see, or both — figures. Shapes like singers, motionless, their forms caught in the ice the way flies are caught in amber. Mouths open. Mid-note.

Three hundred years of mid-note.

"Oh," Felix said. It was all he had. The word fell out of him like a stone into a well.

The child knelt beside him and touched the singing snow, and where her fingers rested the tone shifted, resolved, became for one heart-stopping moment a chord — a chord with a gap in it, a chord missing voices, a chair at a table left empty so long the wood had grown around the absence.

*Come,* she hummed, and the meaning bloomed in him warm as brandy: *Come and see what you were spared. Come and see what you were saved for.*

They walked. The golden light grew stronger, pouring down between the ice-cathedrals, and Felix realized it wasn't sunlight at all — it was memory, or something like it, the residue of the covenant's last sung note still echoing off the vault of the sky, refusing to die. In this realm even abandonment was beautiful. That was somehow the most horrifying thing he'd seen yet.

And yet — and this was the part he would carry with him, the part that terrified him more than Cassian's hunters somewhere behind them, more than the ledger's warnings, more than the vast recognizing presence of magic itself — yet some shuttered room inside Felix was opening. Some sense he'd never known he possessed was waking up, stiff and blinking, like a man roused after decades. He could *taste* this world. Not with his mouth. With whatever he was, underneath the man named Felix Reyes, food-taster of a valley that had been built like a wall around him.

The air tasted of held breath and unfinished music. The ice tasted of grief so old it had polished itself smooth. And underneath everything, faint as a pulse, he tasted a direction. A pull. Somewhere ahead, something was waiting that had his name on it — not Felix Reyes, perhaps, but the name beneath the name, the reason a poison-proof boy had been born on the exact wrong side of a salt line.

The child tugged his sleeve. Ahead, the hills parted around a valley of their own — a mirror of his valley, he realized with a lurch, same shape, same river, same ring of peaks, as if the two realms had been cut from the same cloth and one of them had frozen mid-motion. At its center stood the greatest cathedral of all, its spire lost in cloud, its doors thrown open like arms.

From within it, impossibly, faintly, came a single sustained note.

One voice. Ancient, cracked, unwavering. Holding the line alone.

The child's hand tightened in his. *She waited,* the humming said, and for the first time since the night in the snow, Felix heard the child's own emotion underneath the words, raw and young and unbearably hopeful:

*Mamaerlin waited. Come — before the note runs out.*
