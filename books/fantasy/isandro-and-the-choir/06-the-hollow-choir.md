## 6. The Hollow Choir

The cathedral had been singing when the winter took it.

Felix understood this before he understood anything else about the place, because the ice told him. It stood in great buttressed walls around them, blue as deep water, and inside the ice — suspended like flies in amber — were the shapes of sound itself. Rings of it. Ripples frozen mid-travel through air that had stopped moving a hundred years ago or a thousand; the child's humming suggested no one here kept count anymore.

She walked ahead of him up the nave, her bare feet leaving prints that steamed faintly, as though the ground remembered warmth and resented it. She had not spoken — she never spoke in words, only in that language older than kingdoms, carried in hum and breath and the small bells of her throat — but all day her song had been climbing toward something, note by note, like a woman ascending stairs she both longed for and dreaded.

Now she stopped at the threshold of the choir loft and went silent, and the silence was worse than any warning Felix could have invented.

"Isandro," he said softly, using the name he'd given her because she had seemed to accept it, tilting her head at it like a bird considering a crumb. "What is this place?"

She answered in the old tongue, and the words arrived in his mind already translated, which still felt like swallowing something warm and impossible: *The house where they were singing.*

He stepped past her into the loft, and looked down into the body of the cathedral, and forgot how to breathe.

They were stone. Every one of them. Rows upon rows of singers rising in curved tiers like a hand cupped around an absent flame — men and women and children, robed, mouths open mid-note, eyes closed or wide, hands raised or clasped or flung out in the gestures of a music caught at its crescendo. Stone of a gray so pale it was nearly white, veined with frost, each figure detailed enough that he could see the strain of song in their throats, the effort of high notes in the tendons of their necks. A boy no older than Isandro stood on the lowest tier with his mouth stretched around a note too big for him, fists clenched, giving everything.

None of them had finished.

"They were turned mid-verse," Felix whispered. The cold took his breath and made it visible, a small ghost that drifted over the rail and dissolved. "The whole choir. Mid-*verse*."

Isandro's small hand found his. Her fingers were freezing, but her grip was certain. She sang one low phrase, and it came to him as: *This is what happens when the covenant breaks while it is being kept.* A pause. Then, quieter, in a register of the old language he hadn't heard from her before, one that made his back teeth ache: *Some of them are not stone all the way through.*

* * *

He heard her before he saw her.

It was not singing. It was the memory of singing — a single voice practicing scales in a room far away, thin as thread, coming from behind the altar where the apse curved into shadow. Felix followed it with Isandro pressed against his side, past candle niches that had held candles once, past an altar of black ice polished to a mirror, into a shallow alcove where someone had built a life out of almost nothing: a pallet of woven reeds, a clay pot, a bundle of feathers sorted by size, and an ancient woman sitting upright against the wall with her eyes shut and her lips moving through exercises no one else could hear.

Her skin was gray as the singers outside, and at first Felix thought she was stone too — stone that had learned, over centuries, to keep breathing. Then her eyes opened. They were brown. Warm brown, ordinary brown, the brown of river water in summer, and they were the first warm color Felix had seen since crossing the salt line.

"Well," the woman said. Her voice was cracked and rusted, a hinge unused. "A taster. And a foundling. And neither of you stone." She laughed, and the laugh cost her visibly, rattling in her chest like a coin in an empty jar. "The winter has grown careless."

"You know what I am," Felix said.

"I know what your valley is." She worked her jaw as if loosening it after a long sleep. "A mouth with the teeth pulled. A room built to keep one kind of word out. Sit down, boy, my eyes are bad and I would rather hear you than look at you anyway."

He sat. Isandro did not; she stood very still, staring at the old woman with an expression Felix had never seen on her face — something between recognition and grief, the look of a child finding a coat in a closet that had belonged to someone dead.

"What's your name?" Felix asked.

"Maerlin." She said it the way people say the names of the dead. "Contralto, second circle, left-hand harmony. I held the note of endurance for sixty years." Her mouth twisted. "And then I stopped. And I have been sitting in this alcove ever since, remembering everything except why."

* * *

She taught him the way winter teaches — slowly, relentlessly, without mercy or malice.

"The covenant is not a spell," Maerlin said, on what Felix guessed was the third day, though days were hard to count under a sky that never changed. "Say it wrong and every scholar who ever wrote about us said it wrong. It is not a binding laid on two realms. It is a *song* laid on two realms, and the difference is everything. A binding holds whether or not anyone believes in it. A song must be sung. Continually. Freely. By voices that choose it."

She gestured with a feathered stick at the tiers beyond the alcove, at the hundreds of open mouths.

"We chose it. That was the whole miracle. Every realm has power that wants to swallow its neighbor — yours wanted our magic, ours wanted your permanence, your things-that-stay, your bread that doesn't sing back. The Choir stood between and sang the border into being, and we sang it because we loved both sides of it. Not because we were made to. Not because we inherited the duty." Her brown eyes fixed on him, sharp despite their dimness. "That is the part your crown never understood, boy, and the part they feared most. You cannot seize a freely given thing. You can only kill the givers and hope the song forgets how to be sung."

"And when you killed them," Felix said quietly, "mid-verse—"

"Mid-verse," Maerlin agreed. "The worst possible moment. A verse is a promise being kept. Break it there and the promise breaks *forward* — into all the verses that would have come after. That is why the winter does not end. It isn't weather. It is an unfinished phrase, repeating, waiting for someone to finish it." She coughed, long and wet. "Two realms, unfinished. Everything since has been the echo."

Isandro crept closer during these lessons. On the fourth day she sat beside Maerlin and let the old woman touch her hair, and neither of them spoke, and Felix understood that some recognition was passing between them that had nothing to do with language.

* * *

On the fifth day, Maerlin tried to teach him to sing.

"It will be nothing," she warned him. "You are a valley man. Your voice grew up behind a wall, untrained, unaired, like a lung that never breathed smoke. But I need to hear what the covenant left in you."

He stood in the apse beneath the black ice altar, feeling absurd, and she set him a single sustained tone — the note of endurance, she called it, the note she herself had held for sixty years.

What came out of him was terrible. Thin, wavering, flat. He heard it and winced.

But Maerlin had gone rigid against the wall, her hand pressed flat over her sternum, and her expression was one Felix had never seen on a living face — the expression of the stone singers, perhaps, caught mid-note.

"Do that again," she whispered.

He did. It was still terrible. But this time he felt it too — a faint pull behind his breastbone, as though the note were not coming *from* him but *through* him, as though somewhere far off, on the other side of the salt line, in a valley where nothing strange survived, something had just felt a fingertip tap against glass.

"Why can I do even that much?" he asked. "I'm nobody. I taste soup."

"You are the opposite of nobody," Maerlin said. "Think, boy. What is a poison? A foreign truth the body must refuse. You spent your life refusing truths gracefully, daily, professionally — and the valley's great refusal, the border itself, is made of the same stuff. Magic cannot enter you because you are, in your small mortal way, the same shape as the wall." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to something fierce and urgent. "But a wall is only half a covenant. The other half is a door. And a door that opens freely—" she tapped his chest with one gray finger, "—that is what a voice is. That is what the crown murdered us for failing to grasp. They thought they were killing a lock. They were killing a *choir*. And choirs," she said, "can be reforged. From any throat willing to give itself."

"Give itself to what?"

"To the verse." She settled back against the wall, suddenly very tired, suddenly very old, older than the stone around her. "There is a rite. First verse, reforging. My teacher's teacher helped write it, back when writing it seemed like a foolish precaution." A dry laugh. "We were optimists then. We believed the song would always have singers."

"Teach me the rite," Felix said.

Maerlin looked at him for a long time. Outside, wind moved through the frozen rings of sound, and the whole cathedral creaked like a ship, and somewhere in the tiers above, ice calved from a stone singer's uplifted hand and shattered on the floor, and none of the singers stopped singing.

"You don't know what you're asking," she said.

"No," Felix agreed. "But I've watched a child walk barefoot through snow to find the one person alive who could hear her. I know what asking costs. Teach me."

* * *

She began teaching him that night, and by the light of nothing — the ice gave off a pale luminescence, the trapped songs glowing faintly in their amber — she taught him the first phrases of the reforging rite, and Felix discovered that the old language lived not in the mind but in the body, in the placement of breath, in the shape of the throat, and that his body had apparently been waiting all his life for instructions.

But there was a problem, and Maerlin named it on the second night, and named it gently, which frightened him more than cruelty would have.

"The rite requires a full voice," she said. "Not skill. Fullness. A voice carrying everything its owner has ever been — every taste, every face, every name. The covenant is fed with a self, boy. That is the price of freely given. You cannot give what you have not wholly been." Her brown eyes were kind and terrible. "Mine is nearly spent. Sixty years of holding one note eats a self down to the pith. But mine will carry the first verse. Mine will buy you the beginning."

"And the rest?" Felix asked, though he already knew. He had known since the salt line, since the moment magic had recognized him like an old scar. Some knowledge arrives in the body long before the mind consents to it.

"The rest comes from you," Maerlin said. "From everything you remember. And Felix—" she used his name for the first time, and hearing it in the old woman's mouth, in this place, made it sound like a word in the rite already, "—when the time comes, do not hold anything back. A half-given voice makes a half-made covenant, and a half-made covenant is how we got *here*."

He lay awake that night on the reed pallet beside Isandro, listening to the cathedral creak, watching the frozen songs glow in their rings overhead like constellations that had fallen and been caught. He tried to inventory himself, the way he inventoried flavors — almond, first; bitterness under sweetness, always check; the metallic ghost of a blade that had touched the broth. His mother's kitchen. The king's laugh. The smell of the valley after rain. The names of everyone he had ever tasted soup beside.

All of it, feed for a fire he had not yet agreed to stand inside.

Near dawn, Maerlin woke him. She was standing, which he had never seen her do, one hand braced on the wall, and her head was cocked toward the cathedral's western door with an expression he had learned, in five days, to dread.

"Someone is coming up the valley road," she said. "One man. Alone. And he is walking like a person who has already decided how this ends."
