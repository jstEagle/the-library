## 7. Cassian's Winter

The border did not want him.

Cassian felt it the way a man feels a door held shut against his shoulder — a pressure, a gathering resistance, as though the whole valley had turned its face away and was pretending he had never existed. He stood at the salt line with six men behind him and the wind coming down off the mountains carrying a cold that had no business being in the world, and for a long moment he simply breathed and let the valley refuse him.

It refused everyone. That was its purpose. That had always been its purpose. A wall of forgetting and unbeing, drawn by men who called themselves wardens and were, in truth, jailers — and on this side of it, everything the crown had spent three hundred years pretending was a story.

"Lord Cassian?" Ser Brandt shifted behind him, gloved hands flexing on his sword hilt. The man's breath came out in white plumes that the wind tore apart too quickly. "The horses won't cross. They're refusing."

"Horses are sensible." Cassian pulled off one glove, finger by finger, and pressed his bare palm flat against the air above the salt line. Nothing happened. No flash, no voice, no scar of old magic waking to recognize him. Just cold air and the faint mineral smell of the boundary stones, and beneath it — so faint he might have imagined it, except that he had been hearing it in his dreams since he was four years old — a thread of song.

They had never let him sing. That was the first thing they took, before the name, before the family, before anything: they taught him that his voice was a weapon pointed at the throne, and weapons were kept sheathed or broken.

He stepped over the line.

* * *

Crossing was not death and it was not madness. It was worse. It was *recognition*.

Magic poured into him like water into a cracked jar, filling every fissure the crown had left in him, every hairline fracture where a boy's certainty used to be. He staggered two steps into the snow beyond the border and caught himself on a boundary stone worn smooth by centuries of weather that should not have existed here, and for one terrible, glorious moment he could hear the whole frozen country singing — ice over stone, ice over river, ice over the buried bones of orchards — a vast slow chord sustained so long it had become indistinguishable from silence.

Then it passed, and he was only a man in a winter that did not end, with six soldiers who had crossed because their oaths outweighed their fear, looking at him for orders.

"It recognizes the land," Cassian said quietly. "Not us. We're guests here whether we like it or not. Move fast, stay close, and if you hear singing that isn't mine, you stop walking until I tell you otherwise."

"And if we hear singing that *is* yours, my lord?"

Cassian looked at the grey sky, at the light coming through it thick and pearly, like light through the skin of an onion. Somewhere ahead, past the frozen terraces and the drowned bell-towers standing up out of the ice like drowned men's hands, there was a cathedral full of stone singers with their mouths open mid-note, and in it, if Maerlin still lived, the last true voice of the Choir.

"If you hear singing that is mine," he said, "then I am already lost, and you should do what you came to do."

* * *

They found the first of the stone singers at dusk, or what passed for dusk here — the light simply thickened and went bruise-colored, and the cold deepened from hostile to personal.

The singer stood alone in a field of black ice, robed figure half-sunk in frost, head thrown back, mouth open on a note it had been holding since before Cassian's grandfather's grandfather drew breath. Snow had drifted up around it in a perfect cone. Someone had hung ribbons on its outstretched fingers once, centuries ago; they were frozen now, stiff as wire, still turning slightly in a wind too gentle to move them.

Ser Brandt made a sound low in his throat. One of the younger men, Veyra, crossed herself with the wrong hand — the old sign, the pre-covenant sign, the one the Church had forbidden and forgotten forbidding — and then stared at her own fingers as if they had betrayed her.

"Don't," Cassian said. Not unkindly. "Whatever you're remembering, don't. It isn't yours."

"But my grandmother—" Veyra stopped. Swallowed. "My grandmother used to make that sign when the wells froze early. She said it was for the ones who hold the note."

"The ones who held the note," Cassian corrected. "Past tense. That is rather the point of all this."

But he looked at the stone singer a moment longer than he needed to, and what he felt looking at it was not triumph and not horror but something smaller and more shameful: homesickness. He had been a child among these figures. He had played hide-and-seek in the drifts around them while the adults rehearsed, and the whole cathedral had hummed under his feet like a struck bell, and he had believed — with the absolute, unexamined faith of the very young — that the world was held together by people who loved it enough to stand in one place forever and sing.

Then the crown's men came with wagons and writs, and the children were sorted like coin: the pretty ones to noble households, the strong-voiced ones to the crown's own chapel, and the rest — the rest were never spoken of again, and the covenant was declared a heresy, and the winter began, and everyone agreed it was natural.

Everyone agreed. That was the crown's true genius, Cassian thought, wading on through the blue dusk. Not the murder. Murder was easy; any kingdom could manage murder. The genius was making the survivors grateful. Making a stolen child love the thieves, because the thieves fed him and named him and told him, gently, year after year, that the people who sold him had done it *for his own good*, that the Choir had been dying anyway, that the covenant was a cage and the palace a liberation.

He had believed it for thirty years. He had served the man who signed the order, and the man's son, and the son's son, tasting their suspicions and burying their enemies, and he had told himself the ache under his breastbone was ambition, or conscience, or indigestion — anything but grief.

Until the taster. Until Reyes, who could not be poisoned, standing in the snow with a mute child humming a language older than kingdoms, looking at Cassian across a kitchen table with those honest, hopeless eyes and saying, *I have to take her out of here.*

And Cassian had said, *You will die.* And Reyes had said, *Probably.* And that had been the end of the conversation and the beginning of this pursuit, because Felix Reyes was the kind of man who heard *you will die* and answered *probably*, and such men could not be permitted to unravel three hundred years of careful silence.

* * *

They caught the fugitives at the cathedral steps on the second morning, which surprised no one, least of all Cassian. Where else would they go? The child had been walking toward it in her sleep.

Felix stood at the base of the stairs with the girl behind him, one arm curved back around her like a gate, and even now, even here, hunted across the edge of the world, he looked less like a fugitive than a man caught mid-chore, a taster interrupted between courses. There was frost in his dark hair and terror in his face and absolutely no intention of moving anywhere.

"Cassian." His breath smoked. "You don't have to do this."

"No," Cassian agreed. "I rarely have to do anything. It's a privilege of rank." He descended from the ridge alone, waving his soldiers to a halt, and walked toward the steps with his hands visible and empty. Behind Felix, half-hidden by his coat, the child watched him with eyes the color of deep ice — not cold, he corrected himself. Deep. There was a difference, and he had spent thirty years refusing to learn it.

"I'm going to offer you a bargain," Cassian said. "I'd ask you to hear all of it before you refuse, but we both know you'll refuse, so let me at least say my piece in front of witnesses. The sky can witness. The stones can witness. They've heard worse."

"Cassian—"

"You were born inside the wall," Cassian said. "You grew up in the valley. You tasted the king's soup and slept soundly and believed the world was more or less the shape of your kitchen. And it worked. Do you understand? The wall *worked*. Nothing strange survived there. Nothing magical. Nothing that could hurt you. Your whole life was bought and paid for by the suffering of people on this side of the line, and you never knew, and because you never knew, you were *happy*. That is not a crime, Reyes. That is the entire design."

Felix's jaw tightened. "And the child?"

"The child is the last voice of the covenant, and while she lives and breathes and hums, the winter has a handle to grow by and the crown has a debt coming due." Cassian spread his hands. "Return her to me. Walk back over the line. You'll keep your memories — all of them, the valley, the kitchen, whatever your mother's face looks like. The king will pardon you; I'll see to it personally. You'll taste soup until your tongue gives out, and in twenty years you'll tell yourself this was a fever dream you were wise enough to walk away from. And the winter finishes what it started, and both realms settle into quiet, and no one else has to lose anything."

"Except her."

"Except her," Cassian said, and was surprised at how easily the words came, how little they cost. "Yes. Except her. One child, Reyes. Against two realms going quietly into the ground instead of screaming about it. I've done the arithmetic every night for thirty years. It comes out the same every time."

"And what did it cost *you*?" Felix asked softly. "The arithmetic. What did they pay you?"

The question went in under his ribs like a knife slipped between armor plates, and Cassian hated him for it, hated the accuracy of it, hated that a food-taster with flour still under his fingernails had seen in ten minutes what Cassian had spent three decades not looking at.

"They paid me a name," he said. "A house. A place at the right hand of power. They paid me the story that I'd been rescued." His voice stayed level; he had trained it level for thirty years, and it would stay level now if it killed him. "I was four years old. My mother sang the second verse of the reforging rite, and I sang the descant above it, badly, the way children sing. Then men came with a writ and a wagon, and she put me in the wagon herself, because the alternative was watching me be put in it, and either way the wagon went to the capital. And the Choir sang on without me for another year before the soldiers came for the rest, and I have spent my entire life being told that I was the lucky one."

The wind moved the frozen ribbons on the distant stone singer. Far off, under the ice, something vast shifted its weight and groaned, like a cathedral clearing its throat.

"So yes," Cassian said. "Let the winter finish. Let the world go quiet. Quiet is not the worst thing, Reyes. I've heard the alternative. The alternative is a chord that holds forever and asks everything and gives nothing back but the holding, and I was *in* it, and they took me out, and I have been cold ever since." He drew a slow breath. "Give me the child. Keep your memories. Go home. This is the only kindness anyone will offer you on this side of the line, and I am offering it because you fed me soup once when I was ill and didn't know who I was, and you sat with me anyway. That is the whole of my mercy. It isn't much. It's everything I have."

Felix looked at him for a long moment. Behind him, the child stepped forward — one bare foot on the frozen step, then another — and began, very softly, to hum.

Cassian knew the melody. Of course he knew it. It was the descant. The one he had sung badly, at four years old, in a cathedral that hummed underfoot like a struck bell, before the wagon, before the name, before the cold.

His hand closed around his sword hilt without his permission.

"No," he said, to the child, to himself, to the sky. "No. Don't you dare."

She hummed it again, a little louder, offering it to him across the snow like a cupped handful of water — and high above them, in the mouth of the frozen cathedral, something ancient stirred, and began, very slowly, to listen.
