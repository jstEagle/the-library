## 8. The Price of a Voice

Maerlin sang the first verse at dawn, and the cathedral wept.

Felix heard it before he understood it — a sound moving through the frozen pillars like water through roots, rising from the old woman's throat in a register no human voice should have owned. The ice that sheathed the walls did not melt. It *listened*. Frost patterns shifted, rearranged themselves into spirals, into script, into something older than script. Above them, through the broken crown of the roof, the grey light of the endless winter seemed to lean inward, attentive.

The child sat beside Maerlin on the cracked flagstones, knees drawn up, her bare feet blue with cold. She had refused every shoe they offered her. Her humming had changed since they'd entered the Hollow Choir — deeper now, more urgent — and Felix had stopped trying to translate it word by word. Some things were not words. Some things were weather.

Maerlin's verse was the first verse. Felix knew this because she had told him so the night before, sitting by their small fire of singing-wood, her hands folded like a woman settling accounts.

"The rite has three verses," she'd said. "The first binds the singer to the song. The second binds the song to the stone. The third—" and here her eyes, milky as frost ferns, had found his face with terrible gentleness, "—the third binds the singer to the price."

"And what is the price?"

"You already know. You've known since you crossed the salt line and didn't die. Ask yourself why the valley kept you safe, taster. What is a man who cannot be poisoned? He is a vessel. And vessels exist to be filled — or emptied."

Now, in the dawn light, she filled him.

Her fragment of the covenant-song poured into Felix like heat into cold iron. It was not learned; it was *remembered* — a melody he had never heard and somehow had always carried, folded small beneath his tongue where he kept the memory of flavors. He felt it settle into the hollow places inside him, the ones he had never named: the loneliness of a boy who tasted death for a living, the strange grief of a man whose gift was only ever useful when something went wrong.

When Maerlin finished, the silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

She swayed. Felix caught her arm — papery skin over bird-light bone — and lowered her down against a fallen singer's knee, one of the stone choristers whose open mouth had been mid-note for a hundred years or a thousand. Up close, he could see that the stone mouths were not screaming. They were *holding* a note. Waiting for someone to take it up again.

"First verse," Maerlin whispered. Her breath fogged and did not disperse; it hung in the air, a small ghost. "Done. Now you know what I know. Now you carry what I carried." A smile moved across her face like light under ice. "A hundred years I've been the last voice. Do you know what that is, Felix Reyes? It is being a library with one book, and the book is burning, and you must read it aloud faster than the fire."

"Rest," Felix said. "We have time."

"We have exactly as much time as your voice holds out." She gripped his wrist with surprising strength. "Listen to me. The song will ask its price when you sing the whole of it. It will not ask politely. It will reach into you and take what makes you *you* — because that is the only fuel a covenant burns. Voices freely given. Not inherited, not stolen, not sold." Her gaze flicked, for just a moment, toward the child. "Not taken from children and given to kings. Given. Do you understand the difference?"

Felix thought of Cassian somewhere behind them in the white country, following their trail with soldiers and hatred and a wound that had never closed. He thought of the crown's ancestors, who had murdered the Choir to hold both realms in one fist, and of the winter their murder had loosed — a winter that would not end until the covenant stood reforged, or until everything on both sides of the salt line froze waiting for it.

"I understand," he said.

"No," Maerlin said softly. "You understand the words. Understanding comes when you lose the first thing. And then you will still choose. That is what freely given means — chosen *again*, after you know." She pressed his hand flat against her sternum, and beneath the skin he felt nothing beating. Only a resonance, faint as a tuning fork long after it's been struck. "I am nearly empty, taster. I gave my verse and now there is very little Maerlin left. But listen — listen while I can still say it."

She pulled him close. Her voice was a thread, but it did not waver.

"It will take your memories. Not all at once — it takes them the way you'd eat a feast. Best first. Sweetest first. It will start with your mother."

Felix's chest tightened. "How do you know that?"

"Because it started with mine." For a moment the ancient woman was a girl, and the girl was grieving. "I sang the second verse once, long ago, before I stopped. Before I forgot why I stopped. The song took my mother's name, and I chose to keep singing anyway, and that choice is the only thing about me that never faded. Remember this: when it takes her name, you will feel relief. That is the cruelest part. Grief hurts, and forgetting doesn't. You will want to let go of everything after that. Don't. Hold on. Choose with your eyes open, or the song will choose for you."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, if preparation was possible for such a thing.

Felix walked the cathedral alone while the child slept against Maerlin's side. He catalogued himself the way he catalogued a dish — methodically, lovingly, a taster taking inventory of the last meal. His mother's kitchen: browned onions, woodsmoke, the burnt-honey smell of milk left too long on the stove because she was dancing with him instead of watching it. His father's face, blurred even now, gone early. The king's soup, saffron and leek, the morning everything began. The scholars' sneering voices. The child in the snow, barefoot, humming a melody that made his teeth ache. The taste of fear — copper and wet slate — the night he smuggled her out through the kitchens. The salt line. The moment magic had recognized him like an old scar.

He held each memory up to the light and looked at it, and put it back, and whispered goodbye to it in advance. It felt absurd. It felt like writing his own eulogy in a language he was fluent in and would soon forget.

Near dusk, Maerlin called him to her.

"It's time," she said. "I'll teach you how the fragments join. Mine to yours. Then the child's — she carries the third piece, the largest, the one the Choir hid in her before she could walk. When the three are whole, you sing it from the steps, aloud, into both realms at once. And the song will feed."

"On me."

"On what you love best." She smiled, and it was the kindest, most terrible expression Felix had ever seen. "That's the covenant's honesty, taster. It never lies about the bill."

She took his hands. She began to teach him the joining — how her fragment slotted into his like two halves of a seal, how the harmonies braided, where the rests fell like held breath. And as she taught, she grew quieter, and lighter, and more transparent in a way that had nothing to do with the light. Words she'd used that morning simply stopped existing between them; Felix could see the gaps where they'd been, holes in the conversation shaped like concepts.

By full dark, she could no longer remember the second verse she'd once sung.

By midnight, she could no longer remember her own name.

But she remembered the song. She held onto it the way a drowning sailor holds a spar, and she passed it to him completely, wholly, pressing it into his chest with both hands as though planting a seed in frozen ground.

"There," she whispered. Her eyes had gone clear as a winter sky — empty and bright at once. "It's yours now. All of it. I'm—" She frowned, searching herself, and found only music. "I'm the part that sings," she decided, wonderingly. "Yes. That's what I am. The part that sings."

She died an hour before dawn, sitting upright against the stone chorister, her mouth curved around the shape of a note she was no longer there to hold. The ice of the cathedral took her gently. Frost climbed her body and made her part of the architecture — another singer, mouth open, holding her verse forever.

The child woke and keened — a sound with no language in it at all, pure grief, and Felix understood every word of it anyway.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat by the dead fire with the child's head against his shoulder and rehearsed.

Not the song. He knew the song now; it lived in him like a second heartbeat. He rehearsed the *choosing*. He built the moment in his mind the way Maerlin had told him to: the song reaching for his mother's name, and Felix — knowing what forgetting would feel like, knowing the relief, the terrible soft ease of it — saying *yes* anyway. Saying yes a hundred times, a thousand times, once for every memory the covenant would burn.

"Will it hurt her?" he asked the child quietly. "If I do this. If I become the keystone. Will you remember me?"

The child looked up at him with eyes the color of deep ice over deep water. She hummed three notes. Low. Tender. Final.

Felix translated, because translation was the only gift he had ever been given, and he would use it until it was taken from him.

*Some covenants outlive memory,* she said. *I will keep yours.*

"That's not fair," Felix said, and his voice broke on it. "You're a child. You shouldn't have to carry a grown man's life."

She hummed four more notes, and these were older, and sadder, and they meant: *No one should. That is why the Choir was many. That is why you must make it many again.* Then she laid her small hand over his heart, right where Maerlin had planted the seed of the song, and left it there, warm as a coal, until the grey false dawn came.

* * *

He climbed to the cathedral steps as the light came — such light as this realm had, thin and silver, the sun a pale coin behind permanent cloud. Below the steps the frozen city spread out, spires and bridges all sheathed in singing ice, and beyond it the white waste, and beyond that, impossibly far, the faint dark line of the salt border and the valley he had called home for thirty-one years without ever once wondering why nothing strange survived there.

The child stood at his left. Behind them, in the doorway, the stone choir waited with their held note.

Felix opened his mouth.

The song rose in him like water finding its level — Maerlin's fragment joining his, braiding, completing the first verse, and the verse went out of him into the ice and the ice *answered*, every pillar ringing, every frozen bridge humming, the whole dead city becoming instrument. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever been part of, and he had spent his life around food, which is to say around the closest thing a body can make to joy.

Then the second verse began, and the song asked for its price.

It came for him gently. That was the horror of it. No tearing, no theft — just a warmth spreading through his chest, and a memory lifting free like steam off a bowl, and the warmth saying, in a voice that was his own voice: *this is heavy, isn't it? You've carried it so long. Set it down.*

His mother's kitchen. Browned onions, woodsmoke, burnt-honey milk.

And beneath the memory, holding it up, her name — the word he had spoken ten thousand times before he could speak anything else. *Mama.* No. Her true name. The one his father had called across rooms. The one carved nowhere, written nowhere, alive only in him now, in all the world.

The song reached for it.

And Felix felt the relief Maerlin had promised — the softening, the unburdening, grief dissolving like sugar in warm water — and he understood, with the last of his clarity, that this was the moment. The choosing. Here, now, with his mother's name already half-dissolved on his tongue like snow.

He thought: *I am a taster. My whole life I have taken the bitter first so others could eat sweet. Let this be the last dish.*

He thought: *Take it. Take it all. But I give it — do you hear me? I give it freely.*

He opened his hands, which held nothing, and gave.

The name went. He watched it go — felt it go — and where it had been there was smooth, clean emptiness, and the emptiness did not hurt at all, and that was the worst pain he had ever known.

The song surged through the gap like spring water through a broken dam, hungry now, gathering speed. The next memories rose: his father's blurred face. The king's soup. The kitchens. The scholars. The snow, the child, the knock at his door—

Below, far across the white waste, something dark was moving. Many somethings. Banners he couldn't yet read, and at their head a man on a black horse who had once been a Choir child, coming with soldiers and steel to stop a song with the only argument he had left.

Felix saw none of it. His eyes were closed. His voice was rising toward the third verse, and the song was eating, and he was feeding it — every taste, every face, every year of the valley — and he was still choosing, still saying *yes*, yes, yes, as the place he came from dissolved note by note into the music that would save it.

The last thing he held onto — deliberately, stubbornly, a man gripping a doorframe in a flood — was not a memory at all.

It was the child's hand, warm on his chest.

And then the third verse opened its mouth, and the world held its breath to listen.
