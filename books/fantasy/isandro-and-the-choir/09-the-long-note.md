## 9. The Long Note

The cathedral steps were black ice, and Felix climbed them the way a man climbs toward his own execution — slowly, deliberately, with his eyes open.

Behind him, the child's small hand gripped two of his fingers. Ahead, the doors of the Hollow Choir stood flung wide, as if the building itself had been waiting for this and could not be bothered with ceremony anymore. Wind poured out of that opening like breath from a dead god's lungs, carrying the smell of stone and old snow and something underneath both — a sweetness, faint as memory, that made his chest ache in a place he had no name for.

*You will forget,* Maerlin had told him, three days dead now, her last note still living somewhere in his throat like a swallowed coal. *The song does not take what you offer. It takes what you love.*

He had thought he understood. He had sat through the night with the covenant-song spread across the frozen floor in chalk and ash, learning its shape the way he had once learned the shape of the king's palate — which flavors hid poison, which bitterness was only bitterness. The song had verses like courses. It had a beginning you could learn from a book.

It was the ending that ate.

"Felix." Cassian's voice came from below, flat against the wind. "Step away from her. Step away from the door."

Felix turned. The spymaster stood at the foot of the steps with six soldiers fanned behind him, their armor rimed white, their breath pluming. But it was Cassian himself Felix watched — the way the man's hand did not rest on his sword but hovered near it, the way his eyes kept sliding, traitorously, to the cathedral doors. To the stone singers visible within, mouths open on notes they had held for three hundred years.

"You followed us across the salt line," Felix said. "You felt what I felt. This place knows you too."

"This place *abandoned* me." The words cracked something open in Cassian's voice; he seemed surprised by the sound of it. "I was four years old. They gave me to the crown because my voice was strong enough to matter and my family was poor enough to sell. Do you know what the Choir does with children who are sold instead of given? Nothing. There is no verse for us. No rite. We are simply *gone*, and the singing goes on, and no one—" He stopped. Breathed. When he spoke again his voice was the smooth instrument Felix had known for years, and that smoothness was worse than any rage. "The winter finishes, the realms stay separate, and everyone lives. That is all I am asking. Give me the child. Keep your memories. Go home to your valley and your king and your little prayers of salt and honey."

"And the people freezing on the other side of the border?" Felix asked quietly. "The villages under the snow since autumn?"

"The Choir let my mother freeze in a slum outside its own walls," Cassian said. "I have no verses for them either."

The child tugged Felix's fingers. He looked down. She was not looking at Cassian or the soldiers or the doors. She was looking at Felix, and her face — that still, ancient, unchildlike face — held an expression he had learned to read across a hundred silent nights: *now*. And beneath it, almost hidden, something he had never seen there before.

Fear.

She was afraid. Not of the swords. Of what she was about to give.

"I know," Felix said softly, though he could not have said what he knew. He crouched, so their eyes were level, and pressed her small cold hand between both of his. "Whatever you've been saving — whatever it is — you don't have to spend it alone."

She shook her head once. Then, deliberately, she placed his hand flat against her sternum, over her heart, and held it there.

And Felix understood, with a lurch like stepping off a cliff, that the voice she had been saving all along was not a song.

It was her whole self. Every word she would ever say. The language older than kingdoms lived in her alone now, and to join the covenant she would pour it out entire — the last voice of the Choir, spent to the dregs, leaving behind whatever silence came after.

"No," he whispered. "There has to be another—"

She smiled. It was the first smile he had ever seen on her, and it was worse than tears.

Then she opened her mouth, and began to sing.

* * *

The first note went through Felix like a blade of light.

Not sound — or not only sound. It was the thing underneath sound, the thing every melody he had ever heard had been clumsily imitating. It entered through his bones and rearranged them. On the steps below, two soldiers dropped their pikes simply to cover their ears, and one of them fell to his knees, and neither reaction was enough, because the note was not coming *at* them. It was coming up *through* them, out of some shared deep place where all voices were stored.

Felix opened his mouth and answered.

Maerlin's fragment rose in him — the first verse, the keel of the song — and his voice found it rough and untrained and *true*, and where his true note met the child's perfect one, the air itself caught fire with cold light. The covenant-song assembled itself around them the way a river assembles around rain: inevitably, without effort, each part finding the part it had been severed from three centuries ago. Stone mouths in the dark cathedral began, at last, to close — and reopen — moving again after three hundred years, taking up harmonies that had been waiting, patient as geology, for voices to carry them.

The winter screamed.

That was the only word for it. The sky above the frozen realm tore along a seam no eye could find, and through the tear came a howl like ten thousand winters denied, the accumulated fury of everything exiled, everything locked out, everything the valley's founders had drawn their salt line against. Snow moved sideways. The black ice of the steps shivered beneath Felix's bare feet — when had he taken off his boots? he could not remember; the song had already begun to eat the small memories, the taste of boot-leather, the name of the cobbler who made them—

*Cassian.*

The spymaster was climbing the steps. Sword drawn now, face wild, shouting something Felix could not hear over the music — or perhaps Cassian was not shouting at all anymore, perhaps he was screaming, because the song had found him too, had slipped past his armor and his anger into the sealed room where a four-year-old boy still waited for someone to come back for him.

"STOP—" Cassian's sword came up.

Felix did not stop singing. He could not have stopped if the world ended; stopping was no longer a thing his body knew how to do. But he turned as the blade fell, and he took the blow — not with flesh, though it cut him, a long cold line across the shoulder — but with the song. He sang *into* the blade, into the arm that swung it, into the man behind both, and he sang the only thing he had left that was worth giving: the verse of welcome. The verse Maerlin had died completing. *You are not gone. You were only ever waiting. Come home to your own note.*

Cassian froze mid-swing.

His sword-point touched the ice and stayed there, propping him up like a staff. His lips moved. No sound came out — and then sound came out, a single raw, broken tone, a child's note sung by a ruined throat, the note he had been stolen from before he could finish learning it. It slid into the covenant-song and fit, the way a key fits a lock built for it, and Lord Cassian, spymaster to a crown built on murder, sank down onto the frozen steps of the cathedral and wept like weather.

The song demanded everything then.

Felix felt it turn to him, vast and gentle, the way famine is gentle. It did not ask. It simply opened, and he fell forward into the giving, and the giving was his life.

His mother's name went first. He felt it leave — felt the word *Maren* lift out of him like a bird off a wire, and the wire sang where it had been, and then the wire was gone too, and there was a space in Felix where a mother had been, and even the grief for her began to blur, because grief needs a name to hold onto. Then the taste of honey-bread on festival mornings. Then the smell of the kitchen corridors, the particular hush of the palace at dusk, the king's face, the king's name, the word *king* itself dissolving into pure sound. The valley. The green of it. The safe, dull, blessed green. He clung to it and the song pried his fingers loose one by one, not cruelly — nothing so kind as cruelty — the way spring pries the ice from a river.

He was forgetting the valley. He was forgetting there had ever been a valley.

And the ice broke.

It began at the cathedral's crown, a crack like the first word of a new language, and ran down and outward, through the frozen gardens, the singing ice-fields, the realm of winter entire — and through the salt line, through the border that had stood for three hundred years, into the valley beyond, where snow that had never fallen began, everywhere, to melt. Spring did not arrive. Spring *flooded* — green light pouring through the tear in the sky, water running silver down the black steps, the stone singers' mouths filling with birdsong that had been waiting inside the silence all along.

The child's voice thinned. Spent. Felix caught her as she swayed, and the last of the song passed between them — her final measure flowing into his final measure, two emptinesses making one whole note — and the covenant closed around them like two hands clasping, and held.

Silence, after. Real silence, warm at the edges.

Felix knelt on wet stone in the flooding light, holding a sleeping child, his shoulder bleeding, his heart full — and empty. He searched for the valley and found only a smooth bright blankness, like the place where a tooth had been. He searched for why he was weeping, and found no reason at all.

Below him, Cassian still wept, sword abandoned, face turned up to the green.

And far away — across a border that no longer existed, in a palace where a food-taster's quarters stood empty and waiting — a knock sounded on a door, and no one remembered who had knocked, or why, or what had been carried in from the snow.

Only the melody remained. Hummed by no one yet. Waiting.
