## 10. Spring, Unremembered

Felix woke to the smell of bread and the sound of his own name being called from somewhere below.

"Felix! The king's breakfast won't taste itself!"

He lay still for a moment in the narrow bed of the taster's quarters, watching dust turn in a bar of morning light, and felt the peculiar vertigo of a man arriving in his own life late — as though he had come back from a long journey and found the house rearranged while he was away. Everything was where it should be. The copper pots on their hooks. The ledger of the king's meals open on the desk in his own careful hand. The window with its view of the kitchen garden, where the snow had melted weeks ago and the first green shoots stood in neat rows like an audience waiting for him to begin.

Snow had melted. He tried to remember when it had fallen.

There had been winter — he was sure of it, the way one is sure of a scar without remembering the wound. A long winter. A winter that had lasted past all reason, that had crept indoors and silvered the windowpanes and made the whole valley hold its breath. And then spring had come, sudden and total, the way a held note resolves. Everyone said so. The gardeners spoke of it as a miracle; the priests had declared a feast day. Felix remembered none of it. He remembered soup.

That was the strange thing about himself now, the thing he had learned not to say aloud: he could remember every meal he had ever tasted for the king — eleven years of them, catalogued like prayers — but the season before this one was a smooth white blank, like a page torn cleanly from a book. When he reached for it, his mind slid off, the way a tongue slides off a missing tooth.

*You are whole,* he told himself, as he did each morning. *You are safe. You are Felix Reyes, taster to the king, and nothing strange survives in this valley.*

He dressed, went down, and began to work.

* * *

The tasting was ritual, and ritual was the only language Felix had ever trusted. He stood at the king's left hand in the great hall while the servers brought the morning pottage, and he took up the silver spoon, and he did what he had been born to do: he tasted, and he waited, and he named what he found.

Barley. Wild thyme. A thread of honey against the salt of good stock. The last of the stored apples, cooked soft and sweet-sharp. Nothing wrong. Nothing foreign. He swallowed, counted his heartbeats — ten, twenty, thirty — and nodded to the king.

"Clean," he said. "Honest food."

King Aldric smiled down at him from the high seat, and there was something new in the smile these days, something almost shy. "You've grown thin, Felix," the king said. "Spring agrees with everyone but you, it seems."

"I'm well, Majesty." And he believed it, mostly. He was well. He was only — hollowed, somehow, in a way that didn't hurt. Like a house after the furniture has been carried out with great care, room by room, leaving clean squares on the floorboards where things used to stand. He kept finding the squares. A name he almost knew. A melody that lived behind his teeth. An ache, on certain evenings, shaped exactly like a woman's voice saying *mother*, though whose mother he could not have said.

"Then eat," the king said gently. "Sit with us. It's a feast day, or near enough."

Felix took a bowl and sat at the end of the long table, because the king asked, and because refusing would have required a reason, and reasons were the one thing he seemed to be out of.

That was when he saw her.

She sat at the king's right hand, in a chair taller than she was, dressed in spring colors — green, the green of new leaves — with her dark hair braided close to her head. A child of perhaps eight years. She ate with the unhurried gravity of someone much older, and the courtiers leaned toward her when she moved as if she were a fire they were warming themselves at. He had seen her before. He must have seen her before. She was clearly no stranger; the servants bowed to her, the king watched her with something like awe, and there was a cushioned bench by the hearth in the great hall that everyone seemed to orbit around, the way planets orbit a sun too bright to look at directly.

Her ward, someone had told him once. The king's honored ward. Found in the winter, rescued from it, brought inside. There had been a story. There was always a story.

But when Felix tried to hold the story in his mind, it slipped through his fingers like water through a net, and what remained was only this: the child turned her head across the length of the hall, found him at the far end of the table, and smiled.

It was not the smile of a child greeting a stranger.

It was the smile of someone recognizing a face across a crowded room after years apart — glad, and grieving, and certain. Her eyes were old. Old the way ice is old, old the way mountains are old, and they rested on him with a tenderness that made his chest tighten around something he could not name.

Felix's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

And then — because his body knew something his mind had forgotten — he began to hum.

It came up out of him unbidden, three notes and then five, a melody small and round and patient, the kind of tune a person might carry all their life without ever learning its name. He hummed it the way other men breathed. He had always hummed it, hadn't he? In the kitchens, over the ledgers, walking the walls at dusk. The cooks teased him for it. *What is that song, Felix? Where did you learn it?*

*I don't know,* he always answered, and it was true, and it was the truest thing about him.

Across the hall, the child set down her cup. Her lips moved — shaping words, or something older than words — and though no sound crossed the distance between them, Felix heard her anyway, the way you hear a bell through stone. Not in his ears. Somewhere deeper, in the place where the missing winter should have been.

*Good morning,* she said, soundlessly, in a language older than any kingdom. *You kept it. You kept the song.*

Felix blinked. The hall came back — the clatter of dishes, the king laughing at some joke, the smell of barley and thyme and honey. The child was simply eating her breakfast again, a ward among courtiers, and whatever had passed between them folded itself away into the ordinary morning like a letter slipped into a pocket.

But his hands were shaking, very slightly, around the spoon.

* * *

He worked through the day as he always worked: thoroughly, quietly, tasting everything twice. But the melody would not leave him alone, and by evening he found himself climbing the outer stair to the top of the wall, where the valley opened out below him — the rooftops, the gardens, the river running bright with snowmelt, and beyond it all the border stones standing pale in the last light.

He went there sometimes without knowing why. The guards had learned to let him be. *The taster likes his walk,* they said. *Strange fellow. Good man, though.* And Felix would stand at the wall's edge and look out at the line where the valley ended and the world began, and feel something in him lean forward like a dog at a gate.

Tonight the air smelled of blossoms. Spring had come to stay; the orchards along the river were white with it, and the light lay gold and long across the fields. It was beautiful. He knew it was beautiful the way you know a thing from a description — accurately, completely, secondhand. Some part of him stood just outside the beauty, looking in, and could not say why it did not weep.

Beyond the border stones, the hills rose blue into the dusk. Once — he had no memory of this, only a certainty that arrived whole, like weather — he had thought those hills were the edge of everything. Now he looked at them and felt the opposite: that they were a door, and doors exist to be opened, and somewhere on the other side was a country he had given something precious to keep safe.

*Given.* The word surfaced and hung there. Given, not lost. There was a difference, and the difference mattered, though he could not have explained it under oath.

Behind him, small feet on the stair.

He turned, and the child stood at the top of the wall-walk in the fading light, breathing a little hard from the climb, watching him with those old, old eyes. Neither of them spoke. There was no need. Words, he understood suddenly and completely, had never been the point between them.

She came and stood beside him at the parapet, a small figure in green, and together they looked out at the valley he no longer knew and the world he had never seen.

After a while, softly, she began to sing.

It was the melody. His melody — the one behind his teeth, the one the cooks teased him for — but whole now, unfolded, carrying verses he had never heard and somehow had never forgotten. It rose over the wall and out across the valley, and the blossoms along the river seemed to lean toward it, and far off in the blue hills something answered: a single sustained note, high and clear, like ice learning to become water.

Felix closed his eyes. Tears ran down his face, warm and easy, grief and joy in exactly equal measure, attached to nothing, belonging to no story he could tell.

"What is it?" he asked her, when the note had faded into the dusk. "The song. What is it called?"

The child took his hand. Her fingers were small and warm and certain.

"It isn't called anything," she said. "It's called *yes*."

They stood together until the stars came out, two figures on the wall above a valley full of spring — the taster who had forgotten everything, and the singer who remembered for both of them — humming, between them, a covenant that needed no memory to hold.

Some covenants outlive memory. Some songs are kept not in the mind but in the mouth, passed from lip to lip like flame, and burn brightest in the ones who no longer know what they are carrying.

Below them, the king's household lit its lamps one by one, and the valley settled into peace, and the door in the blue hills stood open, and spring — unremembered, unowned, freely given — went on forever.
