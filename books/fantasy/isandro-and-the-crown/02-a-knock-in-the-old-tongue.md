## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The solstice audit fell on the longest night of the year, which Hazel had always considered a small mercy of the calendar: if the guild's books were going to be dragged into the light, at least the light would be brief.

She worked by candle in the counting room on the third floor, where the windows were shuttered against the cold and the tallow burned with a smell like old pennies. Around her the Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in forty-one ledgers, each one bound in leather dyed a different shade of gray, because Corvane believed that impossibility should never announce itself in color. The ledgers held, among other entries: one bottled echo, purchased from a widow in Gildermere for eleven crowns and resold to a mourning-house for sixty; one left-handed shadow, acquired under circumstances the ledger described only as *delicate*; three jars of yesterday, contents evaporating at a documented rate; and a quantity of sleep belonging to a man who no longer wanted it, stored in a cask that hummed faintly when the moon was full.

Hazel entered all of it in her neat, upright hand. She was good at this—good at making the impossible look ordinary, columns and sums and little marginal notes in her own invented shorthand. That was the whole art of bookkeeping, she had decided years ago: you took the wildness of the world and taught it to stand in rows.

And that was the whole art of being Hazel Montrose, too.

Because Hazel Montrose was not quite a real name. It was a name assembled from spare parts—a surname borrowed from a gravestone in a parish two hundred miles south, a first name common enough to be invisible—and it belonged to a woman who did not exist before the age of fourteen, who had walked into the guild's front door with forged papers and a talent for figures and nothing else behind her, as though she had been conjured out of candle smoke. The guild had not asked questions. The guild, as a rule, did not ask questions about where things came from. It only asked what they were worth.

She dipped her pen, blotted the page, and let herself have the thought she permitted exactly once a year, on audit night, when the loneliness got loud enough to demand its toll:

*I am twenty-six years old and there is no record anywhere in the world that I exist.*

It was not entirely true. There were records—the guild's rolls listed an apprentice bookkeeper, H. Montrose, engaged seven years past, references verified by a clerk who had been paid to verify them. But those records pointed at her the way a signpost points at fog. Somewhere behind the name was a girl with another name, another tongue, another life, and Hazel had spent twelve years building walls between that girl and herself so carefully that she had nearly forgotten which side of the wall she lived on.

Nearly.

There were the dreams, of course. The dreams did not respect walls.

She shook off the thought and turned to the solstice inventory, the one ledger that mattered tonight, because tomorrow morning three masters of the guild would sit across from her and check every figure against every crate in the cellars, and Corvane's temper when the numbers wandered was a thing she had seen grown men weep over. She was halfway down the list—*one mirror that shows the room as it will be in an hour, cracked, value negotiable*—when she heard the knock.

*

It came from below. Not the street door, which was iron-banded oak and announced visitors with a bell Corvane had bought precisely so that no one could ever arrive unannounced. This was the side door, the mud door, the one used by smugglers and debtors and other people with reasons to avoid bells. And the knock itself was wrong—not a fist or a knuckle but something softer, patient, spaced. Three strikes. A pause long enough to hold a breath. Three more.

Hazel set down her pen.

The house was not empty. Two porters slept above the stables, the night porter dozed in the front hall, and Master Corvane himself was somewhere on the second floor with his brandy and his correspondence, which meant the knock was already someone else's problem. She told herself this firmly. She was a bookkeeper. Bookkeepers did not answer doors at midnight on the longest night of the year; that was how apprentices in cautionary tales ended up as entries in other people's ledgers.

But the knock came again, and with it—she would swear this later, under oath if it ever came to that, though nothing ever did—a smell. Wet earth. Woodsmoke. Something green and crushed, like stems broken off a plant she could not name.

She went down.

The stairs creaked their familiar complaints. At the bottom, the side passage was black except for the line of candlelight she carried, and the cold came through the door's seams like water through a net. She stood with her hand on the latch for the length of one breath, telling herself she would simply look through the grate, confirm it was a drunk or a beggar or a porter locked out, and fetch the night porter to deal with it.

She opened the door.

The child stood on the step as though she had grown there, like something the frost had pushed up out of the ground. Seven years old, perhaps eight—small for either. Mud to the knees, mud on her hands, a smear of it dried across one cheek like a scar. Her hair was dark and cut badly, hacked short with something duller than a knife, and her eyes were the color of deep water in winter. She wore a coat that had been made for someone twice her size, sleeves rolled and rolled again, and she was barefoot. Barefoot, in the snow, on the solstice night, and her feet—Hazel looked down and felt her stomach turn—her feet were not blue. They were not even red. They were muddy and pale and utterly unbothered, as though the child had walked through warmth all the way here and the cold had only caught up at the doorstep.

"Child," Hazel said. "Where is—do you have anyone? Are you lost?"

The girl looked up at her. And then she spoke.

The words were not words Hazel knew. That was her first, sane, sensible thought: *I don't know this language.* It had a sound like water moving under ice, vowels that opened and closed, consonants placed soft against the teeth. Four syllables, five, rising at the end like a question, falling like an answer.

And underneath the not-knowing, in some cellar of herself she had bricked over so long ago she'd forgotten the room existed, something stirred and *understood*.

Not all of it. Not the way one understands one's own name. But the shape of it—the meaning arriving ahead of the translation, the way a melody can make you grieve before you know what the song is about. Hazel stood in the doorway with her candle shaking and heard the child say, in a tongue erased from every map and every memory and every honest book in the kingdom:

*Cold. Door. You are the one who keeps the counting.*

"You're freezing," Hazel heard herself say, in her own language, uselessly, absurdly, and reached out, and the girl took her hand without hesitation, without fear, as though she had been sent—as though someone, somewhere, had told her exactly whose door to knock on and how many times.

*

Corvane came down within the quarter hour, drawn by the noise of the girl eating bread in the front hall with the desperate, silent speed of someone who has learned that food can be taken away mid-bite. He stood on the stairs in his dressing gown with a lamp raised, and Hazel watched his face do something she had never seen it do in seven years: stop.

He looked at the child the way he looked at inventory.

"Where did she come from?"

"The side door," Hazel said. "No escort, no note. She knocked."

"People don't knock on that door." He descended slowly. "Girl. Look at me." The child looked at him, chewing. "What's your name?"

The girl said something—three syllables, soft, ending in a sound like a bell underwater—and Corvane's lamp hand trembled, just once, just visibly enough that Hazel saw it and understood that whatever the word meant, Corvane recognized the language, and recognizing it frightened him.

"That," Corvane said quietly, "is not possible."

"Master?" Hazel kept her voice level, bookkeeper-flat. "Do you know what she's saying?"

"I know what it *is*." He set the lamp on the hall table with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something explosive. "That is Vessarine. Or it was. Or—" He stopped himself, jaw working, as though the word were contraband and he'd already said too much. "There is no such tongue. There has been no such tongue for a hundred years. It does not appear in any registry, any census, any record in this kingdom, because the kingdom does not permit it to."

"Then how do I know what she means?" The question escaped before Hazel could weigh it, and she watched it land, watched Corvane's head come around slow as a drawbridge.

"What do you mean, *know*?"

"She said—" Hazel faltered. How to explain it? That the words had arrived in her like rain finding cracks in old stone? "I don't know the language, master. But I understood her. Some of her. Cold, and door, and—" she hesitated, because saying it aloud would make it real, and real things had a way of getting written down, and everything written down could be found. "Counting. She said I keep the counting."

For a long moment Corvane looked at her, and Hazel had the vertiginous sense of standing on the far edge of a thought he was having, a thought she was not meant to see. Then it closed over, smooth as water.

"The cold has addled you both," he said briskly, and the mask of the guildmaster slid back into place, and beneath it, faintly, Hazel glimpsed the merchant doing arithmetic. "She cannot stay in the hall. If she speaks—that tongue—to anyone, we are all in the fire. Do you understand? The Crown pays informants by the word for less than this."

"She's a child, master. She's half-frozen and barefoot—"

"And she is now," Corvane said, "an item in this house. Which makes her my responsibility, and your problem, since you found her." He was already turning toward the back stair. "The counting room. She sleeps there tonight. You'll sit with her—I won't have her wandering, and I won't have her talking to the porters. In the morning we decide what she is."

*What* she is, Hazel noted, not *who*. She bit the observation back behind her teeth, where she kept most things.

*

The counting room was warm at least; its chimney shared a flue with the kitchen. Hazel brought blankets down from the linen press, and bread and honey, and a basin of water in which the girl scrubbed her hands with great seriousness while Hazel knelt and worked the mud from her feet, checking toes for frostbite and finding none, none at all, skin pink and healthy as a summer child's.

"Your feet," Hazel murmured. "You walked a long way, didn't you."

The girl watched her with those winter-water eyes. Then she said a word. One word, gentle, aimed at Hazel like something offered in an open palm.

And Hazel's whole body went cold, colder than the doorway had made her, because she knew that word. Not the way she'd known the sentences at the door—half-guessed, intuited. She knew this word the way you know a scar. It rose in her with a melody attached, a woman's voice humming low in a dark room, rocking, the smell of woodsmoke and crushed green stems, a word sung over and over into a small ear that had been her own—

"Hush," she said, in the dream-language, in the tongue that did not exist, and the syllables came out of her mouth whole and easy as breathing, as though they had been waiting behind her teeth for twenty years.

The girl smiled. It transformed her face entirely, mud and all.

"*Vessari,*" she said, and touched Hazel's wrist, and then, with the terrible uncomplicated honesty of children, asked a question of four words that Hazel understood perfectly and could not bear:

*Why do you hide?*

"I don't—" Hazel's voice cracked. She gripped the washbasin's rim. "I'm not hiding. I'm a bookkeeper. I keep accounts. I am no one in particular."

The girl considered this, head tilted, unconvinced in the way of the very young, who have not yet learned to accept lies politely. Outside, the wind worried the shutters. Somewhere above them, floorboards shifted under Corvane's pacing weight, back and forth, back and forth, a man walking the length of a decision.

Hazel banked the fire, spread the blankets, and got the child settled in the big chair by the hearth, and sat down opposite with the day's ledger open on her knees as a prop, a shield, a thing to stare at instead of the girl's knowing eyes. She wrote nothing. The columns swam.

*One key, animate.* The phrase surfaced in her mind unbidden, absurd, from nowhere—no. Not from nowhere. From the dreams. From the oldest dream, the recurring one, the one she had never told anyone: a stone door under running water, and a voice saying those exact words, and her own hands reaching—

She looked up. The girl was watching her over the blanket's edge, unblinking, patient as the tide.

"What are you?" Hazel whispered—in Vessarine, helplessly, the language coming to her now like a flood through a breached wall, carrying silt and names and a village called Ashfall that she had never visited and could draw from memory.

The girl answered with six words, and this time there was no mistaking them, no hiding behind half-understanding. They went into Hazel like a blade going into a lock.

*I was sent to find you.*

Above them, Corvane's footsteps stopped.
