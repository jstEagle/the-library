## 3. What the Guild Sells

The counting room smelled of tallow and old paper and, faintly, of the child.

Hazel noticed it the moment she opened the door at dawn—underneath the familiar musk of the guild's ledgers, something clean and animal and frightened, like a bird kept too long in a hatbox. Wren had slept curled in the corner behind the iron-banded chest where Corvane stored his private correspondence, using Hazel's own spare shawl as a blanket. She had not been given one. Hazel had left it there anyway, folded small on the floor, and said nothing to anyone about it, because saying things in this house was how things got entered into books.

She knelt beside the girl now, careful not to wake her, and studied her face in the grey light through the barred window. Mud still crusted the hollows under her eyes. Her hands, even in sleep, were curled as though holding something—a handle, a ring, a key. The thought arrived uninvited and sat down in Hazel's chest like an unwelcome guest.

*One child, found on the solstice night, speaking Vessarine.*

Vessarine. She turned the word over again, the way she had all night while pretending to balance columns that refused to balance. It was not a word she had ever learned. And yet when the girl had spoken it—those soft, rolling syllables that sounded like water going over stones—something beneath Hazel's ribs had *leaned toward the sound*, the way a plant leans toward light it has never seen but somehow knows.

Her mother had hummed. That was the shape of the thought that came next, unwelcome as the first. Her mother, dead these fifteen years, whose face Hazel could no longer summon whole—only hands, flour-dusted, and a melody without words. The melody had the same bones as what the child had spoken. Hazel had told herself for years that the tune was nothing, a nonsense song from nowhere, the way orphans collect fragments and build cradles out of them.

She was still kneeling there when the key turned in the lock behind her.

"Up, Montrose." Master Quillon's voice, dry as August. He was the guild's second bookkeeper, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that had long ago stopped seeing anything they did not expect to see. "Corvane wants the solstice inventory reconciled by noon. All of it."

"All of it?" Hazel rose, brushing off her knees. "That's four days' work."

"Then you'd best be quick." Quillon did not look at the child. That was the strange thing, Hazel thought—the way everyone in the guild had agreed, silently and completely, not to look at her. As if she were a stain on the ceiling that would go away if no one acknowledged it. "And Montrose." He paused at the door, and for once he did look—at Hazel, with something almost like pity, which frightened her more than anger would have. "The master has taken the new acquisition into his own ledger. You'll find the entry marked in red. You are not to amend it, annotate it, or question it. Do you understand?"

"I keep the books," Hazel said. "I don't write them."

"No," Quillon said softly. "You don't."

He locked the door behind him. Hazel stood in the silence, listening to his footsteps fade along the gallery, and felt the shape of the morning change around her, the way a room changes when you realize someone has been standing in the dark corner of it the whole time.

* * *

The guild's main ledger lived in the scriptorium, chained to its desk the way holy books were chained in churches, and for the same reason: because what was written in it was considered true, and truth, in the Guild of Impossible Things, was property.

Hazel had worked beside that ledger for six years. She knew its habits the way a wife knows a husband's breathing. She knew which entries were honest and which were cosmetics; she knew that the bottled echo sold to Lady Marchmain had actually been a bottled scream, and that the left-handed shadow purchased by the Theatre Royal had cost the guild two men, one of whom had never entirely come back. She knew all of this because keeping the guild's secrets legible was her job, and because she had made herself so quiet, so precise, so utterly indispensable, that no one remembered she was also a person who could read.

By nine o'clock she had reconciled three years of trade in impossibilities and found the red-marked entry.

It was dated the solstice, in Corvane's own hand—she recognized the cramped, downward-slanting script, the letters pressed so hard they embossed the page beneath. He had written it himself rather than dictate it, which he almost never did. Which meant it mattered. Which meant it was dangerous.

**Item the forty-first, acquired solstice eve. One key, animate. Female, approx. seven years. Provenance: found. Condition: intact, unspoiled, speech-bearing. Function: opens that which was closed. Disposition: held pending valuation. Reserve price: to be set after verification.**

Hazel read it twice. Three times. The candle guttered and she did not move to trim it.

*One key, animate.*

Not *one child*. Not *one refugee*, not *one orphan of the roads*, not even *one anomaly, pending classification*—the guild had categories for everything, she had helped invent half of them. But *key*. A word for a thing made of metal. A word for a thing whose entire purpose is to be used, to be turned in a lock by whoever holds it, and then put away in a drawer until wanted again.

Her eyes went back to *function: opens that which was closed*, and beneath it, in smaller script, added later, the ink a slightly different shade:

**NB — Vessarine confirmed by two independent listeners. Door presumed extant. Gentlemen of quality will pay extraordinary sums for access. Prepare viewing room.**

Hazel set down her pen very carefully, as though it might go off.

She thought: *the door.* She did not know why those two words landed in her with such weight, such terrible familiarity, as if she had always known there was a door somewhere and had simply never been told its address. She thought: *what door? What kingdom? What was closed, and who closed it, and why does a seven-year-old open it?*

And underneath all of that, quieter, in the voice she used for figures that wouldn't reconcile: *they are going to sell her.*

Not hurt her. Not study her, not even particularly examine her. Sell her—or sell what she was for, which amounted to the same thing in a marketplace. The Guild of Impossible Things, which had taken Hazel in off the road at fourteen with forged papers and a name she'd invented on the spot, which fed its people well and paid honestly and asked only that you keep your history out of its books—the guild was going to stand a child up on a table like a vase and let rich men bid on the country behind her eyes.

Hazel looked down at her own neat columns, the solstice reconciliation half-finished, and understood, with the particular clarity that comes only to people who have spent their lives being invisible, that she had been complicit in every entry she had ever balanced. Every impossible thing traded, every shadow bought, every echo bottled—she had made it all *tidy*. She had made cruelty legible and called it professionalism.

She trimmed the candle. She finished the column. Her hand did not shake, because she would not permit it to, and that, more than anything, was the moment she understood how good she had become at exactly the wrong skill.

**From the private ledger of Corvane, Master of the Guild of Impossible Things. Entries transcribed here as written.**

*Solstice +1.* — The child sleeps, eats little, cries not at all. This pleases me. Weeping goods depreciate. I have moved her from the counting room to the east cell, which is warmer and has a better lock. Quillon frets that we know too little. Quillon has fretted at every miracle this house has ever brokered, and every miracle has paid for his pension regardless. Let him fret.

*Solstice +2.* — Verification complete. Two listeners, paid separately, neither aware of the other. Both confirm the tongue: Vessarine, true dialect, not the corrupted market-Vessarine the river folk sometimes counterfeit. This is the real article. There has been no real article in one hundred years. I confess I sat outside the cell last night simply to hear it. Like hearing a dead man clear his throat.

*Solstice +3.* — Correspondence begun. I have written to the Cardinal of the Western Rite (he has dreamed of Vessar since seminary; his dreams can be priced), to Sable Amory (she pays in hard specie and asks no theological questions), and to a certain gentleman at court whose mask is finer than his manners. I have requested discretion from all parties. Discretion, in my experience, is best obtained by telling each buyer that the others do not exist.

*Solstice +4.* — Quillon's clerk, the Montrose girl, reconciled three years of inventory in a single morning. Remarkable creature. Never speaks, never errs, never asks. I have often wondered what a person must have suffered to become so perfectly silent. I have decided it is none of my business. Silence in an employee is worth more than genius. Genius wants things.

Note to self: the girl read the red entry. Of course she did—it is her nature to read. Decide whether this is a problem. Decide by Friday.

* * *

Hazel did not learn of the note to self until much later, and by then it no longer mattered. What she learned instead, that same afternoon, came through the one channel in the guild that Corvane had never thought to police: the deliveries.

The Guild of Impossible Things received, on average, forty parcels a week—crates of bottled weather, coffers of borrowed luck, a monthly shipment of moth-wing silk from a supplier who may or may not have existed. Every parcel passed through the receiving room, and every parcel was logged by Hazel, and every carrier, sooner or later, wanted to gossip. Hazel had spent six years perfecting the art of listening while appearing to count. Carriers told her things they would never tell their masters, for the same reason people tell barkeeps and gravediggers: she seemed like furniture, and furniture keeps secrets.

So it was Tam Ossler, the wine-carrier, leaning on her counter with a cask chit and a loose tongue, who mentioned—in the roundabout way of men who want credit for knowing things—that the master had hired carpenters. Night carpenters. Paid triple and sworn to silence, working in the old viewing hall above the auction room, the one with the tiered seats and the velvet curtains that hadn't been used since the affair with the singing skull.

"Viewing hall," Hazel repeated, writing *one cask, red, 12 gal., condition sound* in her flawless hand. "Whatever for?"

"For showing things, innit." Tam lowered his voice with the delicacy of a man savoring his own importance. "My cousin's lad is one of the carpenters. Says they're building a *plinth*. Big one. Center of the floor, where the light falls. And rails for the buyers, close up, so they can look proper." He winked. "Master's got something rare coming to market, they say. Something nobody's seen in a hundred year."

"Antiquities," Hazel said, in the bored tone she saved for other people's excitement. "The guild does love its dust."

"Dust." Tam laughed and shouldered his empty crate. "If it's dust, girl, then why's the master posted a guard on the east cell? Double guard. Men who don't drink." He tapped his nose. "You didn't hear it from me."

"You know me," Hazel said. "I hear nothing."

But her pen had stopped moving, and the entry for the red cask sat unfinished on the page, twelve gallons of wine suspended forever in a state of unsound condition, and Hazel stared at it and saw, laid out before her with terrible bookkeeper's clarity, the whole architecture of what was coming.

A plinth. Rails. Tiered seats. A cardinal, a smuggler-queen, a masked gentleman of the Crown—she did not know the names yet, but she knew the *type*, because she had logged their purchases for six years. Buyers who collected doors. And in the center of the floor, where the light fell, a plinth built to the proportions of a seven-year-old girl.

*One key, animate. Held pending valuation.*

She thought of Wren curled behind the iron chest, hands cupped around nothing, sleeping the way prisoners sleep—with one ear open. She thought of the melody her mother had hummed, the one with no words, the one that now had words after all, words in a language erased from every map, and how the guild had locked the last living speaker of it behind a better lock because *weeping goods depreciate*.

Hazel had built her whole life on a simple doctrine: be no one, owe nothing, appear in no record, and no one can spend you. For six years it had worked. For six years she had watched the guild traffic in wonders and told herself that wonder was harmless, that impossibility was a luxury trade, that the men who bought bottled echoes were fools and the fools paid for her bread, and that was the whole of the moral arithmetic.

Now the arithmetic had a child in it.

She finished the cask entry. She blotted the page. She squared the ledger, aligned its corners with the desk's edge, and performed, one final time, the small liturgy of order that had been her religion since she was fourteen years old—and found, kneeling at the altar of it, that she no longer believed.

The sanctuary was a marketplace. It had always been a marketplace. She had simply been fortunate enough, until now, to be staff rather than stock.

That night, after the lamps were doused and the guild settled into its creaking sleep, Hazel took the spare shawl from her own cot—her second-best, wool, warm—and carried it down through the galleries to the east cell, where the double guard let her pass with the incurious nod of men paid not to notice anything, including her.

Wren was awake. Children locked in rooms are always awake; sleep is the first freedom they take away.

Hazel crouched at the bars and held the shawl through them, and Wren took it, and looked at her with those grave dark eyes, and said something soft and questioning in the language of a drowned kingdom.

And Hazel—who had spent six years ensuring that no word she spoke could ever be traced back to any name, any place, any origin—heard her own mouth answer, in the same language, badly, haltingly, in accents she did not know she possessed:

*"I'm here. I'm not leaving you."*

She did not know where the words came from. She only knew, walking back up through the dark galleries with her heart hammering like a fist on a door, that she had just committed her first unrecorded transaction—and that whatever Corvane was planning for Friday, she had four days to decide which side of the ledger she stood on.

Above her, through the floorboards, she could hear the night carpenters still working. Building the plinth.

Measuring twice.
