## 4. The Inquisitor's Son

Dorian Vale had learned to lie the way other men learned to pray: early, thoroughly, and with a private shame he never let reach his face.

His father taught him the first lesson when he was nine years old, standing in the archive room of the Inquisition's headquarters while clerks fed ledgers into a brazier. *A lie is not a false thing, Dorian,* Inquisitor-General Vale had said, watching a century of Vessarine history curl into ash. *A lie is a true thing worn inside out. Learn to wear it well and no one will ever see the seams.*

Dorian had worn lies well for twenty-two years now. He wore one this morning, tailored to fit: a wool factor's coat, slightly too good for his means but not suspiciously so, scuffed boots that had been aged with vinegar and ash in his own rooms, and a ledger full of invoices for fleeces he had never bought from shepherds who did not exist. The name stitched discreetly inside his collar was Master Tam Rooker, factor, of Harrowgate. The name was chosen because it was dull. Dullness was armor. No one looked twice at a man whose whole life could be summarized in two words: *buys wool*.

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a narrow-fronted building on Candlewick Lane that managed, through some architectural sleight of hand, to look both larger and smaller than it was—larger because its upper stories leaned out over the street like eavesdroppers, smaller because its door was so unremarkable that a man might walk past it three times before believing it existed at all. Dorian walked past it twice on principle, noting the traffic: a cart of empty bottles arriving (for what?), a woman in guild gray carrying a locked strongbox as casually as a market basket, and above the third-floor windows, a single pane of glass that caught the light wrong. Warded glass. He filed it away.

Rumors of Vessarine speech had reached the Crown's ears three months ago—a mudlark's tale of a child found on the flats speaking words no living tongue should know, passed up through informants until it landed on his father's desk like a stone dropped down a well. His father had sent four men before Dorian. Four men who reported nothing, found nothing, and were reassigned to provincial postings in places where the wine was bad and the weather worse. Dorian had volunteered for the fifth attempt, and his father had looked at him for a long moment with those pale, patient eyes—the eyes of a man who audited the world and always found it short—and said, *Bring me truth, Dorian. Not enthusiasm. Truth.*

So here he stood, Master Tam Rooker, wool factor, with a sample case of fleece under one arm and a heart beating with the steady discipline of a man who had practiced not feeling things since childhood.

He knocked at the guild door.

* * *

The bookkeeper who received him was not what he expected, which was itself worth noting, because Dorian had expected everything.

He had prepared himself for a guildmaster's creature—someone fussy, or sly, or ostentatiously honest. Instead a young woman came down the stairs with a candle stub in one hand and an air of having been interrupted at something more important than him, which most people pretended not to feel and she made no effort to hide. She was plain-dressed, ink-stained at the right cuff, with brown hair pinned up in a style that suggested she had done it once, years ago, and saw no reason to revisit the decision. Her eyes were the color of river water over slate, and they went through him the way a customs officer goes through luggage: thorough, impersonal, missing nothing.

"Guild business?" she asked.

"Wool," Dorian said, and set his sample case on the counter between them. "Tam Rooker, factor out of Harrowgate. I'm told the guild buys in quantity for its... establishments." He let the word hang. Establishments. The Guild of Impossible Things owned properties across the city whose purposes appeared on no tax roll, and every merchant in the district had a theory about them. "I understand you have need of cloth goods. Blankets. Sacking. That sort of thing."

"We buy blankets," she agreed. Her voice was level, unhurried, the voice of someone reading aloud from her own thoughts. "Who told you we buy in quantity?"

"A man at the Woolmongers' Hall." A lie. He had spent yesterday in the Woolmongers' Hall buying credibility with small talk and rounds of ale, planting himself in the memory of a dozen men who would swear, if asked, that Rooker was known to them. "Name of Pettifer."

"Pettifer talks to everyone," she said. "Half of what he says is invention."

"And the other half?"

"Is invoiced." She pulled a ledger toward herself and opened it to a fresh page. "Blankets, twelve-penny wool, we take forty a quarter. Sacking by the bolt. If you can supply undyed felt, there's a standing order. Write your terms and I'll tell you which of them are acceptable."

It was, Dorian reflected, the most efficient brush-off he had ever received dressed as commerce. She was giving him exactly enough business to make him go away satisfied and exactly no access to anything else. He opened his sample case and laid out fleece with the practiced hands of a man who had actually handled wool—which he had; his father believed cover identities should be load-bearing, able to bear the weight of real inspection—and watched her test a lock of it against her cheek, judge its grease content by smell, and write a price in the margin without consulting him.

"You've done this before," he said.

"I do this every day." She did not look up. "That's rather the point of me."

"What's the point of you, then? Professionally speaking."

Now she looked up, and something moved behind the river-water eyes—not warmth, exactly, but a flicker of interest, the way a fisherman notes a shadow under the surface. "I keep accounts," she said. "Everything that comes into this building and everything that goes out of it passes through my hands and my books. If you sell us blankets, I will be the one who records that you sold us blankets, and the one who notices if you ever try to sell us something else instead." She closed the ledger with a soft, final sound. "So. Terms?"

"Your price is fair," Dorian said, which was true, which annoyed him. "I'll have the first delivery within the fortnight."

"Good." She tore a receipt from a spool, wrote his assumed name upon it in a hand so neat it looked typeset, and slid it across the counter. And then, as he took it, she added—lightly, idly, the way one remarks on the weather—"You have ink on your left hand. Factors carry chalk."

For a moment the shop was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

Dorian looked at his left hand. There was indeed a smudge of ink along the edge of his palm, transferred from the forged letters of credit he had written the night before, and he understood with a cold, clarifying clarity that this woman had seen it the moment he set his case on the counter, had weighed it, and had waited—watching how he carried his hands, whether he hid them—to see if he knew it was there.

He smiled, and let the smile reach his eyes, because a guilty man would have hidden it. "Ledger work," he said. "I keep my own books. Chalk doesn't survive the road."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't."

She was lying. He was lying. They both knew it, and neither of them blinked, and Dorian walked out onto Candlewick Lane with a signed order for forty blankets and the distinct, unwelcome sensation of having been *read*—thoroughly, cover to cover, by someone who had then politely shelved him and turned back to her own pages.

He had come to find a rumor. He had not expected to find a rival.

* * *

He returned three days later with the blanket delivery, ostensibly to collect payment, actually to get deeper into the building. The guild's layout defeated him gracefully: the ground floor was counting house and receiving room, the stairs beyond it guarded not by men but by a boy who sat on the landing doing absolutely nothing except existing as an obstacle, and every door above bore locks of a make Dorian recognized from his father's evidence vaults—Crown pattern, or near enough. The Guild of Impossible Things bought its security from the same suppliers as the Inquisition. That was a fact worth a report, and he wrote it that night in cipher, disguised as a wool-market summary.

But it was the bookkeeper he kept thinking about.

Her name, according to the receipt and the delivery docket, was Hazel Montrose. The name meant nothing to any record he could access, which meant either that she was genuinely nobody or that she was very good at being nobody, and Dorian had spent enough years around his father's files to know that the second category was always larger than anyone wanted to believe. She kept the guild's books the way a warden keeps keys. Every transaction crossed her desk; every stranger was measured, priced, and filed. She was, he realized on his second visit, not merely a clerk but a checkpoint—the human border through which the entire guild's commerce must pass.

Which raised the obvious question: what kind of commerce required a border guard fluent in detecting ink?

On his second visit they circled each other over invoices for the better part of an hour, and Dorian began to understand that this was a game she played often and well. She asked questions that sounded like clerical diligence and were actually probes. *How long has your firm operated out of Harrowgate? Who holds your letter of credit? Will you take payment in guild scrip or crown coin?* Each question had a trapdoor in it, and he stepped around each trapdoor with answers he had rehearsed, and he could see her cataloguing his evasions without objecting to them—as though evasion itself were simply another line item, noted and carried forward.

"You're very careful," she said at last, stamping his payment voucher.

"I'm a merchant. Carelessness is how merchants die."

"Mm. Most merchants are careless anyway. They're protected by being boring." She glanced up. "You're not boring, Master Rooker. You're careful. It's different."

"How so?"

"Boring men don't notice when a room is listening to them. Careful men count the exits." She said it without accusation, almost kindly, and returned to her ledger. "First delivery of felt is due Thursday. Don't be late. Corvane hates lateness more than he hates theft."

"Corvane being—"

"The guildmaster. You'll meet him eventually, if you keep supplying us. Everyone meets Corvane eventually." Something crossed her face then, quick as a bird's shadow—distaste, or dread, or both wearing the same coat—and was gone before Dorian could name it. "Thursday," she repeated, and turned to the next customer in line as though he had already ceased to exist.

Walking home through the evening streets, Dorian caught himself replaying the conversation, and stopped, and made himself examine why. It was not attraction, he told himself—or not only that, though he was honest enough to admit the shape of her face had lodged somewhere behind his ribs and showed no intention of leaving. It was recognition. He knew that fluency in concealment. He had been raised in it, schooled in it, marinated in the particular loneliness of people who cannot say a true sentence in company. Watching Hazel Montrose deflect and probe and parry was like watching himself across a counter, and the knowledge settled over him with equal parts fascination and alarm.

Because there was a rule in his father's house, older than any statute: *the ones who hide best are hiding the worst.* And somewhere above that counting room, behind Crown-pattern locks, was a child who spoke a dead language—and somewhere below the bookkeeper's calm was a woman who noticed ink on a stranger's hand and said nothing about it for an hour.

* * *

Thursday he delivered the felt, and Thursday the city delivered him a piece of luck so clean he distrusted it immediately.

He was stacking bolts in the receiving room when the guildmaster Corvane descended—a compact man with silvered hair and the genial, bottomless smile of a host who has poisoned guests before and found the experience profitable—and swept past with two visitors in tow, deep in animated conversation. Dorian kept his eyes on his work, the posture of a man with no interest above his wages, and let his ears do the labor.

"—viewing on the new moon, naturally, private, entirely private," Corvane was saying. "The cardinal will want the southern parlor; the incense is less offensive there. And tell Her Ladyship that if she brings her usual retinue of knives, she may view from the street." Laughter, silk-smooth. "One item only, gentlemen. One item, many buyers. Scarcity is the soul of value."

They passed into the stairwell. The boy on the landing did not move to follow them, which meant the destination was above, in the locked country where Dorian could not yet travel.

One item. Many buyers. A viewing staged like an auction, with a cardinal among the guests—and a cardinal's presence meant Church interest, and Church interest in anything was historically written in fire. Dorian tied off a bolt of felt with hands that were perfectly steady and a mind that was running like a loom. The child. It had to be the child. *One key, animate*—if his father's oldest sealed files were to be believed, that phrase had appeared once before, a century ago, in the last inventory ever taken of a kingdom that officially had never existed.

He needed to get higher than the receiving room. He needed dates, guest lists, prices.

"Master Rooker."

Hazel Montrose stood at the counter, holding his payment envelope, watching him with those slate-river eyes. How long had she been standing there? Long enough. Always long enough.

"Your payment," she said. "And a note." She slid a folded paper across the wood. "Corvane wants a standing quarterly contract drawn up. Felt, blankets, sacking. He's pleased with the quality." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "He's rarely pleased. You should feel honored."

"I do," Dorian said, taking the envelope. Their fingers did not touch, but the space between them felt negotiated, like a border both armies respect. "Will you draw the contract?"

"I draw everything." She tilted her head a fraction. "You'll want to read the penalty clauses carefully, Master Rooker. Corvane's contracts have teeth in unusual places."

"I'm used to reading carefully."

"Yes," she said softly. "I imagine you are."

And there it was again—that exchange of glances across the counter, two liars acknowledging each other's craft the way swordsmen acknowledge a worthy opponent: with courtesy, with caution, and with a curiosity that was far more dangerous than hostility. Hostility you could defend against. Curiosity got under the skin.

Dorian bowed, tucked the envelope away, and climbed out into the grey afternoon light of Candlewick Lane feeling like a man who had just been searched by customs and waved through—both relieved and insulted, both free and marked.

Behind him, in the warm lamplight of the counting house, Hazel Montrose opened his payment envelope, counted the coin twice, and then sat for a long moment looking at the door he had gone through, her pen hovering over the ledger without writing anything at all.

In the margins of her neat columns, in a hand much smaller than her accounting script, she had written a single word during their interview, and Dorian Vale would never know it:

*Vessarine?*

Underneath it, after a moment's thought, she crossed it out.

But she did not cross it out entirely.
