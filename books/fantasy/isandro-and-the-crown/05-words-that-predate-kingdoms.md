## 5. Words That Predate Kingdoms

The counting room smelled of tallow and locked things.

Hazel had brought the candle herself—Corvane's rule was that the child sat in the dark, as though darkness could make an inventory item less animate—but she had discovered on the second night that the girl would not sleep without light, and that Hazel, who had spent four years perfecting the art of not noticing things, could not bring herself to enforce a rule whose only purpose was cruelty with a ledger entry. So there was light now, one stub of it, guttering in a dish of old wax, and by its glow the child sat cross-legged on the counting table among the ink pots like a small bird that had decided the cage was furniture.

She had been there three days. Three days of Corvane's clerks tiptoeing past the door, of the guildmaster's voice through the wall saying *inventory* and *provenance* and *do not let her near the ledgers*, of Hazel carrying up bread and watered wine twice daily and telling herself it was only because no one else would remember to do it.

Three days of listening to the girl talk to herself in a language that made the back of Hazel's neck ache.

It was the ache that finally undid her. Not fear, not duty, not even curiosity—though there was plenty of all three—but a low, persistent pain at the base of her skull whenever the child spoke aloud, like a splinter working toward the surface of skin. Tonight, setting down the tray, Hazel heard the girl murmur something soft and rising, two notes and a fall, and the ache flared so sharply that she gasped.

The child looked up. Her eyes were the gray of rain on slate, and they were fixed on Hazel with the terrible directness of the very young.

"You know it," the girl said—in Vessarine, though Hazel did not yet have the word for Vessarine, did not have any word for what was happening inside her own head. She understood it anyway. That was the horror of it. The words arrived in her mind already translated, as if some clerk deep behind her eyes had been keeping accounts all these years in a language she'd never been permitted to see.

"I don't," Hazel said, in Trade Common, because lying was the first thing she'd ever learned to do well.

But the child only patted the tabletop beside her, once, twice, patient as a stone, and said another word—and this one Hazel knew. Knew it the way you know your own name before anyone teaches it to you. It meant *come*. Or perhaps it meant *stay*. In whatever tongue this was, those might have been the same word; she suspected they were.

Hazel set down the tray. Sat.

* * *

They began, as all lessons must, with bread.

Hazel tore the loaf and held up half. "Bread," she said. "In your language."

The girl considered the bread with grave attention, then said: "*Mírel.*"

*Mírel.* The syllables went into Hazel like warm water into frozen ground, cracking something open. She had heard that word before. Not read it, not been taught it—heard it, sung almost, in a woman's low voice, in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and rosemary, when Hazel was small enough that memory had not yet learned to keep itself.

"Again," Hazel whispered.

"*Mírel.*" The girl grinned, showing a gap where a tooth had recently surrendered. "You say."

"Mírel." Her accent was atrocious. The child laughed, delighted, and corrected her—a softening of the middle vowel, a lift at the end—and Hazel said it again, and again, until the word sat in her mouth like it belonged there, which was the most frightening thing that had happened to her in four years of careful, invisible life.

They worked through the night. Water: *súven*. Candle: *lúmara*. Door: *hessa*—and at *hessa* the child grew solemn, pressing her small palm flat against the counting room's oak door as if the word were heavier than the others, and Hazel felt the weight of it too, felt it settle somewhere beneath her ribs.

Names came last. The girl pointed at Hazel and waited.

"Hazel," she said. "My name is Hazel."

The child frowned, tried it—"Azel"—shook her head at herself, tried again, and then abandoned the attempt entirely and instead pressed her own hand to her chest. "*Rynne,*" she said. Then, tapping the table, the candle, the walls, she repeated it, making it clear that this was not only her name but the name for a thing: a small brown bird that lived in hedges and sang past dusk.

"A wren," Hazel said in Common, understanding dawning. "You're named for a wren."

"*Rynne.*" The girl nodded firmly, as if confirming a contract. And so she became Wren—not by christening but by translation, the first entry in a lexicon that Hazel began, that same hour, on the back of a discarded invoice, writing the Vessarine words in her careful bookkeeper's hand while her heart hammered out warnings she refused to read.

If Corvane found the page, she was finished. She wrote it anyway.

* * *

Sleep took them both near dawn, Wren curled in the blanket Hazel smuggled from her own narrow bed, Hazel slumped against the counting table with the lexicon under her cheek. And in sleep, the memories stopped knocking politely and came through the door all at once.

She dreamed of a village built on a hill above a river the color of hammered tin. Terraced fields going gold in late summer. A chapel with a drowned bell—you could see its crown just below the surface of the millpond, and on feast days the children dove to ring it, and the sound came up muffled and strange, like grief remembered underwater. Ashfall, the dream knew without being told. The village was called Ashfall, and everyone in it spoke *mírel* and *súven* and *hessa*, and a woman with flour on her hands sang while she worked:

*Sleep, my ember, sleep, my coal,*
*the river keeps the bell,*
*and what the world has burned away*
*the water keeps as well.*

Hazel woke with the song whole in her mouth. Every verse. Words she had never consciously known, in a language she had never been taught, rising in her throat like water finding its level.

She sang it before she could stop herself—half-voice, barely breath, sitting in the gray light of dawn with Wren still sleeping—and the child's eyes opened at the first line and filled at the third, and by the end of it Wren was singing too, harmonizing by instinct, and neither of them made any move to explain why they were both crying.

When it was done, Wren crawled across the blankets and put her arms around Hazel's neck, and said, very simply, in the old tongue: "You're one of ours."

And Hazel, holding a seven-year-old stranger who was somehow not a stranger at all, said the truest thing she had said in four years: "I think I am. I think I always was, and nobody told me."

* * *

The day that followed was the longest of her life, because she had to live it as though nothing had changed. She balanced columns. She fetched Corvane his midday tea and endured his pale, appraising stare over the rim of the cup. She walked the guild's corridors with her ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, and all the while the other self—the buried one, the stolen one—walked beside her, pointing things out.

That lullaby: her mother's voice. Not imagined. Remembered. The flour-dusted hands, the kitchen with rosemary in the window—she could put herself in that room now, could feel the heat of the stove on her shins, could see the woman turn and smile down at a child of two or three. Her mother. Who had sung in Vessarine. Who had sung *Ashfall* into her daughter before the world took the words away.

Because someone had taken them away. That was the thing Hazel kept circling, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Children did not forget their mother tongues on their own. Languages were lost to exile, to erasure, to parents who decided—out of love, out of terror—that a child would live longer if she sounded like no one. Somewhere between a kitchen in Ashfall and a counting house in a city whose name Hazel suddenly could not remember mattering, someone had scraped her clean. Had given her a new name, a new city, a new trade, and had died—or vanished, or been taken—without leaving so much as a letter to say *you were born somewhere; you belonged to something; here is the word for bread.*

Her apprenticeship. She turned that over in the afternoon, alone in her alcove, quill motionless above the page. Four years ago, aged fifteen, she had appeared at the Guild of Impossible Things with forged papers and a story about a dead father in the wool trade, and Master Corvane had looked at her for a long, cold moment and hired her on the spot. She had always believed it was her talent for numbers. Now, with the dream-village sharp behind her eyes, she wondered. A guild that trafficked in impossibilities, run by a man who missed nothing—had he seen what she was before she knew it herself? Had he filed her away the way he had filed the bottled echo and the left-handed shadow? *One girl, animate. Provenance uncertain. Value pending.*

Or worse: had whoever brought her here—fled with her, abandoned her, saved her—chosen the guild deliberately, knowing it was the one place in the city where a child with no history could become no one? The guild hid things for a living. Perhaps she had been hidden the way one hides anything valuable: in plain sight, in the inventory, under a false label.

She thought of her forged papers, and of how easily they had passed inspection, and of how very good the forger must have been, and she felt the floor of her life tilt beneath her.

* * *

She went back to the counting room at dusk, carrying more than bread. Under her apron: a stub of charcoal and three sheets of the cheapest paper. In her hands: the excuse that she was checking the room's inventory against the manifest, which was even true.

Wren was waiting, sitting up straight, clearly having prepared something. When Hazel closed the door, the girl patted the floor beside her and began, with the unstoppable momentum of a seven-year-old with news, to talk.

It came out in a flood of Vessarine, most of it too fast for Hazel's waking mind—which was slower than her dreaming one, clumsier, still learning to walk in the old language—but pieces surfaced as the child spoke, and the pieces were enough to chill her. *Bad men.* *Chains.* *Mother said run.* *The door.* *The door, the door.* Wren mimed it: hands pushing open something enormous, then closing, then locking. Then she pointed at herself, at her own chest, and said a word Hazel's dreaming-self supplied instantly: *key*. Then she pointed at Hazel, and said it again.

"No," Hazel said, before she could think. "No, I'm not—Wren, I'm a bookkeeper. I balance accounts. I'm no one's key."

Wren regarded her with the weary patience of the young for the stupid, took Hazel's hand, and pressed it flat against the floorboards. And Hazel felt it—faint, unmistakable, like a pulse through a wrist—a hum in the old wood, a resonance answering the language still living in her blood. The guild's cellars were full of impossible things; the building itself had been raised on older foundations than anyone admitted. And something down in those foundations had heard her sing, and knew her.

She snatched her hand back. Wren nodded, satisfied, and returned to her bread as though she had not just rearranged the architecture of Hazel's existence.

Hazel sat very still for a long time. Outside, the solstice bells were being tested for tomorrow's audit-night ringing, each strike falling through the floor and meeting that same low hum, note against note, like two conversations happening in the same room a hundred years apart.

She unrolled the cheap paper. Dipped the charcoal. And began, in careful strokes, to draw what her dream had shown her: terraced fields, the tin-colored river, the chapel, the bell beneath the water.

"Ashfall," she said aloud, testing the word in Common, then gave it its true name, the one rising unbidden: "*Cenvarra.*" Falling ash. The village named for what had been done to it, or what it had survived, or both.

Wren went rigid. "You know Cenvarra?" she breathed. "Nobody knows Cenvarra. Mother said it's gone off every map. Mother said the men with salt burned the maps first."

"The men with salt," Hazel repeated slowly. Each word landed like a coin dropped down a well. She had never heard the phrase. She had always known the phrase. Somewhere in her, a four-year-old had watched something burn, and the grown woman she became had built her entire life on top of the ash without once looking down.

"Yes," Wren said simply. "That's why I'm the key. That's why they're coming. Mother put me on the boat and said *find the ones who remember*, and I walked a long way, and then I found you." She smiled, gap-toothed, absolute. "I found you first."

*First.* Before Corvane. Before the buyers Hazel had glimpsed being shown into the guildmaster's parlor yesterday, men and women with hungry eyes and heavy purses. Before whatever the Crown's rumors were worth. A child had crossed a kingdom's worth of forgetting to find the one person alive who could understand her, and had been rewarded with a locked room and an entry in an inventory.

Hazel looked down at the drawing drying under her fingers—her village, her mother's village, rendered in charcoal by a hand that had never seen it awake—and made a decision so quiet she almost missed it: whatever Corvane intended to sell, whatever the Crown intended to bury, she would learn all of it first. Every price, every buyer, every fragment. She had spent four years keeping names out of ledgers. She could spend four days putting them in.

Below them, faint through the floor, the bells finished their testing. And beneath the bells, unmistakable now that she knew to listen, the old foundations hummed back—patient, expectant, like a door remembering it had once been a door.

Wren tugged her sleeve and pointed at the drawing, at the little chapel with its drowned bell, and asked a question in the old tongue that Hazel's blood answered before her mind could catch up:

"When do we go home?"

Hazel had no answer. But upstairs, she heard Corvane's tread crossing the parlor, and beneath it, the murmur of voices—buyers' voices—and she understood that the question would not wait for her courage to grow into it.
