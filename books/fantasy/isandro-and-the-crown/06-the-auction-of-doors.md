## 6. The Auction of Doors

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its finest room behind the counting house, down a stair that did not appear on any floor plan Hazel had ever been permitted to copy. She knew it existed the way she knew most things about Corvane's business—by inference, by the wear patterns in candle wax, by the way certain servants carried trays toward walls and did not come back. On the last night of the month, when the fog came up off the harbor thick as wool, she was ordered to carry the wine herself.

"You'll pour," Corvane said, adjusting his cuffs in the mirror of his office. He was a narrow man made narrower by good tailoring, with a voice like a ledger line: precise, dry, impossible to argue with because it was always, technically, correct. "You will not speak. You will not look at faces longer than courtesy requires. You will be furniture, Montrose. It is the one role you play well."

Hazel bowed. Inside her chest, something small and practiced turned over like a page.

She had spent three days copying the guest ledger by then—not the whole thing, only the pages Corvane left locked in the strongbox while he bathed, which was eleven minutes on alternate evenings, and she had learned to pick the lock in nine. The names were written in his own cramped hand, each entry annotated with sums that made her bookkeeper's soul flinch and her conscience burn: *deposit received*, *balance due upon delivery*, *delivery* being a word she now understood to mean a child.

She carried the tray down the hidden stair with Wren's face floating behind her eyes. The girl had not spoken since the solstice night—not in Vessarine, not in anything—but she watched Hazel across the counting room with an attention that felt less like a child's and more like a key studying its lock.

* * *

The room behind the wall was round, windowless, and lit entirely by candles set in niches, so that every guest sat half in gold and half in dark. A table of black oak held the evening's exhibit under a cloth of grey silk, and around it, in chairs that had cost more than Hazel's mother would have earned in forty years, sat the buyers.

Corvane performed the introductions for Hazel's benefit alone; the guests did not trouble to learn a servant's name.

"His Eminence Cardinal Aurelio Voss, of the Chantry of the Sealed Flame." A fat man in scarlet, rings on every finger, smiling the smile of a man who has never once doubted that heaven takes his side. "Mistress Sable Amory." A woman in storm-grey leather, young except around the eyes, where she was older than the guild itself. She wore no jewelry except a single earring shaped like a fishhook, and she looked at Hazel—the actual Hazel, not the furniture—with a flicker of recognition that made Hazel's skin prickle. "And a representative of the Crown, who prefers to remain unnamed."

The Crown's agent wore a mask of white lacquer, featureless as an egg. When it turned toward Hazel, she felt weighed, catalogued, entered into some invisible column.

"Tonight," Corvane said, drawing back the silk, "you will see what I have described to you in confidence."

Under the cloth lay a shallow dish of water, and in the water floated a sliver of something that was neither metal nor bone nor glass—a fragment perhaps the length of Hazel's thumb, pale as a fingernail, and wrong in a way the eye could not fix. Looking at it directly was like looking at a word in a language you almost remembered. The candles nearest it burned without flickering. That was the detail that frightened Hazel most. Fire should flicker. Everything alive flickers.

"The key to the Vessarine door," Corvane announced, "in its second-largest surviving piece. There are others. There is, above all, the animate component—the living key, a child of approximately seven years, unmarked, healthy, and fluent."

"Fluent," the cardinal repeated, wetting his lips. "In the tongue itself."

"In the tongue itself, Your Eminence. Verified by three scholars, two of whom wept."

Hazel poured wine into the cardinal's cup and kept her hands steady through an act of will she would remember for years. *Two of whom wept.* What had they heard? What word had reached out of that small mouth and found some drowned country inside them?

"The Church," Voss said, "has long maintained that Vessar was erased by divine mercy. A whole kingdom given over to pride and old bargains—well. We sealed the door once. We can seal it again. But a tool of sealing must be held by holy hands." He spread his ringed fingers. "I am authorized to offer four hundred thousand crowns, plus the Church's eternal discretion."

"Discretion," Sable Amory said softly, "is the one commodity the Church has never successfully traded." She turned her cup without drinking. "My offer stands at five hundred thousand, and my discretion costs you nothing, Corvane, because I have no institution to embarrass. Only a people who want to go home."

"And where," asked the masked agent, in a voice pitched carefully neutral, a voice trained in rooms exactly like this one, "would your people be going home *from*, Mistress Amory?"

The pause was thin as wire. Hazel, refilling the Crown's untouched cup, saw Amory's hand go still above the table.

"From wherever they are," Amory said. "Exile is a large country. Everyone in this room holds citizenship."

The masked agent laughed—a single dry note, like a stamp coming down on paper. "The Crown offers six hundred thousand and a contract of silence enforceable by statute. Which is to say, by rope."

Everyone at the table smiled. Everyone at the table was lying. Hazel could feel it the way she felt a misbalanced column—not in any single figure but in the shape of the whole. The cardinal's offer was theater; the Church would never let a key exist outside its vaults, whatever it bid. Amory's homesickness was real but her arithmetic was not; smuggler-queens did not spend half a million crowns on sentiment. And the Crown—

The Crown's agent had not once looked at the fragment in the dish.

That was the thing. Hazel understood it with a cold click, somewhere beneath her ribs, as she stood in her corner being furniture. Buyers *looked*. They leaned, they hungered, they calculated distances and guards and resale. The cardinal stared at the fragment like a starving man. Amory studied it like a rival captain studies a ship. But the white mask never dipped toward the table at all—because the Crown did not need to appraise what it already owned.

*It already owns one.*

The thought arrived whole, terrible, certain. Somewhere in a customs vault or an inquisitor's reliquary there was another piece of this pale, unflickering wrongness, and tonight's auction was not an auction at all. It was reconnaissance. The Crown was here to learn who else hunted the door, how much they knew, and whether Corvane's fragment completed theirs.

Her hand did not shake as she set down the decanter. Her hand never shook. That was the problem with being no one; even your terror learned to keep the books.

* * *

Afterward, when the guests had gone up the hidden stair two by two—cardinal first, flushed and sweating; the Crown's agent last, pausing at the bottom step to look back at Hazel for one long, unreadable moment—Corvane counted his deposits and allowed himself the ghost of satisfaction.

"A successful evening," he said, to Hazel, to the room, to history. "Note in the ledger, Montrose. Viewing conducted. Interest confirmed. Final bids due within the fortnight."

"Yes, guildmaster."

He paused at the door, candlelight making a gutter of his shadow. "You handled the wine adequately. You will handle the next viewing also. And Montrose—" the narrow eyes came around, mild as milk, "—the guest ledger stays in the strongbox. Strongboxes are not decorative."

"No, guildmaster."

He left. Hazel stood alone in the round room among the dying candles, beside the dish where the fragment floated in its water, patient as a tooth.

She should go. Every instinct honed over six years of invisibility said *go now, climb the stair, be furniture again*. Instead she crossed to the table and looked down at the thing that had unhinged fire itself, and from very close, in the last light, she saw what she had missed before: fine lines across its surface, worn nearly smooth, arranged in rows.

Not decoration. Writing.

And though she had never seen the script in her life, her mouth moved around the shapes before her mind could stop it, shaping sounds in a language that predated kingdoms, and the fragment in the water trembled—just once, just slightly, like a sleeper hearing her name—and every candle in the room went out.

In the dark, Hazel stood with her heart hammering and her lips still parted around a word she did not know she knew, and upstairs, faintly, through floors and stone, she heard Wren begin to sing.
