## 7. Ashfall on the Map That Isn't

Hazel had forged documents before. She had forged them the way other apprentices practiced scales—daily, dutifully, until the lie of a well-made ledger sat as comfortably in her hand as the truth of one. Guild business ran on paper that could survive scrutiny: bills of lading for goods that had never existed, customs seals copied from memory, dates adjusted by a day or a decade depending on what the buyer needed to believe.

But she had never forged provenance for a child.

The commission lay before her on the counting-room table: three sheets of good rag paper, a pot of iron-gall ink gone brown with age so the writing would look older than it was, and Corvane's instructions, delivered that morning with the smoothness of a man handing over a knife handle-first.

*A certificate of origin for the girl. Vessarine bloodline, documented. Age seven. To be presented at the private viewing.*

Vessarine bloodline, documented. As if a nation could be reduced to a line of ink. As if Wren were a shipment of saffron whose value depended on where the ledger said she'd grown.

Hazel dipped her pen and did not write.

The trouble—the exquisite, terrible trouble—was that she was the only person in the guild who could do this properly. The Vessarine script had not been written in a hundred years; the last hands that shaped its letters had been burned or scattered or made into silence. But Hazel's own hand knew things her mind did not. When she let it move without thinking, it drifted toward curves she had never been taught, flourishes that felt less like invention than recollection. Her mother's hand, perhaps, living on in muscle and marrow.

She could forge a Vessarine document better than any scholar alive.

And that meant Corvane needed her. And that meant, for the first time since she'd walked through the guild door five years ago with a false name and a stolen history, she was visible.

* * *

Dorian Vale had been a wool factor for eleven days, and he was beginning to suspect he would be a wool factor forever.

He sat across the counting-room table from the guild's bookkeeper—a sharp-eyed woman named Montrose who wrote numbers the way his father wrote sermons, with total conviction and no warmth—and tried to care about fleece weights. The guild's import records were the pretext. The Crown's interest was elsewhere: rumors of Vessarine speech, a mute child who was no longer mute, whispers of an auction. His father wanted names, dates, prices. Dorian wanted—

He wasn't sure what he wanted. That was the honest answer, and honesty was the one thing he could never put in a report.

"The third consignment," Montrose said, sliding a page toward him. "Graywether wool, forty stone, shipped from Harrowmere. Your seal says it cleared customs on the ninth."

"It did."

"Customs at Harrowmere was closed on the ninth. Feast of Saint Aldric." She said it mildly, the way one remarks on weather. "Your seal is very good, Master Vale. Better than most. But it was stamped on the wrong day."

Dorian kept his face pleasant and empty, the expression he'd polished through twenty years of dinners at his father's table. Inside, something quickened. She had caught him. Not the cover identity—he'd built that one solid—but the small forgery underneath, the false customs date he'd used to explain why a wool factor had spent two days asking questions in the guild's outer offices.

She had looked. Which meant she was looking. Which meant she was not what she appeared to be either.

"A clerical error," he said. "I'll have it corrected."

"I'm sure you will." She took the page back, and for a moment her eyes lifted to his—brown, level, unreadable—and Dorian had the strange sensation of standing on a bridge in fog, meeting another figure walking the opposite way, each unable to see how far the drop went.

"Master Corvane has a task for you," she added. "Since you're here, and since your seals are—" a pause, delicate as a blade laid flat, "—so convincing. A provenance document. It requires a second hand. Someone from outside the guild, so the attestation carries weight."

"What kind of document?"

Her pen scratched. She did not look up. "You'll want to say yes," she said. "Everyone always does."

* * *

They worked by candlelight in the archive room, because Corvane wanted the thing done quickly and done privately, and because—though neither of them said this aloud—it was easier to lie in the dark.

The document spread between them was a fiction of Hazel's construction: a baptismal record from a parish that had not existed in a century, attesting that a girl-child of Vessarine descent had been born to exiles and registered under a border name. Dorian's part was the attestation seal, the wool-factor's stamp that would make the forgery travel. His part was easy. Hers was the miracle.

He watched her write the old script and felt the hair rise on his arms.

"That's not a reconstruction," he said quietly. It was not a question, and he immediately regretted saying anything at all.

Hazel's pen stopped mid-curve. "What would you know about it?"

"My father keeps an archive." He chose each word like a man crossing ice. "Or kept one. Records of the exile. Fragments of the language, transcribed before the burning. I grew up reading copies of copies. The letterforms you're using match the fragments. Exactly. Down to the way the descenders curl." He turned his stamp slowly on the table. "No scholar alive could reconstruct that. Scholars guess. You're remembering."

The candle guttered. Hazel set down her pen with great care, as though it might go off.

"You have an unusual education for a wool factor, Master Vale."

"I have an unusual education for anyone." He met her eyes. "My father is Inquisitor-General Vale. If you report me, I'll deny it, and you'll have no proof, and we'll both go back to pretending. But I think you won't report me. Because I think you already know what I am, and you've known since the first invoice, and you've said nothing—which means whatever you are, you're not working for the Crown either."

Silence. Somewhere above them, the guild house creaked and settled, full of sleeping conspirators.

"And what do you imagine I am?" Hazel asked.

"Someone hiding," Dorian said. "Very well. From something specific. Something with a name you don't use." He hesitated, then committed himself to the fall. "I'm hunting a door. The one the Vessarines were sent through. My father sealed it—or believes he did—and there are rumors it can be opened again. The Crown wants it found and destroyed. I want to find it first." He paused. "Not to destroy it."

"Why, then?"

"Because my father burned the last archive of a whole nation," Dorian said, "and called it mercy, and I have spent my life auditing that sentence without ever once having the courage to read it aloud. Finding the door is the closest thing I have to an apology. I just haven't figured out yet who the apology is for."

Hazel looked at him for a long moment. Then she picked up her pen and finished the curve she had abandoned, and when she spoke, her voice was carefully, deliberately flat.

"The attestation needs a place of origin. Parishes require geography."

"Yes."

"There's a village." She pulled a sheet of scrap paper toward her, and drew—not the guild's maps, which showed only trade routes and tax districts, but a freehand sketch of coastline and riverbend, quick and sure, from memory that predated her memory. "Here. Where the river meets the salt marsh. There was a chapel nearby; the parish records would have come from it. The village was called Ashfall."

Dorian went still. "Ashfall isn't on any map."

"No."

"It's not on any map because every map that carried it was confiscated and burned in the year after the exile. I've seen the confiscation orders. Four hundred and eleven maps, burned over three days." He stared at her sketch—at the confident little river, the marsh hatching, the chapel marked with a cross that matched nothing in any surviving cartography except, he realized with a cold spreading delight, the fragmentary survey notes in his father's seized archive. "How do you know where it is?"

Hazel began cleaning her pen, unhurried. "A bookkeeper memorizes coastlines. It's a professional habit."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

* * *

That night Dorian sat in his rented room above a chandler's shop and copied her sketch into his field journal, and hated himself a little for doing it, and did it anyway. If he reported the location, his father would send soldiers. Salt and fire, the standard method. He imagined the marsh burning, the chapel stones cracked by heat, and found he could not finish the thought.

If he did not report it, he was committing treason in ink.

He looked at the little cross marking the chapel and understood, finally, the shape of the trap his whole life had been building: that he had always known this choice was coming, had been raised inside it like a seed in dark soil, and that every year of careful neutrality had only been the long delay of a decision already made.

He blew out the candle and, in the dark, whispered the word. *Ashfall.* It sounded like a prayer. It sounded like a confession.

* * *

Hazel did not sleep either.

She sat in the counting room with Wren asleep against her shoulder—the child had begun refusing to sleep anywhere else, and Corvane permitted it, because a calm key was a salable key—and she turned the evening over and over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit.

She had shown him Ashfall. She had drawn her mother's village, her mother's river, the chapel where—she now understood—her parents must have been married, or fled, or both, onto a piece of paper and handed it to the son of the man who had burned the maps. And she had done it because some traitor part of her had wanted, desperately, to be known. Not caught. Known. There was a difference, and the difference was everything, and Dorian Vale had stood on the far side of it looking back at her with the same hunger.

"He's going to burn it," she murmured to Wren's sleeping weight. "Or save it. And I gave him the address for either."

Wren stirred and said something soft in Vessarine, a dream-word, and Hazel's treacherous heart translated it without effort: *home.*

Below the counting room, in the cellars, the guild's ledgers waited for morning. And somewhere in Corvane's private strongbox, Hazel now knew, lay the guest list for the viewing—cardinal, smuggler-queen, and a masked agent whose travel papers bore a customs seal stamped on the Feast of Saint Aldric, the day Harrowmere was closed.

The Crown was already coming to the auction. And she had just sold them the map.

Hazel closed her eyes and pressed her lips to Wren's hair, and began, silently, to plan how to steal back what she had given away.
