## 8. Wren Runs

The smoke comes first.

It slides under the door of the counting room like a grey cat, curling and uncurling, and it smells wrong — sweet and heavy, like flowers left too long in a closed box, like the inside of a mouth that has been talking for a hundred years. Wren presses her nose into her sleeve. The wool there smells of Hazel, of ink and candle-tallow and the little room upstairs where they say words to each other slowly, patiently, words that fit together like stones in a wall.

*Wren. Water. Bread. Sleep.*

Good words. Safe words.

But the smoke does not care about safe words.

Beyond the door, voices rise and fall in a rhythm Wren knows without knowing how she knows it. It is the rhythm of the song the tall woman hums when she thinks no one is listening — up and down and around again, a circle with no door in it. But these voices are men's voices, deep as barrels, and underneath them something rattles — a little bell, a little bell, over and over — and each time the bell rings, the smoke thickens.

Wren climbs down from the high stool. Her feet find the cold floor. She goes to the door and puts her ear against it, and the wood is warm from all the people on the other side, which is strange, because wood should be cold, wood is always cold, except now it is warm like an animal, like something breathing.

Through the crack she can see them: men in red hats, men swinging silver boxes on chains, and the guildmaster Corvane standing very straight with his hands folded, wearing his selling-face, the face he wears when he shows people the bottled echo or the left-handed shadow. He wears that face at Wren too, sometimes, when buyers come. *One key, animate,* the ledger says. She cannot read, but she has seen him tap the page with one finger while looking at her, the way a man taps a barrel to hear if it is full.

The tallest man in the red hat lifts his silver box and swings it, and the smoke pours out thicker, and all the men begin to chant, and the sound goes through the floor and up through Wren's feet and into her teeth.

And then —

Then the walls answer.

Not the walls here. Walls far away. Walls made of pale stone with boats carved on them, walls that smell of salt and bread, walls that had names the way people have names. Wren can feel them the way you feel a memory of falling just before sleep — her whole body remembering a place her mind has never been. A harbor. A bell tower. A woman singing in a kitchen while flour dusted everything gold.

The chanting swells. The bell rings. The smoke reaches her, curls around her ankles like hands.

Wren runs.

* * *

She does not know the cellars, but the cellars know her.

That is the truth of it, though she will never have the words to say so, not in their tongue and not in her own. The stairs go down and down, past barrels and past sacks, past a room where candles burn blue for no reason anyone has ever explained, and the dark ahead of her is not empty. The dark is full. Full of stone that remembers.

The walls remember Vessar.

They remember it the way old people remember — not whole, not tidy, but in flashes, in smells, in the shape of a hand on a doorframe. When Wren runs her fingers along the brick, cool and gritty and slightly damp, pictures come loose from the stone and drift into her head like leaves off a tree:

— A market street with striped awnings, fish laid out on crushed ice, a boy laughing with a whole crab held in both hands.

— A chapel by the water with its doors open even in winter, because in Vessar you did not close doors against the sea; you let it listen.

— A field of white flowers that only bloomed at dusk, and everyone walking out at dusk to see them, whole villages, carrying lanterns they didn't need because the flowers gave their own light.

— And then the flowers going out. All of them. All at once. And a great door, and a long dark, and silence like snowfall.

Wren stops running. She stands in the black between two walls that are not her walls, and she presses both palms flat against the brick, and the brick is warm now, warm like the counting-room door, warm like something alive, and she understands — in the wordless way of children and animals — that the stone is lonely. That the stone has been holding these pictures for a hundred years with no one to show them to. That she is the first person in a century who has come down into the dark able to see.

She says, out loud, in the old tongue, the only language the stone can hear:

*"I'm here."*

*I am here. I am here.* The words bounce back at her from everywhere, from every wall, whispered by every remembered thing, until the cellar is full of small voices saying *here, here, here*, and Wren is not sure whether she said them or the walls did, and it does not matter, because for the first time since the mud and the road and the men with ropes, she is somewhere that knows her name.

Somewhere above, faint as a coin dropped in another room, the chanting continues. The bell rings. The smoke finds its way down the stairs, thin as thread.

Wren follows the walls deeper, away from it, into the part of the cellar where the remembering is oldest.

* * *

Here the stone changes.

The bricks grow paler, and some of them bear marks — not carvings exactly, more like scars, letters worn half-away by time and damp. Wren traces them with one finger. Her finger knows the shapes. Her mouth tries them silently: *el. sen. va.* Little pieces of words, orphaned, waiting.

There is a niche in the wall, and in the niche sits a clay lamp, broken, its spout crumbled, and beside it a cup turned upside down, and beneath the cup — Wren lifts it with both hands, carefully, the way you lift a sleeping bird — a ring of braided hair, gone brown with age, tied with a knot she somehow knows how to untie and knows better not to.

Someone hid this here. Someone who spoke her language knelt in this exact dark and pressed their treasures into the wall so the wall would keep them, and then they went up the stairs and out into a world that was going to forget them, and the wall kept its promise. The wall kept everything. The wall has been keeping it alone for a hundred years.

Wren sits down cross-legged in front of the niche with the ring of hair in her lap, and she begins, quietly, to sing.

She does not know the song. That is to say: she has never learned it, no one taught it to her, and yet it lives in her throat the way swallowing lives there. It is a low song, a round song, the kind mothers sing over cradles and grandmothers sing over graves, and it has a verse about a boat and a verse about bread and a verse about a door that opens only once, and she sings it to the wall because the wall has waited so long, and the wall — she can feel it, the way you feel sun through a window — the wall sings back.

* * *

Hazel Montrose came down the cellar stairs two steps at a time with a lantern held high and terror walking just behind her shoulder.

She had felt it happen. That was the worst of it — not seeing the girl slip away, not hearing the door, but *feeling* it, a pulling sensation behind her breastbone, as if a stitch in her own chest had caught on something and was being drawn steadily downward, into the dark, into the deeps of the guildhouse where even Corvane's maps went vague. She had left the viewing chamber mid-rite, ignoring the cardinal's indignant cough, ignoring Corvane's flinty stare, taking the stairs with her heart hammering out a warning: *the cellars flood at spring tide, the cellars have rooms that aren't on any plan, the cellars ate a factor's apprentice eleven years ago and gave back only his boots.*

"Wren!" Her voice fell into the dark and did not echo. The dark swallowed it whole. "Wren, it's Hazel. It's me."

Nothing. Only the drip of water somewhere, patient as a clock.

She went deeper. Past the wine racks, past the blue candles guttering in their alcove, past the bricked-up archway that every guild member learned on their first day never to ask about. The air grew colder and older. And then, threading through the cold and the old, she heard it.

A song.

Low, round, rising and circling, a melody with no beginning she could find and no end she could bear to imagine — and Hazel stopped dead on the wet stone with the lantern shaking in her hand, because she knew the song. Not knew it the way you know a tune you've heard. Knew it the way you know your own heartbeat: beneath knowing, before knowing, in the place where her mother's voice still lived although her mother had died before Hazel could walk, in the place where a village called Ashfall still stood although every map in the world denied it.

Her lips moved. The words came up out of her like water from a well no one had drawn from in twenty years, accented, imperfect, but *hers*:

*"Sleep, my boat, the sea is kind tonight..."*

And from the darkness ahead, a small voice picked the next line up and carried it home.

Hazel ran.

She found her in a narrow chamber where the bricks had gone pale as bone, sitting cross-legged before a little alcove with a broken lamp and an upturned cup, a ring of braided hair resting in her lap like something rescued from a fire. The girl's face was streaked with tears and grime and absolutely calm — calm the way the sea is calm over a wreck — and when the lantern light reached her she looked up, and she did not flinch, and she did not run.

*"You came,"* Wren said in Vessarine. *"The walls said you'd come."*

"The walls said—" Hazel lowered herself to her knees on the wet stone, careless of the water soaking through her skirts. "What did the walls say?"

Wren patted the brick beside her, matter-of-fact, as if inviting someone to feel a cat purr. *"They remember. They've been waiting such a long time. Nobody came to look."*

Hazel put her palm flat against the wall.

She meant it as comfort, nothing more — a gesture for the child's benefit. But the moment her skin met the stone, the pictures came: the striped awnings, the laughing boy with the crab, the dusk-blooming white flowers, the chapel with its doors open to the sea. They poured into her with the force of a tide, and she gasped, and Wren watched her with the grave patience of someone who has already wept and is now simply waiting for the grown-up to catch up.

"You see them too," Wren said. It was not a question.

"Yes." Hazel's voice cracked on the single word. She looked at her own hand against the ancient brick as though it belonged to a stranger — as though, she thought wildly, it had finally begun to belong to *her*. "Yes, I see them. I don't understand how I—"

*"Because you're ours,"* Wren said simply, the way a child states the weather. *"The walls knew you before you came. They were singing your line of the song before you were even down the stairs."*

Hazel stared at her. Somewhere above them, muffled by floors of stone and commerce, the cardinal's chanting had resumed, and the little bell rang on, and neither of them flinched at it now. Down here the incense could not follow. Down here there was only the drip of water, the warmth of the wall, the weight of a century of waiting pressing gently against their joined hands.

"Hazel," Wren said then, using her name in the old tongue, shaping it carefully, the way she shaped all her new words — and something about hearing her own secret name spoken in the language of Ashfall undid Hazel entirely. She pulled the girl into her arms, and Wren came willingly, tucking her head under Hazel's chin, small and solid and real, smelling of cellar dust and candle smoke and, underneath it all, of salt, of sea, of a country that no longer existed anywhere but here — in a child's mouth, in a wall's memory, in the blood of a bookkeeper who had spent her whole life believing she was no one at all.

"I have you," Hazel whispered into her hair — in English first, and then, deliberately, choosing it, meaning it, in Vessarine: *"I have you. No one takes you. Not the cardinal, not Corvane, not the Crown. I have you."*

Wren's arms tightened. For a long moment neither moved, and the wall sang its low round song, and the lantern flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were listening.

Then Wren lifted her head. Her eyes in the lantern light were very wide and very serious, and she said, in a whisper pitched for Hazel alone, beneath even the hearing of remembering walls:

*"The man with the folded hands came down before. While you were upstairs. He stood right there"* — she pointed at the shadowed archway — *"and he touched the wall, and he read the marks, and he smiled. He said, 'So this is where it sleeps.' Then he saw me watching, and he smiled at me too, and he said—"*

She frowned, reaching for the foreign words, translating them back out of Vessarine into the guild-tongue with a child's terrible precision:

"He said, 'Tell no one, little key. Or tell everyone. It makes no difference now. By tomorrow night, the door will be sold.'"

The lantern flame bent, though there was no wind.

Hazel rose slowly to her feet, drawing Wren up with her, one arm locked around the girl's shoulders, and looked back the way she had come — up the stairs, through the blue candlelight, toward the counting room where a ledger lay open on a desk, listing, in Corvane's neat and patient hand, everything the Guild of Impossible Things intended to sell.

Including, as of tomorrow night, a door.

"Including us," Hazel said quietly, to no one, to the wall, to herself. Her free hand curled into a fist against the warm old stone. "Not this time, Corvane. Not her. Not them."

Above them, faint and patient, the bell rang on — and somewhere between one toll and the next, Hazel Montrose, invisible for eleven years, began to plan how to stop being no one at all.
