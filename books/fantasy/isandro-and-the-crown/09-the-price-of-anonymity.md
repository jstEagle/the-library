## 9. The Price of Anonymity

The summons came the way all of Corvane's summons came: written in his own hand, on paper too fine for its purpose, delivered by a boy who would not meet her eyes.

*Madam Montrose will attend the counting room at the ninth bell. There is a matter of arithmetic requiring her particular attention.*

Arithmetic. Hazel read the note three times by the grey light of her attic window, and each reading made the words feel less like an invitation and more like a noose being measured out, knot by patient knot. In four years at the Guild of Impossible Things she had learned that Corvane never summoned anyone for arithmetic. Arithmetic was what he did to people. It was the word he used when he meant *I have been counting, and I have found you wanting.*

She dressed with the care of a woman preparing to lie to a man who was better at it than she was. Plain grey wool, the collar high. Her hair pinned back in the severe style of a bookkeeper who wished to be forgotten the moment she left a room. She checked the ledger under her floorboard—her own ledger, the one no one knew about, where she kept the true accounts of herself—and considered, not for the first time, whether tonight might be the night she needed to run.

Then she thought of Wren, locked behind the counting room's iron-banded door, and knew that running had stopped being possible some weeks ago, back when a mud-streaked child had spoken a dead language into the winter dark and something old and sleeping in Hazel's chest had answered.

She went down the stairs past midnight, past the guild's sleeping clerks, past the bottled echo on its shelf in the entry hall—she could hear it faintly, as always, someone's laughter from a room no one remembered—and knocked once on the counting room door.

"Enter," said Corvane, "since you were always going to."

---

The counting room at night was a different country. By day it bustled with factors and scriveners; now it held only Corvane, a single lamp turned low, and a table cleared of everything except two chairs, one decanter of wine, and—Hazel's stomach dropped before her mind caught up—a small stack of documents she did not recognize.

Corvane sat with his hands folded, a heavyset man gone soft at the edges but never at the center, his eyes the flat grey of coins kept too long in a miser's fist. He gestured to the opposite chair as though this were any audit, any ordinary night.

"Hazel Montrose," he said. "Or should we begin with the other name?"

Every instinct she owned screamed at once. Four years of practiced blankness rose in her like a wall, and she made herself cross the room, sit, fold her own hands to match his, as if they were two merchants about to haggle over freight rates. "I don't know what you mean, Guildmaster."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. That has rather been the point." He poured wine into both glasses without asking, slid one across. "Drink or don't. I'm not your enemy, whatever you decide to believe in the next quarter hour."

She did not touch the glass. He drank his own with the ease of a man who had already won whatever was about to be won, and pushed the stack of documents to the center of the table with one finger.

"Do you know what I love about ledgers?" he said. "They are honest even when their keepers are not. Every entry leaves a scar in the paper. Press hard enough, write often enough, and the page remembers things its writer hoped to forget." He tapped the topmost document. "These are not guild records. These are older. Shipping manifests from the northern coast, twenty-two years ago. A fishing boat called the *Kindly Tern*, out of a port that no longer appears on charts. Two passengers, names struck through but legible beneath the strike. A man and a woman. And here—" his finger moved down the page, and Hazel felt the room tilt, slowly, like a deck in rough water, "—a third line added later, in different ink. An infant. No name given. Only a notation: *one child, Vessarine-born, to be placed south, records sealed per agreement.*"

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The whole world stood perfectly still. Somewhere very far away, Hazel heard her own breathing.

"You're lying," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "People pay scribes to forge anything."

"They do." Corvane nodded, unoffended. "And I employ the best forgers in the city, so I know forgery when I hold it. This isn't. Look at the watermark. Look at the tide-tax stamp—it was discontinued the year after, which forgers never bother to replicate because they don't know it existed." He leaned forward, and for the first time his voice lost its merchant's smoothness and became something almost gentle, which was infinitely worse. "Your mother sang to you in a language you've spent your life pretending you never heard. You flinch at certain words in old contracts—words in no living tongue, words that appear nowhere in any text we sell. You balance my books flawlessly, but every year on the same week in autumn you make small errors, three days running, as though something in you grieves on a schedule. I have watched you for four years, girl. I did not hire a talented orphan. I hired exactly what I found: a daughter of Vessar, hiding in plain sight, keeping my ledgers because numbers are the one language with no mother tongue."

Hazel's hands, folded on the table, had begun to tremble. She pressed them flat against the wood and stared at the grain as if it might offer her an escape route. Twenty-two years. Her whole constructed life—the apprenticeship forged in another city, the references bought with savings, the name invented on a road south—all of it, seen through, catalogued, filed away in this man's memory like inventory.

"Why?" she managed. "If you knew. Why keep me?"

"Because you were useful, and because knowing was itself an asset." He said it without shame; shame, she understood suddenly, was simply not a faculty he possessed. "A Vessarine in my employ, undocumented, deniable—that is a card a careful man keeps face-down until the table turns. And the table, Madam Montrose, has turned."

He rose, crossed to the counting room's inner door—the iron-banded one, Wren's door—and drew a key from his waistcoat. But he did not open it. He stood with his hand on the lock, looking back at her, silhouetted against the low lamp like a man deciding the price of something.

"The child speaks the tongue," he said. "So do you. Whatever you claim. And in three days, buyers arrive who will pay more gold than this guild has seen in a generation for access to the Vessarine door. There is a ritual, an old binding, inscribed on the door itself. My other scholars can transliterate the script—they can pronounce the syllables—but pronunciation is not understanding. The binding responds to meaning. To intent. To the language *lived*, not recited." His fingers drummed once on the iron. "I need a true translation. Faithful. Complete. And there is precisely one person in this building capable of giving it to me."

"No," Hazel said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to. You want me to help you sell a nation's grave to a cardinal and a smuggler and whatever masked thing the Crown sends to sit at your table. The answer is no."

Corvane smiled. It transformed his face the way frost transforms a window—beautiful, and a sign of killing cold. He returned to the table, sat, and drew from the document stack a single sheet she had not seen before. He did not push it toward her. He simply laid it beside the others, face up, and let the lamplight do its work.

It bore the seal of the Crown's Office of Inquiry. The black wax gleamed like a beetle's shell.

"A correspondence," Corvane said, "between myself and the Inquisitor-General Vale, ongoing these six months. He believes there is Vessarine speech surfacing in this city. He is correct. He also believes the source is a single child, arrived by unknown means, currently held—I use the word loosely—at a respectable guild house." He tapped the letter. "What he does not yet know is that the child is not alone. That the guild's bookkeeper, a woman of no recorded origin, whose references I could unravel in an afternoon, whose true name sits in a struck-through line on a twenty-two-year-old manifest—what he does not know is that she is Vessarine-born. One letter from me. One. And the Inquisitor-General's men come through that door, and you learn what Crown inquiry does with the last speakers of an erased kingdom. They are not cruel men, Hazel. They are worse. They are thorough."

The trembling had spread from her hands to somewhere behind her ribs. She thought of Dorian Vale—of invoices and circling conversation, of a man fluent in the same concealments she was, of the strange gravity between them that she had refused, night after night, to name. *His father.* The Inquisitor-General. Of course. Of course.

"And if I translate?" she asked. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "What do I get?"

"Legitimacy." Corvane said the word like a jeweler naming a stone. "Papers. A history. Hazel Montrose, born in [a river town], documented, taxed, real—every record stitched seamless, going back to the cradle. No more attic rooms. No more flinching at footsteps. You become a person the world must acknowledge, in exchange for which you give me a faithful rendering of the binding ritual, and your silence regarding the child's disposition after the sale." He paused. "And I burn the manifest. The struck-through line, the infant, all of it. Ash. Your mother's boat never existed."

*Your mother.* He said it lightly, a bargaining chip among chips, and something in Hazel went very quiet and very cold, colder than fear, colder than the counting room, cold as the drowned chapel she had dreamed of all her life without knowing why.

"And Wren," Hazel said. "After the sale. What happens to the child?"

"That," said Corvane, "is the buyer's affair."

"She's seven years old."

"She is *inventory*, Madam Montrose. One key, animate. I listed her myself. You entered her into the ledger—you'll recall the entry; your handwriting is on it." He let that land, deliberate, watching her face. Yes. She had written it. Six weeks ago, believing she was recording a foundling for charity's protection, she had written *one child, female, approx. seven years, origin unknown* into the guild's books, and Corvane had amended it in his own hand the next morning: *origin: Vessar. classification: key, animate.* Her handwriting and his, side by side on the same line, complicity in two inks.

"I gave you a fair offer first," Corvane said, rising again, gathering the letters, the manifest, the whole architecture of her destruction back into his arms with the tenderness of a man handling money. "I want you to remember that, afterward. Whatever you decide. I offered you a life. Most people in your position get only a choice of deaths." At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, and spoke without turning around. "You have until the buyers arrive. Three days. Send word to my study either way. And Hazel—" now he did turn, and the lamplight caught the coin-grey eyes, and there was something almost like pity in them, which was the cruelest thing yet, "—the child sings at night. Did you know? Old songs. Songs I'm told were lullabies, once, in a country that no longer exists. The guards find it unsettling. I find it profitable. Think on which of those two things you'd rather be."

The door closed behind him. The lock turned. And Hazel sat alone in the counting room with the untouched wine going bitter in its glass, listening—to the silence, and then, beneath it, faint through the iron-banded door, rising like smoke through floorboards, a child's voice singing in a language that predated kingdoms.

Her mother's language. Her mother's lullaby.

Hazel put her head in her hands and understood, at last, the full shape of the trap: every road out of this room ran through Wren. Translate, and she sold the child to strangers with deep purses. Refuse, and Corvane sold *her* to the Crown, and the Crown did not buy—it burned. Legitimacy or exposure. Betrayal or the pyre. He had built it carefully, over four years, the way he built everything: patiently, in double-entry, so that every debt balanced.

But Corvane had made one error, one small unbalanced figure in his great cold ledger. He believed she was hiding because she was afraid.

She lifted her head. Through the door, the song went on, wavering, stubborn, a small voice keeping a whole nation alive one note at a time. And Hazel realized, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had not been protecting her anonymity all these years.

She had been waiting for a reason to surrender it.

She rose, blew out the lamp, and stood in the dark with her palm flat against the iron-banded door, feeling through the metal the faint vibration of the song, and made her decision—not the one Corvane had priced, not the one the Crown would sanction, but a third thing, unwritten, unbargained, hers.

Three days. The buyers were coming in three days.

She would need a smuggler, a thief, and a miracle—and she knew, God help her, exactly where to start finding the first two.
