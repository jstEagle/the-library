## 10. Confession at Low Tide

The tide went out an hour before dawn, and with it went the last of the harbor's noise. What remained was the creak of the pilings, the drip of water from the crossbeams, and somewhere far off a bell buoy tolling like a patient heart. Dorian Vale stood beneath the customs pier in the smell of salt and rotting kelp, holding a lantern he had not yet lit, waiting for a bookkeeper who might not come.

He had left the message where only she would find it — tucked into the third ledger of the solstice audit, between the columns for *bottled echoes* and *miscellaneous phenomena*, a slip of paper bearing nothing but a time and a place. No name. No seal. If she had any sense, she would burn it and report him to her guildmaster as a spy. He found, to his own surprise, that he was hoping she had no sense at all.

She came down the ladder at half past four, moving carefully on the slick stones, her cloak drawn up against the cold. She did not ask why he had summoned her. That was the first thing he noticed about Hazel Montrose: she never asked questions whose answers she could deduce, and she deduced almost everything.

"You said you wanted to talk about provenance," she said. Her voice was low, pitched under the pilings the way one speaks in a church or a conspirator's cellar. "At this hour?"

"The customs inspectors change shift at five. After that, no one walks this stretch until the fish carts come." He lifted the lantern but did not light it. The dark felt safer. In the dark, a man could say things his waking mind would strangle. "I wanted to show you something first."

He knelt at the base of the fourth piling, where the barnacles grew thickest, and worked his fingers behind a plate of copper bolted there years ago by a man whose name he had never learned. The plate came away with a grinding protest. Behind it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay a packet the size of a prayer book.

Hazel watched him without moving. "You keep secrets under a Crown pier."

"I keep my father's secrets under a Crown pier. There's a difference, though I've spent thirty years failing to find it."

* * *

He had rehearsed this confession a hundred times — in the bathhouse, on the road from the capital, in the narrow bed of the Woolwright's Rest with its mattress that smelled of sheep and other men's dreams. Every rehearsal had failed. Words that flowed easily in solitude turned to gravel in company. But standing here in the dark with a woman who lied as beautifully as he did, Dorian found the gravel loosening, stone by stone.

"My father is Inquisitor-General Aldous Vale," he said. "You may have guessed. We have the same jaw. The same way of standing when we're deciding whether to believe someone."

"I'd wondered," Hazel said. "Wool factors don't usually have inquisitors' posture."

"No. They don't." He unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside were papers — brittle, water-stained, some of them scorched along one edge as if they had been pulled from a fire by hands that cared more for rescue than for care. "Twenty-two years ago, my father burned the Vessarine archive. Not the public registry — that was ash before I was born. I mean the real archive. The one the Crown swore it had destroyed and had instead sealed in the undercroft of the Inquisition house. Ledgers. Census rolls. Parish books. Songs written down by scholars who thought a language was worth saving even while their king erased it. My father carried them out in three crates and burned them in the courtyard over two nights, because one night wasn't enough."

The tide lapped somewhere far below them, gentle as a sleeping animal.

"Why?" Hazel asked. It was barely a question. It was more like a door held open.

"Because he believed it was mercy." Dorian heard his own voice go flat, the way it did when he recited facts too painful to feel. "That was always his genius — he could make cruelty sound like kindness. A nation exiled through a door that could never reopen; a people scattered so thoroughly that even their descendants didn't know what they were. To leave records was to leave grief a place to live, he said. Burn the archive and the wound closes. He made me watch the second night. I was nine. He handed me pages to feed to the fire and told me I was helping to heal the world."

Hazel's breath caught — a small sound, quickly mastered. Dorian glanced up at her. In the darkness he could read only the shape of her stillness, and it was the stillness of someone listening to something that concerned her far more personally than she wished him to know.

"These are what survived," he said, tapping the packet. "I pulled them from the fire. Eleven sheets. I couldn't tell you why. A child's logic — that burning a song felt different from burning a census. I hid them here because this pier belongs to the customs house, and no inquisitor searches another arm of the Crown without paperwork, and my father has never once filed paperwork in his life. He considers it beneath the office." A bitter laugh escaped him. "I have audited my father's crime every day since I was nine years old. I have kept these pages the way a miser keeps coins — counting them, checking them, drawing no interest from them whatsoever. And in all that time I have never once had the courage to call it what it was."

"And what was it?" Hazel asked softly.

"Erasure," he said. "Murder of the dead. My father calls it closure. The law calls it lawful decree. But I sat at that fire, and I fed it, and I knew — even at nine, I knew — that we were not healing anything. We were making sure no one could ever testify that Vessar had existed at all."

* * *

The eastern sky had begun to bruise grey above the harbor mouth. Somewhere overhead, gulls were waking, quarreling about nothing. Dorian rose, joints aching from the damp, and faced her fully.

"Here is why I brought you here instead of simply doing my duty," he said. "Three weeks ago I was given an assignment. Rumors of Vessarine speech — a child, mute or near enough, who spoke words out of a dead tongue. I was told to trace it quietly, identify the source, and report. Standard procedure. Erasure requires vigilance; my father built an entire doctrine around it. *A thing unspoken twice is forgotten thrice.*"

"And you took the assignment."

"I took the assignment." He looked down at the packet in his hands. "And then I arrived at your guild, and I began reading invoices, and I found myself doing something strange. I wasn't hunting the source of the rumor. I was auditing it. That's the only word I have. When I saw the guildmaster's entries — the careful euphemisms, *one item, animate, provenance disputed* — I recognized the handwriting of concealment, because I grew up reading it at my own dinner table. And I realized I didn't want to report what I found. I wanted to understand it. And wanting to understand, rather than to erase, is treason in my family's church."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. The tide had begun its slow return; he could hear it, a whisper of water finding the lower stones.

"The child," she said. "Wren. You know her name."

"I know she's listed as inventory. I know your guildmaster intends to sell access to something, and I know the buyers coming to the solstice viewing include a cardinal of the Ashen Rite, which should frighten anyone who has read history. And I know—" he hesitated, then committed, "—I know that whoever holds that child holds a key to a door my father would burn the world to keep shut. Which means I am either the best-placed inquisitor in the kingdom or the worst traitor it has produced in a century. I've stopped trying to determine which."

"What do you want from me, Master Vale?" Her use of his false surname was deliberate, he thought — a reminder that they were both wearing masks, both fluent liars, and that whatever passed between them now would pass between masks. Yet her eyes in the pre-dawn gloom were steady, and there was something in them that no mask could account for. Grief, maybe. Or recognition.

"I want to offer you my help," he said. "Not my arrest. My help."

* * *

He laid the packet open on a bollard, weighting the corners with barnacle-crusted stones, and lit the lantern at last, turning its wick low. The eleven rescued pages spread out in the yellow light: fragments of census, a child's copybook exercise in a script neither Latin nor common, and across the top of one sheet, in a scholar's careful hand, a title — *Songs of Ashfall Parish, collected against forgetting.*

Hazel went very still.

Dorian had spent his career watching faces for the micro-tells of deception — the swallowed blink, the breath held a half-second long. He knew every trick of concealment, wore several himself. So he saw what crossed her face then, and misread it completely. He took it for fear of the archive itself, fear of proximity to forbidden things, and it made his offer easier somehow, because it confirmed she understood the danger.

"There's a village named on this page," he said, touching the header. "Ashfall. It doesn't appear on any map printed in the last hundred years. I've checked every atlas in the customs library. But the coastal survey charts from before the Exile show a parish chapel at the mouth of the Vessarine road, on ground that is now tidal marsh below the drowned bluffs. If the door my father fears exists anywhere, the old records suggest it was walked through at Ashfall." He looked up at her. "I'm telling you this because I think your guildmaster doesn't know it. He sees a commodity. I see a grave. And graves deserve better than auction."

"You want to protect a grave," Hazel said slowly, "by betraying your assignment."

"I want to stop auditing my father's crime and finally name it," Dorian said. "Yes. Even if naming it costs me everything. I've been a coward for twenty years, Mistress Montrose. I'd like to be something else for whatever years remain."

The tide was coming in properly now, dark water sliding up the stones toward their feet. Above them the pier groaned with the weight of morning beginning elsewhere — carts, voices, the kingdom conducting its business of forgetting. Hazel reached out and touched the edge of the songs page, just the corner, the way one touches a relic or a wound.

"If I helped you," she said, "what would you do? With the child. With the door."

"Not seal it blindly. Not open it blindly. I'd want to know what the binding actually says before anyone speaks it — my father's generation destroyed the records precisely so that no one could ever make an informed choice again. I'd want translation. Honest translation, from someone with nothing to gain." He paused. "Your guildmaster employs translators. But I've watched them, and they flinch from the old script. You don't. I've seen you handle documents in languages you claim not to speak, and your hand moves like it remembers."

For one heartbeat — one long, suspended heartbeat in which the whole harbor seemed to hold its breath — Dorian was certain she was going to tell him. Certain the truth was rising in her like the tide against the pilings, inevitable, unstoppable. Her lips parted. Her fingers curled slightly on the old paper, as if it were warm.

Then she closed the mask again, gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child.

"I'm a bookkeeper," she said. "I remember what I file."

"Of course," he said, and let her keep it. Whatever it was. Some instincts of an inquisitor's son ran deeper than duty, and the deepest of them, he was discovering, was this: *do not force open what someone has chosen to close.* She would tell him or she wouldn't. Either way—

Either way, he was already hers. He understood that now, standing in the cold under the pier with his father's sins spread out on a bollard between them. Love, he had always assumed, would announce itself loudly, with trumpets, the way it did in the romances his mother had hidden from his father. Instead it had arrived sideways, disguised as an alliance, wearing the plain clothes of shared conspiracy, and he had only recognized it by its effect: the sudden, terrifying lightness of having handed someone else the evidence of your treason and finding that you hoped, more than you had ever hoped for anything, that she would keep it safe.

"Take the songs," he said. "Ashfall Parish. Someone should have them who'll do more than count them."

Hazel looked at the packet, then at him. "If I take these, we're accomplices."

"We've been accomplices since the moment you decided not to report me to Corvane," Dorian said. "I checked the audit ledgers. My cover invoices were flagged in a marginal hand three days ago — and then the flag was struck through, neatly, in the same ink. Someone chose to lose my thread. I've been wondering ever since whether I should be grateful or worried."

"Grateful," Hazel said. A ghost of a smile, the first he'd ever seen from her, gone as fast as it came. "Probably grateful."

She took the packet and slid it inside her cloak, against her heart, and Dorian thought: *that is where archives belong. Not in fires. Not in undercrofts. There.*

* * *

They climbed the ladder together and parted at the top without ceremony — two figures emerging separately onto the fish-market road as the lamps went out along the quay, strangers to any watcher, connected by nothing visible at all. Dorian walked toward the customs house with the tide whispering in behind him, filling the space beneath the pier where his confession had lived for twenty years, and he felt scrubbed clean and doomed in equal measure.

He was halfway to his lodgings when he saw the boy.

One of Corvane's runners, the quick silent kind, loitering by the chandlery with the studied idleness of someone paid to wait. The boy's eyes tracked Dorian past, marked him, moved on. Nothing in it a magistrate could call evidence. Everything in it a Vale could call surveillance.

His father's methods. His father's patience. Which meant either the Inquisitor-General had grown suspicious of his son's wool factoring, or Corvane had sold information to the Crown ahead of the solstice viewing — and if Corvane was selling, Dorian thought, cold spreading through his chest, then the first item on the manifest would be the bookkeeper who asked too many questions and struck flags from margins in careful ink.

He turned toward the guild hall, walking faster, the morning suddenly thin and dangerous around him. He had offered Hazel Montrose his help an hour ago. He was only now beginning to understand how badly she might need it — and how little time there would be to give it.
