## 12. Interrogation by Candlelight

They came for him at the hour when the customs house emptied and the clerks went home to their suppers, which told Dorian that his father had chosen the timing deliberately. Inquisitor-General Aldous Vale did nothing without deliberation. He had once postponed arresting a corrupt harbor magistrate for eleven days so that the man's daughter could be married first, and then attended the wedding, and then arrested him at the breakfast table afterward with a courtesy so complete it was indistinguishable from cruelty.

So when the four men in grey entered his office at the Bureau of Provenance—two he knew by name, two he did not—and the eldest said, "Your father requests your presence," Dorian set down his pen, squared the invoices he had been falsifying for three weeks, and stood.

"Will I need my coat?" he asked.

The question was absurd and they all knew it. No one answered it. One of the strangers took the coat from its hook anyway and held it out to him like a servant, which was worse than taking it from him would have been.

* * *

The carriage did not go to the Bastion of Inquiry on the Hill of Chains, where interrogations were conducted in rooms with drains in the floor. It went instead to his father's house on Candlewick Row—the narrow, immaculate townhouse where Dorian had grown up among ledgers and silence, where every clock was wound to the same second and no book sat crooked on any shelf. His father had never believed in the Bastion's methods. "A man who must be broken will lie to stop the pain," he had told Dorian when Dorian was nine years old, teaching him chess by candlelight. "A man who is *understood* will lie to protect something. Find what he protects, and you need never touch him at all."

Dorian understood now that he was not being taken to be broken. He was being taken home.

The house smelled as it always had: beeswax, cold stone, the ghost of pipe smoke though his father had given up tobacco a decade ago as an indulgence. A fire burned low in the study. Two chairs faced each other across a small table, and on the table stood a decanter of the good brandy—the one kept for guests and funerals—and a single lit candle in a brass holder, because his father considered lamplight vulgar for conversation.

"Sit, Dorian."

His father sat opposite him, hands folded, and for a long moment simply looked at him. Aldous Vale was sixty-one years old and looked fifty, a lean man with iron-grey hair cut short and eyes the pale, patient grey of a winter sea. People said Dorian had his mother's face. His father had never once said anything about it, which was its own kind of verdict.

"You were seen," Aldous said at last. "On the night of the twenty-third, leaving the customs vault on Tidewater Stair with a person unknown. The vault seal was breached between the hours of one and three. The fragment designated V-1 is missing." He paused. "You are not under arrest. I want you to understand that first. This is a conversation between father and son. The arrest can come later, if it must, and I would very much prefer it did not."

"The partner I was assigned," Dorian said. "Sergeant Ellery. Did he file a report?"

"He filed a report saying he lost sight of you in the fog and pursued a figure who escaped. A loyal report. Suspiciously loyal." His father poured brandy into both glasses, though Dorian had not accepted one. "I have known Sergeant Ellery for nineteen years. He does not lose sight of anyone. He saw your face, Dorian, and he wrote around it, because he respects me, or fears me, or has debts I have quietly paid. I have not yet decided which explanation flatters him least. But reports can be amended. That is why we are talking tonight, before the machinery starts turning. Once the machinery starts, I cannot stop it. You know how the machinery works. You were trained to be part of it."

Dorian looked at the brandy. His hand wanted it; he kept the hand still.

"I taught you provenance work myself," his father went on, in the tone of a man laying out documents on a table one by one. "The tracing of objects through their histories. So let us trace you. Three months ago you requested transfer to commercial fraud—a demotion, incidentally, from your aptitude—which placed you inside the Guild of Impossible Things' orbit. Six weeks ago you began requesting archives relating to the Vessarine Expulsion, including two documents that had been sealed since before your birth. Four weeks ago you interviewed a wool factor in the Saltmarket who does not exist. And last night—" here his voice did not change at all, which was how Dorian knew the blow was coming— "a courier delivered to my desk a copy of a guest ledger from the guildhouse, listing attendees at a private viewing. Your name is not on it. But the hand that copied it is yours. I have read your script since you were six years old, Dorian. You form your eights like little snowmen. You always have."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a clock counted the seconds with terrible patience.

"You stole the ledger copy yourself," Dorian said. "Or bought it."

"I bought it. From a guild clerk with expensive tastes. Which brings us to the point, because I know you are waiting for the point, and I respect that you are waiting rather than filling the silence with lies." His father leaned forward slightly, and the candlelight moved across his face without warming it. "Tell me about the girl."

There it was. Dorian had rehearsed this moment for three days—not this exact room, not this exact candle, but the shape of it—in the stolen hours after the vault, in Sable Amory's safehouse, in the letters he was writing to Hazel in cipher that might already be compromised. He had rehearsed denials, deflections, counterattacks. What he found, sitting in his childhood study with the snowman-eights evidence on his father's desk somewhere, was that he was tired of lying to this particular man. Not tired enough to confess everything. But tired in a way that felt almost like grief.

"What girl?" he said, and heard even as he said it that it was the wrong choice—not because it failed, but because it succeeded too well, and his father's face registered the failure of his son's courage with something that was not surprise.

"The bookkeeper," Aldous said quietly. "Hazel Montrose. Or whatever her name is. The guild's records show her apprenticed eight years ago with papers signed by a notary who died within the year—convenient. She has no family listed, no history before the guild, no debts, no loves, no vices. In my experience, Dorian, people with no history are either dead or hiding. She is not dead. And you have been seen speaking with her eleven times in six weeks. Eleven. I had you followed, yes. Forgive me. Fathers are permitted one surveillance of their children per decade, I believe. It may even be in statute."

Despite everything, despite the cold walking up Dorian's spine, something in him almost laughed. Eleven times. Hazel would have been appalled. She counted everything.

"She's a source," he said. "She has access to guild inventory. I've been developing her."

"Developing her." His father repeated the phrase the way a jeweler holds a stone to the light, looking for the flaw. "Then you will not object to naming her formally. A protected informant designation. Full immunity, Crown protection, relocation if necessary. All of it standard. All of it generous. You give me her name and her cooperation, she gives me the location of the missing fragment and whatever she knows about the guild's auction, and no one is ruined. Not her. Not you." He paused, and for the first time something moved behind the grey eyes—something old and buried, surfacing like a body in a flooded field. "Not your mother's memory. Because that is what this is really about, isn't it. You think I don't see it. You have spent your whole life auditing me, boy. Every look across the dinner table. Every silence. You believe I burned something that should have been saved, and you have been building your case against me for twenty years, and now you have finally done something with it, and I am asking you—is it worth her? Is it worth this girl, whoever she truly is? Because the machinery, once it turns, does not distinguish between the guilty and the useful. I am offering you the last exit off this road. Take it."

And there it was, laid out with terrible clarity: the pardon, the protection, the trap. His father was not wrong about any of it. That was the horror of Aldous Vale—he was never wrong about the mechanics, only ever about the meaning. He had audited the Vessarine archive into ash and called it mercy. He had raised a son with chess and silence and called it love. He understood exactly what Dorian protected.

He simply could not imagine that Dorian would refuse to trade it.

Dorian thought of Hazel's face in the vault tunnel afterward, lantern-light on her cheek, her breath ragged, laughing the way people laugh when terror and joy arrive in the same instant and neither wins. He thought of her saying, *My name isn't Montrose. I don't know what my name is yet.* He thought of Wren's small hand in hers, and of the word for *mother* that Hazel was learning again like a language drowned and resurfacing.

If he named Hazel, the machinery would take her apart gently, politely, with full immunity and relocation and a new name in a new city, which was to say it would do to her what the Crown had done to Vessar: erase her, efficiently, for her own protection.

"No," Dorian said.

His father closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, the disappointment in them was worse than rage would have been, because rage would have meant Dorian had surprised him.

"Think carefully," Aldous said. "This is not a negotiation. There is no second offer coming. I am telling you, as the man who taught you every tool you used against me, that the road you are on ends in a cell or a rope, and the woman you are protecting ends in a cell regardless, because we will find her—we always find them—and she will face inquiry without your name to shield her. Your silence buys her nothing. It only spends you."

"Then it's mine to spend."

"Dorian—"

"You burned the archive." The words came out of him at last, twenty years of them, quiet and steady, and he watched them land. "I was fourteen when I found the requisition order in your study. Incineration permit, countersigned by you. Eleven crates of Vessarine manuscripts, grammars, genealogies, songs. You didn't even sell them. You destroyed knowledge for free, because erasure was policy, and you signed it, and you came home and ate supper and helped me with my Latin declensions, and you never once—" His voice caught, and he hated it, and pushed through. "You never once told me what we were. What *they* were. People. A country. Mothers singing to children in a tongue you made illegal to remember."

The candle flame bent, though there was no draft.

"I know what they were," his father said, and his voice, for the first time, was not perfectly level. "I was there. I carried the crates myself, Dorian. I was twenty-six years old and I carried them down to the incinerator yard and I stood there while they burned, and I have carried them every day since, in a way you cannot imagine, because you have had the luxury of clean hands and clean outrage." He rose, went to the window, stood with his back turned—a thing Aldous Vale never did, never showed a man his back. "You think I sealed those records out of hatred. I sealed them because a hundred years ago a door was opened that should never have existed, and a nation walked through it, and the world decided that the safest thing to do with a wound like that was to forget it had a body. Every record was a map back to the wound. Every song was a key. I have spent my life keeping keys out of locks. Call it a crime. I have called it worse, at three in the morning, for thirty-five years. But I will tell you what I have learned, son, and you may write it in your case against me: forgetting is not mercy. It is only forgetting. The debt remains."

"Then pay it," Dorian said. "Open the records. Let them be remembered."

"And loose the door?" His father turned. In the candlelight his face looked suddenly older, the iron gone brittle. "Is that what she wants? Is that what the child is for?"

Dorian said nothing, and the nothing was answer enough.

Aldous Vale looked at his son for a long time. Then he straightened, and the softness left him the way water leaves sand, and he was the Inquisitor-General again, and the study was only a study, and the candle was only a candle.

"Sergeant Ellery's report will stand as written for tonight," he said. "At dawn I must file my own. If you tell me the girl's location—if you tell me anything—I will bury what can be buried and shield what cannot. This is the last time I ask."

"I know," Dorian said. "That's why I'm not answering."

Something flickered across his father's face—pride, perhaps, arriving at the worst possible moment, mourning itself even as it surfaced. "You are my son," Aldous said quietly. "God help us both. You are so exactly my son."

He rang the small bell on the mantel. The two strangers Dorian did not know appeared in the doorway with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times.

"The east room," Aldous said. "Not the Bastion. See that he is comfortable. See that he is not comfortable enough to leave." He gathered the brandy glasses, the decanter, the candle—all the props of fatherhood—and set them aside as though clearing a table after a meal no one had enjoyed. At the door, without turning, he added: "Your mother sang, you know. When you were an infant. Songs I never asked her to translate. I forbade it, and she obeyed, and sometimes I think that obedience was the cruelest thing either of us ever did." A pause. "If you find what you're looking for, Dorian—for God's sake, know what it costs before you open it."

Then the strangers took his arms, gently, the way one handles something valuable and confiscated, and led him up the stairs he had climbed as a boy, past the clocks all ticking in unison, to the east room with its narrow bed and its window overlooking the garden, where the shutters would be locked and the door would be watched and the silence would be total.

* * *

They took his boots, his belt, his penknife, his signet ring. They left him his coat, folded on the chair, because his father had instructed it and because Aldous Vale honored even the smallest terms of his own arrangements. The lock on the door was new. Of course it was new. His father had planned this room for him the way he planned everything—thoroughly, in advance, with love, if that word could survive an audit.

Dorian sat on the bed in the dark and listened to the house. Clocks below. Wind in the garden. His father's footsteps crossing the study, stopping, crossing again—the pacing of a man composing a report he did not want to write.

They think I'll wait, Dorian thought. They think I'll sit in this room and grow reasonable, the way I grew reasonable at nine, at fourteen, at twenty. They think duty is a thing you inherit like a nose or a temper, and that eventually I'll put it on and it will fit.

He got up and went to the window. The shutters were locked, but the house was old, and old houses keep their sins in their joinery, and the third shutter on the east rooms had been broken since before he was born—his father had nailed it shut twice and Dorian had pried it open twice at the age of eleven, and apparently no one had ever fixed it properly, because his father fixed problems with paper, not wood.

The nail gave on the fourth pull. Cold air came in, smelling of wet leaves and river.

Below, the garden wall was eight feet, and beyond it Candlewick Row ran empty toward the river, and beyond the river—

Beyond the river was a smuggler's safehouse where a woman who was not named Hazel Montrose was waiting for letters written in cipher, and a child who spoke a dead kingdom's tongue slept with her hand curled around a fragment of a key, and somewhere south, past the salt marshes, a drowned chapel waited beneath the waters of Ashfall with a door in it that opened both ways.

Dorian took off his coat and dropped it first, onto the flowerbed, so it would not make noise. Then he swung his legs over the sill and held himself there a moment, looking back at the dark room, at the narrow bed, at the whole inherited architecture of the life he was refusing.

*Duty revealed as inheritance,* he thought. *And refused.*

He dropped into the garden, rolled, came up running, and was over the wall before the nearest clock finished striking two.

By dawn, when his father filed the report that would set the machinery turning at last, the east room stood empty, the coat lay abandoned in the flowerbed like a shed skin, and on the pillow—because Dorian Vale had been raised by a meticulous man, and some inheritances run deeper than others—sat a single sheet of paper bearing five words in a hand whose eights were shaped like little snowmen:

*I have gone to Ashfall.*
