## 13. What Corvane Bought

The counting room had been transformed.

Hazel noticed it the moment Corvane's clerks escorted her through the door at dawn: the ledgers swept away, the abacus silenced, and in their place a long table of black oak draped in grey linen, upon which lay the documents of Vessar. Not copies—originals. Parchments gone amber with age, wax seals shattered a century ago, ink faded to the color of dried blood. She had spent three years cataloguing the guild's impossible inventory, and she had never once been allowed to touch these. They had lived in the deep vault, listed in her own careful hand as *one archive, linguistic, provenance disputed*.

Provenance disputed. She almost laughed. There was nothing disputed about it anymore. The moment she looked at the first page—the looping, root-and-branch script that seemed less written than grown—her mother's voice rose in her throat like water finding an old channel.

"Sit," Corvane said.

He stood at the head of the table, and he was not wearing his guildmaster's plainness today. He had dressed for this. Silver-grey brocade, a chain of office she'd never seen before, his beard trimmed to a point. A merchant in his Sunday armor. Beside him, at the table's far end, sat a thin man Hazel had never seen before—a small, neat person with ink-stained fingers and eyes like two drops of vinegar, who watched her the way appraisers watch hands.

"This is Master Quillon," Corvane said. "He will be checking your work."

"I was told I would be translating alone."

"You were told many things, Mistress Montrose." He said her name deliberately, letting it hang there, false as a painted sign. "You were told you were a bookkeeper. You were told your discretion would be rewarded. All of that remains true, provided the work is done well." He drew one parchment from the stack and slid it down the table to her. "This is the binding ritual of the Vessarine door. You will render it into Common Tongue. Faithfully. Completely. Every word."

Hazel took the parchment. Her hands were steady; she made them steady, the way she made columns balance. The script swam up at her, half-remembered, half-invented, and beneath the strangeness she could feel the meaning stirring like fish under ice. It was a ritual of closing. Of sealing. Words that would make a door into a wall forever.

"And if I refuse?"

Corvane smiled, and it was the worst thing she had seen all week, because it was kind. "Then I send for the Inquisitor-General, and I tell him that the Guild of Impossible Things has been harboring a Vessarine exile under a false name for three years, and he will do what inquisitors do. Or—" he spread his hands, magnanimous, "—you translate, you are paid handsomely, and when the auction concludes you may walk out of here a free woman with a purse full of crowns and no past at all. I am offering you the thing you have wanted most since you walked through my door, Hazel. To be no one. Permanently."

She looked down at the parchment. Somewhere below them, behind three locked doors, Wren was waiting. Wren, who had given her back the word for *lullaby*. Wren, whose whole nation hung on what Hazel wrote on this page.

"Faithfully," Hazel said. "Completely."

"Every word," Corvane agreed.

She dipped her pen.

* * *

The lie began small, as good lies do.

The ritual was built of paired clauses—binding and witness, lock and key—and Hazel discovered within the first hour that its architecture was a mercy. Each section closed with an invocation, a line addressed to whatever power had exiled Vessar, asking that the seal be made permanent. And each invocation contained a conditional clause, a small hinge of grammar: *let the seal hold, unless the blood remembers; let the door sleep, unless the key is whole.*

Unless. Unless. The old Vessarine scribes had built escape hatches into their own prison, perhaps out of defiance, perhaps out of guilt, perhaps because no people who write can bear to write themselves shut forever.

Hazel translated the invocations flatly. Omitting the conditionals entirely would be too obvious—Quillon, whoever he was, would know the shape of a ritual, even if he didn't know the tongue. So she kept the clauses but bent them. *Unless the blood remembers* became *unless the blood is remembered*, a shift of one syllable, invisible to anyone who did not speak Vessarine, and fatal to anyone who did. Where the original asked that the door open only for the whole key, she rendered it as opening only *to* the whole key—to the Crown's fragment, which they already possessed. Let Corvane sell his auction. Let the buyers bid on a door that would open onto stone.

It was delicate work, the finest forgery she had ever committed, and she had forged provenance documents for smugglers and cardinals alike. By midday her neck ached and her mouth tasted of copper, and Quillon had not spoken once, only turned pages, only watched.

Wren's face came to her between every line. *Say it again,* Wren had begged last night, in the dark of the counting room where Hazel had crept with supper. *Say the song again.* And Hazel had sung it, the lullaby from Ashfall, the one about the river that carried the moon to sleep, and Wren had harmonized without knowing the word for harmony, because some things are older than words.

Hazel bent over the parchment and lied in a dead language, and told herself it was for the child.

* * *

It was near dusk when Quillon spoke.

"The fourth invocation."

Hazel's pen stopped. She had been dreading his voice so thoroughly that its actual sound—dry, unhurried, almost gentle—came as a kind of relief, and then immediately as a kind of doom.

"What of it?" she said.

"You have rendered it *unless the blood is remembered*." He turned the original toward her, one ink-stained finger resting beneath the line. "But this says *unless the blood remembers*. Active. The blood does the remembering. In your version, something remembers the blood—an outside force. A witness." His vinegar eyes lifted. "It changes everything that follows. Whether the seal can be broken from within or only from without."

The silence stretched. Hazel heard the candle flame eating its wick. She heard her own heart, absurdly loud, like footsteps on stairs.

"You read Vessarine," she said.

"A little." Quillon folded his hands. "I was a novitiate in the archives before the Crown burned them. I was fourteen. I hid three books in my coat while the rest went up, and I have spent forty years being sorry in one direction and curious in the other." He tilted his head. "Your Common Tongue is flawless, mistress. Your grammar is beautiful. But you translate like someone who learned this language in a cradle and hates it, and that is a very particular kind of error."

Corvane had gone very still at the head of the table. When he finally moved, it was to take off his chain of office, slowly, link by link, and lay it on the linen like a man setting down a weight he intended to pick up again heavier.

"So," he said quietly. "The fraud begins at line four. Show me."

Quillon showed him. Line by line, hinge by hinge, he laid her mistranslations bare—not with triumph, but with the weary precision of a man correcting a schoolchild's sums. And Hazel sat in the growing dark and listened to her own cleverness dismantled, and understood that she had never had a chance at all. Corvane had known her bloodline before she knew it herself. He had hired a translator who could catch her precisely because he expected her to try. Her treachery was not a risk to him. It was a line item. He had budgeted for it.

"Well," Corvane said at last, and his voice had shed all its kindness now, and was simply the voice of a man reading a loss column. "There it is."

"Guildmaster," Hazel began.

"Don't." He held up one finger. "Do not insult us both. You have cost me the confidence of every buyer at that table downstairs. Do you know what a cardinal does when he learns the merchandise is misrepresented? He doesn't walk away. He tells everyone. And the Crown—" he laughed, short and bitter, "—the Crown will hear of this within the day regardless, because Quillon is not the only one of my employees who reads dead languages, and because you, Hazel Montrose, or whatever your true name is, are worth more to the Inquisitor-General than everything else in this building combined."

He came around the table. She made herself not stand, not flinch, though every nerve screamed at her to run, run now, while he was still talking.

"But here is what you have never understood about me," he said, stopping an arm's length away. "I am not a cruel man. I am a thorough one. And a thorough man knows that a frightened translator runs, and a running translator talks, and a talking translator brings soldiers. So we are going to do this differently." He raised his voice slightly. "Mistress Montrose will be escorted to the third cellar. She will complete a true translation under supervision. And she will remain our guest until the auction concludes."

Two clerks appeared in the doorway. Big ones. The kind the guild employed for moving pianos and persuading debtors.

"The child," Hazel said. Her voice cracked on it. "Where is Wren?"

"With the cardinal's people," Corvane said. "Being prepared for viewing. Incense, I'm told. It calms children." He smiled. "Go with the clerks, Hazel. Please. Don't make me add bruises to the invoice."

They took her arms. And Hazel, who had spent three years perfecting the art of going quietly, who had built an entire life on the principle that survival meant lowering your eyes and walking where you were walked—Hazel felt something in her chest turn over like a ledger page, and go blank.

* * *

The stairwells of the guild house were a maze Corvane believed he owned. He was wrong about that, and Hazel had spent three years making sure of it, because a girl who intends to disappear someday maps every route out of every room she is ever taken into.

The clerks walked her down toward the third cellar, one on each arm, their grip professional and unhurried. At the landing between the second and third levels, the lamp hung from a hook beside the door to the old salt store—and the hook was loose. She had loosened it herself, eleven months ago, and reported it fixed.

"Wait," she said suddenly, staggering, pressing a hand to her side. "The baby—I'm—"

She got exactly the confusion she needed, one heartbeat of it, and in that heartbeat she tore the lamp from its hook and smashed it against the wall.

Darkness. Shouting. Oil flame catching on dry salt dust with a soft whump that lit the stairwell orange for one glorious second—long enough for her to see the clerks' faces, young and terrified, long enough to drive her elbow back into the softer of the two stomachs—and then she was moving, down the stairs two at a time, into the dark, into the part of the building that existed on no ledger she had ever balanced.

The smuggling tunnels ran beneath the guild like veins under skin. Sable Amory's people had cut them generations ago, and the guild had inherited them along with everything else it had bought from worse men. Hazel had found the entrance during her first month, behind a rack of bottled echoes, and she had never told a soul. Now she ran it from memory, hand trailing the left-hand wall, lungs burning, while above her the alarms began—one bell, then two, then the great discordant chorus of the guild roused to hunt its own bookkeeper.

*Counting room,* she thought. *They'll have moved her for the viewing.* No—the viewing was tomorrow. Tonight Wren would still be where they kept her, the little room off the counting room with the bolted door, and the cardinal's incense—

Incense. Chanting. Preparation.

Hazel ran faster.

She came up through the trapdoor behind the wine racks, into a corridor thick with sweet smoke, and the chanting was real, drifting down from the chapel floor above, Latin-voiced and rhythmic, and beneath it, thin and rising, a child screaming in a language only two living people understood.

Hazel followed the scream.

They had Wren in the antechamber off the counting room, and there were two of them, robed acolytes, and they were trying to hold her still while she thrashed and cried words that made the candle flames gutter—words that were not prayers, that were older than their god, and one of the acolytes had begun, stupidly, gently, to chant louder to cover the sound.

Hazel hit the first one with the wine bottle she had snatched from the rack without breaking stride. The second turned, and got Wren's teeth in his wrist and Hazel's knee in the same general region, and then it didn't matter, nothing mattered except the small mud-streaked body that launched itself into Hazel's arms and clung with the total, bone-deep desperation of a drowning thing.

"*Ima*," Wren sobbed into her neck. "*Ima, ima—*"

Mother. The word for mother, in the tongue of a drowned kingdom, pressed hot against Hazel's throat.

"Not yet," Hazel whispered, and her own eyes were streaming, and she did not know if it was terror or grief or joy. "Not yet, little bird. But soon. Hold on to me."

She ran. Through the counting room, past the black oak table with its ruined translation, down the hidden stair, into the dark of the tunnels with the bells screaming overhead and torchlight beginning to swing wild through the cellar grates above. Behind her, the guild she had served for three years—the sanctuary that had been a marketplace all along—shook itself awake to hunt her.

Ahead of her, the tunnel forked. Left ran toward the harbor and Sable Amory's territory. Right ran deeper, toward the vaults, toward the Crown's stolen fragment, toward Dorian Vale and a choice she hadn't let herself think about until this exact moment, running, hunted, a child on her hip.

Wren lifted her head, tear-streaked, listening to the bells.

"They're angry," she said, in Common Tongue, her first unassisted sentence in it, clear as a bell of her own. Then, in Vessarine, pointing down the right-hand passage with a small grim certainty that made Hazel's blood run cold and warm at once:

"That way. The piece of the door is calling. It knows I'm coming."
