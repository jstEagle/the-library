## 14. The Smuggler-Queen's Bargain

The ship that took them out of the city was called *Mercy of Coin*, which Hazel thought was the most honest name she had ever heard. It smelled of tar and salt pork and old lies, and it ran before the wind with no lights at all, and Wren slept against her ribs in the hold like a small hot coal that refused to go out.

They came ashore three days later on a coast that did not appear on any chart Hazel had ever balanced a ledger against—a drowned forest rising black from the shallows, and above it, built into the cliff like something the rock had grown rather than been made to hold, the fortress of Sable Amory.

They were met by women with crossbows who looked at Wren and then at each other in a way Hazel had learned to dread: the look of people recognizing inventory.

But Sable Amory herself came down to the shingle to meet them, and that, Hazel would learn, was not how the smuggler-queen greeted anyone.

She was smaller than her reputation. That was the first thing. The second thing was her eyes, which were the grey of a sea about to decide something, and they went past Hazel entirely and fixed on the child, and stayed there for a long, long moment.

"Well," said Sable Amory, in a voice like gravel rolled in velvet. "So you're real."

* * *

They were given a room with a window—an actual window, glass and all, looking out over the drowned forest—and food that was hot, and a bath that was not a bucket, and Hazel spent the first hour of safety doing what she always did with safety, which was distrusting it item by item.

Wren, for her part, fell upon the food with the solemn ferocity of someone who had learned that meals could be revoked, and then slept for fourteen hours, and woke speaking Vessarine in her sleep, soft syllables rising out of her like bubbles out of deep water. Hazel lay awake listening and understood perhaps one word in five, and wept quietly at every one of them, because they were all words her mother had known.

On the second evening, a knock came—not the sharp rap of a guard but a particular rhythm, two-and-one, two-and-one, and when Hazel opened the door there stood a girl of perhaps fifteen with a tray of wine and a face that stopped Hazel's breath.

Because the girl's features were wrong for this coast. Wrong for anywhere Hazel knew, and yet half-familiar the way a dream is familiar—the high cheekbones, the dark eyes set slightly wide, the olive cast of skin that belonged to a country Hazel had never seen and had, apparently, been born in.

"You're staring," said the girl, in accented trade-tongue.

"I'm sorry," Hazel said. "You look like—"

"Like you," the girl said, unbothered, setting down the tray. "Yes. Madam says you would. She says there are four hundred of us left who look like that, scattered from here to the Salt Reaches, and that we've been waiting a hundred years for somebody to come collect us." She paused at the door. "Her name for it is the Debt. You'll hear her talk about the Debt. Don't interrupt her when she does."

* * *

Sable Amory received Hazel in a room that had clearly once been a chapel and had been converted without apology: pews became ledgers' benches, the altar held scales and strongboxes, and where a saint might have stood in stained glass there was instead a window of clear glass, deliberately, so that the only light in the room was the honest grey of the sea.

The smuggler-queen sat behind the altar-scales pouring wine into two cups, and did not rise, and did not offer a chair, and watched Hazel stand for a moment longer than comfort allowed.

"Hazel Montrose," she said at last. "Bookkeeper. Apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things. Runaway. Thief of a child." She pushed a cup across the altar. "And daughter of Ashfall, though I'd wager my fleet you didn't know that last part until a month ago."

Hazel took the cup because refusing seemed more dangerous than drinking. "You know a great deal."

"I buy information the way other queens buy silk. Sit down, bookkeeper. Standing there makes you look like an invoice, and I don't negotiate with invoices."

Hazel sat. "What do you want?"

"What everyone wants." Sable drank. "What I'm owed."

"The Debt," Hazel said.

Something moved behind the grey eyes—surprise, quickly mastered, and respect following it. "The girl told you."

"A girl brought wine. She said not to interrupt you."

"Good girl. Marry her someday." Sable set down her cup and leaned forward, and now the velvet came off the gravel entirely. "Then listen, and don't interrupt me either, because I have said these words exactly twice in my life and I do not enjoy saying them.

"My grandmother's grandmother walked out of Vessar. Out through the door, before it closed, carrying nothing but a baby and a hatred of kings. Her people were fisherfolk and salt-farmers from the southern coast, and when the door shut behind them they found themselves in a world that had already agreed to forget them. No papers. No names that opened anything. Do you know what a nation without a name becomes, bookkeeper? It becomes cargo. My family has been cargo for four generations—smuggled ourselves, in the end, from port to port, selling the only thing nobody could take from us, which was the skill of moving things that laws say cannot move." She spread her hands, rings flashing. "I built this out of that. Every ship, every vault, every woman with a crossbow on my cliffs—they are all of them descendants of the exiled, or shelterers of the exiled, or people the exiled bought back from slavers with blood money. This fortress is a nation with no land. And I am its queen, and its debt-collector, and its gravedigger, and I am tired."

She let the silence sit. Outside, the sea worked patiently at the drowned forest.

"There is a door," she said. "There are fragments of a key. One fragment sits in a customs vault that your inquisitor friend recently helped empty—I know, because I pay the man who cleans that vault's floors. One fragment, I'm told, is currently running around the coast inside a seven-year-old girl. And the third—" she smiled, thin as a knife's edge, "—the third has been in my family for a hundred years, wrapped in oilcloth under my grandmother's floor, called a curse, called a relic, called bad luck, until three weeks ago when a mud-streaked child spoke Vessarine in a guildhouse doorway and every old story in my family stood up and started shouting at once."

Hazel's cup had gone very still in her hand. "You have it."

"I have it. And I will give it to you." Sable sat back. "For a price."

* * *

The price, when it came, was simple, and that was what made it terrible.

"When the door opens," Sable said, "my people go first. Before the dead of Vessar, before the living, before anyone. First passage. All four hundred of them, through that door, onto whatever shore it opens onto—and then, if there is a shore worth having, they stay. A nation gets its land back. That is the price. Not coin. Not service. Passage."

Hazel set down the cup very carefully, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not let them, not in front of this woman.

"And what comes after first passage?" she asked. "After your four hundred. What happens to the people still inside?"

"That depends on what's inside, doesn't it? You tell me. You're the one who's been dreaming their dreams." Sable's voice softened by a degree, which from her was more alarming than anger. "I'll be plain with you, bookkeeper, because liars waste my time and you strike me as a woman who respects plainness. I have run the arithmetic of this my whole life, the way you run your ledgers. On one side: four hundred living people, stateless, hunted, fading—one more generation and we're scattered past recall, married into oblivion, our tongue a curiosity, our name nothing. On the other side: whoever is behind that door, who have been behind it for a hundred years, who may be alive, may be dead, may be neither. I don't know what they are. Neither do you. But I know what my people are, because I eat breakfast with them."

"And if what's behind the door is a whole nation?" Hazel said. "Tens of thousands. Whole cities, suspended mid-exile, waiting a century for a door that never came."

"Then my four hundred go first, and yours come second, and both nations live, and everyone applauds." Sable's eyes narrowed. "But you didn't come here to ask me that question, so ask the one you actually came to ask."

Hazel looked at her hands. "If the door opens both ways but only briefly—if what passes through cannot return, if the crossing is narrow and the time is short—then first passage isn't precedence. It's survival. Your four hundred might take up the whole of it. Your four hundred might fill the door while mine—" she stopped, hearing herself say *mine*, feeling the word fit her mouth like a key fits a lock it was cut for. "While the others wait outside a closing door. Again."

The word hung between them. *Again.* A century of it, condensed into one syllable.

Sable Amory was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, the gravel had gone almost gentle.

"There it is," she said. "The real ledger. I wondered when you'd open it." She rose, walked to the clear window, and stood looking out at the drowned trees with her hands clasped behind her back like a general reviewing a battlefield of water. "Do you know what my grandmother said on her deathbed? She said: *Amory, when the chance comes, take the door and never mind who else is knocking.* She watched her whole country walk into exile ahead of her, you see. She was at the back of the line. She spent seventy years telling herself that if she'd only been at the front—" Sable shook her head. "Grief does arithmetic too, bookkeeper. Terrible arithmetic. I inherited her sums along with her fragment."

"And you've checked her work," Hazel said quietly.

"Every night for thirty years." Sable turned. "And I have never once been able to make the numbers come out clean. So I'll make you an offer that no queen has ever made and no queen should: I will not decide tonight. Neither will you. We have some days yet—your inquisitor's letters take time to travel, and the tide at Ashfall won't serve for a fortnight. In that time, you find out what's behind that door. How many. Whether they can pass. Whether they *want* to pass—you may discover they'd rather stay in their suspended country than step into ours, and wouldn't that be a mercy. Find out. Then come back to me with true numbers instead of nightmares, and we'll do the sums together, you and I, like honest bookkeepers."

"And if the numbers can't be made to balance?" Hazel asked. "If there simply isn't room?"

Sable Amory looked at her for a long time, and Hazel saw in her face the whole weight of four generations of cargo—people counted, weighed, shipped, lost—and understood that beneath the queen there was a girl who had once asked her grandmother why, and been told *because we were last in line*, and had sworn an oath over that answer that she would never again be last in anything.

"Then," Sable said, "one of us will have to commit the only sin worse than selfishness, which is deciding whose lives weigh more. And I promise you, bookkeeper, whichever of us does it will carry it to her grave and past it." She picked up her own cup and drained it. "Go. Sleep. The girl will want feeding. And Hazel Montrose—" the queen's voice caught her at the door, and it was not a command this time but something rawer, "—when you dream tonight, and they talk to you, ask them what they want. Not what we want for them. Ask *them*. Nobody in a hundred years has thought to ask."

* * *

That night Hazel dreamed, and in the dream she stood in a wheat field under a sky the color of pewter, and the wheat was full of people.

They were not ghosts, quite. They were more like figures seen through thick glass—present, patient, slightly blurred at the edges, going about the business of a country that did not know it had ended. A woman beat a rug from a window. Two boys raced a dog down a lane. An old man repaired a fence with unhurried hands, as though he had all the time that had ever been made.

And they were all of them singing, or humming, or talking, in Vessarine, and the sound of it came through the dream-glass muffled, like music from the next room, like a lullaby through a wall, like everything Hazel had ever lost happening just out of reach.

She tried to call out and had no voice. She pressed her hands to the glass and the nearest figure—an old woman with Hazel's own cheekbones, with her mother's cheekbones, with Wren's—turned slowly and looked at her.

And Hazel remembered Sable's charge, and forced the question up through the dream like a plant through stone, and asked it without sound:

*What do you want?*

The old woman tilted her head. Considered. And then she raised one hand—not beckoning, not waving, but making a gesture Hazel somehow knew, the way she knew the lullabies: the flat palm drawn slowly across the body, outward, the sign her mother had made at the end of every song.

The sign that meant *finished*. The sign that meant *let go*. The sign that meant, in the grammar of a language built for goodbyes:

*We are ready to go home. Show us the road.*

Hazel woke with salt on her face and Wren's hand curled trustingly around her finger, and outside the window the drowned forest stood black against a sky going grey, and somewhere below in the fortress of the nameless nation, Sable Amory was keeping her own vigil over her own sums.

Two peoples. One door. And now, at least, one answer.

Hazel dressed in the dark, woke Wren gently, and went down to find the smuggler-queen, because there were numbers to deliver—and because, far off across the water, in a prison cell in the city, a letter was already being written in cipher that would change the shape of everything, and it was addressed to her.
