## 17. The Inquisitor-General Marches

The cell they gave Dorian Vale was not a punishment. That was the cruelest thing about it.

It had a window, high and barred, through which he could watch the harbor turn from pewter to gold each morning. It had a bed with clean linen, a writing desk, an oil lamp replenished daily by a servant who would not meet his eyes. It had books—approved books, histories of the Crown's founding, treatises on the doctrine of Necessary Forgetting—and it had his father's visits, which came every evening at the same hour, punctual as grief.

"You are not a prisoner," Inquisitor-General Aldous Vale said on the third evening, settling into the chair across the desk as though it were his own study. He always sat with his back very straight, his hands folded, a man who had made stillness into a career. "You are a son being given time to think."

"I've had time to think," Dorian said. "That's how I got here."

His father did not smile. His father had not smiled in Dorian's living memory, though there were portraits of him young, laughing, a man with his sleeves rolled and ink on his fingers—the archivist, before the burning, before the General. Sometimes Dorian looked at those portraits too long and felt he was looking at a stranger who happened to wear his own jaw.

"Then let me put it plainly." Aldous's voice was level, reasonable, the voice he used on heretics he pitied rather than despised. "You will name the woman. The bookkeeper. The Vessarine half-breed hiding in the Guild of Impossible Things under a false ledger-name. You will give me her true name, her bloodline, and the location where she has fled. In exchange, your record is amended. Your confinement ends. You return to work—not the field work you've grown fond of, but real work, doctrinal work. You could be useful, Dorian. You have always been useful when you stopped feeling."

"And if I don't?"

"Then in a month, I try you alongside her. And I will not be able to soften it, because mercy that bends the law is not mercy—it is rot." He rose, smoothed his coat, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame, not turning around. "Your mother used to say you'd break my heart one day. She never said how. I find I'm curious about the method."

The door closed. Dorian sat in the lamplight and thought about the word *method*, and about Hazel Montrose, whose real name he still did not know, and about the letter hidden inside the spine of *On the Governance of Remote Provinces*, which had come to his cell three days ago in a crate of approved reading.

He had read it forty times. It was written in cipher, but the cipher was theirs now, built over months of invoices and provenance documents, and it said only: *Ashfall. Solstice minus nine days. The chapel remembers the water. Come by the coast road, not the king's road. Bring nothing you cannot burn.*

Solstice minus nine days. He had six.

* * *

Escaping was almost embarrassingly easy, and Dorian understood that too. His father wanted him to escape. A son who broke out had chosen his treason openly; a son who sat waiting for trial might still be argued with. The window bar was old. The guard rotation had a gap at the change of the tide. The servant who brought his lamp looked away at exactly the right moment, and Dorian found his own boots—confiscated, supposedly—waiting in the woodshed beyond the wall, along with a purse of coin he did not take because he did not trust it.

He went out through the kitchen garden where his mother had once grown white roses, before she died, before even the burning, and he did not look back at the house where he had been raised to become what his father was. The city took him in its noise like water taking a dropped stone.

By dawn he was on the coast road, and by the second day he understood why Hazel had told him to avoid the king's road: companies moved on it. He saw them from the ridgelines—inquisitorial outriders in their grey-and-scarlet, supply wagons, and once, unmistakably, the salt carts. Dozens of barrels, each stenciled with the Crown's seal. Salt for sealing. Fire for forgetting. They were going to Ashfall, and they were going soon, and they were moving faster than rumor.

He pushed harder. The coast road was a smuggler's road, all switchbacks and collapsed culverts, and twice he paid fishermen to ferry him past headlands the road no longer served. The sea below was the color of hammered tin, and he thought, watching it, about the drowned chapel beneath it, about a door that opened both ways, about everything that exits never returning. He thought about Wren, the small mute-turned-eloquent girl who had given Hazel back a language and taken it with her when she crossed. He had met her exactly once. She had looked at him with the frank appraisal of a child deciding something, and then she had said, in that old tongue that predated kingdoms, a word he did not know, and Hazel had gone very still and later told him, laughing and crying at once: *She called you family. She says the word means the person who chooses you.*

He climbed the last ridge on the eighth day, his boots ruined, his coat torn at the shoulder where a gull had mistaken him for something worth attacking, and looked down at Ashfall.

It should not have existed. Every map in the Crown's archive showed open moor there, unremarkable, uncultivated, deliberately blank. But the exile was thinning—he could feel it in the air, a pressure behind the ears like a storm coming—and the land was remembering itself. There were walls where the map showed nothing. There was a village green gone to meadow, orchards budding out of season, a well at the center of things with clear water standing in it like an eye. And above the drowned valley, half-swallowed by the hillside, the chapel: roofless, salt-white, its bell tower leaning like a man listening.

And between him and it, on the road below, camped in the ruins of the village with their fires already lit, was his father's company.

Dorian stood in the wind a long time. Then he went down.

* * *

They let him through the picket without challenge, which told him everything. Word had traveled faster than he had. An aide led him between the tents to the largest one, where maps were pinned over older maps, and where Inquisitor-General Aldous Vale stood with his back to the entrance, reading a report by lamplight.

"You walked," his father said, without turning. "Eighty miles in eight days, on smuggler roads, with no horse and no escort. You always were your mother's son. She walked out of a burning archive once, carrying what she could hold."

Dorian went cold. "What did you just say?"

Aldous turned. In the lamplight his face was tired in a way Dorian had never been permitted to see before—tired the way stone is tired, from bearing weight so long it forgets it was ever anything else. "Sit down, Dorian."

"Tell me what you said about my mother."

"She was junior archivist at the Vessarine repository. Did you truly never wonder why we kept no portrait of her from before? Why her maiden name appears in no parish register?" He set down the report. "Her name was Maren Ashvale. Her grandmother came out of Vessar in the first years of the exile, before the erasure was complete. When I burned the archive—I, personally, with these hands—I saved three things from the fire. One was the binding ritual. One was a key fragment. And one was a marriage record, because I am not a monster, whatever you believe, and I wanted our sons to know what they were descended from. I could never bring myself to show it to you. Cowardice takes strange forms in righteous men."

Dorian's legs found the chair without his permission. "You're lying."

"I am many things. I am not that." Aldous came around the desk and stood over him, and for the first time in Dorian's life his father touched his face—a brief, hard pressure of the palm against his cheek, like a man checking a fever. "You are one-eighth Vessarine, my son. Which means when this is done, when the door is sealed and the salt is laid and the fire is lit, you will be cleansed too. Don't you understand? That is why I offered you the pardon. Not to save your career. To save *you*. Erasure is not cruelty. It is the only kindness left to a thing that cannot be allowed to exist. I learned that from your mother, who begged me on her deathbed to finish what I started, because she had seen what remembering does to people. It hollows them. It makes them walk eighty miles toward a fire."

"The people on the other side of that door aren't a memory," Dorian said. His voice shook. "They're alive. Suspended, alive, a whole nation mid-exile, and Hazel is going to open the door and let them pass through to rest, and you want to seal them in forever—"

"I want them *finished*," Aldous said, and for one moment the reasonableness cracked and something older showed through, something that had watched a country die and been made to call it justice. "Unfinished things fester, Dorian. Unfinished grief, unfinished wars, unfinished nations. The Crown sealed Vessar away a century ago because it could not bear to look at what it had done, and every year since, the lie has cost us more than the truth ever would have. I have spent thirty years auditing that crime from the inside, hoping someone braver than me would force it into the light. And do you know what I concluded? That no one would. So yes. Fire and salt. Mercy for the guilty and peace for the dead, and no more keys, no more doors, no more children speaking dead tongues in doorways. It ends tomorrow at dawn."

"It doesn't end," Dorian said, standing. "It passes through. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and you know it—you've known it for thirty years—"

"Guards," Aldous said quietly, to the tent flap.

Two men entered. Dorian did not resist them. Resistance was for men who believed the argument could still be won.

At the tent flap his father spoke once more, not looking up from his maps. "I saved the marriage record because I hoped you'd never need it. I hope you understand that I am doing this because I love you. Both of you. All of you. Even the parts that were supposed to stop existing."

"That's not love," Dorian said. "Love is what's happening in that chapel right now, while you sharpen your knives. Love is a woman opening a door she could have walked past. You wouldn't recognize it. You burned it."

He let the guards take him. But the guards were young, and one of them had a sister who had once been arrested for singing, and neither of them tied the ropes very well, and the tent they put him in had a loose back seam, and the picket on the seaward side changed watch at moonset.

Dorian had walked eighty miles to get here. He could walk four hundred yards to a chapel.

* * *

He went at moonset, down through the meadow that had been a green, past the well with its eye of clear water, and the land seemed to lean toward him as he passed, orchard branches creaking without wind, as if the place knew its own. The chapel rose above him, salt-white and roofless, and in its doorway stood a figure with a lantern, and he knew the set of the shoulders before he knew anything else.

"You're late," Hazel said.

"There was a detour," Dorian said. "My father sends his regards."

Behind her, in the flooded nave, the water was beginning to glow.
