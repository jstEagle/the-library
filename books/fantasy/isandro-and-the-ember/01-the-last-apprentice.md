## 1. The Last Apprentice

The road through the Greymarsh Fells did not exist.

That was the first thing Wendell Oyelaran taught every caravan master who hired them: the road you are about to walk is not on any map, was never on any map, and if you speak its name aloud where tax collectors can hear, it will stop existing for you personally, permanently, and possibly painfully.

The second thing they taught was how to walk it.

Wendell stood at the head of eleven mules and nine nervous merchants and read the fells the way a priest reads scripture — by feel, by memory, by the small heresies of the land itself. The heather had been grazed short on the north slope, which meant sheep, which meant a shepherd, which meant somewhere within two miles there was a track wide enough for wheels that no cartographer had ever bothered to record. A kestrel hung motionless over the scree to the east, hunting voles; voles lived in long grass; long grass meant water. Water meant the old drover's path along the Miren Cut, which meant Wendell could shave four days off the journey and avoid the Crown toll at Harrow Bridge entirely.

"Wayfinder." The caravan master, a broad woman named Doss with forearms like ship's cable, drew up beside them. "You've stopped walking."

"I'm working," Wendell said.

"You're standing in heather looking at a bird."

"The bird is the work." Wendell pointed with their walking staff — ash wood, iron-shod, older than Doss's grandmother. "There's water east of us. Where there's water, there's an old path. Where there's an old path, we don't pay the toll at Harrow Bridge, and your wool arrives in Vael's Crossing three days early and three silver cheaper per bale than it would on the king's road."

Doss squinted at the kestrel as though it might be running a confidence scheme. "And if there's no path?"

"There's always a path. There hasn't *not* been a path since before the Godswar. People have always needed to go places the powerful didn't want them going." Wendell started downslope, boots finding the quiet rhythm of ground they'd never walked but somehow knew. "That's the whole trade, Mistress Doss. Everything else is decoration."

Behind them, the caravan groaned into motion — mules complaining, axles singing, nine merchants muttering about the sanity of following a guide who looked barely old enough to shave. Wendell let the muttering wash past. They'd heard worse. They'd heard better, once, from people who were mostly dead now.

That was the arithmetic of the Thornfell Wayfinders' Guild these days. Four members. One guildhouse with a leaking roof. Three paying contracts a year, down from three hundred in the guild's prime, when the Wayfinders' green-and-grey badge opened every smuggler's gate from the Salt Coast to the Pass of Teeth. Master Sefu, who ran what remained of them and whose knees had finally surrendered to forty years of mountain work. Old Marta, who kept the maps and the ledgers and drank enough nettle tea to pickle a horse. Young Tam — not actually young, fifty-two, but young the way the guild measured such things — who handled the horses and the accounts and the slow, humiliating business of writing letters to banks asking for extensions of credit.

And Wendell. Last apprentice. The only one left under sixty, the only one still taking the roads, the last pair of legs carrying a craft that had once moved kings quietly across mountains and now moved wool.

*Four members,* Wendell thought, watching the kestrel drop and rise again empty-taloned. *By next winter, three.*

They pushed the thought down where they kept all the others, in the deep pocket where grief went to be carried rather than felt, and put their whole attention into the ground ahead. Because the ground deserved attention. Because the ground was honest in a way nothing else in Wendell's life had managed to stay.

* * *

They made the Miren Cut by late afternoon, and it was exactly where the kestrel said it would be.

Doss stood at the lip of the hidden valley with her mouth slightly open, watching the old drover's path unspool below them — a ribbon of packed earth and stone, walled with dry-stone dykes gone soft with moss, descending through a fold in the fells that no map in Vael's Crossing showed. It wasn't a secret so much as a forgetting. The world had simply agreed, over centuries, to look elsewhere.

"How?" Doss said finally. "How do you know this? There's no marker. No cairn. Nothing."

"There's everything." Wendell crouched and pressed two fingers to the path's surface, more ritual than necessity. "Feel the stone. It's worn in the middle — cart traffic, generations of it. See the dyke? That's fell-craft, pre-Imperial bonding style, the kind they stopped building after the Godswar. Somebody built this road to last forever and then everybody agreed to forget it." They stood, brushing off their knees. "My masters knew ten thousand roads like this one. Every fold of every range between here and the sea. I know maybe six hundred, and I'm considered promising."

"That's—" Doss shook her head. "That's a library walking around in boots."

"It was." Wendell heard the flatness in their own voice and couldn't quite sand it off. "Get your mules moving, Mistress Doss. We want the ford before dark."

They descended into the Cut, and the day's light turned gold and then copper, and for a while the only sounds were hooves and harness and wind moving through the heather like a long exhalation. Wendell rode point out of habit, though nothing on this road required pointing. Their body knew the way the way a river knows downhill.

It was in these hours — the hours when the road asked nothing and the mind wandered anyway — that the grief usually caught up with them. So Wendell did what Sefu had taught them to do with it. They gave it a job.

*Name them,* Sefu used to say, sitting on the guildhouse steps with his ruined knees stretched out before him, watching the sun go down over Thornfell's crooked tower. *Grief's a pack animal, child. If you don't load it properly, it throws you. Name what you carry.*

Master Ellery, dead eight years now, drowned in the Whiteforde spate guiding a grain convoy nobody else would take in flood season. He'd taught Wendell stars.

Master Corwin, dead five years, coughed his lungs out over one winter because he refused to stop walking until the cough won. He'd taught Wendell weather, and jokes, and which mushrooms lied about being safe.

Apprentice Liss, dead four years, and Wendell could not think about Liss without their hands tightening on the staff, so they named her quickly and moved on, the way you step over a grave you can't bear to look into. Bandits on the Kessick Road. Wendell had been half a mile behind. Half a mile. They had rewalked that half mile in their head so many times the stones of it were polished smooth.

Mistress Anwen, dead two years, quietly, in her bed, which everyone agreed was the best death the guild had managed in a decade. She'd taught Wendell memory itself — the great lattice of routes and names and old treaties between the guild and a hundred vanished peoples, the whole architecture of knowing that lived nowhere but in human heads.

And Sefu was seventy-one, and Marta's hands shook too badly now to draw a straight line, and Tam's letters to the banks came back with thinner excuses each season.

*Four members,* Wendell thought again, and then, because the discipline required it: *and one contract, and nine merchants, and eleven mules, and a road that doesn't exist, and right now, in this exact moment, the craft is alive because I am standing on it.* Stubborn pride was also a load the pack animal could carry. Sefu had taught them that too. *Pride's not a sin in our trade, child. Pride's ballast. Just make sure it's pride in the work, not pride in yourself. The work will outlive you either way. Make sure it does.*

The ford came up on schedule, shallow and gravel-bottomed, and the crossing went clean, and Wendell let themselves feel one full minute of uncomplicated satisfaction before the evening fog rolled up the valley and the work began again.

* * *

They camped in the lee of a fallen watchtower — pre-Godswar masonry, its stones fitted so precisely that Wendell could still trace the old sigils cut into the lintel, worn nearly smooth. Guild marks. Wayfinder marks, from back when the guild had been something other than guides-for-hire, back when the charter said things in Latin-dark formal script that Sefu made Wendell memorize without explaining.

*Until the gods wake,* the old charter said, in its final line. *The Watch endures until the gods wake.*

Everyone assumed it was poetry. Charters were full of poetry. Wendell had recited it at their apprenticeship binding without thinking anything of it at all.

They sat apart from the campfire, as was their habit, eating hard bread and harder cheese and keeping the first watch, and Doss came and sat beside them anyway, which was less usual.

"My grandfather talked about you people," she said, without preamble. "Wayfinders. Said during the famine years, when the Crown sealed the passes, your guild moved food through country that would've swallowed an army. Said half the villages on the north slope are alive because of it. Said the guild never took credit, never took payment beyond cost." She looked into the fire. "I thought he was embellishing. Old men embellish."

"He wasn't." Wendell turned the bread in their hands. "We took payment beyond cost sometimes. We weren't saints. But we moved the grain, yes."

"So why's the guild dying? If you're that good?"

Wendell considered several answers. *Because the Crown built proper roads and posted proper maps and decided that a kingdom should know its own geography, all of it, everywhere, and what the king knows, the king taxes.* Because the smugglers' roads died when smuggling became a hanging offense instead of a fine. Because peace, real peace, the long gray peace of the last century, had made the guild's particular genius obsolete — nobody needed to move secretly through unmapped country anymore, except people like Doss, and there weren't many Dosses, and fewer every year.

But underneath all those true answers was one Wendell had never said aloud, because saying it felt like disloyalty to the dead:

*Because we let ourselves become a relic. We guarded the craft like a treasure instead of teaching it like a fire. And treasures get stolen or forgotten, but a fire has to be handed along, burning, person to person, or it goes out.*

"We're dying because the world changed," Wendell said, "and we loved the old world too much to change with it."

Doss grunted, unconvinced or unpersuaded, hard to tell. "Shame," she said, and got up, and went back to her fire.

Wendell stayed on watch. Above the fells the stars came out in their brutal multitudes, and Wendell found the Navigator's Chain without trying, and Master Ellery's voice said in their memory, *that one's yours, apprentice, that star's been ours since before the Godswar, learn it so well you could find home blindfolded and bleeding* — and Wendell found their eyes stinging and let them, briefly, privately, before wiping them on their sleeve and getting back to work.

There was always work. That was the mercy of it.

* * *

Two days later, on the high moor above Vael's Crossing, with the town's chimneys smoking welcome on the horizon, the caravan was ambushed by weather.

It came out of the west like a thrown blanket — a whiteout squall, sudden and total, the kind that killed experienced walkers twenty yards from shelter. Wendell had seen it coming an hour off, a bruise-colored line on the sky, and had already turned the caravan toward the one place on the moor that could hold them: a hollow beneath an outcrop that appeared on no map, because Wendell's guild had put it on no map, because some shelters were worth more unrecorded.

They crowded thirty-odd souls and eleven indignant mules into a space meant for fifteen, and the storm hit, and the world outside ceased to exist in a roar of white.

In the close dark, pressed against cold stone, Doss shouted over the wind: "How did you know this was here? You've never traveled this stretch! I checked — you told me yourself you hadn't walked the Vael moors in three years!"

"I haven't!" Wendell shouted back.

"Then *how?*"

And Wendell, who had answered this question a hundred ways for a hundred clients — *good memory, good teachers, good luck* — found themselves giving the true answer for the first time in years, maybe ever.

"I don't know," they shouted. "I just know it. The same way I know the paths. My masters taught me the ones they'd walked. But there are hundreds I've never been shown, and I know those too. I've always known them. I thought—" The wind screamed; Wendell waited it out. "I thought everyone in the guild was like that. I asked Sefu once, when I was small. He went strange and quiet and said, *some knowledge is given and some is inherited, and you'd do well not to ask which is which.*"

Doss stared at them through the gloom, and whatever she saw in Wendell's face made her decide not to pursue it, which Wendell was grateful for, because pursuing it would have required Wendell to admit that they had thought about Sefu's answer every single day for twenty years and had never once come closer to understanding it.

The storm passed inside an hour, as moor storms do, and the world reassembled itself — wet, glittering, washed clean. They made Vael's Crossing by dusk, delivered the wool to a grateful factor, collected payment in good silver, and stood in the yard of the Wayfarer's Rest inn while Doss counted out the guide's fee into Wendell's palm.

"For the guild," she said. Then, awkwardly, gruffly: "For however much longer there is one."

Wendell closed their hand around the coins. "There'll always be one," they said, with the stubbornness that was both their inheritance and their choice. "Even if it's me. Even if it's just me and a road that doesn't exist."

Doss almost smiled. "Library in boots," she said, and went inside to her dinner.

* * *

Wendell should have taken a room. Should have eaten a hot meal, slept in a real bed, enjoyed the one luxury of a finished contract. Instead they walked to the edge of town where the post riders changed horses, because Tam's last letter had mentioned a reply expected from the Merrowbank bank regarding the guildhouse roof, and bad news traveled fast and Wendell preferred to meet it standing.

The rider recognized the guild badge — of course he did; the green-and-grey knot was still respected in odd corners of the world — and dug out a single letter. Not from the bank.

From Sefu.

Wendell broke the seal right there in the mud and lamplight, and read it twice, because the first time made no sense.

*Child — come home. Come now, come fast, don't take the main roads. The ember is glowing. It has not glowed in four hundred years, and I don't know what it means, and neither does Marta, and yesterday there were men in the town asking questions about us. Not tax men. Not thieves. Men with grey cloaks and a seal I have only seen in the oldest of our books. Ask no one anything. Burn this letter. Come home.*

*— S.*

Wendell stood very still. Around them, Vael's Crossing went about its evening — cart wheels, tavern noise, a dog barking at nothing — the ordinary world, solid and lit and utterly unaware.

The ember. The guild's heirloom, the size of a fist, cool and dark as riverstone for as long as anyone living had known it, kept in its iron box in the vault beneath the guildhouse because that was where it had always been kept, because the founding charter said to keep it, because *the Watch endures until the gods wake.*

Wendell had held it once, as a child, on the day of their binding. It had weighed nothing at all.

They burned the letter in the stable lantern, watched the ash scatter, and turned toward the mountains.

Home was six days away by the smuggler's roads. Wendell intended to make it in four.
