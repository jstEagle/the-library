## 2. A Prayer Answered

The rain came to Vael's Crossing the way debts came to poor men: slowly at first, then all at once, and never alone.

Isandro watched it from the doorway of the shrine of Saint Verren-the-Forgotten, which was a fitting patron for a man like him, since no one else remembered why Verren had been forgotten in the first place. The shrine was a squat stone thing wedged between a cooper's yard and a dyer's, its roof patched with three kinds of slate and its bell long since sold to pay for the same roof. It smelled of wet stone, tallow smoke, and the river — the river being everywhere in Vael's Crossing, seeping into walls and boots and, eventually, into the bones of everyone who stayed too long.

He had been here four months. Long enough for the town to stop looking at him twice. Long enough for him to learn that Mother Cassa at the ferry house paid in bread and salted fish for someone to sit with her dying husband through the nights, and that the cooper's youngest boy had a cough that would kill him before spring unless someone with more than folk-remedies intervened, and that the dyer's wife prayed every evening to saints whose names she pronounced wrong, and that it did not matter, because the saints had not answered anyone in three hundred years.

That was the arithmetic of his exile. Three hundred years of silence.

Isandro knew the number the way other men knew their own age, because he had stopped counting his age somewhere around the second century. His body had settled, some decades back, into the appearance of a man of forty-odd: grey threading his black hair at the temples, lines around his eyes that were earned rather than grown. The Order had called it *the Long Vigil*, said it was a blessing, a mark of those who kept faith so perfectly that time itself forgot them. He had believed that once. He had believed a great many things once.

Now he believed in rain, bread, and the coughing boy, in roughly that order.

"You'll catch your death standing there," said Mother Cassa from the shrine's single bench, where she sat winding wool with the fierce competence of a woman who had buried two husbands and outlived both their debts. She was the closest thing the shrine had to a congregation, and she attended mostly to complain about the draft.

"I have caught my death several times," Isandro said. "It never takes."

"Hmph." She wound on. "You say things like that and I can't tell if you're joking."

Neither could he, most days. He stepped back inside and let the door swing shut against the rain, and the little shrine closed around him like a cupped hand: one room, one altar, one window of horn-and-oiled-cloth turning the grey daylight the color of old honey. On the altar sat a chipped stone basin for holy water that hadn't been holy in living memory, a candle stub, and a wooden icon of Saint Verren whose face had been worn smooth by centuries of thumbs, so that the saint gazed out at the world as a blank oval, featureless, patient.

Isandro lit the candle. Out of habit.

That was the phrase he used, even inside his own skull, though he had long ago admitted the lie in it. Habit was what you called a prayer when you were ashamed to call it hope. Three hundred years of silence should have cured him. Silence was the Order's other teaching, after all: *the gods are gone; the Vigil is how we honor what we lost.* He had recited it ten thousand times. He had held dying men's hands and told them the dark after death was only rest, and he had almost — *almost* — come to believe there was nothing listening anywhere, that the world was a great empty house with all the lamps put out, and that his own unending life was just the last candle burning down in an abandoned room.

But every dawn, he knelt. Every dusk, he knelt. Habit. Hope. Whatever you wanted to call the stubborn animal refusal of the heart to accept a final answer.

He knelt now, on the cold flagstones, and folded his hands, and said the old words in the old tongue, the one the Order had forbidden in his last century there — *the language of superstition,* they'd called it, before they'd scrubbed it from the liturgies entirely. He said them quietly, half under his breath, the way a man speaks when he doesn't want to wake anyone:

*"Verren who walks behind the world, keepers of the crossed roads, hearers in the hollow places — if any of you remain, if any of you ever were —"*

He stopped.

The candle flame stood up straight.

Not guttered. Not flared. It simply *stood*, motionless as a painted flame, in a room where the door leaked wind and the window cloth breathed with every gust off the river. And the silence — the great, upholstered, three-hundred-year silence that Isandro had come to think of as the fundamental texture of reality, the way fish might think of water — *moved.*

There was no sound. That was the terrible part. What answered him made no sound at all. It was more like pressure, like the moment before thunder when the air goes heavy and your teeth ache and the birds go quiet all at once. The honey-colored light thickened. The blank face of Saint Verren seemed — Isandro would spend the rest of his life failing to describe this — seemed to be *looking* at him, though it had no eyes, had never had eyes, and the attention that fell on him was vast and slow and old beyond the Order's oldest books, older than the Order, older perhaps than the idea of kneeling.

And then, inside the silence, inside the pressure, a voice that was not a voice spoke a name.

*Wendell Oyelaran.*

Two syllables of warmth and one of grief. A name spoken the way you speak the name of a child lost in a crowd — not information but *longing*, a reaching, a hand extended across some distance Isandro's mind refused to measure. Along with the name came images, brief and burning, the way a struck match shows you a whole room in a single flare: a road climbing through pines toward a pass full of broken teeth of stone. An ember, small as a fist, glowing in cupped hands. A guildhouse burning. A face —

The match went out. The flame bent in the ordinary wind. Rain drummed on the roof. Mother Cassa's needles clicked.

Isandro was on his feet without remembering rising. His heart was slamming against his ribs like something trying to get out, or maybe like something trying to get *in*. Three hundred years. Three hundred years of nothing, and then this — this *attention* — and it had not said *be comforted* or *your faith is rewarded* or any of the thousand things the old hymns promised. It had said a stranger's name and shown him fire, and it had felt less like a blessing than like a hand closing around his wrist.

"No," he said aloud. His voice cracked in the middle of it, which embarrassed him even with no one watching except a faceless saint and a wool-winding widow. "No. I'm exiled. I'm finished. Whatever this is, you've made a mistake."

The candle burned normally. The rain fell. Nothing answered, and somehow the nothing was worse — as if whatever had spoken had gotten what it wanted and withdrawn, satisfied, leaving him holding the name like a hot coal.

"Wendell Oyelaran," he whispered, testing it. It tasted of pine smoke and cold high air. He did not know the name. He was certain he did not know the name. And yet some cellar-deep part of him, some floorboard of memory that predated everything he remembered, creaked under it, the way a house creaks when someone walks above.

*You know this person,* that floorboard said. *You have known them longer than you have known yourself.*

"That's impossible," Isandro told the empty shrine, and the shrine, being a shrine, declined to argue.

* * *

He lasted three days.

He told himself he would ignore it. He was good at ignoring things; it had been the bulk of his spiritual discipline for centuries. He sat with Mother Cassa's husband through two bad nights and one quiet one, and held the old man's hand as he went, and said the funeral rites over him in the forbidden tongue because Mother Cassa liked the sound of it and because Isandro was tired of pretending the sanctioned liturgy was anything but furniture. He dosed the cooper's boy with willow bark and honey and a poultice he had learned in a century whose name had been struck from the maps, and the fever broke on the second morning, and the cooper tried to pay him and Isandro took only bread, and felt, briefly, almost peaceful.

Then on the third night he dreamed of the mountain.

He dreamed he was climbing a road that did not exist — he knew it did not exist the way you know things in dreams, absolutely and uselessly — a road of pale stone stitched along a mountainside above a valley drowned in cloud, and the road was lined with waymarkers carved in shapes he almost recognized, and far ahead, impossibly far, something small and red glowed like a coal carried through the dark. And he was not alone on the road. Someone walked ahead of him, hooded against the wind, and turned — only partially, only enough that he saw the line of a jaw, rain-wet, and heard them say, in a voice roughened by altitude and grief:

"You're late."

He woke with tears on his face and the taste of pine smoke in his mouth and his hands aching as if from cold, though the shrine was warm, though it was midsummer, though he had not left the lowlands in forty years.

He lay staring at the ceiling beams and understood, with the flat clarity that arrives only at the worst possible moments, that he was going to follow it. That he had already decided. That some part of him had decided three hundred years ago, or three hundred years before that, in a life he could not remember, and that everything since — the Vigil, the exile, the careful small goodnesses of Vael's Crossing — had merely been him walking in a wide circle around this one point on the map, waiting for the circle to close.

Because that was the other thing the dream had shown him, the thing he had not let himself look at directly until now, lying in the dark with his heart going like a trapped bird: the hooded figure on the road had turned, and even seeing only a jawline and rain, some knowledge older than his memory had risen in him like water into a dry well, and the knowledge said, with unbearable tenderness:

*You love this person. You have always loved this person. You loved them before you had a name.*

He got up. He dressed. He took down from behind the altar the satchel he had packed and repacked and unpacked eleven times in four months — he had told himself it was in case of flood, in case of fire, in case the cooper's boy needed carrying to a physician in Lowmark; he had never once believed himself — and he stood a moment before the blank-faced icon of Saint Verren.

"If this is a trap," he said quietly, "it's a strange one. And if it's a mercy, I don't deserve it. But either way—" His voice failed, steadied. "Either way, I'm coming. Whoever you are. Whatever you are. *Wendell Oyelaran.*"

The candle flame leaned toward him, very slightly, against the draft.

Isandro laughed — a short, cracked, terrified sound — shouldered the satchel, and stepped out into the rain.

* * *

Vael's Crossing at dawn was gulls and fish-stink and the ferry chains groaning. He bought passage east on a grain barge with the last of the coin he'd been saving for a winter he now suspected he'd never see, and while the bargemaster argued about his fare, Isandro looked back once at the town: at the shrine with its mismatched roof, at Mother Cassa's window where a lamp still burned, at the whole small warm life he had built out of the wreckage of eternity.

He had spent three hundred years learning how to be no one. It had taken the gods one night to remind him that he had never once asked *why* he was no one — who had made him so, and what they had taken out of him to do it, and whether the hole they'd left was shaped like a mountain road and a burning guildhouse and a name spoken in longing.

The barge pulled away from the pier. The rain thinned. Far to the east, past the river's last bends, the mountains stood up out of the morning haze like the backs of sleeping animals — and among them, though no map showed it and no road reached it, something waited, glowing faintly, patient as a coal under ash.

Isandro turned his face toward it and did not look back again.

He did not yet know that three days' ride behind him, riders in the Crown's grey livery were asking questions in Vael's Crossing — questions about embers, about temples, about names. He did not yet know that the name *Wendell Oyelaran* was already moving up the river valley faster than any barge could carry it, whispered in wayside shrines and written in ash on hearthstones, hunted by people who wanted it dead and others who wanted it devoured.

He knew only the pull. Only the road. Only the certainty, dread-laced and longing-soaked, that somewhere ahead of him a stranger with a familiar face was running out of time —

— and that for the first time in three hundred years, he was needed.
