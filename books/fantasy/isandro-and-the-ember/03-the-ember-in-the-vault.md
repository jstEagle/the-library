## 3. The Ember in the Vault

The rain came down on Thornfell like a debt being collected.

Wendell Oyelaran stood at the guildhouse window with a cup of barley tea going cold in their hands and watched the water sheet off the eaves into the courtyard below, where the last of the autumn caravans was being tarped and tied down for the night. Four mules. Two wagons. A merchant named Corvas who paid half what the route was worth because he knew half was all the Wayfinders could charge anymore.

Once, there had been thirty of them. Once, the sign over the gate — *the mountain provides the road; we provide the way* — had been repainted every spring by apprentices who complained about it and loved it anyway. Now the paint peeled in long gray ribbons, and the only apprentice left was Wendell themselves, twenty-six years old and older than that in the bones, and the only master was Sefu, who was seventy if he was a day and whose hands shook now when he drew maps.

Old Marta kept the ledgers. Old Bren kept the stables and drank. And Wendell kept walking, because someone had to, because the roads through the Thornfell Mountains did not care whether anyone remembered them or not. The smuggler's cut above Greywater still wanted its switchbacks counted. The pass under Crowspine still shifted after every hard winter, closing one road and opening another like a slow animal turning in its sleep. If no one walked those paths, they would forget themselves. And then the villages on either side would starve quietly, season by season, and no one would ever quite know why.

That was the work. That had always been the work. Not glory — the Wayfinders had never had glory — just the patient, unglamorous labor of knowing where you were.

Wendell drank the cold tea anyway and went down to find Sefu.

* * *

The guildmaster's study sat at the heart of the building, behind two doors, the inner one iron-banded and older than anything else in the house. Wendell had been inside perhaps six times in their life. It was where the charter lived, and the seals, and the vault.

Sefu was sitting at his desk when Wendell came in, but he wasn't working. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the box on the desk before him: black lacquer gone gray with age, banded in bronze, large enough to hold a loaf of bread. Wendell knew it the way they knew their own heartbeat. Every apprentice learned it in their first year, in this same room, with the same words: *This is the ember. It is the guild's oldest trust. You will never open the box. You will never let it out of guild hands. You will not ask why.*

Wendell had not asked why. Twenty years of not asking why sat in their chest now like a stone as Sefu looked up, and Wendell saw that his eyes were red-rimmed and sleepless.

"Sit," Sefu said.

"I've got Corvas's manifest to—"

"Sit, Wendell."

They sat. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the shutters.

Sefu turned the box so it faced them. "Tell me what you see."

"A box I'm not supposed to look at."

"Humor an old man."

It was just the box. Black lacquer, bronze bands, the guild's mark burned into the lid — a road splitting around a stone. Except. Wendell leaned closer, frowning. "There's light coming from the seams."

"There is," Sefu said quietly. "There has been for eleven days."

The stone in Wendell's chest dropped through the floor. "That's not possible. It's been dark since—"

"Since always. Since the founding." Sefu's hands, shaking, settled flat on the desk as if to steady himself against it. "Every guildmaster before me watched that box for their whole tenure and saw nothing. Ash, we called it. Dead ash in a pretty coffin. We carried it because the charter told us to, and the charter was old enough that obeying it felt less like faith and more like breathing." He exhaled. "Eleven days ago, Marta came to me swearing she'd seen a glow under the vault door. I called her a fool. Then I looked."

"What is it?" Wendell asked. And then, hearing the childishness of the question, tried again: "What's *in* it?"

Sefu rose, joints cracking, and went to the iron-banded cabinet against the far wall. From it he took a scroll case sealed with wax older than the guildhouse itself — the founding charter, which Wendell had seen exactly once, at their oath-taking, held up unopened like a relic at a funeral. He broke the seal now. His hands were steadier than Wendell had ever seen them, the way a drunk goes steady at the moment that matters most.

"Read the third clause," he said, and laid the vellum before them.

The script was archaic, the ink brown with centuries, but Wendell had spent their life reading old trail-records and survey-marks, and the words came clear:

*And the Guild shall keep the Ember unopened and unquenched, and bear it in trust, until such time as the gods wake. On that day, the Guild shall know its true charge: to carry the Ember home.*

"Home," Wendell said. "Home to where?"

"The charter doesn't say." Sefu sat back down heavily. "But the oldest maps do. There's a place marked on the founding surveys — a temple, up past the Pass of Teeth, drawn in the hand of Aldric Vane himself. No name. Just a symbol." He pulled a map from the desk drawer, yellowed and soft as cloth, and turned it toward Wendell. In the blank white space north of Crowspine, where every modern map said *UNSURVEYED — GLACIER*, there was a small inked shape: a door, standing open, with light coming out of it.

Wendell stared at it until the lines blurred. "The gods are dead," they said. "Everyone knows the gods are dead. The Godswar ended them three hundred years ago. There's nothing up there but ice."

"Yes," Sefu said. "That is what everyone knows." He tapped the glowing box once, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was sleeping. "So tell me, apprentice. What wakes up dead?"

* * *

They talked until the candles burned down to puddles.

Sefu told Wendell everything he knew, which Wendell slowly understood was almost nothing — a handful of clauses, a symbol on a map, and the accumulated weight of generations of guildmasters who had passed the box along like a hot coal nobody dared examine. He told them about the vow every master took in secret at their investiture: *if it glows, send it home.* He told them he'd sent letters, three days ago, to the two surviving chapters of the guild — the ones in Dellharrow and Southmarch, if anyone even still answered there.

"And if no one answers?" Wendell asked.

"Then it's you and me, and Marta, and Bren, and four pairs of boots between us and the Pass of Teeth." Sefu smiled, and it was a terrible smile, tired and bright at once. "The mountain provides the road."

"We provide the way," Wendell finished, because the litany demanded completion, and something in their chest ached at how small the words sounded in the empty room.

"You could refuse," Sefu said suddenly, looking at them across the candlelight with an expression Wendell couldn't read. "You should know that. Whatever comes, you could walk out that gate tomorrow and no one would call you coward. You didn't swear the master's vow. You're not bound."

Wendell thought about saying yes. They thought about Corvas's caravan leaving in the morning, about the warm ordinary life of manifests and switchbacks and tea going cold at windows. They thought about how badly they wanted that life, how they had built it plank by plank over the ashes of every other hope they'd ever had.

"No," they said. "I'm bound well enough. I'm a Wayfinder."

Sefu closed his eyes briefly, and Wendell would remember, afterward, that he looked less proud than grieved — as if Wendell had confirmed something he'd been praying wouldn't be confirmed.

"Go sleep," he said. "Tomorrow we plan."

* * *

Wendell did not sleep.

They lay in their narrow bed under the eaves listening to the rain slacken, running the charter's words over and over until they wore grooves in them. *Until the gods wake. Carry the Ember home.* It was absurd. It was heresy, practically — the temples taught that the Godswar had burned the gods out of the world like a fire burning out of a forest, and that what remained was theirs alone to steward. Kings had built thrones on that teaching. Whole orders of priests had been founded on it.

And yet the box was glowing.

Somewhere past midnight, the rain stopped, and in the new quiet Wendell heard what they first took for wind moving through the courtyard. Then they heard hooves.

They were at the window before they'd decided to move. Below, in the blue hour before dawn, five riders were coming through the gate — too disciplined for bandits, too plain for merchants. Gray cloaks, hooded against the wet. Their horses were Crown stock; Wendell had appraised enough remounts in border towns to know the deep chest and the branded shoulder. At their head rode a woman whose bearing made even the horses seem to sit straighter.

Inquisitors. Wendell had seen one once, years ago, at a distance, and had spent a week afterward feeling vaguely guilty for no reason they could name.

They dressed fast and took the back stairs down, meaning to wake Sefu, and met him already in the corridor, fully dressed, the black box tucked under one arm as naturally as if he carried it to breakfast every day. His face was terrible and calm.

"How did they get here so fast?" Wendell whispered. "You only sent the letters three days ago. Dellharrow is a week's ride."

"I know," Sefu said.

"So they didn't come because of the letters."

"No," Sefu agreed. "Which means they came because of the glow. Which means someone outside these walls already knew it could glow." He looked at Wendell for a long moment, and whatever he saw in their face made him nod, once, as if to himself. "Vault passage. Go now. Take the west stair, the one behind the linen store. Don't stop for anything. If I'm not at the hollow oak by midmorning—"

"Sefu—"

"—then you take the box and you keep walking, and you don't look back, and you *don't ask why*." For the first time all night his voice cracked, just slightly, on the old formula. "One last time, apprentice. Do as your guildmaster says."

Below them, fists were hammering on the front door — not knocking, hammering, the rhythm of people who expected obedience and had the authority to demand it. A voice carried up the stairwell, level and unhurried and somehow worse for it:

"In the name of the Crown: open. We have questions for the Thornfell Wayfinders regarding a certain heirloom. Open, or we will conclude the guild has something to hide."

*A certain heirloom.* Not *the guild's records*. Not *your charter*. The word *heirloom*, specific as a knife.

Wendell's skin went cold. No one outside the guild knew what the box was. No one had known for three hundred years. The safe world — the small, shabby, beloved world of manifests and cold tea and peeling signs — cracked down the middle like river ice in spring, and Wendell heard it go.

"Go," Sefu said again, gently, and pressed the box into their arms.

It was lighter than it looked. It was warm. Through the lacquer, faint as a pulse under skin, Wendell felt something inside it keeping time with their own heart — and quickening.

They ran. Behind them, the front door splintered, and Sefu's voice rang out through the guildhouse, steady and formal and utterly unafraid, greeting the Crown's inquisitors the way a host greets guests, while Wendell took the hidden stair two steps at a time into the dark beneath the mountain, carrying a god's worth of questions and one warm, waking box.
