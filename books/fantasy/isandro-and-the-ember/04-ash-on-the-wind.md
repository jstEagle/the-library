## 4. Ash on the Wind

The inquisitors came back at moonrise.

Wendell knew it before they arrived — knew it in the way the dogs in the village below went silent all at once, a silence with edges to it, and in the way the ember in its iron casket beneath the guildhouse had begun to hum. Not glow. Hum. A low, patient vibration Wendell felt through the soles of their boots as they stood at the eastern window of the guildhouse, watching the tree line where the road from Vael's Crossing bent out of sight.

Three days since the first visit. Three days since the man with the grey gloves had asked his soft questions — *how old is your charter, Master Oyelaran? What does the guild keep in its vaults? Who founded the Thornfell Wayfinders, and what were they told to guard?* — and smiled like a man who already owned every answer and was only checking the arithmetic.

Sefu had sent word to the other two members that morning. Maren was two days north with a survey party; old Corvo was wintering in the lowlands with his bad hip. No one would come. The guild that had once numbered four hundred strong across nine chapterhouses was, tonight, an old man and his last apprentice in a house full of maps, waiting for fire.

"You should go now," Sefu said behind them. He stood in the doorway of the map room with a lamp in one hand and his walking stick in the other, and he did not look like a dying man's shadow anymore. He looked like Guildmaster Sefu of Thornfell, sixty years on the high roads carved into his face, and Wendell felt their chest tighten at the sight of him.

"They'll ask questions first," Wendell said. "They always ask questions first."

"Questions are how they find out whether burning us will be noticed." Sefu set the lamp down and crossed to the great table where the master map of the Thornfell range lay pinned under brass weights. "I've watched men like this before, child. In the border wars. They don't come back twice unless they've decided."

"The ember—"

"Is not yours to worry about yet." He said it gently, but there was iron under it. "It may be nothing. It may be they're chasing rumors, some drunk caravan hand who saw the vault door and let his imagination run. We sit through the night, we answer politely, we show them nothing, and by morning—"

The humming stopped.

Both of them froze. The silence where the hum had been was worse than the sound, the way a held breath is worse than breathing. Then, from somewhere deep below the flagstones, the ember *flared* — Wendell felt it like a struck bell inside their own ribs, a single note of heat that made the lamp flame lean sideways though no draft moved.

Sefu's face went grey. "It's never done that."

"No," Wendell said. Their mouth was dry. "It hasn't."

And then the first torch crested the hill.

* * *

There were more than three days ago. Wendell counted eleven riders, maybe twelve, moving in the disciplined silence of soldiers who had been told noise would cost them something. They wore no livery. That was its own kind of signature; only the Crown's inquisition traveled unmarked, because only the Crown's inquisition needed no one's permission and wanted no one's memory.

"Back stairs," Sefu said quietly. "Now."

"I'm not leaving you."

"Wendell." He caught their wrist, and his grip was still strong, callused from fifty years of rope and rock. "Listen to me. Everything I have ever taught you, every lesson, every mile — it was for this. Not for guiding fat merchants through smuggler's cuts. For this." His eyes went to the floor, to the vault below, to the ember that had slept under Thornfell for longer than the guild had kept records. "Go down. Take the casket. The west passage comes out in the scree field above the millrace. You know it?"

"I know it."

"You've never walked it."

"I've memorized it."

Something flickered across his face — pride and grief sharing the same small space. "Memorized. Yes. That's my apprentice." He pressed a key into their palm, cold and heavy, older than either of them. "Vault door, then the passage gate. Two turns left, one right, and *count your steps*, Wendell. Maps fail. Counting doesn't."

"And you?"

Sefu smiled, and it was the smile he'd worn when Wendell was twelve and had gotten them both lost above the Greywater falls, the smile that said *we are in trouble but we are in it together.* "Someone has to stand at the front door and be a stubborn old man. It's the one role I was born for."

"Sefu—"

"Solstice," he said. "You take it to the Hollow Temple by solstice. Do you understand me? Whatever happens tonight, whatever they tell you afterward, whatever you decide the truth of that thing is — the charter says *until the gods wake*, and child, I think they are waking. Nineteen days. Go."

Above them, fists began hammering on the guildhouse doors.

* * *

The vault smelled of cold stone and lamp oil, and Wendell tried very hard not to think about why the guildmaster might have had lamp oil carried down in the last three days.

The casket sat on its stone plinth where it had sat for as long as anyone could remember, iron banded with silver, the guild's seal stamped into the lid — a compass rose with eight points instead of four, the extra points marking roads that appeared on no living map. Wendell had dusted it a thousand times. Had been told, a thousand times, *never open it, never touch what's inside, carry it only if the house burns.*

The house was burning. Or would be.

They lifted it. It was lighter than it should have been, the way it had always been lighter than it should have been, and through the iron Wendell felt warmth now — real warmth, alive, like holding a sleeping bird. The hum had returned, faster, urgent, and when Wendell's bare fingers brushed the lid the warmth pulsed once, almost like recognition.

*I know you,* the pulse seemed to say. *Hurry.*

Two turns left. One right. Count your steps.

Behind and above, muffled by stone, came the crash of the front doors giving way, and Sefu's voice — clear, carrying, infuriately calm — saying, "Good evening, sirs. I'm afraid the guildhouse is closed."

Wendell counted steps into the dark.

* * *

The west passage was older than the guildhouse above it. Older than the village, probably. Wayfinder tunnels ran all through the Thornfell bones — escape routes, smuggling cuts, pilgrim ways from before the Godswar, dug by hands that had known the mountain's veins the way a physician knows arteries. Sefu had made Wendell learn forty-one of them blindfolded. *Maps can be seized,* he'd say. *Charts can be burned. Only what's in here* — tapping their temple — *can't be taken from you.*

Forty-one passages. This was number thirty-seven, and Wendell had never once imagined walking it with the guild's whole inheritance in their arms while the house above them died.

The air grew warm ahead — wrong warm — and around step six hundred the tunnel walls began to flicker with orange light bleeding through cracks in the stone. Smoke threaded down through fissures too thin to see. The guildhouse was going up fast. Too fast. Lamp oil didn't burn like that. Something else had been brought up the hill tonight, something the inquisitors fed fires with, and Wendell's stomach turned over as they understood: they hadn't come to search. They'd come to erase. Every chart, every ledger, every record of the Wayfinders' Guild was being fed to flames hot enough to leave no ash worth reading.

They were killing the guild's memory. And memory, Sefu always said, was the only cargo a Wayfinder ever truly carried.

At step nine hundred the passage forked, and Wendell stopped, heart slamming, because the count was wrong. Nine hundred and twelve was the junction. They had counted true. Unless—

Unless the mountain itself had shifted. Unless the tunnel had changed. Unless the ember's waking was doing something to the old ways, waking *them* too, and Wendell filed the thought away with trembling hands because there was no time to be terrified of it properly.

Left. Up. Toward the scree field above the millrace.

The light behind them was brighter now, and with it came a sound that stopped Wendell mid-stride: voices, in the tunnel. Behind. Following. Of course following — a house that burns that completely leaves no bodies to find, and inquisitors who erase memory would want to erase the last apprentice too.

Wendell ran.

The casket bounced against their hip with every stride, warm as a heartbeat now, and absurdly — horribly — Wendell found themselves talking to it, breathless, half-sobbing: *"Almost there, almost there, don't you dare go out, don't you dare—"*

The scree field opened under a sky gone orange at the horizon, and Wendell came out of the mountainside like something born, stumbling onto loose stone, lungs full of smoke, and turned to look back.

Thornfell guildhouse was a lantern against the dark. Every window lit. The slate roof already sagging, the weathervane — the old compass rose, forged by the founders — glowing cherry-red at its peak. Figures moved at the base of the blaze, small and orderly, feeding it, watching it, making sure.

Wendell looked for Sefu in the windows and made themselves stop looking.

He had known. He had poured the oil himself, probably, or ordered it, three days ago when the grey-gloved man first smiled. He had chosen this — chosen to be the last wall between the hunters and the hunted, chosen to burn with the guild's records so that nothing could be read out of the ashes except *the guild is dead, there is nothing here, look elsewhere.*

Stubborn old man. Born for the role.

Wendell knelt in the scree with the casket in their lap and did not cry, because crying took breath and breath took time, and nineteen days was not enough time. Nineteen days to cross the Thornfells in early winter. Nineteen days to reach a temple that appeared on no map, in a valley whose name had been struck from every chart the guild ever kept — except, Wendell realized, the ones in their own head, the ones Sefu had made them learn without explaining, the blindfold lessons, the counting lessons, the *why do I need to know a road that goes nowhere, Sefu? Because someday you will, child. Someday you will.*

He had known. He had been building this escape route into them for ten years, one lesson at a time, the way you lay stones across a river long before anyone needs to cross.

Below, the millrace roared. Above, snow was beginning to fall, fine and dry, the first of the season. On the ridge to the south, a rider broke from the tree line — then another — sweeping the slopes with lantern light, hunting for a survivor the fire had failed to produce.

Wendell stood. Shouldered the casket. Took one long look at the burning house that had been the only home they'd ever known, and gave it the only eulogy a Wayfinder would understand: they turned their back on it and did not get lost.

*North,* said the ember against their spine, warm and certain, and for the first time in their life Wendell Oyelaran listened to the heirloom as if it were speaking. *North, and up, and hurry.*

Nineteen days. Solstice. The Hollow Temple.

They went.

* * *

Dawn found them six miles out, high on a shepherd's track above the valley mist, and that was where grief finally caught them — the way it always does, when the body stops moving and has nowhere left to put the fear.

Wendell sat on a cold boulder with the casket across their knees and shook. Not crying, exactly. Something rougher than crying. Ten years of mornings in that house — Sefu arguing with the chimney, the smell of ink and pine resin, Maren's terrible singing, Corvo's stories told for the hundredth time as if for the first. All of it a pillar of smoke on the southern horizon now, and two of the four of them scattered and ignorant, and one of them ash.

*"Take it to the Hollow Temple by solstice."*

They opened the casket.

They had never opened it. No apprentice ever had; that was the first law of the guild, older than any charter. But the first law had died with the house, and the ember was warm against their palms through the inner wrapping, and Wendell was done with inheriting secrets they weren't allowed to understand.

Inside, on a bed of black cloth gone grey with age, lay a lump of what looked like ordinary charcoal — except that it glowed. Faintly, deeply, the way coals glow hours after a fire seems dead: a dim red heart of light, pulsing slow and steady.

Pulsing. Like a heartbeat. Like something asleep rather than something extinguished.

As Wendell watched, the pulse quickened — and the light reached toward them, a thread of red rising off the coal like heat-shimmer given will, drifting up, hovering an inch from their breastbone. Warmth spread through Wendell's chest, and with it, impossibly, a feeling: not words, not yet, but something older than words. Grief, shared. Recognition, absolute. And underneath it, steady as bedrock:

*You are mine, and I am sorry, and we must be quick.*

Wendell closed the casket with shaking hands and sat very still while the shepherd's mist rolled past below and the sun climbed over the Thornfells and lit the whole broken world gold.

Somewhere behind them, inquisitors were picking through ashes. Somewhere ahead, nineteen days of frozen passes stood between them and a temple erased from every map. And somewhere in the lowlands, Maren and Corvo would hear the news and grieve without knowing why the guild had died, and Wendell could not go to them, could not warn them, could not even bury Sefu.

All they could do was carry.

So they stood, and shouldered the casket, and found the north star's daytime ghost in their mind the way Sefu had taught them, and started walking.

They did not see the figure on the far ridge — a lone traveler, standing motionless in the dawn light, watching them with the expression of a man who has just heard a name spoken in a dream and cannot rest until he learns why.

But the ember saw. And its pulse, for just a moment, sounded almost like hope.
