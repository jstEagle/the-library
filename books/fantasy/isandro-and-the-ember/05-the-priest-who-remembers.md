## 5. The Priest Who Remembers

The voice had not stopped speaking since Vael's Crossing.

Three days on the high road now, and Isandro had learned to live with it the way a man learns to live with a stone in his boot — by pretending, at intervals, that it wasn't there, and failing, at every interval, completely. It did not shout. That was the worst of it. It murmured, the way embers murmur in a banked fire, and it said only one thing, over and over, in a tone that was less command than ache:

*West. The high road past Thornfell. The one who carries my heart.*

He had tried praying it away. He had tried drinking it away, though the wine of the border towns was thin and sour and accomplished nothing but a headache layered over the dread. He had tried, on the second night, kneeling in a roadside shrine with his palms pressed flat against cold stone, saying: *I am exiled. I am nothing. Whatever you are, you have the wrong man.*

The voice had answered with something that was almost laughter — warm, sorrowful, unbearably patient — and then it had shown him a road.

Not told him. *Shown* him. It opened behind his eyes like a door: a track climbing out of the river valley where no track existed on any map he'd ever copied, bending through a stand of black pines, fording a stream beneath an arch of stone so old the moss had eaten its carvings. And at the end of the vision, always, the same image: a figure walking alone on a ridge against a grey sky, small and stubborn, with something bright cupped against their chest like a stolen sun.

Isandro had wept in that shrine. Not from fear, or not only. From recognition. Because when the vision came, some part of him — some part older than his exile, older than his vows, older than the careful blank wall where his childhood should have been — had looked at that road and thought: *yes. I have walked this before.*

He did not know when. He did not know how. He knew only that his feet remembered what his mind had been denied, and that frightened him more than any inquisitor ever could.

So he walked west. What else was there? A priest without a god is just a man with habits, and his habits had carried him three hundred years; they could carry him a little further.

* * *

He found the road on the morning of the fourth day, exactly as the vision promised: a seam in the hillside hidden behind a curtain of ivy-grown rock, invisible until you were standing in front of it, obvious once you were. The stone arch rose above the stream, its carvings worn to suggestion — he traced one with his fingers and felt, rather than saw, the shape of wings folded around a flame.

*Old names,* the voice whispered, and gave him another, unasked: *this is the Sorrowford. Before your maps, it was called the Sorrowford. Before your maps, everything had a name.*

"Whose maps?" Isandro asked aloud, and a heron started from the reeds, and the voice said nothing more, which was its own kind of answer.

He climbed. The track wound up through the black pines, narrow and sure, engineered in ways modern roads were not — switchbacks cut to shed water, culverts of fitted stone still doing their work after centuries of neglect. Whoever built this path had meant it to last, and to be forgotten. Both intentions had succeeded perfectly.

Near midday, the pines broke open onto a long shoulder of moorland, and Isandro saw the figure from his vision.

They were perhaps half a mile ahead, walking with the particular economy of someone who has measured their strength against a long road and refuses to waste a step of it. Hooded, travel-stained, one hand resting near their chest in a gesture that might have been habit or might have been protection. Alone. No caravan, no guard, no companion.

*There,* said the voice, and it was not triumph in it. It was grief. It was a mother's grief, if Isandro had ever known what that sounded like, and he suspected — distantly, through the wall — that he did.

He stepped onto the road.

* * *

"Hail," he called, and immediately regretted it, because the figure stopped dead in the manner of prey animals and soldiers both, and turned with a hand already inside their coat.

Up close, the walker was younger than Isandro expected — twenty-five, perhaps thirty, with wind-chafed cheeks and the deep-tired eyes of someone who had not slept properly in days. Grief sat on them like a garment they hadn't chosen. Their gaze went over Isandro quickly and thoroughly: his boots, his hands, the faded stole he still wore out of habit knotted at his belt, and finally his face, where it stayed.

"You're a priest," they said. Their voice was rough. "Or you're wearing a dead man's clothes."

"Both, possibly." Isandro kept his hands visible and open. Old training. He could not remember which order had taught it to him, which was a sentence he had never spoken aloud to anyone. "I mean you no harm."

"Nobody means harm. Harm arrives anyway." The walker's hand hadn't left their coat. "You've been following me since the ford. Don't bother denying it — I cross water to break scent-tracks, and there's been a crow on my line all morning that doesn't act like a crow."

"I haven't been following you." It was true, and it was useless, and Isandro knew it would sound like a lie. "I was sent to find you. There's a difference, though I grant it won't feel like one."

"Sent by whom?"

And there it was: the question he had been dreading for four days, because there was no honest answer that didn't end the conversation, and no dishonest answer that he could live inside. Isandro had spent three centuries being careful with truth. He found, abruptly, that he was tired of it.

"By a god," he said. "One I don't know. One I'm fairly sure everyone else has forgotten too. It spoke to me in a shrine in Vael's Crossing — first time anything has answered a prayer of mine in longer than I can account for — and it put a name in my mouth and a road behind my eyes, and both of them led here." He paused. "Wendell Oyelaran."

The effect was immediate and total. The walker went white, then grey, then very still, the way water goes still before it freezes, and their hand came out of their coat holding not a blade but a knife after all — short, plain, well-kept — pointed at the ground between them but ready to change its mind.

"Say that name again," Wendell said quietly, "and I'll open your throat and ask the crows what they know about you."

"You won't," Isandro said. "You'd have done it already otherwise. You're grieving, and exhausted, and hunted — I can see all of that, and I'm sorry for all of it — but you're also a Wayfinder, or the last of them, and Wayfinders don't kill men who walk toward them with empty hands." He spread those hands a little wider. "Though I'll admit my certainty rests on guild traditions I may be misremembering, given that I appear to remember things I was never taught."

"That's the second impossible thing you've said in a minute."

"There will be more. I've stopped rationing them."

Something flickered across Wendell's face — not trust, nothing like trust, but the weary recognition of one exhausted person meeting another. The knife did not lower. "The ember," Wendell said. "What do you know about the ember?"

"That it isn't an heirloom." Isandro watched them flinch. "That it's a heart. That it's waking. That someone is hunting sleeping gods and killing whatever stands between the hunters and their prey — I know that last part because I burned, two nights ago, a waystation on the south road where six people died asking questions about you. I read what was left of the walls. Inquisitors' work, Crown-wax seals, and hounds that don't leave tracks."

"How do you know all this?" Wendell's voice cracked on *know*. "Who *are* you?"

"My name is Isandro. I was a priest of the Order of the Quiet Flame, before my exile — except I've begun to suspect the order never existed, or didn't exist the way I remember it, because I can name every rite and recite every canon and there is no record of us anywhere, and I have looked." His own voice was fraying now, and he let it. Honesty was a knife too; it seemed fair to show the edge. "I remember a life I cannot verify. I remember roads I have never walked. Three nights ago something old and vast and grieving used my mouth to say your name, and I have come four days through country I shouldn't know to warn you that the people hunting you have crossed the Greywater, and they are faster than you think."

Silence. Wind moved the moor grass in long silver strokes. Somewhere below, a hawk cried once and was done with it.

"You expect me to believe that," Wendell said.

"No. I expect you to test it." Isandro nodded back down the trail, toward the hidden arch, the Sorrowford, the road with no name. "Ask me where this path goes. Ask me what the locals called the pass beyond it, three hundred years ago, before the maps were rewritten. Ask me anything a stranger couldn't know, and watch whether I answer."

"Why would I—"

"Because your guild kept secret maps," Isandro said, "and mine kept secret roads, and I do not believe those are two different secrets. Do you?"

He watched the words land. Watched Wendell's breath change, the small involuntary catch of a person whose private suspicion has just been spoken aloud by a stranger. The knife wavered.

"The Hollow Steps," Wendell said slowly. "The old route over the Teeth. Every map shows it collapsed fifty years ago."

"It never collapsed. It was closed. Sealed from the far side in the year the guild charter changed — you'd know the year if you'd been allowed to read the founding document, which I suspect you haven't." Isandro heard himself and marveled. He had never known these things yesterday. They arrived as he spoke, surfacing like bodies in a flooded river, and each one made the wall behind his memory tremble. "There's a marker at the seal. A flame in a circle, cut deep, with a prayer underneath in a script nobody living reads."

"I've seen that mark," Wendell whispered. "It's on the vault door. It's on—" They stopped. Their hand went, unconsciously, to their chest again, to the shape beneath the coat, and Isandro understood suddenly and completely what they were carrying and how close it slept.

"The same mark," he said gently. "Yes."

"You're lying. Or you're bait. The Choir uses priests—"

"The Ashen Choir burns shrines. They wouldn't send one walking." Isandro took a slow step forward, deliberately, so that retreat would be a choice and not an accident. "Test me. That's all I ask. If I'm your enemy, you'll know within ten questions. If I'm something else — then neither of us has the luxury of the answer we'd prefer."

* * *

They tested him for an hour, walking, because Wendell refused to stand still and Isandro refused to fall further behind. The questions came like thrown stones — place names, dates, guild signs, counter-signs, the liturgy of a rite Wendell described only halfway and watched to see if he'd complete it. He completed it. He shouldn't have been able to. Each correct answer seemed to cost Wendell something, and cost Isandro more, because with every word the wall in his head shook harder and he began to hear, faintly, on the other side of it, singing.

Then the wind changed.

Wendell felt it first — stopped mid-stride, nostrils flaring, head turning southwest with the sharp instinct of a creature that had spent a week learning what pursuit smelled like. Isandro smelled it a heartbeat later: dog. Wet dog and leather and the iron-and-oil scent of Crown issue, carried up the slope on a gust off the moor.

"Hounds," Wendell breathed. "How — I forded the Greywater at the slate shallows, I doubled back through the burn, there's no way they—"

"They aren't tracking you." Isandro's mouth had gone dry. The knowledge arrived whole and terrible, the way the road had arrived, the way the name had arrived: *they aren't tracking you.* "They're tracking it. The ember. It's waking, and a waking god leaves a scent on the world, and there are hounds bred in the Crown kennels specifically to smell divinity. I know this." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "I don't know how I know this. Wendell — how far to cover?"

"Cover?" A bitter, breathless almost-laugh. "There's no cover on this moor for a mile. The pines start below the ridge, and the ridge is—" Wendell stopped. Turned. Looked at him with an expression caught between fury and surrender. "The Sorrowford runs under the arch. Under the *stream*. There's a hollow — the guild used it for — I've never seen it, I've only read of it, but you'd know, wouldn't you. You'd know where it is."

"I would." And he did: it opened behind his eyes even as he spoke, moss-hung stone, a gap behind the waterfall-fed arch wide enough for two bodies and a secret, airless and dark and old. "But hear me before we run. Once we go into hiding together, we're allied. Your hunters become mine. My enemies become yours. I am exiled, possibly fictional, and hunted by the same dogs, and I am offering you the only thing I have, which is that I remember a world that's been erased — and right now that world appears to be the only map of the truth either of us owns."

The baying came then, distant but unmistakable, rising off the moor in a chorus that raised the hair on Isandro's arms. Closer than the wind had promised. Wendell's face went through three expressions in as many seconds — calculation, fear, and finally a hard, furious resolve that looked, Isandro thought, like inheritance.

"If you betray me," Wendell said, "I will feed you to the ember. I don't fully know what that means yet. I genuinely look forward to finding out."

"Understood." Isandro was already moving. "For what it's worth — I believe you'd manage it."

They ran together down through the black pines, along a road that had no name on any living map, toward an arch of carved stone where wings folded around a flame. Behind them the hounds' voices swelled, eager and certain, and ahead — beneath the water-song, beneath the moss — something old opened its mouth in the earth and waited to swallow them both.

And in the deepest quiet of his mind, the voice said, almost tenderly:

*Now you begin.*
