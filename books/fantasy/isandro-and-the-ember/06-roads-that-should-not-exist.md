## 6. Roads That Should Not Exist

**Wendell**

The high road out of Vael's Crossing was a lie, and Wendell had known it since they were nine years old.

The Crown's engineers had cut it wide and gentle along the valley floor, graded for carts and soldiers and the comfortable illusion that the mountains were tame. But the Wayfinders' maps told a different story. Two miles past the last farmstead, where the road bent around a shoulder of grey stone, a goat track split away to the north — invisible unless you knew to look for the cairn with its third stone set crooked, deliberate as a signature. From there the true road climbed: switchbacks older than any kingdom, cut by hands that had wanted no one to find them.

Wendell found the cairn without slowing. Behind them, Isandro's footsteps stuttered.

"This isn't the road," he said.

"No," Wendell agreed, and kept walking. "It's the *road*."

They had been traveling together for four days now, bound by hounds and fear and nothing either of them would name aloud. Four days of Isandro asking questions like a man taking inventory of a house he'd once owned. Four days of Wendell answering as few of them as possible. The ember rode against their chest in its lead-lined case, warm as a sleeping bird, and every mile north it seemed to warm a little more — or perhaps that was only Wendell's imagination, only the way it had begun to pulse faintly at night, like something breathing under blankets.

The switchbacks took them up through pine into bare granite. By midmorning the Crown's road was a pale ribbon far below, and the wind had teeth. Wendell stopped at a shelf of rock where the path narrowed, more from habit than need, and looked back.

Isandro stood ten paces down the trail, and he wasn't looking at the view. He was looking at the trail itself — crouched, actually crouched, his fingers resting on a groove worn into the stone at the edge of the path. A drainage channel, cut clean and angled, the kind of thing a mason makes when he expects the mountain to weep for a thousand years.

"You know this route," Wendell said. It wasn't a question. It hadn't been a question since yesterday, when Isandro had led them unerringly through a fog bank so thick Wendell had walked into a boulder, on a stretch of the Thornfell map marked *veil weather — trust the cut stones*.

"I remember it." Isandro straightened. His voice had that careful flatness he used when he didn't want to hear himself. "I've never been here. I want to be clear about that. I have never stood on this mountain in my life."

"Then how do you know the drainage cuts face south?"

Silence. The wind filled it, worrying at their cloaks.

"Because," Isandro said finally, "when I close my eyes and think of this pass, I see it from above. I see all of it. Like a map drawn on the inside of my skull." He laughed, short and joyless. "A priest's memory should be full of psalms, Wendell. Mine is full of *contour lines*."

Wendell turned away first. There was an answer to what he'd just said, and they were not ready to give it, because giving it meant opening the guild's maps in front of him, and opening the maps meant admitting what they had already begun to suspect somewhere below the level of thought, in the place where dread lives before it gets a name.

That night they camped in a hollow beneath an overhang, and Wendell waited until Isandro slept — or pretended to sleep; with him it was getting harder to tell — before unrolling the guild's route book by ember-light.

The Thornfell Wayfinders kept two sets of maps. The trade maps went to clients: honest enough, accurate to a fathom, and deliberately blind. The second set lived in Sefu's iron chest, and no apprentice saw them until Sefu judged them ready, which in practice meant never, because Sefu had run out of ready apprentices to judge. Wendell had seen exactly three of the inner routes in eleven years, each shown once, each sworn to memory and forbidden to paper.

They found the pass on the third page of the inner book.

The line ran exactly where they had climbed. Every switchback. Every shelf. And there, inked small beside the drainage shelf in Sefu's cramped hand, a note Wendell had never been shown, never told, never even hinted existed:

*South-facing cuts. Older than the Guild. Do not repair.*

Older than the Guild. The Thornfell Wayfinders claimed two hundred years of history. Sefu had written those words about stonework that predated the founding charter itself — and someone else, long before Sefu, had written them into a priest's skull.

Wendell sat very still, the case warm against their sternum, and listened to the mountain breathe.

* * *

**Isandro**

Isandro did not sleep. He had done his pretending; now he lay on his back watching stars wheel past the overhang's edge and tried to pray, the way a man with a broken leg tries to walk.

Three hundred years of habit. That was the shape of him, though he could not account for most of it. The order had found him — so they said — as a young man with no history, no family, a gap where a life should have been, and they had given him a new one: robes, psalms, duties, exile. He had filled the gap with devotion the way you fill a cracked jar with wax. It held. Mostly.

And then, in a shrine in Vael's Crossing, something had answered.

He turned onto his side. Across the dead fire, Wendell was awake too. He could tell by the breathing — theirs had changed days ago, gone shallow and watchful, the breath of someone carrying more than luggage.

"You're staring," Wendell said.

"You're awake."

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous profession." Isandro sat up. The ember-case glowed faintly through its seams now, a thread of orange light like a coal wrapped in cloth, and in that light Wendell's face looked younger and older at once. "May I ask you something honest?"

"You may ask. Honesty is rationed."

"The route we climbed today. Your guild has maps of it."

"We have maps of everything between here and the coast."

"And who drew them?"

Wendell paused a beat too long. "Guildmasters. Over generations. It's what we do — we keep the roads alive after everyone else forgets them."

"Mm." Isandro fed a twig to the coals, mostly to have something to do with his hands. "Because I'll tell you what I know, since you're being generous with half-answers. I know this pass the way I know my own hands. I know that the fourth switchback has a false ledge that will drop a man forty feet if he trusts it in the dark. I know there's a spring on the northern face that runs bitter in autumn and sweet in spring, and I know—" he heard his own voice tighten and made himself loosen it— "I know a shrine. Halfway up tomorrow's climb. A hollowed stone with a basin, facing east. I have never seen it. I am certain I have never seen it. And I could draw it for you right now, down to the crack in the basin's rim."

The silence stretched. When Wendell spoke, their voice had gone carefully neutral, which Isandro was learning was worse than anger.

"What does the basin look like?"

"Granite. Stained red at the lip, like rust. Or like something else."

"Isandro." Wendell sat forward, and the ember-light caught the fear in their face and made no attempt to hide it. "The stain is ochre lichen. It's in the guild's notes. The crack is recorded. The orientation is recorded. Those notes are in a locked chest in a burned guildhouse, and I have seen them exactly once, and you are describing them to me word for word."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, a night bird called twice and thought better of it.

"So either," Isandro said slowly, "your guildmasters and I have been drinking from the same well."

"Or someone poured the same water into both of us." Wendell pulled the route book back into their lap, and Isandro watched them stare at a page without appearing to read it. "Someone rewrote the roads, Isandro. Out of the world and into your head, out of the world and into our vaults. And left the real ones to be forgotten."

"Why would anyone do that?"

"I don't know." Wendell closed the book. In the dark their voice came quieter. "But I'm starting to think the guild wasn't founded to keep roads alive. I think it was founded to keep something *else* alive. And I think we've been walking its veins all day."

Isandro lay back down and did not pretend to sleep anymore. Above the overhang, the stars moved with the slow indifference of things that had watched this mountain since before names. He found himself praying again — not to any god of his order, but to the voice that had answered him in Vael's Crossing, the voice that had put a stranger's name in his mouth and a stranger's road under his feet.

*If you can hear me,* he thought, *tell me whose memories these are.*

The mountain said nothing. But the ember-case, across the ashes, pulsed once — soft, brief, unmistakable — like a heartbeat answering a knock.

* * *

**Wendell**

The shrine was exactly where Isandro said it would be.

They reached it near noon on the fifth day, on a ledge halfway up the northern face, after a morning of climbing that had gone from hard to strange. Twice Isandro had stopped them before danger — a snow bridge that looked solid and wasn't, a gully that would funnel rockfall by afternoon — and both times the guild's inner notes, when Wendell checked them at the next halt, confirmed him to the letter. Not approximately. To the letter. To the *handwriting*, almost, as if whoever had built these memories and whoever had written these maps had worked from the same original that no longer existed anywhere in the world.

The shrine sat in a hollow of stone like a cupped palm. A granite basin, stained red at the lip. A crack running from rim to base, fine as hair. Facing east.

Wendell stood at its edge and felt the ground tilt under their certainty.

"It's real," they said. Their own voice sounded far away. "You remembered something real."

"I wish I hadn't." Isandro hung back, arms crossed, and Wendell realized he was afraid of it — afraid that touching it would make the remembering worse. "Real means someone put it there. Someone put it there and then erased it so thoroughly that not even your guild's trade maps show this ledge. Erasing a mountain doesn't happen by accident, Wendell. That takes *institution*. That takes centuries."

"Like an order of priests," Wendell said, "with a novice who has no past."

"Like a guild," Isandro returned, not cruelly, "that burns its own letters."

They looked at each other across the basin, and Wendell saw the moment the same arithmetic finished in his eyes that had been grinding through theirs all morning: two locked chests, two keys, one lock.

Wendell knelt. The basin held old rainwater, dark with pine needles, and their reflection floated in it — a face they had been told was theirs, on a body they had been told was theirs, carrying a name Sefu had given them at eight years old with a strange gentleness that Wendell had never, until this week, thought to question.

*Oyelaran.* Sefu had said it meant *ember-bearer* in a dead dialect. Wendell had always assumed that was sentiment.

Now they wondered if it was inventory.

"Touch the water," Isandro said quietly.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because everything I touch lately answers back." Wendell stood, wiping their hands on their coat as if the basin had already gotten under their skin. "Come on. The pass closes by evening if the cloud comes down, and I'd rather not test whether your memory covers whiteout conditions."

But as they turned to go, the ember shifted against their chest — not warmth this time but pressure, a lean, like a dog scenting home — and from deep inside the lead lining came a sound Wendell had never heard it make. Not a pulse. A hum. Low, resonant, almost a note, almost a *word*.

Isandro heard it too. He had gone rigid, his lips parted, and when he spoke it was barely a whisper.

"It knows this place."

"Everything knows this place except us," Wendell said. "That's rather the point."

They climbed on. But Wendell caught themselves, over and over through the afternoon, glancing back at the ledge where the shrine sank out of sight, and doing sums they didn't want to finish. If the roads had been rewritten, what else? If Isandro's skull held a copy of the guild's secrets, who had made the original — and why did a god's sleeping heart beat faster the closer they came to places only the erased version of the world remembered?

At dusk they made camp in the lee of a ridge, and Wendell opened the route book one more time, telling themselves it was to plan tomorrow.

Tomorrow's route ran through a valley the inner maps named in that same dead dialect Sefu had used for *Oyelaran*. The name, transcribed phonetically, was six syllables long, and the margin note beside it — in ink faded to brown — read:

*Do not speak it aloud. It remembers being spoken.*

Wendell looked across the ashes at Isandro, asleep at last or done pretending, his face slack and unguarded, a man built out of borrowed rooms.

"Who wrote you?" Wendell murmured. "Who wrote *any* of this?"

The ember hummed against their ribs — and this time, unmistakably, the hum shaped itself toward the six syllables of the valley's name, patient as a teacher waiting for a student brave enough to repeat the lesson.

Far to the south, back along the road they'd abandoned, Wendell thought they saw lights moving. Too many. Too orderly.

Torchlight, coming on fast.
