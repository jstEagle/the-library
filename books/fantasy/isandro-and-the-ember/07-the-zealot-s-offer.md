## 7. The Zealot's Offer

The trap closed at dusk, in a valley that had no name on any map Isandro knew — which, he was learning, meant very little anymore.

They had made good time for three days. The high road bent north around the shoulder of the Carrow Fells, and Wendell moved through it with the easy certainty of a man walking his own hallway: reading stone, reading wind, reading the small silences where the land said *turn here*. Isandro followed and tried not to stare. Three hundred years of walking, and he had never once felt the ground speak to him. It spoke to Wendell constantly, in a language they clearly took for granted.

It was Wendell who stopped first.

They stood at the mouth of a narrow defile where the road dipped between two walls of grey shale, and Wendell's head came up like a deer's, nostrils flaring at a wind that carried nothing Isandro could catch.

"Smoke," Wendell said. "Not woodsmoke." A pause. "Incense."

Isandro's stomach dropped. He knew that incense. Every priest of the Ninefold Choir had known it, once — myrrh burned over ash, a scent designed to smell like sanctity to those who had never smelled anything holier. But this was not the Ninefold's blend. This was older, harsher, cut with something bitter underneath, something that itched at the back of the throat like the memory of a fever.

"Ashen," he said quietly.

Wendell looked at him. "You know them?"

"I know *of* them." He found he could not look away from the defile ahead. "They were a heresy before I was exiled. They believed the Godswar ended wrong. That the gods should not have been bound or broken but *consumed* — that divinity is power hoarded by the unworthy, and that mortals were meant to take it back. All of it." His mouth was dry. "I thought they were extinct. I thought everyone thought so."

"And now?"

"Now there is incense in a valley with no villages for thirty miles, and we are carrying the one thing in the world they would cross an ocean for."

Wendell's hand went to the satchel at their chest without seeming to decide to move — the unconscious gesture of someone who had been guarding the ember so long it had become part of how they stood. "Can we go around?"

"The fells close to a cliff face on the east. On the west—" Isandro closed his eyes and let himself do the thing that still frightened him more than inquisitors did: he reached into the part of his mind that remembered roads he had never walked. "There's a shepherd's path. Old. It climbs from the valley floor two miles back and comes out above the shale. We'd lose half a day."

"Then we lose half a day—"

"They're already behind us," Isandro said. "Or beside us. Incense means fire, and fire means they lit it so we would smell it. Serah does nothing without wanting it witnessed."

"Serah."

"Mother Serah. If she's here, then—" He stopped, because the words *then the stories are all true* sat in his throat like a stone.

Wendell turned. Behind them, at the mouth of the valley they had just walked through, figures had begun to emerge from the long grass. Not many. Twelve, perhaps fifteen, robed in undyed wool the colour of old bone, their faces shadowed under cowls. They did not spread out the way soldiers would. They simply stood, spaced with terrible calm, as though they had been growing out of the hillside all along and had only now decided to be visible.

"We're surrounded," Wendell said, with the flatness of someone confirming arithmetic.

"No," Isandro said. "Surrounded is what happens when people want to fight you. Look at their hands. Empty. Open." His own hands, he noticed, had gone cold. "This is an audience chamber. Someone wants to talk."

* * *

She came down the slope alone, unhurried, and Isandro knew her before her hood fell back.

He had never seen Mother Serah. He was certain of that, the way he was certain of his own heartbeat — and yet recognition flooded him anyway, hot and unwelcome, the same terrible familiarity he had felt when the voice answered his prayer in Vael's Crossing. She was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, with a face like a weathered door: plain, strong, built to be opened or barred. Her eyes were pale grey, almost colourless, and they held no cruelty at all. That was the worst of it. Zealots were supposed to burn. Serah looked at him the way a physician looks at a wound she intends to heal.

"Brother Isandro," she said, and her voice was warm. Genuinely warm. "Three hundred years, and you haven't changed. They never let you change, did they? The Order. They kept you like a relic and called it service."

Behind him, Wendell went very still. "You know him?"

"I knew the world he came from," Serah said, not taking her eyes off Isandro. "Before your Order rewrote it. Before they took his memory and called it mercy." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, and Isandro wanted to run. "He doesn't even remember me. Do you, brother? The chapel at Sarnholt? You sang vespers so beautifully the stones seemed to lean in?"

"I don't know you," Isandro said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved.

"No," she agreed gently. "But your body knows me. Your heart is beating like a drum right now. Isn't it? Some doors don't need keys. Only pressure."

Wendell stepped slightly forward, putting a shoulder between Isandro and the woman — a small, instinctive motion that Isandro would think about later, at length, in the dark. "You've gone to a great deal of trouble for a conversation," Wendell said. "Fifteen people. A valley. Incense timed to the wind."

"Fifteen is restraint," Serah said. "I have four hundred in the next range over, and I have spent eleven years finding this road. But yes. Conversation first. I am not my predecessors." She folded her hands, and Isandro saw the scars on her knuckles — old burns, deliberate ones, laid in patterns. "I want to offer your friend a gift. And I want you, Brother Isandro, to help me give it."

* * *

They made camp because Serah insisted on it — "Truth deserves firelight; lies prefer daylight" — and because refusing would have meant fighting fifteen of the Ashen Choir on ground of their choosing with an ember to protect and a mountain behind them. So they sat across a small flame from the woman who devoured gods, and she poured tea from a battered copper pot as though they were travellers sharing a shelter, and the whole thing had the horrible intimacy of a dream.

"You know what we are," Serah said to Wendell. "Your priest has told you. Let me tell you what we are *not*. We are not madmen burning temples. We are accountants. When the Ninefold Order bound the gods — and they did bind them, whatever your guild charter says about protecting relics — the world inherited a debt. Divinity does not vanish. It festers. Every prayer that goes unanswered, every miracle that curdles into plague or famine, every god dreaming in its chains and leaking nightmare into the roots of mountains — that is interest accruing. The Crown knows it. Your inquisitors know it. Why do you think they hunt you? Not to save the ember. To put it back in a vault. To keep the debt on their books, compounding, forever."

"The alternative being?" Wendell asked. Their voice was level. Their hand hadn't left the satchel strap since sundown.

"To pay it. To end it. To unmake the divine entirely and return its substance to the world that made it." Serah leaned forward, firelight moving on her scarred hands. "No masters. No cages. No second Godswar waiting in the dark like a debt collector. Just — people. Free people, in a world with no one kneeling."

"And the god in the ember?" Isandro asked quietly. "Does it get a vote?"

Something flickered across Serah's face — not anger. Pity. "Ah, brother. Still asking after the feelings of your jailers' prisoners. They taught you that well." She turned back to Wendell. "Here is my offer, wayfinder, and I will make it only once, because I respect you enough to be honest: surrender the ember tonight, walk away, live. My people will not pursue you. The Crown's inquisitors will, because that is what inquisitors do — but you'll have a head start and my silence. That is the offer for you."

"And for him?" Wendell said, and Isandro heard it — the edge beneath the calm, the thing that had been building between them for days and that neither of them had named.

Serah turned to Isandro, and her grey eyes were kind, and kind was so much worse.

"For him," she said, "I offer the truth. Whole. Unbroken." She reached into her robe and drew out a small object: a shard of grey glass, dull as river stone, and yet the firelight bent strangely around it, unwilling to touch it. A god-fragment. Isandro had read of them in forbidden commentaries — splinters of the divine, harvested from ruined shrines, that the Choir used to *taste* what they hunted. "Hold this, brother, and you will remember. Not fragments. Not echoes. Everything. The chapel at Sarnholt. The faces of the brothers you loved. The life they scraped out of your skull and sealed behind their vows. Three hundred years, returned to you in a single breath." She extended it across the fire. "All I ask is the ember. One sleeping heart, traded for one waking soul."

The shard sat in the firelight between them, and the world went very quiet.

Isandro did not reach for it. He made himself not reach for it, and the effort was the hardest thing he had done since fleeing the shrine at Vael's Crossing. Because here was the wound of it: he *wanted* it. Not abstractly. Specifically, bodily, with a hunger that made his teeth ache. Three hundred years of being a locked room. Three hundred years of praying to gods he couldn't remember out of habit he couldn't explain. He had told himself the hollow was bearable, that a man could live in the present tense if the present was honest — and then a voice had answered his prayer, and a stranger had known his name, and now a woman with kind eyes was holding the key to his own skull and offering it for the price of a sleeping god's freedom.

And the price was so *small*, said the hunger. It's asleep. It will never know. Wendell can walk away. No one bleeds. You get to be a whole man instead of a haunted one.

"That's not restoration," Isandro heard himself say. "That's ransom. You're not giving me my past. You're teaching me that my past can be taken again — by anyone strong enough. That's the lesson, isn't it? That's the whole doctrine. Power decides what's true, so become the power."

Serah's smile didn't waver. "Yes," she said simply. "That is exactly the lesson. You understand us better than your Order ever did."

"I refuse," Isandro said.

He said it and meant it, and the meaning held — but he watched the shard a moment too long as it withdrew into her robes, and he knew she saw it, and he knew she had come all this way precisely to make him watch it.

"I thought you might," Serah said, without disappointment, folding her hands again. "Refusal is also data. Thank you, brother. You've told me something valuable tonight: the Order's chains hold even after the Order is gone. Which means we won't offer again. Next time, we simply take." She rose, brushing ash from her robes, and inclined her head to Wendell with genuine courtesy. "Wayfinder. A last gift, freely given, no trade attached: the pass ahead floods with meltwater in four days. After that, the only crossing is the Bridge at Greywater, and the Crown's men know it too. Choose your road quickly." She paused. "And keep him close. He's worth more than either of you knows."

They watched the Ashen Choir withdraw up the valley slopes, bone-coloured figures dissolving into the dark until only the incense-smoke remained, thinning on the wind.

* * *

They walked another mile by moonlight because neither of them could bear to sleep in that valley, and made cold camp in the lee of a boulder field. Wendell took first watch. Isandro lay on his back and stared at the stars and did not sleep.

The shard had been ten feet away. Ten feet, and one word.

He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. He had refused. He had refused for the right reasons — he was nearly sure they were the right reasons — and yet the refusal had not closed anything. It had opened him. Somewhere behind his vows, behind the pleasant fog the Order had poured into his memory, something had shifted its weight and turned toward the light, the way a plant turns, without permission, without thought. *Sarnholt.* He said the word to himself in the dark and felt it ring against nothing, a bell with no bell inside. He had sung vespers there. Stones leaning in. Was it memory? Was it Serah's poison, planted in the shape of a memory? He could no longer tell the difference, and that was the seed she had really left — not doubt about her, but doubt about himself. If his past could be restored, then it had been *removed*, deliberately, by the Order he had served. And if they had done that once, what else had they taken? What else was he?

"Isandro." Wendell's voice, low, from the watch-fire. "You're awake."

"Yes."

A pause. Wendell came and sat near him, knees drawn up, the satchel cradled in their arms. For a while neither spoke. The mountains ticked and settled around them, cooling stone, distant water.

"She offered you your life back," Wendell said at last. "And you gave it up. For the ember. For—" A hesitation Isandro could hear them steering through. "For this mission. For me, I suppose, in the practical sense."

"For the principle," Isandro said. "Don't inflate it."

"I'm not." Wendell turned to look at him, and the firelight caught the stubborn set of their jaw, the grief they carried like ballast. "I watched your face when she held it out. Nobody gives up three hundred years for a principle. So thank you. And—" the jaw tightened, "—and I'm sorry. That she made you look at it. That anyone made you look at what was taken from you."

Isandro said nothing. There was nothing honest to say that wasn't also dangerous — because the truth was that he had looked, and some part of him was still looking, still standing in that firelight with his hand half-raised toward a door in his own head.

"Sleep," Wendell said finally, rising. "We move at first light. Greywater, if she's telling the truth about the pass — and I hate that I believe her."

Isandro closed his eyes. Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it brought the chapel at Sarnholt: candlelight, singing, stones leaning in — and a figure in the doorway wearing his own face, watching him with pity, mouthing words he could not hear.

He woke before dawn with the taste of incense in his mouth and one thought sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal:

If the Order could steal three hundred years from him without his knowledge — what had they stolen from Wendell? What had *anyone* stolen, to make a wayfinder who could read roads erased from every map, who flinched at the name of a temple they'd never seen?

He looked across the dead fire at Wendell, asleep with the satchel held like a child, and for the first time he wondered whether the ember was the only prisoner they were carrying.

Far below, in the valley they had fled, the incense smoke still rose — and along the ridge behind them, faint in the greying light, movement. Riders. Too disciplined to be bandits. Too few to be the Choir.

The Crown had found them after all.
