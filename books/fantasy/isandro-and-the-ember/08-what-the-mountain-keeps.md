## 8. What the Mountain Keeps

The storm found them an hour before dusk, rolling down off the high shoulders of the Karag Veth like something with intent, and by the time the light went grey and then went out entirely, Wendell had stopped thinking of it as weather and started thinking of it as pursuit.

"Shrine," Isandro said, half-shouting over the wind. He had stopped walking without turning around, head cocked in that way he did when he was listening to something Wendell couldn't hear — a habit that had stopped being unsettling somewhere on the third day of travel and now only made Wendell's chest ache in a way they refused to examine. "There's a shrine. Under the ridge. Old one."

"How old?"

"Older than the maps." He said it the way he said most things about the mountains: flatly, as if reporting facts he'd rather not own. "There should be an overhang. A carved lintel. Look for the lightning-struck pine."

Wendell looked, and there it was — a blackened pine leaning off the slope like a drunk against a wall, and beneath it, half-swallowed by scree, a mouth of stone. The lintel was there too, just barely: a single line of carving worn nearly smooth, though when Wendell dragged their glove across it they felt the ghost of letters under the ice. Not guild script. Something older, rounder, meant to be read by firelight.

Inside, the cave opened wider than its mouth promised — a low chamber maybe ten paces deep, dry, smelling of cold rock and, faintly, of ash that had gone so old it had become almost sweet. And along the back wall, someone had built a shrine.

It was nothing like the gilded altars of Vael's Crossing or the Crown chapels Wendell had passed through caravans all their life. It was three flat stones stacked into an altar no higher than a knee, a niche cut into the living rock above it, and — this was what stopped Wendell's breath — a mosaic of tiny stones set into the floor in front of it, thousands of them, each one placed by hand, forming a spiral that wound inward toward the altar like water finding a drain.

Someone had knelt here. Many someones, over many years. The stones at the center of the spiral were polished to a soft shine by knees and foreheads.

"They came here to pray," Wendell said.

"People always come to the mountains to pray." Isandro set down his pack and began, with the automatic economy of a man who had camped in worse places for worse reasons, to build a windbreak across the entrance. "That's why the maps were rewritten. Easier to erase a god if you erase the roads to him first."

"You say things like that," Wendell said, crouching to touch the edge of the spiral, "and you expect me not to ask."

"I expect you to be tired." He glanced up, and in the gloom his eyes caught what little light remained — grey-green, steady, older than his face. "Ask tomorrow. Tonight we survive."

* * *

They survived it. That was almost the whole of the night's ambition, and they achieved it.

Isandro got a small fire going in the lee of the altar — sacrilege, probably, but the stones didn't object, and Wendell found they didn't have the energy to care. They ate hard bread and dried plums and drank snowmelt from the skin, and the storm outside screamed itself hoarse against the mountain, and somewhere in the small hours the wind changed its mind about tearing the ridge apart and settled instead into a long, low moan, like a voice practicing a lament.

Wendell couldn't sleep.

It wasn't the cold, or the ground, or the memory of Sefu's face in the last light of the guildhouse — though that was there, always, patient as a debt. It was the ember.

They had carried it four days now in its iron-and-glass reliquary case, slung against their chest the way Sefu had taught every apprentice to carry the guild's heart: close, warm, never left behind. For twenty-two years it had been a coal. A dead thing that remembered being alive, kept out of habit and reverence and, if Wendell was honest, superstition. Since the night the guildhouse burned, it had been something else. It pulsed. Slowly, arrhythmically, like a heart remembering its work after a long illness. When Wendell pressed their palm flat against the case — a habit they'd developed without noticing, the way you touch a bruise — the pulse quickened.

And sometimes, at the edge of sleep, it spoke. Not words. More like the shape words would make if they were still deciding whether to exist.

Tonight the shape was insistent.

Wendell sat up. The fire had burned to a red eye and a scatter of coals; Isandro lay on his side near the entrance, wrapped in his cloak, and even asleep he looked like a man standing watch — spine straight, jaw set, hands loose near the knife he never fully sheathed. In the coals' light his face lost its guardedness and became simply young, and tired, and — Wendell looked away before the thought could finish itself.

They lifted the reliquary case from their chest and held it in both hands.

"I know you're awake," they whispered. The absurdity of talking to a lump of ancient fire did not escape them; they did it anyway. "I don't know what you are. I don't know what you want. But you keep—" Their throat tightened. "You keep looking at me. I can feel it. Like Sefu used to look at me when he thought I wasn't watching. Like I was a question he hadn't worked up the courage to answer."

The ember pulsed. Once. Twice. Then — for the first time — it answered in kind, a slow triple-beat, deliberate, unmistakable.

*Yes.*

Not heard. Known. The way you know your own name.

Wendell's hands shook. They looked over at Isandro — asleep, truly asleep, breathing slow — and told themselves the lie they'd been telling since the guildhouse: *I'll wake him. I'll tell him everything. Just — first let me see.*

They opened the case.

The light that came out was not firelight. Firelight flickers; this light breathed. It rose from the ember in a slow column and bent, impossibly, toward Wendell's face, and the warmth of it was not the warmth of heat but the warmth of a hand on the back of the neck, of being known completely and not flinching. The chamber's shadows leaned away. Even the storm outside seemed to hold its breath.

"Don't," Wendell whispered, to the ember, to themselves, to whatever was listening. "Don't show me anything. I can't—"

The ember showed them anyway.

* * *

The temple was burning.

Wendell stood in a colonnade of white stone, and the air was full of ash and singing — hundreds of voices raised in a hymn that was also a scream, and the columns were cracking one by one like teeth, and beyond them the sky was wrong, bruised purple at noon, shot through with light that fell upward. They knew, with the terrible certainty of dreams, that the world was ending here, in this place, at this hour, and that everyone singing knew it too and sang louder because of it.

A woman stood at the top of the temple steps.

She wore robes the color of embers — no, Wendell thought, correcting the dream with a dreamer's precision, robes the color of the ember, of this exact light, this exact breathing gold — and her hair was bound up in the way Wayfinder apprentices bound theirs for ceremony, the knot Sefu had taught Wendell when they were nine years old, hands guiding their hands. Her back was to the burning. She was facing outward, facing the armies coming up the valley road, and she was not afraid.

She turned around.

Wendell's own face looked back at them.

Not similar. Not a resemblance. *Their* face — the same crooked set of the left eyebrow, the same scar through the right one that Wendell had gotten falling out of a larch tree at eleven, except that in the dream the scar was older, silvered, earned by other falls. The same mouth that went thin at the corners when it was holding something back. The same eyes, dark and restless, that Sefu had once described, gently, terribly, as *eyes that are always looking for the road home.*

The woman saw Wendell seeing her, and she smiled — grief and recognition braided together — and she raised one hand, palm out, the way you greet someone across a distance too great to cross.

And she spoke, and the hymn behind her swallowed most of it, but three words came through the fire whole:

"*Remember me kindly.*"

Then the light took everything — the temple, the armies, the woman, the sky — and folded inward like a closing hand, and Wendell was on their knees on the cold mosaic floor of the cave shrine with the reliquary open in their lap and tears freezing on their cheeks and no memory of having fallen.

* * *

"Wendell."

Hands on their shoulders. Warm, steadying, careful the way you're careful with something you think might break. Isandro's face swam into focus above theirs, lit by the rekindled fire — he must have fed it, must have woken and seen them kneeling in the dark with a god's heart open in their lap and done the practical thing first, because he was Isandro, and then come to them.

"You're bleeding," he said quietly.

Wendell touched their nose. Fingers came away red. Behind them, blood had dripped onto the mosaic — onto the innermost ring of the spiral, where the oldest prayers had worn the stone smooth. It looked, in the firelight, like the final mark in a pattern that had been waiting centuries for exactly that color.

"I saw her." Their voice came out scraped raw. "Isandro, I saw—"

"The woman." He said it without surprise, and that absence of surprise frightened Wendell more than the vision had. He eased them back to sit against the altar stones, crouched in front of them, and waited — really waited, the way he did, giving silence room to work — until Wendell found the rest of the sentence.

"She had my face. Not like mine. *Mine.* Older. She was standing in front of a burning temple and there were armies and everyone was singing while it all came down, and she turned and she looked at me like—" Wendell's breath hitched. "Like she'd been waiting for me specifically. And she said *remember me kindly*, and then—"

"And then the ember closed the door." Isandro glanced at the reliquary, lying open on the floor between them. The ember's glow had gentled to a low, watchful shimmer, like a cat pretending it hadn't been staring. "It's been showing you pieces. Hasn't it. Since the guildhouse."

"At the edges of sleep. Shapes. Feelings." Wendell laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I told myself it was grief. Grief does strange things; Sefu always said so. But this wasn't grief. This was a *memory*. Someone else's memory, wearing my face, and I stood inside it, Isandro, I stood inside it and it fit. Like a coat cut for me." They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "What is happening to me?"

For a long moment Isandro didn't answer. When Wendell lowered their hands, he was looking at the blood on the spiral, and his expression was one Wendell had learned to read on the road these past days: the look of a man weighing how much truth a person can carry, and erring, always, on the side of carrying it himself.

"Tell me," Wendell said. "Whatever it is. Whatever you've been not-saying since Greywater. Tell me now, or I swear by every map Sefu ever drew, I will walk out into that storm and keep walking."

"That would kill you."

"Yes," Wendell agreed. "Probably. Tell me anyway."

He exhaled slowly. When he spoke, it was in the low, level voice of a man reciting something he had memorized against his will.

"There was a priestess. Before the Godswar. Before the prisons, before the guilds, back when the gods still walked with their faces uncovered. The oldest songs call her the Ember-Borne — the god of hearths and roads chose her, marked her, poured part of himself into her so that she could walk among mortals as proof the gods kept faith." His eyes came up to Wendell's. "When the war came, when the mortals turned on heaven, she stood on the steps of her god's temple while it burned, and she bought time for the last faithful to flee, and she died there. Every account agrees on that. She died, and the god's heart was taken — hidden, imprisoned, whatever word you prefer — and the victors rewrote the songs until the Ember-Borne was a cautionary tale and then no tale at all."

"But I saw her alive. Standing there. Looking at *me*."

"I know." Isandro's jaw tightened. "I've been trying to decide for two days whether that means the ember remembers her wrongly, or whether—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Or whether the guild didn't only hide you, Wendell. Whether they didn't only erase your childhood. Whether they gave you a life that was never yours to begin with — a face, a history, a *self* — copied from someone the ember loved enough to remember perfectly."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water, each ripple worse than the last.

"No," Wendell said. It came out very small. "No. Sefu raised me. Sefu taught me knots and stars and which berries lie. He called me *little wren* when he thought I was asleep. You don't — you can't build a person out of a dead priestess and call it raising a child."

"No," Isandro said softly. "You can't. Which is why I don't think that's what happened. I think it's stranger than that, and crueler, and I think the ember is the only thing left that knows the truth — and I think it's running out of patience." He reached out and closed the reliquary case with two fingers, gentle as shutting a sleeping child's door. "It showed you her tonight because you asked. What will it show you tomorrow? Next week, at the temple doors? How much of yourself are you prepared to find out was never yours?"

Wendell looked at their hands. Ordinary hands, rope-callused, ink-stained at the nails from a lifetime of chart-making. A woman who died in fire had worn this exact grip on a sword, or a staff, or the neck of a fleeing child. They couldn't know which. That was the worst of it — the not-knowing had gotten inside everything, the way water gets inside stone and waits for winter.

"I don't know who I am," they said, and it was not self-pity; it was inventory, flat and terrible. "Four days ago I was the last apprentice of a dying guild, and that was a small, sad, *knowable* thing to be. Now I'm carrying a prisoner or a parent or my own past, an exiled priest who hears voices is following me because a god sent him, the Crown wants me caged, a sect wants me devoured, and apparently my own face belongs to a woman who's been dead for a thousand years." They laughed again, helplessly. "Sefu, you absolute bastard. What did you do to me?"

"He tried to protect you," Isandro said. "People do monstrous things out of love. I would know."

Outside, the storm had begun to die, the wind dropping register by register until it was only a whisper moving through the lightning-struck pine. Dawn was hours off. Somewhere below them, down the dark slopes, Wendell felt — actually felt, a pressure behind the sternum that was not their own — the ember notice the change in the weather, and go quiet, and listen.

Listening for what?

They got their answer before dawn, though not from the ember.

Hoofbeats. Many, muffled by snow, moving with the disciplined rhythm of soldiers who had been riding all night and intended to keep riding. Isandro was on his feet with his knife drawn before Wendell had finished waking, and when Wendell crawled to the cave mouth beside him and looked down the switchback trail below the shrine, they saw them: torches strung out along the valley road like a lit rosary, and at the head of the column, riding a grey mare with the unhurried confidence of someone who has never once been lost, a woman in the plain dark traveling dress of the Crown's inquisition.

She did not look up at the shrine. She didn't need to.

"We should run," Wendell whispered.

"She already knows where we are," Isandro said. "Running just tells her we're guilty of something besides walking." He paused, and something grim and almost amused moved across his face. "Besides. Look at her pace. She's not hunting us, Wendell. She's *escorting* us. There's a difference, and I think we're about to learn which of us it favors."

Below, the torches turned onto the shrine road, and began to climb.
