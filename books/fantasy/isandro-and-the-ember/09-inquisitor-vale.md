## 9. Inquisitor Vale

The rain had followed Maren Vale for eleven days, from the Crown's capital all the way into the hill country where maps went vague and roads went by names no cartographer would swear to. It drummed on the canvas of the command tent like a clerk tapping a pen, patient and indifferent, and it had soaked through her boots somewhere around the second river crossing and never quite left.

She sat at the camp table with the dispatch case open in front of her and did not read it again. She knew what it said. She had written half of it herself, back when it had been theory — *anomalous thermal signatures at Thornfell, possible relic-class artifact, investigate with discretion* — before the word *discretion* had been struck out by a hand higher than hers and replaced with *recover by any means*.

By any means. She turned her cup of tea, gone cold an hour ago, in a slow circle on the table. Outside, her soldiers were doing soldier things: hammering tent pegs, cursing the mud, feeding the hounds. Sergeant Okonkwo was shouting at someone about wet bowstrings. It was all very ordinary, and she clung to the ordinariness the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood, because nothing about this assignment was ordinary anymore, and she could feel the shape of the thing coming, the way you feel weather in an old wound.

"Report," she said, when the tent flap opened.

Lieutenant Cassel came in shaking rain off his oilskin like a dog. He was young, competent, and honest, which made him exhausting to be around during assignments like this one. "Scouts are back from Thornfell, ma'am."

"And?"

"The guildhouse burned three nights ago." He set a charred scrap of something on the table — a hinge, maybe, or a buckle, blackened past telling. "Deliberate fire. Accelerant in the foundations, lit from four points. Whoever did it wanted the place gone and wanted it gone fast."

"Inquisitors?"

"No, ma'am. Wrong method. Too fast, too clean. And there were bodies." He hesitated, which he only did when the news was bad in a way reports couldn't hold. "One of them was old. Eighty, maybe more. Died at the back door with a sword in his hand. The others—" He stopped.

"Say it, Lieutenant."

"There weren't others. Not whole ones. The fire took them, or something did. But we found tracks leaving the property. Two sets, heading west toward the high country. One light, one heavier, walking close together. And the old man's body—" Cassel rubbed his jaw. "His hands were burned, ma'am. Badly. Like he'd been holding something hot right up until the end. Something that didn't burn *him*, if you follow me. Just his hands, where he gripped it."

Vale looked at the ruined hinge on her table and thought about the file locked in the dispatch case. Three hundred years ago, the Godswar had ended not with victory but with exhaustion — gods dead or vanished, the realm scorched from the Ashlands to the sea, and a generation of survivors so traumatized they had built their entire new order out of one article of faith: *never again*. No temples above ground. No orders of priesthood permitted to hold land. No relics of the old powers left loose in the world. The Crown's Inquisition existed to enforce that last article, and Maren Vale had spent twenty-two years enforcing it, mostly by confiscating lucky charms from frightened peasants and burning forged miracle-relics that some con artist had painted with phosphor.

She had believed, in a tired, workmanlike way, that she was keeping a lid on a pot that had long since stopped boiling.

Then, eight months ago, the shrines had started answering prayers.

* * *

The first case had come across her desk as a curiosity: a village well in the northern marches that gave sweet water when asked nicely and brackish water when cursed at. She'd sent a junior inquisitor expecting fraud. The junior had come back gray-faced and unable to sleep, reporting that the effect persisted under controlled conditions, that it did not match any known mechanism of fraud or folklore, and that the local priest — a licensed, Crown-vetted man who preached the sanctioned doctrine of the Silent Heavens — had resigned his post rather than continue preaching that no one was listening.

Then another shrine. Then five. Then a border town called Vael's Crossing, where an entire congregation had reportedly heard a voice answer a prayer spoken by a stranger, and where the stranger had fled before the Choir's local cell could reach him.

The Ashen Choir. That was the part that kept Vale awake. The Choir had always been a fringe nuisance — flagellant mystics preaching that the gods had been *eaten*, not destroyed, and that mortals should finish the meal. Harmless madness, she'd thought. But the Choir had gone quiet over the winter, and then they had gone *organized*, and organizations that buy horses and hire guides and stop preaching in public are not praying anymore. They are hunting.

And now a guildhouse full of mapmakers and mountain guides — the last of a guild so obscure most tax clerks had forgotten it existed — had been burned by professionals, and its surviving members were running west with something hot enough to scorch bone.

"Ma'am?" Cassel said.

"I'm thinking," Vale said. She opened the dispatch case and took out the folder she hated. Inside, copied in a clerk's careful hand, was the oldest document the Crown archives possessed on the subject: the founding charter of the Thornfell Wayfinders' Guild, four hundred years old, its final clause underlined twice by some archivist with a sense of drama.

*Until the gods wake, the Guild shall keep the ember, and the ember shall keep the way.*

For centuries the archivists had read that clause as allegory. Ember as metaphor for knowledge; waking as metaphor for learning. A nice sentiment for a guild of guides. Vale had read it the same way for twenty years.

Then the shrines had started answering, and she had reread it at two in the morning with a lamp turned up as high as it would go, and she had understood that allegory was a luxury the living afford the dead, and that the dead sometimes stop being dead.

"Two sets of tracks," she said. "Westward. Who else is moving west?"

"The Choir, ma'am. Our informants put a Choir column at Greywater two days ago, maybe forty strong, armed beyond anything they've fielded before. And—" Cassel's jaw tightened. "There's a second party. Unaffiliated. Buying supplies, asking after mountain routes. We think it's the fugitives."

"Fugitives don't ask after mountain routes," Vale said. "Wayfinders *are* mountain routes. Someone's traveling with them who knows the paths." She stood, joints protesting, and went to the map table at the center of the tent. The high country spread across it in contour lines like fingerprints: the Greywater gorge, the hermit roads, and beyond them the wall of the mountains themselves, and somewhere in that wall, marked on no modern chart but penciled faintly onto this one by an archivist working from a pre-war source, a ruin labeled in a dead language.

The Hollow Temple.

"Here's what I know," Vale said, more to herself than to Cassel. "A relic-class artifact has activated after three hundred years of dormancy. The moment it did, every power in the realm started moving — the Choir to devour it, whoever burned Thornfell to take it, and the fugitives to carry it god knows where. Which means everyone believes it matters. Everyone can't be wrong."

"And our orders?"

"Our orders are to recover it before anyone else does." She traced the route west with one finger. "You want to know why? Not the official why. The real one."

Cassel nodded slowly.

"When the Godswar ended, it wasn't because someone won," Vale said. "It was because everything that could burn had burned, and the gods that were left were too wounded to keep fighting, and mortals did something desperate. We don't have the full record. What we have suggests the survivors didn't just survive the war — they *ended* it. They put what remained of the old powers somewhere they couldn't reach each other, or us. And then they built a kingdom on top of the silence and raised children for three hundred years on the story that the silence was natural."

"You believe the old records."

"I believe in patterns, Lieutenant. Right now there is a pattern of shrines answering prayers across half the realm, all within months of each other, all growing stronger. That isn't revival. Revival spreads like fire — here first, then outward. This is synchronized. Something is waking *everywhere at once*, which means something is calling everything awake. And I think the thing calling is whatever sits at the heart of that guild's little heirloom." She tapped the map, on the faint pencil mark in the mountains. "And I think the people carrying it west aren't fleeing toward safety. They're fleeing toward *it*. Toward the temple. Toward the place where it was kept before it was hidden in plain sight among mapmakers."

"Then stopping them is—"

"Is the only chance the realm has," Vale said, "of not finding out what a second Godswar looks like. Because here is the arithmetic nobody wants to do. Last time, the gods fought each other and we were the battlefield. This time, if the old powers wake into a world that has learned to fear them — a world with the Choir screaming *eat them* and half the peasantry remembering how to pray — we won't be the battlefield. We'll be a faction. And factions get spent." She straightened. "I have seen one war end the world. I intend to be unreasonable about preventing the sequel."

Cassel was quiet for a moment. "And the fugitives, ma'am? The ones carrying it? If they're just couriers—"

"They're either couriers, fanatics, or believers," Vale said. "Couriers will surrender it. Fanatics will die holding it. Believers—" She thought of the old man dead at his own back door with scorched hands and a sword in his grip, and felt the familiar weight settle onto her shoulders, the weight she had carried through every hard order of her career. "Believers I'll deal with when I meet them."

* * *

They broke camp at dawn, or what passed for dawn under the rain, and moved west along the river road in a gray procession of horses and mud. Vale rode at the front because her knees preferred riding to walking and her conscience preferred the front to the rear, where commanders go to avoid seeing what their orders cost.

Near midday they passed a roadside shrine — one of the sanctioned kind, a stone niche with a bowl for offerings and a plaque bearing the doctrine of the Silent Heavens: *The heavens are silent because peace is silent.* A farmwife was there setting out bread, and when Vale reined in, the woman flinched and snatched the loaf back as though the inquisitor's shadow might confiscate it.

"Leave it," Vale said quietly. "The offering stays."

The woman stared at her. "Beg pardon, but — you're the King's question-woman, aren't you? You're the one they say takes the gods' leavings away."

"I take false relics away," Vale said. "Frauds. Charms sold by liars." She looked at the little shrine, at the bread, at the woman's cracked hands. "Has yours answered you?"

The farmwife's face did something complicated — fear and hope fighting for the same ground. "My boy's got the lung-rot," she said finally. "The physician's given him till spring. I've prayed at that stone every night since harvest. Two weeks ago, I prayed, and—" Her voice dropped to a whisper, guilty as confession. "Something said my name. Just my name. Soft, like. Like someone waking up next to you."

"What did you do?"

"I ran home and sat up all night with my hand on his chest." She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, angry at herself for crying. "He's breathing easier. Could be the medicine. Could be. But I know what I heard, madam, and I know I'm not the only one, and if you've come to tell me I imagined it—" She lifted her chin, and there it was, the thing Vale had been dreading for eight months, visible even in a muddy farmwife by a roadside: faith, raw and unlicensed, springing up through the cracks of the world like grass through a fallen wall.

"I haven't come to tell you that," Vale said. She nudged her horse on, and did not look back, because looking back would have meant seeing the woman pray.

That was the problem with her orders, she thought, riding on through the rain. The arithmetic was sound. The history was sound. She had read the burned chronicles from the Godswar's last decade; she knew what the old powers had done to the world when they warred, and she knew — better than the zealots, better than the peasants, better perhaps than the frightened young people running west with a stolen coal of divinity — exactly how much silence was worth.

But the arithmetic required her to stand between a dying child's mother and the first voice that had ever answered her, and call it salvation's enemy. The arithmetic required her to hunt two exhausted fugitives across the high country and take from them the thing they had watched friends die for. And the arithmetic rested on an assumption she could not verify and could not shake: that what woke would wake as it had been — vast, warring, indifferent to the small warm lives beneath it.

If she was wrong — if what slept in the ember was something gentler, or if the world had changed enough in three hundred years to hold what woke — then she was not the realm's shield. She was just another jailer dragging a prisoner back to a cell built by terrified men who mistook their fear for wisdom.

She thought of her predecessor, old Inquisitor Halloway, who had spent his last years muttering that the Inquisition's true duty was not to prevent the gods' return but to ensure that when it came, it came *to a realm ready to meet it* — and who had died in disgrace for saying so aloud. She had attended his funeral. Half the capital's clergy had refused to come.

"Ma'am." Sergeant Okonkwo rode up beside her, rain streaming off her helm. "Hounds are restless."

"They've been restless since Thornfell."

"Worse, ma'am. Circling. Whining at the wind." Okonkwo glanced west, where the mountains had begun to build themselves against the sky, blue-black and snow-crowned, wearing the storm like a crown. "Like they smell something on it."

Vale followed her gaze. Somewhere in that blue distance, two sets of footprints were climbing toward a door that hadn't opened in three hundred years, pursued by forty zealots who wanted to eat a god, and behind them all, gaining a mile a day, came the Crown's arithmetic in wet boots.

"Hold the pace," she said. "We make Greywater by tomorrow night."

"And after Greywater, ma'am?"

"After Greywater," Vale said, "we go where the road goes. All the way to the temple, if it comes to that." She settled her shoulders against the rain and the weight, both of which had grown heavier since morning. "Pray to your silent heavens, sergeant. If they've started answering, we may as well get in early."

Okonkwo laughed uncertainly, then saw that Vale wasn't joking, and rode back down the line.

That night, in her tent, Vale wrote her report to the capital by lamplight — *artifact confirmed active, fugitives westbound, Choir massing at Greywater, request authority to engage all parties* — and then, before sealing it, added a single line in her own hand, small enough that a hurried reader might miss it:

*If the archives are wrong about what sleeps, I would know before we dig it up.*

She sealed it, gave it to the courier, and lay down to sleep to the sound of the rain and the distant, directionless belling of the hounds.

In her dreams, a woman with a stranger's face stood in a burning doorway, holding something bright against her chest, and asked her — not angrily, not pleadingly, just wearily, the way one tired professional asks another:

*Whose side are you actually on?*

Vale woke before dawn with the answer she had carried for twenty-two years still lodged in her throat like a stone, and gave the order to ride.
