## 10. The Bridge at Greywater

The rain found us two miles before the river did.

It came down the valley sideways, a grey wall of it, the kind of rain that made even the mountains seem to flinch. I pulled my hood lower and watched Isandro walk ahead of me with his collar up and his eyes on the treeline, the way he'd been watching everything since the Choir's riders crossed our backtrail three days ago. He hadn't slept more than an hour at a stretch. Neither had I. The ember rode against my sternum in its lead-lined case, warm as a sleeping bird, and every time the rain hit my shoulders I imagined I could feel it listening.

Greywater wasn't supposed to be a problem. That was the whole point of Greywater.

"Tell me again," Isandro said, not turning around. His voice carried easily through the downpour — a preacher's voice, built for stone halls, wasted on weather. "About your friend."

"He's not my friend." I caught up to him, boots sucking in the mud of the old drover's road. "He's a client. Or he was. Dagny Holt ran freight for the guild six seasons running. Salt, tin, wool. Never once shorted us a coin."

"And now?"

"Now he runs whatever pays, same as everyone since the guild stopped being worth bribing." I said it flatly because saying it any other way would have hurt. "I sent word ahead from Thornfell that we'd need the crossing held. He answered. That's more loyalty than most people have left."

Isandro made a small sound that might have been agreement or might have been a prayer. With him it was hard to tell the difference. Three hundred years old, or so he claimed — three hundred years of remembering roads that no longer existed, names that had been scraped off maps like wax off a tablet — and still he prayed out of habit to gods who had never once answered him until one did, badly, in a shrine in Vael's Crossing, and ruined his life all over again.

The river announced itself before we saw it: a low roar under the rain, the sound of a lot of very cold water going somewhere fast. Then the trees broke open and there it was — the Greywater in full spate, brown-white and furious, carrying whole pine trunks like matchsticks. And spanning it, swaying gently in the wind, the bridge.

Rope and plank and nothing else. Two hundred feet of it, anchored into cut stone on either bank, sagging in the middle like a tired shoulder. On the far side, a waystation hut hunched under a lean roof, smokeless, dark.

My stomach dropped anyway. Not from the height. From the quiet.

"Dagny should have someone posted," I said. "A lantern. A sign. Anything."

"Perhaps your loyal client is drying off by a fire," Isandro offered, without conviction.

"Perhaps." But I was already reading the ground the way Sefu had taught me, the way only Wayfinders read it — the mud churned at the near anchorage, hoofprints, many, days old and hours old layered over each other. Cart ruts going north. No cart ruts coming back. And scratched into the anchor-stone, half washed away, the guild's own mark: a spiral within a star, the sign that meant *safe passage arranged*.

Someone had arranged safe passage. Just not for us.

I was still crouched over the stone when Isandro said, quietly, "Wendell. Look at the far bank."

I looked. At first there was nothing — rain, rock, the black mouth of the waystation. Then the nothing moved. Shapes detaching from the hut's shadow, four of them, five, spreading along the far anchorage with the unhurried confidence of men who had been paid to be somewhere and had arrived early. One of them raised something long and pale. A crowbar, maybe. Or a lever.

They were working at the anchor-stones.

"They're cutting the bridge," I said stupidly, as if Isandro needed it explained. As if anyone needed it explained.

"Then we have a choice," Isandro said. "We can go around. Three days north to the ford at Marrowstone, if the ford isn't flooded, which in this rain—"

"—it will be."

"—or we cross now, quickly, while the rope is still rope."

Behind us, faint but unmistakable through the rain: dogs. Not the Choir's white-eyed hounds — these were ordinary dogs, big ones, baying on a scent line. The Crown's trackers. Vale's people. Which meant Vale herself couldn't be far behind, and Vale, whatever else she was, was patient enough to let others do her killing.

In front of us: hired blades unmaking the only crossing.

Above us: rain, and the ember, warm against my heart, beating slightly faster than my own pulse. I hadn't known it could do that. I tried not to think about what it meant that I could tell.

"We cross," I said.

"I was afraid you'd say that." Isandro shrugged off his pack and slung it crosswise, tight to his back, the way mountain priests apparently learned before they learned anything else. "After you, Wayfinder."

"No. Together. Weight spread across the planks, never both feet on the same board, hands on the side ropes. If it starts to go—" I looked at him, really looked, water streaming off his jaw, his grey eyes steady on mine, and the instruction I'd been about to give died somewhere in my chest. "If it starts to go, you don't stop to be noble. You run."

"I have never once been noble," he said. "Ask anyone who exiled me."

I stepped onto the bridge.

* * *

There is a particular feeling to a rope bridge in flood — a living feeling, the whole structure breathing with the wind and the water's pull, every plank a small argument about whether it will hold. I've crossed a hundred of them. This one argued louder than most.

Halfway out, the wind came up the gorge in a single long shove, and the bridge swung sideways, and I heard Isandro behind me say something in a language older than the realm — not a prayer, I realized. A counting rhyme. Old road-priests used them to time their steps across bad spans. *One for the rope, two for the plank, three for the water that wants you back.*

"You know this bridge's name?" I called back, when the swing eased.

"It has three," he said. "The map-makers kept only one. The dwarves who built the anchors called it the Liar's Span, because it always looks worse than it is. The drovers called it the Widow's Welcome. And the first people — the ones whose names nobody writes anymore — they called it the Held Hand." A pause, planks creaking under him. "Because a bridge is just a hand that refuses to let go."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said about engineering."

And then the far end of the bridge dropped.

Not much. A foot, maybe less — the sound reached us a heartbeat after the lurch, iron shearing out of stone, and the whole span tilted and began to swing toward the downstream anchor like a pendulum gathering speed. Below us the Greywater reached up, hungry, throwing spray twenty feet high. My hands were on the ropes and my mind went white and clean the way it does at height, at fire, at the moments when thinking is a luxury, and I did the only thing a Wayfinder does: I kept moving. Fast, light, diagonal against the swing, using the pendulum instead of fighting it.

"Wendell!" Isandro shouted. "The anchor—"

"I see it!"

I didn't see it. What I saw, when I finally made myself look, was this: the downstream anchor-stone on the far bank had been split, deliberately, expertly, a wedge driven into the crack — and the main cable was sliding out of its channel by inches, spitting sparks where iron ground on stone. The men were gone from the bank. They'd done their work. They'd been paid to make sure we couldn't follow, and they hadn't cared whether we were on the bridge when it went.

The cable slid another inch. The bridge groaned like a living thing.

And Isandro passed me.

He didn't run past me so much as flow past, one hand on the rope, coat snapping wet behind him, and I screamed at him — I actually screamed, words I don't remember choosing — and he didn't stop. He reached the anchorage with the cable shrieking out of its seat, got both arms around the great rope where it fed into the stone, and set himself against the slide with his heels braced on the wedge-crack and his whole body bent backward into an arc of pure strain.

The cable stopped.

The bridge steadied, quivering, and I understood in one cold flash what he'd bought: minutes. Only minutes. A man cannot hold a bridge. A man can hold a bridge long enough.

"Cross," he said. His voice came out strangled, pressed flat by the weight. "Now. While it's—"

I crossed. There was no other thing to do, though every step away from him felt like a step off a cliff. I crossed the way I'd been trained, fast and light, and the span bucked and swung and the river roared its disappointment below me, and then my boots hit stone, real stone, anchored stone, and I spun around.

Isandro was on his knees, still wrapped in the cable, dragging it back into its channel by fractions. Blood ran thin and pink down his wrists where the rope had flayed them. His face was the color of the water.

"The wedge," he gasped. "Pull the wedge. Pull it and the cable seats — hurry, Wendell, I can't—"

I got my fingers into the crack. The wedge was iron, rain-slick, seated deep, and it would not move, and behind me the dogs were closer, and somewhere upstream I could hear horses — Vale's outriders, closing on the near bank, on the bridge I was about to strand them from, and I thought with a strange distant clarity: *good*. Let them swim. Let the whole Crown learn what it costs to hunt people across their own country.

The wedge gave with a crack like a knuckle breaking. The cable snapped into its channel, taut and singing, and the bridge went still, and Isandro folded forward onto the wet stone like a puppet with its strings cut.

I was on my knees beside him before I decided to move. His wrists were ribbons. His shoulders would carry the bruise of it for a month. When I turned him over his eyes were open, unfocused, finding me slowly, and he smiled — actually smiled, bloody and exhausted and absurd.

"Told you," he rasped. "Never noble. Just stubborn."

"You idiot," I said. "You absolute — you could have died. You almost—"

"Yes," he said simply. "But you'd have crossed alone otherwise."

"That's not a reason."

"It seemed like one," he said, "at the time."

* * *

The waystation had a hearth, dry wood, and a door I wedged shut with an iron poker. The dogs reached the near bank as the last light failed, bayed their frustration at the river, and fell silent. Somewhere out in the rain, Vale's soldiers would be looking at a severed crossing and doing arithmetic. It would take them a day to find another way over. Maybe two. We'd bought ourselves a night, and I intended to spend it keeping Isandro alive.

His wrists cleaned up better than they looked. I bound them with linen and the last of our spirit, and he sat patiently while I worked, watching my hands with an expression I couldn't read.

"You didn't have to do that," I said, for the fourth time.

"You keep saying that like it's a fact," he said. "It's a feeling. Feelings aren't arguments."

"Don't preach at me. I'm holding the needle."

He laughed — a low, cracked sound, half pain — and then winced, and then was quiet, and the rain filled the silence against the roof, and the fire popped, and I tied off the last bandage and discovered that I had not moved my hands away. They were resting on his. His pulse beat under my fingertips, quick and alive, and I could feel the ember answering it through my ribs, two rhythms leaning toward each other like candles in a draft.

I should have pulled back. I want to be honest about that; I should have pulled back, because I knew what it was, what it had been building toward since Vael's Crossing, since the high road, since every morning he walked ahead of me and I watched the rain run off his shoulders and told myself it was professional interest. He was three hundred years old and a liar about half of it. I was a fugitive carrying a god's heart in a box, hunted by church and crown and zealot alike. Nothing about us was safe. Nothing about us was sensible.

"Wendell," he said quietly. Not moving either.

"If you say 'feelings aren't arguments' again, I'm going to throw you in the river."

"I wouldn't dream of it." He turned his hand over beneath mine, slow, giving me every chance to take mine back. I didn't. His palm was warm despite the cold, scarred across the lifeline in ways I suddenly wanted to ask about and knew I shouldn't, not yet, maybe not ever. "I nearly died today," he said. "That clarifies things. It's one of death's few courtesies."

"Does it?" My voice came out strange. "Clarify things?"

"For me it does." He looked at me then, fully, the firelight moving in his grey eyes, and there was nothing priestly in his face at all — no serenity, no distance. Just a man, soaked and hurt and terrified, exactly as terrified as I was. "Three hundred years, and I remember almost nothing of them. Someone took my life and burned it for fuel and left me the ash. I wake every day not knowing who I was. Do you understand what that means? Everything I am is a guess. Except—" his thumb moved, barely, across my knuckles, "—except this. This I didn't remember. This I chose. Every mile, every day, I chose it, and today the river nearly took it, and I found I minded. I found I minded a great deal."

The rain hammered the roof. The fire settled. Somewhere in its case against my chest, the ember pulsed once, slow, like a held breath.

"Isandro," I said.

"Yes?"

"Shut up."

And I kissed him.

It was clumsy. We were both half-drowned and shaking and his lip was split and my nose was cold and none of it mattered even slightly. He made a small sound against my mouth, surprise or relief or both, and his good hand came up to cradle the back of my head like I was something breakable, which at that moment I absolutely was, and the rain came down and the river roared and the world outside the waystation was entirely made of enemies, and inside it there was just this: warmth, and salt, and the astonishing specific reality of him.

When we broke apart, foreheads touching, breath mingling, he said, "For the record, that was not professional."

"No," I said. "It really wasn't."

"I regret nothing."

"You will. I snore. I hoard rations. I once pushed a duke's son into a bog for calling the guild obsolete."

"I look forward to all of it," he said, and kissed me again, softer, and I let him, and for one whole hour the mission did not exist.

Then the ember woke.

Not fully — not the burning, screaming wakefulness of the cave shrine. But heat bloomed through the lead lining, sudden and urgent, and light leaked from the seams of the case in thin gold lines, and Isandro and I came apart like guilty children as the glow climbed the walls of the hut and threw our shadows huge against the beams.

"What is it?" Isandro breathed. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong." I fumbled the case open, and gold light poured out, and the ember hung above my palm without touching it, brighter than I had ever seen it, pulsing — and with each pulse I felt a pull, faint but insistent, pointing. North. Up. Toward the pass. Toward the Hollow Temple.

Toward home, said something in my blood that was not entirely my own. *Hurry. Hurry. Before they finish what they started.*

Outside, far off in the rain, a bell began to ring in the valley below — the chapel bell at Greywater village, tolling fast and arrhythmic, the alarm pattern. And under it, thinner, carried on the wind, a sound I had hoped never to hear again: singing. Many voices, harmonized, joyless, rising.

The Ashen Choir had found the river too.

Isandro met my eyes across the golden light between us. His wrists were bandaged, his hands would barely close, and he was already reaching for his pack.

"How long to the temple?" he asked.

"Four days," I said. "If the Pass of Teeth lets us live."

"Then we'd best not argue with it," he said, nodding at the ember, at the light, at the singing drawing nearer through the storm. "It seems to know something we don't."

The ember pulsed again — and this time, clear as a bell through my bones, I heard it. Not words. A name. My name, spoken in a voice like a hearth at winter's end, patient and afraid:

*Wendell. Come home.*
