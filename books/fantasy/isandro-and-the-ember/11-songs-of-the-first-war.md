## 11. Songs of the First War

The hermit's hut sat where no road could reach it — which was, of course, why we found it.

We had been three days in the high country above Greywater, following a track that existed only because Isandro remembered it existing, and the memory had begun to frighten him more than the cold did. The path climbed through pine and then through nothing at all, just wind-scoured stone and the small stubborn flowers that bloomed in cracks, and on the third evening, when Wendell's hands had gone grey with cold and their jokes had stopped being jokes, he smelled woodsmoke.

"There," he said, pointing to a fold of the mountain where smoke rose thin and blue against the dusk. "Someone lives there."

"Nobody lives up here," Wendell said. "That's the whole point of up here."

"Then someone is hiding." He knew something about that.

The hut was smaller than a chapel, built of driftwood and stone and what looked like the ribs of a boat, though they were forty leagues from any water deep enough to float one. A man stood in the doorway before they knocked — old, old in the way of mountains rather than men, with a face like weathered bark and eyes the color of a frozen lake. He was singing when they saw him. He did not stop singing until they were inside.

He called himself Orison. He would not say whether that was a name or a title or a joke, and after a while Isandro stopped asking.

* * *

They ate barley stew and hard bread and a cheese so sharp it made Wendell's eyes water, and Orison watched them eat with the particular attention of a man cataloguing them for later use. It was Wendell who finally broke the silence, because Wendell could not bear silences the way other people could not bear heights.

"You knew we were coming."

"I heard you coming," Orison said. "Three days ago. Two people walking an old road sing differently than anyone else. They walk like they're apologizing to it." He refilled their bowls without being asked. "You're Wayfinder-blooded, girl. I can smell the guild on you. And you—" he turned to Isandro, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost terrible, "—you are wearing a borrowed life. It doesn't fit you. It never has."

Isandro set down his spoon. Three hundred years he had carried this body, this face, these fragmented memories of a life in Vael's Crossing that ended the day his order exiled him — except that wasn't right either, was it? The exile was real. The memories around it were gauze. He remembered praying at a shrine and remembering nothing else about that day, as if someone had taken a knife to the hours. He remembered loving his god with a whole heart and could not remember the god's name.

"How do you know that?" he asked quietly.

"Because I sang your funeral," Orison said. "Or one of them. You've died more times than you'd believe, priest. Men who carry gods tend to burn through bodies."

Wendell went very still beside him. "What are you?"

"A singer." Orison smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "The last one who remembers the songs properly. Everyone else sings the version the victors wrote."

* * *

He made them wait until full dark. He banked the fire low, so that the light in the hut came mostly from the coals, red and breathing, and he told them it was deliberate — that some stories should only be told by firelight, and some truths should only be heard when you cannot see the face of the person telling them.

Then he took out a harp. It was ancient, its frame dark with handling, strings replaced so many times that none of the original instrument remained except the shape of it — and Isandro thought, absurdly, of himself. Of the body he wore. Of the life he remembered.

Orison began to sing.

It was not a song Isandro knew, and yet his body knew it. His throat tightened on the second verse. By the fourth, tears were running down his face and he could not have said why, except that the melody moved through him like a hand moving through water, finding every sunken thing.

"The Godswar," Orison sang, "was not a war of gods."

The words fell into the room like stones into a well.

"It was a war *against* them. Mortal hands. Mortal cunning. Nine thrones of heaven, and nine chains forged in the furnaces of the lowlands, and nine prisons dug beneath the bones of the world. The songs they teach you in the temples tell you the gods burned each other to ash, that the sky emptied itself in fire, that what divinity survived scattered like sparks and died. That story is a lock. Every song they sing is a lock. And I am the last key that still turns."

Wendell's hand found Isandro's arm and gripped it hard enough to bruise.

"You're lying," Wendell said, but their voice shook. "The guild charter says the gods woke and warred and the world nearly ended. Sefu taught me the histories myself. The Ember War. The Fall of the Nine."

"The guild teaches exactly what it was founded to teach." Orison set down the harp and leaned forward, and the coal-light carved his face into ridges and hollows. "Sit down, child, and let me tell you how jails are built. First you write the crime. Then you erase the jailer's name from the sentence, so the prisoner forgets there was ever a trial. Then you found an order — humble, pious, poor — whose sacred duty is to guard a relic. And the relic guards itself, because a relic is only ever a prisoner with good manners."

The ember. Wendell had carried it against their chest for weeks now, wrapped in wool and oilcloth, and it had been warm the entire time. Warm like a sleeping animal. Warm like a held breath.

"No," Wendell whispered. "No, it's a heart. It's a sleeping heart, it answered us, it—"

"Hearts don't get locked in vaults, girl." Orison's voice was not cruel. That was the worst of it. It was patient, the way winter is patient. "Prisoners do. Your guild didn't inherit a treasure. It inherited a cell, and the keys, and the lie that made the cell comfortable to keep."

* * *

Isandro did not remember leaving his stool. He was standing at the window, looking out at the black mass of the mountain, and his hands were shaking.

Because he understood now. He understood the shape of the hole in his own history. He had been a priest of one of the Nine — he felt it in his marrow, in the way certain prayers still rose in him unbidden, in the way the ember had *answered* him in that shrine in Vael's Crossing, the first voice that had spoken back in three hundred years. His god had not abandoned him. His god had been chained, and Isandro had been chained alongside it, or worse — he had been part of the chaining. Part of the order that kept the locks. And when the truth grew too heavy to bury, they had cut it out of him and thrown him away with the knife still in the wound.

"They imprisoned their own gods," he said. His voice sounded strange to him, scraped raw. "Mortals. They looked at the beings that made them and decided the safest world was an empty one."

"Mortals looked at gods the way farmers look at wolves," Orison said. "Beautiful. Necessary, maybe, once. And absolutely intolerable to live beside." He stirred the coals. "Understand me: I am not telling you the jailers were monsters. Some of them were the best people who ever lived. They had watched a god level a city over an insult. They had watched another demand firstborn children. Fear makes wardens of the gentlest souls. But fear does not make a thing *right*, and it does not make the lie *true*, and the lie has been eating this world from the inside for a thousand years. You can feel it. The prayers that go nowhere. The shrines that answer silence. The priests who grow old whispering into wells."

Isandro thought of his own exile. Of the years he had spent praying into that silence, believing himself forsaken, unworthy, broken — when all along the silence had been manufactured. Engineered. His god had not refused to hear him. His god had been *muzzled*, and Isandro had spent three centuries blaming himself for a chain someone else had forged.

Something in his chest cracked along a line he hadn't known was there.

"And the Choir?" Wendell asked. "The Ashen Choir. Serah. If the gods are already imprisoned, what are they devouring?"

"Fragments. Escapes. Whatever leaks through the seals." Orison's eyes flicked to Isandro, then away. "And soon, if they catch you — the biggest fragment left. Your prisoner, child. Your heirloom. Mother Serah believes she is finishing the war her ancestors started. She calls it mercy. A world with no masters." He shrugged, an old man's shrug, carrying the weight of centuries. "She may even believe it sincerely. Fanatics usually do. But understand what she actually is. She is not a liberator. She is an executioner arriving late to an execution, furious that the condemned is still breathing."

* * *

Later, when Orison slept — or pretended to — Wendell sat by the dead fire turning the ember's wrapping over in their hands, again and again, like a person checking a wound.

"We have to take it to the temple," they said finally. "Sefu's instruction. Take it to the Hollow Temple by solstice."

"Do you still believe that's rescue?"

Wendell was quiet a long moment. The ember glowed faintly through the cloth, a pulse of amber in the dark, steady as a heartbeat. Steady as a heartbeat in a chest.

"I don't know anymore," they admitted. "I thought we were carrying something home. Now I think we might be dragging a prisoner deeper into the prison. And I'm the jailer's daughter, apparently, doing my father's rounds." Their jaw tightened. "Except Sefu wasn't my father, was he? Nothing is what it was. The guild lied. The temples lied. The songs lied. Maybe the only honest thing in this whole story is the prisoner."

Isandro crossed the room and sat beside them. Close enough that the ember's warmth reached him through the wool, and he realized — with a jolt that was half terror, half recognition — that the warmth felt familiar. That somewhere under the gauze of his rewritten life, he had felt this heat before. Held it. Guarded it. Perhaps loved it.

Perhaps betrayed it.

"Wendell." He said their name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "Whatever it is — relic, prisoner, heart — it spoke to me. In Vael's Crossing, before I ever met you. It gave me your name. It sent me to you." He met their eyes. "A prisoner doesn't recruit its own escort. Unless it knows something we don't. Unless it chose its jailers' child deliberately — the one person alive who might open the door."

"Or unless it's clever," Wendell said softly. "Prisoners learn to be clever, Orison said. A thousand years in the dark, learning exactly what words will make the next guard unlock the door." They laughed, and there was no humor in it. "How do I know anything it wants is true? How do I know *any* of it is true? What if we're not rescuing a god? What if we're releasing the thing that burned the world once already — the thing the whole war was *about*?"

"Orison says the war was never about that."

"Orison also says he's the last key that turns. Everyone with a secret thinks their secret is the load-bearing one." Wendell pressed the wrapped ember between their palms, and the glow brightened, dimmed, brightened — answering, always answering, patient as tide. "I keep thinking about Sefu. He died for this. He burned for this. Was he dying to free something, or dying to keep it caged? Did he even know? Did *any* of them know, or did the jailers' children inherit the lie so deep they mistook it for love?"

Isandro had no answer. He wished, suddenly and completely, that he had one — that three hundred years of priesthood had left him anything sturdier than doubt dressed in ceremony.

But he knew one thing. He took Wendell's hands, ember and all, and held them.

"Then we go and ask it," he said. "Not the guild's question. Not the Choir's question. Ours. We stand in front of it at that temple and we ask what it wants, and this time — this time — somebody listens before deciding. Freeing it blind is just another kind of carelessness. Keeping it caged blind is just another kind of cruelty. So we ask. Whatever it answers, we'll know we're the first ones in a thousand years to treat it as something other than a problem to be solved."

Wendell looked at him for a long time. Outside, the wind worried the eaves; inside, the ember pulsed between their joined hands like a second heart, keeping its own counsel.

"When did you become the hopeful one?" they asked.

"About the time I learned my despair was installed," Isandro said. "It's easier to hope when you know the alternative was manufactured."

* * *

They left at dawn. Orison walked them to the edge of his clearing, and there he stopped, and there he sang one verse more — not the locked song, but a different one, older, in a language Isandro's bones recognized and his mind did not. A road-song. A door-song.

"What was that?" Wendell asked.

"A warning," Orison said. "For the temple doors. They will know your blood, child, and they will know his voice, and neither of those things means they will open kindly. Prisons grow strange, over a thousand years. Even the doors start dreaming." He looked past them, up the mountain, toward the white fangs of the Pass of Teeth, and his weathered face did something complicated. "And you should hurry. Last night I heard bells in the valley below. Not Crown bells. Choir bells. Serah has stopped searching the roads."

"She's found another way up," Isandro said.

"She's found the *old* way up," Orison corrected. "The one your maps forgot and mine didn't. She'll be in the pass within two days. And priest—" he caught Isandro's sleeve, and his grip was iron despite the age of it, "—when you stand before whatever waits in that temple, remember what I told you about funerals. You have died before. More than once. Ask yourself, on that threshold, who buried you — and whether the ones holding the shovels will let you live long enough to finish what you're starting."

He released Isandro's arm, picked up his harp, and turned back toward his hut, singing as he went — the locked song again, the victors' version, sweet and false and comforting, the song every temple in the world was singing at that very moment to keep nine sleepers sleeping.

Isandro stood in the cold morning light and found that he could no longer hear it as anything but a lullaby sung to a hostage.

Two days to the pass. The Choir behind them, the truth ahead of them, and between those two jaws, a wrapped ember beating patiently against Wendell's chest — waiting, Isandro thought, the way prisoners wait. Not hopefully.

Precisely.
