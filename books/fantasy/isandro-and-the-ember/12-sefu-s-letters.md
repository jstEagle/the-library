## 12. Sefu's Letters

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the old border stones that had guarded nothing for so long it had forgotten its purpose and simply become geology. Isandro built the fire. Wendell built the walls — of habit, of silence, of every trick the guild had ever taught them about making a small space safe in hostile country.

The ember rode against their chest in its wrappings of wool and oilcloth, warm as a sleeping animal. Since the hermit's song, Wendell had not been able to stop feeling it. Before, it had been an heirloom, a duty, a weight with a name. Now it was a heartbeat that was not theirs, keeping time against theirs, patient as a debt.

They ate hard bread and dried plums and said very little. Isandro kept glancing at them across the fire — the particular glance of a man who has learned something terrible about someone he loves and is waiting for them to learn it too.

"Say it," Wendell said finally.

Isandro turned his hands over the flames. "I don't know what you mean."

"You've been looking at me like I'm a wound all day. Say what you're not saying."

He was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. "The singer's song. The jailers' song. The guild was founded to keep the gods caged." He looked up. "Wendell. You've carried that thing since you were nine years old. They put it on you when you were a child."

"They gave me an honor."

"They gave you a chain and told you it was a medal." His voice was gentle, which made it worse. "I'm not saying they didn't love you. I'm saying love and lies can live in the same house for a long time before anyone notices the smell."

Wendell wanted to argue. They opened their mouth to do it, and found nothing there — no defense assembled, no ready loyalty to hand. Just a hollow place where the argument should have been, and in the hollow, a question they had been refusing to look at since Greywater: *why me? Why did Sefu choose me, of all the guild's children, to carry it?*

"I'm going to check the perimeter," they said, and stood up, and walked into the dark because walking away from fires was easier than standing in their light.

* * *

It was Sefu's satchel that started it, two nights later.

They had taken it from the guildhouse ruins — or rather, Wendell had gone back for it, three days after the burning, while Isandro watched the road and called it madness and then came anyway, because of course he came. The guildhouse had been a black ribcage against the sky, still smoking at the seams. Wendell had walked through the ashes of the only home they'd ever known and taken what could be carried: the guild's second map case, a tin of tea Sefu had hoarded like dragon-gold, and the master's own satchel, scorched at one corner but whole.

Sefu Oyelaran — no relation, though everyone had always assumed there must be, the shared name a small joke of the guild — had been Guildmaster for thirty-one years. He had taught Wendell to read contour lines before they could read letters. He had smelled of pipe smoke and pine resin and ink. He had died in the doorway of the map room with an inquisitor's blade in him, buying ninety seconds with his life, and his last act had been to press the ember into Wendell's hands and say, *Hollow Temple. Solstice. Don't let them bury it again.*

*Don't let them bury it again.* Wendell had taken those words for grief. A dying man's urgency. It was only now, on the ninth night out from the ruins, camped under an overhang with rain coming down in ropes, that they finally opened the satchel properly — because there was nothing else to do, because sleep wouldn't come, because Isandro was asleep by the fire with his prayer beads loose in his open hand and Wendell could not bear to lie awake next to him counting questions.

The satchel held the ordinary archaeology of a guildmaster's life. A spare stylus. A whetstone worn to a smile. Ration chits. A folding cup. And beneath the false bottom — because of course Sefu had a false bottom; Sefu had false bottoms in his boots and his pipe case and possibly his personality — a bundle of letters, tied with guild-cord in the old way, the knot that meant *for your eyes only, and burn after.*

Wendell's hands went cold before they even untied it.

There were eleven letters. The earliest was dated thirty years ago. The latest was dated four months before the burning. All of them were in Sefu's hand except the first, which was in a hand Wendell did not know — cramped, urgent, written fast, the ink blotched where the writer's haste had outrun the nib.

Wendell read the first letter by the light of the banked fire, holding it close, and the world began quietly to come apart.

*Brother Sefu,* it began. *You asked me to write it down so there would be a record outside the vault, and I have cursed you for asking, because now it exists on paper and paper can be found. But here it is.*

*We found the child at the Hollow Temple on the third of Thawmonth, in the inner sanctum, in the cradle of roots before the sealed door. You know this. What you do not know — what only I and Mother Cassia knew, and she is dead — is that the door was open when we came. Open, and the chamber beyond lit from within, and the child lying on the threshold as if set there. As if left for us.*

*The child bore the mark. You have seen it yourself now, on the inside of the left wrist: the closed eye. We told ourselves it was coincidence, a birthmark, a trick of the light. We are liars, brother. We are good people and we are liars, and I am tired.*

*Cassia performed the rite before we ever brought the child down the mountain. You know the one — the same rite the founders used on themselves, the forgetting, the mercy-cut. She said a child who grew up knowing what it was would either be worshipped or hunted, and both would end the same way. She said the ember chose to be near the child, and that the safest place for a god's heart is beside the one person no hunter would think to search: a guild orphan with no history and no name.*

*So we gave the child no history. We cut the mountain out of them and gave them a village that never existed and parents who never lived, and we wrote the false record into the rolls, and we swore each other to silence until the gods woke or the guild ended.*

*The child is healthy. The child laughs. Yesterday the child drew a map of the temple corridors in the dirt without ever having seen them, and when I asked where they learned it, they said, "I didn't learn it, I just know it," and I had to walk out into the yard and be sick behind the woodpile.*

*I am telling you this because you will be Guildmaster someday, and you deserve to know what you will inherit: not a relic. A family. And a lie at the center of it, wearing our faces.*

*Burn this.*

Wendell read it three times. The letters swam and reassembled. Somewhere below them, in the dark, the rain kept falling on stone with a sound like slow applause, like something being patiently, terribly acknowledged.

*The child.* Not named. Deliberately not named, as if the writer could not bear to make it real with ink.

But Wendell knew. Knew the way you know your own hands. The date was the third of Thawmonth, thirty years past. Wendell's birthday — the one the guild rolls gave them, the one Sefu had always celebrated with honey-cakes and a slightly strange expression, as if the day meant more than cake.

And the drawing. *A map of the temple corridors in the dirt.* Wendell stared into the fire and saw it: themselves, small, kneeling in the training yard, scratching lines into the earth while the apprentices crowded around, and Sefu standing at the edge with his pipe unlit, watching with an expression Wendell had spent thirty years misreading as pride.

It hadn't been pride. It had been horror. It had been a man watching a locked room recognize its own key.

* * *

Wendell read the rest of the letters through the night, and the night was long, and by the end of it they understood that they had never been a person at all — only a project, a hiding place, a living vault built out of a stolen childhood.

The letters traced the whole architecture of the lie. Sefu's predecessor writing of the rite. Sefu himself, newly master, writing to a colleague in the southern guilds — *the last of the southern guilds, brother, the rest are gone or worse than gone* — confessing that the child was fourteen and asking questions, and that they had considered a second rite, and that Cassia's ghost had apparently shamed him out of it: *"Better a curious child than a hollow one. We have already taken enough."*

Another letter, later: *The ember has begun to warm when the child is near. I have moved it to the deep vault and the warming stopped. I do not know what that means and I do not want to know.* And then, years on, the surrender of it: *I have given the child the ember. I tell myself it is because they are the finest Wayfinder the guild has produced in a century, and this is true. I also know it is because the heart wants to be near its body, and I am tired of standing between them. If I am wrong, may the mountain forgive me. May the child forgive me. I have never once asked myself which of those forgivings matters more, and I notice tonight that I still haven't.*

The last letter was four months old. Short. Almost calm, in the way of men who have stopped fighting a flood and started measuring it.

*The ember glows now. Not warmth — light. The vault seals are failing; the iron sings at night. Vale's people have been asking questions in the low towns, the right questions, the ones no one should know to ask, which means someone told them, which means the silence is broken from the inside. I believe we have less than a year.*

*I have done one thing more. I have written to the child — no, I have not written, I have told them nothing, forgive me, I am a coward in the specific shape of a kind man. Instead I have done what I can: I have begun teaching them the old roads. The true maps. The ones to the Hollow Temple. I dress it as advanced training. They suspect nothing. They trust me completely, and every day that they trust me is a day I do not deserve, and every day they grow stronger on those roads is a day I buy them.*

*If you find this letter, then I am dead, and the child is running, and I want whoever reads this to understand one thing plainly, because history will call us monsters and it will be half right:*

*We did not steal a child. We saved one, the only way saving was possible. And we loved them. Every one of us, all these years, we loved them — clumsily, guiltily, behind a wall of lies, but we loved them. I have watched them sleep in the infirmary after fever. I have watched them stand on the ridge at dawn with the wind in their coat and thought: there is the most honest creature I have ever known, and I built my life on deceiving them, and I would do it again, because the alternative was their death, and love does not get to be clean when the world is like this.*

*Burn all of these. Or don't. Perhaps someone should know what we were.*

*S.O.*

Wendell sat with the letters in their lap until the fire burned down to red eyes in the ash.

* * *

Isandro woke to a sound he took a moment to identify, because it was so rare it had no ready name: Wendell crying. Not loudly. Worse than loudly — the small, controlled sounds of someone who had learned young that grief was a liability on the road, sobbing the way other people breathed, automatically, trying to keep it quiet and failing.

He crossed the camp without a word and sat down beside them. The letters lay scattered around Wendell like shed feathers. He picked up the nearest one, read a few lines, and set it down very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Wendell—"

"Don't." Their voice was raw gravel. "Don't say anything wise. Don't say *they loved you*, because it's right there in the letters, he wrote it, I've read it six times, and I can't—" Their breath hitched. "I can't make it help. Do you understand? He *loved* me. He says it right there. And he stood in the yard every morning of my childhood knowing what I was, and he let me grieve parents who never existed, and he gave me birthday cakes for a birthday that was just the day they found me on a doorstep like a — like a *delivery* —"

"He was protecting you."

"I KNOW WHAT HE WAS DOING." The shout cracked off the overhang and went rolling down the mountainside, and somewhere below, startled birds burst up into the pre-dawn grey. Wendell pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. When they spoke again it was barely above a whisper. "That's what's unbearable. Not that they lied. That the lie was *kind*. That I can't even hate them cleanly. I've spent two days trying to hate them and I can't, because he died with a sword in him buying me ninety seconds, and you don't get to hate a man for that, you just don't, and so I'm stuck, Isandro. I'm stuck with loving people who erased me, and there's no door out of that room."

Isandro reached out slowly and took one of their hands — the left, turning it over, finding the mark on the inside of the wrist. The closed eye. He had seen it a hundred times and thought it a guild tattoo. He had kissed that wrist on the bridge at Greywater, in the rain, with the god's own mark under his mouth, and neither of them had known.

"The hermit's song," he said quietly. "Nine sleepers. The guild as jailers. I've been turning it over since we left his hut, and there's a piece that never fit." He traced the mark with one finger, feather-light. "Jailers lock things away from the world. But your guild didn't hide the ember from the world, Wendell. They hid it *with you*. Beside you. On your body, for twenty years." He looked up. "You weren't the vault-keeper. You were the vault. And everything they did — the rite, the lies, the false childhood — everything was built to keep that vault sealed. To keep you from ever opening."

"From ever waking it up."

"From ever waking *up*. Maybe both. Maybe there's no difference." His thumb moved over the closed eye, back and forth, a gesture that was half tenderness and half benediction and entirely unconscious. "Cassia cut the memory out of a child to save its life. Sefu spent thirty years paying that debt in secret, teaching you the roads home one lesson at a time. Neither of them gets to be forgiven by me — it isn't mine to give. But Wendell. Listen to me. Whatever they made of you, whatever they cut away — they could not cut out the map in the dirt. You drew the temple before you could write. Who you are got here *despite* them. That part is yours. No one gave it to you and no one can take it back."

Wendell looked down at their wrist, at his finger resting on the mark, and something in their face shifted — not comfort, nothing so easy, but the grim settling of a climber who has found the next hold.

"The letters say the door was open," they said. "When they found me. The sanctum was lit from within, and I was lying on the threshold. Like I was *left*." They laughed, a terrible small sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Thirty years I thought I was carrying the god to safety. Up the mountain, to its temple, like a good apprentice finishing the job. And the whole time—" They stopped. Started again. "The whole time I wasn't taking the heart home. I was the heart, going home. The ember wasn't the cargo. It was the *escort*. It's been escorting me."

The wrapped bundle against Wendell's chest seemed, in that moment, to warm — a degree, no more, the way a sleeping man warms when spoken of kindly. Or perhaps it was only the firelight. Neither of them said anything about it. Neither of them needed to.

Isandro gathered the letters, squared their edges, and held them out. "What do you want to do with them? He asked for them burned."

Wendell took them. Looked at them a long time. Thirty years of a good man's guilt, folded into paper and cord.

"No," they said. "No more burning. No more cutting things out and calling it mercy. Someone rewrote my life once already — I'm not letting anyone edit it again, not even Sefu, not even out of shame." They tucked the letters into the inner pocket of their coat, against the ember, against the closed eye. "These come up the mountain with us. If there's a reckoning at that temple, it happens with the whole truth present. Every page."

Isandro nodded slowly. Then, because he was who he was, and because the sky was going grey at the edges and the Choir was somewhere behind them and the pass somewhere ahead: "Then we walk. Three days to the Pass of Teeth, if the weather holds."

"It won't hold," Wendell said, standing, brushing off their knees, and for one moment — one flicker — the old guild voice was in their mouth, dry and competent, reading the clouds the way Sefu had taught them. Then it faltered. They looked east, toward the pass, toward the temple they had drawn in the dirt before they could write, and their hand drifted unconsciously to their wrist.

"And Isandro." They didn't turn around. "If the door opens the way the letter says it will — if it knows me —"

"Then I'll be standing next to you when it does," Isandro said. "Whatever comes through it."

Wendell nodded once, sharply, the way people nod when accepting a kindness they don't feel they've earned, and shouldered their pack, and started up the trail.

Behind them, in the cold pocket of the abandoned camp, the last of the fire went out — and in the sudden grey dimness, the wrapped ember glowed once, softly, briefly, like an eye opening and closing again.

Like something practicing.
