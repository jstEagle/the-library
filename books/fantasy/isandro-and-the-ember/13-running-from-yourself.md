## 13. Running from Yourself

The letters were still in my hands when I stood up.

I don't remember standing. I remember the firelight guttering in the hollow of the cave shrine, and Sefu's handwriting swimming in front of me — *the child is the god's own; the guild will raise it as one of us; no child should carry what we must hide* — and then I was on my feet with my pack half-buckled and the ember sitting on the altar stone where it had rested all night, glowing its slow, patient red, like a heart that had never once been in a hurry.

"Wendell?" Isandro's voice came from the mouth of the cave, where he'd gone to check the weather. He'd taken to sleeping near the entrance since Greywater, some old soldier's habit I hadn't known about him until that week. "What's wrong?"

Nothing. Everything. The word *wrong* didn't cover it. Wrong was a broken axle, a flooded ford, a client who lied about his cargo. This was different. This was the floor of my life giving way and showing me that there had never been a floor at all.

I picked up the ember.

That was the last thing I did deliberately for a long time. After that I was just moving — out of the shrine, down the goat track behind it, into the black pines with their smell of cold resin and rot, following a slope my feet knew and my mind refused to acknowledge. The ember rode against my chest in its iron case, warm as a sleeping bird, and every step I took away from the temple felt like a step toward something I couldn't name and couldn't bear.

Behind me, faintly, I heard Isandro call my name again. Then again. Then nothing, because I stopped listening, the way you stop listening to your own heartbeat when you're trying very hard not to have one.

* * *

I walked most of the night.

I know this because the moon crossed the whole sky, from the shoulder of the eastern ridge to the notch above the western peaks, and I watched it happen without once deciding to watch anything. My body walked. My body had been trained by eleven years of Wayfaring to walk through anything — scree, snowmelt, night, grief — and it did its work now with a horrible competence while the rest of me went somewhere small and dark and curled up tight.

*They erased my childhood.* That was the sentence my mind kept returning to, turning it over like a stone looking for what lived underneath. Not *Sefu lied to me.* Not even *the guild stole six years of my life.* Just: they erased it. They reached into the place where Wendell Oyelaran began and they cut, and cauterized, and told me the scar was my birthmark. And they did it gently. That was the worst part. Sefu's letters were full of tenderness — *he sleeps so peacefully after the working; he laughs at breakfast as though nothing happened* — tenderness wrapped around an act I could not forgive, performed by people I had loved more than I loved myself.

Because I had loved them. That was the thing the letters killed. Master Sefu teaching me star-shapes over the forge. Old Maren letting me hold the ember on solstice night when I was nine, her hands around mine, saying *someday this will be yours, little wren.* The guildhall smelling of tallow and pine pitch. All of it real, all of it mine, and underneath it all a second childhood running like a buried river — a woman with my face, a burning temple, a god's blood in a foundling's veins — that they had dammed up and paved over and called a life.

And the ember. The ember I had carried since Maren died. The heirloom. The charge. The sacred trust of the Thornfell Wayfinders' Guild, four members strong, three now, two —

It wasn't a relic. It wasn't even, properly, a prisoner, though the singer's story had already turned my stomach inside out over that. It was my mother. Or my father. Or whatever word a god gets. It had been beating against my ribs for eleven years, close enough to warm me, and I had thought of it as *it.*

I stopped walking when the sky went grey at the edges. I was on a shelf of rock above a valley full of cloud, the kind of place a Wayfinder would normally stop and read the wind. Instead I sat down, took off my pack, took out the ember's case, and set it on the stone in front of me like something I was returning.

"I can't," I said out loud, to it, to the valley, to nobody. "I can't do this."

The ember glowed. It always glowed. But for the first time I let myself feel what the glow meant — that it was awake in there, aware, watching me through the iron the way a parent watches a child through a locked door — and I felt it reach for me, gentle, tentative, the way you reach for an animal that has been hurt by hands before.

I flinched away from it.

Then I got up, left the case on the rock, and walked.

* * *

I made it perhaps two miles before the shame caught up with me, and another mile before Isandro did.

He came down the trail behind me without calling out this time — he'd learned that much about me in the weeks since Vael's Crossing, that I go quieter when I'm frightened, not louder — and I heard him before I saw him, the soft crunch of his boots, the click of his prayer beads against his wrist where he still wore them out of habit he hadn't yet decided to break. When I finally turned, he was ten paces back, breathing hard from the climb, frost in his beard, and his face had an expression I recognized from shrines: the look of a man confronting something holy and terrible.

"You left it on a rock," he said.

"I know."

"Alone. In the open. With the Choir able to smell it now." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Wendell. You left your mother on a rock."

"Don't." The word came out of me like a snapped cable. "Don't use that word. You don't get to use that word. You didn't read those letters. You didn't spend your whole life believing in something that turned out to be a cage built around you by the only family you had."

"No," he said quietly. "I spent three hundred years believing in an order that built itself on lies about me. So maybe I don't get to say the word. But I know the shape of it."

I laughed. It came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter. "Three hundred years. Right. The priest who remembers. The man sent by a voice in a shrine. How do I know any of it's true? How do I know you're not part of it — the rewriting, the hiding, the great careful machine that turned a god's child into a guide for hire? Maybe that's what you are. Maybe you're the mechanism. Sent to keep the prisoner walking to its cell."

Something moved across his face — hurt first, fast, and then something older and colder underneath it, and I realized too late that I had chosen exactly the wrong wound to press. He set down his staff. He took off his outer robe and folded it, actually folded it, laid it on a stone with the neatness of a man preparing to do something he might not come back from.

"Say that again," he said.

"You heard me."

"I heard you. Say it again anyway."

"Isandro—"

"*Say it.*"

So I said it, louder, crueler, everything I'd been swallowing since the letters: that he was a stranger wearing a story, that his memories were no more trustworthy than my missing ones, that a man who claimed a voice answered his prayers was either a liar or a madman or a leash, and none of those were things I could afford to love.

He hit me.

Not hard — he pulled it, mostly, a priest's blow, trained and immediately regretted — but it caught my jaw and spun me half around, and I came back swinging because I was twenty-six years old and everything I believed in had burned down in a week and here, finally, was something simple: a body in front of me, a fist, a target. We went down into the wet needles together. I got an elbow into his ribs; he got an arm across my throat, not squeezing, just holding, the way you hold a door shut against a storm. I fought him the way I'd been fighting since the guildhall burned — blind, furious, at everything and everyone and mostly at myself — and he took it, all of it, and did not strike me again.

"Finished?" he said, close to my ear.

"No."

"Good. Neither am I." He shifted his weight so I couldn't roll free, and his voice dropped into that register he used for prayers and for truths, low and unhurried. "You want to know if I'm the mechanism. Fine. Let's test it. If I were sent to keep you walking to the temple, would I have let you walk two miles the wrong way without stopping you? Would I have followed alone instead of binding you and dragging you back? Would I be lying under you right now in the freezing mud letting you break my ribs?"

"That proves nothing. You could be patient. Serah was patient."

"Serah," he said, "never once asked me what I wanted."

I stopped struggling. Not because I was beaten — because he was right, and my body knew it before my pride did. We lay there in the needle-litter, both of us bleeding a little, breathing hard, the grey light coming up through the pines in long cold bars, and the fury drained out of me and left behind the thing it had been protecting, which was grief, pure and enormous, the size of the sky.

"I don't know who I am," I said. My voice cracked in the middle of it. "That's the whole truth, Isandro. Everyone I trusted rewrote me. My memories are a forgery. My name might be a forgery. My face belongs to a woman in a burning temple. And the only thing in the world that knows what I actually am is locked in an iron box on a rock two miles back, and I ran away from it, because if I open that box and it looks at me with recognition — if it *knows* me — then I have to be its child, and I don't know how to survive being its child."

His arms loosened. He sat up slowly, keeping a hand on my shoulder like an anchor, and looked at me with his ruined, patient eyes.

"When the voice answered me in Vael's Crossing," he said, "do you know what it gave me? Not instructions. Not power. A name. Yours. That's all. Three hundred years of stolen memory, and the first thing returned to me was someone else's name — because apparently that's how this works. The gods don't hand you yourself. They hand you someone to walk beside while you find it." He was quiet a moment. "I hated it. I fled the shrine. I've been terrified since the day I met you that I was carrying a message I didn't understand to a person I had no right to. And then Greywater happened, and the bridge, and I understood the message. It was never *deliver the ember.* Sefu's charter says that. The voice never did. The message was: *you will not have to do this alone.*"

"The voice told you that?"

"It didn't have to. You did. On the bridge, in the rain, when you kissed me half-drowned and then apologized for it like it was a tactical error." He almost smiled. "You chose me, Wendell. Before you knew what you were. That choice doesn't dissolve because the ground moved. Nothing true dissolves because the ground moves. That's how you tell it from the rest."

I lay back in the needles and stared up at the pines swaying against the brightening sky, and I cried. Not elegantly. Ugly, hiccupping, whole-body crying, the kind I hadn't allowed myself since the guildhall burned, because I'd been saving all my strength for walking and there was suddenly nowhere left to walk to. Isandro didn't touch me at first. Then he did — pulled me against him, my head under his jaw, his heartbeat steady and mortal and real against my ear, and I clung to that rhythm like a rope over a drop.

"They took my childhood," I said into his collarbone.

"Yes."

"And I'll never get it back."

"No. Some doors don't reopen. Grieve it. It deserves grieving." His hand moved slowly along my spine. "But hear me, Wendell Oyelaran — whoever that turns out to be. A rewritten past is not a void. It's a room with false walls. Behind the walls there's still you. There was always still you. The girl who learned the stars over the forge — that learning was real even if the story around it was a lie. Your skill is yours. Your stubbornness is yours. The way you put yourself between danger and other people — that was never taught. That was born in you. They hid your inheritance, but they couldn't invent your nature. Nature isn't written. Nature is what's left when everything written burns."

I pulled back enough to look at him. His lip was split where my elbow had caught him, or where his own teeth had, and there was a bruise blooming on his ribs, and he looked at me with no anger left at all.

"So what do I do?" I asked. It was not a rhetorical question. It was the smallest, most honest question I had ever asked anyone. "Tell me what to do."

"You know what to do. You've known since you stood up from reading those letters. You just can't do it alone, and you were never supposed to." He took my hand. "We go back. We pick up the ember. You let it look at you. And if it knows you — then we grieve that too, together, and then we finish the road. Not because the charter says so. Because it's *yours*, and you don't leave your own on a rock in the dark."

* * *

We walked back the way I'd come, side by side, neither speaking much. My jaw ached. His breathing was shallow — I kept checking, and he kept pretending not to notice me checking, and eventually I made him stop and let me bind his ribs with a strip of my shirt, kneeling in the trail like a Wayfinder tending a client, except that no client had ever held my wrist afterward and said thank you with his thumb pressed to my pulse.

The ember's case sat on the shelf of rock exactly where I'd left it, silvered with frost, and the cloud below the valley had risen and parted so that morning light came up the slope and struck it, and the glow inside the iron had changed. It was brighter. Steadier. Like something that had spent the night waiting and had decided, in the end, to hope.

I knelt in front of it. Isandro knelt beside me, not touching, present the way he'd promised to be.

I opened the case.

Heat rolled out, dry and clean, like the air above a hearth in winter. The ember sat in its cradle of charred cloth, and its light leaned toward me — there was no other word for it, the light physically bent, the way a plant bends — and I felt it take my measure. Not my fear. Not my guilt. Something underneath both. And what came back to me, through skin and bone and whatever else I was made of, was not words. It was simpler than words. It was the feeling of a hand resting on the top of a child's head. Patient. Unhurried. Certain.

I started crying again, but differently this time, and Isandro's arm came around my shoulders and stayed.

"I'm sorry," I said to the ember. To her. To whatever pronoun fit a heart older than mountains. "I was afraid of you. I'm still afraid. But I'm here, and I'm not leaving you on rocks anymore, and I don't know yet whether I'm taking you home or setting you free or becoming you — but I'm going to find out, and I'm going to do it awake."

The light pulsed once, slow, like a nod.

Isandro exhaled beside me, long and shaking. "There she is," he murmured — meaning me, not it, and I knew it, and I let him mean it.

We repacked the case. We ate the last of the waybread sitting on the shelf with our boots dangling over the clouds, passing the same piece back and forth, and somewhere in the middle of that ordinary, miraculous meal I reached over and took his split-lipped face in both hands and kissed him properly — not in rain, not drowning, not apologizing after. Deliberately. In daylight. He kissed me back like a man setting down a weight he'd carried for three hundred years.

When we broke apart, I was laughing, wetly, stupidly, and he was too, and for one whole minute the mountain was just a mountain and the future was just a road.

Then the birds went up.

All of them at once, off the slopes below us, a ragged gray sheet of them rising out of the pines and streaming east, away from something. Isandro was on his feet before I finished turning, and I felt it a heartbeat later through the stone under my knees — through the ember, humming against my chest like a struck bell.

A smell on the wind from the south. Ash, and under the ash, sweet and wrong, like incense burning over meat.

"They found the shrine," Isandro said quietly. "The cave. Our fire's still warm in there, Wendell. They can smell the ember — and now they can smell where it's been."

I looked south, where the treeline stood dark and innocent, and tightened the straps on the ember's case, and stood.

"Then we don't go back for anything," I said. "We go up. Straight up, through whatever's between us and the Pass of Teeth, and we don't stop until the temple doors are open."

The ember pulsed against my heart — once, twice — quickening, like a pulse climbing toward a run.
