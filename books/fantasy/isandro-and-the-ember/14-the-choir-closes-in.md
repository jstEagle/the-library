## 14. The Choir Closes In

The god-fragment had been weeping for three days.

Serah Voss stood at the edge of the sunken amphitheater and listened to it, the way a farmer listens to rain — not with pity, but with an ear for what the weather promised. The fragment hung in the center of the old stone ring, suspended in a lattice of ash-chains that the Choir's smiths had forged from burned prayer-books, and it wept light. That was the thing no one warned you about gods: they didn't bleed when you cut them. They wept. Golden, slow, luminous tears that slid down the chains and hissed where they touched the ground, and every drop that fell made the grass beneath grow taller for a heartbeat before it blackened.

It was beautiful. Serah had long ago made her peace with that. Cruelty was almost always beautiful; that was how it got its hooks into people. A god's suffering was the most beautiful thing in the world, which was precisely why the world had to be rid of gods.

"Mother." Brother Callen came down the amphitheater steps behind her, boots crunching on old bone-meal. He was young still, round-faced, with the earnest eyes of a man who had never doubted anything, which made him useful and slightly tiresome in equal measure. "The rite is prepared. The singers are fasting. The fragment's strength is—" he consulted the slate in his hands, though Serah knew he'd already memorized it— "less than we hoped. It fought the taking."

"They all fight the taking," Serah said. "So did I, once."

Callen laughed politely, because he thought she was joking. She let him think it. There were perhaps four people alive who knew what she meant, and she had outlived two of them.

Below, in the ring of the amphitheater, the Choir assembled. Forty-one singers in grey robes, faces smeared with hearth-ash, arranged in the seven concentric circles that mirrored the seven heavens the gods had once claimed for themselves. They carried no instruments. Their voices were the instruments, and their bodies were the altars, and tonight one of them would not walk away.

"The fragment is small," Callen said, watching her watch them. "A splinter off some river-god, the scouts think. Barely worth the rite."

"It isn't about worth." Serah descended the steps slowly, her own robe unmarked by ash — the Mother of the Choir wore clean wool, because she was not a singer; she was the hand that guided the singing. "A splinter is enough. You don't drink the ocean to learn the taste of salt."

She stopped at the innermost circle's edge. Up close, the god-fragment resolved out of its glow: a shape like a drowned man crossed with a willow tree, limbs trailing, face — if it could be called a face — turned toward her with an expression she had learned to read across a dozen such rites. Not hatred. Gods didn't hate; hatred required believing your enemy your equal. What it wore was *pity*, vast and ancient and utterly sincere, the way you might pity a moth beating itself against a lamp.

That pity was the worst part. That pity was why she did this.

"You're wondering," she said to it, conversationally, as the singers began the low opening drone beneath her words, "why I would tell you this. You're wondering whether I'm mad, or broken, or bought." She clasped her hands behind her back. "I'm none of those things. I'm going to tell you a story, little splinter, and then we're going to open you like a letter, and you're going to tell us where your kin is hiding."

The drone deepened. The first circle of singers began to turn, slow as millstones.

"I was nine years old," Serah said, "when the Godswar's last after-shock came through my village. Not a war — the war was over by then, everyone said so, the gods were *gone*, locked away, the world made safe. But something leaked. Something one of your jailers missed, or one of your kind left behind on purpose, the way a man leaving a house sets a fire in the pantry." Her voice stayed level, pleasant, almost gentle. "It took the whole valley. Four hundred souls. My mother sang to me while the sky burned, and her voice melted mid-note, and I lived because I was down a well fetching water, and the well was deep, and I heard everything."

The fragment's light flickered. Pity again. *Pity me*, that expression said, *pity us, we suffer too.*

"Yes," Serah agreed softly. "You suffer. Everything suffers. The question has never been who suffers — it's who *answers* for it." She stepped closer to the chains. This close, the god's radiance washed over her like heat from an opened oven, and she felt the old familiar pull, the sweet gravitational longing that gods exerted on mortal hearts, the thing that had built ten thousand temples on ten thousand hills. She felt it and was not moved, and that, more than any doctrine, was her faith. "You answer for nothing. That's the whole architecture of divinity, little splinter. You take worship and give back weather. You take prayers and give back silence. And when mortals build cages to hold you, you let *them* be called jailers, so that when the cages fail, the blame falls on men."

The second circle began to turn against the first. The drone split into harmony, then into dissonance, deliberate and controlled, a chord shaped like a key entering a lock.

"We are not jailers," Serah said. "We are not your enemies. We are the cure. A world without gods is a world without masters. No thrones in heaven means no permission slips for the ones on earth. Every priest who ever told a peasant her dead child was in a god's hands was laundering power — hers up, theirs down. Every miracle was a debt note. Every answered prayer was a leash being tested." She reached out and laid one finger, briefly, against the nearest ash-chain. It hummed. "We are going to finish what the Godswar started. Not imprisonment. *Consumption.* Every fragment, every splinter, every sleeping heart — taken into the Choir, ground into song, given to the people whose prayers were never answered. Divinity, redistributed. That is my gospel. That is the only honest use for a god."

The fragment spoke. It had not spoken before; the fragments rarely did, and when they did it was never in words but in pressure, in meaning arriving fully formed inside the skull like a stone dropped into still water.

*You will become us,* it said. *This is how it begins. One hunger, fed.*

"No," Serah said. "This is how it *ends*. You're thinking of your worshippers. They fed you and became less. We feed on you and become more. That's the whole difference between religion and the Choir, and it's the only difference that matters."

She raised her hand. The third, fourth, fifth circles turned. The chord became a wheel, the wheel became a throat, and the forty-first singer — old Brother Anselm, who had volunteered, who had kissed each of them on the brow at dusk and walked into the ring smiling — stepped forward into the fragment's light and opened his arms.

What happened next happened fast, because the Choir had refined this rite across thirty years and eleven fragments, and efficiency was its own form of mercy. Anselm's voice joined the wheel and the wheel took him, and his selfhood poured upward through the harmonics like water finding its level, and the ash-chains drank the fragment's weeping light, and the fragment screamed — not in sound, in *color*, the whole amphitheater flooding gold-white-gold — and then it came apart. Not destroyed. *Opened*. Like a letter. Like a book. Its knowledge spilled into the waiting vessels of the Choir, forty minds catching a god's scattered memory the way a congregation catches bread.

Serah closed her eyes and received her portion.

It was small — the splinter had been small — but it was enough. A taste of salt from a very small ocean. She stood in the sudden silence while the singers swayed and gasped around her, some weeping, some laughing, one vomiting quietly at the circle's edge, and she sorted through what the fragment had known in its long dim centuries of captivity: a river's course, now changed. A shrine, now ruined. A fear. And threaded through the fear, bright as a brand pressed to paper—

*A heart. A sleeping heart, carried north in a case of guild-leather, against the chest of a child of the Hollow Temple.*

Serah opened her eyes.

The amphitheater smelled of ozone and burnt sugar. Anselm was gone — there was nothing left of him but his robe, folded neatly at the ring's edge by whatever remained of his courtesy — and in the center of the stones, where the fragment had hung, the air still shimmered faintly, and Serah found that when she breathed, she could smell it. Not incense. Not ash. Something older and sweeter and infinitely more precious: divinity, sleeping, moving north at the pace of a young person's walking feet.

"There," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended, and forty faces turned to her. She mastered it. A Mother did not tremble. "Brothers, sisters. Breathe. Do you smell it?"

They breathed. And one by one, the faces changed — wonder, hunger, tears — as the scent settled into them the way a new sense settles into a body, disorienting and undeniable. Callen went white, then flushed, then gripped his slate so hard the wood creaked.

"It's *moving*," he whispered. "Mother, I can feel it moving. North. Fast."

"Not fast enough." Serah turned south, toward the mountains, toward the Pass of Teeth, a jagged rumor on the horizon under building storm-clouds. The ember's trail lay across the land like a road of perfume, and every hour it cooled a little, and every hour their own senses sharpened. The fragment's death had done more than feed them. It had tuned them. Eleven fragments before this one had taught the Choir to hunt; this one had given them noses.

"How far?" she asked.

Callen's eyes had gone distant, tracking something none of them could see and all of them could smell. "Three days. Maybe four, if they push hard. They'll reach the pass before us — the direct road is shorter than ours — but the storm's coming down off the heights, and Wayfinders are careful in storms, and we..." He smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and Serah noted with distant approval that the boy was finally becoming useful. "We are not careful. We have never once been careful. That is our whole advantage."

"Then we go through the storm," Serah said. "Send riders ahead to close the pass's southern mouth. If the Crown's soldiers are already there — and they will be, the inquisitors have been a day behind us since Greywater — then let the Crown and the god-thieves bloody each other, and we walk in over both." She looked back once at the empty ring, at Anselm's folded robe, at the scorched circle where a god had been unmade into a compass. "And someone collect Anselm's robe. He gave us the way north. He'll want it to keep traveling."

* * *

They marched through the night, and Serah did not sleep, because sleep was for people who could afford to dream.

Instead she walked at the column's head and let the new sense wash over her, and in the privacy of her own skull she held a conversation she would never permit herself aloud.

*You will become us,* the splinter had said, and she had answered with doctrine, and the doctrine was true, and it was also — she was honest enough, alone, in the dark, with no one to lead — incomplete.

Because she had smelled the ember now. Truly smelled it. And underneath the sweetness, underneath the ancient sleeping weight of it, there was something else. Something the splinter's memories had shown her without her asking: a woman's face on a burning temple's steps. A child carried down a mountain in the dark. A heart that beat, faintly, in time with a heartbeat that was not its own.

The ember was not alone. Whatever carried it was bound to it — blood-deep, root-deep — and the Choir's new nose could not entirely separate the two scents. God and mortal, twined like a double helix of perfume, and when Serah concentrated on the mortal thread of it, she felt something she refused to name.

Not temptation. She was past temptation; she had fed her own doubts to the rite years ago, along with everything else she loved.

But *recognition*. Somewhere ahead of her, walking north through the rain toward a ruined temple, was a person the gods had marked the way she had been marked — chosen, burdened, conscripted into a war between powers that treated human lives as currency. The difference between them was that Serah had cut herself free, and this child of the Hollow Temple was still carrying the coin.

*We'll free you too,* she thought northward, into the dark and the rain, with all the terrible tenderness of a woman who had watched her mother's voice melt mid-note. *Whether you thank me or not. That's the difference between me and your god, little one. I would let you refuse.*

Behind her, the column sang as it marched — the working hymns, the road-songs, the beautiful grinding dissonances that kept time for forty pairs of boots. Ahead, lightning stitched the clouds above the Pass of Teeth, illuminating the mountains' silhouette: a jaw of black stone, half-open, waiting.

The wind shifted. The scent strengthened.

Serah smiled, and lengthened her stride, and the Choir followed her into the storm like a funeral procession that had decided, this once, to arrive early.
