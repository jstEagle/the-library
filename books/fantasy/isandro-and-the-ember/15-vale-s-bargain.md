## 15. Vale's Bargain

Inquisitor Vale had learned long ago that the mountains did not care who was right.

She had been climbing for three days behind them — two fugitives and an ember that burned in her dreams even now, a hundred miles from the last place she'd seen it. Her soldiers called it a wild-goose chase. She let them. Inquisitors survived by letting people believe convenient things.

But she had seen the guildhouse burn. She had knelt in the ash of Thornfell and lifted what remained of Guildmaster Sefu Oyelaran's signet ring from the char, and she had read the charter folded inside his coat — the old one, the true one, not the sanitized copy the Crown kept in its archives. *Until the gods wake.* Four words her superiors had struck from every official record three generations ago, and one dying man had bled into the snow to preserve.

Vale was fifty-one years old. She had spent thirty of those years doing the Crown's dirty work with clean hands, or as clean as hands could be in this trade. She had broken cults, silenced prophets, buried evidence so deep that even she sometimes forgot where she'd put it. And through all of it she had carried a single private conviction, worn smooth as river stone: that the Godswar had ended because someone had been wise enough to make sure it could never happen again. That the cages were mercy. That forgetting was a kindness paid for in blood.

Now she was beginning to suspect the kindness had been a lie told by frightened people to other frightened people, and that the interest on the debt had come due at the worst possible moment.

The pass narrowed ahead, granite walls shouldering close, and her scout came back down the trail at a jog with his face carefully arranged around bad news.

"Firelight ahead, ma'am. Two travelers. They've made camp in the shrine hollow — the one with the standing stones."

"The shrine hollow." Vale closed her eyes briefly. Of course. The boy — Oyelaran, Wendell, whatever name fit this week — was a Wayfinder. Wayfinders knew the land the way priests knew liturgy, every fold and scar of it, including the places the maps had been ordered to forget. "Anyone else?"

"Tracks, ma'am. A lot of them. Coming up from the south." He hesitated, which he never did. "Barefoot tracks. Dozens."

Vale had fought the Ashen Choir once before, early in her career, back when they'd been a fringe sect burning effigies in the fen-country. Barefoot devotees, ash-smeared, eyes like banked coals. She had thought them exterminated. The Crown was very good at thinking things were exterminated.

"Two enemies," she said quietly, more to herself than the scout. "And only one ember."

* * *

She went forward alone. It was not bravery; it was arithmetic. Twenty soldiers crashing up a shrine hollow would start a battle, and a battle near the ember would end in ways no one could predict. Besides, she wanted to look at them first. She always looked first. Looking was half of interrogation, and interrogation was most of her job.

She came around the last bend with her hood back and her sword belted but sheathed, hands visible, walking at the pace of a tired woman rather than an officer of the Crown. The campfire was small, well-sheltered against the stones. Two figures sat on either side of it.

The priest she recognized from reports: Isandro Vael, exiled from his own order three centuries ago — or so the stories said, and Vale had stopped trusting stories about Isandro Vael when she realized every version contradicted every other. He was younger-looking than any man had a right to be, dark-haired, watchful, sitting with his back to the rock like a man who had learned the hard way never to leave it unguarded.

The other one took her longer to place. Younger. Sharp-featured, windburned, hair tied back with what looked like a bootlace. Sitting cross-legged with a map across their knees — an actual paper map, annotated in a cramped hand, the kind of thing no one under forty bothered with anymore. When they heard her footsteps they didn't reach for a weapon. They reached for the satchel at their side and drew it closer, the way a parent draws a child closer in a crowd.

The ember. Or rather, the satchel that held the ember, wrapped in wool and oilcloth and, if Sefu's letters were accurate, wards stitched by four generations of dead hands.

"Inquisitor Vale," she said. "Crown Office of Inquiry. I'm going to sit down, because my knees are fifty-one years old, and I'm going to talk, and you're both going to listen, and nobody is going to die tonight unless one of us is considerably stupider than I look."

Wendell Oyelaran looked at her the way people look at a debt collector. "You're the one who burned the guildhouse."

"No," Vale said, and meant it, and knew it wouldn't matter. "I arrived after. I interrogated survivors. There were none worth the word. What burned your guildhouse was either the Choir or men acting on orders older than mine — and I have spent three days and a great deal of the Crown's money trying to determine which, because it matters enormously to what happens next."

Isandro's eyes hadn't left her. "Why should we believe anything you say?"

"You shouldn't," she said frankly. "Believe is for children and congregants. Weigh. I'll give you weights." She held up fingers. "One: I'm alone, visibly, and my soldiers are ten minutes back down the trail, which means if you wanted me dead you've had the time to do it and haven't. Two: I know what's following you — barefoot tracks, dozens, coming up from the south — and I'm telling you instead of letting it find you unprepared, which is a strange thing for a hunter to do. Three—" she paused, and this was the part that cost her, "—three: I read Sefu Oyelaran's charter in the ash, and I have not reported its contents to my superiors, which is treason, and I am telling you that too."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the standing stones and made them hum, faintly, like wet fingers on glass.

Wendell set the map down. "What does the Charter say?"

"*Until the gods wake,*" Vale said. "And then the guild's duty changes. It doesn't say how. Sefu died before he could tell anyone, or perhaps he died because he tried." She spread her hands. "Here is my problem, Wayfinder. My orders are to recover the ember and return it to the Crown vaults beneath the capital. Those orders were written by people who believe the Godswar ended because the gods were destroyed. I have recently concluded that the Godswar ended because the gods were *imprisoned*, and that my orders were written by people who either don't know that or are lying about it. Both possibilities end with me carrying a sleeping god's heart into a hole in the ground and calling it safety."

"And you don't think it's safe."

"I think," Vale said, "that a cage is only as good as the jailers, and the jailers are only as good as their memory, and the Crown has proven — repeatedly, catastrophically, over three hundred years — that it cannot be trusted to remember anything inconvenient." She looked at the satchel. "I also think that if the Choir catches you on open ground, they will take that ember, and they will perform their rite, and everything within a hundred miles will die screaming, and then whatever they become will come for the rest. I have read the accounts of the first war. The Choir has not. They think it was a victory. It was a wound that never healed."

Isandro spoke softly. "You sound like a woman who has decided the Crown is wrong and hasn't told the Crown yet."

"I sound," Vale said, "like a woman who has served the Crown for thirty years and would like, once, before she dies, to serve something true." She let that sit. "So here is my offer. Not my orders. My offer."

* * *

She laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only currency she had left.

Sanctuary. The Crown's deepest vaults, beneath the Citadel, warded by the best ward-smiths alive, guarded by soldiers who answered to her personally and not to the Ministry of Doctrine. Protection for the two of them — full pardons, names cleared, the guildhouse deaths formally attributed to the Choir, which was even true. Time. Years, if they needed them, to decide what the ember was and what should be done with it, in safety, with resources, with scholars who weren't trying to eat it.

"A cage," Wendell said flatly.

"Yes," Vale agreed. "A very good cage. But a cage. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. The difference between my cage and the Choir's rite is that mine leaves the god alive to be released later, by someone wiser than all of us, and theirs doesn't leave anything at all."

Wendell was quiet for a long moment. Firelight moved on their face, and Vale watched them decide something — watched the exact moment the grief in their expression hardened into geometry.

"My guildmaster died for this," Wendell said. "Not for the ember. For the *duty*. 'Take it to the Hollow Temple by solstice.' His last words, spoken while the roof came down on him. And I found his letters, Inquisitor. I found the letters he wrote and never sent, the ones where he admits what the guild did — what it did to me, specifically, though I didn't understand that until after he was ash." Their voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse. "Everyone I trusted lied to me to keep a secret. The guild. The Crown. Maybe the gods themselves. And your offer is: bring the secret to us, we'll keep lying to everyone, but comfortably, and with better locks."

"That is roughly the shape of it," Vale admitted.

"Then here is my counter-offer."

Wendell leaned forward, and Vale saw Sefu in them suddenly — the same stubborn set of the jaw, the same refusal to be hurried. She had interrogated the man twice, years ago, over smuggling irregularities that had turned out to be nothing. He had offered her tea both times and refused to answer a single question directly, and she had liked him enormously, and filed him as harmless, and been wrong about that in every direction at once.

"Help us reach the temple," Wendell said. "Your soldiers, your authority, your knowledge of where the Choir is and what they're planning. Get us there by solstice. And when the doors open — and they'll open for me, that part isn't bravado, it's blood — the gods' fate gets decided *openly*. Not in a vault. Not in a rite. Not by the Crown, not by the Choir, not by the guild, not by me alone. Witnesses. Records. Every faction at the table, or as many as will come. Whatever happens in that chamber happens in front of the world."

Vale stared at them. "You're proposing to reopen the Godswar question in front of the entire realm."

"I'm proposing to stop pretending it was ever settled."

"Do you have any idea what that costs? Do you know how many people died to bury that question? Do you know what happens to the provinces when the old cults learn their gods are alive and caged? Every prison-break in history, Wayfinder, every one, started with someone deciding the truth deserved daylight—"

"Yes," Wendell said. "And every tyranny in history started with someone deciding the truth was too dangerous to tell. You've spent thirty years choosing the second mistake. I'm asking you to try the first one. Once. Deliberately. With guards on the doors."

Isandro shifted, and Vale caught the motion — the priest had been listening with the stillness of a man used to being talked over, but something in Wendell's speech had moved him. He spoke without looking away from the fire. "There's a third option neither of you is naming."

"Name it," Vale said.

"The Choir reaches the temple first. Or the Crown decides a truce is cheaper than a trial and buries us all in the pass. Or the god wakes before solstice and none of our arrangements matter." He shrugged, elegant and tired. "Every plan is a prayer, Inquisitor. I was a priest for three hundred years; I know the genre. The question isn't whether the plan is perfect. It's whether the people making it can stand each other long enough to fail together."

Vale almost laughed. It escaped as a short exhale, fog in the cold air. Three hundred years old and the man negotiated like a fishwife. She respected it.

* * *

They talked until the fire burned down to coals. Terms, mostly. Vale would attach twelve soldiers to them — no more, enough to fight a delaying action, few enough to move fast. She would send her fastest rider south with a sealed letter to the Ministry demanding formal observation rights at the temple, knowing it would arrive late or not at all, sending it anyway because the record mattered. She would give them what her scouts had mapped of the Choir's movements: the sect had split, three columns converging on the Pass of Teeth, moving faster than any pilgrims had a right to move, as if something were pulling them uphill.

In exchange, Wendell would not run. Not from her, not from the road, not from the choice waiting at the top of the mountain. If the temple opened and the god asked its child a question, the child would answer it in front of witnesses — even if the answer was one Vale's superiors would burn her for hearing.

"And if I say no?" Wendell asked, near the end. "If I take the ember and vanish into the high country tonight?"

"Then I hunt you," Vale said simply. "And I'll likely fail, because you're a Wayfinder and I'm a bureaucrat with a sword. But I'll fail slowly, loudly, and in your direction, and every hour I spend failing is an hour the Choir spends catching up. You don't need me to catch you, Wayfinder. You need me to not be in your way. Everything else is decoration."

Wendell looked at her for a long time. Then they extended a hand across the coals — calloused, rope-scarred, a guide's hand, a carrier's hand.

"Truce," they said. "Uneasy. Witnessed." A nod toward Isandro. "He remembers things. Apparently that makes it binding."

"It makes it *witnessed*," Isandro corrected. "Binding is between the two of you."

Vale took the hand and shook it. Her father had been a merchant; she knew the weight of a handshake that would be renounced the moment it became inconvenient, and this wasn't one. This was two exhausted people agreeing to carry something together because neither could carry it alone. She had negotiated treaties between duchies that had less honesty in them.

"One more thing," she said, rising, knees cracking. She stood in the cold and looked down at the two of them — the exile and the orphan, the liar's child and the god's child, though only one of them knew both truths yet. "The letter I sent to the Ministry. If my superiors respond, they will order me to seize the ember and abandon you. I want you to know now: I will disobey that order. I am telling you in advance so you can plan around my treason rather than discovering it mid-battle. That is the only gift I have left to give anyone — warning."

She turned to go, then paused.

"The shrine hums," she said quietly. "These standing stones. Did you notice?"

Wendell glanced up at the monoliths ringing the hollow, black against the stars. "Wayfinders call them listening-stones. Old road-magic. Nobody remembers what they listen *for*."

"They're humming," Vale repeated. "They weren't humming when I walked past them an hour ago. They started when you began talking about taking the god's fate to the world." She rubbed her arms against the chill. "Whatever else is true, something up this mountain has opinions about your counter-offer, Wayfinder. Sleep lightly."

She walked back down the trail toward her soldiers, alone in the dark, and behind her the stones went on humming — low, patient, like a held breath before a verdict.

Three days to solstice. The Pass of Teeth lay ahead, and somewhere on its storm-wracked heights, barefoot and ash-marked and singing, the Choir was already climbing.
