## 16. The Pass of Teeth

**Wendell**

The mountain had teeth, and it showed them all at once.

From below, the Pass of Teeth looked like a wound in the world's jaw — a ragged notch between two granite faces where the wind screamed through like something trapped and furious. From inside it, the pass was worse. The path narrowed to a shelf barely wide enough for two abreast, with a drop on the left so sheer that the clouds below them had weight and shadow, and a wall on the right studded with hanging fangs of ice that the wind played like a broken organ.

Wendell walked at the front because Isandro would not let them walk at the back.

"You're the Wayfinder," he'd said that morning, tightening the last knot on his pack straps with fingers gone stiff from cold. "You go first. That's the whole religion of your guild, isn't it? Someone has to be first."

"That's not what my guild taught."

"No," Isandro said quietly. "But it's what Sefu died teaching you anyway."

Wendell hadn't answered. There was nothing to answer with. Three days since Vale's bargain, two days since the truce column had begun its climb — forty Crown soldiers in grey wool, breath steaming, boots wrapped in sacking for silence — and Wendell still did not know how to carry the shape of what they were becoming. A guide. A fugitive. A god's child walking toward its parent's prison with the parent's heart beating in a lead-lined case against their ribs.

The ember was warm. It was always warm now, but in the pass it had begun to pulse, slow and deep, like something waking in stages. When Wendell pressed a palm flat against their coat, they could feel it keeping time with their own heart, and sometimes — this was the part they had told no one, not even Isandro — sometimes it kept time *first*, and their heart hurried to catch up.

"Storm's coming down the col," called Sergeant Brannoc, Vale's second, squinting up through the white haze. He was a broad, weathered man who had not spoken to Wendell once without being spoken to, which Wendell had come to respect more than most conversation. "An hour, maybe less. Inquisitor says we make the shrine cave before it hits or we don't make it at all."

Vale herself was somewhere in the middle of the column, and Wendell hated that they noticed — hated that they'd started tracking her position the way a Wayfinder tracks a landmark, automatically, constantly. She had given them her word and her soldiers, and she had done it with the weary honesty of a woman signing her own condemnation. *If I'm wrong about you,* she'd said, *I'll have helped arm the thing that burns the realm. If I'm right, I'll have helped save it. Either way I'll have disobeyed my orders. So let's both try to be worth it.*

The wind gusted. Somewhere above, ice cracked and calved off the wall and shattered on the path behind them, and the whole column flinched as one animal.

"Wendell." Isandro's hand on their shoulder, steadying. His hair was crusted with frost at the temples; at thirty-something he looked, in this light, every year of whatever impossible age he actually was. "Talk to me. You've gone somewhere."

"I'm listening to it," Wendell said. "The ember. It's — excited. Or frightened. With it those might be the same thing."

Isandro went still. Then, softly, in a language Wendell didn't know but recognized the cadence of — the same cadence the ember hummed in when it dreamed — he said something short and low, a question shaped like a prayer.

The ember pulsed twice.

"What did you ask?"

"*Are you afraid?*" Isandro translated. His eyes, when they met Wendell's, held a dread he wasn't bothering to hide anymore. "It said yes. It said —" he swallowed. "It said *they are already here.*"

* * *

**Vale**

Inquisitor Maren Vale had climbed a great many mountains in service of the Crown, and she had learned one law above all others: the mountain does not care whose side you are on.

She stood at the column's midpoint with her hood back despite the cold — a habit her soldiers found either inspiring or insane, depending on how much they respected her — and watched the storm come down the col like an avalanche that had decided to take its time. Behind them, three thousand feet below, the treeline was already lost. Ahead, the pass bent north around a shoulder of black stone, and beyond that bend, according to the Wayfinder, lay the Hollow Temple and the end of everything she understood about her duty.

Her orders had been simple. Recover the ember. Return it to the vaults beneath the capital. She had disobeyed those orders in front of witnesses, negotiated terms with a fugitive, and committed the Crown's soldiers to escorting the relic to the one place it could never be recovered from again. If she lived, she would hang for it. She had made her peace with that on the first night, sitting alone with a cup of tea going cold, doing the arithmetic she always did: *what is the least bad outcome available, and what will it cost me.*

The least bad outcome was a god freed by choice rather than stolen by zealots, with witnesses, with terms, with the realm's eyes open instead of shut. The cost was probably her life. Fine. She'd signed that receipt long ago.

"Sergeant." Brannoc materialized at her elbow, snow in his beard. "Scouts report movement on the eastern ridge. Could be rockfall."

"Could be."

"Could be forty people in ash-grey robes moving very carefully."

Vale closed her eyes for half a second. Of course. Of course the Choir had found another way up. Mother Serah had sacrificed a god-fragment to hunt them; the woman could smell the ember now, everyone knew it, the whole column had heard the stories whispered around fires — that the Choir's hounds-of-flesh needed no trail, only proximity, and that proximity was closing like a fist.

"How far behind us are they?"

"If they're on the ridge — parallel. Maybe an hour behind in climbing terms. But the ridge converges with our path at the narrows." Brannoc pointed up-trail, to where the shelf thinned to a ledge barely four feet wide, walled on one side by ice and open on the other to a mile of empty air. "There's no way past the narrows except single file, ma'am. If they reach it while we're on it—"

"We're fish in a barrel." Vale was already moving. "Get the Wayfinder. Get the priest. And get every man with a crossbow to the point."

The storm hit before she reached the front of the line.

* * *

**Wendell**

The wind came sideways, then from everywhere, a white roar that erased the world beyond arm's reach. Wendell roped the column in pairs out of pure guild habit, hands remembering knots their rewritten mind had never been taught — another small betrayal by their own body, another gift from the self they used to be — and shouted the old wayfinder's cadence into the gale: *heel to heel, staff to stone, trust the rope and not your eyes.*

They were fifty feet from the narrows when the first arrow came out of the storm.

It took a soldier named Pell through the throat. Wendell never saw him fall, only felt the rope jerk and heard Brannoc bellowing, and then the storm was full of voices — Choir voices, high and ecstatic, singing over the wind. Not a battle cry. An actual song, harmonized, terrible: the Ashen Choir's devotional hymn, the one Serah's people sang while they burned things.

"They're on the ledge behind us," Isandro shouted, pressing close so his mouth was nearly at Wendell's ear. "And above — Wendell, there are climbers on the wall above the narrows, they've been waiting in the ice—"

The ambush unfolded the way avalanches do: all at once, from every direction, inevitable. Ash-robed figures rose out of the white on the flank of the path, crossbows braced against the gale with unnatural steadiness — anchored, Wendell realized with a lurch, by ropes of their own, they had *planned* this, had waited hours in the killing cold for the column to enter the kill-ground. Bolts hissed through the storm. Soldiers died standing up, roped to their comrades, dragging the wounded down with them because there was nowhere to fall except off the world.

And through it all, cutting against the current of the storm, came Mother Serah herself, walking the ledge as if it were a temple aisle, unhurried, bare-headed, her voice carrying impossibly over the wind:

"GIVE US THE HEART AND THE REST OF YOU MAY LIVE."

Wendell's hands shook on the rope. The ember against their ribs was blazing now, hammering, and they could *feel* it feeling them — fear feeding fear, a loop tightening. Beside them Isandro had planted his feet and thrown both arms wide, and he was praying aloud, not the safe formal prayers of his order but raw, stumbling words, holding some kind of line in the air that the bolts seemed reluctant to cross. One struck him anyway, high in the shoulder, spinning him half around; Wendell caught his collar and hauled him against the wall of ice and put their own body between him and the ledge.

"Isandro—"

"I'm fine. I'm fine. Don't stop moving—" Blood ran black in the cold down his sleeve. "The narrows. If we can get through the narrows, the path splits, they can't follow all of us—"

"There won't *be* an us if we run and leave these people to die."

"Wendell—"

"Wayfinder!" Brannoc's voice, raw, from down the line. "Inquisitor wants you at the rear! *Now!*"

* * *

**Vale**

The rear of the column was where the Choir had chosen to break it, and Maren Vale was where she had always intended to be when the bill came due: at the point of payment.

She knelt behind a fallen soldier's body with a crossbow she'd reloaded six times, counting enemies through the white chaos. Thirty, at least, on the ledge and the wall. More coming along the ridge behind. Her soldiers — her good, tired, loyal soldiers, men and women who had followed her into treason on her word alone — were dying in pairs on their ropes, and the arithmetic she did so well had turned brutal and simple: the column could not fight through. The column could not hold. The column could only *spend itself*, and the only question left was what it spent itself buying.

"Brannoc!" She grabbed his arm as he dove into cover beside her, a bolt splitting the air where his head had been. "Take twenty and push the Wayfinder through the narrows. Do not stop. Do not turn around. Get them to the temple doors."

"And you, ma'am?"

"Someone has to close the door behind you."

He stared at her. Twenty years of service lived in his face, and grief got there before understanding did. "Ma'am—"

"That's an order, Sergeant." She smiled at him, small and crooked, the smile she saved for the moments when honesty mattered more than comfort. "You always knew how this ended. So did I. Go."

He went. She watched him gather the survivors with hand-signs, watched the remnant of her column contract around the Wayfinder and the bleeding priest and begin the fight forward through the narrows, and she turned back to face down-ledge, reloading, calm as she had ever been in her life.

Then she stood up out of cover, into the storm, into the arrowsong, and walked toward the Choir.

Later — if there was a later — the survivors would tell it different ways. Some said she fired until the crossbow was empty and then drew steel. Some said she was singing, though that was probably the Choir's hymn bleeding into memory. What was certainly true was that she held the neck of the ledge for eleven minutes, alone with nine soldiers who refused her order to retreat, and that eleven minutes was enough. Enough for Brannoc's group to clear the narrows. Enough for the storm to swallow them.

Enough for a bolt to find the gap under her arm, and another her thigh, and for her to kneel at last in the snow with her blood steaming pink around her, watching ash-robed figures pick their careful way toward her over the bodies of her dead.

Mother Serah came to stand over her personally. Vale had never seen the woman's face this close. It was not a cruel face. That was the worst discovery of the day — it was a *certain* face, serene as stained glass, lit from within by a conviction Vale recognized because she had spent her whole life wearing its twin.

"You're the chief inquisitor," Serah said. Almost gently. "Maren Vale. They'll write about you. The woman who betrayed the Crown for a god."

"The woman who chose the least bad ending available," Vale corrected. Blood filled her mouth; she spat it into the snow. "You'd know something about choosing, if you'd ever let yourself doubt."

"Doubt is how masters keep their slaves."

"Mm." Vale's vision was going grainy at the edges, the storm receding, sounds arriving late. She thought, absurdly, of her tea going cold in the capital. Of her garden she'd never get back to. Of a frightened apprentice with frostbitten cheeks and a god's heartbeat under their coat, climbing away from her right now toward a door in a mountain. "Listen to me, madwoman. Listen, because I bought these words with everything I had left."

Serah leaned closer. Around them, the Choir had gone quiet, sensing a death and giving it ritual room.

"She's coming," Vale said. "Whatever's waking in that temple — she's coming, and she isn't yours, and she isn't the Crown's, and she isn't even the old god's. She's *new*. And when she stands up out of that mountain, you will learn the difference between killing a god and meeting one." Her hand found Serah's wrist, grip shocking in its strength, and pulled her down close enough that only the two of them could hear. "Make it worth the war I didn't start. Both of you. All of you. Make it *worth it* —"

The sentence never ended. The storm took the rest.

* * *

**Wendell**

They heard it — later, much later, when Brannoc finally allowed a halt in the lee of the split-path boulder — from one of the nine, a corporal named Adsett who had crawled away down-ledge with a bolt in his calf and seen the end of it from thirty yards before the storm closed like a book.

Wendell sat on the stone with the ember burning against their chest and did not cry, because the wind would have taken the tears and because crying felt too small for what Vale had done, and then Isandro sat down beside them, grey-faced, his shoulder bound in field dressings, and took their hand, and Wendell cried anyway.

"She disobeyed her orders for us," Wendell said. "She hung for us in advance. And I never — I argued with her. I bargained with her like she was a wall between me and the temple."

"She was a person," Isandro said. "That's rarer than gods. We just watched one die so we could keep walking." He squeezed their hand. "So we keep walking. That's the whole eulogy she asked for. *Make it worth it.*"

Above them, through a tear in the storm, the peak of the mountain showed for the first time in three days: black rock against a bruised gold sky, and near its summit, faint as a scar, the broken silhouette of the Hollow Temple's spire.

The ember pulsed. Once. Twice. And then — Wendell's breath stopped — it pulsed in a pattern, deliberate, patient, unmistakable.

A knock. Something knocking on the inside of a door.

"Isandro," Wendell whispered. "It knows we're coming. It's *waiting* for us."

The storm roared back across the peak, erasing the spire, but not before Wendell had seen what they needed to see. They stood, wiped their face with a frozen sleeve, and turned to the eighteen survivors of Vale's forty — Brannoc, Adsett, the rest, hollow-eyed and bloodstained, looking to a Wayfinder and an exile priest as if the two of them were the last fixed points in a sliding world.

"One hour to the doors," Wendell said. Their voice came out steady, and they were grateful to it, because they did not feel steady at all. "The Choir is behind us and the storm is above us and the god is inside us. We move now. For the Inquisitor. For everyone."

And they began to climb.

Behind them, far below, muffled by wind and distance, the Choir's hymn started up again — but higher now, faster, triumphant, and gaining.
