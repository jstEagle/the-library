## 17. Doors of the Hollow Temple

The Pass of Teeth did not deserve its name. Teeth could be broken, pulled, endured. The pass was more like a throat — a long convulsion of stone that had swallowed a road whole and left only its bones: shattered switchbacks, bridges of black ice over drops that went down into grey nothing, and wind that came off the summit like a scream with intent behind it.

Wendell climbed with the ember against their chest and Isandro's hand in theirs, and they did not look back.

They had buried Vale an hour ago, in a cleft of rock where the snow would keep her. There had been no time for words. Wendell had given her the only rite the Wayfinders knew — a traveler's blessing, spoken over strangers' graves at crossroads for two hundred years. *May the road remember you. May the mountain hold you gently.* It was not enough. Nothing would be enough. But Vale had died the way she'd lived, buying time for other people's choices, and the least Wendell owed her was to make it up the mountain before the hymn below them finished climbing.

Because it *was* climbing. That was the horror of it. The Choir's singing no longer stayed in one place. Wendell could feel it through the soles of their boots now, through their back teeth, through the ember itself — a vibration rising through the stone like water rising in a well. Serah had fed something to her god-devouring rite on the pass, and whatever she'd made of herself and her people, it did not tire, did not freeze, did not stop.

"They're in the switchbacks," Isandro said. He didn't shout; he had learned on this mountain that shouting cost breath, and breath was currency. "Two turns below us. Maybe three."

"Then we have minutes."

"Then we have minutes." His grip tightened on theirs. His fingers were frozen stiff and still warm somehow, the way everything about him was warm, the way he had been warm since the first night on the high road when Wendell had wanted to hate him and couldn't. "Wendell. If the doors don't open—"

"They'll open."

"You don't know that."

"I know it the way I know my own name." Which was, Wendell thought with a wild flicker of humor, not very reliably at all. Their name had been a lie once. Their childhood had been a lie. Sefu's letters had seen to that. But the ember knew them. The ember had known them since the vault, since before the vault, since — if the woman with their face in the burning temple was who Wendell feared she was — since the beginning of everything.

The road ended.

Not gradually, the way roads usually die, fraying into goat tracks and then into suggestion. It ended all at once, sheared off by some ancient violence, and beyond the break there was nothing but a shelf of bare stone thirty paces wide, curving along the mountainside toward a wall of cliff. And set into that cliff, half-swallowed by centuries of ice and lichen, were the doors.

Wendell stopped breathing.

They had imagined the Hollow Temple a hundred times since Sefu pressed the ember into their hands and said *solstice, the temple, go*. They had imagined ruins — tumbled columns, a broken dome, the polite decay of something mortals had built and mortals had abandoned. This was not that. This had never been built by mortal hands at all. The doors were a single seam in the living rock, taller than the guildhouse had been, taller than anything had a right to be, and they were carved — no, *grown*, Wendell thought, the way frost grows, the way crystal grows — with figures that moved when you weren't looking directly at them. A procession of shapes ascending toward a sun at the top. And every figure in the procession wore Wendell's face.

No. Not quite. Older faces and younger faces, faces that were almost theirs and then weren't, faces that shifted as the light changed — but the resemblance ran through all of them like a vein of gold through quartz.

"Wendell." Isandro's voice came from very far away. "Your nose is bleeding."

They touched their upper lip. Their fingers came away red, shockingly bright against the white world. And beneath the copper taste, beneath the cold, they could feel the ember hammering against their sternum like a second heart trying to get out — or trying to get *home*, which amounted to the same thing.

"It knows where it is," Wendell whispered. "It's been asleep for six hundred years and it knows where it is."

"So do they." Isandro turned. Far down the throat of the pass, torchlight had appeared — dozens of torches, moving with terrible steadiness, and the hymn rose with them, and now Wendell could hear the words, and the words were worse than any scream. They were tender. They were loving. *We will unmake your chains, O Sleeper. We will drink your sorrow and be free.*

Serah's voice carried above all of it, clear as a bell across the killing cold: *"Bring me the child!"*

Wendell walked to the doors.

Later they would not be able to say whether they chose to or whether something older than choice walked them there. The shelf of stone rang under their boots like a struck bell. The carved figures watched. The wind dropped — not gradually, but all at once, cut off as if a door had closed on it somewhere above the world — and in the sudden silence Wendell could hear the ember speaking. Not in words. In *recognition*. The way a lock speaks to a key. The way, Sefu's letters had told them, a mother's body remembers a child it has held.

They drew the little Wayfinder knife from their belt. The blade Sefu had given them at thirteen, when they'd first been allowed to mark maps. It had a nick in it from cutting kindling and a stain near the rivet they'd never gotten out, and it was the most ordinary object in the world, and Wendell loved it, and they pressed its edge to their palm and drew it across.

Blood welled, black in the twilight.

They put their bleeding hand flat against the door.

For three heartbeats, nothing. Long enough for despair to begin its familiar climb up Wendell's throat — *you are nobody, you were always nobody, the guild made you a lie and the lie ends here* —

Then the mountain exhaled.

Heat rolled out of the seam, heat like a hearth the size of the sky, and the blood on the stone did not run — it *sank*, drawn inward, and along the whole height of the doors the carved procession began to move. Really move. Figures turning their heads. Figures raising their hands. And at the top of the doors, the carved sun cracked down the middle and opened, and light came out of it, and the light was the color of the ember, the color of the memory-woman's burning temple, the color of the first thing Wendell had ever seen and forgotten.

The doors parted without a sound.

Beyond them: darkness, and warmth, and air that smelled of cedar smoke and rain on hot stone and something else, something vast and patient, something that had been waiting so long that waiting had become its native country.

Isandro caught up to them, chest heaving, sword drawn out of pure habit. "Wendell—"

"Inside," Wendell said. "Both of us. Now."

They crossed the threshold together as the first torchlight crested the shelf behind them, and the doors began, with the same soundless inevitability, to close.

Through the narrowing gap, Wendell saw Serah reach the edge of the stone shelf. She was alone — the rest of the Choir strung out below her, climbing — and she was not running. She stood in the dying light with snow in her grey hair and her arms slightly raised, and she was smiling, and she called out, not in triumph but in something horribly like welcome:

*"Come home, child. We only want what you're carrying."*

The doors met. The light of her torches vanished. And the silence that followed was the deepest silence Wendell had ever stood inside — until, out of it, the god spoke.

* * *

It did not speak the way people speak. There was no mouth, no direction, no before and after to it. The voice simply *was*, the way temperature is, the way weight is — everywhere in the dark at once, and inside Wendell's skull, and inside the ember, all three at once.

***I know that voice,*** it said, and the knowing was gentle and enormous and sad. ***I sang it over you when you were new.***

Wendell's knees nearly went. Isandro caught their elbow, and his hand was shaking too, though his voice, when he found it, held: "What — what is this place? What are you?"

***I am the one whose name your maps erased,*** the god said. ***I am the one your guild was founded to jail. I am the fire under the mountain and the reason the passes stay open and the crops come green to the valley, and I have slept in this stone while the world above forgot me so thoroughly that even my jailers' children thought they were carrying an heirloom.***

Light bloomed — slowly, reluctantly, like dawn deciding whether to bother. It came up out of the floor of a chamber so vast that its far walls were weather, its ceiling a slow drift of luminous dust. They were standing on a causeway of black glassy stone that ran out over a drop filled with soft golden glow, and the glow pulsed, and each pulse matched the beat of the ember against Wendell's ribs.

And in the light, images hung in the air like reflections on disturbed water. The burning temple — the woman with Wendell's face standing before it, arms raised, not fleeing. A circle of robed figures — the first Wayfinders, young-faced and grim — driving stakes of cold iron around a sleeping shape of fire. Sefu, decades younger, kneeling in this very chamber, weeping. And threaded through all of it, fainter, older: a lullaby. A melody with no words that Wendell realized, with a lurch, they had been humming their whole life without ever learning it.

"That song," Wendell whispered. "I've always known that song."

***You learned it where you were born,*** the god said. ***In this room. From me.***

"Then tell me." Wendell's voice cracked down the middle. They were crying and hadn't noticed starting. "Tell me the name. You said the doors would answer, you said — everyone died for this, Vale died, Sefu died, the whole guild burned, and I have carried you across a kingdom on a lie about myself, and I am *owed* a name."

The warmth gathered. The light steadied. And the god gave them what they asked for — but softly, the way you wake a sleeper, the way you say a thing you have not said aloud in six hundred years:

***Vaelith. Ember-of-the-First-Hearth. Mother-of-Passes. I am Vaelith, and I am waking, and waking hurts.***

The name landed in Wendell's chest and detonated quietly. Because they knew it. Not from any book — from the guild charter, from the line Sefu had made them memorize as a child and never explained: *bound to protect the heart of Vaelith until the gods wake.* They had recited it ten thousand times. They had thought it was just an old word.

Behind them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the hymn started again. Closer now. The doors had not stopped the Choir; nothing would stop the Choir; Serah had spent a god-fragment to smell the ember and she would dig through the mountain itself.

Isandro drew breath to speak — to warn, to plan, to do the brave practical things he always did — and the god spoke over him, gently, and only to Wendell:

***Child. My child. Before they come, and they are coming, and there is so little time — I must ask you, because no one has asked me in six hundred years and no one has ever asked you at all:***

The light bent toward Wendell like a face leaning close.

***What do you want?***

Not *what will you do with me*. Not *restore me or destroy me*. Not duty, not the charter, not Sefu's last instruction, not Vale's last breath. Just that. One question, hanging in the golden dark, addressed to the one person in the world who had never once been allowed to answer it.

Wendell stood on the causeway with blood drying on their palm and the whole war of heaven and earth pressing at the doors behind them, and opened their mouth to answer —

— and the far wall of the chamber split open with a sound like the world's spine breaking, and the Ashen Choir poured through, singing.
