## 18. Serah Ascendant

The mountain had been screaming for an hour before Serah understood what it was saying.

She had climbed the Pass of Teeth with forty of the faithful, and the storm had tried to refuse them — wind like a wall of knives, snow that fell sideways, the kind of cold that did not kill quickly but argued with the body, joint by joint, about whether it wished to continue. Three of her people had been taken by the mountain. She had not wept for them. Weeping was a currency she had stopped carrying decades ago, back when she learned that grief spent on the dead bought nothing from the living.

But the mountain was screaming now, and beneath the scream there was a voice.

Serah had heard gods before. That was what made her dangerous, and she knew it. Most people went their whole lives without feeling the pressure of divine attention — that vast, indifferent weight, like being noticed by something too large to have a face. She had felt it at nine years old, in a village shrine in the drought country, when the local river-god's priest had laid hands on her dying mother and *nothing had happened*. The god had been right there. She had felt it listening, felt its attention settle over the shrine like a hand over a candle flame, and it had done nothing. Her mother had died with the god's silence in her mouth.

That was the day Serah understood the truth that the Ashen Choir was built upon: gods were not parents. They were landlords. And the rent was always paid by tenants who could not move out.

Now, climbing through the last switchback below the Hollow Temple, she felt that same attention again — but wrong, inverted, *fading*. The ember was being carried toward the temple's heart, and the god inside it was waking, and waking things reached outward, and everything they touched dimmed.

"Mother." Dainn, her strongest acolyte, half-carrying a limping sister on his back. His lips were blue. "The pass ahead — the soldiers—"

"I know."

Crown soldiers held the narrow throat of the pass above them, maybe thirty of them, dug in behind the rocks with crossbows. Their captain — dead now, Serah assumed; the fighting below had been vicious — had bought time for two fugitives and an ember, and the price of that purchase was still being counted in blood on the stones.

It did not matter. Let the Crown spend its soldiers. The Choir had spent three centuries learning how to walk through armies, because the Choir had understood something the Crown never would: you cannot guard a god. You can only delay the question of what to do with it.

"Form the line," Serah said. "Hymn of Unmaking. Full voice."

Dainn stared at her. "In this wind? Against crossbows?"

"The wind is theirs," Serah said. "The stone is ours."

They formed up in the lee of a granite spine, forty voices ragged with cold and altitude, and they sang.

The Hymn of Unmaking was not beautiful. It had never been meant to be. It was built from the oldest prayers in the Choir's archive — prayers that predated the Godswar, prayers mortals had raised to gods before they learned fear — except inverted, every petition turned inside out. Where the ancients had sung *hear us*, the Choir sang *be heard no longer*. Where they had sung *sustain us*, the Choir sang *sustain yourself elsewhere*. It was a song that asked a god, politely and in perfect harmony, to stop existing.

And the stone listened.

Serah felt it happen the way you feel a tooth loosen — a small wrongness, a shift. The crossbow bolts came down the pass and the Choir walked into them singing, and the rocks themselves seemed to hesitate to hurt them. Two sisters fell. The rest did not break stride. And when the first rank of soldiers saw forty people walking upright through a killing ground as if the mountain had forgotten how to be cruel, several of them simply ran. Not cowardice. Older instinct than that. The instinct of animals when the rules of the world bend.

They took the pass in eleven minutes. Serah stood among the bodies of the Crown's soldiers — so young, most of them, faces she might have fed at one of the Choir's soup-houses in another life — and permitted herself four breaths of stillness. Then she looked up.

The Hollow Temple crouched against the sky like a broken jaw. Black basalt, older than any kingdom, its spire snapped off centuries ago, its doors — she could see them even from here — standing open. Open, and glowing faintly from within, gold light spilling down the steps like something spilled.

Someone had already opened the doors.

"Mother," Dainn breathed beside her. "Is that—"

"That is a god waking up," Serah said. "And we are going to put it back to sleep forever. Move."

* * *

Inside the temple, the world was wrong in the way deep places are wrong.

The corridors were vast and then suddenly intimate, ceilings that should have been fifty feet overhead pressing close, walls breathing heat. The air tasted of iron and honey. Every surface was carved — not decorated, *carved*, the way a scar is carved — with names in a script no living scholar could read, and the script moved when you weren't looking directly at it. Serah caught glimpses: letters rearranging themselves, sentences rewriting, as if the temple were a book someone was still editing.

Her people pressed close behind her, torches guttering in air that seemed to pull inward, toward the heart of the mountain. Twice the corridor forked, and both times Serah chose correctly without knowing why — some animal sense, or perhaps the opposite of an animal sense, the part of her that had spent thirty years unlearning reverence and could therefore feel its gravity, the way a sailor feels the shore he has renounced.

They found the first body at the third turning.

A Crown soldier, young, killed cleanly — not by the Choir. Something else had passed through here. And beyond him, in the great chamber ahead, Serah heard voices. Two voices. Arguing.

She knew one of them. The reports had described the Wayfinder apprentice thoroughly enough: Wendell Oyelaran, twenty-some years, guild-trained, carrying the ember in a warded case against their chest. What the reports had not described was the sound of their voice when it echoed off the walls of a god's own temple — steady, exhausted, and underneath the steadiness something raw, the tone of a person arguing with a parent.

The other voice was not a voice. It came from everywhere, from the walls, from the floor, from the light itself, and it was old the way bedrock is old.

*"...asked you what you want, child. Not what the guild wanted. Not what the Crown fears. What do* ***you*** *want?"*

"I want—" Wendell's voice cracked and reassembled. "I want everyone to stop deciding for me. I want my memories back. I want Sefu alive. I want—" A breath. "I don't know what I want. That's the honest answer. Everyone keeps asking me to have an answer before I've even seen the question."

*Then see it,* said the god. *See all of it.*

And the chamber lit.

Serah rounded the last pillar into the temple's heart in time to watch it happen: light rising off the floor like heat-shimmer made solid, gold and white and threaded with veins of something darker, violet-black, pulsing. In the center of the chamber, on a dais of fused glass where the god's altar had once stood, stood Wendell Oyelaran — arms slightly away from their body, the warded case open in their hands, the ember bare at last.

It was not a coal. Serah had read the Choir's oldest accounts, and every one of them described a coal, a heart-sized cinder, dull until stirred. This was not dull. This was a wound in the shape of a sun. Looking at it directly hurt in a way that had nothing to do with brightness — it hurt the way truth hurts, the way a name you've been avoiding hurts when someone finally says it aloud.

And around the ember, hanging in the light like sediment in disturbed water, were images. Memories. Serah saw them for half a heartbeat before her training closed her eyes to them: a woman with Wendell's face before a burning temple. A guildhouse full of people laughing. A rope bridge in rain. A kiss. A life, whole and continuous, that belonged to no single person in this room because it belonged to all of them at once.

The god was showing its child everything.

"There they are," Dainn whispered. "Mother, the rite—"

"Not yet." Serah's voice was iron. "Look at them, Dainn. Really look. That one is deciding whether to become a god. If we interrupt at the wrong moment, we don't get a rite — we get a war with a newborn divinity at the center of it, on its home ground, inside a temple that answers to its blood." She watched Wendell's face across the chamber — watched the terror and the longing fight there, watched the person stand at the edge of their own annihilation and lean forward instead of back. "No. We wait for the moment after. The moment of commitment. When the choice is made but not yet sealed. That is when a god is softest. That is when we take it."

"And if there is no moment after?" a sister asked quietly. "If the merging completes?"

Serah did not answer immediately. Across the chamber, Wendell raised the ember higher, and the light rose with it, and the god's voice filled the temple like water filling a cup:

*Then let it be done in love, little ember. Let it be done in love, and I will not call it a cage.*

"No," Serah said softly. "There will be a moment after. There is always a moment after. Love hesitates, children. Love looks back. Devouring does not. On my word — and not one breath before — we begin."

* * *

The moment came sooner than she expected, and from the wrong direction.

It came as footsteps — running, uneven, desperate — and a voice hoarse from shouting: "WENDELL!"

Isandro.

Serah had studied the exiled priest for months. Files, reports, the testimony of a defector from Vael's Crossing. An interesting specimen: a man who remembered a life he had never lived, whose prayers had gone unanswered for three hundred years until one day something answered back. The Choir's scholars had theories about him. Broken vessel. Residual vessel. A man hollowed out by the Godswar and refilled with someone else's devotion. Serah's private theory was simpler: he was a man who had loved a god once, in whatever form love took before the world was rewritten, and the god had never bothered to give him back.

He burst into the chamber with blood on his temple and grief all over his face, and he went straight to Wendell as if forty armed zealots were furniture.

"You're doing it," he said. "You're actually — Wendell, wait, just — look at me first. One minute. One minute and then if you still want this, I'll—" His voice failed. He knelt. Right there, in the god-light, he knelt in front of Wendell Oyelaran like a man at an altar, which was exactly what he was. "I broke my vows for you. I'll break this too, if you ask. But don't do this alone. Whatever you choose, don't do it alone."

Wendell looked down at him. And in that look — Serah saw it clearly, even across the chamber, even through the light — in that look, love hesitated.

Love looked back.

"Now," Serah said.

The Choir moved as one body, because they had drilled this for a decade. Forty faithful fanned out along the chamber's rim, each finding their mark-stone — the ancient anchor-points set into the floor by the Choir's founders, who had mapped this temple before the guild ever existed. The marks had waited three hundred years under dust and basalt, and now the Choir's hymn woke them, and lines of ash-pale fire crawled between them, drawing a circle within the god's circle, a snare within a sanctuary.

The Hymn of Unmaking rose to full voice.

The effect was immediate and obscene. The golden light *curdled*. The images hanging in the air — the burning temple, the laughing guildhall, the kiss in the rain — shredded like smoke in wind. Wendell cried out, staggering, the ember flaring wildly in their hands, and the god's voice fractured mid-word:

*No — no, you cannot — this is* ***my*** *child —*

"This," Serah said, striding into the circle's threshold, "is a prison transfer. Nothing more. You were jailed once, old one. We are simply demolishing the jail *and* the prisoner together, so no one builds either again."

She crossed the threshold.

Every step cost her. The god's attention fell on her fully for the first time — that vast, faceless weight — and it pressed on her like deep water, and inside the pressure she heard it speak *to her alone*, in a register below sound:

*I know you, little ash. I knew your mother's mother. I remember the drought. I remember your prayer.*

Serah stopped.

For one lurching instant she was nine years old again, kneeling in a shrine that smelled of incense and failure, holding her mother's cooling hand, praying to a god that was listening and did nothing.

*You prayed,* said the god. *Did I answer?*

"No," Serah said aloud, and her voice carried across the chamber, and every one of her faithful heard it. "You didn't. You never do. That's why we're here."

*And yet,* said the god, and there was something almost gentle in it, something that made her want to scream, *you became a prayer yourself. Everything you have done, little ash — every hymn, every march, every grave — has been a prayer that someone, somewhere, will finally make the world safe. You want what I want. You simply cannot forgive me for failing to deliver it.*

"Enough." She forced her legs forward. The ember lay where Wendell had dropped it — dropped, not surrendered; the apprentice was on their knees at the circle's center, held there by the hymn, light pouring off them like blood off a wound. Serah reached down and took the ember in both hands.

It burned. Of course it burned. She welcomed it. Pain was the Choir's first sacrament — proof of a body that obeyed, proof of a will that pain could not redirect. She lifted the ember above the dais, above the fused-glass altar where three hundred years ago her order's founders had performed this rite for the last time, and she began the words.

*"Ash to ash. Flame to void. What was worshipped, be unworshipped. What was feared, be unfelt. Old one, old liar, old landlord — we return your lease. We return your temples. We return your silence, compounded with interest, unto the final generation—"*

The mountain began to die.

She felt it before she understood it — a vibration in the floor, a groan in the walls, and then the temple itself started coming apart in slow motion, stone flaking into grey powder, the carvings on the walls fading like breath off glass. Outside — she knew without seeing — the snowline was receding, the glaciers slumping, the pines at the mountain's foot browning as if a year of autumn were passing in a minute. The god was not merely being unmade. Its unmaking was draining the land that had grown up around its long imprisonment, roots and all. The Choir's scholars had warned of this possibility in a footnote nobody had read closely.

Serah kept chanting. What else was there? Retreat meant the god lived and the war came anyway, later, on worse terms. Forward meant the world lost a mountain and gained a permanent freedom. She had buried too many people to flinch at adding geology to the ledger.

*"—unto the final generation. Be unremembered. Be unnamed. Be—"*

And then Isandro stood up.

He had been kneeling at the circle's edge, watching the woman he loved get crushed slowly under a hymn, and something in him had finished breaking. Later — if there was a later — he would understand what happened next. In the moment, it was simpler than theology: he could not watch. So he did the only thing left. He stepped into the circle, into the hymn's killing focus, and he began to pray.

Not Choir prayers. Not his order's prayers. He barely believed in anything anymore — the files said so, his own confessions said so. What came out of him was older than belief. Fragments. Half-remembered liturgy in a language the Godswar had erased, words he shouldn't have known, stitched together with plain human desperation: *Hold. Hold. Hold.* He spoke them against the hymn like a man shouting into a hurricane, and impossibly, horribly, wonderfully — the circle heard him.

The ash-pale firelines flickered. Bent. Held — barely, wrongly, warped around the shape of a faithless priest's stubbornness. The devouring rite stuttered. The god's unmaking slowed. The mountain's death slowed with it, from a plunge to a bleed.

Serah watched him hold the circle and felt something she had not felt in thirty years: doubt. Not of her cause — of its sufficiency. Because here was the thing she could not argue with, the thing her entire philosophy had no shelf for: Isandro did not believe. He was praying on wreckage, on habit, on love, and the circle *held anyway*. As if the old words worked regardless of the mouth. As if faith had never been the point — only the speaking.

If that was true, then the gods had never needed worship. Then the drought-god's silence hadn't been indifference. Then her mother had died under a listening sky that simply, terribly, could not act — and everything Serah had built, every pyre and every hymn, had been built on a misreading of a silence.

She crushed the thought. There would be time to be wrong later, if the world survived her being right.

"Hold the circle!" she screamed at her faithful, and they surged forward to drag Isandro out, and he fought them, and Wendell dragged themselves upright through the light with their hands closing on empty air where the ember had been—

—and the chamber went dark.

All at once. Total. The god-light gone, the hymn's firelines gone, the torches drowned in something thicker than shadow. In the black, Serah stood gripping an ember that had stopped burning — gone cool in her hands, cool as a dead hearth, and her skin crawled with the certainty that coolness was worse than any fire.

Somewhere in the darkness, a voice she couldn't locate said, very quietly:

*You're holding my heart, little ash.*

*Be careful with it.*

*My child is coming to take it back.*
