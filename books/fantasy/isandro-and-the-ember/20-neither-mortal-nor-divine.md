## 20. Neither Mortal nor Divine

I woke to the sound of weeping.

Not Isandro's — though he was there, kneeling beside me in the ruined chamber, his hand pressed flat against my chest as if he could hold my heart in place by will alone. The weeping came from everywhere. From the broken stones. From the mountain's roots. From a hundred throats I could not see, scattered across valleys and villages and prison cells and birthing rooms, each of them whispering into the dark the way people do when they have given up on being heard.

*Please. Please. Please.*

I had heard prayers before, of course — or rather, I had heard people say them, the words passing through me like weather. Now they passed *into* me. Each one arrived with its whole weight attached: the cracked voice of a shepherd counting his dead sheep in the snow, the furious bargaining of a mother over a fevered child, the wordless ache of a man who could not remember why he knelt but could not stop kneeling. They did not demand answers. Most of them didn't even expect them. That was the worst part — how many prayers were spoken purely for the comfort of saying them, like letters addressed to no one.

"Isandro," I said.

My voice came out wrong. Not damaged — *layered*. When I spoke, something vast spoke underneath me, an echo with no gap between it and my own throat, so that every word sounded like two voices agreeing perfectly. He flinched at the sound of it. Then he saw my eyes open, and whatever fear he had broke apart into something else entirely, and he gathered me up off the temple floor and held me so hard I felt his heartbeat hammering against mine — one heart now, and one that was not a heart at all but a small captive sun learning to keep time.

"You died," he said into my hair. "For eleven minutes you were — there was nothing. Wendell, there was nothing where you'd been."

"I know." And I did know, in the strange way I now knew everything that had ever been known inside this mountain: I remembered the moment of merging from both sides. I remembered being the girl holding the ember, and I remembered being the ember held. I remembered the god's last thought before it let go — not terror, not relief, but a question asked across three thousand years of sleep: *what will you make of me?*

"I'm still here," I said. "That's the answer. I'm still here."

He pulled back far enough to look at me, and I watched him take inventory the way a Wayfinder reads a trail — the silver threading through my irises, the faint warmth radiating off my skin, the way the dust in the air drifted toward me instead of settling. His face did what his face always did: showed me everything before he decided whether to say any of it.

"It doesn't hurt?"

"No." That was true, and it was also the strangest thing about me now. The grief was all still there — Sefu, Vale, the guildhouse ash, the childhood that had been cut out of me and sewn shut — but it sat in me without biting. As if someone had finally taught the pain what it was for.

Beyond him, the chamber told the rest of the story. The Choir's devouring rite lay shattered mid-circle, its chalk lines scorched to glass, its relics — the teeth and bones and bottled screams they had carried across half a continent — reduced to ordinary dust. Serah's zealots lay where the light had thrown them, unconscious, breathing. The mountain itself had stopped dying; I could feel it the way I felt my own pulse now, a slow green patience reasserting itself through miles of stone, frost retreating from roots, water remembering which way down was.

And in the center of it all, on her knees in the wreckage of her certainty, Mother Serah was awake, and watching me, and *silent*.

* * *

They bound her hands out of habit more than necessity. She let them. Her followers woke one by one, saw what stood in the chamber wearing Wendell Oyelaran's face, and knelt — some of them weeping, some of them laughing, one old man pressing his forehead to the floor and chanting a name in a language that predated every map I'd ever drawn. The name was mine. It was also the god's. It was also, apparently, a third thing neither of us had been alone.

"Stop," I said.

The chanting stopped. So did the wind outside. So, briefly, did my own breath.

That was when I understood what I had become, and what I had to refuse.

Isandro saw it land on me. "Wendell?"

"They're going to worship me." My layered voice made even dread sound like prophecy. "That's what happens next. That's what has *always* happened next. Someone carries a piece of the divine, and people kneel, and the kneeling becomes doctrine, and doctrine becomes an order, and orders burn things. Sefu knew. That's why the guild hid me. Not just from the Crown. From *this*." I looked around at the bowed heads, the wet eyes, the terrible hungry tenderness on their faces. "The Choir spent everything trying to devour a god because gods are power you can take. And these people would spend everything building me a cage of gold and rules, because gods are power you can give yourself to. Same hunger. Different hands."

Serah lifted her head. Her conviction — that absolute, load-bearing faith that had carried her through the sacrifice of a god-fragment and the deaths of her own faithful — was gone, and what remained underneath was worse than hatred. It was a woman staring at the smoking ruin of the only architecture her life had ever had.

"You're going to kill me," she said. It wasn't a plea. It was almost a hope.

"No."

"You should. Every faith I burned, every fragment we devoured, Vale's soldiers on the pass — I did it all certain I was saving the world from masters. And now the world has a new one standing right here, and she's *kind*, which is infinitely worse—"

"I said stop." The chamber shook. Dust fell. Every person in the room went rigid, because the second voice under mine had spoken too, and it was the voice of something that had once shaken mountains for lesser reasons. I closed my eyes and pulled it back, gently, the way you gentle a horse that wants to fight. "I'm sorry. That's exactly what I mean. That's what I *am* now, if I'm careless. If I'm worshipped. A careless god is just a war with better lighting."

I crouched in front of Serah. Up close she smelled of ash and cold iron and thirty years of other people's prayers, all of them answered with fire.

"Here is what happens now," I told her, and made sure the human voice led and the deep one only hummed beneath it. "You will walk out of this mountain. You will go wherever you want — south, over the sea, into some desert where no one's ever heard of the Hollow Temple. You will live a long life, because I am not in the business of killing women who were, in their own arithmetic, doing mathematics with the end of the world. But your rite is dead. Your fragments are dust. Your doctrine—" I reached out and touched her bound hands, lightly, and let her feel what I felt: the vast quiet thing inside me, awake, aware, and *declining*. "Your doctrine dies today. Because the god you wanted to devour and the god they wanted to enthrone are the same god, and she has chosen a third door, and the door is me, and I am not accepting worshippers."

"You can't refuse faith," Serah whispered. "Faith doesn't ask permission."

"It does now."

I stood. And then I did the thing I had already known I would do since the moment I woke weeping with the world — the thing that was, I suspected, the entire reason the ember had chosen me over three thousand years of worthier vessels. I opened myself fully to the prayers still arriving, hundreds of them, thousands, the whole desperate chorus of a continent that had learned to beg — and I answered.

Not with miracles. With refusal.

*I hear you,* I sent, into every kneeling heart within reach of the mountain's long attention. *I am real, and I hear you, and I will not be your master. No temples. No tithes. No wars fought in my name, no inquisitions, no chosen peoples, no punishments. Kneel to each other. Feed each other. That was always the whole of it. That was always what the old ones forgot to say before your ancestors nailed them under mountains.*

It cost me nothing and everything. I felt prayers gutter out like lamps — not all of them, never all of them, people would keep talking into the dark because that's what people do — but the *hunger* went out of them. The appetite for a god to be right about. The permission structure of divinity, dismantled in a single breath. Somewhere below me I felt a hundred would-be prophets wake with empty hands. Somewhere above, in capitals I would never visit, doctrines quietly became stories, and stories became songs, and songs became the kind of thing you dance to instead of kill for.

The old man who had chanted my name lifted his head, looked at me with clear eyes for the first time, and smiled like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body. "Thank you," he said — and it was just a thank-you, human-sized, between two people. That was all.

Serah stayed kneeling a long moment. Then she laughed — one dry, cracked note with no joy in it, the laugh of a woman reading her own obituary and finding it accurate.

"A god that refuses worship," she said. "Do you know what that makes you?"

"Tired," I said. "Mostly tired."

"It makes you *nothing*. It makes all of this—" she gestured at the chamber, the mountain, the merged miracle of me "—nothing but a very powerful woman with opinions."

"Yes," I said. "That's precisely what it makes me. Welcome to the club. We have maps."

Something moved behind her eyes then — not conversion, I want to be clear about that, I don't think Serah converted to anything in her life except the shape of her own defeat. But she looked at me a moment longer, and whatever she saw — the guild apprentice, maybe, the caravan guide, the orphan with rewritten memories who had ended up holding the world's oldest inheritance and choosing to spend it on humility — it undid the last knot in her. She got to her feet unaided.

"The southern passes," she said flatly. "There's a valley beyond Greywater where the Choir took people who left. Farmers. Exiles. People who couldn't stomach what we became." A pause. "They'll need someone who knows how to build things that aren't altars."

"Go, then," I said. "Build."

She walked out past Isandro, past the broken circle, past the soldiers' wary rifles, and none of us stopped her, and I watched her go down the temple steps into the grey morning light — a zealot demoted to a laborer, which might be the only salvation anyone ever actually offered her. At the bottom of the stairs she paused, and did not look back, and said something too quiet for human ears.

But I wasn't human ears anymore. I heard it anyway.

*"Forgive me,"* she said, to no one, to the air, to whatever was listening. *"Or don't. I'll carry it either way."*

I chose to count it as the first honest prayer I'd ever received. I didn't answer it. Some things a person has to carry themselves.

* * *

Afterward there was the long, unglamorous work of aftermath, which no song will ever be written about and which took the better part of two days.

We buried Vale's soldiers on the eastern terrace, facing the pass they'd died holding. Isandro spoke the rites — his order's rites, though he'd tell you later he'd already decided to leave them, and I think he performed them one last time precisely because they deserved a good funeral even if the theology didn't. I stood among the graves feeling every name the way I now felt everything: completely. Thirty-one soldiers. I learned all their names from the memories of the survivors, and I said each one aloud, and when we finished, a young corporal with a bandaged arm looked up at me and asked, hesitantly, whether he should kneel.

"Eat first," I said. "Grieve second. Kneel never. That's the whole liturgy now."

He laughed, wetly, and went to get the stew.

The Choir's surviving faithful drifted away down the mountain in ones and twos, leaderless and disarmed, most of them looking less like fanatics than like people waking from a fever — blinking at the daylight, embarrassed, hungry. A handful stayed, asking questions. I answered what I could honestly, which mostly meant admitting how much I didn't know. What was I exactly? I didn't know. Could I die? Probably, though it seemed rude to test. Did the other sleeping gods feel me now? 

That one I answered truthfully, and it kept me up that night: yes. Far away, under other mountains, under the sea, under the frozen north — old hearts turned over in their long sleep, aware of me the way sleepers are aware of a lamp lit in another room. Awake-ish. Listening. *Waiting.* The Crown would want to know. The world would want to know. And I had made them a promise on a rope bridge in the rain, in front of a dying inquisitor with mud on her face: the gods' fate would be decided openly, not buried again.

Which meant, sooner or later, going down the mountain and starting the hardest work of all — politics.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight, Isandro built a fire in the temple's great hall, in a hearth that hadn't held flame since before the Godswar, and cooked the last of our food in an iron pan older than kingdoms, and we ate sitting on the steps of a dead god's altar like a pair of travelers waiting out weather.

"You're different," he said, watching me across the fire. "Since the merging. Quieter. But not smaller." He turned the pan's handle absently, a priest's fidget repurposed. "How much of you is still you?"

"All of it. That's the strange part." I fed a stick to the flames. "I expected to be diluted. Instead I feel — *wider*. Like a house where they've knocked out all the interior walls. Everything I was is still here. Sefu's lessons. The guild roads. The way you take your tea, which is criminally, unforgivably sweet." I looked up at him. "And everything the god was is here too. Three thousand years of watching mortals through a keyhole. Do you know what she — what *we* — wanted the whole time? Not worship. Not freedom, even, not really. Just to be *included*. To sit at a fire like this one. To be somebody's companion instead of somebody's answer."

He was quiet a while. Firelight moved on his face, and under it, faintly, I could see the other faces he'd worn across three hundred years — the priest he'd been made to forget, the exile he'd chosen, the man on the bridge holding the rope. I loved all of them. That was new information, delivered all at once, and I let it settle.

"Wendell." His voice had gone careful, the way it did before he said true things. "You refused a continent's worth of worship in an afternoon. You unmade a sect older than the Crown. You're going to walk off this mountain and renegotiate the architecture of civilization." He set down the pan. "So I need to ask, and I need you to answer honestly, because I've built my whole life on vows and I know exactly how heavy they get: what am I to you? Because if you're becoming something the world needs, I won't be a leash on you. I'll love you from a respectful distance, in a shrine, like a reasonable man."

"Isandro Vael." I crossed the space between us and took his face in my hands, and let him feel — openly, deliberately, no keyhole, no distance — everything I was. The god's patience and the apprentice's stubbornness and the orphan's ferocious, hoarded love, all of it aimed at him like sunlight through a lens. "I just spent the day refusing to be adored. Don't you dare offer yourself up as a sacrifice on the same afternoon. I don't want a worshipper. I don't want a priest. I want the infuriating man who argued theology with me in the rain and lost."

"I didn't lose that argument," he managed.

"You absolutely lost that argument." I kissed him, and the fire popped, and somewhere far below us the mountain sighed — a sound I recognized as laughter, because apparently I had inherited a sense of humor along with everything else.

When we broke apart, he rested his forehead against mine. "Then what do we do now?" he asked. "Truly. We're standing in a ruined temple at the top of the world, and you're — whatever you are, and I'm about to be an apostate twice over. What does ordinary even look like for us?"

I looked past him, through the temple's shattered eastern wall, to where the solstice dawn was coming up gold over the lowlands — over Vael's Crossing, over Thornfell, over the black scar where the guildhouse had stood, over all the small bright lives that would wake today never knowing how close the world had come to burning. Down there was a guild to rebuild. Letters to write. A charter to amend, honestly this time. Prayers drifting up like smoke, gentle now, asking nothing.

"Ordinary looks like going home," I said. "And opening a school."

His eyebrows went up. "A school."

"For wayfinders." I smiled, and felt the whole mountain smile with me, and decided I'd better learn to keep that private. "Maps, mountains, memory — and this time we teach all of it. The true history. The names that were erased. How to read a road, and how to question whoever drew it." I laced my fingers through his. "Some things we inherit, Isandro. Some things we choose. I intend to choose loudly."

Below us, faint and far, the first bells of the solstice began to ring in the valley towns — and for the first time in three thousand years, nobody anywhere was ringing them for a god.

They were just ringing morning.
