## 21. What We Carry Down

The mountain let us go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long — slowly, finger by finger, with a reluctance that could be felt in the stone.

Isandro walked behind Wendell because that was where he had learned to walk: three paces back, close enough to catch them if they stumbled, far enough to give grief its privacy. The path down from the Hollow Temple was not a path so much as a suggestion the mountain had once made and mostly regretted. Scree slid under their boots. The wind came up the valley in long, exhausted breaths, carrying the smell of rain and cold iron and, faintly, impossibly, of woodsmoke and bread — the smell of the world below, of ordinary things still happening somewhere.

Wendell stopped at the first switchback and looked back.

Isandro did not. He knew what was up there. A door standing open on darkness. A chamber scorched clean by light. And in the middle of it, for one terrible moment before he'd closed his eyes, his Wendell — or someone wearing Wendell's face, taller somehow, brighter, with eyes like coals banked in ash — turning to look at him with an expression he would spend the rest of his life learning to read.

*Come with me,* they had said. Not *come with me up here.* Come *with me* — wherever this new road went, whatever it made of them.

He had said yes. It was the only vow he'd ever taken that felt true.

"Isandro." Wendell's voice came down the slope, roughened by wind and by everything else. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the back of my head like it owes you money."

Despite himself — despite the dead soldiers on the pass, despite Vale's body wrapped in her own grey cloak somewhere above them in the snow, despite Serah walking into exile with her sect shattered around her like a dropped vase — Isandro laughed. It came out cracked and surprised him more than the miracle had.

"I'm staring at the back of your head," he said, "because I'm afraid that if I look at your face, I'll find out you're not real."

Wendell turned. And there it was: their face, the same face he'd met on the high road a lifetime ago, wind-burned and tired, a cut healing over one eyebrow. But underneath the skin there was a light now, faint as dawn through shutters, and when they looked at him he felt it the way he used to feel prayer — a warmth arriving from somewhere else, addressed specifically to him.

"You're real," Wendell said. "That's the strange part. I keep waiting to wake up as the ember, or wake up as nothing, and instead I just... keep being me. Hungry. Sore. Afraid my feet are going to fall off." They flexed their boots in the scree, demonstratively. "Gods aren't supposed to have blisters, I assume."

"Do you know? Are there any left to ask?"

The question landed between them and sat there. Wendell looked away, up the slope, at the smoke still threading from the temple doors.

"No," they said quietly. "There's no one left to ask. That's what I am now. The last one." A pause. "Again. Apparently I collect the role."

They walked on. Three paces back, Isandro counted his blessings and found that they were all the same blessing, repeated.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a fallen pillar — one of the old waymarkers, Isandro realized, a boundary-stone from the First War, its carved god worn to a featureless suggestion of a face. Someone had sheltered here before them. Someone had always sheltered here before them; that was the whole lesson of the mountains.

Wendell slept first, which frightened Isandro until he understood it was trust, not exhaustion. They lay with their head on his rolled cloak, breathing slow, and the light under their skin rose and fell with each breath like a tide.

He should have prayed. For three hundred years — or however long it had been; the singer's true history had scrambled even his arithmetic — prayer had been the architecture of his days. Matins and vespers, the litany of the Ninefold Dawn, the kneeling, the incense, the beautiful machinery of devotion that had turned out to be built over a prison. Every hymn he'd ever sung, sung to a chained thing. Every confession he'd ever heard, heard in a house built by jailers.

He knelt anyway. Not to pray. To renounce.

He had no ritual for it. His order had rituals for everything — for ordination, for exile, for the washing of corpses — but not, he suspected, for this, because no one in the history of the Dawnbound had ever been honest enough to do it. So he made his own, there in the dark, with a sleeping god-child six feet away and a worn stone face watching him with no expression at all.

"I, Isandro of Vael's Crossing," he said, barely above a whisper, "release the Dawnbound, and release the Dawnbound from me. My vows were given to liars, in service of a lie, and I take them back. Not because I stopped believing in holiness." His voice caught. He let it catch. "Because I finally found some."

The words went out into the night air and did not echo. Nothing answered. No voice, no warmth, no sign. And that, too, felt like honesty — the old gods had never answered him either; only the ember ever had, only Wendell's heart, only love. Let silence be the seal on it. Let silence be enough.

When he opened his eyes, Wendell was awake and watching him.

"How much did you hear?" he asked.

"All of it." They sat up, hair wild, ember-light pooling gently in the hollows of their collarbones. "You didn't need to do that, you know. You already chose me. On the bridge, in the pass, in the temple when Serah was unmaking the world. The paperwork seemed redundant."

"Vows matter," Isandro said. "Even broken ones. Especially broken ones. If I don't name what I'm leaving, I'll spend years listening for it, calling me home." He rubbed his face. "I spent three centuries being someone else's instrument. I'd like to find out what I am when nobody's playing me."

"And what are you?"

He considered it seriously, the way he considered everything, which Wendell had once told him was either his best quality or his most infuriating one.

"Tired," he said. "In love. Unemployed."

Wendell laughed — really laughed, the sound cracking open in the cold air like ice on a spring river — and reached out and pulled him down beside them, and for a while neither of them said anything at all, and the mountain kept its counsel, and the stars did what stars do, which is burn without demanding anything of anyone.

* * *

Three days down, they reached the treeline, and the world changed its mind about them.

Green came back. Moss on the stones, resin on the wind, the small busy sounds of a forest that had never heard of gods or wars. Isandro found himself stopping to touch things — bark, ferns, the cold cheek of a stream — like a man confirming the reality of a country he'd been told was destroyed. Wendell watched him do it with an expression of fond exasperation.

"You grew up in a shrine," they said. "Trees are not new."

"Trees were *permitted*. There's a difference." He crouched at the stream and drank from cupped hands, and the water tasted like minerals and melt-snow and nothing else, and it was the best thing he had ever tasted, and he almost wept over it, and decided to let himself. "Everything in the order was permitted. Chosen for you, arranged for you, explained to you. I thought freedom would feel like falling. It feels like drinking water."

Wendell crouched beside him and drank too, and then sat back on their heels and was quiet for a long moment, looking down-valley, where the land opened toward the foothills and, beyond them, faint in the haze, the roofs of a market town.

"They'll know," Wendell said. "Down there. People will look at me and know something's different. I can feel prayers already — little ones, half-formed. A woman two valleys over praying for her son's fever. A shepherd asking for rain. They're not praying to me, they don't even know I exist, but the prayers are... *there*, and I can hear them, and I don't know what I'm supposed to do."

"What do you want to do?"

"Answer them." Wendell's hands curled in the grass. "And that terrifies me, because that's how it starts, isn't it? One answered prayer. Then altars. Then orders, and doctrines, and people telling other people what I want. Then someone decides I'm a prisoner, or a weapon, or a harvest." They looked at him. "Serah wasn't wrong about everything, Isandro. That's the worst part. She saw the trap clearly. She just couldn't imagine any way out of it except burning the whole thing down."

"So don't build the trap."

"It's not that simple—"

"It never is. But—" Isandro took their hand, unfolded the fingers one by one, laced his own through them. "I was a priest for three hundred years. I know exactly how traps get built, because I helped maintain several. They start with intermediaries. With mystery hoarded by the few. With answers that come through institutions instead of directly." He turned their joined hands over, studying them like a map. "So refuse to be mysterious. Refuse altars. When someone prays, answer them plainly, or don't answer at all. Be a person who happens to hear. Not a power. Powers get managed. Persons get loved."

Wendell stared at him. "You've been thinking about this."

"I've been thinking about it since the temple. Since you refused to be worshipped and the Choir's rite just — unraveled. Did you see Serah's face? She had a knife at the throat of a god and the god said *no thank you* and the knife became meaningless. Her whole cosmology died in that moment, and she couldn't even hate you properly, because you hadn't defeated her. You'd just... declined." He smiled, small and wondering. "I think it was the most holy thing I've ever witnessed. And I witnessed it performed by someone who was complaining about blisters twelve hours earlier."

"Don't let that get around," Wendell said. "I have a reputation. Terrifying divine entity. Very ominous."

"Your secret dies with me."

They walked on. The valley widened. Somewhere below, a bell rang — a market bell, mundane, marking nothing but the hour — and both of them stopped to listen to it as if it were the most remarkable sound in creation.

* * *

The town was called Ferrow's Rest, and it had a shrine — a small one, a wayside chapel with a leaking roof and a bell with a crack in it, dedicated to the Ninefold Dawn out of habit rather than conviction. Isandro stood across the street from it for a long time, in the mud, holding a bag of bread and cheese he'd bought with the last of Vale's coin.

"That's yours," Wendell said. "Was yours. Whatever it is."

"Yes."

"You could go in. Light a candle. Out of nostalgia."

"I could." He didn't move. "But I keep thinking about what I'd pray for, and I don't need an intermediary anymore. I live with the answer to every prayer I'll ever have. She's standing next to me eating cheese."

Wendell made a noise that was half laugh, half strangled emotion, and covered it with the bread.

Instead of the shrine, they took a room at the inn — one room, and Isandro paid for it without flinching, because some economies of modesty had died on the mountain along with everything else. The room had a bed wide enough for two, a window over the stable yard, and a water jug, and Wendell washed the mountain off themselves with the concentration of someone performing surgery, and Isandro sat on the bed and watched the evening light move across the floor and tried to imagine tomorrow.

Not the solstice. Not the war-that-didn't-happen. Just tomorrow. Breakfast. A road. Where were they going? What did people do?

"Wendell," he said, when they were both clean and horizontal and the lamp was low. "What do we do now? Genuinely. I have no idea. My entire life was structure. Bells told me when to eat, when to kneel, when to sleep. Take the bells away and I'm — I'm a man sitting in a room."

Wendell was quiet long enough that he thought they'd fallen asleep. Then they shifted, propped themselves on an elbow, and the ember-light under their skin made their face glow faintly in the dark, like a lamp behind parchment.

"Sefu had a plan," they said. "Did I tell you? In the vault, before everything. The guild charter — 'until the gods wake.' Sefu read that clause differently than the founders intended. Not *until the gods wake and we hand them back.* Until the gods wake *and the world decides what it wants.* Sefu believed the guild's real job was the part after. Rebuilding honestly. Teaching people the true maps — the roads, the histories, the names that got erased." They traced a pattern on the blanket, the old wayfinder's knot. "Sefu died believing I'd carry that. And I want to. But I can't do it alone, and I can't do it as a god, because a god who runs a school is just a new kind of cage with better lighting."

"So be a person who runs a school."

"Who happens to hear prayers."

"Who happens to hear prayers," Isandro agreed. "Gently. Occasionally. With a strong preference for questions over commandments."

Wendell's mouth curved. "You've given this a lot of thought for a man who didn't know what breakfast looks like."

"Breakfast I understand. Bread exists. The future was the hard part." He reached over and moved a strand of hair from their face, and the gesture was so ordinary it nearly broke him open. "Rebuild the guild. Teach the true maps. Let me keep the shrine — not the Dawnbound's shrine, a new one, or maybe just a room with a kettle where people can say things out loud. And when prayers drift our way, we decide together which ones to answer. Two people. No doctrine. No choir."

"No choir," Wendell said, with feeling. "Never again anything called a choir."

Outside, the cracked bell of the wayside chapel rang the hour, sour and sweet at once, and neither of them moved to go and fix it.

* * *

They left Ferrow's Rest at dawn, on the road north, toward Thornfell — toward the burned guildhouse and the graves that needed making and the four-member guild that was now, depending on how you counted, one member, one ex-priest, and a god in a walking body who insisted on being counted as staff.

The morning was clear. The mountains stood behind them, white against blue, keeping their secrets the way mountains do — which is to say, openly, for anyone willing to climb.

Isandro walked beside Wendell now. Not three paces back. Beside. It changed the shape of the journey entirely; he could see their face, and they could see his, and when the road forked they chose together, standing in the intersection like fools, arguing cheerfully about which way the old maps lied.

At the second fork, Wendell stopped suddenly, head tilting, the way they did now when a prayer arrived.

"Fever," they murmured. "A child, in a village east of here. Small prayer. Half-asleep mother, doesn't even believe in anything specific, just — *please.*" They looked at Isandro. "Do we?"

He thought about it. Really thought, the way they'd agreed to — no reflexes, no doctrine, just two people deciding.

"We arrive tonight," he said. "We knock on the door like travelers. We ask for soup. And by morning, if the fever breaks, it breaks, and if it doesn't, we stay anyway and hold her hand. No miracles announced. No altars. Just neighbors."

"Just neighbors," Wendell repeated softly. And then, wonderingly, like someone reading their own name written somewhere unexpected: "I think that might be the holiest thing anyone's ever proposed to me."

"I've had three hundred years to prepare material."

They turned east together, onto a road that appeared on none of the old maps and all of the new ones Wendell was already drawing in their head, and the sun came up over the foothills, and the prayers of the world drifted past them like weather — and for the first time in three hundred years, Isandro walked into the day belonging to no order, no god, and no lie, carrying nothing he hadn't chosen.

Though that night, in a village called Marle's Ford, holding the hand of a sleeping stranger while her daughter's fever broke like a storm passing, he would notice something he had no explanation for: a candle burning in the wayside shrine behind them, though no one had lit it.

And the next village over, another.

And the one after that.

Small flames, appearing unbidden, wherever they passed — not altars, not yet, just lights, as if the world itself had begun, quietly and without permission, to keep watch over them both.
