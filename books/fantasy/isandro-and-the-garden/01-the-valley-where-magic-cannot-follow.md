## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The frost came early that year, and Wendell Thornbury met it at the gate with a basket of burlap and twine.

She had been awake since the grey hour before dawn, when the valley still held its breath and the mountains on either side were nothing but darker shapes against the dark. By the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge and laid its first thin gold across the walled garden, she had already wrapped the fig tree in sacking, banked straw around the roots of the rosemary, and carried the potted lemons into the cold frame one by one, their leaves brushing her wrists like small hands asking to be held.

It was work her grandmother had done before her, and her grandmother's mother before that, back through a line of gardeners so old that no one in the valley could say anymore when it began. The Thornbury guild — what remained of it, which was one woman of seventy-three winters and one granddaughter of twenty-two — kept the last walled garden in Merrowdale Vale. Everyone said so. Everyone said it with a kind of fond, half-puzzled respect, the way people speak of things they have stopped questioning: *the Thornburys and their garden, the Thornburys and their walls.*

No one asked why the walls were there.

Wendell knelt beside the beds where the winter lettuce slept under cloches of blown glass and pressed her bare fingers into the soil, just to feel it. The earth was cold but not dead — never dead, not here, not even in the deepest weeks of winter, though Wendell had long ago learned not to wonder too loudly about that either. And beneath her touch, faint as breath on a window, the soil *answered*. A warmth rose through the black loam into her palms, a slow green pulse like a heartbeat heard through a wall, and where her fingers had rested, the dirt shimmered. Just for a moment. Just a gleam, as if someone had scattered ground pearl among the clods.

She pulled her hand back and curled her fingers closed, and the shimmer faded, and the garden was only a garden again.

*Careful,* her grandmother always said, though she had never needed to. *Careful, child. Hands in your pockets when folk come by.*

Wendell did not know what her gift was called. There was no word for it in the valley, because there was no magic in the valley — everyone knew that too, the way everyone knew the walls were for keeping out deer. Magic belonged to the old stories, to the realm beyond the mountains that the maps marked with a border of little silver stars and no names. Whatever lived in Wendell's hands had never been given a name because naming it would mean admitting it existed, and admitting it existed would mean asking why it existed in a place where such things could not follow.

So she called it nothing. She hid it the way she hid everything else — behind competence, behind routine, behind the plain brown apron and the shears worn bright at the handles. She was simply Wendell Thornbury, gardener's apprentice, last apprentice, who grew the finest winter greens in three parishes and whose roses bloomed two weeks early every spring, which was luck, everyone agreed, or good soil, or the shelter of the walls.

The walls. She glanced up at them now as the light strengthened — old stone, older than any house in the vale, taller than seemed needful, furred with moss on the north side. Her grandmother had once told her, when Wendell was small enough to be lifted onto the wall's flat coping and dangle her heels over the herb beds, that the stones had been set by hands that knew songs no one sang anymore. Wendell had asked what songs. Her grandmother had gone quiet in that particular way of hers, the silence closing like a gate, and had said only: *Hand me that twine, love.*

That was how questions went in the Thornbury household. You learned to stop asking them, or you learned to ask them sideways, disguised as ordinary talk, and even then you got only slivers. Thirteen years of slivers. Thirteen years since the night her parents rode out of the valley and did not ride back, and her grandmother had stood in the gateway with a lantern held high until the oil burned out, and then had come inside and begun teaching Wendell the garden as if grief were a chore that could be scheduled between the pruning and the mulching.

Wendell had been nine. She remembered almost nothing of that night now except the smell of the lantern oil and the sound of her own name being spoken by her mother at the gate — not *Wendell*, but something else, something longer, half-swallowed, like a word from another language. When she tried to reach for the memory it slid away, smooth as a stone in deep water. She had stopped trying years ago. Some stones you let lie.

The morning bell of the parish church rang across the vale, thin and clear, and Wendell rose, brushed the soil from her knees, and went in to breakfast.

* * *

Her grandmother was already at the table in the low-beamed kitchen, sitting very straight despite the ache in her joints, her white hair braided and pinned with the severity of a woman who considered vanity a form of untidiness. Two bowls of porridge, a pot of honey, bread still warm from yesterday's baking. The same table, the same benches, the same iron hook by the hearth where the guild's tools hung — and above the hook, high up near the smoke-blackened rafter, the guild sigil carved into a plank of wood so old its grain had risen like waves frozen mid-break.

Wendell had looked at that sigil all her life without truly seeing it. It was a circle of interlaced branches, and within the circle, a seed. She had assumed it was simply what gardeners carved. It was only this autumn, scrubbing the beam for the harvest feast, that she had noticed the branches were not oak or ash or anything that grew in the valley. They had leaves like none she knew, slender and silver-veined, and the seed at the center had eight points, like a star, or like a flower seen from directly above.

She had meant to ask. She had not yet found the courage.

"Sit," her grandmother said, without looking up. "Eat while it's hot."

Wendell sat. For a while there was only the small music of spoons and the tick of the fire and, far off, a cock crowing in the village below.

"You felt it coming," her grandmother said at last. "The frost."

"I saw it in the leaves two days ago. The alder by the millstream turned before the others." Wendell hesitated. "And the garden told me. Sort of. I don't know how else to say it."

Her grandmother nodded slowly, as if Wendell had reported the weather. "Good. That's the gift working right — quiet, small, useful. A gardener's gift." She said the words the way she said her prayers: correctly, completely, and with her eyes fixed somewhere else. "Keep it that way."

"It's getting stronger." Wendell turned her bowl a quarter-turn on the table, an old nervous habit. "Grandmother, I have to tell you — the shimmer. It used to fade before I could hardly see it. Now it stays. Yesterday Mrs. Ellery came for her order of sage and I'd touched the cuttings not an hour before, and she said, *my, what a frost-glitter you've got on these*, and laughed, and I laughed too, but—"

"But it wasn't frost."

"No. It wasn't frost."

Her grandmother set down her spoon. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadow, and for a moment she looked less like an old woman than like something older wearing one — a stone outcrop that had decided, for convenience, to have a face. Then she sighed, and she was Grandmother again, tired and sharp and beloved.

"The vale is safe," she said. "Whatever the reason, whatever the old purpose of this place, the simple truth is that nothing of that other world can live here. It cannot follow you past the boundary stones. What you carry is a coal; the valley is wet snow. As long as we stay—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening around some word she would not spend. "As long as we stay, you are only a gardener. Remember that. Be only a gardener."

"And if I don't want to be?"

"Wanting," said her grandmother, "is how people get hurt."

She rose, stiffly, and began gathering the bowls, and the conversation was over, the way all their conversations ended — not concluded but abandoned, like a path that simply stops at a bramble hedge. Wendell washed the dishes and thought about the sigil on the beam and the eight-pointed seed, and outside the window the first true flakes of the season came down soft and silent over the garden walls, erasing the world's edges one by one.

* * *

Three days later, the herald came.

Wendell was in the village, at the market square, trading a crate of late apples and a bundle of dried herbs for salt and lamp-oil, when she heard the horse on the cobbles — a big grey destrier, war-trained, its hooves ringing in a way that made the market noise falter and dip. The rider wore the crown's livery, blue and silver, and behind him trotted two men-at-arms with the bored alertness of soldiers doing dull work in a place that had never given them trouble.

The herald dismounted in front of the moot-hall, unrolled a parchment tall as his arm, and nailed it to the ancient door with four strokes of a hammer that sounded, to Wendell, unnervingly like nails going into a coffin lid.

"By order of Her Majesty the Queen," he read, in a voice trained to carry across courtyards, "and for the safety and health of all her subjects: be it known that the affliction called the Gift is declared a peril to the realm. All persons possessing or suspected of possessing the Gift shall surrender themselves to crown authority within thirty days of this proclamation. Persons harboring or concealing the gifted shall be liable to the same judgment. So says the crown. So says the law."

He rolled the parchment, remounted, and rode on toward the next village, and the market breathed again and resumed itself — but differently, the way a room resumes after a stranger has passed through it. People went back to their haggling. They also, Wendell noticed, went back to looking at one another.

An affliction. She stood very still among the stalls with her basket on her arm, and the word went through her like a splinter working toward the bone. She had known, in the abstract, that the crown had grown strange these last years — that taxes had climbed, that travelers spoke of blighted crops in the lowlands, of rivers fouling, of children born with eyes the color of storm-light. But those were stories from beyond the passes, from the poisoned country where the covenant — *where what?* she thought, catching at the edge of her own mind — where something had gone wrong. Stories did not nail themselves to doors.

"Proclamation's been posted in six vales already," said Tam Osgar, the smith, to anyone listening, in the tone of a man performing unconcern. "They say the queen's got a sickness in the royal household. They say the gifted are blamed for it."

"They say a lot of things," said the alewife.

"They say there's a hunt forming. Proper hunt, with a captain out of the capital. Young fellow. Rell, the name was — some say he's vale-born himself, left as a boy for the crown's service."

The name went into Wendell like a needle finding an old scar. *Rell.* She saw him instantly, unbidden: a boy of eleven with skinned knees and a laugh like a thrown stone, racing her along the top of the garden wall — the only child ever allowed up there besides her — both of them shrieking as the drop yawned on either side, her grandmother's voice rising from below, furious and frightened: *Down! Both of you, down this instant!* He had gone away the next summer, taken by an uncle into the crown's service, and had written twice, and then not at all, and somewhere in the intervening years the boy had become Captain Rell of the hunt, and Wendell had become a woman who still, shamefully, sometimes dreamed about a wall and a laughing boy and a drop on either side.

*Persons harboring or concealing the gifted shall be liable to the same judgment.*

She walked home faster than she needed to, her basket bumping her hip, and the flakes of the earlier snow had melted but the cold remained, a clean hard cold that smelled of stone and coming winter. Behind her the village murmured. Ahead the road climbed through bare fields to the garden walls, grey against the grey sky, and she thought: *they are only walls, they are only walls, they are only walls* — the way one repeats a charm precisely because one no longer believes it.

* * *

Her grandmother was waiting in the gateway, and she had been crying.

It was such a shocking sight that Wendell stopped dead in the lane. In twenty-two years she had seen her grandmother angry, weary, silent, fierce, and once — the night the fever nearly took her — afraid. She had never seen her cry. The old woman's face was dry now, scrubbed hard, but the eyes were rimmed red and the hands gripping the gate's iron latch were trembling, and behind her, through the open gate, Wendell could see the kitchen door standing wide and the smell of smoke on the air that was not hearth-smoke.

"What is it?" Wendell said. "What's burning?"

"Come inside." Her grandmother's voice was level, which somehow was worse than tears. "We have much to do, and less time than I hoped. Thirty days." She laughed, a short terrible sound. "I had thought we might have years. I had thought perhaps never. Foolish, to think a debt can sleep forever."

"A debt?" Wendell stepped through the gate and her grandmother shut it behind them, shot the bolt — the bolt, which Wendell had never once seen drawn — and took her granddaughter's face in both cold hands.

"Wendell." Not the plain name. Something moved underneath it again, that half-swallowed other name, surfacing and sinking. "Listen to me. Tonight I will answer your questions. All of them, or as many as the hours allow. But first you must understand one thing, and hold it, whatever comes after." Her grip tightened. "The proclamation is not about sickness. It is not about the queen's fears, though her fears are real enough. It is about us. About the guild. About what we were, and what we failed to do, thirteen years ago this winter — the night your parents rode out and did not come home."

The lane, the walls, the falling light — everything tilted, quietly, the way the ground tilts in the moment before an earthquake announces itself properly. Wendell heard her own voice from a great distance.

"The covenant," she said. "You're going to tell me about the covenant."

Her grandmother closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were clear and dry and full of something Wendell had never seen in them before, and would not learn to name for many chapters yet: the look of a woman standing at the end of a road she had spent thirteen years pretending did not exist.

"Yes," she said. "God forgive me, yes. Come inside, child. The fire is lit, and the records are ready, and there is no more time to be only a gardener."
