## 2. Thirteen Years of Silence

The morning after the herald came, frost had written its white script across the garden beds, and Wendell knelt among the winter roses with her sleeves pushed back, pretending that her hands were not shaking.

She had not slept. The proclamation burned behind her eyes every time she closed them — *all persons possessing the gift of enchantment are hereby commanded to surrender themselves to crown authority* — and beneath it, in smaller letters, the promise of reward for those who reported their neighbors. She had read it three times before the words stopped swimming. The fourth time, she had noticed that the parchment bore a seal she recognized: the crowned rose of House Vaelin, pressed deep into the wax as though the queen herself feared it might be forgotten.

Now, in the grey light, Wendell worked the frost from the roses' crowns with bare fingers, and where she touched them, they brightened. It was barely anything. A flush of color returning to petals that should have stayed brown until spring. A straightening of stems. The faintest shimmer, like heat-haze over water, that lifted off her skin and sank into living things and made them *more*.

Her grandmother called it the family knack. Called it green thumbs, good blood, luck with growing things. Had spent thirteen years teaching Wendell to call it nothing at all.

Wendell sat back on her heels and looked at her hands. They looked like ordinary hands: broad-palmed, dirt-caked at the nails, a scar across the left thumb from a pruning knife when she was nine. But the shimmer was still rising off them, thin as breath in cold air, and for the first time in her life she looked at it without flinching away and thought, *this is what they mean. This is what they will hang people for.*

Inside the cottage, her grandmother was coughing again.

* * *

Maren Thornbury had been dying by inches for two years, the way old oaks die — slowly enough that everyone pretended it wasn't happening, visibly enough that no one could quite forget. Her hands, once strong enough to turn soil frozen hard as iron, now trembled so badly she needed help holding a cup. Her mind wandered sometimes, drifting back down years like a leaf on slow water, and she would surface calling names Wendell didn't know, or names she knew too well and never spoke aloud.

But this morning, when Wendell carried the tea into the low room where the fire was banked and the guild records lay in their locked chest against the far wall, Maren's eyes were clear. Clear and watching. She had heard the herald's horn yesterday; everyone in the valley had heard it. She had been waiting, Wendell realized, for exactly this.

"Sit down, child," her grandmother said, "and don't pretend you came in here about tea."

Wendell set the cup down anyway, because her hands needed something to do, and then she sat on the stool across from the chair where Maren ruled what remained of the Thornbury guild from beneath three blankets and a shawl the color of moss.

"The herald," Wendell said. "The proclamation."

"Yes."

"They're hunting people like me." Saying it aloud made it real in a way the reading hadn't. Her voice sounded strange in her own ears, smaller than she expected. "People with the gift. Grandmother, I've hidden it my whole life because you told me to. You never said why. You only said the valley doesn't like it, that magic can't follow us here, that anyone who shows it draws trouble. I believed you. I built my whole life inside that sentence. And now the crown has ridden into our valley with soldiers and a seal, and they are offering silver for children." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, and kept going anyway. "So either you lied to me, or you knew something you never told me. Which is it?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment. The fire ticked. Outside, a wren scolded something in the hedge, and the sound seemed very loud.

"You have your mother's way of asking questions," Maren said at last. "Like pulling teeth. No anesthesia. Just grip and pull."

"Maren." Wendell almost never used her grandmother's given name, but she used it now, deliberately, the way one sets a blade on a whetstone. "Please."

The old woman closed her eyes. When she opened them again, something in them had shifted — some door long barred, standing open onto darkness.

"The guild served a covenant," she said. "There. That's the first tooth out. The Thornbury guild was never just a gardening guild, whatever the valley folk believe, whatever I let you believe. We were keepers. Tenders of a boundary. There is another realm, Wendell — a realm that touches ours along certain thin places, and this valley sits over the thinnest of them all. For six hundred years there was an accord between the two realms. We kept the garden that stood at the hinge of it. Not this garden — the *old* garden, the true one, whose walls you can still find if you know where to look under the brambles on the north slope. Everything we grew, everything we knew about coaxing life from stone and salt and shadow, we learned from *them*. From the other side."

Wendell's mouth had gone dry. "Them. The people of Isandro."

She said the name without thinking, and then wondered why she had said it at all — why it rose to her tongue whole and familiar, like a word from a song she'd been sung before she could remember. Maren flinched as if struck.

"So you do remember fragments," the old woman whispered. "I hoped you wouldn't. I prayed you wouldn't."

"Remember what? Grandmother, what happened—"

"Thirteen years ago," Maren said, and her voice had gone flat and hard, the voice she used for weather reports and funerals, "the covenant broke. All at once, in a single night. The bridge between the realms — there was a bridge, child, a bridge of living light, and your parents walked it a hundred times — it severed. Every plant in the old garden died between one moonrise and the next. Every gift in the valley went silent. Yours included. You were five years old, and you woke up screaming that the flowers had stopped singing, and by the next morning you couldn't remember that they ever had." Her hands twisted in the blanket. "And your mother and father were gone. Taken, fled, dead — I don't know. I have spent thirteen years not knowing, and it has eaten me alive from the inside, and I would spare you that if it cost me everything."

The room seemed to tilt. Wendell gripped the edge of her stool. Thirteen years. Her whole conscious life. Her parents had gone over the ridge one autumn evening to check the boundary stones — that was the story, the only story, the story she had memorized by heart and repeated to herself on every anniversary — and had never come back, and the searchers found nothing, and grief had settled over the valley like a long winter.

And none of it was true. Or rather, all of it was true except the shape of it, the meaning of it. They hadn't gone to check stones. They had gone — somewhere. Somewhere with a bridge of light.

"What broke it?" she asked. Her voice came out hoarse. "What severed the bridge?"

"I don't know."

"You must know *something*—"

"I know what I saw," Maren said, "and what I saw was the sky over the north slope turning the color of a bruise, and the light going out of the bridge like a snuffed candle, and your mother's face in the last moment before—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together until they went white. "No. That's all you get. That's more than I swore I'd ever give."

"*Before what?*" Wendell leaned forward. "Grandmother, please. If the covenant broke, does that mean it can be mended? Is that why the gift is coming back — why my hands shimmer, why the roses sing to me again when they haven't since I was small? Something is changing. I can feel it in the soil. I felt it yesterday when the herald rode through, like the ground itself holding its breath. If my parents were taken because of the covenant — if they died for it — then I deserve to know. I have a right—"

"You have a right to stay *alive*," Maren snapped, and the force of it shook her into a coughing fit, deep and wet and terrible, and Wendell was on her feet with the teacup before she had decided to move, holding her grandmother's shoulders, feeling how narrow they had become, how like a bird's bones.

When the coughing passed, Maren sagged back against the chair and caught Wendell's wrist with both trembling hands. Her grip was still stronger than it had any right to be. Guild hands. Keeper's hands.

"Listen to me," she said, and her eyes were fierce and wet and ancient. "You asked me once, when you were seven, why we never spoke of your parents. Do you remember what I told you?"

"Some wounds don't heal in daylight."

"And it was true, as far as it went. But here is the rest of it, and you will carry it to your grave: there are questions that are doors, and there are questions that are wells. You fall into a well, child, and no one hears the bottom hit. Your parents asked the wrong question on the wrong night, and whether it killed them or saved them I cannot tell you — but I know that everything I love has a way of vanishing when it stands too close to the truth of that night. So I am telling you, as your grandmother and as the last keeper of the Thornbury guild: *do not ask more.* Not of me. Not of anyone. Burn the wondering out of yourself like blight from a cutting. Let the past lie where I have buried it. Promise me."

Everything in Wendell wanted to promise. Thirteen years of training — of lowered eyes and gloved hands and the careful, practiced dullness that kept the valley from looking twice at her — rose up in her throat and offered itself as the answer. Say yes. Keep the peace. Keep the walls.

But she thought of the proclamation nailed to the market post, ink still sharp, promising silver for the names of gifted children. She thought of the shimmer rising off her fingers like smoke. She thought of a name she shouldn't know — *Isandro* — sitting on her tongue like a seed she'd swallowed years ago and never digested.

"I can't," she said quietly. "I'm sorry, Grandmother. I can't promise that."

Maren closed her eyes. A single tear cut through the mapped terrain of her cheek, and she did not wipe it away.

"I was afraid you'd say that," she said. "You have her stubbornness too. God help us both." She released Wendell's wrist and turned her face toward the fire. "Get out, then. Go tend your roses. And Wendell — if a stranger ever comes to that gate, asking after the guild, asking after the covenant, asking after *you* — you send him away. Whatever he offers, however he pleads. Send him away and come tell me, and let an old woman decide what's worth waking up for."

"A stranger," Wendell repeated. "Why would a stranger—"

"*Out.*"

* * *

She went out. But she did not go to the roses.

Instead she walked the garden wall, the full circuit, as she had done ten thousand times since childhood — but never like this, never with new eyes. The wall was old work, older than the cottage, older than the village; the stones were fitted without mortar in a pattern that hurt to look at directly, some trick of the eye that made the courses seem to shift and flow like water held still. Her grandfather had called it guild-work. Everyone had always called everything guild-work and left it at that, the way you leave a name on a door you never open.

At the northern corner, where the wall met the slope, there was a gate. There had always been a gate — rusted shut, choked with ivy, leading nowhere but the bramble-choked north field. Wendell had played against it as a child. She had hung from its bars and swung and grown up and stopped seeing it.

Now she put her hand on the iron, and the ivy pulled back from her touch — actually *pulled back*, curling away like a creature avoiding a hot coal — and beneath the rust she saw that the bars were not iron at all, or not only iron. There was a grain in the metal, a living pattern, and when her skin met it the shimmer leapt from her fingers unbidden and the gate *sang*. One low note, felt more than heard, thrumming up through her palm and into her sternum, and for half a heartbeat the air beyond the gate rippled like the surface of a pond, and she saw — or almost saw — a road that wasn't there. A road of pale stone running north through country that glowed faintly, softly, the way the roses glowed when she sang to them in the dead of winter.

Then it was gone. The gate was rusted iron. The field was brambles and frost. The wren was scolding in the hedge, and her heart was slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out.

Thirteen years, she thought. Thirteen years of silence, and the silence is cracking everywhere at once.

Behind her, in the cottage, her grandmother began to cough again — or perhaps to call her; at this distance, with this blood roaring in her ears, she couldn't tell. The sun had cleared the eastern ridge and laid long gold light across the garden, across the wall, across the buried gate, and everything the light touched seemed to be waiting. The roses. The stones. The soil itself, dark and patient, holding things beneath it the way the valley held its secrets: carefully, completely, and not forever.

Wendell stood at the gate with her hand still resting on the cold bars, and she understood, with the certainty of a woman watching frost claim a late crop, that the quiet life she had tended all these years — the safe, small, carefully pruned life her grandmother had grafted her onto — was already dying. Something else was growing up through it, root by relentless root, and no amount of pruning would stop it now.

She turned back toward the cottage, toward the coughing, toward the chest of guild records and the grandmother who had lied to her out of love for thirteen long years —

— and stopped.

Because at the bottom of the garden, slumped against the gatepost where the lane met the wall, there was a man.
