## 3. A Stranger at the Gate

The frost came early that year, and Wendell was glad of it.

Frost meant work, and work meant not thinking. She had spent three days since the herald's visit moving through the garden like a woman walking on the crust of a frozen pond, listening all the while for the crack beneath her feet. The proclamation still hung in her mind the way the paper hung in the market square — *all who bear the gift are to be surrendered to crown authority* — and every time she turned a spade of earth or deadheaded the last of the marjoram, she caught herself watching her own hands as though they belonged to someone else. As though they might betray her.

They had not betrayed her yet. Not visibly. But she knew what they did when no one looked: the faint shimmer that passed from her palms into living things, the way cut stems sealed themselves a half-day sooner than they should, the way seedlings leaned toward her in their trays like sunflowers toward noon. Her grandmother called it nothing. Called it *green thumbs*, called it *the guild way with plants*, called it anything except what it was. And Wendell had learned, over twenty-two years, to call it nothing too.

But nothing had a weight now. Nothing had teeth.

She worked the herb beds until her knuckles ached, tucking straw around the rosemary and the sage, and the whole time the valley lay quiet around her in that particular late-autumn hush, smoke rising straight from the chimneys of Thornbury village below, the hills standing brown and patient under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Magic could not follow into this valley. Everyone knew that the way everyone knew the litany of the harvest festival, without knowing why or when anyone had decided it. Nothing strange grew here. Nothing strange survived here.

So when the gate groaned, she did not think *magic*. She thought *wind*.

Then she thought *someone is at the gate*, because the wind did not have hands.

* * *

He was slumped against the ironwork like a coat someone had hung up carelessly, and for one absurd heartbeat Wendell thought he was dead. Then his chest moved — a long, ragged pull of breath, the kind that sounds like tearing cloth — and she was through the gate and kneeling beside him before she had decided to move.

"Sir? Sir, can you hear me?"

His eyes opened. That was the first wrong thing.

Not because they were strange-colored, though they were — a grey so pale it was nearly silver, like light through ice — but because of how he looked at her. People who collapse look at whoever finds them with relief or fear or the flat animal confusion of pain. This man looked at her the way you look at a door you have been beating on for days when it finally opens. He looked at her like recognition.

"Wendell," he said.

Her name. But not her name — not the way anyone said it. Not *Wendy*, which the village children had called her until she outgrew it. Not *girl*, which old Master Fenwick had grunted at her across the potting benches. Not even *Wendell Thornbury*, formal and full, the way the tax assessor read it off his ledger. He said it low and wondering, the way you say the name of something you have grieved, and the sound of it went through her like a needle finding an old scar she hadn't known she carried.

"How do you—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Who are you?"

He tried to rise. She saw then the full ruin of him, and her stomach turned over. His coat had been fine once — dark green wool, silver-threaded at the cuffs — and was now burned through in places, torn in others, stiff with dried blood that was very nearly black. And beneath the tears in the fabric, along his forearms where his sleeves had fallen back, his skin was *wrong*. It was ridged and furrowed like the bark of an old oak, grey-brown and cracked in fine lines, and where the wounds crossed it the bark had split and wept a sap that had hardened to amber beads.

She should have screamed. She understood that later, looking back — that any sensible girl in a magicless valley, kneeling beside a bleeding man whose flesh had gone to tree, would have run for the constable and a cudgel. Instead she heard herself say, in the voice she used for frightened animals and broken things:

"You're hurt. Let me get you inside."

He laughed at that — a short, cracked sound that ended in a cough. "Inside," he repeated, tasting the word. "You'd take me inside."

"It's freezing and you're bleeding on my gravel." She got her shoulder under his arm. He was lighter than he looked, or she was stronger than she knew; either way, they made it three staggering steps toward the garden door before his knees went, and she more caught than carried him down onto the flagstones by the wall, in the lee of the espaliered pear tree that her grandmother's grandmother had planted.

"Stay," she told him, the way she'd tell a dog, and ran for the basin, the clean linen, the willow-bark tincture that the guild kept against just such emergencies — though the guild had never, in her lifetime, had such an emergency.

When she came back he had not moved, except that his hand had found the base of the pear tree and rested there, palm flat against the trunk, and — she would spend a long time afterward trying to persuade herself she had imagined this — the tree had *leaned*. A degree, perhaps two. The way a plant leans toward light. The espaliered branches, pruned flat against the south wall for sixty years, had curved minutely toward the dying stranger as if toward a window.

Wendell stood very still with the basin in her hands and felt the ground tilt under everything she believed about her valley.

* * *

She worked on him in the potting shed, because the house felt suddenly too exposed, its windows too visible from the lane. She cleaned the wounds — deep gashes across his ribs and shoulder, made by something with edges too clean to be a blade — and bound them, and got willow tincture and honeyed water into him, and all the while the questions piled up in her throat like birds on a wire.

He let her work in silence for a long time. Then, watching her hands wrap the linen around his forearm where the bark gave way to ordinary skin at the wrist, he said: "You don't ask what I am."

"I'm asking plenty of things. Just not yet."

"That's not how people are."

"No," she agreed, tying off the bandage. "People scream and fetch the constable. My grandmother says I've never once done anything in the correct order."

Something moved through his face at the mention of her grandmother — something quick and pained, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Thornbury," he said. "The guild is still—"

"There is no guild." She said it flatly, the way you close a door. "There's a walled garden and two women who keep it, and everyone in the valley thinks we're eccentric herb-women. Whatever my grandmother's family used to be, it ended before I was born."

"Not before you were born," he said quietly. "Thirteen years ago. Ask her."

The words landed in her like stones dropped down a well. Thirteen years ago. The night her parents vanished, if you counted vanished generously — her grandmother never spoke of it, and the village had settled long ago on the gentle fiction of fever, of an accident on the mountain road, of the thousand soft explanations communities invent for the holes in each other's lives. Thirteen years ago. The man in her potting shed, bleeding bark, saying it like a date he had counted every day of.

"What happened thirteen years ago?" she asked.

He closed his eyes. "Later," he said. "When I can stand up when I tell it. There's something you need to do first, and I need you to do it while I'm still brave enough to watch."

"Watch what?"

He fumbled at his collar with fingers that moved stiffly, as if the joints ached. Beneath his shirt, on a cord of braided leather gone dark with sweat and blood, hung a stone.

It was the size of a hen's egg, roughly, but heavier-looking — a shard of something between crystal and amber, shot through with veins of gold that seemed, when she tilted her head, to shift position slowly, the way roots grow. It was warm. She knew that before she touched it, because the air above it shimmered very slightly, the way the air above her own hands sometimes shimmered, and she felt a sensation she had no name for — a kind of answering, a tuning-fork hum in the bones of her wrists.

"Don't—" he started.

She touched it.

The world *rang*.

There was no other word her mind could find for it. Sound poured up through her fingertips and into her arms and chest and teeth, but not sound exactly — it was what sound would be if sound were alive, if it were growing, if it were a seed cracking open in dark soil somewhere behind her sternum. Light moved under the stone's surface, the golden veins brightening like sap rising, and every plant in the potting shed turned toward her. Every plant in the potting shed *turned toward her* — the tray of forced bulbs, the cuttings under glass, the bundle of drying lavender hanging from the beam — swiveled on their stems like faces at a name being called, and outside, muffled by the shed walls, she heard the pear tree creak against its wall, straining.

And her hands — her hidden, shameful, nothing-at-all hands — were shining. Faintly, softly, the way moonlight shines through a curtain: a shimmer rising off her skin like heat-haze, pouring down her arms and into the stone, feeding it, and the stone drinking her like rain.

She snatched her hand back. The ringing died. The plants sagged back to their ordinary postures, and the silence afterward was enormous.

"What was that," she whispered. It was not a question. It was a plea dressed as a question. "What was *that*?"

The man was watching her with an expression she could not read — grief and awe and something like terror, all held very carefully behind his pale eyes. "That," he said, "was a keystone recognizing its keeper."

"I don't understand."

"No," he said gently. "You wouldn't. They took the understanding away thirteen years ago, along with everything else. But the stone does. And the garden does. And I do." He pushed himself up against the potting bench, wincing, until he was sitting upright, and looked at her with an intensity that made the small shed feel smaller. "I walked eleven days to reach this valley, Wendell, on a leg that should not have carried me, because everything else has failed and you are the last thing left. You are the heir of the Thornbury guild. You are the covenant's living heir, whether your grandmother ever told you so or not — and I am beginning to suspect she never did."

"The covenant," Wendell repeated. The word felt ancient in her mouth, a word from the bottom of a well. "What covenant?"

"The one that keeps your valley safe," he said. "And the one that is killing mine."

Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the bare branches of the pear tree against the wall, and somewhere down in the village a dog began to bark — a thin, urgent sound, the sound of something disturbed that cannot yet name what disturbs it. Wendell looked down at her own hands, resting open on her knees, and for the first time in her life she did not try to make them still. They were trembling. Under the skin, faint as starlight through water, the shimmer was still there, ebbing slowly, reluctantly, like a tide that had tasted the moon.

"Tell me," she said. "All of it. Start with your name."

The man smiled — tiredly, crookedly, as if the muscles had forgotten the shape. "Isandro," he said. "My name is Isandro. And I'm afraid I'm the reason your garden leans."
