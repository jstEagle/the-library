## 4. Isandro

He woke to silence.

Not the silence of sleep, which is a country with its own weather, its own birds, its own slow tides of breath — but the silence of absence. Isandro lay still on a bed he did not recognize and listened to the thing that was missing, the way a man missing a tooth cannot stop probing the gap with his tongue.

Magic. The valley had none.

He had crossed a hundred borders in his long service, and every one of them had hummed, had *sung*, had pressed against his skin like the membrane of a drum. Even the thinnest, most neglected thresholds had whispered to him of the other side. This one did not whisper. This one had been cut, cauterized, closed so long and so completely that the land itself had forgotten what it was. He lay in a narrow room with whitewashed walls and a ceiling crossed by dark beams, and he felt as though he had been buried alive in wool.

He was alive, though. That surprised him.

He remembered the crossing — or the end of it. The boundary was not meant to be crossed from his side anymore; that was the whole point of a broken covenant, that the doors swing shut. He had forced it the way a drowning man forces a window, and the passage had taken its toll in the only coin he had: himself. He remembered light like white fire along his arms. He remembered the smell of turned earth. He remembered falling.

And he remembered her.

*The heir.* He had known her on sight, though he had never seen her face before — the covenant's mark was written in her the way a river is written in a valley, invisible until you knew to look, and then so obvious you wondered how the world had hidden it. Thirteen years he had searched the borderlands for a thread of the old guild's blood, and the thread had been here all along, growing roses behind a wall, hiding the shimmer of her hands from a valley that would burn her for it.

Wendell. The name surfaced in him like a fish rising to break the skin of a pond. She had given it grudgingly, kneeling in the dirt beside him with her hands pressed to his wounds, and the moment her gift had touched him — untrained, unguarded, leaking out of her like light through a cracked shutter — his own failing power had *leaned into it* the way a starving man leans toward a lit window.

He sat up too fast and the room tilted. Sweat stood on his brow. His body, which in his own realm could have mended a wound like this in the space of a heartbeat, was working at the speed of clay. Of mud. Of *mortal* things. He looked down at his forearms, where the bark-scarring climbed from wrist to elbow in ridged, gray-brown plates, and pressed a thumb against the newest wound beneath them. It throbbed. It would take days to close here. Perhaps weeks.

*Thirteen turns of the moon*, said the voice of his mistress, the Lady of the Threshold, as he had last heard it, fading behind the closing door. *Thirteen, and then never. Find the heir, guardian. Beg her. Steal her. Carry her if you must. But do not come back without her.*

He had not told her that. He had told her almost nothing, in fact — that he was from beyond the boundary, that he was the covenant's guardian, that the moon's thirteenth turn would seal the realms apart forever. All of it true. All of it a wall built carefully in front of the one truth he could not yet make himself say, because saying it aloud would make him the kind of man he had sworn never to be: the kind who asks a stranger to pay a price she cannot see.

*Not yet*, he told himself. *When she trusts me. When she understands what is at stake. There is time.*

There was not time. There were perhaps nine turns left, and each one would find him weaker here, in this magicless country, like a fish in air. But he told himself there was time, and the telling had the smooth, practiced feel of a lie he had told before.

* * *

He found the kitchen by following the smell of woodsmoke and rosemary, moving carefully, one hand on the wall. The house was old in the way that guild houses were old — not built so much as *grown*, he suspected, in the years when the guild still had the knack for it, every beam and lintel shaped by hands that understood wood as a living collaborator and not merely a material. Even cut off from magic for thirteen years, the house remembered its making. He could feel it under his palm like a dog too old to bark, thumping its tail.

Wendell stood at the stove with her back to him, and even before she turned he saw the way the rosemary on the sill had turned toward her. Not noticeably. Not enough that a crown hunter would see. But the pot of it had swiveled on its saucer by a hand's width, all its little gray leaves straining toward her like faces toward a fire, and she had not noticed, because she had spent her whole life not noticing — because noticing would have killed her.

She turned. She had not slept; he could see it in the bruised skin beneath her eyes, and in the way she held herself, arms crossed, a wooden spoon held like a small sword.

"You shouldn't be up," she said.

"I've been worse." It was true. He did not tell her how much worse.

"You were bleeding through bandages I changed twice in the night. You shouldn't be *walking*."

"Your bandages were well made." He lowered himself into a chair at the scrubbed table, honestly grateful for it, and watched her decide between anger and curiosity. Curiosity won, as he had hoped. Curiosity was the heir's inheritance as much as the gift was.

"Your grandmother," he said, "is she —"

"Asleep. Finally. She took a draught of valerian or I'd have had no sleep at all, the way she was carrying on." Wendell set the spoon down but did not sit. "She says I should have left you at the gate to die. She says the guild is *dead*, and anything that comes looking for it is either a ghost or a vulture."

"Your grandmother is wise," Isandro said, "and afraid, and both of those things are older than her fear of me. Ask her what the guild was afraid *of*, someday, when she can bear to answer."

Something flickered across Wendell's face — the same hunger he had seen in the garden, the hunger of a woman who had spent her life being told not to ask. "She says the covenant broke thirteen years ago. She says it the way you'd say a person died. Not *ended*. *Broke*." Her jaw tightened. "And then you fall through my gate calling me by a name nobody's used since my mother — *heir* — with a stone in your pocket that sings when I touch it. So." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, planting both elbows on the table. "Talk. And don't tell me you need rest. Dead men don't need rest. You're not dead, so talk."

He had planned this speech for years, in the long watches of his crossing-search, and now that it came to it, every prepared word crumbled like dry bread. What remained was the truth, and he gave it to her as plainly as he could.

"My name is Isandro. I was — *am* — the guardian of the covenant between your valley and my realm. There were once twelve of us, one for each gate, and I kept the western door, the one that opened into your garden's oldest hedge. When the covenant broke, we guardians did not die, because we were not wholly of either realm. We *faded*. I have spent thirteen years fading, Wendell, and watching my realm fade with me."

He turned his hands over on the table, let her see the bark-scarring without flinching from it, though flinching was what it deserved. "This is what fading looks like, in the end. The realm is forgetting itself. Rivers forgetting their courses. Trees forgetting how to leaf. People—" he hesitated, "—people forgetting the names of the ones they love. The covenant was the treaty between the worlds, the law that let magic live in both without either being drowned in it. It was made by your guild and mine together, long ago. It was broken thirteen years ago, on a single night, and the breaking is killing my realm slowly and poisoning yours faster than you know."

"The Road of Ash," Wendell said quietly. "The villages east of here. The crops that come up wrong. My grandmother said the blight was natural."

"The blight is a *symptom*. The land on your side of the boundary is sick the way a body is sick when the heart stops beating but the blood has not yet stilled. Magic is not gone from your world, Wendell. It is *trapped*, backing up along the border like floodwater behind a broken dam, and it is coming through in wild places, in wrong ways. Your crown has noticed. Whatever your heralds are proclaiming about the gifted — that hunt, those proclamations — it is not superstition. The crown is afraid of exactly the right thing, for exactly the wrong reason."

He watched her absorb this, watched her hands curl on the table. "And the stone," she said. "The keystone."

"One of four. The keystones were the covenant's anchors — the four corners of the treaty, set in your world at the four compass points of the old guild's keeping. When the covenant broke, they were scattered and hidden, some by friends, some by enemies, some by the breaking itself. Without them, the covenant cannot be remade. With them—" he met her eyes, "—with them, and with the heir, it can. That is why I came. That is why I crossed a boundary that should have killed me. The moon is on its ninth turn since the breaking began to fail in earnest. On the thirteenth, the seal closes forever. No crossing. No remaking. My realm dies behind a shut door, and yours—" he gestured at the walls, the valley, the garden, "—yours drowns slowly in the flood it can no longer hold back."

Silence. The rosemary on the sill trembled, though there was no wind.

"You said *heir*," Wendell said at last. Her voice was very controlled, the control itself a kind of trembling. "You called me that at the gate. I'm a gardener. I'm the last apprentice of a guild that my own grandmother won't even name. My parents were—" she stopped. Started again. "Nobody told me I was heir to anything but weeds."

"The heirship is not a title. It is a *thread*. The covenant was not only written; it was *woven*, and woven things need a living thread at their center. Your guild's bloodline was that thread. When your parents vanished, the thread did not break — it passed to you. It has been in you all your life. It is why your hands leave shimmer on living things. It is why the stone sang for you and not for me, though I have carried it for thirteen years." He leaned forward. "I know what I am asking. I am asking a woman who has hidden her whole life to walk out of hiding into the open, toward a crown that hunts her, through a land that is coming apart. I am asking her to do it for a stranger and a realm she has never seen."

"Then don't ask," Wendell said. "Not yet. Tell me one thing first, and tell me true." Her eyes were gray-green and utterly steady. "My parents. Thirteen years ago. The night the covenant broke. Were they there?"

The question went through him like a spear through water — no resistance, straight to the center. He had wondered when she would ask it. He had dreaded it, and he had hoped for it, because it was the door to everything.

"I don't know," he said, and it was the truth, and the truth was its own kind of wound. "The breaking happened on *their* side of the boundary, in the valley, at the western gate. By the time I fought my way to the threshold, the gate was sealed and the night was over, and what was done had been done by people I never saw. I have spent thirteen years not knowing, Wendell. I know what it is to have a hole in the middle of your life with your own name written around its rim."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide — not to trust him, not yet, but to *need* him. Need was the seed trust grew from, and he was a guardian of growing things before he was anything else.

"The moon," she said. "Nine turns. And then what happens to me, if I say no? If I stay here and keep my roses?"

He had an answer prepared for that too — something gentle, something about the wild magic rising, about the hunters, about how hiding would not hold. All of it true. None of it the truth.

"You will be in danger either way," he said. "The gift in you is getting stronger as the boundary fails. Hiding it will become impossible. And the crown's hunt will not pass over this valley forever." All true. All a wall. Behind the wall, the thing he could not say: *the covenant's remaking has a price, and the price is paid in memory, and you are the one who must pay it.*

"Grandmother will say no," Wendell said. "She'll bar the door. She may already be sharpening the kitchen knife." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "But she burned something in the yard last night, after she thought I was asleep. Papers. Guild papers, I think. She stood over the fire a long time." Her eyes came up to his. "People don't burn their history unless they're afraid someone's coming to read it. Is someone coming to read it, Isandro?"

He thought of the heralds, the proclamation, the hunt. He thought of Captain Rell, whose name the Lady of the Threshold had spoken with the weight of a stone dropped in a well — *the heir will not run from a stranger; she will run from a friend turned hunter* — and he thought of the fire in the yard, and the seed-packet he had glimpsed in the old woman's sleeve.

"Yes," he said. "Sooner than either of us wants."

Wendell nodded slowly, as if he had confirmed something she had already decided in the dark hours before dawn. She rose, went to the window, and stood looking out at the walled garden, at the roses that had turned toward the house overnight, every bloom in the bed facing the kitchen window like a congregation.

"Then eat something," she said, "because I have questions, and you're going to answer every one of them, and I don't interrogate men on an empty stomach."

He almost smiled. Then, through the window, over her shoulder, he saw it: at the valley's far rim, where the road came down through the pass, a line of riders. Small at this distance. Orderly. Moving with the unhurried confidence of people who have never once been refused.

The crown had come to the valley.

* * *

They stood together at the window, and Isandro felt the moment settle over the house like frost.

"Six riders," Wendell said. "Maybe seven. That's not a levy. That's not tax collectors."

"No," Isandro said. His side ached where the wound pulled; he ignored it. "That is a hunt that has already found its scent."

"Then we have until they reach the gate." She said it calmly, terribly calmly, the calm of a woman measuring a flood against a door. "Grandmother is still asleep. If I wake her, she'll either bar the door and burn with the house, or she'll—" Wendell stopped. Her hand had gone to her apron pocket, and Isandro saw, with a guardian's eye, the small shape of a seed-packet there, and the corner of a folded paper that could only be a map.

The old woman had known. The old woman had been preparing her for *years*, perhaps — burning the records, hoarding the seeds, folding the maps — and had simply been waiting, all this long silence, for the night her granddaughter finally asked the right question. Or for the morning she stopped needing to.

"Isandro." Wendell did not look away from the riders. "The stone you carry. The keystone. If the covenant can be remade — if I'm what you say I am — then there's a part of this I'm meant to play that you haven't told me. Isn't there."

It was not a question. He looked at her profile against the brightening window, at the garden leaning toward her, at the riders descending the pass, and he felt the lie he had been carrying since the garden — *there is time, there is time, I will tell her when she trusts me* — grow heavier than the wound in his side.

"There is," he said. "And I will tell you. I swear it. But not here, and not with them coming down the pass."

She turned and looked at him then, fully, and he saw that she knew — not what he was hiding, but *that* he was hiding, and was choosing, for now, to let him. It was worse than anger. It was a debt being quietly entered in a ledger.

"All right," Wendell said. "Then here's what happens. I wake my grandmother. You get to the tunnels under the greenhouse — there's an old guild door behind the potting bench, and if you're what you say you are, it will know you. We go down, we go out, and we go *now*, before those riders reach the wall." She was already moving, pulling a satchel from under the sink, her hands quick and sure. "And Isandro —"

She paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the hall, and for one moment she was not a hunted gardener but the heir of a covenant, and the whole house seemed to lean toward her the way the roses did.

"When you tell me the rest — the part you're not telling me — don't wait for a moment you think I can bear. There's no such moment. I learned that the night my parents didn't come home."

She went to wake her grandmother, and Isandro stood alone in the kitchen with the keystone humming against his chest like a second heart, and understood that he had just been given a deadline more terrible than the moon's: the truth, before she asked again. He pressed his hand to the stone and whispered, in the language of his fading realm, the guardian's oldest prayer — the one for travelers, and liars, and men who love what they must lead to the knife.

Above the valley, the ninth moon set pale and thin over the pass, and the riders came on.
