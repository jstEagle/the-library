## 5. The Hunt Begins

The herald came on a Tuesday, which Wendell would remember afterward as the cruelest detail of all — that the world should end its old shape on the most ordinary day of the week.

She was in the garden when she heard him: the flat clop of a government horse on the lane beyond the wall, and beneath it the murmur of neighbors gathering the way water gathers before it finds a drain. She set down her trowel. She wiped her hands on her apron out of habit, though there was no one to see them clean.

Through the gap in the gate she could see him plainly. A crown herald, in the queen's gray-and-gold, mounted on a horse too fine for their dusty lane, unrolling a scroll with the ceremony of a man who had done it forty times that season and would do it forty more. His voice carried the way voices carry when they have been trained to carry — not loud, but inescapable, sliding through shutters and over walls into every kitchen in the valley.

"By order of Her Majesty, Queen Sarelle the Second, Protector of the Realm: let it be known that the affliction of the gift — the wild magic lately returned to certain persons of this kingdom — is declared a plague of the blood. All persons so afflicted are to be surrendered to crown authority within thirty days' time. Those who harbor the gifted shall be judged as the gifted themselves. Those who surrender them willingly shall be compensated."

He paused there, and Wendell saw his eyes move across the gathered faces — measuring, cataloguing, the way a buyer measures cattle. "Hunters of the crown will arrive to assist in identification. Cooperation is the duty of every loyal subject."

*Compensated.* The word sat in Wendell's stomach like a swallowed stone. Not *rewarded*. Compensated — as though turning in your neighbor were a labor, a service rendered, something owed payment for the trouble of it.

The neighbors did not look at one another. That was the worst part. Old Marta from the mill, who had held Wendell on her hip when she was small enough for hips to hold; Tam Wicker, whose sons had stolen apples from the guild orchard every autumn of their boyhood while Grandmother pretended not to see — they stood shoulder to shoulder in the lane and studied the ground between their feet, each of them already performing the arithmetic of who among them might be worth thirty days' silver.

Wendell drew back from the gate. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had left faint green shimmer on the gate's iron latch where she'd gripped it — the metal itself seeming, for a heartbeat, to sprout. She scrubbed at it with her apron until it faded.

In the house, Grandmother was standing at the window, and she was not surprised. That frightened Wendell more than the proclamation had. Her grandmother stood with her arms folded and her mouth a thin line, watching the herald's horse recede down the lane, and her face held no shock at all. Only the grim satisfaction of a woman proven right.

"You knew," Wendell said.

"I knew it was coming." Grandmother turned. In the dim kitchen her eyes looked like river stones, smooth and cold and older than the house around them. "I told you last month, when the first riders passed through toward the capital. I told you what the burning in the east meant. The world outside this valley has been remembering what magic is, Wendell, and remembering makes people afraid, and afraid people make proclamations." She crossed to the hearth and stood before it, looking down into the cold ash as if reading something written there. "Thirty days. It will be less. Hunters travel faster than paper."

"Grandmother—"

"They will come here." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she said *the frost will come early this year* or *the blight is in the south beds.* "This valley has been clean of magic for thirteen years, and clean places draw dirty attention. They will want to know why we are clean. They will want records. Names. Guild rolls." Something flickered across her face then — quick, quickly buried — and Wendell understood, with a lurch, that her grandmother was not afraid of the hunters finding something.

She was afraid of what she herself would have to burn before they arrived.

* * *

They came four days later, at dawn, and they came quietly, which was somehow worse than drums would have been.

Wendell woke to the smell of smoke that was not the hearth's smoke — sharper, resinous, paper-smoke — and came downstairs to find her grandmother feeding the guild's records into the fire, page by page, with the unhurried care of a woman tending seedlings.

The guild records. Wendell had grown up with those books. Great ledgers bound in green leather gone soft as cloth, their spines stamped with the sigil she had never been allowed to ask about — the flowering branch inside a circle. She had dusted them. She had been scolded, once, at seven years old, for drawing in the margins of the oldest one. They contained planting calendars for years before her birth, soil charts, rainfall tallies going back generations. Ordinary things. Gardener's things.

Except that now, watching the pages curl, she could see that some of them were not ordinary at all. One book gave up its pages reluctantly, and in the brief flare of light before the flames took them Wendell saw diagrams she had no name for — circles within circles, a bridge drawn in lines of gold leaf that seemed, even burning, even dying, to hold a little light of its own. Another page showed two hands clasped, rendered in ink that shifted color as the heat rose: green to silver to a red like an open wound.

"Grandmother!" She crossed the room and caught the woman's wrist above the fire. "What are you doing? Those are ours — those are the guild's —"

"The guild is three old women and a girl," Grandmother said, not pulling her hand away, not looking at her either. "The guild has been dead thirteen years, child. We only forgot to bury it. Now the crown has come to dig, and I would rather it found ash than answers."

"What's in them? What did you just burn?" Wendell's voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it. "I grew up with those books. You never let me read half of them, and now you're burning them without — you don't get to decide alone what our history is worth!"

Her grandmother finally turned and looked at her, and whatever was in her face made Wendell go quiet. It was not stubbornness. It was grief, worn smooth by thirteen years of carrying, and terror underneath it, fresh as a new wound.

"I decided alone thirteen years ago," the old woman said softly, "when I chose to raise you in ignorance rather than in danger. I have regretted it every day since, and I would choose it again every morning until I die. That is what love looks like from the inside, Wendell. It looks like a lie you keep telling to keep someone breathing."

Before Wendell could answer — before she could decide whether to shout or weep or demand, finally, after seventeen years, the truth — hooves sounded on the lane.

Many hooves.

Grandmother moved faster than Wendell had ever seen her move. She swept the last unburned ledger off the table — a slim volume Wendell didn't recognize, wrapped in oilcloth — and pressed it deep into the banked coals at the back of the hearth, where it would smolder unseen. Then she took Wendell by both shoulders.

"There is a packet in my sewing basket. Under the blue thread. Take it. Do not look at it here. And whatever happens in the next hour, you are a gardener's granddaughter and nothing else. Your hands are chapped from work and nothing else. Say it back to me."

"Grandmother, who is —"

"*Say it back to me.*"

The knock came before Wendell could speak. Three blows, measured and official, on a door that had never once in Wendell's life been locked.

* * *

There were six of them, and they filled the kitchen the way a rockslide fills a streambed.

They wore the queen's gray, but not the herald's finery — these were working soldiers, road-dusted, their armor practical and scarred. At their center stood a man whose presence rearranged the room around him: tall, broad through the shoulders, perhaps five years older than Wendell, with dark hair cut short in the military fashion and a face that might have been handsome if it ever permitted itself ease. He wore a captain's bar at his collar, bright and new.

Wendell knew him.

She knew the way he stood with his weight slightly on his left leg, an old habit from the childhood injury he'd gotten falling out of the guild's plum tree. She knew the small pale scar through his left eyebrow, from the day they'd dared each other onto the millpond ice and the ice had won. She knew him the way you know the shape of your own hand — Rell, Rell Wessin, who had run these lanes beside her from the time they could both walk, who had helped her bury a songbird under the pear tree when she was nine and cried harder than she did, who had joined the crown's army at sixteen because there was nothing left in the valley for a restless boy and everyone said he'd make something of himself.

Captain Rell. Standing in her grandmother's kitchen with six armed strangers and a rolled warrant in his gloved hand.

"Wendell." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. For half a heartbeat something moved behind his eyes — recognition, memory, maybe even gladness — and then discipline closed over it like water over a dropped stone. "Ma'am. Sir." He nodded to Grandmother. "We're conducting a survey of the valley households. Crown business. There will be questions."

"Survey," Grandmother repeated. Her voice was dry as August. "At dawn. With six soldiers."

"The gifted don't keep honest hours, ma'am." One of the others said it — a lean man with a crossbow slung across his back and the easy cruelty of a man who enjoyed his work. Rell's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly, but he didn't correct him, and Wendell felt that silence like a slap. The boy who had cried over a songbird would have corrected him. The captain let it stand.

"We have reason to believe magical artifacts may be concealed in this region," Rell continued, producing the warrant and reading from it in a flat, official cadence. "Particularly items associated with the old gardening guilds. Any such materials are to be surrendered immediately. Households failing to declare—" he paused, and for just a moment his eyes found Wendell's, and she could not read what lived in them, "—will be searched."

"The Thornbury guild kept records," Grandmother said. "Gardening records. Planting calendars. Rainfall. I burned them last month when the damp got into the storeroom. Mold spreads, you know."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true — the mold had been real, once, years ago. Wendell marveled at how easily her grandmother spent truth as cover for lies, and hated herself a little for admiring it.

Rell studied the old woman for a long moment. Then he nodded to two of his men. "Search the house."

The search was thorough and strangely gentle, as searches went. They went through the pantry shelves and the linen chests and the loft where Wendell slept, handling her spare dresses, her few books, the dried flowers she kept in a jar on her sill — ordinary flowers, lavender and yarrow, though one soldier held the jar up to the light for a long moment before setting it back exactly where it had stood. Wendell stood in the middle of her own kitchen with her hands folded like a penitent and watched strangers learn the intimate geography of her life, and beside her Grandmother stood like a fencepost, immovable, giving them nothing.

Rell himself did not search. He walked the room slowly instead, and Wendell realized he was looking at her — not searching her, but *watching* her, cataloguing her the way the herald had catalogued the crowd. And once, when the lean soldier with the crossbow brushed past her close enough that his sleeve touched her wrist, Wendell saw Rell's hand twitch toward his sword hilt and stop.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. But she had seen it, and she thought — she would think about it for many nights after — that he had seen it too.

In the garden, the search turned up nothing, because there was nothing to find. Wendell's shimmer left no evidence a soldier could read. The plants leaned toward her invisibly, secretly, and the hunters trampled rows she'd spent the spring coaxing, and called it procedure.

At the gate, pausing, Rell turned back. His men were already mounting. The morning light came through the pear tree behind him and made his shadow long across the garden path, reaching almost to her feet.

"Your garden's looking well," he said. Just that. Quietly, under the sound of tack and harness. "The tomatoes especially. Better than anyone else's in the valley. Better than they've any right to be."

Wendell's heart stopped, started again, wrong.

"It's the soil," she said. "Good soil here."

"Mm," said Captain Rell. And there — there, in the last second before he turned away — was the boy from the millpond ice, the boy who had buried the songbird, looking at her from behind the captain's gray eyes with an expression she couldn't name and couldn't afford. A warning? An apology? A promise?

"Thirty days," he said aloud, in his official voice, for the benefit of his men. "Cooperation is the duty of every loyal subject." Then he swung up into the saddle, and the hoofbeats faded down the lane, and the valley settled back into its morning quiet like a wound closing over a splinter it cannot push out.

* * *

That night, after the lamps were out, Grandmother came to Wendell's room and sat on the edge of her bed in the dark, the way she had when Wendell was small and feverish.

"He knows," Wendell whispered. It wasn't a question. "Rell knows. About me. About the garden. He's known since we were children, hasn't he? He used to watch me — I remember him watching, and I always thought —" She pressed her palms against her eyes. "He'll report it. He has to. It's his duty now. Duty is the whole point of him."

For a long time her grandmother said nothing. When she finally spoke, her voice was very low.

"When your parents went away, Rell Wessin was eleven years old. He came to the funeral-that-wasn't — we buried empty coffins, you remember, or you don't, you were too young — and he stood at the graveside holding your hand so hard his knuckles went white, because you wouldn't stop asking when they were coming home, and no one else would touch you." The bed frame creaked as she rose. "Boys become men, Wendell, and oaths become chains, and sometimes the chain and the boy grow into each other so thoroughly that neither can tell anymore which one is being worn. I don't know what he'll do. Neither does he. That is the most dangerous kind of man the crown ever arms."

She moved toward the door, then stopped.

"The packet. Under the blue thread. Have you opened it?"

"No. You said not to look at it here."

"And I meant it. But tonight, after I'm asleep — take it, and the map folded inside it, and read what's written on the map's margin. Only the margin. Nothing else." Grandmother's silhouette filled the doorway, thin and straight-backed against the lamplight from the hall. "And then, Wendell, you must decide something I have spent thirteen years praying you would never have to decide. Because the hunters will come back. Men like that always come back. And next time they will not be led by a boy who once loved you."

The door closed. Wendell sat in the dark a long while, listening to her own heart.

Then she reached beneath the floorboard where she hid her childhood treasures, and took out the packet wrapped in blue thread, and unfolded the map her grandmother had hidden there — a map of the valley and the mountains beyond it, drawn in faded ink on paper gone soft with age, marked with a route no road followed and a symbol she recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water:

The flowering branch inside a circle.

And beneath it, in her grandmother's cramped, urgent hand, a single line:

*If they come for you, child, follow the tunnels under the guild wall, and find what your mother left at the boundary. Ask it why it broke.*

Outside, far off in the direction of the pass, a dog began to bark — and did not stop.
