## 6. Leaving the Valley

The seed-packet was heavier than it should have been.

Wendell turned it over in her palm as she crouched in the dark of the guild cellar, feeling the weight of it — not the weight of paper and seeds, which was nothing, but some other weight, the way a stone dropped into deep water is heavy with everything above it. Her grandmother had pressed it into her hands with fingers that shook, and had said only: *When you find soil that remembers, plant them.* Then she had gone back upstairs to burn the guild records, one page at a time, feeding four hundred years of covenant into the kitchen stove while the smoke rose from the chimney like a confession no one would hear.

Above her, through the floorboards, Wendell could hear the hunters.

They had come at dusk — six riders and a wagon, torches unlit but carried openly, the way men carry things they intend to use. Captain Rell's voice had carried across the garden wall, and it was his voice more than anything else that had made her grandmother's face go grey. Not because it was a stranger's voice. Because it wasn't.

*Burn the ledgers first,* Rell had said. *The garden after. The Thornbury girl is to be found alive.*

Found alive. The words had settled over Wendell like frost. Alive, but not free; alive the way a specimen is alive, pinned and labeled and kept. She had thought of the proclamation nailed to the market post, the crown's seal dripping wax-red in the rain: *all who carry the gift are to be surrendered to the crown's physicians for examination and keeping.* Examination and keeping. She had imagined what those words meant in practice, and her imagination had been kind, and even so it had been enough to make her sick.

Now she knelt in the cellar among jars of preserved pears and bundles of dried lavender, and Isandro knelt beside her, and between them lay the flagstone her grandmother had shown them — the one with the guild sigil worn nearly smooth, a sprig and a crescent, older than any record the fire upstairs was eating.

"The tunnels run under the mill stream," Isandro said quietly. His voice was still rough from his wound, and he spoke the way a man speaks when he is rationing his strength. "They were cut so the gardeners could move without being seen. Your guild understood something about secrecy."

"They understood something about being hated," Wendell said. "It's not the same thing, but it comes to the same doors."

She worked her fingernails into the seam of the flagstone. It resisted, then gave with a grinding reluctance, exhaling a breath of air from below — cold, mineral, smelling of wet stone and old roots. A ladder of iron rungs descended into blackness. Wendell looked at it and felt something in her chest tighten. This was the way out. This was also the way away from everything she had ever been.

"Your grandmother," Isandro said. "Will she come?"

"She won't leave." Wendell heard her own voice go flat and strange. "She says someone has to be here when they ask their questions. Someone who can give them answers that are true enough to satisfy and false enough to protect." She swallowed. "She burned forty years of her life tonight so I could run. I don't think she expects to see me again, and I don't think she wants to. Wanting things gets people hurt. That's the first lesson this valley ever taught me."

Isandro was quiet for a moment. In the dark his bark-scarred face was hard to read, but his eyes caught what little light there was, and they were fixed on her with an expression she couldn't name — grief, maybe, or guilt, or the particular sorrow of a man watching someone pay a debt he had carried to her door.

"Wendell," he began.

"Don't," she said. "Whatever you're going to say — don't say it here. Say it when we're somewhere the ground doesn't have ears." She swung her legs over the opening. "I've spent my whole life learning how to stay. Give me one night to learn how to go."

She climbed down before he could answer, into the cold and the dark, and the flagstone slid shut above her like an eye closing.

* * *

The tunnel was older than she had expected, and stranger.

Her lamp — a stub of candle in a tin holder, taken from the cellar shelf — showed walls of fitted stone furred with pale root-hairs, and every few yards a niche carved into the rock where, once, lamps must have stood. Some niches still held stubs of tallow, centuries old, soft as butter. The air moved gently past them, always toward the outside, as if the tunnel itself were breathing out.

Isandro walked behind her, one hand on the wall. He moved badly — she could hear it in the uneven drag of his step — but he moved without complaint, and when the ceiling dipped low he folded himself down with a patience that seemed practiced, as though he had spent years making himself smaller than he was.

"How far does it go?" he asked.

"To the mill. Maybe half a mile." Wendell kept her voice low, though there was no one to hear. It felt wrong to speak loudly underground, in a place that had been waiting so long. "My grandmother used to tell me stories about the guild gardeners walking these halls at night, carrying seedlings to the boundary. I thought they were just stories. Everything I thought was just stories turns out to have been instructions."

Ahead, the tunnel forked. The left passage sloped down toward the sound of water — the mill stream, running somewhere beneath them, a low continuous hush. The right passage was blocked by a fall of stone, old and settled, and Wendell did not waste her candle on it. She took the left.

The sound of water grew. And with it came something else — a sensation Wendell had no name for, a faint pressure behind her eyes, a warmth in her fingertips, like the moment before a fever breaks. She stopped.

"What is it?" Isandro asked.

"I don't know." She flexed her fingers. "It feels like — standing at a window in winter, with the fire behind you. Like you know if you open it, the cold will come in, and part of you wants it to."

"That's the boundary," Isandro said softly. "You're feeling it because you're gifted. The valley has been holding you back your entire life, Wendell. Holding all of you back. What you're feeling now is the edge of the hold."

"And beyond it?"

"Beyond it, everything the hold was keeping out."

They went on. The tunnel ended at a wooden door, green with age, set into a wall of living earth threaded with tree roots as thick as her arm. Beyond it, Wendell knew, lay the culvert under the mill race, and beyond that the riverbank, and beyond that the fields, and beyond the fields the ridge, and beyond the ridge —

Beyond the ridge was everywhere else. Everywhere else, where magic lived, and where she did not yet know what she was.

She put her hand on the door. It was damp and cold and utterly ordinary, and she found herself hesitating over it the way you hesitate over a letter you have written and cannot unsay.

Behind her, muffled by half a mile of stone and earth, she heard it: a crackle, faint but unmistakable, and beneath the crackle a smell threading down through the ground — resin and smoke. The garden. They were burning the garden early. Not at dawn, as promised, but now, tonight, while the wind was low and the neighbors asleep.

Wendell's hand went still on the door.

She could see it without seeing it: the walled beds her grandmother had taught her to weed, the espaliered pears she had pruned every winter of her life, the glasshouse with its panes like held breath, the herb spiral, the cold frames, the roses her mother had planted before Wendell was born — all of it going up in orange light against the dark, and the hunters standing around it with their torches, watching, because that was what men did around fires. Watching something die and calling it duty.

Something broke open in her chest, quiet and enormous, like ice shifting on a pond.

She didn't cry. She was surprised by that. She had expected to cry, had perhaps been saving tears for exactly this, and instead what came was worse than tears: a terrible clarity, a stillness, in which every detail of the garden presented itself to her memory at once and asked to be kept. The smell of tomato leaves on her fingers. The sound the glasshouse made in hail. The exact green of new pear leaves, brighter than any other green in the world. *Keep this. Keep this. Keep this.* As if memory were a seed-packet too, and she were already planting.

"Wendell." Isandro's voice, very gentle. "We cannot stay. If we stay, the burning was for nothing."

"I know." Her voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of itself. "I know. I'm coming."

She opened the door.

* * *

The culvert let them out beneath the mill, where the wheel hung motionless and the stream ran black and quick over its stones. Wendell crouched at the mouth of the culvert and looked up at the sky, and saw, above the trees to the south, a glow that was not dawn. It pulsed gently, orange against the stars, and sparks rose from it in slow spirals, and each spark that rose was a thing she had tended, rising to become nothing.

She watched until her eyes stung. Then she turned away, and they went along the streambank through the alders, fast and silent, Isandro limping but keeping pace, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that the burning hadn't already said better.

The valley road was empty. They avoided it anyway, cutting across pasture where the sheep lifted their heads and watched them pass with round yellow eyes. The land rose. Behind them the glow of the garden sank lower and lower among the trees, and finally the ridge intervened, and Wendell lost it, and losing it was somehow final in a way that watching it had not been. You can watch a thing burn. Losing sight of it means you will never watch it again.

They climbed through bracken and gorse under a sky salted with stars. The moon was up — a thin crescent, low in the west, and when Wendell glanced at it Isandro followed her glance, and she remembered what he had told her in the cellar of her own home, in the voice of a man confessing: *the thirteenth turn*. Thirteen turns of that thin hook of light, and then the realms would close forever, and everything on both sides of the ridge would wither or starve or forget. Twelve remained. She counted them without wanting to, the way you count the stairs in the dark.

The crest of the ridge was crowned with a stand of old beeches, and the boundary ran beneath them — she knew it the way you know the edge of a roof in the dark, without seeing it. On this side, the valley: her valley, magicless, safe, suffocating. On the other side, the land fell away toward country she had never walked, and the night beyond the beeches looked no different from the night before them, and yet her skin prickled at it, the way skin prickles before lightning.

She stopped at the line. She didn't decide to stop; her body stopped, the way a body stops at the lip of deep water.

"It won't hurt you," Isandro said. He had come up beside her, breathing hard from the climb. "I want to be honest with you, because I haven't been able to be fully honest yet, and I'm sorry for it. Crossing may feel like drowning. It may feel like burning. For someone with your gift, untrained, the return of magic is — a great deal. But it will not harm you. I will not let it."

"You can't promise that," Wendell said. "You don't know what I am. Neither do I."

"No," he agreed. "But I know what the covenant made, and I know what its heir must be. And I know," he added, more quietly, "that you have been carrying a gift in a place built to smother it for twenty-three years. Whatever happens on the other side of that line, Wendell — it has been happening to you your whole life already. This is only the first time you'll be allowed to feel it."

Wendell looked back, one last time, down the long slope of the valley. The garden-glow was gone now, hidden entirely. Somewhere down there her grandmother sat in a kitchen that smelled of burnt paper, listening to hunters' boots on her floors, giving them answers true enough to satisfy and false enough to protect. Somewhere down there Captain Rell was doing his duty, and hating it, or not hating it, and she could not bear to decide which was worse. The valley lay quiet under the crescent moon, beautiful and blind, the way it had lain for thirteen years, dreaming that safety was the same thing as peace.

"Goodbye," she said — to the valley, to the garden, to the girl who had lived in both — and stepped across the line.

* * *

The world came in like a tide.

There was no other word for it. One moment she was standing in cold bracken under ordinary stars, and the next, everything — *everything* — arrived at once, and Wendell's knees went, and Isandro's arm caught her before the ground could.

It was not light, though there was light in it. It was not sound, though it roared. It was — presence. The night itself leaned against her, dense and alive, and every living thing within reach of her senses turned toward her like flowers toward sun. The beeches overhead — she could feel them, all of them, hundreds of years of slow gold-green thought, and they *knew* her, they bent their attention upon her the way a congregation bends toward a bell. The grass at her feet sang — not in words, in pulses, in the language of roots and rain. The moths in the dark traced lines of faint silver meaning. The soil beneath her shoes was not dirt but a vast slow animal, breathing, digesting, remembering, and it recognized her, it *recognized* her, and the recognition poured up through her feet and her bones and her teeth like a river reversing its fall.

And underneath all of it, answering it, rising in her like sap in spring, was the gift.

It had never been like this. In the valley it had been a shimmer, a stain, a faint greening of whatever she touched — a secret she'd spent her life pressing flat. Here it was a second heartbeat, vast and hungry and joyful, filling her chest, her throat, her hands, and her hands — she looked down at them and gasped — her hands were *glowing*, softly, green-gold, and where the light fell, the bracken stirred and straightened, and moss leapt up the nearest beech trunk in a rush of velvet, and a dead fallen branch at her feet budded, leafed, blossomed, all in the space of three heartbeats, white flowers opening in the starlight out of bare dead wood.

"Oh," she said. Or tried to say. The sound came out of her like a sob.

"Wendell. Look at me." Isandro's hands were on her shoulders, his scarred face close, his eyes fierce with something between fear and awe. "Breathe. Don't push it away and don't chase it. Let it pass through you like weather. You are the bank, not the river. Do you hear me? You are the bank."

"I can't—" The gift surged again and she swayed, laughing and crying at once, because it was unbearable and it was *magnificent*, because she had spent her whole life a deaf woman in a silent house and now the whole world was singing to her and she could not make it stop and did not want it to stop, ever, even as it threatened to sweep her away. "It knows me. Isandro, it *knows* me, everything knows me—"

"Yes." His voice cracked slightly on it. "That is what you are. That is what they took from the valley. That is what the crown wants to put in a cage." He gripped her shoulders harder, grounding her, an anchor thrown into floodwater. "Breathe with me. In. Now out. The bank, Wendell. Be the bank."

She breathed. In. Out. The roaring did not stop, but it began, slowly, to find banks of its own — to flow *through* her rather than *at* her, and she discovered, dimly, astonishingly, that she could shape the channel a little, could widen here, narrow there, the way her grandmother had taught her to shape water in the irrigation channels of the garden beds. Gardening. It was gardening. All of it, all along, had been gardening — the whole wild singing world was a bed gone to riot, and she had been apprenticed to tend it since before she could walk.

Gradually, gradually, the flood became a river, and the river became bearable, and bearable became — almost — beautiful. She knelt in the bracken, shaking, drenched in sweat despite the cold, and the white-blossomed branch beside her shed one petal onto her knee, deliberate as a kiss.

Isandro crouched beside her, saying nothing, letting her be.

For a long time Wendell simply breathed and listened. The night was so full. Every silence in the valley had been full of this, all her life, and she had walked through it deaf and called the deafness peace. Grief moved through her again — for the garden, for her grandmother, for the gray safe years — but it moved through her the way the magic did now, a current in a channel, and it did not sweep her away.

At last she said, "What am I?"

Isandro was quiet a moment. When he answered, his voice was careful, and underneath the care she heard something else — guilt, maybe, or fear, held tightly leashed.

"You are the last apprentice of the Thornbury guild," he said. "And the guild was never a guild of gardeners. Not really. It was the keeper of a covenant between your world and mine, and the covenant needs an heir, and the heir — " he stopped. Started again. "The heir has work ahead of her that I can't explain tonight. Tonight you should sleep, and eat, and learn to be the bank. Tomorrow we walk. There are four keystones, Wendell, and the moon is turning, and we are late."

He said it lightly, but she saw his eyes flick upward to the crescent, and she saw what crossed his face before he mastered it — and it was not urgency alone. It was dread. He knew something about the price of this work. He had known it since the garden, since the moment he collapsed among her beans and spoke her name, and he was carrying it the way she had once carried her shimmer: hidden, flattened, pressed down into silence.

Later, she would remember that look. Later, it would matter enormously. But that night, kneeling in flowering bracken under a strangers' sky, with the world singing through her like a struck bell, Wendell Thornbury only nodded, and rose on unsteady legs, and turned her back on the valley forever.

Behind them, far to the south, a single spark from the burning garden rose higher than the rest, caught the wind, and drifted north — following them, it seemed, into the dark.

Wendell watched it go, and did not know why she raised her hand as if to catch it.
