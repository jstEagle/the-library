## 7. What the Gift Costs

Isandro woke before dawn to the sound of Wendell screaming.

Not the scream of a nightmare — he had heard those in the three nights since they'd fled the valley, muffled and brief, the sounds of a woman who had learned early that crying out invited questions. This was different. This was awake.

He found her in the ruin of the shepherd's cottage where they'd sheltered, on her knees in the corner where a tangle of bindweed had forced its way through the collapsed wall. The weed had grown. That was the wrong word for it, he thought, crossing the room — *grown* implied patience, seasons, the slow arithmetic of roots. This bindweed had *erupted*. It coiled across the floorboards in thick green ropes, climbing the broken rafters, and at its heart knelt Wendell with her hands buried in it as though she were drowning and it was the only thing to hold.

"Wendell." He kept his voice low, the way you spoke to a spooked animal, or to fire. "Let go."

"I can't." Her head turned toward him and her eyes were wrong — not glowing, nothing so clean as that, but over-bright, dilated wide, as if she were looking at him from very far away through a keyhole of light. "It's — I felt it dying, the bit outside the wall, frost took it last night, and I only meant to — " She swallowed. The vines tightened around her wrists. Small white flowers were opening along their length, dozens of them, blooming out of season, out of order, straining toward her like faces. "I only meant to help."

"You did help. Now it wants more." He crouched at the edge of the green mass, careful not to touch it. In his own realm this would have been a child's mistake, corrected by any hedge-teacher with a word and a switch of rowan. Here, in a land where magic had been starved for thirteen years, everything that grew was starving too, and one careless kindness could become a flood. "Gift is not charity, Wendell. It's a door. And doors don't care what walks through them in either direction."

The bindweed shivered. One of the flowers turned — actually turned, its face rotating on its stem — to follow his voice.

"Tell me how to stop," she whispered.

"Breathe. Name five things you can hear."

"What?"

"Name them. Out loud."

Her brow furrowed. "Wind. A bird — some kind of lark. Water, under the floor. Your breathing." A pause. "My own heartbeat."

"And your hands," Isandro said gently. "Your hands are yours. Not the vine's. Feel where you end and it begins."

For a long moment nothing happened. Then, slowly, like a tide going out, the brightness in her eyes receded. The vines loosened, slackened, and began — with an almost audible sigh — to wilt back into ordinary weed, ordinary hunger, ordinary green. Wendell slumped forward onto her hands among the dead-and-living tangle, shaking.

Isandro sat down beside her in the dust and did not touch her. Not yet. Touch was a language, and they were still learning each other's grammar.

* * *

They ate breakfast on the cottage's broken doorstep: hard bread, a pear from her pack that had ripened three days too fast because she'd carried it against her side. Isandro watched her turn the fruit over in her fingers and decided, watching her decide whether to eat it or apologize to it, that the lesson could no longer wait.

"Do you know why the crown hunts the gifted?" he asked.

"Because we're dangerous." She said it flatly, the way you repeated something you'd been told so often it had lost its shape. "Because magic is a plague. Because of what happened thirteen years ago."

"Three answers," Isandro said, "and none of them true. Or rather — all of them true, and none of them the reason." He broke his bread and gave half to a robin that had been eyeing them from the wall; the bird took it without fear, which told him something about how much of his nature still clung to him even here, in this drained and quiet valley. "The crown hunts the gifted because the gifted are untrained. Because a girl can kneel in a ruined cottage and nearly drown in a weed, and there is no one left in this whole kingdom to tell her why."

"There's you."

"Yes." He let that sit between them. "There's me. One guardian, wounded, far from home, teaching in borrowed words. Multiply me by nothing, Wendell. There is no one else. Thirteen years ago every teacher, every guild, every covenant-keeper in this valley was severed from the people they served. Do you know what an untrained gift does?"

"Grows weeds."

"It grows whatever it's pointed at, and it points itself at fear." He set down the bread. "A child who is frightened of the dark and has a gift for light will burn the house down trying to make a candle. A boy who grieves and has a gift for water will flood a village with his tears. It isn't malice. It's never malice. It's a scream with power behind it. And when enough screams happen close together — " He gestured, taking in the valley beyond the cottage, the gray fields, the nervous villages, the roads with their new-crowned proclamations fluttering on every post. "The queen doesn't hate you, Wendell. She's afraid of you. All of you. And she has thirteen years of burned houses and drowned fields to point at when anyone asks why."

Wendell was quiet for a while. The lark sang somewhere above the ruin.

"My grandmother knew," she said at last. "She knew what I was. She made me bury it. Made me wear gloves in the garden when I thought it was about dirt under my nails." Her jaw tightened. "She was trying to keep me safe."

"She was trying to keep you hidden. They are not always the same thing." Isandro said it softly, but he watched it land — watched her flinch and then, slowly, nod, because she had already begun to suspect it herself. "Hiding a gift doesn't shrink it. It just means no one teaches you the walls of the garden. So today, I teach you the walls."

* * *

He took her to the stream behind the cottage, where the water ran cold and quick over brown stones, and he made her sit with her hands in her lap and do nothing at all, which he could see within a minute was the hardest thing he could have asked of her.

"The first law," he said, "is that gift follows attention. Where your mind goes, your power goes. So we begin with where your mind is allowed to be."

"That's it? Attention?"

"That is everything. Everything else is decoration." He knelt opposite her, the stream chattering between them. "Watch. I'm going to attend to the water — only attend, nothing more — and you're going to tell me what changes."

He opened himself to the stream the way he had done ten thousand times in his own realm: not pushing, not pulling, simply *noticing* it so completely that the noticing became a kind of touch. The water brightened. That was the only word for it — the brown stones beneath the surface seemed to come up closer, the current to run clearer, as if the stream remembered it was made of mountain snow.

Wendell's breath caught. "You didn't *do* anything."

"I did the only thing. I paid attention without wanting anything back." He closed the connection and the stream went ordinary again, and her face fell a little, and he almost smiled. "Yes. It feels like loss. That feeling is the second law: the gift will always offer you more. It will always say *just a little further, just a little deeper.* The stream can give you its whole history if you ask — every rain that fed it, every fish, every foot that ever crossed it. And it will give it gladly, because water loves to be known. And you will drown, Wendell. Not in the water. In the knowing."

"Is that what happened with the bindweed?" Her voice was small.

"That was a kinder version of it. The bindweed was dying and you pitied it, and pity is an open hand. It poured itself into you looking for rescue, and you poured yourself into it looking to help, and for a moment there was no border between you at all." He held up one finger. "First law: attention. Second law: the gift always offers more than you can carry. Third law —"

He stopped.

Because her hands, resting open in her lap, had begun to shimmer. Faintly, like heat-haze, like moonlight on still water — the same shimmer he had seen the first night in her garden, on the leaves she'd touched. But now it wasn't fading. It was gathering. And the grass around her knees was leaning toward her. Not bending in wind — there was no wind — *leaning*, blades curving inward like a congregation, like a crowd pressing close to hear good news.

"Wendell," Isandro said carefully. "What are you remembering?"

Her eyes had gone distant again, but softly this time, unfocused rather than drowning. "Nothing. I mean — the stream. You said water remembers, and I thought about — " She blinked. The shimmer guttered. The grass eased back, blade by blade, disappointed. "I thought about my mother's hands. She used to wash my hair in a basin like that stream water. Cold off the hills. I haven't thought of that in years."

Isandro sat very still.

In his realm, gifts had shapes. His own answered to boundaries — thresholds, walls, the liminal places where one thing ended and another began; it was why the covenant had chosen him for its guardian, why he could stand at the bridge between realms and not be torn apart by standing in two worlds at once. Others answered to stone, to birdsong, to the turning of seasons. But in all his years as guardian he had never felt a gift shaped quite like hers.

Growing things, yes. That was plain. But underneath it, braided through it so tightly he couldn't yet tell which was root and which was branch —

Memory.

The grass leaned toward her grief. The bindweed bloomed toward her panic. The pear had ripened in the warmth of being carried. And now the mere shape of a remembered tenderness had called the living world close around her like moths.

*Of course,* he thought, and the thought landed in him with a weight he hadn't expected, cold as riverbed stone. *Of course it would be memory. Of course the heir of a covenant sealed in memory would have a gift that answers to memory.*

He said none of this. He looked at her kneeling in the leaning grass, waiting for him to go on, trusting him already — three days of flight and terror, and she trusted him, and the fourth law of the gift rose in his throat like a stone he had to swallow past:

*A guardian never lies to the one he guards.*

He swallowed it.

"Third law," he said instead, "is that your gift knows you better than you know it. It goes where your heart goes. Which means the work is not to control the gift, Wendell. The work is to know your own heart well enough that you're never surprised by where it leads."

* * *

They practiced until the sun stood high and then past it, and she was a better student than she believed and worse than she needed to be, which is to say she was exactly herself. She could hold the stream's attention for a count of thirty before it began pouring its memories into her. She could wake a dormant apple tree to blossom and rein it back before the blossoms became a burden the branches couldn't bear. Twice she slipped — once into a blackberry bramble that tried to climb her arm like a lover, once into a patch of mushrooms whose slow underground thoughts nearly took her somewhere she might not have come back from — and both times he talked her back, naming things, grounding her, his voice the rope she climbed.

By late afternoon she sat in the cottage doorway, hollowed out and shining, eating the last of the bread.

"It's like holding water in my hands," she said. "Open palms. The moment I try to grip it, it's gone — or worse, it's everywhere."

"Now you understand it better than the queen ever will." Isandro settled beside her, at a careful distance. "Magic isn't a weapon. Weapons want to be swung. Magic wants to be *met*. Like another person." He paused. "Which is why the crown's machine will fail, someday, whoever builds it. You cannot hold a person in a cage and get their gift. You only get their screaming. And screaming, as I said, has power behind it."

She turned to look at him. In the low gold light her eyes were tired and clear and searching, and he realized too late that he had taught her too well — that attention was a door, and she had just opened hers, and it was pointed at him.

"You've been holding something back," she said. "Since the garden. Since the first night. Every time I ask what the covenant needs from me, you answer with the next step and never the one after it. You talk in laws, Isandro. Three laws today. Is there a fourth?"

The stone in his throat again. The stream chattered on behind them, carrying its harmless memories downhill.

"A guardian's first duty," he said, "is to bring the heir to the bridge alive and able to stand. Everything else serves that. When you need to know the rest, you'll know it — not because I've hidden it, but because knowing it too soon would put a weight on you that could break you before you're strong enough to carry it."

"That's not an answer. That's a law-shaped hole where an answer should be."

He laughed despite himself — a short, startled sound, the first in longer than he could measure. "You'd have made a fine guardian."

"Don't change the subject."

"I'm not." He met her eyes, and gave her what truth he could afford, which was more than none and less than enough. "I will tell you everything before the bridge. On my name, on my office, on the covenant itself. But not today. Today you've given enough — I watched you pour yourself into a bramble and a stream and a tree, and you came back every time, and that is the bravest thing I've seen since I crossed into this valley. Let tomorrow's weight be tomorrow's."

She studied him for a long moment. He felt her attention on him like sunlight, like the lean of grass, and understood suddenly and completely that this was what it would be like to be known by her — that her gift and her gaze were the same thing, and that lying to her was not merely dishonesty but a kind of vandalism.

"Fine," she said finally. "But I'm keeping count, Isandro. Every question you defer. I have a gardener's memory for debts."

"I believe you." And he did, and that was the trouble.

She went inside to sleep, and he stayed on the doorstep as the light failed, listening to the stream, feeling the faint pull of the growing things around the ruin all orienting themselves — grass, weed, ivy, the old apple tree gone wild by the wall — toward the small warm presence sleeping inside, the way iron filings find a lodestone, the way the lost find north.

Untrained, the crown feared her. Trained, she would be worse — for the crown, and for him. Because he had felt the shape of her gift today, its second root, the one braided under the first, and he knew now what the covenant would ask of an heir whose power answered to growing things *and to memory*.

He had known since the garden. He had told himself he would tell her when the time was right.

Sitting alone in the dark with the plants leaning toward her window, Isandro admitted, at last and silently, that there was no such time — that every hour he waited made the truth heavier and the trust thinner, and that the debt she was keeping count of was growing interest.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow I'll tell her.

Beyond the eastern hills, thin as a paring, the ninth moon was rising — and somewhere far away, in a marsh that forgot, a third keystone waited, patient as memory, for the heir who did not yet know what it would cost her to reach it.
