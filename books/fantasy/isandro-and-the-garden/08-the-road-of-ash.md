## 8. The Road of Ash

The valley let them go the way a mother lets go of a child's hand at a schoolhouse door — reluctantly, and only because it must.

Wendell looked back three times before the hills closed behind them. The first time, she could still see the smoke from her garden rising thin and gray against the morning, a wound in the sky where the guild's last walls had stood. The second time, she could see only the hills. The third time, she stopped looking altogether, because Isandro had gone quiet beside her in the particular way of a man who is letting you grieve and does not trust himself to say so.

They walked north through country she had never seen, though she had studied its maps in her grandmother's ledger-room a hundred times. Maps, she was learning, were a kind of lie. They showed roads and rivers and the neat rectangles of farmsteads. They did not show what the land had *become*.

She noticed it first at the edge of the barley country, two days out of the valley.

The barley was wrong. Not dead — that would have been a mercy, an honest thing, a field that had failed. This field had *succeeded* at something else entirely. The stalks stood too tall, taller than a man, and their heads were not heads of grain but something in between: the pale, furled suggestion of leaves, of branches, of a crown that no barley had ever worn. The wind moved through the field and the sound it made was not the dry whisper of grain but the deep, creaking sigh of a forest remembering itself.

"Stop," Isandro said, and caught her arm.

She had already taken a step toward the field. She hadn't decided to. Her hand had risen of its own accord, fingers half-curled, the way they did when she was about to touch something living — and she realized with a cold turn of her stomach that the field was leaning toward her. All of it. Thousands of stalks bending, slowly, silently, like an audience rising.

"They can feel you," Isandro said quietly. "Everything growing can feel you now. You've crossed the boundary, and the land here is — hungry. It's been thirteen years without anyone to listen to it."

Wendell lowered her hand. The barley straightened, grudging, the way a dog lies back down when its master turns away. She looked at the field for a long moment, and what she felt was not fear, exactly. It was something worse. It was *recognition*. The barley was not wrong, some small voice in her said. The barley was only saying what it had always wanted to say, and no one had listened for thirteen years, and she understood exactly what that was like.

"We should keep to the road," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she meant it to.

They kept to the road.

* * *

The village of Harrow's End announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and tallow and something underneath both, a sourness like milk left out in the sun. Then the houses: low, gray-timbered, huddled together at the bottom of a shallow bowl of land as if they were afraid of the horizon. Every window had a charm nailed above it. Wendell had seen charms before, in the valley — little braided knots of straw, horseshoes, bundles of dried herbs, the folk-magic of people who had no real magic at all and needed something to nail above their doors anyway.

But these charms were different. These were not against bad luck or lightning.

These were against *people*.

She saw it as they came down the lane: iron nails driven through sprigs of rowan, hung at every threshold. Chalk circles around the wells. And on the village green, a post — a tall, fresh-cut post, its wood still pale, with iron rings set into it at the height of a standing person's wrists.

Isandro saw it too. His jaw tightened, but he said nothing, and they walked on, two travelers, a man with a walking staff and a woman in a gardener's coat with a seed-packet sewn into its lining. Ordinary. Invisible. She kept her eyes down and her hands in her pockets and thought: *I could have been nailed to that post. I could have been born in this village and nailed to that post, and no one in the valley would ever have known my name.*

An old woman was drawing water at the well. She looked up as they passed, and her eyes went to Wendell's hands before they went to her face — a habit, Wendell realized, a practiced habit, the way a farmer checks a horse's teeth. Looking for the shine. Looking for the shimmer that living things left on her skin, the faint green-gold luster she had spent her whole life hiding and could not, apparently, stop producing.

Wendell pulled her hands deeper into her pockets. The old woman's gaze slid away, satisfied or unconvinced, Wendell couldn't tell which.

"You'll want to be gone by dark," the old woman said. Not unkindly. That was the terrible part. Her voice was the voice of someone giving useful directions to a stranger. "The hunt comes through on the north road tomorrow. Captain's men. They pay silver for names." She lifted her yoke, the buckets swinging. "Silver's more than most of us have seen in a year."

"We're only passing through," Isandro said.

"Aye." The old woman's mouth twisted. "They all say that."

* * *

They took a room at the village's only inn because the light was failing and Isandro's strength was failing faster — she could see it in the way he leaned on his staff now, in the gray undertone of his skin, the bark-scarring along his jaw looking less like a mark and more like a spreading rot. He ate little. He slept less. When she woke in the night, she found him sitting by the window with the shutter cracked, looking north, at the sky, at the moon — at the moon, always, counting something she couldn't see.

"Twelve more turns," he said, when he noticed her watching. "Give or take."

"Give or take?"

"The moon is not a clock." He almost smiled. "It's a door. And doors don't always close when you expect them to."

She sat up in the narrow bed, the blanket around her shoulders. Below, in the common room, the last of the villagers were drifting home, and she could hear their voices through the floorboards — low, tired, threaded with that sour note she'd caught in the air outside.

"They're afraid," she said.

"They have reason to be."

"The covenant was supposed to protect them. That's what it was *for*, isn't it? The guild kept the balance, and the balance kept the land whole, and the land kept the people fed." She heard her own voice rising and couldn't stop it. "My grandmother's guild. My parents. Thirteen years they've been gone, and this is what's left. Barley that wants to be a forest. A village with a post for nailing people to."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was gentle in a way that made it worse.

"You're angry at the right people, Wendell. But be careful where you point it."

"Who else is there to point it at? The guild is ash. My grandmother is dying. My parents—" Her throat closed. "My parents are a question I can't even ask anymore. So yes. I'm angry. And I'm angry at *myself*, because I'm the heir of the people who let this happen, and I didn't even know there was anything to let happen. I watered roses, Isandro. I watered roses while the world outside the wall was — this."

He came and sat on the edge of the bed, careful of the narrow space between them, the way he was always careful of distances. "Then let the anger be a compass," he said. "Not a fire. A fire burns whatever's nearest. A compass at least points somewhere."

She wanted to tell him that was the sort of thing people said when they weren't the ones burning. She didn't. She lay back down and stared at the ceiling beams, and somewhere below, a villager was saying a name — she caught it through the floor, half-drowned in the inn's creaking — a name, and the word *gifted*, and the word *silver*.

She did not sleep again.

* * *

They left Harrow's End before dawn, taking the river road north, and it was on the river road that Wendell saw the water run backward.

She stopped so abruptly that Isandro walked three paces before he noticed. "What is it?"

She pointed.

The river ran beside the road, wide and brown and sluggish — and it was flowing uphill. Not violently. Not with any drama. It simply flowed the wrong way, calmly, persistently, north toward its own source, as if it had forgotten which direction rivers were supposed to go and no one was left to correct it.

"That's not possible," Wendell said.

"Much of what you'll see on this road is not possible," Isandro said. "The covenant didn't just hold the two realms apart. It held the world's *agreements* together. Water agrees to run downhill. Seeds agree to grow into what they were planted as. Seasons agree to follow one another. When the covenant broke, all those agreements became — negotiable." He looked at the backward river with an expression she was learning to read: not wonder, but a kind of weary grief, the grief of a man seeing a friend's face gone strange with illness. "The land isn't broken, Wendell. It's *unbound*. And unbound things remember what they were before they agreed to be anything at all."

The crops that remember being forests. The rivers that run backward. Her grandmother's ledger had a phrase for it, she remembered suddenly, a phrase in the old guild hand that she'd always taken for poetry: *when the hedge fails, the wild comes home.* She had thought it meant weeds. She understood now that it meant everything.

They walked on. The backward river ran beside them, going home the wrong way.

* * *

The second village had no name on any sign, because the sign had been burned.

Wendell saw the marks of it from a long way off — the black scar on the green where something had been put to the torch, the new timber patching a roof, the way the dogs watched them from under carts with the flat, unblinking stare of animals that had learned what men were capable of. The village had been punished for something. She didn't have to ask what. She saw the answer chalked on the wall of the well-house, in a crown official's careful hand, meant to be read by everyone:

*HARBORING. FINED AND BURNED. LET THIS BE A LESSON.*

Under it, in a different hand — smaller, hurried, half-erased by rain — someone had written: *she was only a child.*

Wendell stood in front of the wall for a long time.

"She was only a child," she repeated. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, far away. "Someone here hid a gifted child. And the crown burned — what? A house? A barn? And fined them, and marked them, and the whole village will hate that child now, wherever she is, whoever she was. Because of what she is." She turned to Isandro, and her hands were shaking, and she was horrified to find that the shaking wasn't only grief. It was power. She could feel it rising in her the way heat rises off a stone in summer, answering her fury, the seedlings of the roadside weeds turning their heads toward her like sunflowers. "Isandro. The weeds are *looking at me*."

"Breathe," he said. He didn't touch her. He had learned, in the days since the boundary, not to touch her when the gift rose. "Breathe. You're not a hearth-fire, Wendell. You're a gardener. What does a gardener do when a plant grows wild?"

She dragged air into her lungs. In. Out. The way he'd taught her. *Don't fight it. Don't feed it. Hold it like water in cupped hands.* "She — prunes it. Guides it. Gives it a frame to grow on."

"And when there is no frame?"

"Then it takes whatever shape it can." The shaking slowed. The weeds subsided, slowly, reluctantly, like a crowd disappointed by its prophet. Wendell pressed her palms against her thighs and felt the green-gold shimmer fade from her skin, and looked up at the chalked words one more time. *She was only a child.* "This is the cost," she said quietly. "This is what my guild's failure looks like. Not the barley, not the river. This. A burned sign. A child nobody will name. People nailing iron above their doors against their own neighbors."

"Yes," Isandro said. He didn't soften it. She was grateful for that, in a distant, bruised way. "And it will get worse before the thirteenth turn. Fear is a crop too, Wendell. It grows fastest in broken ground."

* * *

They were three miles past the nameless village when the boy found them.

He came out of a stand of birches at the road's bend — a thin, quick boy of perhaps ten, barefoot, with the wary set of a creature that had learned to move between shadows. Wendell saw him before Isandro did; she felt him, actually, a small bright pressure against her gift, like a candle flame seen across a dark field. Her whole body turned toward him before her mind caught up.

The boy froze when he saw them. His eyes went to Isandro — to the bark-scarring, the wrongness of him — and went wide. Then he turned to run.

"Wait!" Wendell called, and then, without thinking, without any decision at all, she did the thing she had done a thousand times in her garden with wilted seedlings and sulking roses and frightened new transplants. She opened her gift toward him like an open hand. *It's all right. Nothing here will hurt you. Nothing here is hungry for you.*

The boy stopped. Turned back. Stared at her with an expression she recognized from her own childhood mirror — the look of someone who has spent their whole life hiding a light and has just seen it answered in a stranger's face.

"You're one," he said. "You're *one of them*."

"I'm a gardener," Wendell said. And then, because it was a lie and he would know it, and because she was tired of lies: "Yes. I'm one."

The boy looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached into his shirt and pulled out a small cloth bundle, and unwrapped it with the care of a priest handling relics, and held it out to her.

Inside was an apple. A perfect apple, red-gold, fresh — impossibly fresh, in a country where the crops remembered being forests and the rivers had forgotten their way. And around its stem, faintly, pulsing softly like a slow heartbeat, glowed a thread of light that Wendell recognized with a jolt that went through her like a bell being struck.

Keystone-light. Or something very like it.

"Where did you get this?" Isandro asked, very quietly, and all the weariness had gone out of his voice, replaced by something sharp and bright and urgent.

"The lady in the wood," the boy said. "The one who remembers everything. She said — she said a gardener would come, walking north with a man made of trees. She said to give it to her." He thrust the apple toward Wendell again, insistent now, urgent. "She said to tell you: *the orchard remembers the first covenant, and so does the fruit.*" His eyes were enormous. "She said to tell you that you're running out of moons. And that the road ahead is watched."

Wendell took the apple. It was warm in her hands, warmer than fruit should be, and through her gift she could feel it — not just an apple, but an *agreement*, an old one, a living contract written in sweetness and seed, older than the guild, older than the breaking. Somewhere north, a woman who remembered everything was waiting.

"Watched by whom?" Isandro asked.

The boy glanced back the way he'd come, at the road they'd walked, at the thin line of dust rising on the southern horizon where the road bent out of sight.

"By the crown," he said. "They're a day behind you. Maybe less." He was already backing toward the birches, bare feet silent on the leaf-litter. "The lady says don't take the north road. The lady says the north road is where they're herding you." And then he was gone, quick and small and bright, swallowed by the trees, leaving Wendell holding a warm apple and the knowledge that the hunt had found their scent — and that someone in the wood had known, all along, exactly where they would be.
