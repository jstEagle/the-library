## 9. Captain Rell

The rain had followed them out of the low country for three days, and Captain Rell had learned to read it the way he once read Wendell Thornbury's moods: by what it refused to say.

He crouched at the edge of the orchard road, fingers pressed into the churned mud where a cart had passed in the night — but not only a cart. Beside the wheel-ruts, half-filled with water, was the print of a bare foot. Long, narrow, the heel too deep for a man of ordinary weight walking on level ground. And beside it, smaller, quicker, the print of a boot with a patched sole that he recognized because he had teased its owner about that patch when they were fourteen years old, sitting on a wall above her grandmother's garden, splitting a stolen apple between them.

*You walk like you're apologizing to the ground,* he'd said.

*At least I don't stomp like the king's own cavalry,* she'd answered, and thrown the apple core at his head.

Rell closed his hand slowly around the mud until it squeezed out between his fingers. Then he stood, wiped his palm on his cloak, and called back down the column in a voice that gave away nothing at all.

"Tracks head north through the orchard. Two on foot, one favoring his left side. We'll pick up the pace."

Behind him, twelve riders in crown grey shifted in their saddles, and Serah Vane — the queen's own eyes, seconded to his hunt with a writ signed in ink the color of dried blood — turned her horse alongside his. She had a face like a closed ledger and eyes that never finished reading you.

"Bare feet," she said. "In this weather."

"He's not from here," Rell said. "The wounded one. His people go unshod in their own country, or so the old stories say." He kept his tone flat, bored, the voice of a man reciting facts he'd memorized for an examination. "The girl will have given him her boots by now if his feet are bad. Watch for a second set of prints wearing them."

Vane studied him a moment longer than was comfortable. She always did. Then she wheeled her horse north without another word, and Rell let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding, and hated himself a little for how good it felt to have lied well.

* * *

He had known Wendell Thornbury since before either of them had names for what they were to each other.

That was the trouble with growing up in a valley so small the church bell could be heard from every doorstep: there was nowhere for a friendship to hide while it became something else. They had climbed the same pear tree behind the guild wall. They had fished the mill pond with bent pins and caught nothing and called it a fine afternoon anyway. When his father died — the winter Rell was eleven, coughing blood into a rag by the fire while the physicians shook their heads and charged double for it — it was Wendell who came every day for a month and sat with him in the cold room where no one else wanted to be, saying nothing, doing nothing, just *being there*, which was the only medicine anyone had ever offered him that worked.

And when the crown's recruiting officer came through the valley and offered a poor captain's orphan a uniform, a wage, and a way out, it was Wendell who stood at the edge of the square and did not ask him to stay. He remembered that most of all. She had watched him sign the papers with her hands folded tight, and when he looked up at her she smiled, and the smile was real, and it was the worst thing anyone had ever done to him.

*"Go be somebody,"* she'd said. *"Come back and tell me about it."*

He had come back. Twice a year, at first, then once a year, then in letters that grew shorter as his rank grew longer. And somewhere in those thirteen years of absence, the valley had become a place he visited instead of a place he was from, and Wendell had become a memory he kept in the same pocket as his mother's face and the smell of his father's workshop — precious, and fading, and never quite examined too closely, because some things you do not look at directly unless you are prepared for what looking costs.

Then the proclamation had come down from the capital like a landslide, and the word *gifted* had been written across every doorpost in the kingdom, and Rell had been given a company and a commission and a list of names.

Wendell Thornbury's name had been third from the top.

* * *

They found the burned garden at dusk.

It was still smoking when they rode up — the guild walls breached, the glasshouses shattered, the beds of rare and careful things blackened to skeletons. The hunters who had come ahead of him, the ones under Lieutenant Corvane, had done thorough work. Too thorough. Rell sat his horse at the gate and looked at the ruin of the only beautiful thing in the valley and felt something in his chest turn over slowly, like a stone in a streambed.

"Report," Vane said, dismounting.

Corvane came at a jog, helmet under his arm, sweat cutting clean lines through the ash on his face. "Guildhouse empty, Captain. The old woman — the grandmother — dead in her chair. Heart, by the look of her, though there's burn damage; she may have set the fire herself. Records burned. Tunnels under the east wall, old ones, guild-built — we found the grate forced from inside. They went out that way, hours ahead of us." He hesitated. "There was also this."

He held out a seed-packet. Plain brown paper, wax-sealed, stamped with a sigil Rell knew better than his own family crest: the thorn-and-bloom of the Thornbury guild, the mark that had been carved over the valley's wells and stitched into its harvest banners for as long as anyone could remember.

"Found where?" Vane asked.

"Dropped near the tunnel mouth, my lady. Or left."

Vane took it, turned it over twice, and passed it to Rell without ceremony. "The heir keeps her tools close. She'll want this back." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Or she left it for us to find, and it's bait, and she's cleverer than her file suggests. Either way, Captain, we ride tonight."

Rell put the packet in his breast pocket. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"The orders," he said, and made himself say it plainly, in front of the men, because that was the safest ground. "Confirm them for me again. All gifted taken alive."

"All gifted taken alive," Vane repeated, and now she did smile, thin and cold as frost on a window. "Unharmed, unmarked, and delivered to the palace conservatory within the fortnight. The queen has uses for them, Captain. Living uses." She glanced back at the smoking garden. "Dead ones would be a waste of the crown's investment. See that your men understand. Any hunter who kills a gifted subject answers to me personally, and I am not a forgiving correspondent."

Rell nodded and turned to give the order, and his voice did not shake, and he counted that as the day's single victory.

But that night, in camp, he took out the seed-packet and broke the seal, and inside found not seeds but a strip of paper folded eight times, covered in his grandmother's-era handwriting — no, in *the* handwriting, the one he'd seen on the guild ledgers Wendell used to copy out as punishment for talking in class. A map. A route north through the orchard country, past the drowned chapel at Merrow's Ford, drawn in a hand that trembled slightly at the river crossings.

And beneath the map, in different ink, fresher, hurried:

*If you're reading this, Rell, then I'm sorry, and I'm not sorry, and both of those are true. You'll understand which parts later. Please don't follow us. Please follow us. I know.*

He read it four times. Then he fed it to the campfire piece by piece and watched the words curl and go, and sat alone long after the fire had burned down to its red arithmetic, listening to the rain come back.

*Please don't follow us. Please follow us.*

She had known he would be the one holding it. She had trusted him with the map precisely because he was hunting her — because a map in a hunted woman's pocket is safest in the pocket of the man who wants to catch her gently.

Or she had judged him correctly, and that was worse.

* * *

They gained on them over the next two days. Not quickly — Rell saw to that, with a master's collection of small delays: a wrong turning at a fork he pretended to misread, a halt to rest horses that didn't need resting, an hour lost interrogating a shepherd who had, coincidentally, seen nothing. Each delay was defensible. Each was deniable. Each bought Wendell perhaps twenty minutes, and twenty minutes multiplied by a dozen small treasons might amount to a life.

Vane noticed. Of course she noticed. She said nothing, but she began riding closer to him, and her notebook — he had seen it once, briefly, a slim black book in which she recorded things in a cipher he did not recognize — appeared more often in her hands when his name was mentioned.

On the third night he dreamed of the pear tree.

They were children in the dream, the way memory flattens and brightens things, and the pears were impossibly green and the sun was warm on the wall, and Wendell was laughing at something he couldn't hear, and then the dream shifted the way dreams do, and he was standing in the burned garden, and the pear tree was a black skeleton against a grey sky, and Wendell stood among the ashes in her patched boots with a stranger's blood on her hands, and she said, in a voice like the space between bells: *"Which oath, Rell? You only get to keep one."*

He woke with his hand on his sword hilt and the camp silent around him except for the sentry's slow footsteps and the enormous indifferent noise of the rain.

* * *

The tracks told the story plainly enough by the fourth day, and the story frightened him.

The bare-footed man's prints were failing. Shorter stride, deeper heel-drag, a stumble marked in the mud outside an abandoned barn where they had clearly sheltered — and beside the stumble, a smear of something dark that Rell knelt and touched and did not taste, because he already knew. Blood. Old blood, thinned by rain, but too much of it, and the trail of it leading on northward with a stubbornness that told him everything about the man who was bleeding and the woman who refused to leave him.

And threaded through all of it, faint but unmistakable to a man who had grown up in a gardener's valley, were the other signs. Grass greener along the path where the girl had walked, green out of season, green in defiance of the calendar. A dead hedge beside the road pushing out one impossible bud. Wildflowers opening in the ditch in a line as straight as a fence, following her footsteps like dogs following a whistle.

Magic. Returning to the land the way blood returns to a numbed limb — painfully, and all at once, and in the wrong places.

The villages noticed. In the last hamlet before the ford, the doors were shuttered and someone had painted the crown's anti-gift sigil on the well, crude and fresh, and an old man spat at Rell's boots as he passed and called him a *witch-catcher* in a voice that made clear which side of the word he considered the insult. The mood of the country was turning. Rell had served long enough to know what a kingdom looked like when it was deciding whom to be afraid of, and he did not like the view.

"They're close," Vane said, reining in beside him at the ridge above Merrow's Ford. Below, the river ran high and loud, and beyond it the drowned chapel's tower rose out of its flooded churchyard like a finger raised in warning. "A day. Perhaps less, if the big one doesn't last the night." She looked at Rell sidelong. "Your quarry is dying, Captain. That changes the arithmetic. A dead guardian is worth nothing to the conservatory. If we cannot take him alive, the orders permit—"

"I know what the orders permit."

"Do you?" Vane's voice stayed pleasant, conversational, terrible. "Because I've been watching you, Captain Rell, and I confess I can't make you out. You delay. You question. You stand in burning gardens looking like a man at a funeral. And yet you've led this hunt better than any officer I've ridden with, because you think like your quarry — you know where she'll go before she does." She smiled her frost smile. "Loyalty and love wear very similar boots, Captain. From behind, they're indistinguishable. I'd hate to misidentify yours."

The rain fell between them. Down in the flooded churchyard, something pale moved beneath the water — or seemed to. Rell thought of the chapel on the map, and the wraiths the old stories assigned to such places, and the fragment of a night thirteen years gone that even he half-remembered: bells ringing at midnight, his mother pulling the curtains shut, the whole valley holding its breath around some wound nobody would name.

"My loyalty is to the crown," he said.

"Yes," Vane agreed lightly. "That's what I'd assumed. I do so hate being surprised." She gathered her reins. "We take them at the chapel. Alive, Captain. Both of them. The queen is very specific about the girl."

Rell watched her ride down toward the ford, and stood a long moment on the ridge with the rain running off his chin, and let himself, finally, look directly at the thing he had carried in his pocket for thirteen years.

He loved her. He had loved her since the pear tree, and he had spent a career building a man who could survive not saying so, and now the crown had handed him a writ and a company and pointed him at her like a loaded crossbow, and somewhere ahead of him, in a drowned chapel full of the dead's unfinished business, Wendell Thornbury was kneeling beside a dying stranger and choosing — freely, knowingly, with open eyes — a path that ended either in the queen's cages or in a country Rell could not follow her into.

*Which oath, Rell? You only get to keep one.*

He turned his horse down the slope, and gave the order to advance, and told himself — the way he had told himself for thirteen years — that he would decide when the moment came, and that he would know the right choice when he saw it.

Below him, far off through the rain, at the edge of the flooded churchyard, a light flickered that was not torchlight and not lightning. It moved the way green things move: slowly, and with intent.

And Rell spurred forward, because whatever happened at the chapel, he would not learn it secondhand.
