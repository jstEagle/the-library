## 10. The First Keystone

The drowned chapel announced itself by smell before sight: old stone and river silt, the green rot of things that had been holy once and were now merely wet. Wendell smelled it through the trees a full hour before they saw the water, and she thought, absurdly, of her grandmother's root cellar — that same cold breath rising from the earth, promising preservation and decay in equal measure.

"Chapel of Saint Vessia," Isandro said, pushing aside a curtain of willow branches. "Or it was. Before the breaking, this whole valley was orchard land. The river took it when the covenant failed."

The water lay before them like a sheet of dark glass, and beneath it, unmistakable even through the murk, stood the chapel itself — roofless, its bell tower leaning at an angle no honest architecture would permit, its arched windows glowing faintly from within with a light that had no source Wendell could name. It did not look drowned. It looked *waiting*.

"The keystone is down there," she said. Not a question. She could feel it, or feel something — a low pull behind her sternum, like the ache of a tooth, like homesickness for a place she'd never been.

Isandro nodded. His bark-scarring caught the afternoon light, ridged and pale along his jaw. "The first of four. And the best guarded, because it was the first to be lost." He crouched at the water's edge and studied it the way he studied everything — as though reading instructions written somewhere only he could see. "There's a wraith here. A memory-wraith."

"A what?"

"A thing that eats remembering. Not minds — minds it leaves alone. It takes the *memories* themselves, drinks them out of you like marrow from a bone. The guild used them once, I'm told. Kept them as archivists." His mouth twisted. "The old guardians thought a wraith would make a fitting keeper for a stone whose whole purpose is to hold what must not be forgotten."

Wendell's hands had gone cold inside her gloves. She pulled them off, flexed her fingers, watched the faint shimmer rise off her skin like heat-haze — stronger here, so close to the borderlands where magic pooled and gathered. "How do we get past it?"

"We don't." Isandro turned to look at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not fear exactly, but the careful blankness of a man arranging his features over a wound. "It lets us pass if we give it nothing to take. Memories tied to strong feeling are what it wants. Grief. Love. Terror. You have to walk through it thinking about nothing at all. About bread. About weather. About the price of turnips."

"And you?" she said. "What will it take from you?"

He smiled, and it didn't reach his eyes. "I've had thirteen years to practice thinking about turnips."

* * *

They went in at dusk, when the light on the water went copper and then grey, because Isandro said the wraith's attention followed the sun the way a plant follows warmth. There was a path under the surface — old paving stones, raised just enough above the silt that their tops broke the waterline, a stepping-stone road leading straight to the chapel doors. Someone had built it deliberately. Someone had wanted pilgrims to walk out across the flood to worship.

The water came up past Wendell's knees, then her waist, shockingly cold, pulling at her clothes like hands. She kept her breathing slow and shallow and fixed her mind, as instructed, on turnips. On the knobbly yellow ones her grandmother grew along the south wall. On how they tasted roasted with a little honey. On whether honey would keep until winter, and whether—

Her mother's face rose unbidden, warm and laughing, hair escaping its braid, saying *Wendy-love, mind the roses* —

She slammed the door on it so hard she gasped aloud. The water around her shivered. Somewhere below, in the flooded dark between the stones, something vast shifted its attention, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound.

*Nothing,* she told herself fiercely. *Turnips. Turnips.*

Beside her, Isandro moved through the water without disturbing it, which should have frightened her more than it did. She was beginning to collect a small catalogue of things about him that should have frightened her more than they did.

The chapel doors stood open — great oak things gone soft as cork, furred with weed. They passed between them into the nave, and the water fell away as though a line had been drawn on the floor, pooling at their ankles and going no further, and Wendell understood that they had crossed into a place where the rules were different. The air inside was dry and cold and smelled of candle smoke, though no candle had burned here in years.

Moonlight fell through the broken roof in a single column, and at the bottom of that column, on the altar, sat the keystone.

It was smaller than she'd imagined — a wedge of pale stone no bigger than a loaf of bread, carved all over with script that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it. It hummed. She felt the hum in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the scar-tissue of the old burn on her palm, and every growing thing in her body leaned toward it the way seedlings lean toward a window.

"There," Isandro breathed. "Quickly, before—"

Before. The word had barely finished forming when the moonlight changed.

It thickened. That was the only word for it — the column of silver light congealed, gathered itself, and stood up. What stood in it was shaped like a woman, or like the idea of a woman, tall and robed in drifting grey, with a face that was smooth as an egg and utterly featureless except where eyes should have been: two deep wells that reflected, impossibly, a starry sky.

Wendell's whole body screamed at her to run. She made herself stand still, made herself think of turnips, of weather, of the price of wool in the autumn market, and the wraith drifted closer, closer, its well-eyes fixed on her with a hunger she could feel like a hand pressed flat against her chest, searching for a door.

Then it stopped. Tilted its head. And Wendell felt it find the door after all — felt it slip past her barricade of turnips and wool-prices and touch, gently, almost tenderly, the thing she had buried deepest.

Her mother's face bloomed in the air between them.

Not a memory of her own making — those had gone soft and gilded with age, worn smooth like river stones. This was sharp. This was *true*. Her mother standing in the guild garden at night, torchlight on her face, her hands — those long clever hands Wendell knew better than her own — pressed flat against the garden wall, and her expression was not laughter but terror, and she was saying something to someone out of frame, saying it urgently, saying—

"*—the heir, it has to be the heir, she's only a child, there has to be another way—*"

"No," Wendell said aloud, and the word tore out of her before she could stop it, and the moment she spoke, the wraith *moved*.

The chapel vanished.

She was in the garden — the real garden, the guild garden, thirteen years ago, rendered in detail so complete she could smell the night-stock and hear the moths against the lantern glass. Rain coming. A storm coming, something worse than rain. Her parents stood at the garden's heart beside a circle of standing stones she had never seen in her life, stones that had no business being in her grandmother's vegetable beds, and they were singing — no, not singing, *speaking* words in a language that made the air vibrate, and around them the world was tearing like wet paper, and beyond the tear was a darkness that breathed.

And her mother turned, in the vision, and looked directly at her — at Wendell, standing thirteen years away and helpless — and her face was the worst thing Wendell had ever seen, because it was full of love and full of apology and full of goodbye, and her mouth formed words that the storm swallowed:

"*Forgive us. Remember the garden. Remember—*"

"Wendell!" Isandro's voice, very far away. "Let it go! Don't fight it, don't feed it — *let go!*"

But she couldn't let go. It was her mother. It was her father's hand on her mother's shoulder, his laugh-lines, the scar on his thumb she'd forgotten she remembered. Thirteen years of wondering, thirteen years of the shape of an absence, and here it all was, whole and burning, and the wraith was drinking it in, drinking *her*, and she could feel memories peeling loose — her sixth birthday, the smell of her father's coat, the sound of her own name said in her mother's voice —

A hand closed over hers. Isandro. His grip was iron and his skin was cool as riverstone, and he was speaking words in that vibrating language, the covenant-language, and the world shuddered.

"*No,*" he said to the wraith, in a voice that filled the chapel. "*This one is not yours. The heir is not yours. Take from me instead.*"

And he opened himself to it.

Wendell felt it happen — felt the wraith turn from her like a current reversing, felt it pour into Isandro, and heard him make a sound he tried very hard to swallow, a small hurt noise like a man setting a broken bone. Grey light flickered under his skin. Something left him. She would never know what; he would never say. But when the wraith withdrew, sated and slow, sinking back into its column of moonlight, Isandro stood swaying in the shallows with his eyes shut and his jaw clenched, and when he opened his eyes again they were wet, and he looked at her with an expression she couldn't read at all.

"The stone," he said hoarsely. "Take the stone. Now. While it sleeps."

Her legs barely held her. She waded to the altar on legs made of cold water and grief, and lifted the keystone, and the humming stopped — or rather, the humming *changed*, settled, became something like recognition. Like a key finding its lock. Warmth spread up her arms from the stone, and the moving script stilled, and for one heartbeat she felt it speak to her, not in words but in certainty: *you are the one. you are the one who was promised.*

Then the chapel groaned.

The water at their ankles began to rise — fast, fast, the dry floor flooding in seconds, and overhead the broken roof was closing, stones grinding together like teeth, and the column of moonlight flared white.

"It knows," Isandro gasped. "The theft woke it — run —"

They ran. Through the rising water, up the nave as the doors ahead began to swing shut, weed-furred oak grinding inward, and Wendell threw herself sideways through the narrowing gap with the keystone clutched to her chest, and Isandro came through behind her half a heartbeat later, and then they were outside, under the open sky, stumbling up the stepping-stone road while behind them the drowned chapel sealed itself like a fist, the water churning, the bell tower sinking with a groan that Wendell felt in her back teeth.

They did not stop until the willow curtain fell closed behind them and solid ground rose to meet them, and then Wendell collapsed onto the bank, soaked and shaking, the keystone heavy in her lap, and laughed — a wild, cracked sound she didn't recognize as her own voice.

"We have it," she said. "We actually have it."

Isandro sat down beside her, wringing river from his sleeve, and did not answer right away. When he did, his voice was quiet. "At a cost. It took something from me, Wendell. I don't yet know what. I'll tell you when I do." He paused. "And it gave you something too. I saw its face when it showed you them."

Wendell looked down at the keystone. Its warmth bled through her wet clothes into her skin, steady as a heartbeat.

"I saw my parents," she said. "Thirteen years ago. The night they disappeared. They were in the garden — there were stones in the garden, Isandro, standing stones, I've walked past that ground my whole life and never seen them — and my mother was trying to stop something, and she called me *the heir*." Her throat closed. She forced the words through it. "She said *forgive us*. She said *remember the garden*."

The fragment of the vision still burned in her mind, sharper than any true memory she owned: her mother's mouth shaping swallowed words, her father's hand, the tear in the world. And underneath the grief, underneath the shaking, something else had taken root — something with teeth. Hunger. Thirteen years of questions, and now one answer, and it was not enough. It would never be enough. She wanted the rest of that night the way the drowning want air.

"What happened to them?" she asked. "Isandro. What happened to my parents?"

He was silent a long moment. The river moved. Somewhere far off, the drowned chapel's bell — sunk, sealed, silent — rang anyway, one low note across the water.

"They paid the covenant's price," he said finally, and would say nothing more.
