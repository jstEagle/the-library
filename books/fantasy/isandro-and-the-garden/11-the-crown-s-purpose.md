## 11. The Crown's Purpose

The town was called Harrowmere, and it had learned to be quiet.

Isandro felt the silence before he saw the place — a hush that pressed against his skin like a held breath, the particular stillness of people who had trained themselves not to look at one another. He walked its single street at dusk with Wendell a half-step behind him, her hood drawn low, and he marked the way doors closed as they passed. Not slammed. Closed. Gently, carefully, the way one closes a door on a sickroom.

"They're afraid," Wendell murmured.

"Yes." He kept his voice equally soft. "But not of us. Not exactly."

He had been in the human realm for weeks now, long enough to feel the wrongness of it in his bones — the flatness where magic should have pooled, the way sound fell dead in certain hollows, the way living things grew slightly crooked here, reaching always toward something they could not name. Thirteen years of the covenant broken, and the land itself had begun to lean like a starving plant toward a window that no longer existed. He understood the hunger. He shared it. Every hour in this magicless valley-land was an hour spent breathing thin air.

What he had not understood, until Harrowmere, was what the humans were doing about it.

* * *

They found the notice nailed to the well-post, printed in the crown's black ink on good paper — good paper, Isandro noted, which meant money behind it, which meant conviction.

*BY ROYAL DECREE OF HER MAJESTY THE QUEEN: All persons possessing the unsanctioned gift are hereby commanded to present themselves for registration and care. The crown offers protection, provision, and healing to those afflicted by the wasting plague of uncontrolled magic. Shelter will be given. The afflicted shall want for nothing.*

"Care," Wendell read aloud, and her voice had gone strange. "Protection. They make it sound like—" She stopped.

"Like mercy," Isandro said quietly.

"Like a hospital."

"And perhaps to some who surrender themselves, it is." He touched the paper's edge. The ink was fresh; this decree was recent, newer than the hunting proclamations they'd fled. The crown had changed its language. That frightened him more than the hunters had. "A lie told gently is still a lie, Wendell. But lies this gentle are told by people who need to believe them."

She looked up at him, and in the last grey light her eyes were the green of new leaves, and he thought — not for the first time, and with a guilt that sat in him like a swallowed stone — how much she did not yet know. How much he had not told her.

"We should find lodging," she said. "There's an inn at the far end."

"There is," he agreed. "And we won't be staying there."

* * *

Because Isandro had heard it. Faintly, beneath the town's careful hush — a hum. Low, rhythmic, wrong. Not the singing of a keystone, nothing so clean. This was a grinding, a churning, a sound like roots being torn from soil in slow motion, over and over, coming from somewhere beneath or beyond the eastern edge of the town.

Wendell couldn't hear it. Her gift was still half-dormant, muffled by the magicless land, and she slept that night — truly slept, exhausted from the road — in the hayloft of a farmstead whose owners took coin without questions. Isandro sat awake beside her and listened to the machine.

He had heard such a sound once before, long ago, in the archives of his own realm, in the memory-glass records of the first covenant's making. The old guardians had spoken of severance-magic: the art of cutting, of amputation, of taking a living bond and making it a wound. It was forbidden knowledge even among his people. No guardian had studied it in nine hundred years.

Someone in the human realm had remembered it. Or reinvented it. And someone was feeding it.

Near midnight, he could bear it no longer. He left Wendell sleeping — she stirred when he rose, murmured something, settled again — and went out through the loft's high door into the dark.

* * *

The machine was housed in what had been a granary, before. Isandro knew granaries; he had built their counterparts in his own realm, wove-barns where the harvest-spirits were thanked. This building still bore the shape of its old purpose, but its windows were now shuttered with iron, and crown soldiers stood at its doors in pairs, and around its foundation someone had laid a ring of salt and ash and cut stone that made Isandro's teeth ache to look at — a containment circle, crude but effective, designed to hold power in rather than keep it out.

He circled the building twice, keeping to the shadows of the fence-line, and found what he was looking for at the rear: a ventilation grate, rusted, its bolts half-eaten by thirteen years of neglect. Beyond it, the humming was loud enough to feel in his chest, and threaded through it — faint, flickering, desperate — he heard voices.

Not voices speaking. Voices *burning*. The sound of minds being used as fuel.

He stood in the dark with his hand pressed flat against the cold grate, and he listened, because listening was the least he could do, and because a guardian's first duty was witness.

Through the grate he could see only a slice of the interior: a vast floor, machinery of iron and glass and cut stone arranged in concentric rings around a central column, and along those rings — cages. Small ones. Dozens of them. And in the cages, people.

They sat very still, most of them. Some rocked. One woman near the outermost ring held her hands pressed to the bars of her cage, palms outward, and from her palms came a soft golden light that ran down copper conduits into the machine's heart, steady as water from a spring. As Isandro watched, the light guttered, thinned, went out. The woman slumped. A soldier turned a valve somewhere, and after a moment the light kindled again — weaker. Dimmer than before.

They were draining them. Slowly, methodically, the way one milks a goat, or bleeds a fever patient. And when the light failed entirely—

Isandro had been a guardian for two hundred years. He had watched realms be born. He knew what it looked like when a gifted soul was emptied. The body lived on. Sometimes for years. But the person — the laughing, grieving, remembering person — that went out like the woman's light, and did not return.

He crouched in the dark behind the granary and, for the first time since crossing into the human realm, he was afraid in a way that had nothing to do with his own weakening.

* * *

He learned more before dawn, because fear is an excellent teacher of patience.

A carriage came at the fourth hour, lacquered black, bearing the queen's personal seal. From it descended a man in grey robes — not a soldier, something worse, a scholar — carrying a ledger, and he was met at the granary door by an officer whom Isandro recognized from wanted-notices: Captain Rell's superior, a hard-faced woman named Vess.

The scholar spoke quietly, but the night carried voices, and Isandro's ears were not human ears.

"—the Queen requires assurance," the scholar was saying. "The trial must proceed on schedule. She has staked everything on it. If the severance fails—"

"It will not fail," Vess said. "We have forty-one registered. Output holds at capacity. The third ring came online last week."

"The third ring." The scholar flipped a page. "Her Majesty will want to know the attrition rate."

Vess paused. "Twelve percent per month."

"Acceptable," the scholar said, and wrote it down, and Isandro pressed his knuckles against his mouth to keep silent, because twelve percent per month meant that of the forty-one souls in that building, five would be empty shells by the next moon, and the scholar had called it *acceptable*, and the terrible thing — the thing Isandro would carry for the rest of his long life — was that the scholar had not said it with cruelty. He had said it with the weary compassion of a physician noting a dosage. He believed he was saving more than he was spending.

"Does she ever come?" Vess asked, and there was something almost human in her voice, something tired. "The Queen. Does she ever see them?"

"She sees the reports." The scholar closed his ledger. "Captain Vess — you lost family to the blight-years, I'm told. Your brother."

"My sister," Vess said. "The magic took her mind before the coughing took the rest. She burned a village green trying to light a birthday candle. Eleven people dead, and her screaming the whole time, begging it to stop." She looked at the granary. "So don't ask me if I see them as fuel, sir. I see them as what my sister would have become, if someone had caught her in time. The Queen is going to cut this sickness out of the world. I'd have given anything for someone to do that twelve years ago."

Isandro closed his eyes.

*That* was the truth of it, then. Not cruelty. Not conquest. Fear — old, justified, worn smooth as riverstone by grief. The queen believed magic was a plague because in every way she could measure, it was. The broken covenant had poisoned the land, warped the gift, turned blessing into blight. Untrained gifted children burned villages. Crops failed. Rivers ran backward. And the crown, seeing the sickness everywhere and knowing nothing of the wound beneath it, had concluded that the only cure was amputation.

They were not butchers. They were surgeons operating on a patient they had misdiagnosed — and the surgery would kill the patient, because the sickness was not in the limb. It was in the severed bond itself. Cut the realms apart forever, drain every gifted soul dry, and the blight would not end. It would simply become permanent. The valley would die slowly instead of quickly, and both realms with it, and the queen would die believing she had saved her kingdom.

The enemy is not evil, Isandro thought, walking back through the grey pre-dawn. The enemy is afraid. And fear with an army behind it is the most dangerous thing either realm has ever produced.

* * *

Wendell was awake when he returned, sitting up in the hay, wrapped in her cloak, and the look on her face told him she had heard him come in and heard something else besides — something in the quality of his silence.

"You found something," she said.

He climbed up into the loft and sat across from her, and for a moment he considered lying. It would have been easy. She trusted him — trustingly, completely, with the whole unguarded openness of someone who had never been betrayed by a guardian before. He could tell her the granary held grain stores for the army, or a garrison, and they would slip away north and she would never know what the crown's mercy looked like from inside a cage.

But he had listened to those burning voices all night. He owed them more than a comfortable lie.

"There is a building east of town," he said. "Formerly a granary. Now it houses a machine."

"What kind of machine?"

"One that runs on the gifted." He watched her face as the words landed. "Forty-one registered persons, caged in rings around a central engine. Their magic is drawn out through copper and glass and fed into the mechanism. When their strength fails, they are... maintained. Kept alive. Kept producing." He made himself say it plainly, without softening, because she deserved plainness. "The scholars call the attrition acceptable."

Wendell had gone very pale. "Producing what?"

"That is the part that matters." He leaned forward. "The machine is a severing-engine, Wendell. It is being built to do permanently what the covenant's breaking did accidentally — to cut the human realm free of mine, forever, and to burn out every trace of the gift in doing so. The keystones we seek, the bridge, the covenant itself — all of it will be severed at the root. There will be nothing left to restore. Nothing left to inherit."

"But why?" Her voice cracked on it. "Why would anyone—"

"Because your queen believes magic is killing her kingdom." He held her gaze. "And she is not wrong, Wendell. That is the horror of it. Magic *is* killing her kingdom — because the covenant broke, because the gift now runs wild and poisoned, because children like the ones in that granary burn and scream and die of things that should never have touched them. She has watched the blight take her crops and her people for thirteen years. She has concluded that the disease is magic itself. So she is harvesting the infected to power a cure."

"A cure that kills the patient."

"A cure that kills *both* patients." He reached out and took her hands — her gardener's hands, the faint shimmer of her gift barely visible even now, and he thought of the woman in the outermost ring with her palms against the bars. "Your realm and mine are one body, Wendell. They have been since before either realm had names. Sever them and you do not heal the wound. You make it eternal."

For a long moment she said nothing. The barn breathed around them — the horses shifting below, wind in the thatch, the distant, ceaseless hum from the east that she could not hear and he could not stop hearing.

"The woman," she said finally. "The one whose light went out. You saw her."

"I saw many of them."

"No — I mean..." She pulled her hands from his and pressed them over her own face, and when she spoke again her voice was muffled and shaking. "I keep thinking about my mother. Thirteen years ago. The night everything broke. Everyone says she and my father abandoned us, betrayed the guild, ran. But what if—" Her hands lowered slowly. "What if they didn't run? What if they were *taken*? What if they're in a place like that granary, right now, in a cage, in a ring, giving their light to—"

"Wendell."

"If she's alive," she whispered. "If my mother is alive in one of those cages, and I've spent thirteen years thinking she chose to leave me—"

He moved then, without deciding to, and gathered her against him, and she let him, her forehead hard against his shoulder, her breath ragged. Above them the rafters creaked. Below, the horses stamped. And far to the east, beneath the earth and the salt and the iron, the machine ground on, patient as winter, fed by forty-one small lights that the crown called acceptable losses.

He held her and said nothing, because there was nothing true he could say — not yet, not all of it. The full cost of the covenant still sat unspoken between them, a debt he had known about since the garden and had not yet found the courage to name. Every day he waited made the eventual telling a betrayal. Every day he told her less than everything, he became a little more like the scholar with his ledger, weighing what she could bear against what she deserved.

*We leave at first light,* he thought. *North, to the drowned chapel, to the first keystone, before the hunt finds us, before the machine's shadow reaches further.* And after that — after that he would tell her. He had to. There were only so many nights left before the moon's thirteenth turn, and he would not let her stand at the bridge believing the price was anything less than it was.

But that was tomorrow's cowardice. Tonight he only held the heir of the covenant while she grieved for a mother who might be caged in the dark, lit by her own stolen light, and he understood at last the shape of the war they were truly fighting.

It was not guardians against crowns. It was not magic against iron.

It was fear against memory — and memory, alone, unarmed, was all they had.

In the east, past the shuttered granary and its forty-one dimming lights, the sky was beginning to pale. Somewhere north, in a drowned chapel beneath black water, the first keystone waited — and whatever guarded it had already begun, Isandro sensed, to dream of Wendell's parents' faces.
