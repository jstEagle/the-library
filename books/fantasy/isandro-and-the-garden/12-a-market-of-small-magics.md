## 12. A Market of Small Magics

The town announced itself before they saw it — a smell on the wind that made Wendell's eyes sting and her mouth water at once: woodsmoke and frying onions, tallow and wet wool, and beneath it all something sweeter, greener, like cut stems in a florist's shop. After nine days on the Road of Ash, where every village had watched them pass with shutters closing and every well had tasted of iron, the smell was almost unbearable. It smelled like people. It smelled like living.

"Emberfall," Isandro said, pausing on the ridge with his hand shading his eyes. The bark-scarring along his jaw caught the late light, silver-grey against skin that had regained some color since they'd crossed into lands where the covenant's poison thinned. "Or what's left of it. It was a market town once, before the breaking. Now it's—" He hesitated, searching for the word.

"A refuge," Wendell said.

"A hiding place," he corrected gently, "that pretends to be a market."

She understood what he meant when they came down off the ridge and through the palisade gate — an old gate, sagging on its leather hinges, guarded by a boy of perhaps twelve who looked them over with the flat professional gaze of someone twice his age and waved them through without asking their names. Asking names was dangerous here. Everyone understood that.

The town was built like a secret. Houses leaned together over narrow lanes as if whispering; laundry lines crossed overhead strung not just with shirts and sheets but with bundles of drying herbs, strings of onions, cages of pigeons — anything that could justify a covered walkway between one door and the next. And everywhere, woven into the ordinary commerce of a market town, were things Wendell had spent her whole life hiding.

A woman at a corner stall sold bread, and while she handed a loaf across to a customer her free hand moved absently above a tray of seedlings, and the seedlings *leaned* toward her fingers like cats seeking a scratch. A man repaired pots in an open-fronted workshop, and when a crack ran too deep for glue he hummed three low notes and the clay remembered itself, knitting closed with a sound like a sigh. Two children chased each other through the square, and where they ran, the puddles from the morning rain rose up in small bright shapes — a fish, a bird, a running horse — that held for a heartbeat and collapsed, laughing, back into water.

Wendell stopped in the middle of the lane and stared, and no one stared back, because there was nothing here to stare at. That was the miracle of it. A child lifted water into the shape of a horse and the woman selling bread didn't even glance up.

"Close your mouth," Isandro murmured, but he was smiling. "You'll catch flies."

"I've never—" She couldn't finish it. She'd never seen the gift used in the open. Not once, not in twenty-three years of hiding a shimmer under her gloves and keeping her hands in her pockets and flinching whenever a plant did something too beautiful too fast near her fingers. Her whole life had been a held breath. This town exhaled.

"It's called Emberfall because of the fire," Isandro said quietly, steering her toward a lane where stalls crowded shoulder to shoulder under awnings of dyed canvas. "Thirty years ago the crown burned a gifted village two valleys east. The survivors came here. The lord who held this land then was old and half-blind and owed a debt to one of them, so he looked the other way. The new lord looks the other way too, for a price. Half the coin in this market goes into his purse, and the other half goes into bribes deeper down. It holds. For now."

"For now," Wendell repeated. The words sat cold in her stomach. She thought of the proclamation nailed to the guild gate, of the herald's painted face, of the word *surrendered*. A town like this wasn't safe. It was just a larger version of her grandmother's walled garden — a place where the hiding was communal, and therefore more fragile, and therefore more precious.

They walked the market because Isandro said they needed food and because neither of them said aloud what both of them knew: they needed also to be among people, briefly, before the road took them back into ash and silence. Wendell bought apples from a grower whose orchard, she realized after the second bite, had been coaxed into bearing out of season — the fruit was perfect, unnaturally sweet, and the grower watched her eat it with quiet pride rather than fear. She bought a twist of salt and a heel of hard cheese. She let herself haggle over a wool scarf, badly, and paid too much, and found she was grinning about it.

It was the smallness that undid her. Not great workings, not wonders — a loaf that stayed warm hours past baking because the baker's daughter liked it that way. Boots that shed mud because the cobbler had asked the leather politely. A gutter candle that burned steadier than any flame had a right to, because the chandler's dead wife had always hated the wind. Small magics, worn smooth by daily use, threaded through a life the way thread is threaded through cloth until you can't find the seam anymore.

*This is what it could have been,* she thought, standing in the golden light of late afternoon with an apple in her hand and juice on her chin. *This is what the valley could have been, if the covenant hadn't broken. If I hadn't been raised to flinch.*

The thought turned, as her thoughts had learned to do lately, and showed its underside: *this is what the crown wants to burn.* Somewhere behind them, Captain Rell was following the road with hunters at his back, and every person in this square — the leaning seedlings, the self-mending clay, the children and their water-horses — was a name on a list that hadn't been written yet.

She bought the scarf mostly to give the seller's nervous hands something to do.

* * *

They found lodging above a tavern called the Drowned Bell, a low-beamed room with one window and a bed wide enough for two strangers to share without touching, which was how they'd slept since the garden burned. Isandro went down to listen — he was good at listening, had a way of sitting in a corner with a cup he never finished and becoming part of the furniture — and Wendell said she would wash and mend, and instead stood at the window watching the market empty as dusk came down.

That was when she saw the lanterns.

They came out one by one as the light failed, not the plain oil-lanterns of the town but small glass things hung in doorways and windows, each holding a flame of a different color. Green in the herbalist's window. Blue over the cooper's door. A soft gold in the upper room of the house opposite, where an old woman sat silhouetted, lighting hers with a touch rather than a taper. Down in the square, the children were back, going door to door with a little cart, and at each doorway someone gave them a candle-end or a coin, and the children sang something too faint for Wendell to catch.

"What is it?" she asked when Isandro came up. She hadn't heard the stairs.

He stood beside her at the window. For a long moment he didn't answer, and the bark along his cheekbone caught the colored light of the lanterns below, green and blue and gold moving over his face like water.

"The Vigil," he said finally. "They do it every night. One flame for each thing they're afraid will be taken." He pointed. "Green for growing things. Blue for water and weather. Gold for memory — that's the one they fear losing most, now. There are stories coming out of the capital." His voice went flat. "Stories about what happens to the gifted the crown takes alive."

Wendell's hands tightened on the windowsill. She thought of the machine Isandro had described in fragments, in the careful way he described things he didn't want her to look at directly. *What the crown wants them for.* She still didn't know. He still hadn't told her everything, and she had begun to keep a private tally of the things he withheld, the way her grandmother had kept a tally of years.

Below, the old woman across the lane finished lighting her gold lantern and set it in the window, and then, as if feeling Wendell's gaze, looked up.

Their eyes met across the narrow street. The old woman didn't smile or nod. She simply looked at Wendell for a long, unhurried moment — at her face, and then lower, at her hands, bare now because Wendell had long ago stopped wearing gloves where no one knew her — and then she raised one finger, slowly, and beckoned.

"Wendell," Isandro said, and she realized she was already turning from the window.

"She wants to talk to me," Wendell said. Her heart was doing something strange, quick and heavy at once. "Isandro. She looked at my *hands*."

"That could mean many things."

"No," Wendell said. "It meant one thing. You saw it too."

He had. She could tell he had, and that he didn't like it. But he only said, "Then we go together," and reached for his coat.

* * *

The house opposite was older than its neighbors, timber black with age, and it smelled inside of dried lavender and lamp oil and something else underneath — a smell that hit Wendell like a fist the moment the old woman opened the door. Loam. Cut grass. The particular green scent of a walled garden in high summer.

Her grandmother's garden. The guild garden. The smell of the first place she had ever felt safe.

"You'd better come in," the old woman said. Her voice was dry as seed husks. "Before the whole street smells it on you too."

Inside, the single room was crowded the way a nest is crowded — baskets of herbs hanging from every beam, jars in ranks on shelves, a loom pushed against one wall with work abandoned on it, and in the middle of it all a table scrubbed pale with use, on which sat a gold lantern and nothing else. The old woman — she moved slowly, one hand pressed to her hip as if it pained her, though Wendell noticed the hand was also never far from a long knitting needle stuck through her bun like a dagger — poured three cups of something hot and bitter without asking whether they wanted it.

"My name is Maren Cole," she said. "I was hedge-witch to the Thornbury guild before you were born. Before the breaking." She sat down across the table and looked at Wendell with eyes gone pale as river-washed glass. "I knew your grandmother. I knew your mother."

Wendell's cup stopped halfway to her lips. Beside her, Isandro had gone very still, in the particular way he went still when something was happening that he had not planned.

"You knew my mother?"

"I knew her hands," Maren said. "A gardener knows another gardener's hands. Yours are the same. The same long fingers. The same way you hold them when you think no one's looking — curled slightly in, like you're afraid of touching something by accident." She took a slow sip. "You've been doing it all evening. In the market. Every time you passed a stall with living stock, your hands curled in. I watched you for an hour, girl. You hide it better than most, but hiding leaves marks, same as scars do."

"Why are you telling us this?" Isandro asked. His voice was courteous and cold. Wendell had learned to hear the warning under it.

"Because I spent an hour deciding whether to," Maren said placidly, "and I decided that a woman who taught herself to curl her hands away from growing things deserves better than to walk into what's coming blind." She set down her cup and leaned forward, and the lamplight moved in her pale eyes. "Show me your sigil."

"I don't—"

"Your left wrist. Under the sleeve. The guild marked its own, the way beekeepers mark their hives. Show me."

Wendell's heart was pounding. Slowly, feeling as though she were undressing in front of a stranger, she pushed back her left sleeve. There, faint as a watermark, was the mark she had borne all her life without knowing what it was — a circle of interlocking leaves, so old and so pale she had assumed it was a birthmark. Her grandmother had told her it was nothing. A stain. A quirk of her skin.

Maren looked at it and closed her eyes, and for a moment her face was so full of grief that Wendell forgot to breathe.

"There it is," the old woman whispered. "Thornbury. Last hive left standing, and I'd have sworn the swarm was dead." She opened her eyes. "Do you know what you are, girl? Do you know what that mark means?"

"It means I'm hunted," Wendell said. "The herald said—"

"The herald doesn't know what it means. The queen might. Her grandmother did." Maren's voice dropped, and the room seemed to lean in around it. "The Thornbury guild weren't gardeners, child. Not the way the valley remembers them. They were *keepers*. The covenant between your realm and the other one — the one this gentleman comes from, don't look surprised, I can smell the far side of the bridge on him like smoke on a coat — that covenant didn't hold itself. It was tended. Pruned. Bound and bound again, season after season, generation after generation. Your guild were the hands that did the tending. And the mark on your wrist isn't a birthmark. It's a graft. The covenant lives in the bloodline, and the bloodline lives in you."

The words landed in Wendell one after another, each one heavier than the last. *The covenant lives in the bloodline.* She thought of her grandmother's silence. Thirteen years of it. The night her parents vanished. The seed-packet pressed into her hand while the garden burned.

"I don't understand what you want me to do," she said, which was not the same as saying she didn't understand.

"I want you to leave," Maren said flatly. "Tonight, if you can manage it. Tomorrow at the latest. And I want you to stop walking toward whatever it is you're walking toward, because I can see it in both your faces — you're going somewhere, and the somewhere is bad, and you think it's worth it." She held up a hand before either of them could speak. "I'm not done. Because there's one more thing, and you'll forgive an old woman for making you sit through it, because nobody sat me down and made *me* listen, thirty years ago, and I've regretted it every day since."

She drew a breath. The gold lantern between them flickered, though there was no draft.

"Your guild knew the price," Maren said. "Whatever it is you're going to try to fix — the bridge, the covenant, the broken thing between the realms — the Thornbury guild knew, better than anyone alive, that mending it has a cost. Not a coin-cost. Not a labor-cost. A *price*, the kind that's paid in the only currency that can't be earned back." Her pale eyes fixed on Wendell's, and her voice, dry as it was, trembled on the last words. "I watched them pay it once. I watched what it took, and what it left behind. Your mother stood in my kitchen the week before she vanished, girl, and she knew — she *knew* what was coming, and she asked me to remember one thing, and I have remembered it for thirteen years waiting for someone to come ask me, and no one ever did, until tonight, when you walked into my market with your hands curled in and your mother's face and your guild sigil showing at your wrist like a wound."

The room was absolutely silent. Somewhere outside, faintly, the children were singing the vigil song, going door to door with their little cart, gathering candle-ends against the dark.

"What did she ask you to remember?" Wendell whispered.

Maren Cole looked at her for a long moment, and Wendell saw the old woman weigh something — the safety of silence against the debt of a promise thirteen years kept — and watched the promise win.

"She asked me to tell whoever came wearing the sigil: *the covenant does not take what is offered freely. It takes what is hidden. Whatever they tell you the price is, it is a lie, and the truth is worse, and the truth is also the only way through.*" Maren's voice cracked on the final words, and she pressed her lips together until it passed. "Those were her words. Near enough. I was younger then, and frightened, and I may have lost a piece of it. But that much I'm sure of. *It takes what is hidden.*"

Wendell became aware that Isandro had stopped breathing. She didn't look at him. She couldn't. Something was happening in her chest, vast and slow, like ice shifting on a lake in spring — because she had seen the way he went still at certain words, had catalogued the things he didn't say, had felt the shape of the secret between them like a stone under a blanket she'd agreed not to lift. *It takes what is hidden.*

"Thank you," she said to Maren, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her.

"Don't thank me." The old woman was already rising, stiff-handed, moving to a shelf of jars with the briskness of someone who could not bear to stay still inside her own grief. "Thank me by leaving before dawn and by staying alive. Take this." She pressed a small jar into Wendell's hands — a salve, green-gold, smelling overwhelmingly of the guild garden. "For his scarring. It won't heal it, nothing heals it this side of the bridge, but it will ease the pull when the weather turns. And take this." A second jar, smaller, unlabeled. "For you. You'll know when you need it. Gardeners always know."

At the door, Maren caught Wendell's arm — her grip stronger than her frame promised — and pulled her close, and spoke into her ear alone, so low that Wendell knew the words were not meant for any other ears in the world.

"One more thing, and then I'll let you go to your doom like all the rest of them. Your mother's message — the one I just gave you — she made me swear to give it whole or not at all, and I gave it whole. But there was a second half to that night, girl. A question she asked me, at the door, with her shawl already on." The old woman's breath was warm and shaking. "She asked me: *if the price must fall on someone, and you could choose who — would you choose yourself, or the one you love?* And I never answered her. I was a coward, and I never answered, and she smiled at me like she already knew, and she walked out into the dark, and I have been answering her every night since, at that window, with that lantern." Maren pulled back and looked at Wendell with her river-glass eyes. "So I'm asking you now, so that somebody answers her at last: when the time comes — and it is coming, I can smell it on you both like weather — who will you choose?"

Behind her, in the lamplight of the little room, Isandro had turned toward the door, and his face, when Wendell finally made herself look at it, was the face of a man listening to his own sentence being read.

Wendell opened her mouth to answer — and found she could not, because the true answer was already in her chest, and it was not the one she wanted it to be, and saying it aloud would change everything.

Outside, the vigil lanterns burned green and blue and gold, and the moon, one turn closer to its thirteenth, rose thin and white over the rooftops of Emberfall.
