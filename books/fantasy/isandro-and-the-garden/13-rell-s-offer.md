## 13. Rell's Offer

The free town of Hollowmere kept its secrets the way an old woman keeps her teeth: carefully, and only when it suited her. Its market stalls folded away at dusk into walls that had been built to fold, its chimneys burned smokeless wood, and its lanterns were hooded so that from the crown road above — if any hunter bothered to look down — the town would seem no more than a scatter of sleeping farms.

Wendell had learned its rhythms in three days. She learned that the baker with the burn-scarred hands was deaf to anything said below a certain pitch, which made him the safest man in town to talk to about magic. She learned that the well water tasted faintly of iron and something older, something green, and that when she drew it up her gift stirred in her palms like a cat waking. She learned that Isandro slept badly, propped against whatever wall was nearest, his bark-scarred arm cradled like a broken wing, and that he always woke before she did but pretended otherwise so she would not feel watched.

And she learned, on the fourth night, that someone else had learned all of these things too.

She felt him before she saw him — not with her gift, for Rell was as magicless as she had once believed herself to be, but with the older sense, the one that lived in the spine. The sense of being known. She was coming back from the well with two full pails when the alley between the cooper's shop and the tannery seemed to grow a shadow that had not been there before, and the shadow said her name.

"Wendy."

No one had called her that since she was nine years old. No one alive had earned the right to it except one person, and that person was supposed to be three days' ride behind them, hunting them.

The pails went heavy in her hands. Water slopped over her knuckles, cold enough to hurt, and she was grateful for it because it gave her somewhere to put the fear. She set the pails down slowly, the way her grandmother had taught her to set down anything that might be a weapon or might be a wound, and turned.

Captain Rell stood at the mouth of the alley in plain clothes — no crown livery, no hunt-badge, just a brown coat and a sword worn low the way farmers wore them, which fooled no one who knew how he moved. He had filled out since she'd last seen him properly, across the guild wall, half a lifetime ago; his jaw was harder, his eyes were older, and there was a new scar through one eyebrow that she wanted desperately to ask about and knew she never could. But his stance was the same. Weight on the back foot. Hands loose. The stance of a boy who had spent his childhood losing fistfights to her behind the tannery in their own valley and had never once held it against her.

"Rell," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"You look well," he said, "for a dead woman."

"I'm not dead."

"No," he agreed. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

They ended up, absurdly, in the loft above the cooper's shop, because Rell insisted on somewhere with two exits and Wendell refused to go anywhere with fewer than two exits herself, and the loft had a hay-chute and a stair and both were visible from where they sat on opposite sides of a barrel of staves. Between them: a candle stub, unlit. Neither wanted light on this.

He had come alone. She believed that much — she had checked the street twice from the loft's small window, and Rell had let her check, standing patiently by the barrel with his hands folded, a man making himself easy to count. That patience frightened her more than an ambush would have. Ambushes were simple. Patience meant choices had already been made.

"Three days," she said. "You've been three days behind us since the ash road. You could have taken us at the ford. You could have taken us asleep outside Marrow's Cross. Why didn't you?"

"Because I'm not taking you anywhere." He turned his hands over, studied the palms as though the answer might be written there. "I'm here to offer you a way out. The only one left."

"The crown doesn't offer ways out."

"The crown offers gallows and worse, yes." His jaw worked. "But Captain Rell writes the reports out of this district, and Captain Rell has recently discovered that reports can be... incomplete. There was a fire on the ash road. A collapse in the flooded chapel country. Bodies were recovered — or enough of bodies, given what the wraiths leave. Identification was difficult. Grief-stricken survivors confirmed the remains were those of the fugitive gardener and her unknown accomplice." He looked up. "It's a good story. I've told better ones with less true in them."

Wendell sat very still. Somewhere below them the cooper's dog shifted in its sleep, chains clinking, and the sound seemed very loud.

"You want me to die on paper," she said.

"I want you to *stay* dead on paper. There's a difference, and the difference is the rest of your life." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment he was nine years old again too, sitting across from her on the guild wall, explaining with great seriousness why the gardeners' covenant must be about vegetables. "Listen to me, Wendy. Listen, because I'm only going to be able to say this once, and then I have to walk out of this town and become a liar for the rest of my career. The hunt isn't going to stop. It's not even really a hunt anymore — it's a harvest. They're not bringing the gifted to trial. Have you seen what happens to the ones they take?"

"I've seen the wagons," she said quietly. She had. On the ash road, a week ago: iron-barred carts rolling east toward the capital, and faces at the bars, and one woman singing to keep the children calm, a song about a garden, of all things, and Wendell had stood in the ditch with her heart breaking and her gift rising uselessly in her throat like a scream.

"Then you know. And you know what's chasing you isn't six riders anymore. It's forty. It's the queen's own wardens now, not district men — men who don't know you, don't owe you anything, and don't write sloppy reports. I can't hold them off forever. I can't hold them off past the next moon turn, honestly. So." He spread his hands. "Here is the offer. You disappear tonight. North, over the fells, alone. You send word to me through the baker — he owes me, don't ask — one word, *safe*, once a month, nothing more. And in exchange—"

"In exchange," Wendell said, because she had known it was coming since the alley, had felt it coming the way you feel weather in an old scar, "you want Isandro."

Rell flinched. Actually flinched, a small involuntary movement, and she understood that he had hoped she wouldn't say the name, that saying it aloud made the thing real in a way his careful phrasing had tried to avoid.

"He goes to the crown," Rell said. "Alive. Unharmed — my word on it, such as my word is worth these days. And you walk north a free woman, dead to every ledger in the kingdom, and live out your life with your hands in the dirt somewhere the queen's writ runs thin. That's the trade. That's the whole trade. I won't pretend it's kind. I'll only say it's the kindest thing on any table."

"And what do they do with him?" she asked. "At the capital. With the ones they take."

"I don't know."

"You *do* know."

"Wendy—"

"You've seen the machine." She hadn't meant to say it. The word came out of her like a splinter working free — Isandro had used it once, late at night, *the crown is building a machine*, and had gone gray and silent after, and she had carried the word since without knowing its weight. Now she watched it land on Rell, watched his face do something complicated and terrible, and knew. "You've seen it. Or the orders for it. That's why they want the gifted alive. That's why the hunt changed. What does it do, Rell?"

"It doesn't matter what it does." His voice had gone flat, the voice of a man reading from a document he hated. "What matters is that it needs them. And what matters is that the queen believes — truly believes, I've stood in the room while she said it — that magic is killing this kingdom. The blight, the stillbirths, the rivers running backward. She thinks if she cuts it out of the world like a tumor, her people live. She's wrong, probably. I think she's wrong. But she is not a monster, Wendell. She's a mother who lost a child to the blight and has an army of clever men telling her she can make it mean something." He rubbed his face. "Gods. Listen to me. Defending her. Three years ago I'd have spat."

"And now?"

"Now I've read the requisitions." He laughed, short and joyless. "Now I know what a kingdom costs."

* * *

The candle stub sat between them, unlit, and Wendell found herself staring at it as though it were a keystone, as though it might show her something. Outside, Hollowmere went about its hidden evening — a cart wheel, a shutter, the baker's dog again — a whole town practicing invisibility, and she thought: *this is what surrender looks like when it survives. This is what we become if we run.*

"If I give him to you," she said slowly, "you falsify my death. One report. One lie. And you spend the rest of your career one careless clerk away from the gallows, because if anyone ever pulls the district files and cross-checks the chapel recovery against the wagon manifests—" She stopped. "Rell. What happens to you if they discover it?"

He didn't answer immediately. In the dark his face was mostly shadow and the pale line of the scar.

"They hang traitors," he said at last. "Quickly, usually. The wardens are less patient than the old guard was." A pause. "My mother gets a letter. My sister gets my pension, if the paperwork survives me, which it won't. And somewhere a report gets filed under *resolved* and everyone feels the kingdom is a little safer." He shrugged, a boy's shrug, helpless and defiant at once. "I've done the arithmetic, Wendy. I did it before I rode in. I'm not asking you to save me. I'm asking you to let me buy you with the only coin I have left."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing left in this kingdom is."

She got up. She couldn't sit still anymore; her body demanded motion the way it demanded air. She walked the length of the loft, past the hay-chute, past the window with its sliver of hooded lamplight, and her gift rose with her agitation — she felt it in the floorboards, in the old oak staves in the barrel, a faint green attention turning toward her like flowers finding the sun. She clenched her fists until it settled. Even now, even here, she could not afford to glow.

"Why?" she asked the window. "Why any of this? You're sworn to the crown. You were sworn when we met on the ash road, and you still sounded the horn. You hunted us through the poisoned country. You nearly took us at the ford. What changed?"

"Nothing changed." His voice came from behind her, low. "That's what I need you to understand. Nothing has changed since we were children. I have spent eleven years being very good at duty, and every single day of it, some part of me was standing on the guild wall watching you storm off after losing an argument about vegetables, thinking: *she'll come back tomorrow. She always comes back.*" A breath. "Then the hunt started, and the names went up on the proclamation boards, and yours was on them, and I stood in the square in my captain's coat and realized that everything I'd sworn to protect included the right to put a rope around the neck of the only person I ever—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I sounded the horn on the ash road because the horn was the law. I've regretted it every hour since. I let you go at the ford because the law and I are no longer on speaking terms. Do you see? I haven't betrayed my oath, Wendy. My oath betrayed itself. I'm just the poor fool standing where the two halves of it meet."

She turned around. He was still seated, hands hanging between his knees, and he looked — tired. Not the performative weariness of a man negotiating, but the deep structural tiredness of a bridge that has been carrying too much for too long and knows exactly which cable will go first.

"Isandro stays with me," she said.

"Wendy—"

"You asked. Here's my answer. No."

"Think about what you're refusing." He was on his feet now too, and there was desperation in it finally, the patience cracking. "You're refusing safety. You're refusing a life. For *him* — a man you've known a season! A stranger who fell into your garden bleeding! What is he to you?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. She turned it over in the dark of the loft, honest as she had ever been with anyone, including herself. What was Isandro to her? A guardian of a broken covenant. A liar by omission — she had caught the shape of the secret he still carried, the cost he wouldn't name, and it sat between them like a stone neither would move. A man whose skin remembered bark. A man who looked at her sometimes with an expression she had no shelf for yet, an expression that made her feel both found and endangered.

"He's the first person," she said, "who ever looked at my hands and wasn't afraid of them. Including me. Including you, Rell — don't argue, I remember. I was twelve and I made the guild roses bloom out of season and you stepped back like I'd shown you a snake." She smiled, and it hurt. "He's the reason I know what I am. And I am not going to hand the reason I exist to the machine that eats reasons."

"So it's him or your life."

"It's not a bargain at all, is what it is. You're offering to sell me my own heartbeat and calling it mercy." She crossed back toward him, close enough now that she could see the candlelight-less dark of his eyes, and softened her voice because anger would not carry this. "Rell. I love you. I have loved you since before I knew there were other kinds of loving, and I will love you when we're both old and on opposite sides of whatever comes. And I am telling you, as the person who knows you best in all the world: if you take him to the capital, whatever they make you complicit in will hollow you out so thoroughly that the man who came to this loft tonight will be dead long before the gallows. I'm not saving him instead of you. I'm trying to save you both, and you're making it very difficult."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he laughed — a real laugh, brief and cracked and young, the laugh from behind the tannery.

"There she is," he said. "There's the girl who wins arguments about vegetables." He scrubbed a hand through his hair. "You know what the worst part is? I believe you. Eleven years of duty and I stand in a hayloft and I *believe* you, because you've never once lied to me, and I have built my entire life on the assumption that you were the one fixed point that didn't move." He exhaled. "Fine. Fine. The offer stands until moonrise tomorrow. If you change your mind — the baker, one word, *yes*. If you don't, I file the report at dawn either way, because whether you run or not, Captain Rell is going to record that two fugitives died in the chapel country, and then I am going to spend the rest of my life being a liar with excellent handwriting."

"You don't have to do that."

"Yes, I do." He picked up his hat from the barrel — she hadn't even noticed it there — and settled it on his head, and just like that he was a captain again, or the shape of one. "Because if the report says you're dead, the wardens ride home. Forty men stop hunting you. Whatever you and your guardian are doing — and I know it's something, I've seen the keystones hum, I'm not blind — you'll do it with a kingdom's worth of steel looking the other way. That's worth my neck. That's the whole calculation. Don't make it sentimental; I couldn't bear it."

He moved toward the stair, and panic rose in her — sudden, childish, enormous — the panic of watching someone walk out of a story you aren't ready to end.

"Rell."

He stopped. Didn't turn.

"When this is over," she said, "whatever this becomes — the covenant, the machine, all of it — find me. However it ends. Find me, and we'll sit on a wall somewhere and settle eleven years of arguments about vegetables."

His shoulders moved. It might have been a breath; it might have been something else entirely.

"Aye," he said roughly. "If there's a wall left standing."

* * *

She watched him from the loft window as he crossed the hidden street below, unhurried, nodding to the baker's shutters as though greeting an old creditor, and slipped out through the town's folding dark the way he had come. She watched until there was nothing to watch. Then she stood a long time with her forehead against the cool wood, listening to her own heart conduct its stubborn, unreasonable business.

*He offered you your life,* she told herself. *You refused it. Both things are true. Hold them together.*

Below, a door opened — the soft, deliberate creak of the cooper's stair — and Isandro came up into the loft carrying bread and a lantern he didn't light, because he had learned Hollowmere's rhythms too. He took one look at her face and set the bread down.

"Someone was here," he said.

"An old friend." She pushed off from the window. Her legs were steadier than she expected. "He's buying us three days, maybe four, maybe more. After that we're officially dead, and the hunt rides home, and whatever comes next comes without forty wardens at our backs." She managed something that was almost a smile. "We should sleep. Tomorrow we ride for the queen's conservatory, and I have to grow us through a palace wall."

Isandro looked at her for a moment longer than the words required — he had gotten good at hearing what she didn't say, which was a dangerous skill for a man keeping secrets — and then he nodded slowly and sat down against the wall, and the loft was quiet, and somewhere north a false report waited to be written in a loyal man's careful hand.

Wendell lay back in the hay and stared at the rafters and did not sleep, and in the black hours before dawn she heard, faint and far and unmistakable, the first thunder of the thirteenth moon gathering itself beyond the hills.
