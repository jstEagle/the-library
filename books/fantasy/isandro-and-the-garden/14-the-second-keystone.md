## 14. The Second Keystone

The map that the old woman in the free town had pressed into Wendell's hands was drawn on vellum gone soft as cloth, and it showed the queen's city as a ring of concentric circles — the outer walls, the merchant quarter, the palace grounds — and at the very center, inked in faded green rather than the black of the walls, a small square marked with a leaf.

The conservatory.

Isandro had known it would be there. He had known, too, what it would mean to ask her to go inside it, and for three days of travel along the pilgrim road toward the capital he had been rehearsing the asking, and every version of it was a lie.

Not entirely a lie. That was the poison of it. Each rehearsal contained truth enough to pass her inspection: *the second keystone lies behind the conservatory's glass; the wards there are living things, grown not built; only a gift like yours can open a way through them.* All true. What he did not say lived underneath those truths like a stone under moss — that the conservatory's wards were grown from covenant-wood, the same wood as the bridge, the same wood as the keystones themselves, and that covenant-wood answered to memory as readily as it answered to growth. That when Wendell put her hands into that living wall and asked it to part for her, it would look at her — at all of her, the way the wraith in the drowned chapel had looked at her — and it might show her things. And that if she saw far enough, she might see the price.

He could not allow that. Not yet. Not until they stood before the bridge with all four stones and no other road left. He told himself it was mercy. He told himself it was strategy — that a frightened heir might refuse to walk into the queen's own garden, and without the second keystone there would be nothing left to protect her *from*. He told himself these things in Isandro's voice, in his mother's voice, in the voice of the first guardian whose wraith waited somewhere ahead of them with the fourth stone and a confession Isandro already dreaded. The voices agreed with him. That was how he knew they were lying.

* * *

They came to the capital on the evening of a market day, when the roads were thick with carts and the gates were thick with guards who cared more about smuggled brandy than about two travelers in road-dusted cloaks. Isandro kept his hood up and his scarred hands gloved. In the human realm his bark-marked skin drew stares even in shadow; here, under the smoky gold of the city's thousand lamps, it would draw worse than stares. There were caged people in this city whose magic was being siphoned through the crown's machine, and though Isandro did not yet know where they were kept, he could feel them — faintly, the way one feels a fire in a distant room, a warmth that is wrong because it should be free.

Wendell felt it too. He watched her feel it, watching her face in the lamplight as they passed through the merchant quarter: the small tightening around her eyes, the way her gloved hand drifted toward a planter of winter kale outside a chandler's shop and the kale leaned after her fingers, just slightly, like a dog straining at an invisible rope.

"You feel them," he said quietly.

"I don't know what I feel." She pulled her hand back. "It's like hearing someone crying through a wall and not knowing which house."

"The palace," he said. "I think it's the palace."

She looked at him then, and in her eyes was the question he had been dreading since the free town — not *what* but *why*, the question of a gardener who knows that a wilting plant has a cause, and will not rest until she has found it. He gave her the answer he had prepared, the partial one, the one shaped like a whole.

"When we have the keystone," he said, "there will be time to follow the crying."

It was the first lie he had ever told her outright, and it sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

The conservatory abutted the palace's eastern wing, its glass roof a hill of green-black panes catching the moon, its walls — where they were not glass — grown rather than built. Isandro saw it first from the bell tower of a disused chapel three streets away, and something in him turned over painfully, the way a body turns over in sleep when it dreams of home. Because he knew this architecture. It was covenant-work, or had been once: the old guilds of both realms had built with living material in the centuries before the breaking, and the queen's ancestors, whatever else they had become, had inherited the craft and bent it to their own purposes. A garden made into a vault. Growing things taught to be a prison.

"They grew it," Wendell said beside him. She had come up the tower stairs silent as a cat and now stood at the narrow window, her breath fogging the cold stone. "Isandro — the walls are *grown*. That's guild work. That's—" Her voice caught. "That's Thornbury work. My grandmother's grandmother might have trained the hands that made this."

"Or the hands that made it trained hers," Isandro said. "The guilds served both realms once. When the covenant broke, some stayed in the human lands and took crown service. It wasn't treachery. It was survival."

"And now the crown uses their craft to cage people like me." She said it flatly, without heat, and that flatness frightened him more than weeping would have. Three weeks ago she had been a woman who hid the shimmer of her hands. Now she spoke of the crown's cruelty the way one speaks of weather.

"The keystone is inside," he said. "Under the central dome, if the old records are right. Grown into the root-stock of the oldest tree — a white ironwood, in the first realm called the *vaelthorn*, the tree the first bridge was cut from. The keystones were seed and branch of it. The queen keeps the second stone the way a miser keeps a coin she doesn't understand: locked in her strongest room, never guessing the room itself is the lock's weakness. Living wards answer to living gifts. Built walls answer to nothing but force, and force brings soldiers. We go through the garden, Wendell. Through the growing wall. And you're the only one who can ask it to open."

He felt her go still beside him. Not afraid — he had learned the difference by now, had learned her stillnesses the way he had once learned the moods of the border-mists. This was the stillness of a mind working, roots spreading through dark soil.

"There'll be wards on the wards," she said. "Guards?"

"At night, a walking patrol on the glass gallery above the dome. Two men, changing each hour. The wards themselves do most of the guarding — anything that enters the growing wall without invitation, the wall takes. I've seen what covenant-wood does to thieves." He paused. "It doesn't kill quickly."

"But it will know me." She turned from the window. In the dark her eyes held the last of the moonlight. "You said it yourself. Living wards answer to living gifts. If I put my hands into that wall and ask it to open, it will read me. Won't it? Like the wraith did."

Here it was. Here was the moment he had rehearsed for three days, and every rehearsal collapsed now like wet paper, because she had walked straight to the edge of the truth and stood looking down into it with her gardener's patience, waiting for him to hand her the honest thing.

*Tell her,* said every voice that had ever loved her, and Isandro understood suddenly that the voices were not agreeing with him anymore.

"It will read you," he said. "So we keep the reading shallow. You ask for passage — nothing more. A door, not a conversation. Don't let it pull you deep. The deeper the wood looks, the longer it holds you, and the gallery guard changes at the turn of the hour. We will have minutes, not conversations."

Every word true. Every word arranged, like flowers over a grave, to hide what lay beneath. She studied him for a long moment, and he bore it the way one bears a wound being cleaned, and at last she nodded.

"A door, not a conversation," she repeated. "All right."

That night he did not sleep. He sat in the chapel's cold loft with the first keystone wrapped in wool against his chest, feeling it hum its low, patient hum, and he thought about the nature of doors. A door is a kindness, he told himself. A door is what you give someone so they need not see the whole house at once. She will thank me. She must survive long enough to thank me or hate me, and either is better than the alternative, which is that she learns the price tonight, in a hostile garden, alone with the knowledge, and refuses — refuses the bridge, refuses the covenant, refuses to pay — and the realms bleed out together while she stands in her valley mourning a choice she was never allowed to make cleanly.

But underneath the reasoning, coiled and patient, was the simpler thing, the thing he had known since the garden where he had fallen at her feet: that he was not protecting her mission. He was protecting his place in it. Because the price of the covenant, spoken plainly, was this — *all memories tied to what the heir loves most* — and Isandro had watched her across three weeks of roads and rivers and ash, and he knew with a certainty that felt like a bone he had broken long ago: he was what she loved most. Or he was becoming it. And the day she learned the price, she would learn that loving him was the very thing the covenant would take from her, and she might choose, then, to stop — to fold the love up small and put it away, the way one puts away a coat in summer, against the day it will be needed no longer.

He could not bear it. That was the truth beneath the truths. His protectiveness had curdled, and he knew the taste of the curdling, because the first guardian's story was written in his bones: love, withheld from the price, cost more. But knowing a warning and heeding it were different countries, and he stood in the wrong one, and the moon was turning overhead whether he stood anywhere at all.

* * *

They went over the wall two nights later, on the dark of the moon's last quarter, when the sky was overcast and the watch-fires burned low. Ropes borrowed from a warehouse, grapples Isandro had forged himself from border-iron that remembered being star-metal. Wendell climbed like someone who had spent her life on orchard ladders, which, he supposed, she had.

The conservatory's growing wall rose from the palace grounds on its eastern side, a hedge gone monumental — twenty feet of woven living green, oak and hawthorn and stranger things, leaves glossy black in the night, the whole of it breathing. Isandro felt it breathe. Covenant-wood did not sleep the way ordinary trees slept; it dreamed shallow and watchful, and as they crouched in the ornamental yew-shadow at its base, he felt the wall's attention turn toward them like the attention of a great animal scenting wind.

Wendell pulled off her gloves.

He had seen her bare-handed gifts before — the sickroom herbs leaping green, the dying hedgerow along the Road of Ash flushing with impossible bloom. But this was different. Here she knelt before a wall that had been taught, over generations, to be a weapon, and she laid both palms flat against the woven trunks, and closed her eyes, and *asked*.

The wall shivered. Leaves stirred though there was no wind. Somewhere high above, in the glass gallery, a guard's boots crossed the dome and paused, and moved on.

*A door,* Isandro willed her silently. *Ask for a door. Nothing more.*

But the wood was already reading her — he could see it in the way the bark beneath her hands flushed pale, vein by vein, like skin remembering a blush — and the reading was going deeper than a door required. Her breath changed. Her lips parted. The shimmer that lived in her hands rose up her wrists like water finding its level, and the wall began, very softly, to glow.

"Wendell." He touched her shoulder. "Come back. Door. Only the door."

Her eyes opened. For a heartbeat they were not entirely her own — wide and green-lit and full of something seen — and then she blinked, and was herself, and the wall before them groaned and *parted*, trunks unwinding from trunks, weaving an archway tall enough to ride a horse through, floored with a path of living root worn smooth as flagstone.

"What did you see?" he asked, before he could stop himself.

She was quiet a moment, flexing her fingers, watching the shimmer fade.

"Growth," she said at last. "Just — growth. Old growth. Faster than anything I've ever—" She shook her head, smiling faintly, unsteadily. "It wanted to show me more. I didn't let it. Door, not a conversation. Right?"

"Right," Isandro said, and the coal in his chest burned a little hotter.

Inside, the conservatory was a cathedral of glass and green. Moonlight fell through the paned dome in great silver sheets, and beneath it grew the queen's collection: trees from every province of the human realm, ferns from the border fens, roses forced into bloom out of season, all of it immaculate, all of it *potted* — and that was the obscenity of the place, Isandro realized, the thing that made his bark-scars itch. Nothing here was planted in earth. Everything lived root-bound in vessels, displayed, owned. A kingdom's worth of growing things, permitted to live but not to spread.

And at the center, beneath the dome's highest point, stood the white ironwood.

It was vast — forty feet of silver-barked trunk rising to a canopy that brushed the glass, older than the palace around it, older perhaps than the palace's dynasty. Its roots had been given a sunken bed of true soil, the only such bed in the whole shining prison, and Isandro understood why: you cannot pot a vaelthorn. You can only contain it, and contain it badly, and pray. Half-buried among the great roots, glowing with a light that had nothing to do with the moon, lay the second keystone.

Wendell made a sound low in her throat, half reverence, half grief.

"It's *starving*," she whispered. "Look at the crown — the dieback, the dead limbs. They've got it rooted in good soil and it's starving anyway. It's been cut off from—" She stopped. Turned to him. "From the other realm. From everything that fed it. Thirteen years, and it's been standing here dying slowly, and they've built their vault around a corpse-in-progress and called it strength."

Above them, boots crossed the gallery. They froze in the shadow of a cycad's fronds, pressed together, her heart hammering against his arm, and waited out the long minute of the guard's passing. Then she moved — low and quick between the pots, straight to the ironwood's root-bed, and knelt, and reached into the dark between the roots.

The keystone came free with a sound like a sigh.

It was smaller than the first — a wedge of white wood shot through with veins of silver, warm as a living thing, humming against her palm the way the first had hummed against his. Wendell held it up to the moonlight, and tears stood in her eyes without falling, and Isandro felt the old dread and the old love rise together in him like twin tides.

"We have it," she breathed. "Two of four. Isandro, we—"

The lights came on.

Not firelight — lantern-glass flaring along the gallery, half a dozen of them at once, and voices, and the crash of a door thrown wide at the conservatory's far end. Boots on stairs. Many boots.

"—movement in the dome sector, ward-signal tripped, seal the east entrance—"

Isandro pulled Wendell down behind the ironwood's vast trunk as lantern-light swept the floor below. His mind, trained by centuries of guardianship, ran the exits and found them closing one by one: east door sealed, gallery above filling with crossbows, the growing wall's archway — he glanced — already knitting itself shut, obedient to its masters' alarm, erasing the door she had made as though it had never existed.

They were trapped in the queen's garden with her stolen treasure, and the wall that had opened for Wendell's gift would not open twice.

Then Wendell did something he did not expect. She looked at the keystone in her hand, and at the starving tree above her, and her expression changed — became the flat, weather-speaking calm he had heard in her voice on the Road of Ash.

"Isandro," she said softly, "when I touched the wall, it wasn't showing me growth. I lied too."

The lanterns swept closer. Below them, at the base of the ironwood, the soil of the root-bed had begun — very quietly, very impossibly — to move.
