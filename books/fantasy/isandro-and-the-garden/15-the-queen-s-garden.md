## 15. The Queen's Garden

The conservatory doors were taller than any Wendell had ever seen, wrought iron and glass gone green with age, and they opened at a touch — which frightened her more than locked ones would have.

"They're expecting us," she whispered. "Or they don't think anyone would be fool enough to come."

"Both, probably." Isandro crouched beside her in the shadow of the hedge they had grown that afternoon, a wall of thorn and bloom that now looked as though it had stood in the palace grounds for fifty years. His bark-scarred hands were steady, but his breathing wasn't. "Remember what I told you. The gardeners' entrance is through the potting sheds. We go in as servants of the soil. No one looks twice at dirt."

"No one in this palace has ever touched dirt," Wendell said, and was surprised by the bitterness in her own voice.

They crossed the open ground in the last grey light before dawn, two figures bent like laborers, Isandro pushing a wheelbarrow they'd found tipped behind the stables, Wendell carrying a bundle of tools she didn't know how to use. The palace rose above them, pale stone catching the first light, beautiful the way a blade is beautiful. Somewhere inside it, according to everything Rell had told them before he turned back toward his own ruin, was the second keystone — kept not in a vault but in the queen's own garden, because the queen believed, or wanted to believe, that she had tamed magic the way one tames a rose.

The potting shed smelled of loam and cut stems, and for one absurd moment Wendell felt homesick. Then her hand brushed a shelf of seedling pots and every seed in them turned toward her fingers like faces toward the sun, and the homesickness curdled into something colder.

*They grow things here,* she thought. *Things that reach.*

Isandro pressed a finger to his lips and led her along the service corridor, past sacks of bone meal and coils of twine, to a door of frosted glass. Through it, the conservatory spread out like a captured sky.

Wendell had imagined greenhouses before. She had imagined rows of citrus trees and ferns and orchids under glass, the kind of room a wealthy house might keep. What lay beyond the door was a cathedral.

The central hall soared three stories, its glass roof ribbed with iron painted white, and every inch of it was planted. Vines climbed the columns. Trees grew from marble floors, their roots splitting the stone in patient triumph. Flowers hung in cascades from tiered balconies of brick, and the air was so thick with scent and pollen that Wendell could taste it, sweet and green and wrong.

Wrong, because nothing here was growing *toward* anything. In her grandmother's garden, even half-dead, plants had leaned — toward light, toward water, toward her hidden hands. Here, every living thing leaned inward, toward the center of the hall, straining like drowning people toward the surface of their own drowning.

At the center stood the machine.

She had no other word for it. It was built into the heart of the great hall where a fountain should have been: a ring of black iron tall as three men, engraved with script that hurt to look at directly, its letters rearranging themselves when she blinked. Within the ring hung chains — dozens of them, hundreds — and from each chain hung a crystal the size of a fist, and every crystal glowed with a slow, sick pulse, like hearts beating in jars.

And around the ring, in cages of glass and brass, sat the gifted.

Wendell stopped walking. Isandro's hand closed on her shoulder, but she barely felt it.

There were perhaps thirty of them. They sat or lay on benches inside their cages, and they were not starved, not beaten, not marked in any way a passing eye would catch. They were simply *empty*. A young man with silver threading through his hair stared at his own hands as though he'd never seen them. An old woman rocked gently, humming a tune that changed every few bars, as if she kept forgetting which song she was making. A girl who couldn't have been more than ten sat pressed against the glass of her cage, watching the crystals pulse, and her eyes followed each glow with the vacant hunger of someone watching a fire they can no longer feel.

Chains ran from each cage to the great ring. And as Wendell watched, the nearest crystal flared — and the old woman stopped humming mid-note, her mouth still open, and something left her. Wendell saw it leave. It came off her skin like heat-shimmer, like breath in winter, a thread of pale gold that drifted up the chain and sank into the crystal, and the crystal brightened, and the woman swayed, and when she began humming again it was a different song, and her face had forgotten why she'd been sad.

"They're being drained," Isandro breathed. He sounded as though the words cost him blood. "Not killed. *Harvested.* Their gift, and—" He stopped.

"And what," Wendell said. Her voice came out flat and strange.

"Their memories, Wendell. Look at their eyes. Magic and memory are braided — you know that better than anyone. When they siphon the gift, they take the life that grew around it. Every year those people lived with their power. Every person they loved with it. It all goes into the crystals."

The girl in the near cage lifted her hand and pressed it flat against the glass, and Wendell — before she could stop herself, before Isandro could catch her wrist — stepped forward, and the girl's eyes found hers.

For a moment there was nothing there. Nothing at all, a window with the lamp blown out.

Then the girl's brow furrowed, very slightly, the way a plant furrows toward a warmth it cannot name, and her lips moved.

"*Green,*" she mouthed. "*You smell like green.*"

Wendell's vision blurred. She became aware that the plants of the whole conservatory had turned — every leaf, every tendril, every hanging blossom rotating gently toward her like a field of sunflowers finding the sun, and the motion made a sound, a vast dry whisper, and somewhere across the hall a bell chimed softly, an alarm woven from jasmine and wire.

"Wendell." Isandro's grip was iron now. "The keystone. The keystone, and then we go."

She tore her eyes from the girl. The keystone — the second one, the one Rell's stolen plans had shown them — was set above the great ring itself, mounted in the iron like a jewel in a crown, glowing with a light that was almost the same gold as the threads draining out of the captives. Of course it was. Of course the queen had built her engine around a piece of the covenant, the way a thief builds a shrine around a stolen saint.

"We can't leave them," Wendell said.

"We can't free them. Not tonight. If we break the siphons while the machine is live, the backlash—"

"I know about backlash." She thought of the Road of Ash, of rivers running backward, of crops that remembered being forests and grieved for it. "I've walked through what happens when power is taken and never given back."

Isandro was silent. Above them, the glass roof was paling toward morning, and somewhere in the palace a clock struck five.

"Then we do both," he said at last, and hated himself visibly as he said it. "Keystone first. Then whatever we can do in the time we have."

Getting up to the ring meant crossing the open floor, past cage after cage, and Wendell made herself look at each of them as she passed. It felt like a debt. A young man whose fingertips sprouted tiny white flowers when he dreamed — the flowers were still there, blooming and wilting over and over, the only part of him still working. A fisherman with webbed scars between his fingers, asleep, murmuring tide-chants in a language his children would have known. A woman—

A woman sitting very straight on her bench, hands folded in her lap, looking at nothing.

Wendell stopped.

It was the hands. She knew hands; a gardener knows hands the way a musician knows instruments. These were broad-palmed, long-fingered, with dirt worked so deep into the knuckle-creases it had become part of the skin, and the right thumbnail was split in a way that only comes from decades of prying stones out of stubborn earth. Wendell had watched hands like these her whole childhood, moving over seedlings, coaxing, firm and gentle at once. She had learned everything she knew by imitating hands like these.

Her mother's hands.

The woman was perhaps forty-five. Thirteen years ago she would have been thirty-two. Her hair was brown going grey at the temples, tied back with a strip of cloth. Her face was weathered and plain and calm, and when Wendell stood before her cage, the woman turned her head with the slow courtesy of someone greeting a stranger, and smiled.

"Hello," the woman said. Her voice was warm and rough as bark. "Are you new? They bring new ones sometimes. You get used to it. Well." She considered. "You don't, actually. But you stop minding that you don't."

Wendell could not speak. Her throat had closed around something enormous.

"You've got good hands," the woman went on, nodding at Wendell's own. "Soil under the nails. That's rare, in here. Most of them were town-gifted, candle-flicker stuff, pretty tricks. But you—" she squinted, and for one heartbeat something moved far down behind her eyes, something vast and buried turning over in its sleep, "—you're a grower. I can always tell growers. We hold more. That's why it hurts more when they take it."

"What's your name?" Wendell managed. It came out as a croak.

The woman laughed softly. "That's the funny thing. I keep almost remembering it. It's right there." She tapped her temple. "Like a word on the tip of your tongue, except it's been on the tip of my tongue for thirteen years. I know I had daughters." She said it simply, without self-pity, the way one reports weather. "Two of them, I think. Or maybe the memory of having daughters is all that's left, and I invented the number. But my hands remember small hands holding them. Little fists, right around my thumbs. So I know I was somebody's mother, once. Whoever I was, I must have been a good one. My hands are too gentle to have been anything else."

The conservatory whisper rose again around them, leaves straining, and Wendell realized tears were running down her face and did not remember starting them.

"My name is Wendell," she said. "Wendell Thornbury. Of the Thornbury guild. Of the valley."

The woman's smile stayed perfectly pleasant. Perfectly empty. And then — the smallest thing, a thing Wendell would carry for the rest of her life — the woman's folded hands tightened, just once, just slightly, knuckles whitening and releasing, the way a gardener's hands tighten around a trowel when she hears frost is coming.

"That's a nice name," the woman said. "It sounds like a place I used to know. Or a place I was going to take someone. Isn't it strange, the things that stay?" She unfolded her hands and reached out and laid her palm against the glass, and Wendell put her own palm against the other side, and through the cold pane the two hands matched exactly, line for line, callus for callus, split thumbnail and all.

"Mother," Wendell whispered. "Mama. It's me. It's Wendell. I came back. I came to find you."

Something flickered. Deep, deep down, past thirteen years of siphoning, past the stripped rooms and the emptied shelves of her mind, something turned toward the voice the way every plant in the building was turning toward Wendell now — blindly, desperately, a root seeking water through stone. The woman's calm face trembled at its edges like a reflection in a disturbed pond.

"I don't—" she began, and her voice cracked, and for a moment it was not a stranger's voice at all but one from a dream Wendell had spent thirteen years trying to reconstruct, a voice singing over a cradle, a voice saying *don't cry, little sprout, things grown in love hold longest* — "I don't know you. But I know I would walk through fire for you. Isn't that the strangest thing? They took everything and my hands still won't unclench. Whoever you are — whoever you were to me —"

"Bells," Isandro said urgently. "Boots. Wendell, we have minutes."

The woman heard them too. Whatever was left of her practical soul surfaced, and her grip on the glass firmed, and suddenly she was brisk, competent, a gardener giving orders across a row of beans.

"Go," she said. "Whatever you came for, take it and go. Don't waste it on me — I'm mostly gone, love, and what's left of me wants one thing only." Her eyes, her mother's eyes, held Wendell's with terrible clarity. "Find whoever did this. And tell them my hands remember. Tell them the hands remember *everything*, even when the head forgets. Hands keep score."

Above them, mounted in the black iron, the second keystone pulsed.

Wendell wrenched herself away. There was no graceful way to do it; she simply tore her palm from the glass and ran, past the sleeping fisherman, past the boy with the dying flowers on his fingertips, past the little girl who whispered *green* as she passed, up the iron stair to the base of the great ring where the keystone burned in its setting.

It was guarded by wards — of course it was — but the wards were written in the same covenant-script as the keystones themselves, and when Wendell reached out with her gift instead of her hands, the script recognized her. Recognized its heir. The wards parted like curtains, like a mother drawing blankets back from a child's bed, and the keystone came free into her palms with a hum she felt in her teeth, in her sternum, in the split-earth core of her.

Gold light spilled from it. Below, thirty heads turned toward the glow, thirty pairs of emptied eyes filling, for one moment, with something that was almost hope.

"Take it and go," her mother's voice called up from below, steady as a planting bell. "Don't look back till you're outside the walls. Gardeners' rule. You never look back till you're outside the walls."

Wendell looked back anyway. She owed her that.

Her mother was smiling at her, hands folded, calm as noon, and as Wendell met her eyes one last time, the woman raised one hand — those broad, split-nailed, unforgettable hands — and made a small gesture Wendell's body understood before her mind did: two fingers pressed to the heart, then flung outward. *Grow well. Grow far.*

Then boots were thundering in the corridors, and Isandro was pulling her down the stair and along the glass wall toward the service door, and behind them the great ring shuddered and brightened as if sensing its meal escaping, and the little girl in the nearest cage pressed both palms to the glass and called out in a clear, carrying voice:

"*Come back for us, green lady! Come back and open the windows!*"

Wendell ran. Outside the walls, in the grey wet dark of the palace gardens, with the second keystone burning against her chest and her mother's voice ringing in her skull — *the hands remember everything* — she finally let herself understand what hope had just been handed to her, terrible and shining:

Her mother was alive. Her mother was *reachable*. Whatever the machine had taken, it had not taken everything — and if a stolen memory could surface, then stolen memories could be given back.

All she had to do was find out how.

And behind them, deep in the conservatory, the machine began, very softly, to sing.
