## 16. What Was Taken

The woman with her mother's hands sat by the eastern wall of the conservatory, where the glass had gone green with age and the light came through filtered, like light at the bottom of a pond. She was mending a shirt that was not hers — Wendell could see that much even from across the row of cages, could see the careful way she turned the fabric, the little tuck of her thumb before each stitch, the way she bit the thread instead of cutting it.

Her mother had bitten thread exactly like that. Had held a needle exactly like that, in the kitchen of the guild house, on evenings when the frost came early and there was nothing to do but mend and talk. Wendell had watched those hands for years without knowing she was memorizing them.

Now she stood three rows away, behind iron bars painted over so many times they had gone soft-edged and dull, and she did not know what to do with her own hands at all.

* * *

Isandro had told her not to look at them. That had been his first instruction, back when they'd slipped through the conservatory's service door among the gardeners' carts: *Don't look at their faces. Look at the keystones' trail. If you look, you'll stop moving, and if you stop moving we die here.*

She had promised. She had lasted four rows.

The caged gifted sat or lay or paced in their long iron pens, twenty of them, thirty, more than Wendell could count without counting, which she refused to do because a number would make it real. They were fed well. That was the horror of it — someone had thought about their meals. There were blankets. There was straw that looked recently changed. The crown had built a place where people could be kept alive and emptied at the same time, and it had been built by someone who cared about comfort, who had perhaps wept while designing it, and that was worse than cruelty. Cruelty she knew how to hate. This she only knew how to stand in front of.

Each pen held its occupant in a lattice of thin copper wire, strung floor to ceiling like the strings of some vast instrument, and from each lattice a single line of thicker cable ran up into the dark of the ceiling rafters, where the machine lived — the thing Isandro had named and refused to describe. The wires hummed. Not loudly. It was the hum of a hive in winter, of a held breath, and as Wendell passed each cage the humming changed pitch, very slightly, the way a room changes when someone new enters it.

The woman's lattice hummed lowest of all.

Wendell stopped. She could not help it. The woman looked up from her mending, and Wendell saw her face, and it was not her mother's face — it was thinner, older, lined around the mouth in ways her mother's had never been — and relief went through her like a blade going in, because for one whole terrible second she had believed, and belief like that could crack a person open where they stood.

"You shouldn't be here," the woman said. Her voice was rough with disuse. "They come at dusk. You don't want to be here at dusk."

"I'm not—" Wendell's throat closed. She tried again. "I'm looking for something. A stone. It would be old, and it would—"

"Hum," the woman said. "Yes. I know what you're looking for." Her needle paused mid-stitch. "I can feel it through the walls. It's been singing to me for three days. Nobody else hears it." Something moved behind her eyes then, something quick and hungry and terribly familiar, and Wendell recognized it because she saw it in her own reflection sometimes: the look of a gift pressing against a locked door. "You're one of us."

"I'm not one of anything."

"Your hands are shaking," the woman said gently. "Gifted hands shake when they're near a keystone. Mine used to. Before they took—" She stopped herself, and Wendell watched her do it, watched her fold the sentence away somewhere deep, and understood with a cold sliding certainty that this woman had learned to hide things inside herself the way Wendell hid the shimmer of her hands. Survival taught the same lessons everywhere.

"What did they take?" Wendell asked.

The woman set down the mending. Folded it. Set it on her knee. All the small deliberate motions of someone buying time.

"They call it pruning," she said. "There's a chair. There are wires, the same as these, but finer. They ask you questions and pull the answers out through your scalp, one at a time, like drawing thread off a spool. You feel it leave. You feel the shape of it go — not the words, the *shape*, the weight of it, the way it fit against all your other memories. And then it's gone and there's just a smooth place where it was, like a riverbed after a drought." She smiled, and it was the worst thing Wendell had seen yet in this whole poisoned country, because it was a kind smile. "I don't remember my daughter's name. I remember that I have a daughter. I remember loving her. The love is still there — they couldn't take that, or didn't want to, I don't know which is crueler. But the name is a smooth place now. I sit here some days and I try to walk around the edges of it, and I can feel how big it was. Like a house you grew up in, burned down, and you're standing in the field where it stood and you can still feel where the rooms were."

Wendell gripped the bars. The iron was cold and paint-flaked under her palms.

"Your daughter," she managed. "How old is she?"

"She'd be—" The woman's brow creased, and Wendell watched her reach into the smooth place, watched her press against it the way a tongue presses a missing tooth. "Thirteen years. Thirteen years since anyone came. So she'd be grown now, or nearly. I was taken when she was small." Her eyes came up, and they were brown, ordinary brown, nothing like her mother's grey, and yet the hands — the hands were the same hands, and Wendell could not stop looking at them. "Why are you crying?"

"I'm not."

"You are. And you're gripping those bars like you know them." The woman leaned forward, and the copper lattice sang softly around her, and Wendell felt the hum slide up her own arms and settle in her chest, in the place where her gift lived, dormant and listening. "Who are you? What's your name?"

"Wendell."

Something flickered across the woman's face — not recognition, nothing so kind as recognition, but the ghost of a path her mind had once walked, worn away now, grass-grown. "That's an odd name. Guild name, isn't it? Gardener folk." She tilted her head. "You have guild hands. I know guild hands; I worked the palace conservatory before they put me in it. We used to trade letters with the valley guilds, before the silence. Before thirteen years ago." Her voice dropped. "Everyone says thirteen years ago like it's a door slammed shut. Do you know what happened thirteen years ago?"

"No," Wendell said. "Nobody will tell me."

"Because nobody knows. Or nobody who knows can say it." The woman picked up her mending again, absently, the needle finding its rhythm as though her hands needed work to think. "But I'll tell you what I remember — what's left of it. I remember the night the sky over the border went wrong. Everyone remembers that; it wasn't taken from anyone, it was too big to take. The bridge of light out past the Ash Road — it tore. Like cloth. Like something pulled a seam that should never have been pulled. And the same night, two people came through our town heading for the border, a man and a woman, running, both of them guild-marked, both of them carrying stones that hummed." The needle stopped. "Stones like the one singing behind your eyes right now. Don't pretend I can't feel it. You've got one on you, or you've touched one recently, and either way you're standing in a place you don't understand."

Wendell's hand went to her satchel without her permission. Inside it, wrapped in the seed-packet cloth her grandmother had given her, the first keystone pulsed its low warm note — the note it had sung since the drowned chapel, louder now, insistent, as though it knew it was near its sibling.

"Two people," Wendell said. "Guild-marked. Running for the border. What did they look like?"

The woman looked at her for a long moment. And Wendell saw her decide — saw the decision move through her like weather, caution giving way to something older and more generous, the instinct of a woman who had lost everything except the wish to be useful.

"The man was tall," she said slowly, feeling her way along the smooth places, reading the shapes of the holes. "Dark-haired. He walked like he wasn't from here — I remember thinking that, that he moved wrong for a human man, too smooth, like water deciding to be a person. And the woman—" Here the needle stilled completely. The woman's whole body went quiet, the way a forest goes quiet before something breaks cover. "The woman I remember better. I remember her turning around. She heard something behind her — hoofbeats, maybe, or hunters — and she turned, and she pushed her hair out of her face, and she had grey eyes. Grey like frost flowers. And she looked frightened, but not for herself. That's what I remember most. Frightened *for*. She was carrying something against her chest, wrapped up, and she held it the way you hold—"

The woman stopped.

The needle dropped from her fingers and rolled across the straw.

"Oh," she said. Very quietly. "Oh, you have her face."

* * *

Afterward Wendell could never have said how long they knelt there, she on the outside of the bars and the woman on the inside, foreheads nearly touching through the copper lattice, which sang between them like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Because the memory had come loose. That was the thing about pruned minds, the woman had explained, gasping, laughing, crying, all three at once — the thieves took the memories but the taking left channels, scar-tissue paths through the mind, and sometimes, when something pressed hard enough against the smooth places, what lived on the far side leaked through. And Wendell's face, Wendell's grey frost-flower eyes, Wendell's name spoken aloud in a voice that had said it before — all of it had pressed, all at once, against every channel at once.

"It failed," the woman whispered. She was holding Wendell's hands through the lattice now, both of them, the way you hold the hands of someone at a deathbed. "Oh, sweetheart. Oh, you poor child. They paid it. Your father and mother paid the price — the whole price, everything the covenant asked, I can see it now, it's coming back in pieces, it's coming back like blood through a bandage — they gave everything. I remember your mother telling me. We were friends. Were we friends? Yes — yes, we were friends, she stayed with me the night before they went, she was so calm, she said the price was meant to be survivable, she said the guild had paid it before, generation after generation, and the covenant held, and the gardener forgot and the realm healed and everyone lived. She said it like a recipe. She said it like she was comforting *me*." The woman's grip tightened until it hurt. "And they went to the bridge. And they spoke the covenant. And the bridge *tore*."

"That's not—" Wendell heard her own voice from very far away. "Grandmother said they abandoned it. She said they ran. She said—"

"Someone lied to you." The woman said it flatly, with the terrible clarity of a person who has had everything soft stripped out of her and has no patience left for kindnesses. "Or someone believed a lie so long it became true in her mouth. Your parents did not run, child. They *paid*. They gave the covenant everything it asked — every memory, the full sacrifice, the whole of what they loved — and the covenant took it and the bridge tore anyway, and the magic died in your valley the same hour, and the realms began to bleed, and your parents—" Here she faltered, and Wendell watched her grope along another smooth edge, another burned house. "They didn't die at the bridge. That's the part that doesn't come. They didn't die *there*. They came back. Someone brought them back, or they crawled back, empty as winter trees, and then—"

"And then?" Wendell's voice cracked down the middle.

"And then the crown came for them. Because the breaking was blamed on the gifted — you must know that, everyone knows that, that's why we're all in cages — and two guild-marked people found empty on the border road, that was proof, that was *evidence*, they were taken to the palace and put in chairs with fine wires, and whatever was left in them was pulled out thread by thread." The woman was crying now, silently, the tears running into the lines around her mouth. "I think your mother fought it. I think she hid something. I remember — I remember her saying, the night before, *if it goes wrong, if it fails, I'll leave a door in myself, I'll leave a message where they can't prune it* — she said the deepest memories grow roots, she said some things root below where any wire can reach — and then the sky tore and everything after that is smooth, smooth, smooth—"

The hum of the lattice rose. Somewhere above them, in the rafters, the machine shifted in its sleep, and the cables shivered, and down the rows of cages the gifted stirred like wind moving through a wheatfield.

"Dusk," the woman said sharply, all at once herself again, fierce, practical, her mother's-hands hands pushing Wendell back from the bars. "It's dusk. They come at dusk. Go — go *now*, go find your stone, and listen to me, listen—" She seized Wendell's wrists through the lattice one last time, and her brown eyes bored into Wendell's grey ones, and the last of the leaking memory poured through her voice like light through a cracked door:

"They didn't abandon you. Remember that, whatever else they take from me for saying it. Your parents bought the realms thirteen years with everything they had, and it wasn't enough, and it wasn't their failing — the price was *wrong*, do you understand? The price itself was wrong. One person paying alone was always going to tear the seam. Find out why. Find the door your mother left. And when you get out of here—"

Boots. Many boots, in the corridor beyond the glass, and lantern-light beginning to swing across the far wall.

"When you get out of here," the woman finished, releasing her, already reaching calmly for her fallen needle, already the picture of a prisoner mending a shirt, "*run toward the bridge, not away from it.*"

Wendell ran. Down the rows, past the humming lattices, past the sleeping machine's black cabling, toward the shadow where Isandro waited by the service door — and behind her the woman's voice followed, pitched low but carrying, the way voices carry in gardens:

"And tell my daughter — if you ever find her — tell her my name was Maren, and I never once forgot that I loved her!"

The lanterns swung. The first guard rounded the glass wall. And Wendell, sprinting into Isandro's arms, into the dark of the service corridor, carrying a truth that had cracked her open like frost splitting stone, did not see the guards reach Maren's cage — did not see them take her away, not to her pen, but downward, toward the chairs with the fine wires, to pay, tonight, for the memory she had given away.

She only knew, as Isandro pulled her through the dark, that her parents had paid everything and been called traitors for it. That the price was wrong. That her mother had left a door somewhere inside her own mind, rooted below the reach of any wire.

And that somewhere ahead, past the Ash Road, a torn bridge of light was waiting to be asked why.
