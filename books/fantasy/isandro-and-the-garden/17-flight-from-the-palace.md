## 17. Flight from the Palace

The alarm did not sound like bells. It sounded like the conservatory itself waking up.

Isandro felt it first in his teeth — a low harmonic shudder running through the glass panes overhead, through the iron ribs that held them, through the soil beds where the queen's stolen garden grew in perfect, joyless rows. Then came the light. Every lantern along the gallery flared from amber to white, and the shadows of the caged gifted leapt and scattered like startled birds.

"They know," Wendell said beside him. Her voice was very quiet, the way it went quiet when she was most afraid. "Isandro. They know we're here."

He had known for three breaths longer than she had — long enough to understand *how* they knew. The second keystone hung at his hip inside its wrapping of oiled cloth, and it had stopped humming. It had gone silent as a held breath, and silence, in a place built to listen for magic, was louder than any song.

"Then we stop being careful," he said, and pulled the cloth away.

Light poured out of the stone — not white like the alarm-lanterns but green, deep and living, the green of the old realm's spring forests, of moss on the covenant-bridge, of every growing thing he had guarded for two hundred years before the breaking. The nearest cages answered. He heard them answer: a faint stirring, a rustle, a moan. The captives pressed toward the bars of their prisons like plants toward sun.

"Wendell." He caught her wrist. Her pulse hammered against his fingers. "The keystone. Take it to the bridge — don't wait for me, don't come back for me—"

"No." She said it simply, the way she said everything that mattered. "You knew I'd say no when you asked."

He had. That was the trouble with her. Two hundred years of guarding a covenant between realms, and no one had ever refused him so thoroughly, so kindly, so *certainly*.

"The east door," he said instead. "I'll open the cages. You take the keystone out through the orangery. Rell's men are stationed at the west gate — he'll have moved them by now or he won't. Either way, you run."

Her hand closed over the keystone, and Isandro watched something pass between her and the stone that he had never seen in any guardian's hands — not in his predecessor's, not in his own. The light bent toward her. The stone *leaned*, the way a seedling leans toward a window. As if it had been waiting thirteen years not for a guardian but for a gardener.

"Go," she said to him, echoing him, and then she was moving through the steam and the green light, small and swift and carrying the fate of two realms in a satchel over one shoulder.

* * *

The cages were worse up close. Isandro had seen them from the gallery when they infiltrated, had made himself look only long enough to count exits, because looking longer would have undone him. Now there was no time to be undone, and so he looked.

Forty-one cages of glass and cold iron, arranged in the conservatory's heart like an orchard of suffering. Each held a gifted man or woman, some young, some old, one who could not have been past her twelfth year. Wires of silvered copper ran from iron bands at their throats and wrists back into the great machine at the conservatory's center — the severing engine, the queen's monument to her fear — and where the wires touched skin, Isandro could see what they took. Not blood. Not breath. *Memory*. It ran along the copper in thin bright threads, threads of color and shape and name, and pooled in the machine's reservoirs like sap tapped from a wounded tree.

They were being drunk dry. Slowly, methodically, in the queen's own garden, under glass, among the roses.

A woman in the nearest cage lifted her head when his shadow crossed her. Her eyes were the worst thing he had ever seen — not empty, but *fading*, like a photograph left too long in sun. She had been beautiful once; you could see the architecture of it still, beneath the erosion.

"You're one of them," she whispered. "One of the free ones. Why did you come *in*?"

"To let you out." He set his palms against the cage's iron frame and let his gift rise.

It came harder than it should have. The valley behind them had been magicless, and this palace sat near enough its border that the air here was thin, starved, resistant — and the machine drank greedily from anything magical that came near. But the keystone's passage through the room had changed things. The air remembered being whole. Isandro pushed into that memory the way fingers push into wet clay, and the iron groaned, and the lock — a mechanism, not a spell, because the crown feared spells even as it harvested them — sheared apart.

The woman stumbled out into the aisle. She did not thank him. She turned and looked back at her cage with an expression Isandro recognized from mirrors, from the faces of the broken-covenant years: the look of someone who cannot tell whether they have been freed or merely moved.

"Go," he told her. "East corridor, down the servants' stair, the third door is unbarred. There are others coming behind you."

There were not. There was only him, and forty-one locks, and perhaps four minutes before the guard reached the conservatory doors. He went to the next cage.

* * *

Wendell ran.

He lost sight of her almost immediately — the orangery swallowed her, all that white glass and fog and the pale shapes of citrus trees grown from cuttings of a country the queen had never visited — but he felt her, the way he had felt her since the night she touched the first keystone in her walled garden and the hum of it had answered something in her blood. A warmth moving away from him through the cold palace. A candle carried down a long dark hall.

*Don't come back for me,* he had said, knowing she would, and now he made himself mean it, made himself work faster, because if she came back he would never forgive himself and if she didn't he would never forgive himself either, and there was no version of this night that ended clean.

The third cage held a man whose gift, according to the tag wired to his ankle, was rain. He wept without sound as Isandro broke the lock, and the tears that fell were saltless, spent dry, another thing the machine had taken. The fourth held twins who shared one gift between them — they spoke in each other's voices, finished each other's memories, and when the cage opened they clung to one another and to nothing else, because everything else had been drained.

"Which way is out?" the elder twin asked. Fifteen, maybe. Voice like a cracked bell.

"East. Follow the woman ahead of you if you can. If you can't—" Isandro tore a strip from his sleeve and pressed it into the boy's hand. It was nothing, a scrap of cloth, but he breathed on it, one long exhale, and the cloth took the faintest shimmer, the smell of rain on hot stone. "Follow this. It will pull toward open sky."

The boy stared at the scrap. "What are you?"

"Gone," Isandro said, "if you're still standing here in ten seconds."

* * *

He was on the ninth cage when the guards came.

He heard them before he saw them — boots on the gallery above, the flat clatter of drawn crossbows, a voice calling positions in the clipped cadence of soldiers who had drilled this exact response. The white lantern-light sharpened. Somewhere below, the machine's reservoirs began to churn, and the captive in cage ten began to scream, softly, continuously, as the drain-wires woke and started pulling.

*Not tonight.* Isandro put both hands against the ninth cage and stopped being gentle.

The iron shrieked. Glass starred and burst outward. The drain-wires snapped like harp strings, and the backlash ran up them into the machine, and somewhere in the conservatory's heart something large and expensive made a sound like a struck bell dying. The guards on the gallery hesitated — he could feel their hesitation, taste it, fear has a flavor — and in that hesitation he broke cages ten, eleven, twelve, working blind now, gift roaring through him wilder than he had allowed it since the breaking, since the last time he had torn something open rather than tend it.

Thirteen. Fourteen. The freed ones were running, a ragged river of them pouring toward the east corridor, and the guards had found their courage and were firing down into the aisles, bolts splintering the citrus trees in the orangery beyond, and Isandro thought with a strange distant clarity: *she planted these trees herself, the queen, I read the grafting records, she loves this room, and she filled it with cages.*

Fifteen. Sixteen. His vision was going gray at the edges. The thin air, the machine's hunger, the sheer expenditure — he was spending himself like a miser in a fire, and he knew the arithmetic of it, knew exactly how much of himself remained, and kept counting anyway.

Seventeen. Eighteen—

A bolt took him high in the left shoulder and spun him against the cage frame.

* * *

Pain, white and total. The floor. Iron under his cheek. Above him the gallery rail, and a soldier leaning over it drawing again, unhurried now, certain.

The bolt never flew.

A hand closed on the soldier's crossbow — a big hand, a soldier's hand, gloved in the grey leather of the crown hunt — and wrenched the weapon aside, and Captain Rell leaned over the rail with his face arranged into official blankness and said, in a voice pitched to carry, "Cease fire! The prisoner is to be taken alive — crown orders, direct from the Lord Inquisitor. Anyone who fires again answers to me."

And then, while twenty crossbows lowered in obedience, Rell looked down at Isandro on the floor, and for half a heartbeat the official blankness cracked, and what showed underneath was thirteen years old and sitting on a garden wall sharing stolen plums with a girl who talked to plants.

*Rell.* Isandro had met him twice before this night, across drawn blades and across a campfire's worth of lies, and had catalogued him as an obstacle, a decent man wearing a bad oath. He had not understood until this moment that the oath was eating him alive from the inside.

Rell dropped from the gallery. He landed beside Isandro, hauled him up by the collar with theatrical roughness, and marched him toward the east corridor, shouting, "Moving the prisoner to holding!" — and the moment they rounded the corner, out of every sightline, his grip changed from a chain to a crutch.

"Third door," Rell said through his teeth. "Servants' stair. Your people went down it ninety seconds ago. Go."

"You'll be—" Isandro started.

"I know what I'll be." Rell's jaw worked. Somewhere behind them the machine was still screaming its broken-bell note, and the alarms still strobed, and the captain of the crown hunt stood in a palace corridor committing treason in real time. "She got the stone?"

"She got the stone."

"Then go. Both of you go, and don't stop until—" He stopped. His eyes went briefly, unbearably, toward the conservatory, toward the cages he had walked past every day for a year and saluted the guards outside of. "Tell her—" he began, and couldn't finish it, whatever *it* was, thirteen years of unfinished sentences crowding his throat. He shoved Isandro instead, hard, toward the stairwell door. "Tell her nothing. Tell her you escaped on your own. If they think I helped a stranger, it's a mistake. If they think I helped *her*—"

Boots. Many boots, at the far end of the corridor, and a voice calling, "Captain Rell! Report!"

Rell straightened. The blankness came down over his face like a visor. He seized Isandro's arm one more time — and this grip was a prisoner's grip, painful, correct, deniable — and turned him to face the approaching squad.

"I found him," Rell announced, loud and flat. "Injured, attempting to reach the captives. I have him secured."

The squad commander's eyes narrowed. "Why is your weapon sheathed, Captain?"

Isandro saw it happen — saw Rell understand, in the space of a single breath, that there was no story clever enough, no position careful enough, that the arithmetic had already failed. And he saw what Rell did with that understanding, which was to square his shoulders and plant his feet and say, with the ghost of something that was almost a laugh:

"Because if I'd wanted him dead, sergeant, you'd have found him dead."

* * *

Isandro ran.

He took the servants' stair three steps at a time, shoulder screaming, gift guttering, following the faint pull of his own breath-magic still clinging to a strip of cloth somewhere ahead of him in the dark. Behind him, muffled by stone, he heard the sounds of Rell's arrest — shouts, a scuffle, the terrible orderliness of it, no fight at all, a man letting himself be taken because fighting would spend lives he wasn't willing to spend.

*Escape bought with other people's sacrifice.* The thought kept pace with him down every turning. The woman in the first cage, walking back out into a world that had forgotten her name. The twins with their single shared gift. Forty-one cages and he had opened nineteen, and twenty-two souls were still wired to the machine, still draining, and the price of the nineteen was a captain in irons and a trail of evidence leading straight to the girl he loved.

He burst out through the third door into the night — the kitchen yard, the herb beds silvered with frost, the wall — and there she was.

Wendell stood at the foot of the wall with the keystone's green light leaking through her satchel seams, waiting. Of course she was waiting. He had told her not to come back for him and she had waited at the one exit he could not miss.

"Isandro—" She saw the shoulder, the blood black on grey cloth, and her face did something complicated and furious and afraid. "You're hurt. You're—"

"Nineteen," he gasped, reaching her. "Nineteen cages. Twenty-two still inside. And Rell—"

"I know." Her voice cracked on it. "I saw them take him. From the wall. I *saw*—"

"He chose it." Isandro gripped her arms, made her look at him, made her hear it, because he could already see her turning toward the palace, toward the walls, toward the impossible. "Wendell. He chose it. He knew the cost and he paid it laughing — I watched him do it. The only way to honor it is to make it worth something. The bridge. Tonight. Before the moon sets."

Behind them, above the palace roofline, the sky was doing something wrong. The clouds had begun to turn, slowly, around a point above the conservatory, and a thread of pale light was rising from the building's heart into the storm — the machine, wounded but alive, drinking deeper to compensate, pulling memory out of twenty-two remaining souls and out of the city's sleeping thousands, anything, everything, to finish the work the queen had begun.

The thread of light bent, at its highest point, toward the moon.

Two turns left. Maybe fewer, now. The machine was learning to hurry.

Wendell looked at the sky, and at the wall, and at him. Then she laced her fingers together and made a stirrup of her hands, the way gardeners do, the way you lift someone over a gate.

"Up," she said. "You first. Your shoulder can't take the climb."

He put his boot in her hands and marveled, one last time before the wall took them both into the dark fields beyond, at how she always knew the practical thing, the tender thing, the thing that kept them alive — and had no idea, none at all, what the covenant would ask of her when they finally reached the bridge.

That confession could wait. Everything could wait, except the moon.

They went over the wall together, and ran.
