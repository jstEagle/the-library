## 18. The Broken Bridge

They smelled the border before they saw it.

Wendell had thought she knew every scent the land could make — cut hay, river mud, woodsmoke, the green rot of a compost heap in high summer. But this was none of those. This was something older and stranger: ozone and honey and cold stone, as if a storm had married a beehive inside a mountain. It grew stronger with each mile of the last day's walking, until Isandro stopped answering her questions entirely, his head tilted like a hound's, listening to something beneath the world.

"It remembers us," he said at last, when she pressed him. "The border. Even broken, it remembers what it was."

They crested a ridge as the sun went down behind them, and there it was.

Wendell had imagined many things on the long road from the drowned chapel — a wall of thorns, a curtain of fire, a door standing alone in a field. She had not imagined this.

A bridge of light.

It arched out of the earth before them like the rib of some vast buried creature, translucent and pale gold, wide enough for six to walk abreast, rising in a shallow curve toward a horizon that was not quite a horizon. Where it met the ground, the light sank into the soil and vanished, and the grass around its roots grew in perfect concentric rings, each ring a different shade of green, as though the seasons themselves had been combed outward from it. And then, thirteen paces up the arch — she counted without meaning to; her hands had always counted growing things — the bridge simply *stopped*.

The break was clean. Not shattered, not crumbled. Cut. The severed end hung in the air like a candle flame frozen mid-flicker, shedding slow motes of gold that drifted down and never touched the ground. Beyond it, where the bridge should have continued into the far country, there was only air, and beyond the air a darkness that was not night — a darkness with texture to it, deep and breathing, like looking down a well that looked back.

"That's your realm," Wendell said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"The edge of it." Isandro stood beside her, and the bark-scarring along his jaw caught the bridge-light and gleamed like polished walnut. "What you're seeing is the wound. When the covenant broke, everything past the cut became *elsewhere*. Not gone. Elsewhere." He paused. "It is very difficult to explain elsewhere to someone who has never fallen into it."

"Have you?"

"Once. Briefly." His hand drifted to his side, where the scarring ran deepest. "I would not recommend it."

She should have been afraid. Some part of her noted, distantly, that she ought to be — that any sensible person confronted with a severed bridge into another world would feel fear first and everything else after. But what rose in her instead was grief, sudden and specific, the way one grieves a garden blighted overnight. She knew this shape. She had known it before, though she could not have said when or how. The curve of the arch felt familiar under her eyes the way a stair feels familiar under a foot in the dark.

"I've seen it," she whispered. "Haven't I. Before."

Isandro turned to look at her. In the bridge-light his eyes were the color of new leaves held up against the sun. "You were seven," he said gently. "Your parents brought you to the border once, in spring. You picked flowers on the near slope while they spoke with someone on the far side. You don't remember because—" He stopped himself, and Wendell saw the words he swallowed go down hard. "Because thirteen years is a long time," he finished.

But that wasn't what he had been about to say. She was learning to read the places where he edited himself, the small hesitations like weeds marking where something had been pulled out by the root. There were more of them now than there had been in the beginning. Whatever he was not telling her, it was getting heavier to carry.

*Later*, she told herself, and did not believe herself.

They made camp in the lee of the ridge rather than approach the bridge in the dark. Isandro built no fire — "the border sees heat the way an eye sees movement" — so they ate cold bread and harder cheese, and Wendell sat wrapped in her cloak watching the severed bridge glow against the dark like a sentence interrupted mid-word.

The second keystone lay in her lap. They had carried it from the queen's conservatory wrapped in three layers of cloth, and even through all three she could feel it: a low pulse, patient as a seed waiting out winter. Since the palace — since the woman with her mother's hands who did not know her own daughter's name — the stone had felt different against her skin. Warmer. Attentive. As if it, too, had seen what she had seen, and was waiting for her to be ready to look at it again.

"You should sleep," Isandro said.

"So should you."

"I'll take first watch."

"You always say that. You never sleep." She turned the keystone over in her hands. Through the cloth, its pulse quickened faintly, like a heartbeat answering a touch. "Isandro. What happens tomorrow? Truly. Not the version where you protect me from the worst of it. The true one."

He was quiet for a long moment. The motes of gold drifting from the broken bridge caught in his hair and stayed there, tiny constellations.

"Tomorrow we learn whether the keystones can do what I hope they can do," he said. "And then we cross, or we don't."

"And if we don't?"

"Then the moon completes its thirteenth turn, and the realms seal forever, and everything on both sides of that cut begins to die. Slowly here. Quickly there." He said it flatly, the way a physician says the name of a disease. "The valley's magiclessness was only ever the smallest symptom. The sickness spreads outward from the wound. You've seen the Road of Ash. That is year thirteen. Imagine year thirty."

She had seen the Road of Ash. She carried it behind her eyelids: the wheat that remembered being forest and grew in the shapes of oaks, the river running backward with fish in its mouth gasping for water they'd already passed. Villagers with torches and frightened eyes, blaming people like her for a breaking they hadn't caused and couldn't name.

"Sleep," Isandro said again, softer. "Tomorrow will ask more of you than today did."

She slept, fitfully, and dreamed of a garden she had never planted, full of flowers whose names she almost knew.

* * *

Dawn at the border did not arrive so much as seep. Light gathered in the bridge itself first, running along the arch from root to broken end like sap rising, and only then spilled outward across the land. By the time the sun cleared the eastern hills, the whole structure burned pale and steady, and the darkness beyond the cut had receded to a bruise-colored shimmer.

Wendell walked the last stretch alone.

Not by design — Isandro had stopped at the outermost ring of grass, some twenty paces back, and when she glanced at him he shook his head once. "It will know me," was all he said. "Let it meet you first."

So she crossed the perfect rings of grass alone, feeling them change under her boots — each ring a different temperature, a different season's ghost — until she stood at the bridge's foot and laid her palm against the light.

It was warm. Warm as sun-warmed stone, warm as a living thing, and it pulsed once beneath her hand, and then again, quicker, and Wendell felt something answer in her chest — the same answering she had felt the first time she touched a keystone in her garden, the recognition she could not explain, except that now it was not a spark but a floodgate trembling on its hinges.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you're *hurt*."

She had not meant to say it aloud. But it was true, and the truth of it went through her like a blade through wet earth. The bridge was alive — had always been alive, a grown thing, a *planted* thing, the greatest work of gardening ever done by human or other hands — and it had been cut, and it had lain here bleeding light for thirteen years, and no one had come.

Her gift rose before she called it. She felt it move down her arm and into her palm, that faint shimmer she had spent a lifetime hiding, and where it met the bridge the light flared — flared and *reached*, curling up over her wrist like a vine finding a trellis, and for one dizzying heartbeat Wendell felt the whole arch shudder from root to broken end, felt it strain toward her the way a starving plant strains toward a window—

"Wendell." Isandro's hand closed on her shoulder, drawing her back. "Gently. It knows what you are. If you offer it hope you cannot yet deliver, you will hurt it worse."

She stepped back, shaking. Her palm glowed faintly where the bridge had touched it, the mark fading slowly, reluctantly, like a handprint in frost.

"It's in pain," she said. "All this time. Thirteen years, and it's been lying here in pain, and everyone just—"

"Yes." Isandro's voice was quiet. "That is what breaking a covenant means. Not a law ending. A living thing wounded." He looked up at the arch, and Wendell saw — truly saw, for the first time — how old the grief in his face was. How long he had carried it. "Now you understand why the keystones matter. And perhaps you are ready to understand what they will show you."

He drew the second keystone from his pack. Together they climbed to the place where the bridge ended — the clean-cut edge, the frozen flame, the darkness breathing beyond — and knelt there, and set the stone upon the light.

The keystone sang.

There was no other word for it. A single note, low and clear, that Wendell felt in her teeth and her sternum and the roots of her hair, and the note spread outward through the bridge the way dye spreads through water, and the light began to *move* — gathering, thickening, rising off the arch in slow golden ribbons that wove themselves into shapes above their heads.

"Watch," Isandro murmured. "And whatever you see — remember that memory is a country too, and this is its border."

The ribbons of light became a scene.

* * *

She saw a valley — her valley, but younger, wilder, the river running bright and unbroken, forests crowding close to the fields instead of retreating from them. She saw the two realms not as two but as one continuous country, human villages and other-country glades threaded together like warp and weft, and folk moving between them freely: tall figures with bark-dark skin and leaf-green eyes walking openly among farmers, children of both kinds chasing each other through orchards that bloomed in two worlds at once.

And she saw the making.

A circle of gardeners — human, robed in grey-green, their faces worn and serious — kneeling in a ring with tall figures of the other realm, and between the two companies a bed of bare earth. Into that earth they placed four stones, and Wendell's breath caught, because she knew those stones, had carried two of them across half a kingdom, had bled for one in a drowned chapel. The keystones. Planted, not set. Buried like seeds.

And then the gardeners spoke.

Their voices rose together, old words in a rhythm like planting-song, and the vision gave her the words whole, translated by some grace of the stone, and Wendell heard the covenant spoken for the first time:

*Two realms, one root. What one suffers, both sustain. What one spends, both repay. Let the bridge grow from shared ground, and let it be tended, generation unto generation, by the guild of the gardeners — and let the tending have its price.*

The light-figures in the ring bowed their heads.

*The price,* said the eldest gardener, a woman with white braids and hands like knotted tree-roots, and Wendell leaned forward despite herself, her heart hammering, because here it was at last, the thing Isandro would not say, the thing her grandmother had forbidden her to ask —

*The covenant lives on memory. Memory is its soil. And so the guild shall pay in memory: each keeper of the bridge, at the end of their service, shall give up what they hold most dear — every memory bound to their deepest love — that the bridge may drink and live.*

The vision turned, and showed her what that meant.

She saw a gardener — the same white-braided woman, decades older still — standing at the bridge's foot with her family around her, and Wendell watched the woman's face as the price was taken: watched her look at her husband, her children, her grandchildren, with love entire and unhidden, knowing that when the light faded she would look at them again and see strangers. Watched her choose it anyway. Watched the bridge drink, and brighten, and the realms flourish another generation.

And the vision kept turning, showing her keeper after keeper across centuries — men and women giving up husbands, wives, children, mothers, the faces of their valleys, the sound of particular laughter — each one choosing, each one weeping or serene, each one forgotten by their own hearts within the space of a breath. Generation unto generation. Four hundred years of it. A guild built out of people who had loved something so much they were willing to have it taken from them, so that everyone else could keep what they loved.

The golden figures dimmed. The vision narrowed, funneled, focused — and Wendell felt the keystone shift beneath her hands, felt it turn toward her like an eye.

It showed her a night thirteen years ago. Rain. Her parents' kitchen, the one she remembered from fragments, lamplit, steam on the windows. Her mother's face — the face she had found again in the palace, hollowed and blank, but here whole and fierce and afraid. Her father packing a bag with hands that shook. Her grandmother at the door, blocking it, saying words the vision let Wendell hear as if she stood in the room:

*"There has to be another way. She's seven years old, Maren. If the price falls to the heir—"* 

*"It won't fall to her," her mother said. "We'll pay it ourselves. We'll pay it twice over if we must. Keep her from the bridge, Mother. Keep her from the bridge until it's done."*

And the vision followed them — Wendell could not stop it, could not look away, her fingers locked on the singing stone — out into the rain, up the slope, to the border, to the bridge, where her mother and father knelt where Wendell knelt now, and spoke the old words, and offered memories of their daughter, their home, their whole lives, freely given, twice the measure, to heal a wound that was already opening—

And the bridge refused them.

Wendell saw it happen. Saw the light rise and reach for their offering and draw back, shudder, recoil — saw the arch begin to crack even as her parents poured their love into it — heard, in the vision's terrible silence, a voice that was not a voice speak through the stone itself:

*The price must be paid by the heir. Freely, or not at all. There is no substitute. There never was.*

Her father shouting. Her mother turning back toward the valley, toward the house, toward the sleeping child — and the crack widening, the light failing, the darkness beyond the cut leaning in like a tide — and her parents running, not away but *toward*, toward the failing bridge, hands joined, pouring the last of themselves into the breach the way one packs a wound — and the light swallowing them, and the cut completing itself, clean as a blade-stroke, and the darkness closing over the place where they had been.

The vision ended.

The keystone went silent. The dawn was very quiet around it. Somewhere below, wind moved through the ringed grass, and the severed bridge shed its slow golden motes onto ground that would never receive them.

Wendell knelt without moving. She was aware, distantly, of Isandro beside her, of his hand hovering near hers and not landing, of the words he was not saying pressing against his teeth. Her body felt very far away, a tool she was holding rather than inhabiting.

"They didn't abandon anything," she said finally. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "They tried to pay it for me. And it killed them. And it didn't work." She looked down at her own hands, the gardener's hands, the heir's hands, the faint shimmer already stirring in them as if they understood their inheritance better than she did. "The price is mine. It was always going to be mine. My mother knew, and she died trying to stand between me and it, and the covenant wouldn't even let her do that."

"Wendell—"

"What does it take?" She turned and looked at him, and she watched him understand, in real time, that the sheltering was over — that she had walked through the vision and come out the other side asking the question directly, plainly, with no room left to plant mercy in. "From me. At the bridge. If we reforge it. What does the covenant take from *me*?"

The silence stretched. The bridge hummed its low wounded note. Far away, a bird began the day, indifferent.

"Everything you love most," Isandro said at last, barely above the wind. "Every memory bound to it. For you, Wendell — for the heir of the Thornbury guild, who loves the way your mother loved — that would mean the valley. The garden. Your grandmother. The road we've walked. Every flower you've ever named." He closed his eyes. "It would mean me."

The words fell into the morning like stones into a well, and Wendell listened for them to land, and waited, and waited, and heard nothing — because there was no bottom. There was only the drop.

She rose to her feet. The bridge-light moved on her face, warm and grieving, and beyond the cut the darkness breathed, patient as winter.

"When," she said, "does the moon turn?"
