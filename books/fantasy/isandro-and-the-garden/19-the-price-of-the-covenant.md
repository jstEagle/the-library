## 19. The Price of the Covenant

The bridge had spoken, and now Isandro could not stop hearing it.

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, half a mile back from the border, because neither of them could bear to look at the severed span any longer than they had to. The vision still clung to the air around the fire like smoke — the four keystones rising from the earth at the world's first morning, the voices of two realms speaking one vow, and beneath all of it, quiet as a stone dropped into deep water, the price.

*What is joined by memory must be paid for in memory.*

He had known it would come to this. He had known since the garden — since the moment he opened his eyes in a valley where magic could not follow and saw a woman kneeling in the dirt with her hands buried to the wrist in soil, weeping over a stranger she did not know, and understood in one sickening instant what she was and what she would have to lose. He had told himself a hundred lies since then. That there would be another way. That the covenant might accept a lesser offering. That he would find the words, eventually, some gentle phrasing that would make an unbearable thing bearable.

There were no such words. There never had been. And now the bridge had said it plainly, in the old tongue, in front of her, and the hour of lying was over.

Wendell sat across the fire from him with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, staring into the flames. She had not spoken since they left the bridge. The second keystone lay between them on a bedroll, wrapped in cloth, and even through the wrapping Isandro could feel it humming — a low, patient vibration, like a held breath. It knew what it had shown her. It was waiting to see what she would do with it.

"Wendell." His voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "We need to talk about what the bridge showed."

"I know what it showed." She did not look up. The firelight moved across her face and found no expression there to hold onto. "It showed a riddle dressed up as a promise. *Paid for in memory.* Whatever that means, I'm sure you've already decided what it means, so you may as well say it."

He had rehearsed this conversation for thirteen years' worth of nights compressed into a few weeks of road, and every rehearsal had failed him. Now, with her eyes finally lifting to his — wary, tired, and underneath the wariness and the tiredness something that had begun, against both their better judgments, to look like trust — every word he had prepared turned to ash in his mouth.

So he said it plainly, the way he should have said it in the garden, the way he should have said it on the Road of Ash, the way he should have said it a dozen times when cowardice wore the mask of kindness.

"The covenant cannot be reforged without a sacrifice. Not blood. Not gold. Not anything the crown or the guilds ever imagined it wanted." He made himself hold her gaze. "Memory. The heir who speaks the vow gives up everything bound to what she loves most. Every memory tied to it — torn out whole, root and branch, the way a tree comes up when you pull hard enough. Not just the person. Everything connected to them. Every place, every season, every small moment that carries their shape inside it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark beyond the tower, a nightbird called and got no answer.

"And for me," Wendell said slowly, "that would be—"

"You," Isandro finished, before she could make him say it, because making her say it would be cruelty and he had done enough of that already. "Your memories of me. Of this journey. Of—" His voice caught, and he hated it, and pushed through anyway. "Of everything since the night I fell into your garden. And more than that. Memory does not respect boundaries, Wendell. It grows the way your gift grows — along paths, through roots, touching things you didn't know were connected. The valley itself. The magic coming back into your hands. The guild. Your parents, if the covenant's breaking is braided through those memories, and it will be, because everything about you is braided through it." He heard his own voice go flat and careful, the voice of a man reading a sentence he wished did not exist. "You would not forget a man named Isandro. You would forget why flowers lean toward you. You would forget why certain songs hurt. You would stand in a garden full of plants you somehow know how to tend, and you would not know why your hands were crying."

Silence. Long enough that the fire burned down a finger's width, long enough that the keystone's hum seemed to swell to fill it.

Then Wendell laughed. It was not a kind sound.

"Since the garden," she repeated. "That's what you said. *Since the garden.*" She unfolded herself and stood, and the firelight fell away from her face, leaving only her outline against the dark ruins. "How long? How long exactly, Isandro? Since the first night, when you woke up and looked at me like you were already grieving me? Since the tunnels, when you told me to run and wouldn't say why? Since the chapel, when the wraith showed me my parents and you pulled me away before it could show me more?" Her voice rose with each question, cracking on the last one. "Every time I asked you what the covenant cost, every time I asked what my mother's message meant, every time I asked why you flinched when I talked about the future — you knew. You stood there and let me walk toward this with my eyes open, and yours shut."

"Yes." He stayed on his knees by the fire because standing felt like something he had no right to do. "I knew."

"Why?"

And there it was — the true question, the one under all the others, and he owed her the truth of it even though the truth was ugly.

"Because I loved you." The words came out of him like something pulled with forceps. "Because from the first week I loved you, and I am a guardian of the old covenant, and I have watched what love does to vows. My predecessor broke the covenant rather than pay its price, and the breaking killed the valley's magic and put your parents in the ground or somewhere worse, and I swore I would not repeat that. But knowing a trap and not stepping in it are different arts, Wendell. I thought — I told myself — that if I could get us to the bridge, if I could buy enough time, I would find a third path. A mercy. Some clause, some loophole, some forgotten verse in the old law that would let the realms be saved and let you keep yourself." He shook his head. "There is no third path. I searched for one the entire road, and the bridge just told us both what I already knew: the covenant is not cruel, but it is exact. It takes what it takes. And I let you love me — I let myself love you — while carrying the knowledge that the end of our road was your forgetting."

She was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had gone very soft, which was worse than shouting.

"The worst part," she said, "is that I understand. I understand why you didn't tell me. If someone told me on the first day that loving this man meant losing him completely, I'd have built walls so fast and so high that nothing would ever have gotten in. And then the covenant would have broken, and everyone would have died, and it would have been tidy and safe and wrong." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I understand, Isandro. That's what makes it unbearable. You took the choice away from me for weeks because you were afraid of what I'd choose, and you were right to be afraid, and I hate you for it, and I hate that I hate you for it."

"I know."

"Do you?" She dropped her hand and looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and heartbroken all at once, and he thought he had never seen anything so beautiful or caused anything so wounded. "Do you know what it's like to find out that the person you — that the person—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, more dangerous. "My whole life, people decided things for me. The guild hid my gift. My grandmother forbade my questions. The crown declared what I was worth. And I told myself: at least with him it's different. At least with him I'm choosing. Every mile of this road, I chose. And now I learn that the road was paved with your silence, and my choosing was a story you let me tell myself so I'd keep walking."

"That's not—" He stopped himself. Because part of it was true, and she deserved the true part along with the defense. "Some of it is. I won't insult you by denying it. But hear this much, Wendell, because it's the only clean thing I have left: I never once tried to turn you from this road. I never lied to steer you. I withheld the ending, but I never touched the walking. Every step toward the keystones was yours. If I had wanted to save you from this, truly, I had a hundred chances to lead you somewhere safe and let the realms tear apart. I couldn't do it. I couldn't choose you over the world, and I couldn't choose the world over you, and so I did the coward's arithmetic and carried the secret and hoped the road would solve what I couldn't."

"And the road solved it by showing me the bill." She laughed again, that terrible laugh. "Thirteen years ago my parents paid this price. Did you know that? Did the bridge show you that too, or did you already carry it like you carried everything else?"

The question landed like a blade between ribs. "I suspected. The timing of the breaking, the failure of the last reforging — yes. I suspected they had attempted it."

"They gave up everything," she whispered. "Everything tied to what they loved most. Which was each other, and which was me. They forgot their daughter, Isandro. They tore me out of themselves root and branch, and it *failed*, and they don't even know what they lost." Her breath shuddered. "And you watched me grieve them my whole life and said nothing. You let me believe they abandoned us. You let me build my whole self on top of a lie you were keeping warm in your pocket."

"I let you keep the lie that kept you standing," he said quietly. "Yes. I did. And I would do it again, and I am not proud of it, and I am done pretending otherwise."

She stared at him. The fire had burned low; the shadows had come up around them like water. Between them the keystone hummed its patient note, indifferent as the moon.

"So what happens now?" she asked. "Two turns left. Four keystones — well, two, and two to find. A queen with a machine. An army at our backs. And the heir to the covenant has just learned that saving everything costs her the only thing she—" She stopped again, jaw tightening. "What's your plan, guardian? What's the next step on the road you paved?"

"My plan," Isandro said, "is whatever you choose. That has always been the truth of it, even when I was too much a coward to say so. The covenant needs its heir willing. Not coerced, not deceived at the threshold, not dragged. Willing. If you refuse, the realms sever, and the world ends slowly instead of quickly, and I will spend whatever remains of my life beside you in the wreckage, because I have learned my predecessor's lesson too late to be noble about it. If you accept—" His voice finally broke, and he let it. "If you accept, then I will hold your hand while it takes you, and I will remember everything alone, for both of us, until I die. Those are the roads. I built neither of them. But I will walk whichever one you walk."

For a moment he thought she would speak. Her mouth opened; her hands unclenched at her sides. The firelight caught the tears on her cheeks and made them look like something growing, silver and quick, the way dew looks on a spiderweb at dawn.

Then she turned and walked into the dark.

Not running. Walking — steady, deliberate, away from the fire and the tower and him, out past the ring of light into the black country beyond, where the grass whispered and the border-bridge glimmered faintly on the horizon like a scar that hadn't decided whether to heal. He started to rise. He forced himself to stop. Chasing her now would be one more decision made for her, one more mile of the road he had already paved too much of.

"Wendell," he said to the dark, not loudly. "Come back when you're ready. I'll be here. However long it takes. I'm good at waiting — it's the only vow I've ever kept properly."

The dark did not answer. The nightbird called again, and this time something far off answered it, or perhaps it was only wind through the ruined tower, or perhaps it was the keystone humming its held breath, waiting, always waiting, for the heir to decide what she was willing to become.

Isandro sat down alone by the dying fire and did not sleep. He watched the spot where she had vanished until the stars wheeled and the sky went grey, and in the coldest hour before dawn he permitted himself, for the first time since the garden, to imagine the world after — the world where she came back and said yes, and forgot him, and lived. He imagined her hands in soil. He imagined flowers leaning toward her for reasons she would never know. He imagined telling her, someday, some small thing — a joke, a kindness, the color of a river at dusk — and watching it land on her like rain on glass.

And then, because hope is its own kind of cruelty, he imagined the other world too: the one where she did not come back at all.

When the sun rose, the bedroll where she had slept was empty, the seed-packet her grandmother gave her was gone from her pack, and the second keystone still lay wrapped by the ashes, humming — but the footprints in the frost led away from the bridge, south, back the way they had come, alone.

* * *

He found the trail easy to follow and harder to bear. She walked fast and did not hide it; she wanted him to follow, or she wanted him to see that she hadn't waited to be followed, and he could not tell which, and maybe neither could she. By midmorning he caught sight of her on a ridge ahead, a straight-backed figure moving through the bracken with the wind pulling at her hair, and he did not call out. He simply walked behind her at a distance that said *I am here* and nothing else, the way you walk behind a deer you have wounded and do not wish to startle, though the wound in this case was his own making and the deer had every right to gore him.

At noon she stopped at a stream and drank, and he stopped too, fifty paces back, and they existed in that arrangement for a quarter of an hour: two people drinking from the same water, saying nothing, the silence between them louder than the hunt had ever been.

It was she who finally broke it, without turning around.

"How much?" she said. Her voice carried flat across the water. "Exactly how much? Not the poetry version. The ledger. If I say the vow, what is left of me afterward?"

Isandro closed the distance slowly, stopping at the stream's edge a stone's throw from her. "You keep your name. Your skills — the guild taught your body, and the body keeps what the mind loses. You keep your grandmother, if she lives; she is not bound to what you love most, only what you do. You keep Rell, though you may not know why his face makes you sad. You keep language, craft, the garden in your hands." He swallowed. "You lose me. All of me. And you lose the valley's magic — the shimmer, the gift, the sense of the green world listening. Because the magic came to you through the covenant, and the covenant will take back what it lent. You would be a gardener again. Only a gardener. The finest in the valley, and never know why the work felt like prayer."

"Only a gardener," she echoed. Something moved through her face — grief, temptation, disgust at the temptation, he could not read which. "That used to be everything I wanted. Before you fell out of the sky into my beans."

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned at last, and her eyes were dry now, scoured clean, terrible in their calm. "Because here is what I keep circling, Isandro. My parents paid this price and it *failed*. The bridge said the covenant demands memory — but it didn't say whose, or whether one heir paying alone is even enough anymore. The covenant broke once with the full price paid. Why would it hold the second time? Unless—" She stopped. He watched the thought move through her like weather. "Unless there's something the bridge didn't show. Something my mother sealed in my head, maybe. Something I haven't chosen to look at yet."

His heart turned over. "Wendell—"

"Don't." One word, quiet, and it stopped him colder than any shout. "You've spent weeks deciding what I can bear to know. You're done. From here on, guardian, you wait outside doors like everyone else." She picked up her pack. "I'm going to look. Tonight, alone, with the keystone. And whatever my mother left me — whatever the real price is, or the real way, or the real reason my parents died for nothing — I'll decide what to do with it myself. You can walk behind me if you want. Fifty paces. Like a man following a debt."

She crossed the stream on the stones, not looking back, and climbed the far bank into the afternoon light, and Isandro stood in the cold water-shadow of her absence and understood that he had finally, fully, lost the right to protect her — and that this, too, was a kind of love, the kind that hurts the protector more than the protected, and that he would carry it the rest of his life whichever world survived.

He followed her at fifty paces, as instructed, into the dark that was coming.
