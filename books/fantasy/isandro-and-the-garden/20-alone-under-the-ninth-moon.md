## 20. Alone Under the Ninth Moon

The fire was small, and Wendell built it small on purpose.

She had learned that much from Isandro in their weeks on the road — that a fire's size told a story to anyone reading it from a distance. A big fire said *here I am, come and find me*. A small one said *a traveler, nothing more, passing through*. And no fire at all said *I am hiding, and hiding is a confession*.

So she fed the flames just enough deadfall to keep them breathing, and she sat with her knees drawn up against her chest, and she let herself be furious.

It was a clean fury, at first. That was the strange mercy of it. For years her anger had been a damp thing, smoke without flame — anger at the hunters, at the crown, at the whispering villagers of the poisoned road, at a world that had decided people like her were contraband. But this fury had a single name on it, and the name was *Isandro*, and it burned hot and bright and almost pleasantly, the way a fever burns before it breaks.

*Liar.* The word turned over in her mind like a stone in a streambed, worn smooth with handling. He had known. Since the garden — he had said as much, standing there in the dark with his bark-scarred hands open and empty, confessing it the way a man confesses to a murder he committed long ago and has been carrying ever since. He had known since the garden what the covenant would ask of her, and he had walked beside her anyway, day after day, mile after mile, letting her believe the road led somewhere she could survive intact.

No. That wasn't fair, and she knew it wasn't fair even as she thought it, because fairness had never once been on offer in any of this. He hadn't lied, exactly. He had *withheld*, which was its own species of lie but a quieter one, and he had withheld it because telling her would have meant losing her — and he had been right, hadn't he? She had walked away from him into the dark. She was sitting alone under the ninth moon because he had told her the truth.

But he had waited until the truth could do the most damage.

Wendell pulled her cloak tighter and glared at the fire as though it were responsible. Above her, through a ragged ceiling of pine branches, the ninth moon rode high and thin, one turn past halfway, four turns from sealing. Four turns. Thirteen years of silence, thirteen years of a broken covenant bleeding poison into two worlds, and now everything came down to four turns of a moon and a choice she did not know how to make.

*All memories tied to what she loves most.*

That was the price. Not death, not blood, not anything so simple as the old stories promised. Memory. Every memory bound up in the thing she loved best in all the world — and when she had stood at the broken bridge and understood what that meant, she had understood it all at once, the way ice understands it is going to crack: not gradually, but everywhere at once.

Because the thing she loved most was not a thing. It was not the guild garden, though she loved it with the whole root-system of her life. It was not even the valley itself. It was the journey — the drowned chapel where she had first held a keystone and heard it hum like a struck bell; the market of small magics where an old woman had pressed her thumb to the guild sigil and wept; the conservatory full of caged light; the marsh where she had found Isandro hollowed out and nameless and had rebuilt him memory by memory, story by story, *Isandro, your name is Isandro, you are the guardian of the covenant, you carried me across the flooded ford when my strength gave out* —

It was him. It had been him for longer than she had allowed herself to know, and the covenant wanted every scrap of it. It wanted the garden gate and the seed-packet and the way he said her name like it was a word in a language he'd been trying to remember. It wanted all of it, and it would leave her standing whole and healthy and alive in a reforged world, tending flowers she'd have no reason to love, beside a stranger she'd have no reason to trust.

And he had known. Since the garden.

"Damn you," she said quietly to the fire, and the fire, being only fire, did not answer.

But something else did.

The second keystone lay wrapped in cloth at the bottom of her pack, and even through the wool and the leather she felt it stir — a hum, low and patient, like a hive waking in spring. She had felt it humming for hours, she realized. All through her fury it had been humming, gently, insistently, the way a hand might tap her shoulder. And she had ignored it, because she was angry, and because the last time a keystone had shown her something true, it had shown her a woman with her mother's hands behind glass, and she still had not recovered from that.

The hum deepened. Grew warmer. Almost — she searched for the word and found it reluctantly — almost *kind*.

"You can't have him," she said aloud, to a stone. "You can't have any of it."

The hum did not change. It simply waited, the way her grandmother used to wait when Wendell was small and stubborn, arms folded, certain that the child would come around eventually because the truth always won in the end. Grandmother had waited like that over seedlings too. *You can't argue a plant into growing, Wendell. You can only give it what it needs and step back.*

Wendell sat for a long time, listening to the fire tick and settle, listening to the wind move through the pines with that faint wrongness the broken land had taught her to hear — a half-beat of magic underneath, wild and unanchored, the sound of a realm bleeding. Then she unshouldered the pack and drew out the keystone and set it on the flat rock beside the fire, unwrapped.

It caught the moonlight and did something moonlight shouldn't do. It held it. The pale glow pooled in the stone's depths like water in a cup, and within that glow, shapes began to move.

"I already saw what you showed me," she said. "At the bridge. I know the price." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "I don't need a lesson. I need — "

She stopped. Because she didn't know what she needed. That was the terrible, naked truth beneath the fury: she had walked away from Isandro with no plan at all, only the animal certainty that she could not stand beside him one more hour while carrying what he'd given her. She had walked away, and now she was alone in the dark with a stone and a fire and four turns of the moon, and fury, she was discovering, made a very poor tent. It kept out nothing.

The keystone's glow brightened. And in it, unmistakably, deliberately, she saw faces.

Her mother's. Her father's.

The fury went out of her all at once, the way a candle goes out — not gradually but completely, leaving a darkness that rang.

"No," she whispered. But her hands didn't move. Her hands stayed where they were, cupped loosely around the stone's warmth, and the vision opened like a gate.

* * *

She had seen fragments before. In the drowned chapel, the memory-wraith had worn her parents' faces like borrowed clothing, and she had fled with the first keystone clutched to her chest and a splinter of a night lodged in her mind — rain, a door, voices raised in a language of urgency. At the bridge, the second keystone had shown her the covenant's making, and the price spoken plainly, and she had understood then that her parents' disappearance and the covenant's breaking were the same event wearing two names.

Now she saw all of it. Now the keystone gave her the whole night, unbroken, and she understood that this was why it had hummed at her all evening, patient as planted seed: not to torment her, but because grief left untended goes septic, and someone — something — had decided she had suffered long enough in ignorance.

Thirteen years ago. Rain. The guild house with its lamps lit, the walled garden silvered and streaming, and inside it her parents standing before the four keystones arranged in a square on the flagstones, glowing like captive moons.

They had gone willingly. That was the first thing the vision gave her, and it went through her like a blade finding the seam of a shield. No hunters at the door, no crown decree, no flight in the night. They had *chosen*. Her father had knelt and kissed the top of her head — she was seven, asleep upstairs, or supposed to be — and her mother had smoothed his hair the way she smoothed soil around a new transplant, firm and tender at once, and they had spoken the words together, the old words, the ones Wendell had heard at the bridge and would carry to her grave:

*Two who choose the price with open eyes.*

Two. Not one. The covenant of old had never demanded a single sacrifice; it had asked for a pair, bound and willing, so that what was given was shared and therefore survivable. Her parents had offered themselves together, both of them, all their memories of the other realm and of each other's magic — and Wendell watched, helpless inside the vision, as the keystones drank from them, as the bridge of light between the realms flickered and strained and *held* —

And then broke anyway.

She saw the reason. She saw it the way the keystones had recorded it, impartial as rain: a flaw in the offering. Her mother had held something back. One memory, one small green thing, hidden deep where the covenant's hunger could not reach — a memory of her daughter, of a seven-year-old girl asleep in a bed above the garden, of a promise made over a cradle before Wendell could even hold up her head. *She will know what she is. Whatever it costs, whoever tries to keep it from her — she will know.*

The covenant, cheated of its full price, had failed. The bridge had torn. The valley's magic had died in a single night, and the backlash — the vision showed her this too, showed her the raw wound of it — the backlash had taken her parents' memories of *everything*, not just what they'd offered. They had wandered out of the ruined garden at dawn as strangers to themselves, and the first guardian's wraith had found them, or they had found it, and somewhere in the years since they had been gathered up by the crown's men with the rest of the gifted, and her mother was behind glass in the queen's conservatory right now, alive, empty, holding a stranger's memories in place of her own.

Wendell came back to herself on her hands and knees beside the fire, and she did not remember falling.

The pine forest swam. The ninth moon swam. She was crying, she realized distantly, the way you realize you've been shouting only after your throat hurts — great silent sobs that shook her without asking permission, and she let them, because there was no one to see and no one to be strong for, and because her mother had held back one memory to keep a promise to her, and the keeping of that promise had broken the world.

*It wasn't cowardice,* she thought. *It wasn't failure. It was love, spent in the wrong currency.* Her mother had tried to pay the covenant's price while withholding a single coin, believing love could be carved out of the offering and smuggled through — and the covenant had taken everything instead, and the realms had bled for thirteen years because of it.

And Isandro knew this. Of course he knew this. He was the covenant's guardian; the record of that night lived in his bones. He had looked at Wendell in the garden and seen the daughter of the two people whose sacrifice had failed, and he had said nothing about the price because — oh, and she saw it now, saw it with a clarity that hurt worse than the fury had — because he had been afraid of exactly this. Afraid that if she learned how her parents' offering had shattered, she would conclude that the covenant was unpayable, that the price was a trap, that love withheld or love surrendered both ended in ruin. He had protected her from the truth the way her mother had protected her from the memory, and both protections had been cages.

She knelt by the fire until the shaking stopped. Then, slowly, she wiped her face with her sleeve, and picked up the keystone, and spoke to it as she would speak to a plant that had been frost-bitten — gently, firmly, without false comfort.

"She hid it," Wendell said. "My mother hid a memory. In the offering. Where?"

The stone's glow shifted. And Wendell felt it — not saw, *felt* — a thread of attention turning, delicate as a root-tip seeking water, and it did not reach toward the stone at all.

It reached toward her.

Toward her head. Toward the space behind her eyes where she kept the guild sigil and the smell of rain on hot flagstones and the weight of her grandmother's hand on her hair. Toward thirteen years of ordinary remembered life — except that it had never been entirely ordinary, had it, because her gift had slept in her all those years like a seed in frozen ground, and seeds do not sleep alone.

"There's something in me," she breathed. "She put it in *me*."

The keystone hummed assent, warm and low, and the vision rose one last time — brief, fragmentary, a final page turned toward her: her mother in the ruined garden at dawn, memoryless of the realms but not yet emptied, kneeling in the wet grass with her hands pressed to her own temples, doing the last work of her life in secret and in haste. Sealing. Hiding. Planting, Wendell thought, and the word arrived with the rightness of a key turning: her mother had *planted* something in her daughter's mind, deep, deeper than dream, wrapped in wards of forgetting — a message, sealed not against discovery but against *timing*. It would stay dormant, invisible, harmless, until the moment its keeper chose to look. Until Wendell herself, with open eyes and deliberate will, reached for it and said: *show me.*

Her mother had left her a door. Hidden in the wall of her own remembering. Waiting thirteen years for her to find the handle.

Wendell sat back on her heels. The fire had burned down to a red heart of coals, and the ninth moon had crossed a hand's width of sky, and somewhere behind her eyes a sealed room stood waiting, and she understood — with the same bone-deep certainty she felt when her hands hovered over dying stock and *knew* what it needed — that once she opened it, there would be no closing it again. Some things, once seen, could not be unseen. Some doors, once opened, swung only inward.

If she looked, she would carry whatever her mother had left her for the rest of her life. If she looked, she might learn that the price her parents paid had been wasted, or worse than wasted. If she looked —

If she looked, she might learn there was another way.

The thought arrived quietly, without fanfare, the way the first green shows in a burned field: not proof, not promise, just possibility, pushing up through ash. Her mother had been a gardener of the Thornbury guild, and gardeners did not leave messages unless the messages mattered, and her mother had known the covenant's mechanics better than anyone alive. If there had been only the one price, only the one terrible arithmetic, her mother would have left her a warning. Mothers left warnings. But her mother had left a *message* — sealed, timed, deliberate — and messages were for instructions.

Wendell looked down at the keystone in her hands, and beyond it at the coals, and beyond them at the dark wall of the forest, and beyond the forest, miles south and east, at the place where a man who had lied to her by protecting her was probably sitting alone at his own small fire, feeding it just enough to say *traveler, nothing more*.

Four turns of the moon.

"Grief is a season," her grandmother used to say, deadheading the roses with brutal tenderness. "You don't get to skip it. But you don't get to live in it either."

Wendell closed her eyes. She found the door — it was easier than she feared, the way a path is easy to find once you know it exists, a faint track worn through the undergrowth of her own mind, leading down and inward to a place that smelled, impossibly, of the old guild garden in high summer. Behind it, something waited. Something patient. Something that had been waiting since she was seven years old, planted by hands that had loved her enough to break the world for her.

Her fingers, in the space behind her eyes, closed around the handle.

"Show me," she whispered — to the keystone, to the dark, to her mother. "I'm ready. Show me what you planted."

The door swung inward.

And her mother's voice — warm, urgent, familiar as rain — said her name.
