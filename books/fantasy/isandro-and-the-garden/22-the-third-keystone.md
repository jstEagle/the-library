## 22. The Third Keystone

The marsh did not announce itself. That was the first cruelty of it.

Isandro had expected a border — a line of dead reeds, a change in the wind, some sign that said *beyond here the world forgets*. Instead he simply walked out of the hills one grey morning and found the ground softening beneath his boots, and the air thickening like breath on glass, and only when he was already ankle-deep in black water did he understand where he had come.

The Marsh of Forgetting lay in a shallow bowl between two ridges of slate, and it was beautiful, which he had not expected either. Mist moved over it in slow ribbons, silvered by a sun that never quite broke through. The water was the color of old pewter, and it held no reflections — or rather, it held reflections that were almost right and then subtly wrong: his own face looking back at him with an expression he had never worn, a sky above him with too many stars for midday. Here and there, trees stood drowned to their shoulders, their crowns still green after what must have been centuries, because nothing in this place decayed. Nothing in this place *ended*. It simply drifted loose from everything that had ever anchored it.

He knew the stories. Every guardian of the covenant knew them. The third keystone had been set here deliberately, at the covenant's making, because the marsh was the one place in either realm where memory could be unmade without violence — where a thing could be taken gently, so gently the giver might never know it was gone. The founders had called it mercy. Isandro had always thought that word was doing terrible work.

He stood at the edge for a long time, water seeping cold through his boots, and let himself feel the shape of what he was about to do.

Wendell was gone.

Three days now since she had walked away from him into the dark below the broken bridge, her face lit by the second keystone's dying glow, her voice stripped of everything warm. *You knew.* Two words, and they had undone him more thoroughly than any blade. He had watched her go until the dark took her, and he had not followed, because following would have been worse than letting her go — because she had needed, rightly, to be alone with the truth of what he had hidden from her. He had known since the garden. Since the first night, nearly. He had known what the covenant asked of its heir, and he had let her walk toward it blind, telling himself there would be time, telling himself the truth could wait until she loved him enough to forgive it.

There had been no time. There was never time. And now the twelfth moon was rising somewhere behind the clouds, and one turn remained before the realms sealed forever, and the third keystone sat somewhere in this drowning silver country waiting for a hand brave enough or foolish enough to claim it.

He could not do it alone. He knew that too. The keystones answered to the heir — they hummed for her touch, woke for her gift, bent toward her the way all growing things bent toward her. His hands were guardians' hands; they could carry the stones, but they could not wake them. Without Wendell, the fourth keystone was beyond reach, the bridge beyond reach, everything beyond reach.

But she was not coming back. He had to accept that. She had every right not to come back. So he would take the third keystone if taking it was possible, and hold it, and wait at the bridge on the twelfth night in case she came anyway — and if she did not, then he would stand alone before four sleeping stones and fail, and the failure would be his to carry into whatever came after the sealing.

That was the plan. It was a thin plan, a plan made of pride and grief, and he saw that clearly even as he stepped off the last solid ground and waded into the mist.

* * *

The marsh tested him in small ways first.

A hundred paces in, he tried to remember the name of the woman who had taught him the guardian's oaths, and found a shape where the name should have been — a tall figure, a voice like dry leaves, hands that smelled of cedar — but no name. He stopped, heart hammering, and dug for it, and after a long moment it surfaced: *Maerlin*. Maerlin, who had made him recite the covenant's words until he spoke them in his sleep. He laughed shakily at his own fear and kept walking.

Two hundred paces further, he reached for the memory of his mother's garden in the realm of Isandro — the terraces above the white city, the night-blooming vines his mother had coaxed along the walls — and found the terraces, the walls, the vines, but the garden had no scent. He stood in the middle of a memory that had been hollowed out like a gourd, and this time the fear did not pass so quickly.

*It takes the edges first,* the old accounts said. *Color, scent, name. It saves the center for last, because the center is what you would fight hardest to keep, and the marsh is patient.*

The marsh was patient. He felt its patience everywhere — in the water holding his legs, in the mist drifting past without wind, in the drowned trees watching him with their patient green crowns. It was not hungry, exactly. Hunger wanted. The marsh simply waited, the way a river waits, and let things come apart on their own.

He tied a cord around his left wrist with nine knots in it, one for each year of his service as guardian, and made himself a rule: every hundred paces, he would stop and speak aloud three things he refused to lose. His name. His purpose. Her.

"Isandro," he said, standing in water to his knees, mist beading in his hair. "Guardian of the covenant. And Wendell Thornbury, who tends gardens, who laughs with her whole chest, who is angry with me and has the right of it."

The words went out into the silver air and did not echo. But they stayed in him, and he counted that a victory, and walked on.

* * *

The keystone revealed itself at dusk, though dusk in the marsh was hard to name — the light simply thinned until the world ran out of color. It stood on a low mound at the marsh's heart, a ring of higher ground around which the black water curled like something cradling it. The stone itself was perhaps as tall as his chest, roughly pillar-shaped, its surface moving. Not carved — *growing*, the way frost grows on glass, patterns blooming and dissolving across it faster than the eye could follow. Where the other keystones had hummed, this one was silent, and the silence around it was enormous, a silence with weight, pressing against his ears like deep water.

He climbed the mound on hands and knees, mud sucking at him, and knelt before the stone, and understood immediately why the founders had chosen this place.

The other keystones were locks. This one was a keyhole cut into forgetting itself. To wake it, the heir would have to open herself to the marsh — offer it the edges of her, the colors and scents and names, let it drink until the stone drank full. It was a test written in the covenant's oldest grammar: *what will you surrender to keep what you love?* The founders had built the question into the very land.

And he was not the heir. His hands on the stone would wake nothing. But the marsh did not care whose memories it drank, and the stone's silence pressed against him like an invitation, and he was tired — three days of walking on grief and guilt, three days of rehearsing apologies to a woman who was not there to hear them — and the marsh offered rest. Offered the softening of the sharp edges. Offered, for a little while, not to remember what he had done.

"No," he said aloud, and the word fell flat into the silence.

But he did not leave the mound. That was the mistake, and he made it knowingly, which was somehow worse. He told himself he would rest an hour, no more, and keep his knots counted, and speak his three things at every turning of the hour. He built a small fire on the mound's crown — the only ground in the marsh that would burn — and sat with his back against the keystone, because it was the driest surface and the least honest reason, and watched the mist close over the water like a lid.

He spoke his three things. *Isandro. Guardian. Wendell.*

An hour later he spoke them again, and noticed that when he said *guardian*, the word arrived slightly late, like thunder after lightning.

Another hour, and the fire had burned down to embers, and he realized he could no longer picture the white city. He knew — he *knew*, the way one knows a fact in a book — that he had been born in a city of white stone above a river, but the image would not come. He pressed his palms against his eyes and pushed, and got fragments: a stairway, a bell, the smell of bread. No streets. No gates. No city.

"It takes the edges first," he whispered, and heard how thin his voice had become.

He untied the cord and retied it, knot by knot, making his fingers work, making himself count. Nine knots. Nine years. He could not remember all nine years, but he could remember Maerlin, and the oath, and the bridge of light where he had first seen a human girl standing in a garden that magic could not enter, and —

The girl. The garden. Yes. Yes, that was the center, that was the thing he had sworn to keep. A garden in a valley where nothing shimmered, and a woman kneeling in the dark soil with her sleeves rolled up, and the way she had looked at him when he said her name — not Wendell, the other name, the old name, the name no one used, the name that had made her go pale and step back and look at him like a door opening onto a room she'd sealed —

The name would not come.

He said her name instead — *Wendell, Wendell* — and clung to it, and the marsh let him have it, because the marsh was patient, and it had already drunk the edges of everything else, and it could afford to wait.

* * *

Time went strange after that. He thought days passed, or hours; the mist gave nothing back. He ate what remained in his pack without tasting it. He spoke his three things until his throat was raw, and then two things, and then — he caught himself once, sitting upright against the stone in the grey half-light, saying only *Isandro, Isandro, Isandro*, like a man calling for help down a well, and did not know how long he had been saying it.

The stone drank through his back. He could feel it now, a slow pull at the base of his skull, gentle as sleep. And the terrible thing — the thing he would have wept over, if he could have remembered clearly enough what weeping was for — was that it did not hurt. Losing the white city had not hurt. Losing Maerlin's face had not hurt. Each memory went out of him the way warmth goes out of a cooling stone, so gradually that there was never a moment he could point to and say *there, that was the last of it*. The marsh kept its promise of mercy. It was killing him kindly, and he was too far gone to be afraid.

At some point he dreamed — or the marsh showed him something like a dream — of a garden under a walled sky, and a woman's hands in the earth, and he knew the hands loved him, and he could not find the face above them, and he woke with wet cheeks and no idea why.

"I'm sorry," he said to the hands, to the mist, to whoever might hear. "I'm sorry I waited."

The mist did not answer. The stone drank.

* * *

She came at what might have been morning.

He heard her before he saw her — boots in black water, unhurried, deliberate, the walk of someone who had learned to move through dangerous country by refusing to fear it. He turned his head, and the motion cost him something; his neck had grown stiff against the stone, as if roots had begun considering him.

A figure came up out of the mist onto the mound. Brown coat, travel-worn. Dark hair tied back with a strip of cloth. A sword at her hip worn like an apology rather than a promise. And her eyes — her eyes went to him first, then to the keystone, then back to him, and they were wide and wary and bright with a wariness that had grief underneath it, and he knew her.

He knew her the way the body knows. Before thought, before name. Something in his chest lurched toward her like a plant toward light, and he heard himself say, in a voice scraped raw, "I know you."

She stopped. The wariness sharpened. "Do you."

It was not a question that wanted an answer. It was a wall. And behind it — he could see it even through the fog in his mind — behind it was the anger, three days old and banked like a fire covered with ash, and the hurt, older than the anger, and under both of them something that had brought her here, across the poisoned road and the broken bridge's shadow and into the one place in creation she had every reason never to enter.

"You're the guardian," she said. Not a question either. "From the bridge. From—" Her jaw tightened. "You came to the valley. You carried the first keystone. You let me believe—" She stopped herself, breathed, started again, flatter. "I came for the stone. Not for you. Tell me how to take it, or get out of my way."

*I let her believe.* The phrase rang in him like a struck bell, and he understood distantly that it referred to something important, something that was the reason for the ash-covered fire in her eyes, but the shape of it slid away when he reached for it. He looked down at his own hands, muddy, trembling, and found that he could not remember why they were shaking.

"I can't tell you how to take it," he said slowly, feeling for each word like steps in dark water. "I think — I think it isn't mine to wake. I think it needs—" He frowned. The thought was there and then it wasn't, like a fish under ice. "Someone. Someone the stones answer to."

"They answer to me." She said it bitterly, and knelt beside the keystone, keeping half the mound between them, and laid her palm against its moving surface.

Nothing happened. The patterns bloomed and dissolved, indifferent. She pressed harder, and her gift rose in her — he felt it, a green pressure in the air, the drowned trees around the mound leaning infinitesimally toward her — and still the stone stayed silent, drinking, drinking, taking whatever she offered and giving nothing back.

"It's not empty," she said at last, quietly. "It's full. It's been fed." And then she turned and really looked at him, and he watched the understanding arrive in her face the way frost arrives on a window — spreading, inevitable, terrible. "How long have you been sitting here?"

"I don't know." Honest. It was the only coin he had left.

"How much have you lost?"

He tried to take inventory and the inventory came back in fragments. "A city. White stone. A teacher — Maerlin — no. Maerlin?" He said the name twice, testing whether it would stay. "Nine years. I had nine years of something. I have—" His hand closed around the cord at his wrist, nine knots, and he stared at it without recognition, and felt her see him do it, and felt, dimly, the shame of being seen.

"Wendell," he said suddenly, because it was the one thing left, the center, the thing he had fought for. "Your name is Wendell."

Something crossed her face — pain, and under the pain a fierce, unwilling tenderness. "Yes," she said. "And yours?"

He opened his mouth.

Nothing came.

He searched, and searched, and the place where his name had lived all his life was smooth and empty as river stone, and the marsh had taken it so gently he had never felt it go. He sat against the keystone that was drinking him and could not say who he was, and the woman he had crossed a world to find looked at him with tears standing in her eyes, and he did not know why she was crying, only that he wanted, more than he had ever wanted anything, to make it stop.

"I had one," he whispered. "I'm sorry. I had one."

"Yes," she said. Her voice cracked and steadied. "You did. It's Isandro." She said it carefully, like setting down something breakable. "Isandro. Guardian of the covenant. You've kept it for nine years. You came to a garden in a valley where magic couldn't follow, and you were dying, and I pulled you out of the dark, and you called me by a name no one uses anymore." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, angrily. "And you lied to me about the price. And I walked away. And then I sat under the ninth moon and I watched my parents die for this covenant, and I found what my mother left me, and I came back — not for you, do you understand? For the stone. Because the moon is turning and I am done letting other people pay for my sake."

He nodded, holding each fact as she gave it, the way a man bailing a boat holds water in his cupped hands. *Isandro. Nine years. A garden. A lie. A mother's message.* None of it was memory. All of it was hers, loaned to him, and he felt the strangeness of that — being a man assembled out of someone else's telling — and also, underneath the strangeness, a stubborn warmth. She had come back. Whatever she said, whatever the ash and the anger, she had come back.

"The stone," she said, and knelt again before it, and this time she did not touch it. She studied it, head tilted, and he saw the gardener come into her face — the patience, the attention, the habit of asking what a living thing wants rather than what it resists. "It's a marsh-keystone. It drinks. My mother's message said the price could be shared — willingly, by two, with open eyes." Her gaze flicked to him, and away. "Maybe this stone works the same way. Maybe it doesn't want to be *taken* at all. Maybe it wants to be *fed* — properly, by choice, instead of by theft." She looked at his wrist, at the nine-knot cord, at the hollowed man wearing it. "It's been feeding on you for days. That's why it won't wake for me. It's already full of you."

The words settled over him with a horror he could only half feel, the way one feels cold through numb skin. "Then take it," he said. "Wake it. Use what it took. I don't — the city doesn't matter, the years don't matter, none of it matters except—"

"Except what?" she asked, very quietly. "Say it. Say what matters. While you still can."

He reached for it, and for a moment the center held — a garden, a walled sky, hands in the earth, a name no one used — and then the mist shifted, and the stone drank, and it began to go. He felt it going. He grabbed for it the way a drowning man grabs light on the surface of water.

"Gardens," he said. "Growing things. And — and you. You matter. Wendell. You matter, you matter, you—"

Her hand closed over his mouth.

"Not like this," she said, and her voice was fierce enough to shake. "Not as a man the marsh emptied out. If the price is shared, then it's shared — do you hear me? Both of us, with open eyes, or not at all. I didn't crawl back into this cursed water to watch it eat you while I stand by and *remember for you*." She took her hand away. She was crying again, and furious about it, and magnificent, and he thought — with the last clear corner of his mind — *this is why. Whatever I forgot, this is why.*

"So here is what happens now," Wendell said. She sat down on the wet grass beside him, shoulder to shoulder against the silent, drinking stone, and pulled her pack around, and took out the seed-packet her grandmother had given her, and a stub of charcoal, and a scrap of paper. "We are going to sit here, and you are going to listen, and I am going to start at the beginning. A garden in a valley where magic cannot follow. A herald with a proclamation. A stranger at the gate." Her voice steadied as she went, gathering force like water finding its channel. "And you are going to hold on to every word, Isandro, because until we wake this stone, I am the only memory you have — and I swear by my guild and my mother's grave that I will not lose a single piece of you to this place."

She dipped the charcoal and began to write.

Behind them, softly, for the first time in centuries, the keystone began to hum.
