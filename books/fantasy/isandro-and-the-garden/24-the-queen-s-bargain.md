## 24. The Queen's Bargain

The rain had followed Rell for three days.

It came down in gray sheets over the hill country north of the palace, filling the wheel ruts of the old drover's road, soaking through his cloak until the wool hung heavy as chainmail. He did not mind it. Rain was honest. Rain did not pretend to be anything but what it was — cold, relentless, indifferent to rank and oath alike. He had spent eleven years learning to read weather instead of people, because weather never lied about what it wanted from him.

He rode with no banner, no escort, no seal. His captain's insignia sat in his saddlebag wrapped in oilcloth, and he had not looked at it since they took it from him. Not took it — he had set it on the quartermaster's table himself, both hands, like a man laying down a sword that had grown too heavy to lift. The tribunal had been brief. *Aiding the escape of prisoners of the crown. Abandoning your post during the severing preparations.* They had not asked him why. Perhaps they had been afraid to ask, or perhaps they already knew, and the knowing was the disgrace. Stripped of rank, stripped of the hunt, given a horse and one day's grace to be beyond the city walls before anyone decided whether a former Captain of the Hunt should simply disappear into the same cells he had once filled.

He had ridden north instead of east. North, toward the border country, toward the broken bridge, toward her.

Wendell Thornbury. The name had lived in his mouth for twenty years without ever quite being spoken aloud. It lived there now, under his tongue, where he kept everything that mattered: the memory of a girl of nine standing on a garden wall with mud to her elbows, daring him to climb; the memory of her grandmother's kitchen, bread and rosemary and the particular hush of a house that kept secrets; the memory of the day she left the valley — no, the day he watched hunters' torches eat the guild garden while she slipped away beneath his feet, and he stood in the smoke and let her go, and knew even then that letting go was only the first payment on a debt that would ruin him.

It had ruined him. He was free now, which was another way of saying he had nothing left to lose, and men with nothing left to lose made dangerous couriers.

* * *

He found their camp at dusk on the fourth day, in the lee of a fallen watchtower that the maps still marked as standing. He saw the fire first — small, shielded, disciplined — and then the horses, and then the shape of a man sitting apart from the flames with his face turned toward the rain, as if listening to it. Isandro. Even at this distance Rell could feel something wrong about him, something that made the hair on his arms lift: the air around the guardian seemed thicker, greener, as though the rain fell more reluctantly there.

And by the fire, hood back, hands outstretched to the heat — Wendell.

She had changed since the valley. That was the first thing he understood, before she ever saw him. The girl on the wall had been all sharp edges and stubbornness; the woman who turned at the sound of his horse moved differently, slower, as though she were carrying something enormous and had learned to walk around its weight. Her eyes found him through the rain and went wide, then narrow, then — worst of all — unreadable.

"Rell." She said it flatly, the way you name a storm coming over the ridge.

"Wendell." His voice cracked on it. Twenty years, and it cracked anyway.

Isandro rose without hurry. Rell felt the man take his measure across forty paces of falling water, and felt, absurdly, like a boy caught poaching. "You're alone," Isandro said. His voice carried strangely, unbothered by the rain.

"I'm alone." Rell dismounted slowly, showing empty hands, though his hands were never anything else these days. "No escort. No orders I'm bound to follow anymore. No rank worth the word."

"You were arrested." Wendell had come to the edge of the firelight. "In the palace. We saw them take you."

"I was arrested," Rell agreed. "I was also released. Which tells you something, if you think about it. The queen doesn't release traitors out of mercy. She releases them when she has a use for them."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, an owl called, and Rell noticed — filed away without understanding — that the owl's call seemed to bend slightly toward the camp, as if the night itself leaned in to listen.

"So you've come as her messenger." Wendell's voice was steady. Her hands were not; he saw them curl closed at her sides. "After everything. After you broke a machine core with your bare hands and laughed while they dragged you out—"

"I didn't laugh."

"I heard you laugh, Rell. Through three locked doors."

He had laughed. He remembered it — the strange lightness of it, the moment when the choice stopped being a choice and became simply the last true thing left to do. Breaking the machine's heart had cost him... he frowned, reaching for the memory and finding it oddly smooth, worn like a river stone. There had been a price. He knew there had been a price. Something was missing from inside him, some room in the house of his childhood with the furniture gone, and he could not remember what had stood there. He only knew the shape of the absence, and that the absence was shaped like her.

That was why he had come north. Not for the queen. For the shape of the absence.

"I'll tell you what she offered me," Rell said. "But you should sit down. And you—" he glanced at Isandro, and made himself hold the guardian's green-dark gaze, "—you especially should hear this, because I think you already know half of it, and I'd wager my other hand you haven't told her the rest."

* * *

They gave him tea he suspected had been grown in another world — it tasted of honey and thunderstorms — and he sat close to the fire because his bones had gone old somewhere between the tribunal and the hills, and he talked.

"The machine is dead," he began. "Truly dead, not sleeping. I made sure of that. But the gifted it drained are still alive, most of them, held in the palace cells and the workhouses — thirty-one that I counted before they took me. Some can't speak anymore. Some can't stop speaking. The ones who worked longest on the siphon lines have lost..." He paused, working his jaw. "There's a woman in the third cell row who doesn't know her own name. She signs whatever they put in front of her. She smiled at me once, and it was the worst thing I've ever seen, and I've seen the Road of Ash."

Wendell had gone very still. Rell saw Isandro's hand move — barely, a few inches across the space between them — until it rested near hers without touching. A courtesy. Or a claim. Rell looked away from it.

"The queen knows the machine failed," he went on. "She knows the realms are tearing anyway, that the border bleeds whether she severs it or not. She's not a fool, whatever else she is. She's a woman who watched her husband die of a wasting sickness the year the covenant broke, who watched her fields sour and her rivers turn, and concluded that magic was the poison. Thirteen years of grief dressed up as policy." He shrugged. "I served that grief for six years. It wears a crown very well."

"And now?" Wendell asked quietly.

"Now she's frightened in a new way. Your escape from the palace, the bridge opening, the keystones moving — her scholars tell her the covenant can be reforged. Made whole. Magic returning to the valley, the border healed, the two realms knitted back together." He turned his cup in his hands. "And she would rather burn her own kingdom than allow it."

"Why?" Isandro said. "A healed border serves her realm too. The blight ends. The crops return."

"Because a healed border means magic in the human realm, Isandro. Permanently. Freely. In every village and every child born after, gifted or not, the wildness leaking back into the world she has spent thirteen years scrubbing clean. She buried a husband in a poisoned kingdom. She will not hand her son a haunted one." Rell met the guardian's eyes. "You of all people should understand fear like that. You've seen what untrained gift does. So has she. She just drew the opposite lesson."

Silence, except for the rain and the fire. Then Wendell said, "Get to it, Rell. What did she offer?"

He made himself look at her. It was harder than facing the tribunal had been.

"She offers peace. A real one — she'll sign it, seal it, bind her heirs to it. The hunt ends. Every order rescinded. The thirty-one captives released, healed as far as healing can reach, given land and pardon. The gifted of the human realm left in peace for the first time in thirteen years." He swallowed. "Every gifted person in the human realm. Free. Safe. Under the queen's own protection."

Wendell's breath caught — he saw the hope hit her before she could guard against it, and hated himself for what came next.

"In exchange," he said, "the reforged covenant seals magic out of the human realm. Forever. The border heals, the realms knit whole — but the knitting draws a line. All the wildness stays on Isandro's side of it. The valley returns to what it was when you were a girl: a place where magic cannot follow. Where your gift sleeps. Where no child is ever again born with shimmer on their hands, and no mother ever again has to hide one."

* * *

The fire burned down a little while nobody spoke. Rell drank his strange tea and watched the two of them absorb it, and understood that he was watching a marriage being proposed by a third party — a marriage between Wendell and her own erasure.

"It's a lie," Isandro said at last, softly. "It cannot work that way. The covenant isn't a wall, it's a weaving. You cannot weave one thread out of the cloth and call the cloth whole."

"Her scholars believe otherwise."

"Her scholars have never touched a keystone."

"Probably not," Rell agreed. "But consider what she's offering to believe. Consider what it costs her to offer it. This is a queen who built a machine out of living people. And she's willing to dismantle it, free them, end the hunt — if the price is paid on the other side of the border. If the magic goes home and stays home." He set down the cup. "It's a mercy, Wendell. That's what makes it obscene."

"A mercy," Wendell repeated. Her voice had gone distant. She was staring into the fire, and Rell recognized the look — she was seeing faces. Thirty-one of them. A woman with her mother's hands, maybe, signing papers she couldn't read. "Thirty-one people walked out of those cells. Children yet unborn who won't spend their lives running. And all it costs is..."

She stopped.

"All it costs is you," Rell finished, because someone had to say it and it was never going to be Isandro, whose silence had the specific weight of a man who had known the terms all along. "All it costs is the valley staying blind. All it costs is your gift going back to sleep, and everyone like you on this side of the line going back to sleep, forever, so that the waking ones can be safe." He leaned forward. "I've thought about nothing else for nine days. Riding. Not sleeping. And here's what I keep coming back to: it would work. Gods forgive me, I think it would actually work. The queen keeps her word when the word is iron — I've seen her honor terms she despised. The captives would live. The hunt would end. You could go back to your garden, Wendell. Grow roses. Never feel the shimmer again. Never run again. Die old, in a valley at peace, having bought that peace with—"

"With myself," she said. "With everyone like me. With the children who come after, born different in a kingdom that has legally, permanently, mercifully decided they must never wake up." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and clearer than anything he had seen in years. "That's not mercy, Rell. That's the old covenant with a kinder face. That's exile with a ribbon on it. My grandmother's guild kept a covenant like that once — a valley sealed against magic, a gift hidden, a shimmer smothered under gloves and shame. And how did that end? The covenant broke anyway. The poison came anyway. My parents paid the price and it *failed*, because a covenant built on hiding things is just a wound learning to dress itself."

"I know." His voice came out rough. "I know. I told her negotiator the same thing, word for almost word, and he wrote it down and thanked me and sent me here anyway. Because the queen believes hope is a disease, Wendell, and this bargain is her quarantine. She'd rather amputate healthy limbs than risk the infection spreading. And she thinks — she genuinely thinks — she's saving everyone. Including you."

"Then she's wrong."

"Yes," Rell said. "She's wrong. But wrong queens with armies are still queens with armies, and the thirteenth moon is four turns out, and you need her borders open and her soldiers stood down to reach that bridge alive. She knows that too. That's why she sent me." He smiled, and it tasted like ash. "She sent the one man whose heart she could predict. Disgraced, memory-broken, in love with the heir since we were nine years old. She didn't send a messenger. She sent a knife with a familiar handle."

* * *

Later, when the fire had been fed and Isandro had withdrawn to the edge of the camp — watching the dark, or pretending to, though Rell noticed the guardian's attention never fully left the two of them — Wendell walked with him out past the fallen tower, into the rain, the way they used to walk the valley roads at dusk when the world was small enough to be survived.

"You knew," she said, when they were out of earshot. "When you covered our escape. When you broke the machine. You knew what she'd eventually ask of me."

"I hoped I'd be dead first." He said it lightly. It landed heavy, and he let it. "Wendell. Listen to me, because I may only get to say this once, and there's a hole in my head where the saying of it used to live." He turned to face her, rain streaming between them. "I don't remember our childhood. I know that now — I've known it since the palace, since the backlash took its price. I know there was a garden wall. I know there was a girl on it. I know her name, because everyone always told me her name, the way you memorize the name of a town you loved before the war. But the remembering itself is gone. Someone paid for my choice with the best part of me, and I would make the same trade again tomorrow, and that's the whole of what I understand about covenants and prices and love." His jaw tightened. "So when I tell you the queen's bargain is obscene, believe me. I'm the proof of what it asks. I'm what a person looks like afterward — walking, loyal, functional, and hollowed out in exactly the shape of the thing they loved. She's offering you that shape, Wendell. She's offering it to every gifted soul in the realm, and calling it safety."

Wendell reached out and gripped his forearm, hard, the way soldiers do, the way children do. "Rell—"

"Don't thank me. Don't refuse yet, either." He stepped back, out of her grip, because if she held on much longer he was going to say things that belonged to a boy on a garden wall and not to the wreck standing in front of her. "Take her offer to your guardian. Take it apart together. See what it's woven from. And then decide — not as the covenant's heir, not as the queen's enemy, not as anyone's symbol. Decide as the girl who dared me to climb."

"She's not asking me to decide as a girl. She's asking me to decide as a sacrifice."

"No." Rell swung back up into the saddle, gathering the reins, and the old captain's authority settled over him one last time like a cloak he was finally allowed to put down. "Sacrifices are taken. You'd be choosing. That's the whole horror of it — and the whole hope. Because a chosen thing can be refused."

He turned the horse north, toward the border, toward the bridge he had no right to approach and every intention of approaching.

"One more thing," he said over his shoulder. "The queen's negotiator mentioned — casually, the way men mention things they want repeated — that her scholars have located a fourth keystone. Old records. A guardian's tomb, they think, out past the marsh country. She knows where it lies, Wendell. She knows, and she's letting you ride toward it, because a bargain looks better to the desperate when they're tired and nearly done." He nudged the horse forward, then paused. "Whatever waits in that tomb — whoever waits — don't let her choose the ground of the last battle for you."

He rode into the rain, and behind him he heard — or imagined he heard — the night itself exhale, and somewhere far off in the dark, an owl call bending, bending, bending toward the sound of a flower that had not yet been planted, waiting for its name.
