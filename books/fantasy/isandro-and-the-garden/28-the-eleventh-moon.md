## 28. The Eleventh Moon

The moon rose over the borderlands like a coin held up to the light, and Wendell counted its turns the way she had once counted seedlings — with a gardener's dread of frost coming too soon.

Eleven. Only two remained.

She stood at the edge of the last ridge and looked down at what the old maps called the Sundered Span: a bridge of light that had been light once, thirteen years ago, before the breaking. Now it was a wound in the world. The river below ran both ways at once, half of it flowing toward Isandro's realm and half flowing back against itself, and where the bridge had been there hung a scar of pale radiance, torn down the middle like a seam ripped open. Through the tear she could see — not clearly, never clearly — the suggestion of another country. Towers like white candles. Trees whose leaves caught the starlight and gave it back silvered. A sky a shade too blue for this world.

Home, or the memory of home, on the far side.

"Four stones," Isandro said beside her. He said it quietly, the way he said everything now, as if loudness might crack something. He had recovered from the marsh — mostly. There were still gaps in him, small ones, like moth-holes in cloth. Sometimes he would pause mid-sentence and she could see him groping for a word, and her chest would tighten with a fear she refused to name: *what if the next gap is me?* "Four stones, one night, two turns left. We made it."

"We made it," Wendell echoed, and did not say what they were both thinking: *made it is not the same as finished.*

The four keystones rode in the satchel across her back, and she felt them the way she felt weather coming — a pressure, a hum, four separate heartbeats pressed against her spine. The first stone, cold as chapel water. The second, warm from the queen's conservatory, still carrying some residue of all the stolen magic that had passed near it. The third, which had nearly eaten Isandro's mind and still whispered sometimes at night, murmuring in a voice like her own. And the fourth, taken from the wraith of the first guardian, heavier than it should have been, as if grief had mass.

Her hands shook. She folded them into her sleeves so Isandro wouldn't see.

They descended toward the bridge through land that grew stranger with every step. This close to the border, the broken covenant leaked. Grass grew in spirals. A stand of birches had fused into a single trunk with forty crowns, and when the wind moved through them they chimed instead of rustled. Wild magic pooled in the hollows like fog, and Wendell walked through it feeling it press against her gift the way water presses against a door — patient, enormous, waiting to be let in.

Isandro stopped at the foot of the bridge and just looked at it for a long time.

"I crossed here the night it broke," he said. "I was on the far side. I watched it tear." His hand rose halfway toward the pale scar of light and fell again. "Thirteen years I have been trying to get back to this spot. It looks smaller than I remembered."

"Everything does," Wendell said, "when you finally reach it."

He glanced at her then, and something passed between them that needed no words — the whole long road of it compressed into a single look. The ash road and the drowned chapel. The market of small magics and Rell's offer under the cornering moon. The queen's conservatory and the woman with her mother's hands. The marsh, and the terrible weeks of rebuilding him memory by memory, telling him their own story until he knew it better than his own name. She had given him every day of their journey like a woman laying out plates at a feast, and somewhere in the telling she had stopped being afraid of him and started being unable to imagine the world without him in it.

Which was, of course, exactly the problem. Exactly the price.

Two nights from now, if her mother's message proved true, the covenant would take from her every memory tied to what she loved most. She had chosen it with open eyes. So had he. They had spoken the words to each other in the dark after the fourth keystone, plainly, without flinching — or flinching only where the other couldn't see.

But that was two nights away. Tonight, they had to survive until moonrise tomorrow, when the bridge would stand open for the span of a single turning.

Wendell unslung the satchel and knelt to check the stones, because checking was something to do with her hands. The sigils carved along each keystone's length glowed faintly at her touch, answering her gift the way plants answered it — leaning, reaching, recognizing their heir. Her mother's voice lived in her head now, in the sealed-and-opened memory: *the covenant can be reforged differently — shared willingly by two, rather than taken from one — but only if both choose it with open eyes.* Two. She looked up at Isandro, silhouetted against the torn light of the bridge, and wondered what memories the covenant would take from him. What did he love most? She knew the answer, and knowing it was a kind of ache she carried everywhere now, like a stone in her boot.

"The ground's humming," Isandro said suddenly.

She felt it a heartbeat later — a vibration rising through her knees, through the earth itself, rhythmic and heavy. Not the wild magic's pulse. Something mechanical. Something with wheels and iron and many, many boots.

"They followed us." Isandro turned, and the light of the bridge caught his face, and for the first time since the marsh she saw real fear in it. "I thought we'd lost them past the ash road. I thought—"

The crest of the ridge behind them blackened with movement.

They came over the hilltop in a line that did not end. Crown soldiers in the queen's grey, ranks upon ranks, pikes catching the moonlight in a bristle of silver. Behind them rolled wagons, and behind the wagons came the thing Wendell had seen only once before, in the conservatory's depths, and had hoped never to see again under open sky.

The machine.

It was bigger than she remembered — or perhaps out here, under the actual moon rather than a palace roof, it simply looked more like what it was. An engine of iron and glass and caged light, mounted on a carriage drawn by twelve horses whose eyes had gone dull and milk-white. Cables ran from its core into iron spheres the size of houses, and inside those spheres, faintly visible through slitted vents, shapes moved. Wendell's stomach turned over. She knew what was inside the spheres. People. Gifted people, drained and dreaming, their magic siphoned off to feed the machine's hunger, their memories already stripped to make room.

The queen had fed it well on the march.

And above the machine, on a raised platform draped in grey silk, stood a figure Wendell recognized even at this distance by the way everyone arranged themselves around her — the way water arranges itself around a stone.

The queen had come herself.

"She wouldn't," Isandro breathed. "A monarch doesn't come to the field for a severing. It's done by decree, by engineers—"

"She's not here for the engineering," Wendell said. Her mouth had gone dry. "She's here because she has to watch. You told me yourself — she believes magic is the plague killing her kingdom. Would you send someone else to kill the thing that's murdering your children?"

The army halted at the crest of the ridge in perfect silence, hundreds strong, and the silence was worse than shouting. Then a single rider detached from the line and came down the slope alone, unhurried, reins loose, and Wendell knew the shape of him before the moon cleared his shoulder.

Captain Rell.

He reined in twenty paces away and sat there a moment, looking at the two of them standing before the broken bridge with a satchel of stolen keystones, and Wendell searched his face for the man who had offered to falsify her death, who had covered their escape from the palace, who had broken the machine's core laughing — no. That hadn't happened yet. That was a future she had glimpsed in visions and feared and hoped for, tangled together. The Rell before her now was still the crown's captain, still sworn, still carrying his oath like armor plate. But his eyes went to her first, before Isandro, before the bridge, and stayed a moment too long.

"Wendell Thornbury." He said her name like a man setting down something heavy. "You were seen crossing the ash road. After the palace, Her Majesty doubled the hunt. I'm sorry. There was no losing her after that."

"You could stop riding with them," Isandro said flatly.

"I could," Rell agreed. "And then some other captain leads them here, one who won't give you an hour's warning, one who won't let you reach the bridge at all." He dismounted slowly, deliberately, keeping his hands visible. "I'm here because the queen sent terms. She wants me to deliver them personally, because she believes — correctly — that you'll listen to me when you won't listen to a herald."

Wendell's heart was pounding. "Terms."

Rell looked at the bridge, at the torn light, and something flickered across his face — wonder, maybe, or grief. "Tomorrow night, when the moon completes its eleventh turn, the machine will begin the severing. The realms will be cut apart permanently. Magic will die in this world entirely, and with it, the plague — that's what she believes, and she has buried two children and a husband believing it, so good luck arguing her out of it with philosophy." He turned back to them. "Or: surrender the keystones and the gifted in your company, and the crown will call off the hunt. Every gifted soul in the cages walks free. Pardons. Land. The queen swears it on her line."

"And the catch," Wendell said, because there was always a catch, and Rell's silence confirmed it before he spoke.

"The catch is that the pardons hold only if the realms are severed anyway. The keystones surrendered to the machine. Magic sealed out forever." He met her eyes. "She's offering to free everyone you love in exchange for making sure none of you ever feel it again."

The wind came down off the ridge and moved through the chiming birches, and for a moment neither side spoke.

"That's not mercy," Wendell said quietly. "That's the old covenant with a kinder face. Exile dressed in silk. I've already refused that bargain once, Rell."

"I know." He almost smiled. "That's why she sent me. She said, 'Tell the heir that stubbornness is a luxury of the living.' She wanted you to hear it from someone who—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "From a friend."

"Are you?" Wendell asked. "Still?"

Rell took longer than he should have to answer, and the answer, when it came, wasn't words. He reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper — the queen's seal, unbroken — and held it out. Then, as Wendell reached for it, he turned his wrist and let it fall to the grass between them, unread.

"Consider the terms delivered," he said. "Officially. Whether you read them is between you and your conscience." He remounted, and paused, and looked at her the way he had looked at her across a childhood of shared bread and stolen apples, before oaths and hunts and grey uniforms came between them. "The machine needs a full night to charge before it can fire. Tomorrow, sundown to moonset. If you're going to do whatever it is you're going to do — you'll be doing it while four hundred soldiers watch, and while that thing drinks the border dry." He gathered his reins. "I'd plan for less time than you think you have."

"Rell." He stopped. Wendell stepped closer, close enough to see the exhaustion ground into him like dirt into a gardener's knees. "Whose side are you on?"

He held her gaze for a long moment, and then he said, "Ask me again tomorrow night," and wheeled his horse and rode back up the ridge, and did not look back.

* * *

They made camp in the ruins of an old toll-house at the bridge's near end, low walls of mossy stone that gave them cover from the army's sightlines, though nothing could screen them from the sound. All night the machine drank. Wendell felt it the way she felt a blight moving through a garden — a slow, greedy pull at the roots of things. The wild magic pooling in the hollows thinned hour by hour. The chiming birches fell silent one crown at a time. By midnight the grass around the toll-house had gone grey and stiff, remembering how to grow but forgetting why.

Isandro sat against the wall with the keystones arrayed before him in the moonlight, studying the sigils for the hundredth time, and Wendell watched him instead of sleeping.

"Tell me again," she said. "What happens tomorrow. All of it. I want to hear it in your voice, not just my mother's."

He set down the third keystone — the whisperer — carefully, as if it were listening. "At moonrise, the bridge opens. One night only; the covenant's death-throes give us that much. We carry the four stones to the center, to the anchor-point where the first guardian spoke the original binding. We speak the covenant as your mother wrote it — two voices, willing, open-eyed. The stones take the price. The bridge knits. The realms heal." He paused. "And the machine tries to stop us, because a healed covenant means magic lives, and the queen would rather kill the kingdom's soul than risk its body."

"There's a gap in that plan," Wendell said. "It's shaped like four hundred soldiers."

"Yes." He didn't pretend otherwise, and she loved him a little for that — for never lying to her about the size of things. "Rell bought us an hour's warning tonight. Maybe he can buy more tomorrow. Maybe the captives in those spheres, if they're freed, will pull enough power back from the machine to slow it. Maybe the wild magic at the border, what's left of it, will answer its heir." He looked at her then, and the moonlight did something to his face, made the bark-scarring along his jaw look almost like filigree. "Maybe is all we've ever had, Wendell. Since the garden."

Since the garden. She closed her eyes and let herself remember it — the walled garden, the shimmer her hands left on living things, the herald's proclamation nailed to the gate, and the wounded man calling her by a name no one used. It felt like another life. In a way, it was; in two nights, it would be the only life she had left, stripped of him, stripped of the valley's magic, stripped of everything except hands that knew how to grow things and a grief she wouldn't be able to name.

"Isandro." She opened her eyes. "When the covenant takes its price from you — what will it take?"

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"Everything since the bridge tore," he said finally. "Thirteen years of searching. The guild records I memorized. Your grandmother's face when she burned them. The road. The chapel. The marsh." His voice didn't waver, but his hands, she noticed, had gone very still in his lap, the stillness of a man holding himself together by force. "You. It will take you. And I chose it anyway, with open eyes, because a world where you live and don't know me is worth more than a world where neither of us lives at all."

The words went through her like a root through packed clay — slow, inevitable, splitting her open somewhere deep.

"And if there's another way," she said. "If my mother's message means what I think it means. If the price can be *shared* instead of—"

"It can't be dodged," he said gently. "Whatever shape it takes, it gets paid. That's what the first guardian's ghost taught us. Love withheld from the price cost more than the price ever would." He reached over and touched the satchel, and the four keystones hummed together, a chord like distant bells. "We pay it. Both of us. Willingly. That's the whole of the new covenant, and the whole of our courage, and there's nothing left over for cleverness."

Wendell looked at the bridge — the torn light, the far country's silvered trees, the sky a shade too blue — and felt the clock of the moon turning in her blood.

* * *

Near dawn, she woke to find Isandro standing on the toll-house wall, motionless, staring at the ridge.

The army was moving.

Not advancing — arranging. By torchlight, in perfect discipline, the crown's soldiers were building a ring around the bridge. Wagons circled. Pikes planted. The great iron spheres of the machine were winched off their carriages and set into the earth at four points around the span, cables running inward like the spokes of a wheel, and Wendell understood with a lurch what she was seeing: they weren't going to wait for the bridge to open and then attack. They were going to hold the ground itself. Whatever happened at moonrise tomorrow would happen inside a cage of iron and stolen magic, with the queen watching from her silk-draped platform and four hundred spears between Wendell and the anchor-point.

And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, Wendell felt the machine beginning to drink the border dry — pulling at the wild magic in the soil, at the torn light of the bridge, at the covenant's last dying strength — charging itself for the severing, sundown to moonset, exactly as Rell had said.

Less time than she thought. Less time than any of them had.

Isandro climbed down and stood beside her, and for a while they watched the cage build itself around their hope.

"One turn left," Wendell said. "One night. One chance, inside all that."

"Yes." He took her hand. His palm was cool, bark-smooth, familiar as her own heartbeat. "So we spend it well."

Above the eastern hills, the eleventh moon began to set, thin and white and merciless, and Wendell counted its descent the way she had once counted the days between frost and planting — knowing exactly what was coming, and choosing, with open eyes, to meet it growing.
