## 29. The Machine Awakens

The bridge of light stood open for the first time in thirteen years, and it was the most terrible thing Isandro had ever seen.

It arched across the border like a rib pulled from the world's chest — a span of woven radiance, silver at its roots and gold where it climbed toward the realm on the far side. Through its lattice he could see his homeland: the terraced orchards of the high vale, the rivers that sang in chords, the towers of the covenant-citadel wrapped in their perpetual spring. Home. He had crossed this bridge a thousand times as a young guardian, had run its length barefoot in the season of first blossoms, had never once imagined that seeing it again would feel like grief.

Because it was dying even as it opened.

The light flickered along its eastern third, guttering like a candle in wind, and where it guttered the air behind it showed something else — not darkness but *absence*, a nothing that hurt to look at, the way a missing tooth hurts the tongue. The border between realms had been torn for thirteen years, held together by nothing but habit and the stubbornness of small magics, and now the keystones in Wendell's satchel were pulling the wound wide so that it could be closed properly.

And behind them, down the slope of the human valley, came the sound of drums.

"They're here." Rell's voice, low and flat, from the ridge above. The captain had shed his crimson officer's coat somewhere in the last mile; without it he looked younger and harder, a man made entirely of decisions he hadn't wanted to make. His sword was drawn. "Gods. Look at the size of it."

Isandro turned and looked, and felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world.

The crown's army filled the valley mouth like water filling a bowl — ranks of pike and crossbow glinting under the eleventh moon, banners snapping, and at their center, drawn by forty oxen on a cradle of iron timbers, the machine.

He had seen it only in fragments before: rumors bought from frightened clerks, a glimpse of its blueprints in the queen's conservatory while Wendell cut the second keystone from its living trellis. But rumor and blueprint had not prepared him for its *presence*. It was less a device than a cathedral built by something that hated cathedrals — a sphere of black iron caged in silver filigree, veined with channels through which light moved. Not the warm gold of covenant-light. Something colder. Whiter. The light of things being taken.

Even at this distance he could hear it. A hum below hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, and threaded through it — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable once you knew what to listen for — voices.

*They're already feeding it,* he thought. *She's been feeding it all along.*

"Isandro." Wendell's hand found his arm. She stood at the bridge's threshold with her palm pressed to the first keystone, which floated an inch above her fingers, turning slowly, humming in harmony with the three others packed in oilcloth at her hip. Her face was lit from below by their glow, and she looked — there was no gentler word for it — *consecrated*. Like something out of the oldest stories his grandmother had told him, back when the covenant was still spoken of as a living thing rather than a wound. "I can feel them. The captives. They're in the army's center, in wagons — I can feel their magic being pulled out of them like thread off a spool."

"How many?"

Her jaw tightened. "Dozens. Maybe more than we freed from the palace. And one of them—" she stopped, swallowed, started again. "One of them has my mother's hands."

* * *

They had argued about this on the road, in the marsh country, in the ruins of the drowned chapel, and every argument had ended the same way: with Wendell saying *the bridge needs me*, and Isandro saying *the captives need us*, and both of them staring at each other across the arithmetic of an impossible night.

There was no clean way to do this. That was the truth none of the old stories told. In the songs, the hero reached the bridge as the enemy arrived and everything resolved in a single bright stroke — the stone set, the words spoken, the dawn coming up golden. But the songs did not have to hold a keystone steady while a border tore, or count how many cages could be broken in the minutes before the moon completed its turn, or choose which desperate thing to leave undone.

"The queen will bring the machine to the border," Isandro said now, watching the slow advance of the oxen. "Not to fight us. To finish it. If the severing completes while the bridge is open—" He couldn't finish. He didn't need to. They had all seen what the machine's partial work had done to the Road of Ash, to the backward rivers, to the crops that remembered being forests. Full severance would do that to *everything*, to both realms, a scar from horizon to horizon.

"Then we split," Wendell said. It wasn't a question. It hadn't been a question since the palace.

"I should go with you to the cages—"

"You can't." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "The keystones answer to me. If the bridge starts to close early, someone has to be standing here holding it open, and it can't be me, because I'm going to be running toward the army." She touched his cheek then, briefly, her fingers smelling of loam and rain, and Isandro thought — as he had thought every day since the confession beneath the broken bridge, since she had walked away into the dark and then come *back*, having found her mother's message, having chosen him anyway with her eyes wide open — *I do not deserve this woman, and I will spend whatever remains of my life trying to.* "Rell knows where they keep the wagons. Go. Break what you can break. And Isandro—" her voice caught, steadied, "—come back. Whatever happens on that bridge tonight, come back."

He kissed her knuckles, because he could not trust himself to kiss her mouth and let go after. Then he turned to Rell.

The captain was looking at the army he had served for eleven years with an expression Isandro recognized from mirrors: the look of a man watching his own history burn and finding, to his surprise, that he is mostly relieved.

"They'll know your face," Isandro said.

"They'll know worse than my face if I let them put those people in that machine." Rell rolled his shoulders once, the way he did before every battle, a habit from some academy yard long ago. "Wagons are ninth and tenth in the column. Queen's guard around them — twelve men, maybe fourteen. I trained half of them." A pause, and something moved behind his eyes, quick and painful. "Sergeant Ovist. Corporal Dane. Good soldiers. They think they're guarding prisoners." He picked up his sword. "Let's go teach them better."

* * *

They went down through the bracken in the dark, two shadows among many, and Isandro felt his gift waking as they descended out of the border's dead zone and into the thin, returning magic of the human vale.

It came back the way feeling returns to a numbed limb — painfully, gloriously, all at once. The grass whispered under his boots in the old language. The moonlight thickened until he could almost swim in it. And beneath everything, growing louder with every step, the machine's hunger: a great cold suction in the fabric of the night, drawing magic toward itself the way a drain draws water.

He understood suddenly, viscerally, why the gifted who had been near it too long went quiet. Why the captives in the queen's conservatory had stared at walls with empty eyes, their memories stripped, their power siphoned into channels of silver filigree. The machine didn't just take magic. It took the *self* that magic grew from. It was eating people and calling it engineering.

"Ninth wagon," Rell breathed. They had come around the column's flank through a fold of ground the army's scouts had dismissed as impassable — the same fold of ground, Isandro suspected, that Rell himself had mapped as a lieutenant, years ago, when he still believed the crown's wars would be honorable ones. "Two drivers, four guards on the tailgate. Ovist walks point on the left. Dane's on the right rear."

"And the rest of the army?"

"Watching the bridge. Watching *her*." Rell's mouth twisted. "The queen wants the heir taken alive. Everyone else is expendable. Which means—" he drew a breath, "—which means the moment we hit these wagons, every eye that isn't on the bridge turns here. We buy her minutes, maybe. Not more."

"It will be enough."

"You believe that?"

"No," Isandro said honestly. "But I believe in her, and she believes it, and between the three of those beliefs we may yet make a true thing."

Rell looked at him for a long moment — the guardian from the other side of the world, the man his orders said to take alive, the reason his career lay in pieces behind him on the road — and then, unexpectedly, he laughed. It was a short, rough sound, mostly breath.

"My grandmother used to say the old families talked like that. Like every sentence was a proverb." He shook his head. "She'd have liked you. Come on."

* * *

What followed, Isandro would remember afterward in flashes, the way trauma files its receipts: in fragments, out of order, each one burning.

The sentry at the column's edge, folding softly sideways as Rell's pommel met the base of his skull — an act of mercy performed with a soldier's economy, and Isandro understood in that instant exactly how much of Rell's love for Wendell lived in his hands.

The tailgate guards turning, shouting, the shout cut short as Isandro spoke a word of the old tongue and the ground itself rose — not violently, nothing so crude, but *insistently*, roots lacing upward through the packed earth of the road and taking the men's ankles like patient green fingers. They went down confused rather than bleeding. One of them, a boy hardly older than Wendell, stared up at Isandro with pure animal terror and said, "Please — I have sisters —"

"Then you'll want to live to see them," Isandro said, and bound him gently in bramble, and ran on.

The wagon door tearing open under Rell's blade and Isandro's combined strength, and the smell that rolled out — blood and straw and the ozone stink of spent magic — and the people inside.

They were caged like the others had been caged, collared in silver, slumped against the bars. But unlike the conservatory captives, these still had eyes. These were fresh. The machine had been drinking from them for hours, not months, and some ember in each of them still burned.

A girl with river-colored hair lifted her head as the door opened. She looked at Isandro — at the bark-scarring that climbed his forearm where the wild magic had marked him, at the faint luminescence the border air woke in his skin — and her cracked lips shaped a single word:

"*Guardian.*"

"Yes," he said, working the collar's clasp, his hands shaking. "Yes. I'm sorry it took so long. I'm sorry for all of it."

Behind him, Rell was breaking the second cage, and Sergeant Ovist — good soldier, loyal man — had arrived at a run with his halberd leveled and stopped dead, staring at his captain kneeling in the wreckage of his own oath, freeing the crown's prisoners by torchlight.

"Captain Rell," Ovist said, and his voice was very strange. "Sir. What — what are you doing?"

"What I should have done thirteen years ago," Rell said, without turning around, "when they first told me what the machine was for. Stand aside, Tomas. Or don't. But either way, lower that halberd, because I've known you since you were nine years old and I'd hate for this to be the night I ruin your whole life."

A silence. The machine hummed. Somewhere far off, at the border, a horn sounded — the queen's signal, the beginning of the end.

Ovist lowered the halberd. He did it slowly, like a man setting down something heavy he had carried a very long way, and then he said, in a voice that cracked in the middle: "My sister's in the tenth wagon, sir. She has the weather-gift. They took her in the spring."

For a moment no one moved. Then Rell put his hand on the sergeant's shoulder, once, hard, and said, "Then let's get her out," and the war, for those few men in that small circle of torchlight, quietly changed sides.

* * *

At the border, the world began to tear.

Isandro felt it even from the wagons — a wrongness rolling through the ground like the first tremor of an earthquake, and then the light. The bridge flared white, blinding, and beyond it the absence spread, edges crawling outward like frost across glass. The queen's voice carried over the army's ranks, amplified by some artifice of the machine, clear and cold and — this was the horror of it, the thing Isandro would carry forever — *reasonable*.

*"People of the valley! For thirteen years magic has crept back into our fields and our children, and with it the rot, the blight, the wasting sickness that has killed our harvests and our sons. Tonight it ends. Tonight we cut it out at the root!"*

She believed it. That was the unbearable part. Isandro had seen her face in the conservatory, had heard the grief underneath the steel. The plague *was* real. The blight *was* killing her kingdom. She simply — tragically, catastrophically — had the diagnosis backwards. The sickness was the wound. Magic was the flesh trying to heal. And she was about to amputate the limb to save the body, and call it mercy.

"She's activating the full array," the river-haired girl gasped, clutching Isandro's arm as they hauled the last captives from the wagons. Her gift stirred weakly around her, a shiver of mist. "I can feel it drinking. Guardian — it's drinking *deeper*. If it finishes the severing while the bridge is open—"

"I know."

"—then everyone on that bridge dies. Both of her. The heir and the guardian. The machine doesn't care whose side the magic is on. It just takes."

Isandro looked east, toward the bridge, toward the small blazing figure at its threshold with four keystones wheeling around her like moons, holding open a door between worlds with nothing but her will and her love. The absence was spreading along the span. She was feeding herself into the gap to keep it from closing wrong, and the machine was pulling at her from the other side, and between the two forces she was being stretched thinner than any soul should bear.

"Wendell," he whispered.

And then the machine screamed.

There was no other word for it. The black sphere shuddered in its silver cage, and every channel of stolen light flared at once, and the sound that came out of it was the sound of forty stolen voices crying in unison — the girl's mist-gift, the boy's green-speech, a hundred small magics wrenched past their limits — and the border tore.

Not widened. *Tore.* A ragged gash opened in the air beside the bridge, and through it poured not the gentle chaos of returning magic but something rawer, older, the unplumbed dark between realms, and things began to come through it. Not monsters, precisely. Memories, maybe. The borderland's orphans, thirteen years of lost and leaking magic given sudden form, spilling into the human valley like water through a cracked dam.

The army broke. Pikes dropped. Men ran. Even the queen's voice faltered mid-proclamation.

"Hold the line!" Rell roared, absurdly, uselessly, at no one — and then laughed at himself, wild-eyed, blood on his cheek from a flying stone. "Forget the line. There is no line. Get them out — all of them — get them to the tree line!"

Isandro stood in the chaos with the freed captives streaming past him and made the choice that had been waiting for him all night, all year, all life.

"Rell." He grabbed the captain's arm. "Get them clear. All of them. You know the ground; I don't."

"And you?"

"The machine." Isandro's voice came out low and certain, and he was surprised to find he meant it. "Someone has to reach the core before the queen feeds it the rest of the array. Someone who understands what it's drinking and how to make it choke." He was already moving, the old tongue rising in his throat, the wild magic of two realms gathering in his hands like a storm deciding where to strike. "Go, Captain. Your sergeant's sister is waiting. And tell my—"

He stopped. Swallowed.

"Tell her I'm coming back," he said. "Whatever happens. Tell her I keep my promises."

Then Isandro, last guardian of the covenant, turned and ran into the heart of the machine's light — and behind him, at the torn and burning border, he heard Wendell cry out his name across the whole width of the breaking world.
