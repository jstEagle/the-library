## 30. The Queen's Memory

The machine's song had changed.

Wendell felt it through the soles of her boots before she understood it with her mind — a deep, grinding note beneath the shriek of tearing light, like a root being wrenched from soil it had held for a century. The bridge of the covenant blazed above her, half of its arch still standing in the air where thirteen years ago it had been severed, and now the queen's machine was pulling at even that remnant, unraveling thread by golden thread.

She ran.

The battlefield — if it could be called that — was chaos without sides. Crown soldiers fought not Isandro's people but the machine's own backlash, thrown backward by pulses of raw magic siphoned from the caged gifted at its heart. She passed a young hunter on his knees, hands pressed to his ears, weeping without knowing why. She passed a woman in palace livery whose shadow moved wrong, lagging half a heartbeat behind her body. The drained ones, she thought. The ones they'd fed into the engine. Their magic was being burned as fuel, and everything it touched came apart at the seams.

*Isandro*, she thought, and forced herself not to turn toward the sound of fighting near the wagons where the captives were held. *Rell is there. They have their work. You have yours.*

Her work was the queen.

She found her at the top of the ridge overlooking the border, standing beneath a canopy of black silk that did nothing to keep out the light of the dying bridge. Four guards flanked her, and beside her stood a thin man in grey robes — the royal arcanist, Wendell realized, the one whose designs the machine embodied — his fingers dancing across a console of brass and bone, feeding coordinates into the severing.

The queen herself was smaller than Wendell had imagined. Smaller and older. In the proclamation woodcuts she was a figure of iron and certainty, crowned and terrible. Here, lit from below by the machine's sickly glow, she was a woman of perhaps fifty-five with silver threading her dark hair and lines around her mouth that spoke of years spent clenching her jaw against grief. Her eyes were fixed on the bridge, and there was nothing triumphant in them. There was only hunger — the desperate, hollowed-out hunger of someone watching the thing they believed would save them, praying it would work because the alternative was unbearable.

Wendell's hand closed around the four keystones strung at her throat. They were warm. They were always warm now, since the marsh, since Isandro — no. She could not think of him now. She could not afford the ache. Later. If there was a later.

She stepped out of the shadows.

"Your Majesty."

The guards moved as one, blades clearing scabbards, and the queen lifted one hand — a small motion, almost weary — and they stopped. Perhaps it was curiosity. Perhaps it was that Wendell made no move toward any weapon, only stood in the machine-light with her palms open and her voice steady, a gardener's daughter in a coat scorched by three realms' worth of travel.

"You're one of them," the queen said. Her voice was low, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had given orders in worse storms than this. "A gifted one. Come to beg."

"No." Wendell took another step. The guards tensed; the queen's hand stayed them again. "I've come to show you something. And I need you to understand — I know why you built your machine. I'm not here to call you a monster."

Something flickered across the queen's face — surprise, quickly mastered, but real. "You know nothing about me."

"I know about your son."

The words fell between them like a stone into still water.

Wendell had learned it three nights ago, in a tavern in the last free town, from an old woman who traded in small magics and older gossip: the queen had not always hated the gifted. Thirteen years ago — *thirteen years ago, everything was thirteen years ago* — the crown prince had fallen ill with the wasting plague, the same sickness that had crept through the kingdom's villages for over a decade, thinning children and old folk alike, resisting every physic and prayer. And in desperation, before the fear had hardened into policy, the queen had sought out a gifted healer.

And the healer had failed. Or seemed to. The boy had died anyway, and something in the queen had died with him, and what rose in its place was the woman who decreed that magic itself was the disease.

"His name was Aldric," Wendell said quietly. "He was nine. You brought a healer named Maren Ostwick to his bedside, and when he died anyway, you had Maren imprisoned, and you told yourself the magic had failed him. That magic had *killed* him. That if you cut it out of the world, no other mother would lose what you lost."

"Stop." The queen's voice cracked like ice underfoot.

"The healer didn't fail him," Wendell went on, and her own voice trembled now, because she had seen this truth in the keystones' visions and it had kept her awake for two nights. "No healer could have saved him. Because the sickness that was killing your son wasn't a sickness of flesh. It was the covenant breaking. When the bond between the realms tore, everything the covenant had sustained began to starve — the land, the harvests, the lives. The plague isn't magic, Your Majesty. The plague is the *absence* of it. Magic isn't killing your kingdom. Its death is."

"I said *stop*—"

"Thirteen years ago," Wendell said, "the first guardian broke the covenant. Out of love. To spare someone the price of keeping it. And everything since — every wasted field, every fevered child, every village that turned on its neighbors — has been the wound refusing to close. Your son was the first great casualty. He will not be the last. And your machine—" she gestured toward the blazing, screaming bridge, toward the cages below where human beings were being emptied like wineskins, "—your machine won't heal the wound. It will make it permanent. It will kill the patient to cure the pain."

For a long moment there was only the machine's grinding song and the wind off the borderlands, carrying ash.

Then the queen said, very softly, "Show me."

Wendell hadn't expected that. She had prepared for refusal, for guards, for the flat wall of a grief that had calcified into doctrine. She had not prepared for a woman who, at the edge of everything, still wanted — needed — to *know*.

She unstrung the keystones from her neck. Four stones, each no larger than a plum, each humming with the memory of the covenant's making: earth-dark, water-pale, fire-veined, and the fourth, the newest-won, shot through with a green like new leaves. She held them cupped in both hands, the way she had once held seedlings over the guild's cold frames, and let her gift rise through her arms and into them.

"Whatever you see," she said, "it happened. This is not my invention. These stones remember what the world has forgotten."

She opened her hands.

* * *

The vision took them both.

Wendell had walked inside the keystones' memory twice before, and each time it was like stepping into a river — the current of the past closing over her head, carrying her where it willed. But this time she was not alone, and this time she was not a passenger. She had asked the stones for a specific remembering, and the stones, being what they were — seeds of the covenant, grown from the first vow ever spoken between the realms — obliged her.

They stood in a garden.

Not any garden Wendell knew, though it resembled her guild's walled plot the way a mother resembles a daughter. This was the original: the First Garden, planted at the moment the covenant was struck, when the realm of Isandro and the valley of men had agreed to share their borders the way good neighbors share a fence-line — each tending their side, each drawing water from the same deep well. She saw the first guardian, tall and bark-skinned, kneeling in loam beside a human woman with dirt under her fingernails, and she saw the vow pass between them like pollen on the wind, invisible and everywhere at once.

"This is what it was," Wendell said. Beside her, the queen stood rigid, her hands clasped so tightly the knuckles had gone white. "Not domination. Not infection. A *tending*. The magic of Isandro's realm fed your valley's soil, and your valley's seasons gave Isandro's realm its rhythm. Neither could be whole alone. Every harvest your kingdom ever counted on grew from this."

The vision shifted. Years folding like pages. The garden flourishing. Children — human and otherwise — chasing each other through rows of impossible flowers. And then—

The wound.

Wendell braced herself, but seeing it again was no easier the second time. The first guardian, the bark-skinned man, standing at the boundary with tears cutting channels through the moss of his cheeks, speaking the words of unbinding. The covenant tearing like a seam ripped open. And the land on the human side of the line — she watched it happen, watched the color drain upward out of the fields like sap retreating from a cut stem, watched wells go bitter, watched the first fevers bloom in the nearest village like blight in a leaf.

Beside her, the queen made a sound. Small. Involuntary. The sound of a woman seeing, for the first time, the true face of the enemy she had declared war on.

"It spread," Wendell whispered, as the vision carried them forward through the years of quiet ruin — the plague moving outward from the broken border in slow rings, season after season, taking the weakest first. "It's still spreading. Your physicians called it a wasting sickness. Your priests called it a judgment. It was neither. It was thirst. The whole kingdom, dying of thirst at the edge of a well, because someone convinced you the well was poison."

The vision showed her more. It showed her Maren Ostwick — the healer the queen had summoned to Prince Aldric's bedside — and Wendell saw what the histories had never recorded: Maren had not failed. Maren had *known*. She had knelt by the dying boy and felt with her gift the great emptiness upstream of him, the severed covenant drinking the life out of everything downstream, and she had done everything a single gifted woman could do — poured her own strength into the child like water into cracked earth — and the earth had been too cracked, the drought too long, and the boy had slipped away despite her, and she had wept over him as if he were her own.

"She tried to tell you," Wendell said. Her voice was barely audible over the machine's grinding. "After. She tried to explain what she'd felt — that the sickness came from the broken border, that it could be mended. And you wouldn't hear it. Because if she was right, then your war was against the wrong enemy, and your son had died for nothing, and—"

"*Enough.*"

But the queen did not look away from the vision. She could not. The keystones, gentle and merciless, showed her the rest: the arrests beginning, the gifted dragged from their homes, the first cages built, the arcanist's designs drawn up in chalk and blood-ink, the machine rising year by year like a monument to a misunderstanding. And threaded through all of it, inescapable, the image of a small fair-haired boy growing paler in a bed hung with blue silk, while his mother sat beside him holding a hand that grew lighter and lighter, until there was nothing in it at all.

The vision ended.

They stood again on the windy ridge, in the machine-light, in the present tense, where nothing had yet been decided and everything was already burning.

* * *

The queen did not speak for a long time.

Wendell let her. She knew something of what it was to have the ground open under everything you believed — she had felt it in her grandmother's kitchen, thirteen years of silence cracking open in a single evening. You could not rush a person through that falling. You could only stand nearby and wait for them to land.

When the queen finally spoke, her voice was stripped of command. What remained underneath was worse, and truer.

"Maren told me," she said. "The night after the funeral. She came to me in the mourning hall, past every guard, and she knelt and she said, *Your Majesty, the sickness is not in the blood. I felt it. It comes from the border, from something broken there, and it can be mended.*" The queen's jaw worked. "And I had her taken to the cells. Because I could not bear it. Because if she was right, then I had sat by my son's bed hating the wrong thing, and there was no enemy left to punish except myself."

"Your Majesty—"

"Do not console me." It was not cruel; it was exhausted. "I have been consoled by better liars than you for thirteen years. I built a machine out of my grief and called it justice. I filled its belly with people who might have healed my son, if I had listened, if I had been brave enough to be wrong." She turned at last and looked at Wendell fully, and her eyes were wet and furious and terribly clear. "You want to know the worst of it? I *knew*. Somewhere in me, all these years, I knew. Grief makes cowards of us, gardener. It made such a coward of me that I mistook my cowardice for conviction, and I fed it children."

Below them, the machine screamed. One of the pulses of backlash rolled up the ridge, and the black silk canopy snapped like a sail, and the guards staggered, and the queen did not move at all.

"Stop it," Wendell said. "You can stop it. Order the arcanist to shut it down. Release the captives. The bridge is still standing — damaged, but standing. We have the four keystones. We can reforge the covenant tonight, before the thirteenth turn, and the plague ends. Not in a generation. *Tonight.* Your kingdom lives. No other mother loses what you lost."

For one heartbeat — one — Wendell saw it happen. Saw the yes form behind the queen's eyes. Saw the woman beneath the crown reach for it, hungry as the starving reach for bread.

Then the queen looked down at the machine, at the cages glowing at its heart, and Wendell watched the yes die.

"I can't," the queen said.

"You just—"

"You don't understand what it *is* now." The queen's composure was back, but it was the composure of a drowning woman gripping wreckage. "It isn't a machine anymore. It's been feeding for hours. Two hundred gifted souls poured into its core — do you know what that much gathered magic becomes, once it's given a purpose? It has a shape now. A will, of a kind. My arcanist built the frame, but the power—" she laughed, and there was nothing of humor in it, "—the power no longer entirely answers to me. If I order it stopped, it may stop. Or it may tear loose and take the border, the bridge, the valley, my army, and everyone in those cages with it. I fed it. I cannot simply unfed it. That is what I have learned tonight, gardener, among other things: that grief, given enough fuel, stops being something you carry and becomes something that carries *you*."

"Then we find another way to stop it," Wendell said fiercely. "There's always another way. I'm the covenant's heir — my gift answers to growing things, to mending things. Give me access to the core. Let me try to draw the stolen magic back out of it, give it back to the people it was taken from—"

"And if you fail?"

"Then I fail. But I'll fail trying to save them instead of standing here explaining why saving them is impossible."

The queen studied her for a long moment. Something moved in her face — recognition, maybe, or envy, or the ghost of who she had been before the blue silk room and the empty hand. "You remind me of Maren," she said quietly. "She also believed there was always another way. They broke her in the cells, in the end. I signed the order myself." She closed her eyes. "Go, then. Go down to your friends and your stones and your impossible mending. I will not order my guards to stop you. It is not mercy — do not thank me. It is only that I find, tonight, that I can no longer remember why any of this was supposed to be worth it."

"But the machine—"

"The machine is mine," the queen said, "and so is its consequence. That much, at least, I will not hand to anyone else." She turned back toward the bridge, toward the tearing light, and lifted her chin the way Wendell imagined she had once lifted it over a small bed hung with blue silk. "Run, gardener. Whatever you're going to do — run."

Wendell ran.

She was three steps down the ridge when the ground shuddered beneath her, deeper than any pulse of backlash, and she turned in time to see the machine's core flare white — brighter than it had ever burned, bright enough to throw the queen's shadow huge and wavering across the hillside — and in that light she heard, faintly, impossibly, a sound she knew.

Laughter.

Rell's laughter, wild and defiant and utterly unafraid, ringing out from the heart of the machine as it went nova-bright against the twelfth moon.
