## 31. Rell's Stand

The machine sang.

That was the worst of it, Rell thought — that it was beautiful. He had expected the queen's great severing engine to groan and clank like a siege ram, all iron and cruelty. Instead it *sang*, a high silver note that threaded through the chaos of the battlefield like a needle through cloth, and every time it swelled, another thread of light tore loose from the severed bridge across the valley and drifted upward like a dandelion seed on the wind.

He crouched behind an overturned supply wagon at the edge of the crown camp, his captain's cloak traded weeks ago for a smuggler's grey, and watched the thing work. It stood in the center of the army's ring like a heart in a ribcage of pikes: a lattice of white metal taller than three men, shaped — and this was the detail that had kept him awake for two nights — shaped like a tree. A bare tree in winter, branches spreading, and from every branch hung a slender glass vessel, and in every vessel floated a thread of light.

People. The threads were people. Or what was left of them after the siphons had finished.

Rell had grown up believing the crown protected things. That had been the whole grammar of his life: protect the valley, protect the road, protect the harvest, protect the people who could not protect themselves. His father had said it over the supper table until the words wore grooves into him, and when his father died Rell had simply climbed inside the groove and let it carry him — through the academy, through the border patrols, through the day they pinned the captain's knot on his shoulder and told him what the hunt was for.

*The gifted are to be taken alive.* He had repeated those orders to himself like a prayer for years, telling himself that alive meant safe, that the crown must have a reason, that reasons existed somewhere above his pay grade in rooms he would never see.

Then he had seen the rooms.

* * *

Three days ago, before the army marched to the bridge, he had been marched through the machine-camp as a prisoner — disgraced, arrested, his sword taken and his name struck from the rolls. They had not killed him. That should have been his first warning. Instead they had given him a tent, a meal, and an audience with the queen's artificer, a narrow woman named Vess whose fingers were stained permanently silver from the machine's metals.

"You covered their escape," Vess had said, not unkindly. "The heir and her guardian. We know."

"Then kill me," Rell had said, "or stop wasting my time."

"Oh, we don't want you dead." She had led him out under the awning where the glass vessels hung in their rows, dozens of them, each one holding its small captive light. "We want you to understand what you helped run from. Look at them. Do you know what they are?"

He had looked. He had wanted to look away and found he could not. One of the vessels held a light that flickered in a rhythm he almost recognized — quick, quick, slow — like a laugh he'd heard once across a garden wall.

"The gifted burn too hot," Vess said. "Magic is a fever, Captain. It got into this kingdom thirteen years ago when the old covenant broke, and it has been killing us ever since — the blight, the stillbirths, the rivers running wrong. The queen is not cruel. She is a physician. This machine takes the fever out of the ones who carry it, and uses the heat to close the wound between the worlds forever. No more magic. No more plague. Everyone lives."

"And them?" His voice had come out strange. "The ones in the glass?"

Vess had paused, and he had given her credit for that pause, later, in the dark, when there was very little left to give anyone credit for.

"They live," she said. "Mostly. They won't remember much. But they'll be *clean*."

Clean. He had turned the word over all night in his tent, turning it like a stone with something dead underneath. Clean of what? Of the shimmer Wendell used to leave on leaves when she thought no one was watching? Of the way she could coax a stubborn vine along a trellis just by humming to it? Of everything that made her *her*?

He remembered being nine years old, and Wendell Thornbury — seven, gap-toothed, ferocious — dragging him by the wrist into the guild garden because he was crying about his father leaving for the border patrol, and pressing his palm flat against the trunk of the old pear tree and saying, *feel that, it's breathing, everything's breathing if you hold still long enough.* And he had held still. And for one impossible moment he had felt it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, and stopped crying.

He had spent twenty years telling himself he'd imagined it.

He had spent three days knowing he hadn't.

* * *

Now the army was at the bridge, and the machine was singing, and the sky over the valley was coming apart in slow bright seams.

Rell moved through the camp the way he'd been trained to move through enemy ground: low, patient, invisible. The guards' attention was fixed outward, toward the bridge, toward the figures standing at its far end — the heir and her guardian, small against the vast broken arch of light, four keystones arranged around them like the points of a compass. The queen herself stood on a raised platform beneath her banners, watching, her face lit silver by the machine's glow. Rell had never seen the queen in person before. He had expected grandeur. What he saw instead was exhaustion so profound it looked like grief, a woman staring at a wound she genuinely believed she was closing.

She wasn't wrong about everything. That was the trap of it. The blight was real. The dying villages were real. Rell had ridden through the Road of Ash himself, chasing Wendell, and seen crops that grew in the shapes of forests they used to be, and rivers running backward, and children born silent in towns where no child had been born loud in years. The kingdom *was* sick. The covenant's breaking *had* done it — Isandro had told him that much, in the market town, in the brief mad hour when they had all been on the same side.

But the cure — the cure was a row of glass vessels full of stolen light, and a machine built like a winter tree, and a forever with no magic in it anywhere, which meant no more pear trees breathing under a little girl's hand, ever, for anyone, in either world.

Rell reached the edge of the machine's lattice and pressed himself against the cold white metal.

Up close it did not sing anymore. Up close it *screamed*, in a register only the bones could hear, and he understood suddenly that the song was a lie the machine told outward while inward it did its terrible work. The nearest vessel hung at his eye level, and inside it the thread of light pressed against the glass like a face against a window.

He knew her. Not Wendell — someone else, a girl from the free town, a candle-mage who had shown him how to light his pipe without flint and laughed at his astonishment. Her name had been Sera. Her light was dimming even as he watched, drawn down through the lattice's roots into the machine's heart, and from the heart up through the branches into the sky, feeding the tear in the world, widening it, hastening the severing.

The moon overhead was nearly full. The twelfth turn. One night left, and the bridge stood open for that night only, and the machine was going to eat the open bridge before anyone could cross it and speak whatever needed speaking.

Unless the machine stopped singing.

Rell put his hand flat against the lattice, the way a seven-year-old girl had once taught him to touch a tree, and held still, and listened.

And the machine — God help him — the machine breathed back.

* * *

There was a core. Of course there was a core; Vess had been proud of it, showing him the schematics in that awful silver-fingered lecture, pointing out the heartwood of the winter tree where the harvested light was bound into a single knot before being fed to the sky. *The keystone of the whole design,* she had called it, meaning nothing by the word, or everything. *Strike that and the severing fails — but no one strikes it, Captain, because the backlash unbinds everything stored near it. Every memory we've siphoned. Every thread. It doesn't destroy them, precisely. It releases them. All at once. Into whoever is closest.*

Into whoever is closest.

He had asked her, then, in a voice he'd kept very level: released how?

*Scattered,* she had said. *Poured into the nearest mind like water into a cup. Most minds break. The gifted sometimes survive it, they're built to hold more than one life — but a plain man, an ordinary soldier—* she had shrugged, elegant and terrible, *—an ordinary soldier would lose himself. Everything he is, washed out and replaced by a flood of strangers' remembering.*

So that was the price. There was always a price; he was learning that about this whole wretched business, that every door in it opened onto a tollbooth. Wendell was standing at the bridge right now preparing to pay hers — he didn't know the details, but he knew Wendell, and he had watched her face these past weeks whenever the keystones showed her anything, and he knew the shape of dread when he saw it. She was going to give up something enormous. Something that would hollow her. He had understood that much without being told, the way you understand weather coming.

And here was the joke — here was the thing that made him want to laugh, actually laugh, out loud, in the middle of the queen's army — here was the thing:

He could pay it for her.

Not her price. He couldn't carry that; it wasn't his to carry, it was braided into the covenant itself, into names and realms and moons. But this one. This one was his. The machine's core, the flood of strangers' memories, the washing-away of everything he was — an ordinary soldier's price, paid by an ordinary soldier, except that the ordinary soldier happened to love the covenant's heir and had loved her since she was seven years old and fierce and gap-toothed, and had spent twenty years pretending otherwise because duty came first, duty always came first, duty was the groove he climbed inside—

Duty. Yes. Let it be duty. Let him frame it in the old grammar, protect the valley, protect the harvest, protect the people who cannot protect themselves. Let him stand here with his hand on the screaming white metal and tell himself it was his oath, finally, at last, correctly aimed.

He knew better. He knew it was the pear tree. He knew it was a girl saying *feel that, it's breathing.*

* * *

The battle came to him rather than the other way around. He heard it first — the horn from the bridge, the roar of the front line surging forward, Isandro's voice rising over it all in a language that made the air itself stand at attention. Then the camp erupted, guards peeling away toward the fight, and the space around the machine emptied as though a hand had swept it clean.

Rell went up the lattice.

It was built for climbing, that was the obscenity of it — handholds and footholds worked into the white metal like the branches of the tree it imitated, as if the artificers had wanted their engine to be beautiful even in function. He climbed past vessel after vessel, and in each one a dimming light, and he could not help them, not now, not one by one, there was no time and no way and his hands were shaking and he climbed anyway, higher, toward the heartwood where the light gathered thick as honey.

Sera's vessel hung near the top. As he passed it her thread pressed against the glass again, and whether it was recognition or only the random motion of dying light he would never know, but he stopped, one arm hooked through a branch of the machine, and put his palm against her prison.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry I took so long to pick a side."

Then he climbed the last few arms to the core.

It sat in the crook of the highest branches like a fruit: a sphere of woven light, dense and slow-turning, threaded through with filaments of white metal that pulsed in time with the scream Rell felt in his teeth. Around it the air smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Below him the battle raged at the bridge — he could see Wendell now, a small figure with her hands raised, plants erupting around her in defiance of every law the valley had taught her, and beside her the guardian with his bark-scarred arms spread wide, holding back the sky.

She would remember him. That was the arithmetic of it, the cruel simple sum. When the core broke and the flood came, everything Rell was would wash out of him — his father's voice over supper, the academy yard, the smell of his first horse, twenty years of patrol roads and frostbitten mornings and the captain's knot on his shoulder. And Wendell. All of Wendell. The garden wall. The pear tree. The taste of stolen apples. The way she said his name like it was a place she'd lived.

She would keep those. He would not. She would walk out of this night carrying both their shares of him, and he would wake tomorrow a stranger to his own life, and some part of him — the part that was already grieving, already standing at his own graveside taking notes — understood that this was not a tragedy. It was a trade. The cheapest trade anyone had offered him in years.

Because the covenant was going to ask her to forget too. He didn't know the details, but he knew the shape, and the shape was this: she would pay with the thing she loved most, and the thing she loved most was standing next to her at the bridge with bark on his arms. She would forget Isandro. She might forget the whole journey, the whole awakening, the whole wild year of becoming what she was always meant to be.

But she would not forget Rell. Whatever else the price took, the childhood was hers — the wall, the apples, the pear tree breathing. Unless the flood took it from the other end. Unless the last person in the world who remembered being nine years old and crying under a pear tree was the one who broke the machine.

Someone had to keep it. Someone had to hold the memory of her whole life so that when she came out the far side of her sacrifice, hollowed and new, there would be one person left who knew who she had been before. And if that person couldn't remember her afterward — if keeping the memory meant losing the keeper — well.

Well. Duty finally aligned with love. It had only taken thirty-one years and the end of the world.

Rell laughed. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him like water from a struck spring, helpless and huge, and the nearest guards — the few who had stayed — turned and stared at the madman clinging to the top of the queen's engine, laughing at the sky.

"What?" he called down to them, grinning, tears cutting through the grime on his face. "You've never seen a man do his job?"

He wrapped both hands around the core.

It was warm. That undid him a little — that the thing was warm, that all that stolen light had a body-heat to it, that Sera and the candle-girl and the hundred others in their glass vessels were in there, pressed together, alive enough to be warm. He thought: *I'm sorry. I'm sorry it ends like this, spilled and scattered. I hope some of you find your ways home.*

He thought: *Wendell Thornbury, feel that, it's breathing.*

He pulled.

The core resisted for one heartbeat — one long, singing, screaming heartbeat in which Rell felt the whole machine notice him, feel his grip, understand his intention with a kind of dumb animal clarity — and then the filaments parted like stems, and the sphere of woven light came free in his hands, and the world went white.

* * *

The flood took him the way a river takes a dropped lantern.

He had braced for pain and there was none, which was somehow worse. There was only opening — every door in him flung wide at once, and through the doors came everyone. A shepherd's forty summers on a hillside he'd never seen. A midwife's hands, remembering ten thousand births. Sera, laughing, lighting his pipe with a snap of her fingers, over and over, a dozen different firesides. A boy in the drowned chapel singing to fish. An old woman's whole vocabulary of herbs. Griefs. Weddings. Names beyond counting, pouring through him, filling him, replacing him, the lantern-glass dissolving even as the flame passed through—

And underneath it all, sinking last, going down like a coin in deep water: a garden wall. Green apples, sour and perfect. A gap-toothed girl with dirt on her nose saying *feel that, it's breathing, everything's breathing if you hold still long enough.*

Hold still, then. He held still. He held still inside the flood with the last of himself cupped around that single memory the way you cup a match in wind, and he felt it go — felt the wall blur, the apples fade, the girl's voice thin into a sweetness he could no longer attach to any name —

— and he laughed. Somewhere, in whatever he was now, in the white and the pouring and the endless strangers' morning, something that had been Captain Rell laughed, because it had worked, because the machine's scream was dying, because below him the great winter tree was going dark branch by branch and the glass vessels were cracking open one by one like buds, releasing their lights into a sky that was beginning, very slowly, to knit itself closed.

The bridge still stood. He couldn't see it anymore, couldn't see anything, but he could feel it the way you feel a held breath — the whole world holding its breath at the threshold of the twelfth moon, waiting for two people to choose.

*Choose well,* he tried to say, and had no mouth to say it with, and said it anyway, into the flood, into the light. *Choose well, Wendell. And when you forget — and you will forget, one of us always does —*

*— plant something.*

The last of him went out like a lamp going down at dawn: not extinguished, just overtaken, the flame indistinguishable at last from the growing daylight all around it.

On the field below, the machine stood silent for the first time since it had been built, its branches bare, its vessels empty, its song finished. And in the crook of its highest branches sat a man in a smuggler's grey coat, eyes open, smiling at a sky he no longer recognized, waiting — patiently, the way gardeners wait — to find out who he was going to be.

Far off at the bridge, the twelfth moon reached its height, and the keystones began to sing.
