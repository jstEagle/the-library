## 32. The Twelfth Moon

The bridge stood open for one night only.

Wendell felt it before she saw it — a change in the air along the border, the way the pressure shifts before a storm breaks, or the way a held breath finally releases. For thirteen years this stretch of the river had been nothing: gray water, gray stone, the severed stumps of light-arches jutting from either bank like broken teeth. Now the space between them trembled with something that was almost sound and almost color, and where the bridge had been cut, the cut was knitting itself closed — slowly, reluctantly, like flesh remembering how to heal.

She stood at the human edge with her boots in the mud and watched it happen, and she could not make herself take the first step.

Behind her, the plain was full of firelight. The queen's army had made camp in a great crescent, close enough that Wendell could hear the creak of harness leather and the low murmur of two thousand soldiers who had come to watch a miracle or a catastrophe and did not know which they would get. The machine that had torn at the border sat silent now, its core shattered, its caged gifted freed into the dark — some of them wandering the camp in a daze, some already asleep by the fires like children after a long illness. Rell was somewhere out there too. She had seen him carried to the healers' tents an hour ago, laughing still, though the laugh had gone strange and empty at the edges, and when he'd looked at her he had looked at her the way a man looks at a kind stranger.

He didn't remember her name. He remembered his oath, his rank, the shape of his sword in his hand. Everything else — the creek behind the guild wall, the stolen apples, the summer they were nine — had gone into the machine's breaking, spent like coin so that tonight could happen at all.

*This is what it costs,* she thought. *This is what it has always cost.*

And tonight she would pay it herself. Willingly. With open eyes.

"Wendell."

Isandro's voice came from just behind her shoulder, soft as falling leaves. He had learned, over these long months, exactly how quietly to approach her — not because she startled easily, but because there were moments when she needed to be approached at all, and he had made himself patient enough to wait for them.

She didn't turn. "It's opening faster than your books said it would."

"Books are written by people who never stood here." He came up beside her, close enough that their sleeves touched. In the trembling light of the half-made bridge, his bark-scarring caught silver, the ridges along his jaw and forearms gleaming like wet wood. He looked tired down to the bone. They both did. The last three days had been fire and running and Rell's ruin and the queen's long, stricken silence on her hilltop, and neither of them had slept more than an hour at a time since the machine woke.

But the keystones were whole. All four of them, wrapped in cloth against Isandro's chest, humming together now for the first time in thirteen years — a chord so low Wendell felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her back teeth, in the soles of her feet, as if the ground itself had begun to remember a song.

"The moon," she said.

Above the eastern ridge, fat and white and one night short of full, the twelfth moon was climbing. Tomorrow night it would complete its turn, and whatever happened here tonight — whether the covenant was reforged or whether the realms tore apart forever — would be decided under this moon or no moon at all.

Isandro followed her gaze. "We have until it sets."

"That's six hours."

"Five, if the sky stays clear." He paused. "You've counted too."

"I've been counting since the ninth moon." She laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and cracked. "Since before that, maybe. Since the garden. Since you fell through my gate bleeding and said my name like you'd been carrying it a long way."

He reached over and took her hand. His palm was warm, warmer than a human's, warmer than it should have been, and the faint green shimmer that lived in her skin rose to meet his touch the way water rises to meet rain. When their hands joined, the grass at their feet leaned toward them — both of them, together, a slow green bow — and somewhere behind them in the camp, a freed captive lifted her head as if she'd heard music.

"We don't have to do this," Isandro said quietly. "I need you to hear me say it one more time, with the bridge actually open, with the moon actually up. We could walk away. North, past the ash country, past the free towns. There are places the crown will never reach now. We could live."

"And the realms bleed into each other until both of them die."

"Yes."

"And everyone we freed goes back into cages when the panic comes again, because the panic always comes again."

"Yes."

"And my mother's sacrifice, and my father's, and Rell's — all of it for nothing."

He was silent a moment. "Yes," he said again, softer. "All of that. And I am telling you anyway, because you asked me once never to lie to you about the price, and I intend to keep that promise even now. Especially now."

Wendell turned to look at him at last.

She had tried, in the dark hours of the last week, to imagine what it would be like — the moment after. Not the speaking of the covenant; she had rehearsed that until the words lived in her mouth like her own heartbeat. But the moment *after*, the morning after, when she woke in a valley she no longer knew was magicless and found no stranger's warmth beside her, no memory of why her hands knew certain plants, why she flinched at the smell of woodsmoke, why she sometimes said a name in her sleep that no one around her recognized. She had tried to imagine being that woman, the gardener who forgot, and every time she tried, some animal part of her mind slammed the door shut and refused.

That was the terror. Not death. Death would be simple. This was worse than death: it was dying while someone else went on living inside your life, tending your griefs without knowing they were yours.

"I'm frightened," she said. It seemed important to say it plainly, here at the threshold, with open eyes. That was the whole point of the covenant her mother had described. *Two who choose the price with open eyes.* Fear was allowed. Fear was honest. What the old covenant had done — what the first guardian had fled from, what had broken everything — was pretend the price could be dodged, hidden, paid by trickery instead of consent.

"So am I," Isandro said.

"You're not. You remember everything. You lose nothing. For you this is a door closing on one room and opening onto another."

"It isn't." His grip tightened on her hand. "Wendell. Listen to me. If the covenant takes you tomorrow — if it strips every memory of me out of you and leaves you standing in that valley whole and safe and *stranger to me* — do you know what I will be? I will be a man who got everything he wanted. The realms saved. The covenant reforged. My people safe behind a healed border. And none of it worth anything, because the one person I crossed a dying world to find won't know my face. I will spend the rest of my life loving someone who looks at me with courtesy. That is not losing nothing. That is the worst thing I can imagine, and I have imagined it every night since the marsh, since I stood in the forgetting-water and felt my own name dissolve, and understood — really understood — what I was asking you to pay."

The wind moved along the river. Out over the water, the half-built bridge shivered, its unformed light rippling like a reflection disturbed by a thrown stone.

"You knew," Wendell said. "In the garden. From the first day. You knew the price and you let me fall in love with you anyway."

"Yes." He didn't look away. "And I have hated myself for it every day since, and I would do it again, because the alternative was letting the world end alone. Both things are true. I've stopped pretending they aren't."

She studied his face — the scarring, the exhaustion, the eyes that were older than any human's eyes and yet, somehow, younger than hers right now, because hers had aged years in months. She thought about the drowned chapel and the wraith wearing her parents' faces. She thought about the conservatory, and the woman with her mother's hands who hadn't known her. She thought about her mother's voice, sealed in memory, unlocked under the ninth moon:

*They took the price from one and called it duty. It failed because duty is not the same as gift. Find another way, little sprout. Make it a gift. Make it shared. Two who choose it with open eyes — not because the covenant demands, but because love agrees.*

Love agrees.

Her heart was hammering so hard she could feel it in her throat. But underneath the terror — underneath, tangled with it, inseparable from it — was something else. Something that had been growing in her all this road-long year, quietly, the way her gift grew things: without effort, without permission, simply because it was alive and she kept choosing to tend it.

She loved him. She had loved him since the garden, since the keystone hummed under her fingers, since the boundary flooded her with magic and he had knelt in the mud beside her and talked her through the drowning of it, hour after hour, never touching her without asking first. She loved him in the ordinary ways and the impossible ones, and the covenant was going to take him out of her memory like a splinter drawn from the heart, and she was going to let it, and the only reason she could bear it — the only reason it was bearable at all — was that it wasn't a theft. It was a gift. Her gift. Freely given, with open eyes, to two worlds that needed it more than she needed her own happiness.

Terror and tenderness, at the threshold, holding hands.

"All right," she said.

Isandro went very still. "All right?"

"All right. Let's go say the words." She turned to face the bridge, and her voice steadied as she spoke, finding ground beneath itself. "Before I lose my nerve. Before the moon changes its mind. Before the queen on that hill decides mercy was a mistake and sends what's left of her army down here. Right now, while I'm terrified and tender and certain, all three at once. Because my mother said it has to be all three, Isandro. Not courage without fear — that's just ignorance. Not fear without tenderness — that's just duty, and duty is what broke it the first time. All three. Open eyes. So we go now, while I have them."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he bowed his head, and pressed her hand to his forehead, the way his people greeted a guardian — the gesture he had told her once was reserved for oaths and griefs and the naming of children.

"As you say," he whispered. "Heir of Thornbury. Gardener of the covenant. As you say."

---

They walked out onto the bridge together.

The mud gave way to stone at the waterline — old stone, foundation-stone, the bones of the original crossing that had survived the severing — and then the stone gave way to light. Not solid light, not yet; the bridge was still knitting itself, and where the arches had been cut their regrowth hung in the air like frost forming in fast motion, patterns blooming and dissolving, the two realms reaching toward each other across the gap and not quite meeting. Walking on it was like walking on the surface of a frozen lake that remembers being water. Every step sang. Every step gave a little, and held.

Below them the river ran strange and slow, thick with reflected stars that shouldn't have been there — stars from both skies at once, the human constellations and the other ones, overlapping, arguing, gradually learning each other's names. Wendell kept her eyes forward. If she looked down too long she would think about falling, and if she thought about falling she would think about what waited at the far end of the crossing, and if she thought about that she would stop walking, and she could not stop walking. Not now. Not with the moon climbing and the words already rising in her throat like sap in spring.

Halfway across, the four keystones began to sing.

Isandro unwrapped them from the cloth at the center of the span, where the old covenant-stone still stood — a waist-high pillar, cracked through its middle, its carvings worn nearly smooth. Thirteen years of weather had gotten into the crack. But when he set the four stones upon it, one at each quarter, the crack began to glow, and the singing swelled from a hum to a chord to something that was almost language, and Wendell felt it move through her body like a tide coming in.

She knew the words. Her mother had given them to her, sealed in memory, and she had carried them through ash country and free towns and palace corridors and war, and now they came up out of her the way water comes up out of a well — not recited but *drawn*, pulled by the stones' thirst.

She looked at Isandro across the covenant-stone. The light of the bridge turned his face silver and green. He was crying. She realized distantly that she was too, had been for some time, the tears cold on her cheeks in the river wind.

"Together?" she asked.

"Together," he said. "On the third line. Where the old rite splits into giver and receiver — we speak both parts. Both of us give. Both of us receive. That's the whole difference, Wendell. That's the whole new thing your mother saw. Say it like you mean to keep it."

"I mean to keep it."

The moon stood directly overhead now, impossibly bright, one turn from full. Down on the plain, the army had gone quiet — two thousand people watching a bridge of light, and not one of them making a sound. Somewhere among the fires, Wendell was almost sure, a woman with her mother's hands was watching too, though she wouldn't know why her chest ached.

Wendell laid her palms flat on the covenant-stone. Isandro covered them with his own. The stone was warm as loam in sun, warm as a living thing, and through it she could feel the four keystones pulsing like hearts.

She spoke the first line, and Isandro's voice joined hers on the second word, and the bridge blazed.

*"What was sundered by fear, let fear no longer hold—"*

The crack in the stone filled with light. The river below stopped moving, every star in it fixed and burning.

*"What was bought with one grief, let two griefs mend—"*

The keystones rose a finger's width off the pillar, turning slowly, humming in harmony, and Wendell felt the first tug at the edges of her mind — gentle, questioning, like a hand testing a door. *Not yet,* she told it. *Hear us first.*

*"What was taken in secret, let it be given in sight—"*

Her mother's voice, in memory, saying: *open eyes, little sprout, open eyes.* The tug grew stronger. The bridge arched overhead at last, complete, a roof of woven light spanning the whole gap, and beyond it — visible now for the first time in thirteen years — the far shore of Isandro's realm, trees taller than cathedrals, leaves silver in a moon that was also this moon, one moon shared between two worlds.

*"Two who choose the price with open eyes—"*

Here Isandro's voice broke, and Wendell's carried the line alone, and she found she was smiling through the tears, because this was the line her mother had written, the line that changed everything, the line that meant the price was not a wound but a wedding-gift:

*"—give it freely, freely, freely, and let love agree."*

The keystones rang like bells.

The light went through her.

It did not hurt. That was the terrible, tender surprise of it — it did not hurt at all. It was like the moment at the boundary when magic had flooded back into her, overwhelming and beautiful, except this time what flooded her was *leaving*: every memory tied to what she loved most, drawn upward through her palms into the stone, gently, gently, the way her gift drew a seedling from its husk. She felt the garden go first — the walled garden, the gate, the smell of thyme after rain — and then the guild records burning, and then the tunnels, and then—

—and then Isandro's hand tightened on hers, and she held on, and she did not look away from his face, because open eyes, open eyes, all the way to the end—

His mouth was moving. Saying her name. Saying something else, something urgent, something that sounded like it mattered more than anything—

The realms knit closed and whole around the wound.

The moon completed nothing. The bridge held. The covenant, reforged, took what it was given.

And Wendell Thornbury, heir of the guild, gardener of two worlds, stood on the border of everything with her eyes open and her hand in a stranger's hand, and began to forget.

* * *

Far away, on a hill above a quiet army, the Queen of the human realm lowered the spyglass she had not used in an hour, because there was nothing left to watch but light, and light needed no watching.

Beside her, wrapped in a soldier's cloak, the woman with the familiar hands stirred in her sleep and murmured a name — a young woman's name, a gardener's name — and did not wake, and did not know why she was weeping.

And on the bridge, in the last of the twelfth moon's light, Isandro held on to a hand that was slowly, gently, letting go of him — and bent close, and whispered the thing he had been trying to say before the covenant took her hearing of it, the thing he would spend a year, and a garden, and everything he had, finding a way to say again:

"When you forget me, forget me slowly. I'll be waiting in the flowers."
