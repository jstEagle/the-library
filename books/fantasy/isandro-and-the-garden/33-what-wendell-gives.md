## 33. What Wendell Gives

The bridge held open like a held breath.

Wendell stood at its center with Isandro's hand in hers and the four keystones arranged around them in a ring of pale light, each one humming a different note — the drowned chapel's stone low as a bell underwater, the conservatory's bright and bitter, the marsh-stone whispering, the wraith's stone silent in the way that graves are silent. Together their voices braided into something almost like music, almost like a name being spoken very slowly by the world itself.

One night. The bridge would stand for one night only, and the moon above it was already leaning toward the horizon, fat and white and merciless, counting down the hours she had left to be herself.

She had thought, walking up through the broken ranks of the crown's army, past the smoking ruin of the machine, past Rell standing bloodied and grinning amid the wreckage of the thing he'd died a little to kill — she had thought the terror would be the worst part. It wasn't. The worst part was how quiet it was now. How the wind moved through the bridge's light without sound. How Isandro's thumb traced circles on her knuckles, slow and steady, as if they were only two people waiting out a storm on a porch.

"You're shaking," he said.

"I'm cold."

"You're not cold. You've never been cold a day since you crossed the boundary." His voice was gentle, and it was the gentleness that made her eyes sting. "You're allowed to be afraid, Wendell. I'd think less of you if you weren't."

"I'm not afraid of dying." She looked at their hands, at the bark-textured scarring along his wrist where his own realm's magic had marked him years ago, at the dirt under her own nails that no amount of travel had ever managed to scrub away. A gardener's hands. They would stay a gardener's hands, even when everything else was gone. That was something. She clung to it like a root clinging to a riverbank. "I'm afraid of forgetting that I was ever afraid. Of losing even the fear. Of waking up tomorrow with nothing missing and not knowing there was ever anything to miss."

Isandro turned to face her fully. In the bridge's light his eyes were the color of deep water over old stone, and she let herself look at him — really look, greedily, cataloguing him the way she'd once catalogued seeds: the line of his jaw, the scar through his eyebrow from the chapel wraith, the way his hair fell forward when he bowed his head. *Remember this*, she told herself, knowing even as she did it what a useless prayer it was. Memory was not a seed packet. It could not be saved against winter.

"Wendell," he said. "Tell me again why."

It was the fourth time he'd asked, and she understood why he kept asking. He wanted to carry the answer with him. He wanted, when the moon completed its turn and the covenant sealed and she looked at him across a garden wall without recognition, to have some part of her reasoning preserved somewhere outside her own head — a cutting taken before the frost.

"Because my mother tried to pay this price alone," Wendell said, "and it broke her, and it broke the covenant anyway, because a covenant paid by one is just a wound dressed as a bargain. Because the first guardian loved someone enough to refuse the price, and her refusal killed the valley's magic and my parents and thirteen years of blight. Because every road we've walked since the garden has shown me the same truth wearing different clothes: love that is withheld from the cost costs more. And because—" her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding things from him now seemed obscene— "because I choose you. Not instead of the world. *For* the world. There's a difference, and my mother knew it, and I know it too."

The keystones flared softly at the words, as if the old covenant itself were listening. Perhaps it was. Perhaps it had always been listening, all these years, holding its breath inside the broken stones, waiting for an heir who would speak to it plainly.

* * *

They had prepared the rite as her mother's message instructed, word by word, in the days between the eleventh moon and this one. There was no grand liturgy. The guild had never been a church; it had been a garden, and gardens run on practical things. Water. Light. Willing hands.

Wendell knelt first, at the center of the keystone ring, and pressed both palms flat to the bridge's surface. The light came up through her skin warm as sun-warmed soil, and beneath it she felt the two realms the way she felt the roots of an old tree through the floor of a cellar — vast, tangled, wounded, alive. On one side, Isandro's realm: silver forests and singing rivers, cities grown rather than built, a whole world slowly starving as the border scarred over. On the other, her valley: brown fields, poisoned wells, children born these thirteen years without so much as a flicker of the gift, a kingdom so frightened of magic it had built a machine to strangle it forever.

Two realms bleeding out from the same wound. And the wound was a memory that had never been paid.

"The covenant asks," Isandro said, kneeling opposite her, his hands covering hers on the light. His voice took on the cadence of the old words, the ones the second keystone had shown them, spoken plainly at last: "*What do you bring?*"

"I bring the heir's portion," Wendell answered. Her voice didn't shake. She was oddly proud of that. "All memory bound to what I love most. Freely given, with open eyes, for the knitting of the realms."

"And what do you love most?"

And there it was. The question at the heart of everything, the question she had walked four hundred miles and lost a grandmother and a childhood friend and nearly herself to answer.

She looked at Isandro.

"I love most the man who came stumbling through my gate with bark on his skin and a dying stone in his hand," she said, "who called me by a name no one uses, who taught me that a gift is not a curse and a covenant is not a cage. I love the garden we will never plant together. I love the mornings I will never have. I love him with the part of me that is oldest, the part that was gardener before I was born, and I give every memory of him back to the covenant that made us both — and I do it *willingly*, and I want it written into the stones that I was not forced, and I was not tricked, and I did not look away."

The ring of keystones blazed. Somewhere far below the bridge, she felt the two realms shudder toward each other like sleeping animals scenting home.

Isandro's composure cracked then — finally, blessedly, after all his guardian's steadiness — and he leaned forward and pressed his forehead to hers, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, because there was nothing language could do that touch hadn't already done better.

"When it takes it," he whispered, "it won't hurt. The wraith showed me. It's like falling asleep in a garden. You'll just... set something down, and stand up lighter, and walk away."

"I know."

"You'll rebuild the guild. Your grandmother's records are gone but your hands remember the craft, and hands are deeper than heads. You'll be happy, Wendell. I need you to know that. Whatever I am to you afterward — a stranger, a new gardener, nothing at all — you'll be *happy*. That's not a consolation. That's the design of the thing. The covenant doesn't take joy. Only the remembering."

"I know," she said again, and then, because honesty was the whole point, the entire architecture of this night: "But I don't want to be happy if happiness means never having known you. Isn't that terrible? We've come all this way to save two worlds and I'm standing here grieving in advance like a selfish child."

"It's not selfish." He pulled back far enough to look at her, and he was smiling, and the smile was wrecked and radiant. "It's the truest thing anyone's said on this bridge tonight. Grieve in advance. Grieve loudly. I'll hold it for you. I'll hold all of it — every hour, every argument, every time you fell asleep mid-sentence talking about soil composition. Someone should keep the archive."

"Someone should," she managed, laughing and crying at once, which she was learning was simply what love did at the end of things.

* * *

The moon touched the hills.

There was no more time for talk, and perhaps that was mercy. Wendell settled herself at the center of the ring, and Isandro knelt behind her with his arms around her shoulders and his chin resting on top of her head, the way they had slept on the Road of Ash when the nights were cold and the poisoned land moaned around them. Her mother's message had said the price must be paid in contact — *two who choose it with open eyes* — and so they had chosen this: she would pay, and he would witness, and his heartbeat against her spine would be the last thing the remembering-Wendell ever knew.

"Say it with me," he murmured.

Together, in the old tongue the keystones had taught them, they spoke the closing words:

"*What was sundered, let it knit. What was stolen, let it return. What was owed, let it be paid — freely, wholly, and in love.*"

The keystones rose.

They lifted from the bridge's surface and began to turn around her, slowly at first, then faster, four stones orbiting the place where she knelt, trailing light like comets' tails, and the light reached for her — not violently, not like the machine had torn at the caged gifted, but gently, the way morning reaches through a window. It found the threads of her memories and began, with infinite care, to sort them.

She felt it find the valley first. Every shimmer her hands had ever left on living things. The smell of her grandmother's drying shed. The proclamation nailed to the market post, the ink still wet. The night the guild tunnels breathed cold air on her face as hunters burned the garden above. All of it lifting free, glowing, offered up — and she let it go, thread by thread, feeling each one leave not as loss but as release, like deadheading a spent bloom so the plant could flower again elsewhere.

Then it found Isandro.

Oh, it found *everything*.

The gate. The first sight of him crumpled among her roses, bark-skinned and bleeding light. The hum of the keystone under her fingers, answering something in her blood she hadn't known was there. Crossing the boundary and feeling magic flood back into her like a river returning to a dried bed — overwhelming, beautiful, frightening, *his face watching her take her first real breath in thirteen years*. The drowned chapel and the wraith wearing her parents' faces. The market town where an old woman had gripped her wrist and said, *the covenant demands a price, child, and your guild knew it*. The offer Rell made in the lantern-light, and her refusal, and the terrible tenderness of a friend choosing treason. The queen's conservatory, and the woman with her mother's hands. The bridge of light severed thirteen years ago. The confession under the ninth moon — *he has known since the garden* — and the black fury of walking away from him, and the marsh, and finding him kneeling in the forgetting-water with her name gone from his mouth, and the long nights of telling him their story piece by piece, rebuilding him, *remembering for two*—

"No," she heard herself say, small and human, gripping his forearm hard enough to bruise. "No, wait, not that one, not the marsh, please, that one's mine, I earned that one—"

"I have it," Isandro whispered against her hair, and his voice was breaking and whole at once. "I have all of it. Every single one. I'll keep them safe. I *swear* it, Wendell, on the stones, on the garden, on everything either of us has ever loved — you are not lost. You are *kept*."

The light paused. The whole bridge paused. Even the moon seemed to lean closer, and Wendell understood, in the last clear moment of her old life, that the covenant was giving her a courtesy it had never given anyone: a final breath between the taking. A threshold moment. An open door.

Her mother had stood in a doorway like this once, and stepped through alone, and the stepping-through-alone had failed.

Wendell laced her fingers through Isandro's. She turned her head and kissed his knuckles, the scarred bark-ridge of his wrist, tasting salt that might have been his tears or hers or both.

"With open eyes," she said. "Both of us. Always both."

Then she opened her hands, palms up, empty, the oldest gesture her guild knew — the gesture of a gardener offering seed to soil — and said, clearly, so the stones would carry it down through the roots of both worlds forever:

"*Take it. I give it gladly. Grow something good with it.*"

The light came down like rain.

* * *

It did not hurt.

He had told her it wouldn't, and he was right, and somehow that was the cruelest thing of all — that the surrender of everything she was would feel like lying back in warm grass at the end of a long day's work. The memories rose out of her and did not tear; they *transplanted*, root systems intact, lifted from her keeping and carried across the bridge into the mind of the man who held her, and she felt them go the way a tree feels its seeds go on the wind: with grief, and with a strange fierce pride, and with the unshakable knowledge that nothing truly ends that was ever planted well.

The valley went first, and then the fear, and then the hunt, and then the road, and then —

and then him, all of him, every hour, going out of her like light going out of a lamp at dawn, so gradually and so gently that she could not mark the moment it finished, only that at some point she was kneeling on a bridge of light with her hands full of nothing, beside a man whose name she did not know, and the moon was setting, and the world was whole.

Below her, felt rather than seen, the realms knit closed.

She felt it happen in her body before her mind could form any thought about it: the great wound in the fabric of things drawing shut like soil closing over a planted seed, the two realms settling against each other not as strangers but as neighbors, old roots crossing the border freely for the first time in thirteen years. Magic moving back into the valley like water finding its old channel. Balance, everywhere, at last — the whole vast garden of the world coming into season after a long and killing winter.

Around her, the four keystones sang one final chord and went dark, becoming merely stones, their work done for another generation or another age. The bridge of light softened, thinned, became a road, became a path, became a green track winding down into a valley that smelled, suddenly, overwhelmingly, of rain and growing things.

The man behind her loosened his hold. She felt him hesitate — felt, without understanding why, the enormous care in the way he withdrew his arms, the way a person moves around something precious and breakable.

"Are you well?" he asked.

His voice was rough. Strangers' voices aren't usually rough, she thought distantly, but the thought slid off her like water off a leaf. She got her feet under her and stood, and the valley spread out below her in the pre-dawn grey, and it was beautiful, and it was home, and she knew it the way she knew her own hands.

"I think so," she said. "Yes. I'm well."

She frowned slightly, pressing a hand to her chest, where something ached — a grief with no object, a mourning with no grave, a shape of absence so precise it could only have been cut by something that had once fit there perfectly.

"What's wrong?" the stranger asked quietly.

"Nothing." She shook her head, and laughed a little, embarrassed. "Nothing's wrong. Everything's — the garden needs rebuilding, is all. My grandmother's garden. It burned, I think? I don't remember why I know that." She looked down at her empty hands, and then, because her body knew what her mind had forgotten, she held one of them out to him. "Come on, then. If you're going to stand there, you can help me work. There's a lot to plant."

He took her hand.

His grip was careful, and warm, and it trembled — though whether from exhaustion or something else, the woman who would have known was gone, carried over the bridge into the keeping of the man now looking at her with thirteen years of her life held behind his eyes like water behind a dam.

"Lead the way," Isandro said.

And Wendell Thornbury, covenant-heir, last apprentice of the guild, turned toward the valley with no memory of what she had paid for it, and began, hand in hand with a stranger she felt she ought to know, to walk down into the garden.

Behind them, on the horizon, the thirteenth moon slipped below the hills — and somewhere in the dark earth of the valley, in soil that remembered everything, a seed her mother had left began, very quietly, to grow.
