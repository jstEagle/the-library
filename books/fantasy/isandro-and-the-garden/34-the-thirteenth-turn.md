## 34. The Thirteenth Turn

Isandro had thought he understood what it meant to wait. He was the covenant's guardian; waiting was half his office. He had waited through winters in the deep places of his realm where time moved like honey through cold glass. He had waited at the border bridge for a messenger who never came, thirteen years ago, and learned that some waits do not end — they only change shape.

But nothing had taught him to wait like this: standing on the human side of the reforged bridge while the moon above him swelled toward its thirteenth turn, and the woman beside him did not know his name.

She stood close. That was the cruelty of it and the mercy both. Wendell Thornbury leaned against the bridge's new-woven light as though it were a garden wall she'd rested against a hundred times, her hands — those hands that left faint silver shimmer on everything living — folded loosely at her waist. She watched the moon with the patient attention of someone who had spent her whole life reading weather off leaves.

"Do you feel that?" she asked.

He did not trust his voice to answer plainly. "Feel what?"

"Like something turning over," she said. "Under the world. Like soil before rain."

*Like me,* he thought. *Turning over under the world.* He said instead, "The covenant is settling. It will take until the turn completes."

She nodded, satisfied, and Isandro looked away from her because looking at her had become an act of discipline. Thirteen nights ago she had held his hand and paid the price with open eyes, and every memory of him had gone out of her like water out of a lifted sluice-gate — clean, complete, without residue. She remembered the guild. She remembered her grandmother's death two winters past. She remembered Rell, though Rell no longer remembered her childhood either, having paid his own smaller price to break the machine. She remembered the hunt, the flight, the keystones — all of it in the abstract, the way one remembers a story heard long ago. But of Isandro himself there was nothing. Not his face. Not his voice. Not the weight of his hand in hers as the light took her memories one by one and she had smiled through it, because she had chosen it, because the alternative was the end of both worlds.

The asymmetry of it sat in his chest like a stone planted too deep to dig out.

* * *

The last hours of the twelfth night had been quiet. The queen's army had withdrawn in ragged order after the machine died, after Rell laughed and broke its core and forgot her name mid-laugh. The caged gifted had been carried free — some walking, some weeping, some silent in ways that Isandro feared would take years to mend, if they ever mended. The queen herself had knelt on the scorched grass of the border field, an old woman suddenly, holding the truth Wendell had shown her the way one holds a hot coal: unable to drop it, unable to hold it still.

And Wendell and Isandro had walked up onto the bridge together, four keystones singing around them in a chord that made the air itself seem thin as parchment, and they had spoken the words her mother had left sealed in her daughter's memory:

*Two who choose. Two who see the price entire. Two who pay it willingly, each their own.*

He had expected the covenant to take from them equally. That had been the promise of the willing form — shared rather than stolen. And in a sense it had been equal: the magic that poured back into the valley as the realms knit closed drew its balance from them both, and he felt it leave him, felt his own long guardianship dissolve into the fabric of the border like salt into bread.

But memory — memory had taken only hers.

He had understood why even as it happened, the way one understands a law of nature by watching it work. The covenant required the heir to forget what she loved most, so that the border could be woven from love freely given rather than love clutched and kept. His memories were the counterweight. Someone had to remember. Someone had to carry the whole shape of what had been lost, or the sacrifice would mean nothing — a debt forgiven with no record of the lending. The covenant was old and wise in the way of old things, and it had made its choice without asking him, and he would have refused if asked, and perhaps that was exactly why it hadn't asked.

So he had stood on the bridge holding her hand while she forgot him, and she had turned to him at the end with a stranger's polite, tired courtesy, and asked who he was, and he had said, "A gardener," because it was true, and because it was the only word he could manage that wouldn't crack open the world again.

Now the thirteenth moon hung fat and white over the valley, one turn from full completion, and the covenant held. He could feel it holding — a vast slow pulse beneath the earth, like a heart remembering its rhythm after a long illness.

* * *

They came down from the bridge together in the grey hour before dawn, because there was nowhere else for her to go and he could not bear to leave her alone on the field among the machine's wreckage. She talked as they walked. That was the thing he kept discovering: she talked the way she had always talked, opinions about drainage and soil pH delivered with the same dry certainty, the same habit of naming plants by their Latin names just to watch people flinch. None of it was performance. The covenant had taken his face from her memory and left her self perfectly intact, and watching her be entirely herself in his presence without knowing him was like watching a fire burn in a room where the walls had been repainted — everything right, everything wrong.

"The guild garden will need rebuilding from the roots," she said, kicking a charred stick off the road. "Hunters burned it. I keep — I don't know why I keep thinking about it. It's just a garden."

"It isn't," Isandro said quietly.

She glanced at him. "You're the gardener. You'd say that."

"I would." He almost smiled. "I'm told I say little else."

"You haven't said much of anything since the bridge." Her eyes narrowed — that sharp assessing look he knew, the look she'd given the seed-packet, the map, the first keystone, every locked door between her and a truth. "Who are you? Really? You were on the bridge when the covenant — whatever happened, happened. You fought beside us at the border. Rell trusted you. I can tell I trusted you, because my body trusts you, which is annoying, because my head has nothing to work with."

"Your grandmother's guild served the covenant," he said. "I served it longer. From the other side."

"The other side of what?"

"The border. The realm your valley was walled against." He watched her absorb this — watched the gardener's mind go to work on it, turning it over like unfamiliar soil, testing its weight. "Magic could not follow across. That was the covenant's gift and its cage. Thirteen years ago it broke, and everything since — the wild magic, the poisoned roads, the crown's fear — grew from that breaking."

"And now it holds again." She stopped walking. Above them the moon sat heavy on the ridge line, and the first birds were starting up in the hedgerows, tentative, as if unsure the dawn had been authorized. "Then why are you here? On this side. In the valley." She gestured at the fields rolling down toward the town, grey-gold in the pre-dawn, ordinary, human. "If you serve the other realm."

Isandro looked at the valley for a long moment. He had crossed into it once before, dying, carrying a keystone and a hope he hadn't dared name. He had lain in her garden among the herbs she'd grown with hands that shimmered, and she had knelt over him calling him by a name no one used, and he had known her before she knew herself.

"I have nowhere else to be," he said. Which was true, and also the smallest possible version of the truth, the way a seed is the smallest possible version of a tree.

* * *

The valley had changed overnight. That was the thing the travelers on the road kept saying to each other, half-fearful, half-laughing — changed overnight, as if a season had skipped ahead. The blight on the western orchards had receded, the bark healing in visible rings. The well-water tasted sweet again. In the ditches along the road, flowers Isandro recognized from his own realm — moonwort, star-thistle, the pale lantern-bells that opened only for honest grief — had come up through the mud as though they'd been waiting thirteen years underground for permission, which, he supposed, they had.

Magic returning in balance. Not the wild flooding thing that had torn through the borderlands during the broken years, but a measured green tide, rising to its old marks. He could feel it settling into the valley's bones, and he could feel, beneath it, the covenant's pulse keeping time — and threaded through the pulse, faint but unmistakable, the signature of her sacrifice. Every growing thing in the valley carried a trace of what she had given. The whole landscape was, in a sense, her memorial, and she walked through it unaware, occasionally frowning at a lantern-bell as if trying to place where she'd seen one before.

She couldn't place it. She had never seen one. They had crossed the Road of Ash together and she had pressed one into his hand — *for the pockets of people who've forgotten how to grieve* — and he had carried it ever since, dried to a whisper, in the inner pocket of his coat.

At the edge of town they parted, briefly. She went to find Rell — she had said she needed to see him, needed to understand why the sight of him hurt in a way she couldn't account for, and Isandro had not tried to stop her, because that wound was not his to dress. He went to the guild garden.

It was worse than he remembered. The hunters' torches had taken everything — the walls cracked, the beds blackened, the great pear tree that had stood since the guild's founding split down the heartwood. He walked the rows of ash and ruin in the early light and read the garden the way he read all gardens: as a text written in loss. Here the herb beds where she had hidden her shimmering hands. There the gate where a wounded man had collapsed out of another world. The soil itself was still rich underneath the char; fire took the surface and left the deep ground. Gardeners knew this. It was, he had always believed, the single most important thing gardeners knew.

He worked until his hands ached. Clearing, turning, testing. By midday he had a plan drawn in his head, and by late afternoon he had the beginnings of it in the ground — the first new bed laid out along the old lines, because the old lines were good lines, laid by generations of Thornburys who had understood things about this soil that no one alive remembered understanding.

Wendell found him there at dusk.

She stood at the broken gate a long moment before speaking. "Rell doesn't remember me," she said. Her voice was level in the way that cost effort. "Not our childhood. Not anything before three years ago. He says he gave something up, at the border, to break the machine. He says he'd do it again." She came into the garden, stepping over the char, and stopped at the new-turned bed. "He looks at me like I'm a stranger he's decided to trust anyway. And it hurts. It hurts like something's missing that I should have, and I can't even tell if the missing thing is mine or his."

"It's yours," Isandro said. "What you gave at the bridge — part of it was tied to him. To what you were to each other, once."

She stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Because I was there."

"You were there," she repeated slowly. "On the bridge. Before. During." Her eyes searched his face with that hunger he had seen in her since the drowned chapel — the hunger for the past, for the fragments the memory-wraith had shown her, for the parents whose faces she barely held. "Did I know you? Before the bridge?"

The question hung between them in the failing light. He could lie. He had, in a sense, been lying since the bridge — by omission, by gentleness, by the small daily cowardice of *a gardener*. He could tell her the shape of it without the substance: *you knew me, we traveled together, we—*

Or he could tell her the truth, and watch her learn that the covenant had taken from her the very person standing in front of her, and see whether that knowledge would break the fragile peace the sacrifice had bought.

"The covenant took your memories of what you loved most," he said finally. "That is all I will say tonight. The rest is yours to grow into, or not. I won't force a bloom."

Something moved through her face — frustration, mostly, and beneath it a flicker of something older and more helpless, the expression of someone reaching for a word on the tip of the tongue and finding the whole language gone. Then she looked down at the new bed, and her gardener's eye took over, and he watched her read the layout, the spacing, the choice of the old lines.

"That's the guild's pattern," she said slowly. "That's — how do you know the guild's pattern?"

"I told you. I've served the covenant a long time."

"From the other side."

"Yes."

"But you're laying out a garden in our pattern, in our valley, on the night the covenant settled." She looked up at him, and the last light caught her eyes, and for one unguarded moment she looked at him the way she had looked at him on the bridge — not with memory, but with recognition, the body's knowledge outrunning the mind's. "Who *are* you?"

"A gardener," Isandro said gently. "Hired, if you'll have me. This garden will need tending for years. And I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I have a packet of seeds. They were given to me by a woman who burned her guild's records to save her granddaughter. I think they were always meant to be planted here."

* * *

He showed her the seed-packet that night, in the ruined potting shed where they sheltered from a rain that smelled, for the first time in thirteen years, of his own realm — of ozone and blossom and the green dark under old forests. The paper was soft with handling. Grandmother Thornbury's sigil was stamped on it in wax that had long since crumbled.

Wendell turned it over in her shimmering hands. "Grandmother gave me a packet like this," she said. "The night the hunters came. She burned everything and gave me this and a map, and I never — I got distracted, there wasn't time, and then the bridge, and—" She frowned. "I don't know what happened to mine. Isn't that strange? I can remember the giving of it perfectly and I can't remember what became of it."

"Perhaps it was spent," Isandro said. "Seed-packets are for planting."

"No." She was shaking her head slowly, and he saw the gardener in her rise up over the grief, the professional instinct that had carried her through every horror of the journey. "No, these aren't ordinary seeds. Look at the weight. Look at the—" She held one up to the lamp, a small dark grain with a faint inner light, like a star seen through smoke. "These are covenant seeds. My grandmother was guild-master. If she saved one packet from the burning, it was the packet. These would be from the original covenant-garden. From before the wall." Her voice dropped. "From the other realm."

"Yes," Isandro said.

"Then they shouldn't grow here. Nothing from there grows here. That was the whole point of the wall."

"It was," he agreed. "Thirteen days ago."

They looked at each other across the potting bench, and outside the rain fell soft and steady on a valley learning to be magical again, and somewhere over the ridge the thirteenth moon climbed toward the top of its arc.

"We plant them tomorrow," Wendell said. "At moonset. That's when the guild always planted anything that mattered." She paused, and a small crease appeared between her brows — the crease it made whenever a memory knocked and found no one home. "I know that. I don't know how I know that. I know a hundred things about this guild that I have no right to know anymore, and none of them include you, and that is driving me mad, and I would like you to explain yourself someday. Soon. When you're ready."

"I will," Isandro said. "When it can be done kindly."

"Kindly," she muttered, wrapping herself in her cloak on the cot across the shed. "Men and their kindness. It's usually just cowardice with better manners."

He laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the bridge — and she looked over at him sharply, startled, as if the sound had struck some bell in her that had no clapper anymore. For a moment neither of them moved. Rain on the roof. Lamp guttering. Her eyes wide in the half-dark, searching his face for something she had lost and could not name.

"I dream about you," she said abruptly. "I didn't want to say it. It's inappropriate, I don't know you, but I dream about you, and in the dreams I'm not confused, I know exactly who you are, and I wake up and I've usually been crying, and I have never in my life been so tired of crying over a stranger."

Isandro sat very still. The stone in his chest shifted — not lighter, never lighter, but turned, the way one turns a seed in the palm to check which end the root will come from.

"Dreams are where the covenant can't reach," he said at last. "It took your waking memory. It had no jurisdiction over your sleep."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," he said, rising to trim the lamp, "that some things were planted deeper than the price could dig. Tomorrow we plant the rest."

She was quiet a long time. When he glanced back, she was watching him with an expression he had last seen on the bridge, in the last minute before the light took her — tenderness without object, love with its address burned away, directed at him simply because he was what stood nearest to the hole in her.

"Goodnight, gardener," she said.

"Goodnight, Wendell."

Her breath caught. Just slightly. Just enough.

Outside, the thirteenth moon reached the top of its turn and began, imperceptibly, to come down the other side — and in the blackened beds of the guild garden, unseen by anyone, the first of the covenant seeds had already split its husk in the warm rain, putting down a root that glowed faintly silver, reaching for a depth of soil where nothing, not even a covenant, could ever dig it out.
