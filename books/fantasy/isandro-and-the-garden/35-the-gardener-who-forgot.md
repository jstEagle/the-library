## 35. The Gardener Who Forgot

The frost came early that year, and Wendell was glad of it.

Frost meant work. Frost meant wrapping the fig in burlap and banking straw around the rose roots and carrying water before dawn so the pipes wouldn't split. Frost gave her hands something to do that did not require her to think, because thinking had become a country she could no longer enter — a door in a wall she kept touching, finding it smooth and blank, finding nothing behind it but the flat stone of her own skull.

She knelt in the ruined garden with her bare fingers in the cold earth and did not know why she was crying.

It happened like that now, more often than she admitted to anyone. Grief arrived without an address. It came when she turned the compost and found a curl of pale root shaped like a hand; it came when the wind moved through the birches at the garden's north wall and made a sound like someone laughing low, just out of sight; it came, worst of all, when she worked among the flowers, because there were flowers here she knew how to tend and did not remember planting, and some of them made her chest ache so badly she had to sit down among them and breathe until the ache passed through her like weather.

Her grandmother's doctor would have called it melancholia. The village women called it mourning sickness, and looked at her sideways, and asked gently after dead relatives. But everyone Wendell had ever loved was alive. Her grandmother slept upstairs in the rebuilt cottage, thin as winter reeds but breathing. Rell lived, though changed. Even the strangers from the palace — the freed captives scattered now across the valley like seeds on wind — lived.

So what, then, was she grieving? What funeral had she attended that she could not recall?

Wendell pressed her palms flat against the soil and felt the faint shimmer rise in them, the gift answering the ground the way a struck bell answers its own ringing. Green light threaded between her fingers, delicate as spider silk. The nearest plants leaned toward her — she had noticed this weeks ago, the way a field of wheat leans after the sun — and she let them lean, and said aloud, to no one, "I don't know who taught me this."

That was the strangest part. Not the power itself, though the power was strange enough: it had come back to her whole, or nearly whole, the morning she'd woken in her own narrow bed with frost ferns on the window and no memory of how she'd gotten home. Her hands remembered everything. They knew the depth at which to set a bulb, the angle of a graft, the exact pressure that coaxed a reluctant seed to break its coat. They moved through the garden like a musician's hands through a piece learned in childhood, and Wendell stood inside her own body watching them play music whose name she could not say.

*Thornbury*, the guild had been called. She knew that much; the sign above the gate had been half-burned but legible, and she had hammered it straight herself. *Guild of Gardeners*. There had been records once, her grandmother said, but they were lost in the fire. There had been other apprentices once, but they were gone. There had been a purpose, a duty, an old arrangement of some kind —

"An old arrangement," her grandmother had repeated, when Wendell asked, and her eyes had gone wet and distant, and she had said only, "You'll have to forgive me, child. I find I can't hold the shape of it anymore. It's like trying to remember a dream by noon." And then, gripping Wendell's wrist with surprising strength: "But you were loved. Whatever else is gone, know that you were loved past all sense and reason."

Wendell believed her. That was the cruelest thing — she believed her completely, and the belief filled nothing. Love that has been given leaves a shape behind, like a cup leaving a ring on wood, and Wendell went about her days fitting her hand into shapes she could not see.

She rose, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and got back to work.

* * *

The guild garden had been burned in the autumn of her forgetting — she knew this the way she knew the scar on her own forearm, without knowing the story of it. Half the wall was blackened stone. The glasshouse had collapsed into a lacework of charred frame and shattered pane, and for the first two weeks she had done nothing but stand in the wreckage, weeping, while her hands trembled with the need to begin.

Now, five weeks on, the beginning had been made. She had cleared the beds one by one, working from dawn until her shoulders screamed, sorting burn from soil, salvage from ash. Under the ruins of the potting shed she had found things that undid her quietly: a trowel worn to fit a grip narrower than hers, a child's watering can painted with crooked blue stars, rows of clay pots labeled in a handwriting that made her throat close — a quick, slanted, impatient script, letters leaning forward as if their writer were always halfway to somewhere else.

She kept the labels. She didn't know why. She kept them in a tin on the kitchen shelf and took them out sometimes in the evening and read the plant names over and over — *moonwort, hush-lily, king's sorrow, evening-thread* — names she had never heard spoken anywhere in the valley, names that belonged to no book in her grandmother's house, and each one landed in her like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without striking bottom.

*King's sorrow.* Who had named it that? Whose sorrow? Why did the name make her want to lie down in the cold dirt and stay there?

On this frosted morning she planted.

The seed-packet had been in her pocket the day she woke — she knew this because the packet had been in the pocket of the dress she'd been wearing, travel-stained and torn at the hem, a dress she had no memory of owning. Inside: a dozen seeds, small and dark and dense as drops of night, wrapped in paper inscribed with the same slanted handwriting as the pots. No instructions. Only a single line beneath the seeds, written small:

*Plant where the light falls last.*

She had carried the packet around for three weeks like a coal she was afraid to drop and afraid to quench. Where did the light fall last in a valley ringed with hills? In the west, obviously — but the west of what? The garden? The world? She had walked the boundary of the ruined garden at dusk again and again, packet in hand, feeling foolish, feeling desperate, watching the shadows lengthen.

And then yesterday, clearing the fallen stones near the old sundial, she had found it: a bed of soil sheltered by the garden's high western wall, deep and dark and untouched by the fire, where the sun lingered longest before the hills drank it. Of course. Of course. Her hands had known before her mind caught up; they had already loosened the soil before she understood what she was doing.

She knelt there now, in the last of the morning's light, and opened the packet.

The seeds smelled of something she couldn't name — not any flower of the valley, not loam or rain or green growth, but something older and stranger, like the air in a room long shut, like summer held in amber. Her gift rose to meet them unbidden, shimmering up her arms, and the seeds stirred in her palm like sleeping birds.

"One at a time," she told herself, and her voice cracked on the words, and she didn't know why. "One at a time. It's only planting. You've done it ten thousand times."

Had she? Ten thousand times, with whom, for whom? She pressed the first seed into the dark earth and the tears came again, silent and useless, sliding warm down her cold face, and she planted the second seed through them, and the third, and did not stop.

* * *

Rell came on the fourth day of the planting, riding up the valley road in a plain gray coat with no insignia, his captain's sword left behind — or so he said. He looked thinner than the boy she remembered, which was itself a strange thought, because when she tried to summon the boy he had been, she found mostly fog: a name, a warmth, a sense of shared summers, and almost nothing else. She knew they had been friends since childhood. Everyone in the village said so. But the childhood itself was a locked room, and when she pressed her ear to its door she heard only wind.

He dismounted at the gate and stood a moment looking at the garden — at the cleared beds, the mended wall, the new shoots already greening in the western bed, unnaturally fast, unnaturally lush — and something crossed his face that she might have called homesickness if she hadn't known he'd never lived here.

"You've brought it back," he said.

"It was never mine to bring back." She brushed the soil from her knees. "I'm only the one still here."

"Wendell." He said her name carefully, the way a man sets down something fragile. "May I?"

She led him through the gate. He walked the paths slowly, and she watched him watch the garden, and saw that his hands wanted to touch things and didn't dare. At the birches he stopped. The wind moved through them, making its low laughing sound, and Rell went very still.

"I keep having dreams," he said, not turning around. "Since — since everything. Since the bridge." He rubbed the back of his neck, a gesture so familiar it startled her, though she couldn't have said where she'd catalogued it. "I dream about this garden. About running through these beds when I was eight years old, and a woman with grey braids shouting at us for trampling the chamomile, and — " He stopped. Frowned. "Except I don't remember being eight. I don't remember the woman. I remember the dream, that's all. Like reading a letter about a place I've never been."

"The backlash," Wendell said quietly. Everyone spoke of it that way now, the backlash, as if it were weather. When Rell had broken the machine's core — she knew this story too, secondhand, from the freed captives and the queen's own shaken proclamation — the flood of stolen memory it released had taken his along with it. All of it, the captives said. Everything he was.

"Not all," Rell said. "I remember my mother's kitchen. I remember my oath. I remember you." He turned, and his eyes were steady and sad. "I remember you standing in the queen's courtyard with light coming off your hands like water, and I remember thinking: *there she is. There's the girl I — *"

He stopped again. His jaw worked.

"And?" Wendell asked, very softly.

"And nothing follows," he said. "I reach for it and there's a hole. I know the sentence starts with *the girl I* and ends somewhere important, and the middle is just — gone. White space." He laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what that's like? To feel a word missing out of your own heart?"

Yes, Wendell thought. Yes, I know exactly what that's like. My whole heart is white space.

Aloud she said, "Come inside. Grandmother will want to see you. There's soup."

"There's always soup with your grandmother."

They both froze. The words had come out of him easily, reflexively, a groove worn by a thousand repetitions — and neither of them knew why he should know it. Rell stared at her. Wendell stared back. Somewhere in the western bed, absurdly, the new shoots leaned toward them both.

"I'll get the soup," Wendell said, because someone had to move, and if no one moved the silence would open under them like water.

* * *

They ate at the kitchen table while her grandmother dozed in her chair by the fire, and Rell told her the news from the capital in the flat voice of a man delivering a report he doesn't believe in.

The hunt was ended — formally dissolved by royal decree, the gifted declared under the crown's protection rather than its suspicion. The queen had changed. Everyone said so. She had come back from the border with white in her hair that hadn't been there before, and she had ordered the machine broken apart and its pieces buried, and she had begun, of all things, to build gardens. Palace gardens, city gardens, a garden at every garrison where hunters had once been billeted. No one understood it. The court whispered that grief had unhinged her.

"She knows something we don't," Rell said. "Or remembers something. She wouldn't say. But when she signed the dissolution order she looked at me — I was the only hunter in the room — and she said, 'Tell the gardener, when you find her, that the debt is acknowledged.' Just that. *The debt is acknowledged.*" He shook his head. "Debt for what?"

"For what," Wendell echoed. The words sat strangely in her mouth, like a key that fits a lock she cannot find.

Outside, the light was failing. Rell helped her clear the bowls — their movements dovetailing in the small kitchen with an ease that hurt, passing plates without looking, stepping around each other in the narrow space like dancers who had forgotten the dance but not the floor — and then he stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands, not quite able to leave.

"I'll be back," he said. "If that's — I mean, I've taken a cottage in the village. I thought I'd stay a while. If you can bear it."

"Of course I can bear it." She managed a smile. "We're friends, aren't we?"

"We are." He said it firmly, like a man nailing down a board over a gap. Then, softer, at the gate, glancing back at the western bed where the twelve new shoots stood silvered in the moonrise: "Those aren't valley flowers, Wendell. Whatever you're growing there. I've ridden every road in this kingdom and I've never seen their like."

"No," Wendell agreed. "Neither have I."

"But your hands know them."

"My hands know them," she said, and heard the grief in her own voice, naked and objectless, and watched it land in Rell like an arrow in water.

He left. She stayed in the garden long after, in the cold, kneeling by the western bed. Twelve shoots now, slender and strange, their leaves like green flame. As she watched, one of them — the largest, the one she had planted first, the one her hands had handled most tenderly — turned, very slightly, away from the moon.

Toward the east. Toward the hills. Toward the border no one spoke of, where the realms had knit closed and whole, and beyond which lay a country she had never seen and could not remember and missed, bone-deep, past all reason, like a name forever on the tip of her tongue.

"Where does the light fall last?" she whispered to it. "What am I supposed to do with you?"

The shoot did not answer. But when she laid her hand beside it, the shimmer rose in her palm, and the little plant leaned into her fingers the way a cat leans into a known hand — trusting, certain, waiting — and Wendell Thornbury knelt alone in her garden, grieving something she could not name, and waited with it for whatever was coming.

Three mornings later, a stranger came up the valley road carrying nothing but a traveler's pack and a seed-packet, and stopped at the guild gate as if he had been expected there all his life.
