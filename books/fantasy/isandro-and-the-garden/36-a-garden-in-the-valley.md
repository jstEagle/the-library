## 36. A Garden in the Valley

The valley had a smell now.

Isandro noticed it the first morning he crossed the boundary with nothing but a seed-packet in his coat and a name that was not his own on his tongue — a green, wet, living smell, like rain finding soil after a long drought. Thirteen years this valley had smelled of dust and stone and the particular emptiness of a place where magic could not follow. Now it breathed. He stood at the edge of the old guild garden, where the walls still bore the scorch-marks of crown torches, and he let the smell of it fill him until his eyes stung.

*She is here,* said every root in the ground beneath his feet. *She is here, she is here.*

He had learned, over one long year of exile and return, to listen to what growing things said. It was the first lesson of his order, older than the covenant itself: before you tend a garden, you must hear it. And every blade of grass in this valley was saying her name — not *Wendell*, exactly, but something underneath the name, the shape of her gift woven into the soil like thread through cloth. She had reforged the covenant. Her magic was in the ground now, in the water, in the light. She had given up every memory of him to do it, and the land remembered her for it, the way stone remembers the hand that shaped it.

Isandro pressed his palm flat against the ruined garden gate and let himself feel all of it — the ache, the vastness of the ache — and then, as he had taught himself to do, he folded it away into the place where gardeners keep the things they cannot yet use.

Then he walked down into the village to find work.

* * *

They were suspicious of him at first, as well they might be. A stranger, tall and quiet, with scarring along his forearms that looked — if you looked too long — like bark. The valley had suffered enough strange things in thirteen years to distrust anything that arrived without explanation. But the harvest had been strange that season too: wheat that grew a hand's breadth taller than any seed sown in living memory, apple trees blossoming twice, a spring behind the mill that ran sweet where it had run bitter for a generation. The valley wanted a gardener. That much was plain to anyone who walked its lanes and saw how the wild new magic was coming up through the earth like teeth — beautiful and unruly and half-understood, brambles that bloomed out of season, hedges that leaned toward certain houses and away from others.

The reeve was a broad woman named Maren with flour on her sleeves and grief in her face — Isandro recognized grief when he saw it; he had carried enough of his own. She looked him over in her kitchen while her youngest daughter peered around the doorframe.

"You're not from here," she said.

"No."

"Where from, then?"

"Far," Isandro said, which was true in more ways than she could know. "Over the mountains." Also true. He had learned, in the human realm, that truth told slant was still truth, and he would not lie outright — not about this, not about anything that mattered. He simply would not tell the whole of it. There was a difference, he told himself, though some nights the difference felt thin as frost.

Maren narrowed her eyes. "We've had strangers before. Tax men. Hunters." The word *hunters* came out flat and hard, and her daughter flinched at the doorframe. "That's done now, they say. The queen's edict. But folk here remember."

"I'm not a hunter," Isandro said. "I'm a gardener. I'd like to see the old guild garden, if anyone still holds the key."

Something moved in Maren's face — surprise, and under it a wariness that softened by a degree. "Nobody holds the key. The last guildmaster burned with it, near enough." She studied him a long moment. "You know about the guild, then."

"I know gardens," Isandro said. "I know what a walled garden is *for*."

And that, apparently, was answer enough, or near enough. Maren gave him the loft above her stable, bread at her table, and a wage paid in eggs and coin, and in exchange he would take the guild garden — what was left of it — and make something grow there again. "It's an ill job," she warned him. "Half the village crosses the street rather than look at those walls. They say it's cursed. They say the Thornbury girl cursed it, going off the way she did, and dying — if she died — for whatever it was she did up at the border." Maren's voice dropped. "My sister's boy was taken by the hunters, the year of the machine. He came back hollowed out. Doesn't remember our names. So don't ask me to weep for the gifted, stranger. But I won't say the guild deserved burning either. Nobody here knows what to think anymore."

"I understand," Isandro said quietly. "I'll begin tomorrow."

He did not tell her that he had been there — at the border, on the night the bridge stood open, on the night the machine broke and Rell laughed as the backlash took his memories. He had seen it all. He had held Wendell's hand while the covenant took everything she loved most out of her mind, and he had walked away from her sleeping body carrying the whole weight of what she no longer carried, and he had spent a full turn of both moons deciding what a man owed to someone who had forgotten he existed.

The answer, he had finally decided, was this: everything, and nothing. Everything he could give without taking. Nothing that would disturb the peace she had bought so dearly.

* * *

The guild garden was worse than he had hoped and better than he feared.

The fire had taken the glasshouse entirely, and the southern beds were ash to a spade's depth, and the great pear tree that must have stood at the garden's heart for two hundred years was a black skeleton against the sky. But walls hold heat and memory both, and the northern beds had survived, and when Isandro knelt and pushed his fingers into the soil he found life there — seeds lying dormant, roots holding on, the whole patient underground parliament of a garden waiting for someone to give it permission.

"All right," he murmured to them. "I'm here. Begin."

He worked from dawn until the light went amber, and the work was prayer and penance and joy all braided together, and he did not once let himself think of the door in the far wall — the little painted door, blue once, that led out of the garden toward the lane where the cottage stood. He thought of it constantly. He worked anyway.

By the third week, things began to happen that made the village children press their faces to the gaps in the wall. The blackened pear tree put out a single branch of white blossom in the wrong season. The well in the corner, dry since the burning, filled overnight with water clear as glass. And in the bed nearest the ruined glasshouse, Isandro planted the contents of the seed-packet — the packet Grandmother Thornbury had pressed into Wendell's hands on the night the hunters came, the packet that had traveled over mountains and across a severed border and back again, and that Isandro had carried through everything because it was hers, because it was the first thing she had ever trusted him to hold.

The seeds came up in three days. They should have taken three weeks. They came up silver-green and eager, and when the wind moved them they made a sound almost like speech, and Isandro knelt beside them in the dark of his second week and wept into the soil, quietly, the way gardeners do, letting the earth take what it takes.

Because he knew these plants. They were covenant-flowers — the old guild's signature bloom, the flower that grew only where the covenant's magic ran, the flower that answered to names. He had seen them last in the realm of Isandro, in the fields of his childhood, before he was chosen as guardian and learned that guardians are named for the office, not the man. He had never known they could grow here. Not until she reforged the bridge between worlds. Not until her sacrifice opened the way for magic to live in this valley again.

Everything growing in this garden was growing because of what she had given up.

He turned the soil over that thought every morning, the way he turned the beds. Some mornings it was bearable. Most it was not.

* * *

He saw her for the first time on a market day, six weeks after he had come to the valley.

He had known she was there — the whole valley knew; the woman who had saved them all, the heir of the Thornbury guild, returned from the border with no memory of why her hands knew plants or why she woke some nights calling a name no one could give her. The village spoke of her in the careful, reverent, slightly frightened way people speak of those who have done something enormous on their behalf. She had rebuilt the guild garden's outer walls herself, they said, stone by stone, though she could not have said what the garden was for. She kept her grandmother's cottage. She sold herbs and cuttings at market, and her roses bloomed out of season, and she laughed easily and often, and sometimes, mid-laugh, she would go quiet as if listening to something very far away.

Isandro had planned, with a discipline he did not fully trust, never to seek her out. Fate — or the garden, which amounts to the same thing — had other plans.

He was buying nails at the ironmonger's stall when the crowd parted and she came down the lane with a basket on her hip, arguing cheerfully with the baker's boy about the price of rye flour, and the world went narrow and ringing the way it does before a storm.

She was thinner than he remembered. Her hair was shorter; she had cut it herself, badly, in the way of someone who cuts hair for reasons that have nothing to do with fashion. There was a smudge of soil on her jaw. She was wearing gloves — good leather ones — and he understood why even before he saw what happened when she passed the flower-seller's cart: every bloom in the cart turned toward her, slowly, all together, like faces turning toward the sun, and the flower-seller stared at his own stock with his mouth open, and Wendell laughed and said something kind and self-deprecating that Isandro couldn't hear over the blood in his ears, and bought a paper twist of sweet peas she clearly didn't need.

She passed within three feet of him.

She glanced at him — the brief, polite glance one stranger gives another in a crowded lane — and her eyes, grey-green as new leaves, met his for perhaps the length of one heartbeat.

Nothing. No flicker. No pause. To her he was a tall stranger with bark-scarring on his arms, standing at a nail stall, unremarkable, forgettable. The covenant had taken its price cleanly. Every memory of him, gone as if it had never been planted.

"Morning," she said, because she was courteous, because she was Wendell even without her memories, courteous to strangers in lanes, and then she was past him, and the crowd closed, and Isandro stood holding a bag of nails he did not need with his heart breaking in the old familiar place.

*Good,* he told himself, walking home with his spine straight and his eyes hot. *This is what you chose. This is what she paid for. You will not undo her purchase with your loneliness.*

But that night he dreamed of the bridge of light, and of her hand in his, and of her voice saying, *whatever it asks, I choose it with open eyes* — and he woke in the loft above the stable with tears on his face and the seed-packet's empty paper under his pillow, and he lay looking at the rafters until dawn, memorizing the ache, because the ache was all he had left of her, and he refused to waste even that.

* * *

Winter came gently to the valley, the first gentle winter in thirteen years, and the covenant-garden grew.

Isandro built the glasshouse anew, salvaging what glass he could and trading labor for the rest. He laid out the beds in the old guild pattern — he had found the pattern in his own memory, in the training every guardian received, for the guardians had always known the gardeners' craft; the two orders had been mirror and counterpart since before either realm had names. North beds for the hardy and the healing. South beds for the tender exotics that, now that magic lived here again, might actually survive. A spiral bed at the center, where the pear tree stood, for the covenant-flowers themselves.

The children of the village began to come, first one and then a dozen, drawn the way children are drawn to anywhere something strange and wonderful is happening. He set them to weeding and watering and the endless meditative work of picking stones from soil, and he taught them the first lesson of his order without quite meaning to: *listen to what the plant wants before you decide what it needs.* One girl, the reeve's youngest, had a flicker of the gift in her — Isandro saw it the way you see a candle lit in a distant window — and he said nothing about it to anyone, only gave her the seed-tray with the hardest germinators and watched her hands learn patience. There would need to be a guild again, someday. There were gifted children being born in this valley now, and the world had just finished learning what happened to the gifted when no one sheltered them. But that was a bridge for another season. For now: soil, and water, and light.

Wendell came to the wall in the first week of the frost.

He was mulching the spiral bed when he heard the gate — the small side gate, the one he had repaired — and looked up, and there she stood with snow in her dark hair and her breath steaming, staring at the covenant-flowers' silver-green rosettes sleeping under their straw blankets.

"They're not supposed to grow here," she said. It was not an accusation. It was wonder, worn thin with confusion. "I've grown every plant that grows in this valley. In my grandmother's books there's nothing like these. Where did you get the seed?"

"A friend," Isandro said. His voice came out steady. He had practiced. "Someone who traveled."

She looked at him then, really looked, and he stood very still inside himself and let her do it — let her search his face for whatever it was that made her frown faintly, that made her head tilt, that made her say, slowly:

"Have we met? Before — I mean—" She stopped, and he watched her reach for something that wasn't there, the way a tongue reaches for a tooth newly gone. "You seem..." She shook her head. "Forgive me. People say I do this. Since the border. There are holes in me, and sometimes I meet someone and I can't tell if they're a hole or not."

His chest was a held breath. *Tell her,* said every root in the ground, every bloom in the spiral bed, the whole garden leaning toward her like the flower-cart had leaned. *Tell her your name. Tell her everything.*

*No,* said the other voice, the one built from a year of resolve. *She paid. Let the payment stand. If remembering comes back to her, let it come back grown, not grafted.*

"We haven't met," Isandro said gently. "I'd remember it."

Something in her shoulders eased, and something in her face — he could not name it, and dared not hope — dimmed. "Right," she said. "Sorry. It's the flowers, I think. They do something to me. When I'm near them my hands itch, like they know a job that the rest of me has forgotten." She crouched, gloved fingers hovering over the straw, not touching. "What are they called?"

"Covenant-flowers," Isandro said. "In the old tongue, they're called *the flowers that answer to names*. There's a story that if you speak the right name to one, it blooms."

"Whose name?"

"The name it was planted for." He kept his voice level, gardener's-plain, giving nothing away. "Every seed remembers the person it was sown in honor of. That's the story, anyway. My people took their stories seriously."

"Your people," she repeated, and looked up at him sharply, and for one terrible, luminous instant he thought — *she sees it, she feels it, the body remembers even when the mind forgets* — and then a shout came from the lane, one of the village boys calling her name, some errand, some small emergency of ordinary life, and the moment broke like ice on a puddle.

She rose, brushing snow from her knees. "I'll come back," she said. "When there's time. I want to see them bloom." A pause at the gate, her hand on the latch, her back to him. "What did you say your name was?"

"Gardener," Isandro said, "for now."

She laughed — a short, startled laugh, the laugh of someone who has heard an odd bell ring somewhere deep in a house she thought she knew — and then she was gone down the snowy lane, and Isandro stood alone among the sleeping covenant-flowers with his heart pounding like a fist on a door.

* * *

That night he planted the last seed in the packet.

He had saved it all this time — through the flight, the border, the long gray months of deciding — one seed, the largest, darkest, most patient of them all. He had not known why he saved it until tonight. He dug the hole in the center of the spiral bed with his bare hands despite the cold, and set the seed in, and covered it, and knelt there a long while with the snow beginning again around him, soft as forgetting.

He knew what it was. He had known since the marsh, since the night he lost his own memories and she rebuilt him with stories — she had told him, in the telling, what the willing covenant would ask, and later, in the first bloom of the reforged world, the keystones had shown him what her mother had left behind: not only a message, but a planting. A seed sown in honor, waiting in the deep memory of the soil for the one condition that could wake it.

*A flower that only grows when spoken to by name.*

Her mother had planted such a seed somewhere — he was nearly sure of it, nearly sure it waited already in some corner of the valley for Wendell's voice. But Isandro was a guardian, and guardians hedge their hopes. If her mother's seed failed, or slept too long, or if fate needed a second door — then there would be this one. His. Planted in the covenant-garden, in soil soaked with her sacrifice, tended by the one person in two realms who had reason every single day to want it to bloom.

He pressed his palms to the cold earth over the seed.

"Bloom when she says your name," he whispered. "Not mine. Hers. Wait for her. However long it takes — wait for her."

The snow fell. The garden breathed. Somewhere across the dark valley, in a cottage with a banked fire, a woman who had forgotten him was dreaming — he was sure of it, sure the way he was sure of seasons — dreaming in a language she had never learned, of a bridge of light and a hand holding hers.

Isandro stayed kneeling until the cold drove him in, and the last thing he did, before climbing to his loft, was to write on the seed-packet's empty paper, in the old tongue, in ink he mixed from covenant-flower petals, a single line:

*Some things are planted deeper than memory. Tend them anyway.*

And in the morning, when the village children came tramping through the snow to their lessons, they stopped at the spiral bed and shouted for him, and he came running, and there — impossibly, out of frozen ground, in the dead of winter — a single green shoot stood a finger high, unfurling its first leaf toward the cottage on the hill.

None of them could say why it was growing. None of them could say what it was waiting for.

But it was growing.
