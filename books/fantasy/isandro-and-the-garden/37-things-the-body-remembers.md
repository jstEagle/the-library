## 37. Things the Body Remembers

The frost came early that year, and Wendell woke before dawn to cover the seedlings with straw, as she had done every autumn since she could hold a trowel. Her hands knew the work without her. That was the strange mercy of her life now — the body remembered what the mind had lost, and so long as her fingers were in the soil, she did not have to grieve anything in particular.

She had stopped trying to name the grief months ago. It was like weather. It arrived without explanation and left the same way, and she had learned to let it pass through her rather than fight it, because fighting it only made her weep over trays of marigolds, and the marigolds deserved better.

The new gardener was already in the walled garden when she came through the gate.

He had been there three weeks. He'd arrived on a gray morning with a letter of introduction from some guild downriver — she hadn't read it closely — and a canvas satchel that held, as far as she could tell, nothing but seeds. He was tall and quiet and moved through the beds like a man who had loved gardens for a very long time, longer than his face suggested. His hands were wrong for a laborer's hands, too fine-boned, too careful, and there was a scar along his forearm like a branch-split in bark that he never spoke about and she never asked after. She had learned not to ask after things. Asking opened doors in her that led to rooms with no furniture, rooms where something had been taken out and the shape of it still pressed into the floor.

"Morning," he said, not looking up from the row of winter kale he was thinning.

"Morning." She knelt at the far end of the same row, because that was how they worked — always near each other, always with a bed's width between them, an arrangement neither of them had discussed and both of them kept as carefully as a vow.

The mist was lifting off the valley. Beyond the wall, the orchards were turning gold, and the mountains to the east stood clear for once, their high passes dusted white. Somewhere past those passes lay the border, though no one called it a border anymore. The covenant had been reforged — everyone said so, though no one could tell her by whom, or when, or why the guild records all bore scorch marks, or why the queen's edicts now read *the gifted shall be sheltered* where they had once read *surrendered*. The world had changed while she wasn't looking, it seemed. Or perhaps while she *was* looking, and simply couldn't hold on to any of it.

Her gift had come back with the covenant. That much she knew, the way you know your own pulse. The shimmer her hands left on living things — the faint green-gold light she had spent her whole childhood hiding under gloves and long sleeves — was hers again, unhidden, even welcomed. The children who came to the guild's lessons on rest-days watched her coax seedlings into bloom and clapped their hands as if it were a fair trick. She performed for them gladly. But alone, in the dark, she sometimes held her glowing palms up to her face and felt like a woman reading a letter written in her own handwriting that she could not remember writing.

"You've got frost-burn on the basil," the gardener said.

"I know. I saw."

"The third pot should come inside."

"I *know*." She heard the edge in her own voice and didn't understand it. He was only trying to help. He was always only trying to help, and she was always snapping at him for reasons that lived below the waterline of her memory, in whatever drowned country her grief had come from.

He took the rebuke without offense. He always did. There was something almost practiced in his patience, as though he had learned long ago exactly how much of her sharpness to absorb and how much to deflect, the way one learns the moods of a particular horse, or a particular season.

They worked. The sun climbed. And the plants leaned.

Wendell noticed it first with the rosemary. She was pruning it, and the new gardener passed behind her to reach the comfrey bed, and every stem of rosemary within arm's reach of them both turned — slowly, gently, unmistakably — toward the space between their two bodies, the way sunflowers turn toward the sun.

She went still.

It happened again with the beans. She was training them up their strings and he was watering the next bed over, and the whole row curved toward them, leaves trembling, tendrils reaching across the gap like fingers stretching in sleep.

"Wendell." His voice was quiet. He had seen it too. He was standing very still among the wet greens, and his face had gone carefully blank in a way that made her think of doors closing.

"They're doing it again," she said. "The plants."

"I see it."

"It's me. My gift does this — things grow toward me, sometimes, when I'm—" She stopped. Because it wasn't just her. She could feel it now, feel it plainly, the way you can feel a chord played slightly wrong: her gift rising in her hands like warmth from a hearthstone, and meeting something answering from him. Not his gift — he had none, or none she could sense; she would have known, she knew the taste of other gifted folk now the way she knew the taste of rain coming. No. What answered in him was older and stranger. A resonance. As if her power were a bell and he were the shape of its silence.

"When you're near me," she said slowly, "they lean toward *us*. Both of us. I've been watching for a week." She hadn't meant to admit that. "I thought I was imagining it."

"Plants do odd things in autumn," he said. "Cold makes them—"

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended, and she saw him flinch, actually flinch, a small motion in his shoulders like a man braced against a blow he has been expecting for years. "Don't explain it away. Please." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated that too. "Everything gets explained away. Everyone is so kind to me, so careful, and nobody will tell me anything true. Why are you here? Three weeks you've worked beside me and I don't know anything about you except that you're the finest gardener I've ever seen and you look at me like—"

She stopped again. Because she couldn't finish that sentence either. Like what? Like a man reading a letter? Like someone keeping a vigil?

He set down the watering can. For a moment she thought he would leave — there was a readiness in him, a coiled thing, as though leaving were a skill he had mastered — but instead he crouched by the bean row and held out his hand, palm up, above the soil.

"Watch," he said.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened; he had no gift. But the nearest bean plant stirred anyway, straining toward his open hand, and then — Wendell's breath caught — toward the space between his hand and hers, where the air itself seemed to thicken, to go green-gold at the edges, though neither of them was calling any light.

"That's not me," she whispered.

"No," he said. "I don't believe it is."

"What is it, then?"

He looked up at her, and for a moment his careful face failed him entirely, and what was underneath it was so raw and so old and so patient that she took a step back without meaning to, her heel finding the soft turned earth of the bed.

"I don't know," he said. "I honestly don't know. But I don't think it's harming anything." He rose, brushed off his knees, and picked up the watering can again, and just like that the door closed and the careful man was back. "The third pot," he said gently. "Really. The basil won't forgive you twice."

* * *

That night she dreamed in a language she had never learned.

She knew it had never been learned because part of her — the part that stood at the edge of dreams, taking notes, the part that stayed awake while the rest slept — recognized its own ignorance. The words came out of her mouth fluently, rolling and rooted, full of long vowels like wind through wheat and consonants like stones settling in a streambed, and she understood every one of them, and she could not have translated a single sentence to save her life.

In the dream she was walking a road of white stone through a forest of trees she had no names for, trees with silver bark and leaves that chimed. Someone walked beside her. In dreams you never see the face of the one who matters most; the dream keeps them turned away, or keeps your eyes lowered, or fills their place with light. But she knew the walker the way she knew her own heartbeat, and they were talking — she was talking — saying something urgent and tender, saying it over and over in that rooted, rolling language, and each time she said it the trees around them grew taller, and the chime of the leaves resolved into something almost like words themselves, almost like—

She woke with the dream-words still in her mouth.

She lay in the dark of her room above the guild hall and said them aloud, carefully, the way you carry water without spilling it. They sounded beautiful. They sounded *familiar*, which was worse than beautiful, because familiarity without origin is a kind of haunting. Her tongue knew the shapes. Her throat knew the music. Nothing above her collarbone knew anything at all.

She got up and lit the lamp and wrote the words down phonetically in the back of her grandmother's old almanac, sounding them out syllable by syllable, and when she finished she sat looking at the nonsense letters and felt tears running down her face without any grief attached to them, the way a cut bleeds without asking permission.

There were seven words. She was certain there were seven. And she was certain — with the same deep-body certainty that told her how to graft a rose or when the first frost would come — that the seventh word was a name.

She could not say it. Every time her mouth moved toward it, the dream closed around it like a hand, and she woke a little more, and the name slid back beneath the water.

* * *

The dreams did not stop. They came every night now, and each morning the language sat a little deeper in her, and she began catching herself humming fragments of it while she worked, little melodies of speech that made the seedlings in the cold frames unfurl a day early.

She told no one. Who would she tell? The guild elders were kind and useless; they looked at her with such gentle pity whenever she asked questions about the covenant, the breaking, the thirteen missing years, that she had learned to swallow the questions whole. Rell came sometimes — Captain Rell, her oldest friend, though *friend* had gone strange in her mouth since the hunt, since whatever had happened during the hunt that everyone tiptoed around — and Rell remembered things she didn't, but when she asked him about the border, about the bridge, about the night her parents vanished, his face did something complicated and wounded and he said, "You're better off, Wen. Trust me. Some knowledge costs more than it pays." Which was the sort of thing people said when they were protecting you, or protecting themselves, or both.

So she carried the language alone, and she carried the leaning plants alone, and she carried the new gardener's careful face alone, and the weight of it all settled into her shoulders until one gray afternoon she found herself standing in the middle of the herb bed with her hands full of cut sage and no memory of having cut it, weeping again, weather moving through her again.

The gardener found her there. He didn't ask what was wrong. He simply took the sage from her arms, set it in the basket, and stood beside her in the drizzle, close enough that their sleeves nearly touched, and the whole bed of herbs turned toward them — thyme and sorrel and the last stubborn lavender — leaning in like a congregation, like a crowd drawing near a fire.

"Do you feel that?" she asked.

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"I told you. I don't know."

"But you're not afraid of it."

He was quiet for a long time. Rain dripped from the eaves of the glasshouse. Somewhere beyond the wall, a cart rattled through the village square, and a child laughed, ordinary sounds, human sounds, the sounds of a valley that had forgotten it had ever been silent.

"No," he said finally. "I'm not afraid of it. Whatever it is — whatever *we* are, standing together like this — I don't think it means us harm." He looked down at the leaning herbs, and something moved across his face like cloud-shadow. "I think it might be the truest thing I've ever seen."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed softly. "It isn't."

She should have demanded more. She was the guild's heir — she knew that much, at least; the sigil was tattooed over her heart and the elders bowed to her and the deed to the garden bore her name — and she had the right, probably, to demand answers from anyone who worked her soil. Instead she heard herself say, "Will you be here tomorrow?"

"Every day," he said. "As long as you'll have me."

And there it was again, that undertone, that double-bottomed boat of a sentence that floated lightly on the surface and held something vast and dark beneath. *As long as you'll have me.* As if the having were in question. As if he had asked once, long ago, and was still waiting for the answer, and had made his peace with waiting forever.

* * *

That night the dream changed.

She was back on the white road in the silver-leaved forest, and the unnamed walker was beside her, closer than before, and this time the dream let her hear the seventh word. Not speak it — hear it. From outside herself, as if someone else were saying it, calling it across a garden, calling it the way you call a name you have said ten thousand times and expect to say ten thousand more:

*Isandro.*

She woke sitting bolt upright, the word ringing in her ears, her heart slamming, tears already on her face — and this time the grief came with them, huge and specific and utterly sourceless, a flood with no river, mourning with no grave. She sat in the dark repeating the name under her breath like a charm against drowning, and the strangest thing happened: the plants on her windowsill — the little pots of basil she had finally brought inside, the trailing ivy, the stubborn rosemary cutting — every one of them turned toward her in the moonless dark, straining toward the name as if the name were the sun.

*Isandro,* she said again, aloud, testing it, tasting it.

The rosemary bloomed.

Out of season, overnight, impossibly — every needle-tipped stem suddenly crowned with tiny pale flowers, filling her dark room with scent, and somewhere far away, or perhaps not far away at all, she heard — or imagined she heard, or perhaps her blood merely remembered hearing — a voice answer her across a garden, saying her own name like a prayer:

*Wendell.*

She was on her feet before she knew it, barefoot, lamp unlit, heart hammering out a rhythm older than her grief. Downstairs, through the sleeping guild hall, out into the cold garden where her breath hung silver in the air — and there, at the far end of the walled beds, in the plot no one had planted yet, in the empty earth she had been saving for spring —

a single flower was opening.

She had never seen its like. Silver-stemmed, seven-petaled, glowing faintly green-gold in the dark, glowing the exact color of the shimmer her own hands left on living things. It had not been there yesterday. It had not been there an hour ago. It grew where nothing had been planted, in soil that had lain fallow all season, and it was turned toward her — waiting, she understood with a certainty that lived below all her knowing, in the country the flood had drowned —

waiting for her to say the name again.
