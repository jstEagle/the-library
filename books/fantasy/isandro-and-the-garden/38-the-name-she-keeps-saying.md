## 38. The Name She Keeps Saying

The first bloom came on a morning so still that Wendell could hear the frost leaving the grass.

She had risen before the sun, as she always did now, and gone out into the covenant-garden with her sleeves rolled and her hands bare against the cold. The garden had been Isandro's work for most of a year — his patient, wordless, maddening work — but lately her hands had begun to know it too, the way they knew the guild garden's rows without knowing why. Her fingers found the soil's moods. They knew when the moonvetch wanted turning and when the silverwort needed to be left alone to sulk. They knew things her mind had never been taught.

That morning, in the eastern bed where nothing had ever grown — the bed Isandro had planted last autumn with seeds from a packet she did not remember giving him, though her handwriting was on it — something had opened.

It was a flower unlike anything in either realm, though of course she only knew one realm now, didn't she, whatever the dreams said. It stood on a stem like green glass, slender and trembling, and its petals were the color of the moment just before dawn: not white, not gold, but the color light makes when it is deciding what to be. There were seven petals, and each one held a drop of dew that did not fall no matter how the stem swayed.

Wendell knelt in the wet grass and did not touch it.

She had learned that much about herself in the past year — that some things must not be touched until you understand what touching will cost. The gift in her hands answered to growing things, and to memory; the old woman in the market town had told her that once, or someone had, in a voice like ash falling. She couldn't remember who. There were holes in her the size of seasons, the size of people, and she had learned to walk around them the way you learn to step over the loose stair that creaks.

But this flower. This flower made the holes ache.

She leaned close. The dew-drops caught the graying sky and held it, seven small skies. And beneath the scent of the petals — beneath something like rain and something like woodsmoke and something like a name spoken into hair at night — she caught another fragrance, faint and impossible:

Her mother's hands. Crushed thyme and beeswax and the particular soap from the guild kitchen that no one had made in thirteen years.

Wendell sat back on her heels. Her heart was doing something strange, beating in a rhythm she didn't recognize, quick and then slow, quick and then slow, like a door being knocked upon from the inside.

"Hello," she said softly, because her grandmother had taught her that all living things deserve a greeting, and because speaking felt safer than the silence that was pressing in around the flower like water around a stone.

The flower turned toward her voice. Not drifted — *turned*, deliberate as a face.

Wendell's breath left her in a thin white ribbon. "Well," she managed. "You're an honest thing, aren't you."

She looked over her shoulder toward the gardener's cottage at the garden's edge, where a lamp was already lit. He rose early too. He always rose early too, and she had stopped asking herself why that comforted her, why the sight of his silhouette at the window made something in her chest lean like a plant toward light. Some questions she had learned not to ask. The valley was full of them now, growing wild along the fences of her mind.

But the flower was turning again, following her gaze to the cottage, and then turning back to her, and there was something in the motion — something almost imperious, almost *imploring* — that broke through every careful fence she'd built.

"What do you want?" she whispered.

The dew-drops trembled. And in the smallest of them, the one lowest on the innermost petal, she saw — or thought she saw, or remembered seeing, which by now amounted to the same thing — a reflection that was not the sky. A woman's face. Dark-haired, laughing, with a smudge of soil on her cheekbone and eyes the exact color of Wendell's own.

"Mother," Wendell said, and the word cracked down the middle.

* * *

She did not go to the cottage. Later she would understand that this was the gift working in her, the gift answering to memory, steering her the way water is steered by stone — but in the moment it felt only like instinct, like the same blind knowledge that put her hands in the soil each morning. She stayed kneeling in the frost-wet grass while the sun came up over the valley and lit the covenant-garden gold and silver and green, and she spoke to the flower the way she had once, in a life she couldn't recall, spoken to growing things to make them open.

"I don't know your name," she told it. "I don't know how you came here, or who planted you, though I think I know whose packet you came from." Her voice shook. "There are things I've lost. I know that the way you know weather coming. I feel the shape of them — the empty places. There's a person-shaped hole in me. Maybe more than one. Maybe—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Maybe a whole year of holes. Maybe more."

The flower listened. She could feel it listening, the way she could feel the moonvetch listening, the way she had always been able to feel it even before she knew that feeling was a gift and not a strangeness.

"My mother left me messages once," Wendell said. "I don't remember that either. But my hands remember. When I work the soil in the old guild beds, my hands do things — little ceremonies, little courtesies — and I think those were hers. I think she taught me with my hands because she knew my head would be robbed." The anger came up in her then, sudden and hot, at whoever had done the robbing, at whatever price had been paid, at the whole silent architecture of loss she lived inside. "So if you're hers — if she grew you, if she sent you — then tell me. Please. I'm asking. I have spent a year asking everything except the one question that matters, and I am so tired, and I am so—"

Her voice failed.

And the flower, patient as a parent, waited for her to finish grieving before it gave her the next thing, which was this: as the sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the seven dew-drops full, they flared — all seven at once, like lamps lighting — and from the heart of the bloom rose a scent so specific, so devastatingly particular, that Wendell made a sound she had never heard herself make before.

Rain on hot stone. Woodsmoke. Cedar and starlight and something green and wild from very far away.

And underneath it all, unmistakable, woven through it like a thread through cloth: a man's voice saying a name.

Not her name. His.

She didn't know how she knew it was his. She knew the way she knew the garden. The way her hands knew the soil. The name arrived in her not as information but as recognition, the way a key arrives in a lock — and somewhere behind her ribs, in the person-shaped hole, something moved for the first time in a year.

"*Isandro*," Wendell said.

The world changed.

* * *

It did not come back all at once. That was the mercy of it, and also the cruelty — it came back the way spring comes, in waves, in insistences, in things pushing up through ground that had seemed dead.

First the garden. Not this garden — the other one, the walled guild garden, the last of its kind, where she had hidden the shimmer of her hands and believed herself safe. The herald's proclamation nailed to the gate. Her grandmother's burning records. The seed-packet pressed into her palms with a map folded inside it, and the smoke rising behind her as she ran.

Then him. Him, collapsing through her gate with bark-like scarring climbing his throat, calling her by a name no one used — and oh, oh, she remembered the name he'd called her, she remembered how it had sounded in his ruined voice, and she remembered the keystone humming under her fingers like a struck bell, and she remembered—

The tunnels. The torchlight. Crossing the boundary with magic flooding back into her body like a river returning to a dried bed, overwhelming, beautiful, terrifying, and his hand closed around hers, steadying her, anchoring her, and his face above her in the dark of the border hills.

The road of ash. Crops that remembered being forests. The drowned chapel and the wraith wearing her parents' faces. The market of small magics and the old woman's warning. Rell at the crossroads, offering her a falsified death, and letting her go anyway. The queen's conservatory, the caged gifted, their memories stripped — and the woman with her mother's hands, who had not known her, and the stolen memory that surfaced anyway: *they paid the price, and it failed.*

The bridge of severed light. The vision of the covenant's making. Isandro's confession in the dark — *reforging requires a memory-sacrifice from its heir, all memories tied to what she loves most* — and the sound of her own footsteps walking away from him into the cold.

Her mother's sealed message, unlocked under the ninth moon: *shared willingly by two, rather than taken from one.* The marsh of forgetting, and finding him there hollowed out, not knowing her name, and the long tender labor of rebuilding him story by story — *this is who you are, this is what we did, this is why I came back for you* — and understanding, in the telling, exactly what the willing covenant would ask.

Rell's bargain. Her refusal. Rell choosing her side and becoming a hunted traitor. The fourth keystone and the first guardian's ghost, weeping out her confession: love withheld from the price cost more. The eleventh moon, the army, the machine. The queen's face as the truth showed her what her fear had built. Rell breaking the core, laughing, paying with his childhood the way Wendell would pay with her love.

The twelfth moon. The bridge open for one night. Four keystones. Two voices speaking the covenant with open eyes.

His hand in hers.

*Hold on to me,* he had said. *Whatever it takes from you, hold on while it goes.*

And she had. She had held on. She had felt the covenant come like a tide, gentle and absolute, lifting the memories out of her one by one — the garden, the road, the chapel, the marsh, the bridge — and last of all, saved for last, kept longest, him: his voice, his scarred hands, his name, the weight of his forehead against hers at the threshold of everything —

Gone.

And then waking in a valley that hummed with returned magic, in a world remade, with holes in her the size of a year, and a stranger gardening at the edge of her life who watched her when he thought she wouldn't see, whose silence had a shape she recognized without knowing why, whose hands — she had noticed, she had always noticed — bore scars like bark.

* * *

Wendell came back to herself kneeling in the eastern bed with her arms wrapped around her own body and tears freezing on her cheeks, and the flower blazing gently in the morning light, and the name sitting in her mouth like a stone she had carried across a country without knowing it.

Isandro.

He was standing at the garden gate.

She didn't know how long he'd been there. Long enough. His face — she could read it now, she could read every line of it, and the reading broke her — his face was the face of a man watching the thing he had spent a year hoping for and dreading in equal measure arrive at last. Hope and dread had fought to a standstill on his features and left only a terrible stillness.

"You knew," Wendell said. Her voice came out ruined. "You've known all year."

He didn't deny it. He never would have; she remembered that about him now, one of ten thousand things, the integrity in him like grain in wood. "I hoped," he said quietly. "Your mother's message said the flower would grow only when spoken to by name. I planted it the week after the covenant took you. I have watered it every morning for a year and told myself every morning that maybe it would never bloom, and maybe that would be kinder, and every morning I hated myself a little for watering it anyway." His hand tightened on the gate. "I couldn't stop tending it. It was yours. It was the last thing of yours I had."

"Why didn't you tell me? Why didn't you just say your name?"

"Because it had to be yours." His voice frayed. "Because the covenant took your memories willingly given, and I would not take them back any other way. Because if I said *Isandro* to you a hundred times, and the hundred-and-first time you finally said it back, it would be my doing, not yours — and your mother's message was clear, Wendell. It grows when *you* speak. Love can't be handed back. It has to be chosen again." He laughed, and it was half a sob. "Do you know how many mornings I stood at that gate wanting to shout it across the garden? Do you know what it cost me to watch you look at me like a stranger and be polite to me and thank me for the compost?"

"Yes," Wendell said. "Yes, I know exactly what it cost, because I remember now, I remember *everything*, and I remember that you did the same thing for me in the marsh. You forgot me entirely and I rebuilt you, and you sat there taking my stories like medicine, and you never once pretended to remember more than you did." She got to her feet. Her legs were shaking. The frost was melting off the grass around her in a widening circle, steaming faintly, the gift answering the storm in her blood. "You let me forget you for a year rather than steal back what I gave."

"It wasn't stealing back," Isandro said. "It would have been."

"It would have been," she agreed, and crossed the garden to him.

She walked through the eastern bed without caring what she trampled, past the moonvetch and the silverwort, past the young trees from the other realm that had taken root along the southern wall, and the plants leaned after her as she went — she felt them leaning, all of them, the whole garden bending toward her like a congregation — and Isandro opened the gate and met her in the path between the beds and she stopped one step away from him, close enough to see that his eyes were wet, close enough to smell cedar and starlight and rain on hot stone.

"I said your name," she told him, "and I remembered everything, and do you want to know the first thing I remembered? Not the garden. Not the road. Not the bridge." Her breath caught. "Your hand. In mine. While it all went. You held on to me while I lost you, and you were the last thing to go, and I think — I think some part of me refused to let all of you leave. I think that's why the holes ached. I think you're still in there, Isandro. I think you were in there the whole time, the way a seed is in the ground all winter."

"Wendell—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and the gesture was her mother's, she knew that now, she knew everything about her mother now. "Let me finish, because I have a year of unfinished sentences to get through and I intend to start immediately." She was crying and laughing at once and it was undignified and she did not care. "I chose the price with open eyes. Both of us did. We said the words together, and the covenant took what it took, and it was worth it — the realms are whole, the valley lives, the children in the guild school have magic and memory side by side. I would pay it again. I need you to hear that: I would pay it again, gladly, a hundred times. But I am never — *never* — doing it without a way back. Your mother-in-law, if we're being technical, was smarter than both of us. She planted the way home inside the price itself." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "A flower that only grows when spoken to by name. She hid a key inside the lock. That devious, brilliant, beloved woman."

Isandro made a sound that was mostly laughter and mostly grief, and stepped forward, and stopped himself, one step short, his hands opening and closing at his sides — asking permission, even now, even after everything.

"Oh, for the love of both realms," Wendell said, and closed the distance herself.

Holding him was like holding the answer to a question she'd been asking in her sleep for a year. Her body knew him — her hands found the bark-scarring at his throat without looking, her cheek fit against his shoulder as if the shape had been kept warm for her — and the garden responded, she felt it respond, every living thing in the covenant-garden surging at once, a green tide of joy running through root and stem and leaf, blossoms opening all down the rows in a soft successive popping like distant applause. The seven-petaled flower in the eastern bed lifted its face toward them and the dew-drops blazed and somewhere, impossibly, in their light, Wendell would have sworn she heard her mother laugh.

"I kept saying a name," she murmured into his shoulder. "All winter. Rell told me — he visits, he remembers me now if not our childhood, it's a start — he told me I talked in my sleep sometimes, and it was always the same word, and none of us knew what it meant." She pulled back far enough to look at him, to look her fill, a year of looking owed. "*Isandro*. I kept saying your name in the dark, Isandro, and I didn't even know why. My mouth remembered what my mind had paid away."

"The body keeps what the covenant cannot reach," he said softly. "I told you that once. On the road, teaching you to hold the gift. You didn't believe me."

"I believe you now."

Above them, high and thin in the brightening sky, the moon hung pale and nearly full — waxing toward its turn, the first turn of the new count, the new covenant's first turning year. Thirteen turns from now it would complete a cycle, and the guild would hold its first renewal rite, and the price would be shared instead of stolen, two voices instead of one sacrifice. They had planned it together in the weeks since the reforging, in the long evenings in the guild hall, with the queen's blessing lettered on vellum and Rell's grudging signature at the bottom. She remembered planning it. She remembered all of it. Everything fit now; the holes were full; the loose stair was mended.

Almost.

Because there, at the edge of the fullness, at the rim of her restored memory where the covenant's tide had receded, something glinted that she had not yet examined — a single thread left loose, deliberately, she realized, deliberately left by the flower, by her mother's design. One memory withheld from the flood. One thing the seven-petaled bloom had kept back, waiting, the way a gardener holds back the last seed until the season is right.

Wendell went very still in Isandro's arms.

"What is it?" he asked, because he knew her, he had always known her, he could read a change in her breathing across a room.

"She left me one more thing," Wendell said slowly. "In the flower. Something it hasn't shown me yet." She turned her head toward the eastern bed, where the bloom stood luminous among the trampled frost, its seven dew-drops catching the risen sun — and in the innermost drop, the lowest one, the one that had held her mother's face, something else was reflected now. Not a face. A place. A hillside she did not recognize, under a moon she had never seen from this valley, and a figure standing on it, waiting, with a seed-packet in one hand.

"Mother," Wendell breathed, "what did you plant?"

The flower opened one petal wider, like a hand uncurling, like a door.

And in the drop of dew that did not fall, the figure on the far hillside raised the seed-packet — and waved.
