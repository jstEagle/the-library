## 40. Isandro and the Garden

The morning came up gold through the guild garden's new eastern wall, and Isandro was there before the light, as he had been every morning for a year.

He knelt in the loam with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, his hands dark to the wrist, and he did the work the way the old records said the first gardeners had done it: slowly, and with attention, as if each seed were a word in a sentence that must not be mispronounced. The soil of the valley remembered being magicless. It held a kind of gratitude now, a looseness, an eagerness — things went down into it gladly and came up astonished. He had learned to plant by feel here, because the valley's ground answered differently than the ground of his own realm. In Isandro, earth received what you gave it. Here, earth *conversed*.

This morning he was setting out the moonbell roots that had come through the bridge with him, wrapped in wet cloth, half-dead from the crossing, and which had taken a full season to decide they were willing to live on this side of the sky. They were willing now. Their pale shoots had broken the surface three days ago, curled like sleeping fingers, and when the sun touched them they would open into bells of white glass that rang — not audibly, not exactly, but in the register where Wendell's gift lived, in the place behind her sternum where growing things spoke to her.

He knew her footsteps before he heard them. Every plant in the garden knew them first. Along the whole southern bed, the feverfew leaned east, toward the gate, toward her, a slow green tide of attention, and Isandro felt the pull of it in his own chest like a second heartbeat learning to keep time.

She came through the gate with two cups of barley tea and dirt already on one knee, which meant she had been out since before dawn at the children's plot below the hill, where the guild's first students had their own small beds.

"You're talking to them again," she said.

"I'm listening," he said. "There's a difference."

"There is." She handed him a cup and sat down beside him in the dirt without ceremony, without care for her dress, the way she had sat beside him on the Road of Ash and in the drowned chapel and on the border bridge under the twelfth moon, when the world had been ending and neither of them had known yet how it would be mended. "The moonbells are saying something. I can't make it out yet."

"They'll say it when they're ready. That's what bells do."

She drank her tea and looked out over the garden — *their* garden, though she did not know the history of that word between them, only its present weight — and Isandro watched her look, and let himself love her in the quiet way he had practiced for a year, the way a man loves a thing he has already lost once and been given back.

Because that was the truth he carried alone, folded small against his ribs: he remembered everything. The garden, the gate, the keystone humming under her fingers. The road. The marsh where he had forgotten her name and she had rebuilt him out of stories, memory by memory, like a gardener coaxing a dead tree back into leaf. The bridge. The price. Her hand in his while the covenant took from her everything they had been to each other, and her face as she paid it — not afraid, *choosing*, choosing with open eyes exactly as her mother's message had promised could be done — and then the moment after, when she had looked at him with the polite blankness of a stranger and asked if he was the new gardener the council had sent for.

He had said yes. It was the truest lie of his life.

One year. And every day of it, he had chosen not to tell her. Not because he feared her grief — he had learned her courage too well for that — but because her mother's message had been specific, and he had read it a hundred times in the year since, in the living script of the covenant-garden itself: *the remembering must come from her, or it will not hold.* A memory forced open is a wound. A memory grown is a flower. He was a guardian; guarding was the whole of him; and so he guarded the door of her past the way he guarded seedlings — patiently, in the dark, trusting the season.

Besides. There were worse lives than this. There were worse lives than loving someone who did not remember loving you, when you got to spend every day beside her among growing things that leaned toward you both.

"Wendell!" A child's voice from the gate — Tam, seven years old, gap-toothed, gifted with a touch that made bread rise twice as high and made his grandmother weep with relief instead of fear. Behind him came the others, six of them now, the guild's first students since the restoration, trailing mud and wonder. "Wendell, the beanstalk grew *overnight*, it went over the fence, Marta says it's trying to climb to the moon—"

"It's trying to climb toward the sun," Wendell said, standing, brushing off her knees. "Everything does. Come show me."

Isandro stayed kneeling among the moonbells and watched her go, her hand resting a moment on each child's head as they crowded past, the faint shimmer of her gift waking green along the beds she passed like a tide following a boat. The last student, a solemn girl named Ivy who could hear what stones dreamed, paused beside him and looked at the moonbell shoots with her old-woman eyes.

"They're waiting for something," Ivy said.

"Yes."

"Waiting isn't sad," she said, in the tone of a child stating physics. "Waiting is just growing slowly."

Then she ran after the others, and Isandro laughed — really laughed, the first full laugh of the morning — and put his hands back in the earth.

* * *

Wendell had a secret, and it was getting heavier.

It sat in the bottom of her seed-chest, in a paper packet gone soft and brown with age, and it had sat there for a year, and she had not planted it, and she could not have told anyone why — not Rell, who came up from the village every few weeks with his careful, patient face and his pockets full of questions he no longer knew how to ask; not Grandmother, whose mind had gone soft as overripe fruit but whose hands still moved in the old gestures whenever Wendell brought her cuttings; not even Isandro, who noticed everything about plants and nothing, apparently, about her silences, or who noticed those too and was too courteous to name them.

The packet had been in the seed-chest when she'd reopened the guild house after the reforging. She had assumed it was the same packet Grandmother had pressed into her hands the night the hunters came — the night the garden burned and the tunnels carried them out under the smoke. But when she'd opened it, the seeds inside had been wrong. Not wrong — *impossible*. Silver-grey, shaped like tiny crescent moons, cool to the touch in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Cool the way the keystones had been cool. Cool the way the border bridge had been cool under her feet.

And written on the packet, in ink that had faded but not vanished, in a hand she somehow knew the way you know a face from a dream:

*For my daughter. Plant it when the garden is real.*

Her mother's hand. She was certain of it the way she was certain of certain other things that had no evidence — that feverfew leaned toward kindness, that the third bed wanted rosemary, that the man who knelt every morning in the eastern plot with dirt to his elbows was the truest thing in her life, though she could not have said when she had learned it or why the knowing ached so pleasantly, like a muscle remembering an old dance.

She didn't remember her parents. That wound had been old before the covenant, sealed by time and then resealed by sacrifice, and the healers had told her some doors close forever. She had grieved them twice, in effect — once as a child, once as an heir — and both times the grief had been strangely shapeless, mourning a portrait rather than a person.

But the packet was a person. The packet was a voice.

*Plant it when the garden is real.*

Well. She looked out over it now, from the top of the hill, while the children chased each other between the beds below. The guild garden stood whole within the covenant-garden, and the covenant-garden stood whole within the valley, and the valley — the valley that magic had once been unable to follow, the valley that had been a locked room for thirteen years — was green in a way the oldest villagers swore it had never been, even in the stories. Plants from both realms shared the rows now: valley barley shoulder to shoulder with moonbell, Isandrian glassfruit ripening on trellises beside honest valley plums, and everywhere the strange hybrid things that happened on their own, unprompted, as if the two floras had been separated lovers making up for lost time. The queen's edict hung fair-copyied on the guildhouse wall, signed in the crown's own hand: *the hunt is ended; the gifted are citizens; the covenant is shared.* Rell's name was on it too, in smaller letters, as the crown's witness — his redemption purchased with the memories the machine's backlash had taken, so that he remembered Wendell as his captain's daughter, a comrade, a duty honorably discharged, and nothing more. He never seemed unhappy about it. Some erasures were mercies. She had learned to let him have his.

The garden was real. By any measure her mother might have meant, the garden was real.

So why had her hands shaken this morning, taking the packet from the chest? Why had she carried it all day in her apron pocket like a coal?

Because she knew, the way she knew things about plants, that this was not a planting. It was a *door*. And every door in her life that had opened onto her mother had led somewhere that cost her something.

Below, Isandro was showing Tam how to stake the runaway beanstalk, his voice low and amused, the children orbiting him like moths. When he laughed, the whole eastern bed turned toward the sound.

Wendell put her hand flat over her apron pocket, over the crescent seeds, and felt them hum — faintly, patiently, the way the first keystone had hummed under her fingers in another life she couldn't recall.

Tonight, she decided. Tonight, after the children went home. Tonight she would stop waiting.

Waiting wasn't sad. Waiting was just growing slowly. But everything, eventually, climbed toward the sun.

* * *

Isandro found her at dusk in the eastern plot, kneeling where the moonbells bloomed, a trowel in her hand and the little silver packet open on her knee.

He stopped at the gate. He did not mean to stop — his body simply refused the next step, the way it had refused, a year ago, on the far side of the bridge, when the covenant closed around her memories like water closing over a stone. Because he knew what she held. He had known since the first week, when he'd seen the packet in her seed-chest and recognized the seal — his own realm's wax, the guardian's mark, pressed by hands long dust. Her mother had carried it across the bridge thirteen years ago. Her mother had paid the price, and failed, and left this anyway: one seed, grown in the space between realms, that could only live where both magics mingled. That could only be woken by a name.

*Say the name,* the covenant-garden had whispered to him all winter, in the language of root and bell. *When she plants it, say the name, and stand back, and trust her.*

He trusted her. Trusting her was the easiest discipline of his long, strange life.

But his body still stopped at the gate, because the last door of the year was opening, and on the other side of it she would either remember him or she would not, and he had built a whole quiet life inside the not-knowing, and doors do not care what men have built against them.

She looked up. Dusk had gone violet in the west, and the moonbells had begun their silent ringing, and her face in that light was the face from the garden, from the road, from the bridge — the face he had loved in two lifetimes, one of which only he remembered.

"You knew about these," she said. Not accusing. Wendell did not accuse; she *inquired*, the way she inquired of the earth, gently, expecting truth.

"I suspected," he said. "I hoped."

"What are they?"

"A bridge," he said. "The last one. Your mother grew it." He came forward at last and knelt opposite her across the prepared bed, and the moonbells rang harder, and somewhere behind them the feverfew leaned, and the beans strained at their stakes, and every living thing in the garden bent toward the two of them like grass toward wind. "She couldn't save the covenant her way. So she saved a seed of it. For you."

"For me," Wendell repeated. She looked down at the crescent seeds in her palm. "Isandro—" She stopped. Frowned. "Why did I say that?"

His heart went very still, and very loud.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "I've wondered for a year."

She looked at him for a long moment — that searching look, the one that had been crossing the distance between them all year like a bird testing a wind — and then she did the thing she always did with mysteries too large to solve from above. She knelt lower. She put her hands in the soil. She planted.

Three crescent seeds, in a triangle, in the richest earth of the eastern bed, watered from the cup she'd drunk her tea from, because Wendell believed gifts should be given back to the garden in the same vessel they arrived in. Then she laid her palms flat on the ground over them, and closed her eyes, and Isandro watched her gift move through her like dawn moving through a valley — the shimmer rising in her hands, sinking into the soil, finding the seeds, waking them.

Nothing happened.

Then everything happened.

The ground breathed. He felt it through his knees — a single vast inhale, the whole garden drawing in at once — and from the triangle of planted earth a stem rose, silver and swift, unfurling leaves like the phases of the moon, climbing, climbing, until it stood taller than either of them and opened, at the height of Wendell's heart, a single flower.

It was a bell, like the moonbells. But it was also a keyhole. But it was also, unmistakably, a door.

"Isandro," Wendell said again, and this time she said it the way you say a word you have just discovered you have been saying your whole life — in dreams, in a language she'd never learned, to a face behind her eyes. Her hands came off the soil. They rose, trembling, toward the flower, toward him, undecided. "Isandro. Isandro. Why do I know — why do I *know*—"

"Because you chose it," he said, and his voice broke on the words he had kept for a year. "Because you paid for it. Because you stood on the bridge under the twelfth moon and gave up every memory of me rather than let the realms bleed out, and the covenant took them, and it *held*, Wendell, it held, the realms are whole because of you — and your mother knew that a price freely paid can't truly take what love won't surrender. So she left you a way back. She left you a door that only opens from your side."

The flower chimed — audibly, this time, one clear note like a struck glass — and the note went through Wendell like spring goes through frozen ground.

Isandro saw it happen. He would see it every night for the rest of his life, he thought: the exact moment the year fell away from her eyes. The blankness breaking like ice on a river. The recognition flooding up from wherever her memories had been kept — not returning, *blooming*, all of them at once, the garden and the gate and the road of ash and the marsh where she had named him back to himself, the bridge, the price, his hand in hers — and her face doing four things in the space of a breath: shock, grief, joy, and then a ferocious, tearful, laughing clarity, as if she had walked into a dark room and found it full of light.

"You *waited*," she said. "A year. You knelt in the dirt every morning and you waited, you absolute—"

"You told me to," he managed. "Your mother told me. The remembering had to come from you—"

"You could have *said*—"

"And risked forcing a door that had to grow?" He was crying too now, helplessly, the guardian's discipline gone entirely to ruin. "Wendell. Gardener. Heir of the covenant. I have spent a year loving you in the only grammar I was permitted, and I would have spent thirty. I would have spent a hundred. Waiting is just growing slowly."

She crossed the space between them and took his face in her dirty hands and kissed him, and every plant in the garden leaned in — the moonbells ringing, the beans straining their stakes, the feverfew bowing like a congregation, the new flower pouring its single note over them both like a blessing — and when she pulled back she was laughing and weeping in equal measure, and she said:

"The garden is real."

"It's real," he agreed.

"Then we have work to do," said Wendell Thornbury, last apprentice and first mistress of the restored guild, keeper of the reforged covenant, the woman who had paid the price and grown herself a door back through it. She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, leaving a streak of loam across her cheekbone, and looked around at the garden glowing in the dusk — both realms' flowers sharing one soil, the children's plots dark and dreaming on the hill, the valley beyond breathing easy under a moon that had finished its thirteenth turn a year ago and begun, quietly, hopefully, counting again.

Above them, the new flower released its first seed. It caught the last light, silver as a crescent moon, and drifted down toward the unplanned earth at the garden's edge — toward the wild strip where nothing had been planted and anything might grow.

Wendell watched it fall, and her hand found Isandro's, and she smiled the smile of a woman who has learned that endings, tended properly, are only seeds.

"Come on," she said. "Let's see what comes up."

*Here ends* Isandro and the Garden.
