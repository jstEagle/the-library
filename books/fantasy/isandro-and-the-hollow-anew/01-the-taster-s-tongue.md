## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The broth was almond and leek, finished with a thread of saffron, and it was going to kill Dame Ovelin if Isandro let it.

She knew this the way she knew rain before it fell — not by thought but by a settling in the body, a certainty older than language. The copper pot sat on the preparation table between her elbows, steam curling up into the rafters of Hollowmere's under-kitchen, where the stones sweated even in autumn and the air always tasted faintly of tallow and thyme. Isandro lifted the ladle, blew across its surface with the unhurried care of long practice, and sipped.

Nothing.

Not nothing of flavor — the flavor was there, delicate and green, the leeks sweet from the morning market, the saffron doing its quiet golden work. She tasted all of it. What she did not taste was the bitter ghost beneath, the one that had been folded into the broth sometime between the second straining and the third, when the kitchen had emptied for the bread delivery and only Cook's new scullery boy had remained. A ghost of bitterness like a coin held too long in the mouth. Like iron filings. Like something that did not belong in food at all.

Isandro set down the ladle and looked at the pot for a while.

Outside, the valley was doing what the valley always did: holding its breath around the palace, wrapping the terraced gardens and the mill-race and the grey slate roofs in that particular stillness that visitors called peaceful and servants called Tuesday. Hollowmere sat in its bowl of hills like water in a cupped hand, and nothing strange ever reached it. No wonders came over those ridgelines. No horrors either. That was the whole point of the place, though no one said so aloud; you simply grew up knowing the world beyond the passes was a story told to frighten children, full of magic and ruin, and here in the valley the stories ran out of breath at the border stones and died.

Here, soup was just soup.

Except when it wasn't.

"Isandro." Cook's voice came through the doorway, gravel over warm bread. "Dame Ovelin's tray. She's waiting."

"Coming," Isandro said, and did not move yet.

She stood instead, wiping her hands on her apron, and considered the broth the way she considered all her work: professionally, thoroughly, with a pride she kept folded small and out of sight because pride in a taster was like perfume on a corpse — it drew attention to exactly the wrong thing. Fourteen years she had done this work. Taken to the kitchens at nine years old, an orphan of no account with a clean tongue and no family to miss her, and taught by old Master Ferren the three rules of the trade: taste everything twice, show nothing on your face, and never, ever be interesting.

Master Ferren had died six years ago, quietly, in his sleep, which in their profession counted as a triumph. Since then the duty had been hers alone. Every dish meant for the regent's table passed first across Isandro's tongue — the morning porridge, the noon pottage, the evening's three courses and the sweetmeats after, the wines and the waters and the little honeyed spoonfuls taken for digestion. In fourteen years she had swallowed enough poison to kill a garrison. Arsenic in a wedding feast's wine. Hemlock ground fine into a winter stew. Something nameless and oily in a jar of preserved plums that had made her lips go numb for two days while everyone assumed she simply had a cold.

None of it had ever touched her.

That was the thing about Isandro Dunmore that no one discussed, not even her: poisons passed through her like rumors through a town where nobody speaks. They arrived, they lingered a moment against her tongue like guests uncertain of their welcome, and then they were gone, leaving no harm behind. The physicians who had examined her as a child — back when anyone still bothered — had shrugged and pronounced her constitution remarkable and left it at that. Remarkable. As if she were merely a woman with excellent teeth or unusually good hearing. As if it were a small thing, a charming quirk, and not the strangest fact of her existence.

She had stopped asking questions about it around her twelfth year. Questions were interesting, and interesting was forbidden.

But the broth was poisoned, and someone in this kitchen had poisoned it, and that was a problem with a shape and edges.

* * *

The under-kitchen at midmorning held perhaps a dozen souls, and every one of them found somewhere else to look when Isandro crossed the room.

It was not hostility. Hostility would have been easier. It was the careful, practiced blankness of people managing a dangerous animal — or an unlucky one. The scullery boys scrubbed pots that were already clean. The pastry women bent close over their marzipan roses as though the fate of the realm hung on each petal. Marta, who had once shared a bunkroom wall with Isandro for three years and had taught her a card game and half a dozen songs, now weighed flour with the fixed attention of a woman defusing something.

"Morning, Marta," Isandro said anyway, because fourteen years of saying good morning deserved better than surrender.

"Mm," said Marta, to her flour.

Isandro took the tray. White cloth, silver pot, the regent's own bowl and spoon, laid out with the precision ritual demanded. She carried it through the service passage and up the stair into the daylight of the great kitchen, where the fire roared and the noise was human and ordinary, and even there the conversation dipped as she passed — not silence, never quite silence, but a softening, a turning-away, the sound a room makes when it is pretending you are not in it.

She had learned not to hear it. Mostly.

Dame Ovelin received her in the small morning room off the west gallery, where the light came through leaded glass and lay in pale diamonds on the floor. The regent of Hollowmere was past seventy and straight as a lance, silver hair bound in a net of jet beads, hands folded on the arms of her chair like two sleeping hawks. She ruled the valley in King Aldric's name, though everyone understood the king's name was mostly a formality this far from the capital — Aldric's grip reached the passes only as far as taxes and edicts, read aloud by criers and resented in equal measure.

"Dame," Isandro said, setting down the tray. "Almond and leek broth, saffron, cream finish. Tasted at the third straining and again just now. It's clean."

A pause. Small, but Isandro had spent her life reading pauses the way other people read letters.

"It's clean," Dame Ovelin repeated. Her eyes — pale, quick, the eyes of a much younger woman wearing an old face — rested on Isandro a moment longer than courtesy required. "You've eaten?"

"A spoonful, dame. As always."

"And you feel well."

"I feel nothing, dame." The formula, worn smooth as a stair-stone from use. "No bitterness, no numbness, no heat behind the ears. The broth is fit for your table."

Something moved behind the regent's expression — there and gone, a fish under ice. "Good," she said. "Thank you, child. You may leave the tray."

Isandro bowed and went, and it was only halfway down the west gallery stairs that she realized what had snagged her: *you've eaten.* Dame Ovelin had never asked that before. In fourteen years the question had always been *is it safe*, never *did you take any into yourself*. As if the regent had suddenly become curious about what happened inside Isandro's body after the tasting. As if something had changed overnight in how the old woman looked at her.

Or perhaps Isandro was tired. She had slept badly — dreams of doors, oddly, doors standing open in blank white fields, and a humming she could not locate, low and patient, like a note held under the floor of the world. She woke with the hum still in her ears and it had not quite left all day.

Tired. That was all.

* * *

The poisoner, it turned out, was the scullery boy.

She found him at the washing troughs after the noon service, a thin creature of maybe fifteen with chapped red hands and the haunted posture of someone carrying a secret heavier than his shoulders were built for. He scrubbed a pot that gleamed already. He scrubbed it like penance.

Isandro set herself beside him at the trough, took up a cloth, and began drying the dishes he washed. For a while neither spoke. Water ran. Somewhere above them, the great kitchen shouted about venison.

"You put something in the regent's broth," she said quietly.

The boy went white to the lips. The pot clattered against the trough's stone lip. "I don't— I never—"

"The almond and leek. Bitter underneath, like iron. I tasted it." She kept her voice level, almost gentle; terror made people stupid, and she needed him useful. "I want to know who gave it to you and why. And then I want you to listen very carefully, because I'm about to save your life."

He stared at her. Water dripped. His eyes went from her face to the tray she'd carried and back, and Isandro watched him do the arithmetic — watched him realize that the taster had tasted, and the taster was standing here unharmed, and therefore—

"There's a man," he whispered. "Came to the postern two nights back. Grey hood. Said he was from the capital. He gave me a vial and silver and said the regent was a traitor to the crown and it was my duty. He said—" The boy's voice cracked. "He said if I didn't, he'd know, and he'd come back for my mother. She works the laundry, dame. Worked. She's got the shaking sickness, she can't—"

"All right." Isandro set down the dish. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside, something was moving fast, sorting, weighing. A grey hood. From the capital. Poison aimed at Dame Ovelin in King Aldric's name — which meant either the king's men believed the regent disloyal, or someone wore the king's authority like a stolen coat. Either way, the boy was a loose thread someone might pull, and threads got cut. "Listen to me. You'll say nothing to anyone. Not confession, not drunkenness, not your mother. If anyone asks, the broth was perfect and you saw nothing. Can you do that?"

He nodded, wide-eyed.

"Good. And tomorrow you'll find me, and we'll decide what to do about the man in the grey hood. Because he'll come back, and next time I'd like to be standing behind the door when he does."

She left him at the trough and climbed the back stair to the loft where she slept, alone, as tasters always slept alone — apart, even in a building full of people. The little room was cold and smelled of cedar and lamp oil. Below her window the valley lay in the last gold of afternoon, the mill-race flashing, the terraces striped with shadow, the border hills standing dark and patient on every horizon like walls around a garden.

Safe. Contained. Nothing in, nothing out. That was Hollowmere, and Isandro had loved it her whole life without ever examining the love, the way you love a room you've never left.

She sat on her cot and turned the day over in her hands.

A poisoning attempt. A grey hood from the capital. The regent asking strange questions. And underneath it all, that hum from her dream, still faintly present at the edge of hearing, like something enormous sleeping under the floor of the world and dreaming in its turn.

And — she admitted it now, in the privacy of her own skull, where admissions cost nothing — underneath everything else, older than all of it, the other thing. The crack. The hairline flaw in the contentment she wore like a well-fitted coat.

Because here was the truth she did not say, even to herself, more than once a season: she was good at her work, and her work was all she had, and her work consisted of being trusted absolutely by people who would not eat beside her. Fourteen years of keeping the regent alive, and no one in this palace would share a bench with her at supper. She knew poisons better than the physicians and could not remember the last time someone had touched her hand. She was the most necessary person in Hollowmere and the loneliest, and some nights the loneliness pressed on her chest like a hand, and she would press back and say: *you have work, you have purpose, you have a roof and a craft and a valley that keeps all the world's monsters outside its hills.*

And most nights that was enough. Most nights.

Tonight she stood at her window a long time, watching the light die over the border hills, and thought about the boy at the trough, and the grey hood, and the way Dame Ovelin's pale eyes had lingered. Thought about how a single question — *you've eaten?* — could make fourteen years feel suddenly provisional, like furniture in a house that might be sold.

Somewhere beyond those darkening ridges was a world she had never seen. A world with magic in it, supposedly, and ruin, and free cities glowing on the coasts, and roads that went places other than in circles. She had never wanted it, precisely. Wanting it would have been a kind of ingratitude, and besides, the valley was designed — she'd been told this much, in the nursery rhymes children sang without understanding — the valley was designed so that wanting such things slid off you like rain off slate.

But tonight, watching the hills, Isandro felt the design strain. Felt, for one clear ringing moment, like a bell struck by accident: the hum under the world answering something in her blood, faint and patient and vast.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold glass until the feeling passed.

Below, lamps were being lit along the terrace walks. The kitchen would be starting the evening service, and the regent's table waited on her tongue, and there was work, thank all the quiet gods of Hollowmere, there was always work.

Isandro Dunmore went down to do it, telling herself the day's unease was only a bad night's sleep, and believing it almost completely, and not noticing — because why would she, why would anyone — that the candle on her windowsill had leaned its small flame toward her as she rose, faithful as a dog, and bent back upright only when the door closed behind her.
