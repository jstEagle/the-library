## 2. Proclamation at the Well

The morning the criers came to Hollowmere, Isandro Dunmore was elbow-deep in a barrel of pickled ramsons, fishing out cloves for the regent's breakfast broth.

She heard them before anyone else did — not because her ears were better than the scullery girls', but because she had spent eleven years learning to hear the small wrongnesses of the palace kitchens: the change in a knife's rhythm when a cook was angry, the particular silence a lie made when it passed between two servants over a chopping block. The criers' bells were a new sound, and new sounds in Hollowmere were almost always bad ones.

"Out," said Marta Voss, the head cook, before Isandro could even wipe her hands. "You too, all of you. Well court. Now."

The kitchen emptied like a bowl tipped over. Isandro fell into step at the back, as she always did, close enough to the wall that no one would have to make room for her. It was a skill she'd perfected without ever deciding to: the art of being present but unclaimed. The taster walked with us today. The taster stood by the well. The taster drank what we could not.

The well court was already filling when they came out into the grey-gold light of early autumn. Servants from the laundry, grooms still smelling of straw, the under-steward with his chain of office clutched in one fist as though it might be taken from him. Above them, on the steps of the record house, three men in the livery of King Aldric — grey and gold, the colors nobody in Hollowmere had worn until five years ago — unrolled a scroll the length of an arm.

One of them carried a bell. He rang it twice, and the whole court went quiet the way water goes quiet before it freezes.

"Hear the word of Aldric, rightful king of Vessia," the crier called, in a voice trained to carry across market squares and into dreams. "By decree of the Edict of Cleansing, let it be known throughout the valley provinces and all lands sworn to the crown—"

Isandro stopped listening to the words for a moment because she was watching Marta Voss instead.

She had never seen the head cook afraid. Not during the fever winter, when half the palace staff coughed blood into their rags and kept working anyway. Not the year the river flooded the cellars and drowned a season's worth of salt pork. Marta Voss was a broad, red-handed woman built like a bread oven, and fear did not fit her face; it sat there now like something borrowed, badly.

"—all persons bearing the old gift, or any sign or symptom thereof, must be surrendered to the crown's hunters within thirty days of this proclamation," the crier read on. "Surrendered persons will be examined and, if found clean, released. Persons concealing bearers will share their sentence. Persons fleeing will be hunted."

He paused, and unrolled more scroll.

"The signs are these." His voice changed here, flattening, becoming clinical, and somehow that was worse. "First: immunity to poison, where no tolerance has been earned. Second: flame that bends toward the bearer, or away, against the wind's judgment. Third: healing that comes too fast, or wounds that close without cause. Fourth: dreams that arrive in another's sleep. Fifth: the hearing of locked things — doors, chests, sealed letters — humming."

Isandro felt the list go through her like a fishhook finding flesh.

*Immunity to poison.* She stood very still among the crowd, hands folded, face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman listening to news about other people. Around her, the court murmured — low, frightened sounds, the noise of people doing arithmetic with their neighbors' faces. Who healed too fast? Whose grandmother had been strange? Whose child talked to locks?

But Isandro wasn't doing arithmetic about her neighbors.

Her immunity had always been explained, in her own mind, by the only story available: she had been apprenticed to the taster's trade young, dosed in careful increments, her body hardened against venom the way a blacksmith's palms are hardened against iron. That was the official account. That was the account Dame Ovelin herself had signed off on, years ago, with a nod and a purse of coin. *Tolerance earned.*

Except she had never earned it. She knew that now, standing in the well court with the crier's voice falling over her like ash. She had tasted poisons in her first month of service that should have killed a grown ox, and she had felt nothing — not sickness, not numbness, not even the ghost of a tingle on her tongue. The old taster who trained her, a sour man named Corvic, had called her a marvel. Had called her a natural. Had drunk himself to death within the year, muttering about how some mouths were born closed and some were born shut.

*Where no tolerance has been earned.*

The first sign. Hers.

And the fifth — *the hearing of locked things* — she pushed away from that one quickly, the way you push away from a hot stove, because there had been moments. Small ones. Standing outside the regent's private pantry and feeling, distinctly, a hum behind the door, like a struck tuning fork wrapped in wool. Assuming it was the wine racks settling. Assuming it was her imagination, which was what a sensible woman assumed, in a valley where magic could not follow.

Magic could not follow into Hollowmere. Everyone knew it. It was the reason the palace had been built here, generations back — a place of stone and running water and deep cold earth where the gift simply died, the way a candle dies in a vacuum. The old kingdom of Vessia had burned itself out everywhere else, but Hollowmere was clean. Hollowmere was safe. That was the whole point of the valley, the whole comfort of it. You could live your whole life here and never once wonder whether you were more than you appeared.

So why, then, was the second sign — *flame that bends toward the bearer* — sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal?

Because of last week. Because of the night she'd stayed late trimming wicks in the kitchen and noticed, idly, that every candle flame in the room leaned toward her hand as she passed, a dozen small orange tongues straining after her like hungry dogs. She had told herself it was the draft from the door. There had been no draft from the door.

She had not thought about it since. She had been very skilled, all week, at not thinking about it.

"—let every household head certify within ten days that no bearer resides under their roof," the crier was saying. "Let every guild, every inn, every kitchen—" he paused, and Isandro could have sworn his eyes moved across the crowd and found hers, held for half a heartbeat, moved on — "every kitchen and pantry and tasting-chamber submit its people for examination. The hunters come within the month. Make ready."

He rolled the scroll. The bell rang once more, a flat, final note, and the three grey-and-gold men descended the record house steps and walked out of the court without looking back, the way men walk when they know exactly how much fear they've left behind them.

For a long moment nobody in the well court moved. Then the murmur broke loose all at once, rising like steam, and the crowd began to dissolve into knots — heads together, voices dropped, eyes flicking sideways. A laundress grabbed her daughter's wrist hard enough to leave marks. Two grooms who had been friends for years suddenly found reasons to stand apart.

Isandro walked back to the kitchen beside Marta Voss, and neither of them said anything, and the silence between them had a shape she didn't like at all.

* * *

The kitchen, when they returned, was wrong.

It took her a few minutes to name the wrongness, because everything looked ordinary — the fires banked, the dough resting under cloth, the day's work laid out in its usual patient rows. But the ordinary things were being done too carefully. The scullery girl scrubbed a pot that was already clean. The under-cook trimmed the same leek four times. And Marta Voss stood at the great hearth with her back to the room, feeding something into the flames.

Isandro went back to her station and began measuring barley for the noon pottage, and watched, sidelong, the way she had learned to watch everything in this palace.

Marta was burning papers. Small ones, torn first — Isandro could see the torn edges going up — fed to the fire a piece at a time, unhurried, methodical, the way you burn something when you want the burning to look like nothing. Ash-work. Every old kitchen had its ash-work: remedies, charms, little recipes against the evil eye, written in a dead shorthand and passed hand to hand for a hundred years. Half-harmless. Half-superstition. The kind of thing every household in the valley kept in a drawer and never spoke of, because everyone knew magic couldn't follow into Hollowmere, so what harm could a paper charm do?

Everyone had known that. Right up until this morning, when the crown had sent men with a bell and a list, and the list hadn't cared what followed into Hollowmere and what didn't. The list only cared about signs.

Marta glanced over her shoulder. Their eyes met. For one naked moment the head cook's face was open, and Isandro saw in it not suspicion but something older and sadder — the look of a woman counting her people and wondering which of them she'd have to give up.

Then the shutters came down, and Marta turned back to her fire, and said, loudly, in her kitchen voice: "Barley wants another hour. Taster — the regent's broth won't taste itself."

"No, mistress," Isandro said.

She set the barley aside and began the broth. It was a simple one — capon, white wine, sweet roots, a thread of saffron — and she made it the way she always made it, hands moving through the motions while her mind ran elsewhere, down darker corridors.

Thirty days. The hunters come within the month. *Surrendered persons will be examined and, if found clean, released.*

Found clean. By whose measure? By what instrument? She imagined standing before some grey-hooded examiner, tongue out like a naughty child, while he pronounced upon her mouth — the mouth that had never once flinched at henbane or arsenic or the black adder's brew Corvic used to keep locked in an iron box. Would they call that clean? Or would they call it the first sign?

*Immunity to poison, where no tolerance has been earned.*

There was no version of the examination that ended well for her. If they believed the official story — trained tolerance, earned the honest way — then she was clean, and fine, and free. But the official story lived in ledgers, and ledgers could be read, and somewhere in the record house was a page with her name on it and a date: the day she'd arrived at the palace, aged nine, already immune, tested before a single dose had ever touched her lips. Corvic had recorded it. *The girl feels nothing. Remarkable. Uncorrupted by training.*

Uncorrupted by training. She had read those words a hundred times without hearing them. Now they rang in her skull like the crier's bell.

A girl who felt nothing, before any training. Where no tolerance had been earned.

She stirred the broth. The saffron bled gold into the white wine, slow threads unraveling, and she thought: *this is what it looks like when something hidden starts to show.*

* * *

At midday she carried the regent's tray up through the service passages, as she had eleven hundred times before, and it was on the stairs that she heard the humming.

She stopped. The passage was empty — just her, the tray, and the narrow window letting in a bar of pale light. Below her, at the bottom of the stair, was the door to the regent's private cellar. A heavy oak door, iron-banded, triple-locked, holding the regent's best wines and, rumor said, certain bottles no one was permitted to name.

It was humming.

Not loudly. Not the way machinery hums. More like the memory of a bell — a vibration she felt in her back teeth more than heard in her ears, steady and patient, as though the door had been singing to itself for years and had only just now decided she might be someone worth singing to.

Isandro stood on the stairs with the tray balanced on her palms and made herself breathe.

*Locked things hum,* the crier had said. *The hearing of locked things.*

No. No, that was nonsense, that was for other people, that was for the burned kingdoms outside the valley where the gift still grew like weeds. Here in Hollowmere the air was clean of it. Here the stones themselves drank magic out of anything that carried it. That was doctrine. That was the bedrock the whole valley stood on. She had built her entire life on that bedrock — her usefulness, her safety, her strange invulnerable tongue, all of it resting on the certainty that whatever she was, the valley had smothered it, or it had never existed at all.

And yet the door hummed. And yet the flames had leaned. And yet the first sign on the king's list fit her like a glove fitted by a careful tailor.

She realized she was gripping the tray so hard her knuckles had gone white, and made herself relax, finger by finger. Spilled broth on the regent's stairs would raise questions. Questions were the one thing she could no longer afford.

Dame Ovelin received her in the small morning room, as always. The regent was a spare, silvering woman with a scholar's stoop and a soldier's eyes, and she greeted Isandro today exactly as she always did — a nod, a gesture to the side table, the covered dishes waiting in their row.

"Taste," Dame Ovelin said.

Isandro tasted. Capon broth, spoonful by spoonful, held on the tongue for the full count of ten. Bread from the center of the loaf. The honeyed pears. The dark northern wine, a swallow swirled and held. Nothing. Nothing, nothing, nothing — her faithful, terrible nothing, the emptiness that had kept her alive and kept her alone for eleven years.

Today, for the first time, she wished she could feel the poison. Just once. Just enough to prove she was ordinary.

"Your hand is steady," Dame Ovelin observed. She was watching Isandro over the rim of her own cup, and her eyes were doing something they rarely did: lingering.

"Mistress?"

"You've served me eleven years, Dunmore. In all that time your hand has never shaken, your tongue has never lied, and your face has never once shown me anything you didn't choose to show." The regent set down her cup. "This morning, in my court, I watched you listen to a royal proclamation, and your face showed me nothing at all. Which is correct. Which is what I pay you for." She paused. "But I find myself wondering what it cost you."

Isandro heard herself say, calmly, in a voice she barely recognized as her own: "Nothing, mistress. The edict concerns the gifted. I am a taster. My gifts are a strong stomach and a good memory."

It was a lie. Not a large one — not a treasonous one — but a lie nonetheless, delivered smoothly, without a flicker, to the most dangerous woman in the valley. And the worst part, the part that made her stomach turn afterward for reasons that had nothing to do with poison, was how easy it had been. Eleven years of truthfulness, and the machinery of deceit had started on the first try, oiled and ready, as if it had been waiting all along.

Dame Ovelin studied her a moment longer. Then she smiled — a thin, unreadable thing — and dismissed her with a wave.

"Take the rest of the day," she said. "The kitchens will manage. Rest, Dunmore. I suspect we shall all need our strength."

*Rest,* Isandro thought, walking back down through the service passages with empty hands and a full head. *She knows something. Or she suspects. Or she is simply a careful woman in a careless season, and I am reading shadows onto stone.*

But the regent had signed off on her immunity years ago. The regent had read Corvic's ledger. The regent knew — had always known — that no tolerance had been earned.

And the regent had given her the day off, on the first day of the thirty.

* * *

Evening came down over Hollowmere like a lid. Isandro took her supper alone in the taster's narrow room off the kitchen corridor — a privilege, everyone said, a mark of trust, though it had always felt more like quarantine — and ate bread and hard cheese and listened to the palace settle around her.

Through the wall, muffled, she could hear the kitchen staff at their own supper. Their voices were lower than usual. Once she caught her name — just the shape of it, *Dunmore*, passed between two mouths like something being weighed — and then a hush, and then talk of safer things.

They're frightened of me already, she thought. Or for me. With servants it was often the same thing.

She looked at her hands in the lamplight. Ordinary hands. A cook's hands, a servant's hands, nicked and scarred and stained with saffron. She turned them over and tried to remember the exact angle of the candle flames leaning toward her, and could not decide whether she had imagined it, and understood, with a cold clarity that felt like falling, that the not-deciding was itself an answer.

Thirty days.

Somewhere beyond the valley's edge, in the wide world she had never seen, men in grey hoods were packing instruments and mounting horses. Somewhere a list was being copied, and copied again, and carried from town to town by men with bells. And somewhere on that list, in some clerk's careful hand, the words were already written that described her — the first sign, maybe the second, maybe the fifth — describing her as precisely as a key describes a lock.

She blew out the lamp and lay in the dark and did not sleep.

And beneath her, below the floor, below the foundations, below the cold stone bones of the valley itself, she began — very faintly, very far away, or perhaps only in the failing cork of her own sealed self — to hear something humming.
