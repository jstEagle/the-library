## 3. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The candle was a crime before I ever lit it.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the one thing I have left of the woman I was that morning. Stealing it had been easy — too easy, the way all my small transgressions were easy. A stub of tallow from the great hall's stores, tucked into my apron pocket while Marta counted spoons and no one looked at the taster, because no one ever looks at the taster. We are furniture that swallows. We are the pause between the kitchen and the king's throat.

But the flint I took on purpose. And the matches — three of them, wrapped in waxed cloth so they wouldn't spoil in the damp — those I took with my heart hammering like a fist on a door I didn't dare open.

I waited until the palace slept.

* * *

Hollowmere at night is a held breath. The valley cups the town the way two hands cup water, and everything inside it — the slate roofs, the millpond, the crooked bell tower of the Chantry of Stillness — sits quiet under a sky that never quite fills with stars. I used to think that was just how skies were. I was twenty-two years old before Sef Ardenne's smuggled maps taught me that other places burn with them, whole rivers of light, and that Hollowmere's dimness had a name: suppression. The valley drank the sky the way it drank everything else that refused to sit still.

Magic cannot follow into Hollowmere. Every child learns it as a lullaby before they learn it as law. *Nothing follows you down, little one; nothing strange, nothing bright.* The old kingdom of Vessia had been full of strange and bright things once, or so the forbidden stories went, and when King Aldric unmade it — struck its name from every chart, every ledger, every gravestone — the gifted were gathered up and settled in valleys like this one, where their gifts would starve quietly and die. That was the mercy, they said. That was the kindness. Better a slow hollowing than a pyre.

I had lived my whole life inside that mercy without knowing I was its object.

The servants' quarters sat above the wash-yard, a long low room of straw pallets divided by hanging sheets, and I had the corner nearest the wall because seniority meant something even among people who wouldn't eat beside me. Tasters ate apart. Tasters always might be poisoned; it made us poor company at table, a joke nobody said aloud. I'd built my whole self inside that arm's length. I'd told myself it was a kind of freedom.

That night I lay listening to the breathing of eleven sleepers until I was certain none of it was feigned sleep, and then I rose, and took my stolen candle, and climbed.

There is a place behind the Chantry where the hillside steps up in terraces — old work, older than the town, though everyone agreed not to wonder about it. Kitchen gardens in summer, bare dirt in the cold months, and at the top, half-swallowed by ivy, a low stone wall that marked the edge of the regent's grounds. Beyond it the terraces fell away toward the mill-race, black and loud even in winter, and past the race the valley wall rose sheer, grey stone going up and up into the dark until you couldn't tell rock from night.

No one went there after dark. The Chantry bells rang the hour of stillness at midnight and again at four, and between those hours the valley itself seemed to enforce a curfew deeper than any Dame Ovelin could decree. Dogs wouldn't bark. Owls didn't hunt. Even the wind moved carefully.

I crouched against the old wall with my back to the drop and my hands shaking, and I lit the candle.

One match. The flame caught on the second scrape, and there in the cup of my palms it stood up straight and small and ordinary, a thumb of yellow light, and for one long moment nothing happened at all, and I felt the first edge of relief —

— and then the flame leaned.

Not flickered. *Leaned.* It bent toward my left hand, toward the bare fingers curled around the tallow, the way a stem bends toward a window. Slowly. Deliberately. As if it had remembered something.

I stopped breathing.

I turned my hand over slowly, palm up, and the flame followed — tipped further, stretching, a thread of fire straining across the dark gap between wick and skin like a dog testing the length of its chain. And beneath my breastbone, in a place I had never had a name for because I had never needed one, something *answered*. Not heat exactly. Not cold. A kind of attention, waking, turning over, saying without words: *yes. here. me.*

I snapped my hand shut and the flame righted itself, innocent as anything, and I sat there in the dark with my heart going so hard I could hear it in my ears, and I did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do since the well that morning.

The edict. Aldric's Edict of Cleansing, read out by criers at the market well while the whole kitchen stood silent as salted meat. *All bearers of the gift must be surrendered to the crown's hunters, or hunted.* And then the crier had read the symptoms, because Aldric wanted neighbors informing on neighbors and neighbors need instructions: flames that lean. Water that remembers being poured. Locks that turn for some hands and not others. Food that sours in the presence of the marked, or — and here the crier's voice had gone flat and careful — food that does *not* sour, does not poison, does not harm, when it should.

Food that does not harm when it should.

Twenty-two years of broth. Twenty-two years of arsenic and bitterleaf and the grey powder they called widow's flour, swallowed in front of the court while ladies fanned themselves and the regent watched me with her soft, terrible patience. Twenty-two years of feeling *nothing*, and being proud of it. Proud. I had walked through the kitchens with my chin up thinking myself iron-made, thinking my body a fortress, thinking immunity was the absence of magic in a valley built to starve it.

It wasn't absence. Sitting against that wall with a bent flame dying between my fingers, I understood it the way you understand a fall — all at once, from the inside. My body wasn't a fortress. It was a vessel. Sealed. Stuffed full to the brim with twenty-two years of everything the poisons carried and everything they woke, corked tight by the valley's suppression, holding, holding, holding —

And the cork, whatever it was made of, had begun to fail.

Because the flame had never leaned before. I would have noticed. You notice when fire moves wrong. Something had changed, and it had changed the same week the hunters' proclamations went up on every door in Hollowmere, and whether that was coincidence or cause I couldn't say, and not knowing was its own kind of poison.

* * *

I should have run then. I know that now. I want to be gentle with the girl I was, but gentleness isn't the same as excuse: I should have taken my savings from the loose brick, should have gone out through the mill-race that very night, wet and terrified and alive. Instead I did what servants do, what the powerless do, what I had trained myself twenty-two years to do.

I buried the evidence.

The candle went back in my apron, the match-end ground to ash under my heel and scattered. I sat until my legs stopped trembling, watching the mill-race churn below — running water, I thought, without knowing why the thought comforted me, why the phrase felt less like a description than an instruction — and then I went down the terraces with my face composed into the flat servant-blankness that had kept me fed my whole life, and I lay on my pallet among the breathing sleepers, and I stared at the underside of the roof beams until the bells rang four.

I did not sleep. But somewhere in the grey before dawn I must have drifted, because I dreamed of the tasting hall — the long table, the silver covers, Dame Ovelin at the head with her folded hands — and in the dream every dish was uncovered and every dish was empty, and Ovelin smiled at me across all that polished nothing and said, in a voice like a key turning, *What are you, Isandro Dunmore?*

I woke with the word *taster* already forming on my tongue, the safe answer, the only answer I had ever had.

By morning it tasted like a lie.

* * *

Morning came anyway. Mornings in the palace kitchens came whether you wanted them or not — that was the one mercy of service, that grief and terror both got scheduled around the bread ovens. I washed, I dressed, I braided my hair back the way I always did, and I looked at myself in the scrap of polished tin nailed by the door, and the face looking back was my own face, which suddenly seemed like the strangest thing about me.

*You're the same*, I told it. *Nothing happened. Fire leans near drafts. There was a draft.*

The face didn't believe me either.

Down in the kitchens, the world was performing its usual morning: Marta screaming at the scullery boys about the state of the copper pots, the bread ovens roaring, two girls arguing over who'd let the cream scald. Ordinary noise. Ordinary steam. I tied on my apron and took up my knife and started on the pile of onions as if I were still the person who had come down these stairs ten thousand times before, and for perhaps a quarter of an hour I almost managed it.

Then Tam came through the yard door with his arms full of kindling, and everything rearranged itself around him the way it always did — not because he demanded it, but because he was twelve and loud and lit from inside in a way the rest of us had paid too much to keep. He dumped the wood by the hearth, saw me, and grinned.

"Taster Isandro." He said my title like a joke we shared, because to Tam everyone's job was a joke worth sharing. "Marta says if you poison the regent's breakfast she'll have your teeth for a necklace."

"Marta can get in line behind the poison," I said, and he laughed, and the sound of it went through me sideways, because last night I had learned what the edict's hunters listened *for*, and Tam — sweet, impossible Tam, who whistled at the stable cats and swore they came to him across the whole yard, who'd once sworn the padlock on the tack room opened for him "when it liked me" —

Tam, who talked to locks, and locks, I realized with a cold sliding feeling, might have been talking back.

"Isandro?" He'd caught my stare. His grin faltered into something younger. "You've got a look."

"Eat something," I said, which was what I always said, and pushed the end-crust of yesterday's loaf at him, and he took it, and bit it, and chewed, and lived, and I turned back to my onions and gave the knife something to do besides shake.

I could warn him. The thought arrived whole and terrible. I could take him aside, could tell him to stop whistling to the cats, stop charming the padlocks, stop being exactly what he was, because the grey hoods were coming — everyone knew they were coming, the criers had only read the beginning of it — and the edict did not care that he was twelve. The edict especially did not care that he was twelve. Children were named first in the lists. Children's gifts ran brightest before the valley finished starving them.

But warning him meant telling him *why*, and telling him why meant saying aloud the thing I had spent one entire night burying, and if I said it aloud then it was real, and if it was real then I was real — a bearer, a marked one, a name on a hunter's list — and everything I was, everything I had carefully built out of routine and usefulness and the small pride of a cast-iron tongue, would have to be taken apart in the space of a sentence.

So I chopped onions. And Tam ate his crust and went off to muck stalls, whistling, and the cats came running from three directions, and I watched them come and felt the floor tilt gently under my certainty.

* * *

Dame Ovelin sent for me at midmorning.

I'll write about that summons in its place — about the private tasting, the honeyed wine, the way her eyes rested on me like a hand on a scale. But standing in the yard waiting to be fetched, wiping my hands on my apron over and over, what I remember most is the light. Hollowmere light, that pale filtered grey-gold that never quite sharpened into true sun, falling on the herb beds and the well cover and the far grey shoulder of the valley wall, and I thought: *this is the ceiling of my life. This much sky, this much world, measured out to me in a bowl I cannot see past.*

Magic cannot follow into Hollowmere. That was the founding promise, the whole architecture of the place. Nothing strange, nothing bright.

And yet last night a candle flame had bent toward my finger like a flower finding the sun, and something under my ribs had answered it, patient and enormous, the way the Door of Anew — a story I didn't yet know I'd spend a year chasing — waits under stone.

The valley had held me for twenty-two years. I had thought I was holding still.

They were not the same thing, I was beginning to understand. One of them was a choice.

The page boy came for me then, and I went, and behind me in the kitchen Marta was already shouting about the noon fish, and Tam was whistling, and the cats were coming home, and every ordinary sound of it rang like glass — beautiful, and thin, and about to break.
