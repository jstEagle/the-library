## 4. The Regent's Kindness

The summons came at the hour when the kitchen fires were banked and the scullery boys had gone to their pallets, and it came in a form Isandro had never seen before: not shouted down the stairwell by an under-cook, but delivered on paper, folded twice, sealed with the regent's own wax.

Marta, the flour-woman, brought it to her where she sat mending the strap of her tasting spoon — a small ritual, that spoon, worn smooth as river stone from ten years of her mouth. She read the seal's crest twice because the first time made no sense.

*Isandro Dunmore is requested to attend Dame Ovelin in the morning room, after matins. Private service.*

"Private service," Marta repeated over her shoulder, reading upside down with the ease of a woman who had spent thirty years deciphering other people's lists. "In eleven years I've never heard of a private service."

"There's a first time for everything."

"Not in this kitchen there isn't." Marta took the letter, turned it toward the last candle as though the paper might confess something the ink withheld. "Dame Ovelin doesn't eat private meals. She eats in the great hall where everyone can see her plate, because that's how a regent survives — visible, predictable, poisoned by nobody. Everyone knows that."

"Then perhaps it isn't about eating."

Marta looked at her then, really looked, and something in the older woman's face closed like a shutter going up over a window. It was a look Isandro knew well — the look servants gave her when they remembered what she was. Not a servant herself, exactly, and not staff, and not gentry. The taster. The woman whose tongue was a tool owned by the household, kept sharp and kept apart. Marta had been kind to her once, years ago, when Isandro was new and sick with homesickness for a village she barely remembered. But kindness to the taster was like kindness to the executioner: it never quite survived the memory of what the person was *for*.

"You should sleep," Marta said, and carried the letter off toward the hearth, and Isandro did not see whether it went into the fire or into an apron pocket, and found she cared more than she wanted to.

She did not sleep. She lay in her narrow room above the buttery — a room better than most servants' rooms, because a taster could not be allowed to share walls where someone might slip something into her supper and spoil the instrument — and listened to the valley wind come down off the mountains and die against Hollowmere's eaves. Magic cannot follow here. She had known that since childhood the way you know the floor will hold you: the old kingdom of Vessia had been hollowed out, its wonders scooped away like the flesh of a fruit, and the valleys that remained were places where nothing uncanny could live or enter or even pass through. It was why the palace had stood so long. It was why Aldric, three valleys east, needed hunters and edicts instead of sorcery to do his work.

It was why, when she thought about the candle flame bending toward her finger two nights ago — bending, leaning, *reaching*, like a dog hearing its name — her mind kept circling back to the same impossible arithmetic. Magic cannot follow into Hollowmere. Therefore whatever had moved that flame was not magic. Therefore she was safe. Therefore she could sleep.

She did not sleep.

* * *

The morning room sat on the palace's east face, where the sun came in low and gold across a floor so polished it looked wet. Dame Ovelin received her standing, which was itself strange; regents received people seated, from behind tables, with distance as armor. This woman stood by the window with her hands folded at her waist like a girl at a dancing lesson, silver-haired, straight-backed, dressed in grey wool without a single jewel. She had ruled Hollowmere for nine years in King Aldric's name, and she had done it, everyone agreed, by being exactly what she appeared: plain, pious, patient, kind.

Kindness, Isandro thought, watching her smile, was such an efficient trap. No one guarded against it.

"Taster Dunmore." Ovelin's voice was warm bread. "Thank you for coming. I hope the hour didn't trouble you."

"The hour serves the household, my lady."

"Yes, they all say that." The regent laughed softly, as if Isandro had been witty rather than obedient. "Come to the table. I've had them bring things."

Things. On the long table by the window sat a breakfast that would have fed the garrison: dark bread and pale butter, honey still in its comb, smoked trout, a custard trembling in its dish, a pot of tea breathing steam, and — Isandro's eyes snagged on it — a tureen of broth, covered, with a single ladle beside it like a witness waiting to testify.

"I'm told your palate is the finest in three valleys," Ovelin said. "That you can name every herb in a soup blindfolded. That you once detected hemlock in a wine so well-made the vintner himself couldn't find it."

"That was luck, my lady."

"It was not luck. I asked the vintner. He wept." She gestured to a chair — placed directly opposite hers, close enough that their knees might touch under the table if either leaned forward. "Sit. Eat with me. I find I'm tired of eating alone behind walls of protocol, and you are the one person in this palace whose tongue I trust absolutely."

There it was. Isandro sat, because refusing would have been louder than any answer, and laid her hands in her lap, and smiled the small modest smile that had kept her alive and unremarkable for eleven years.

Ovelin served her. That was the second strangeness — the regent of Hollowmere lifting the tureen herself, ladling broth into Isandro's bowl with the care of a mother feeding a child, steam rising between them. "Taste first," Ovelin said. "Always taste first. I know your habits. Indulge them."

So Isandro tasted. Broth of good stock, marrow bones, leek, a thread of thyme, salt drawn from the eastern pans — and underneath, faint as a whisper through a wall, something bitter and green. Her tongue catalogued it before her mind caught up: *wolfsbane, prepared slow, the bitterness coaxed down until only the tail of it remained.* Enough to sicken. Not enough to kill. Not nearly enough to kill a healthy woman.

Enough, she realized with a coldness spreading through her chest, to kill a sick one. Or an old one. Or to make a death look like fever.

Or — and this thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome — enough to test whether a certain taster would flinch.

She let none of it reach her face. Eleven years of practice held. She swallowed, set down the spoon, and said, "Beautiful stock, my lady. Marrow bones, leek, thyme. Salt from the eastern pans, I think. There's a bitterness at the end — late onions, perhaps, or a scorched herb. Nothing harmful."

Silence. The fire ticked in the grate. Outside the window, a gardener's rake whispered across gravel, the whole ordinary world going on while the two women at the table conducted whatever this was.

"A bitterness at the end," Ovelin repeated. She tasted the broth herself, delicately, eyes never leaving Isandro's face. "I don't taste one."

"Most people wouldn't. It's very faint."

"Mm." The regent set down her own spoon and folded her hands, and for a moment the warmth in her face receded like a tide, showing the rocks beneath — not cruelty, nothing so legible as cruelty, just *attention*, vast and patient and total. "You are wondering why you're here."

"I hadn't presumed to wonder, my lady."

"Liar," Ovelin said pleasantly. "Everyone wonders. Wondering is free. Now — I'll tell you a story, Taster Dunmore, and you'll tell me if you've heard it before, since stories are a kind of food too and yours is the finest palate in three valleys."

She rose and walked to the window, silhouetted against the gold morning, and spoke to the garden rather than to Isandro.

"Two nights ago, a chambermaid named Sela was carrying linens past the chapel vestry. She saw light under the door where no light should have been — the vestry has been shut since the old king's time. She looked through the keyhole. She saw a young woman kneeling among the dust, holding a candle, and the flame of that candle was bent — bent sideways, Taster, toward the woman's hand, like wheat leaning into wind. And when the woman rose and blew the candle out, the smoke didn't rise. It *curled*. It wrote something in the air, a shape, a letter, and then it was gone."

The rake outside had stopped. Isandro could hear her own pulse in her ears, slow and enormous, a drum in a procession.

"Sela reported it, of course. To me, before anyone else, because I have cultivated a household where loyalty runs to me first." Ovelin turned from the window. Morning light caught the fine lines around her eyes, and Isandro understood suddenly that the kindness had never been a mask over something else; it was simply the shape this woman's attention took, the way a snare takes the shape of grass. "The description of the young woman was incomplete. Small. Dark-haired. Servant's dress. Seen at night, in poor light, through a keyhole. It could be half the maids in this palace."

"But you think it's me."

"Do I?" Ovelin tilted her head. "Why would you say that?"

Because the summons came the same night. Because the broth is laced and you watched me drink it. Because you are measuring me right now with those warm grey eyes the way I measure poison, drop by drop. Because everything about this room is a question and you are waiting for me to answer wrong.

What she said was: "You summoned me alone, my lady. Alone is how one speaks to a suspect."

"Alone is how one speaks to a friend," Ovelin corrected gently. "Suspects get guards. You'll notice you have none. Walk out that door whenever you like." She returned to the table, resumed her seat, poured tea for them both with her own hands. "But before you go, indulge an old woman. Tell me about yourself. Where were you born?"

"Hollowbrook, my lady. A village on the south rim."

"Parents?"

"My father was a cooper. My mother died bearing me."

"And how does a cooper's daughter come to be the finest palate in three valleys?"

"I was tested at twelve, my lady. All children are. The guild taster found my tongue unusually clean."

"Unusually clean." Ovelin smiled and stirred her tea, though Isandro had watched her add neither sugar nor milk. "Yes. I've read your file. Tested at twelve. Remarkable result. Apprenticed at thirteen. In service at fourteen. Eleven years without a single error, without a single illness, without — and this is the detail I find most charming, Taster — without a single day's sickness of any kind. No fevers. No flux. Not even a head cold. Eleven years." She looked up. "Have you ever been ill, Isandro Dunmore? Truly ill? Do you remember what it feels like?"

The room had gone very quiet. Or perhaps the room had always been this quiet and Isandro was only now hearing it — the tick of the fire, the absence of the rake, her own breath.

"No, my lady," she said. "I've been fortunate."

"Eleven years of fortune." Ovelin drank her tea. Set down the cup. Aligned it with the saucer's painted ring, precisely, a woman who aligned everything. "You know what's happening in the world, I assume. Aldric's edict. The cleansing. They're calling it the Edict of Purity in some places, Cleansing in others; the usurper was always fond of renaming things. Every bearer of the gift must be surrendered to his hunters. Every harborer punished. Every valley searched." She sighed, and the sigh sounded genuinely weary, and that was the most frightening thing yet — that the weariness might be real. "Hollowmere has always been safe, of course. Magic cannot follow here. Whatever the gift is, it starves in our air. That was the design, ages ago, when they hollowed the old country. So I have told my people: you are safe, be at peace, tend your ovens and your looms."

She reached beneath the table — slowly, deliberately, letting Isandro watch — and drew out a small object wrapped in linen. She set it on the cloth between them and unfolded the linen with two fingers.

A candle stub. Burned down to a thumb's length. Its wick was black and bent, and the wax around it had hardened in a pattern Isandro recognized with a sickness that started in her stomach and climbed: the wax had not dripped downward. It had flowed *sideways*, frozen mid-pour, as if pulled toward something that was no longer there.

"This was found in the vestry," Ovelin said. "After your shift ended that night. After the kitchen recorded that you left the main hall for the space of perhaps a quarter hour — a gap one of the under-cooks noted, because gaps in your schedule are as rare as your illnesses." She rewrapped the candle with the same unhurried care. "Now. I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to understand that I am asking it as your protector. Because if the hunters come to Hollowmere — and they will come, Aldric's patience is not infinite — then whatever I believe about you determines whether you stand in this kitchen next winter or burn in a cart on the road east. So." She folded her hands. "Were you in the vestry two nights ago?"

Every instinct Isandro possessed, every year of the honest, invisible, dutiful life she had built, rose up and answered *yes* — yes, it was me, the flame bent, the smoke curled, I don't know what I am, please help me. And beneath that instinct, colder and newer, another voice: *she has already decided.* The candle was evidence gathered, not evidence found. The broth was a test already scored. This kindness was a scale, and Isandro was being weighed, and the weight had been fixed before she ever entered the room.

She lied.

"No, my lady," she said. "I wasn't in the vestry. I've never been in the vestry. That night I went to the chapel yard to be alone — I do that sometimes, after service, when the kitchens are loud — but I never went inside. I've never seen that candle before."

And it came out smooth. That was the horror of it. Smooth as poured cream, steady as water finding its level, with just the right grain of nervousness in it — a slight catch, a lowered gaze, hands folding tighter in her lap. She had rehearsed nothing. She had simply opened her mouth and the lie stepped out fully formed, wearing honesty's clothes, curtsying at the door.

*I've tasted a hundred poisons,* she thought, *and never once felt myself die. But I know exactly how this feels.*

Ovelin studied her for a long moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the kitchen bell rang for the morning bread, muffled by floors and distance, a world going on without her.

"Good," the regent said at last, warmly, terribly. "Then it was someone else. Someone small and dark-haired, seen through a keyhole, who left a candle that behaved impossibly in a valley where nothing behaves impossibly." She rose, and the audience was clearly over, and Isandro rose with her, legs steady through some mercy of the body. "Eat something before you go. Take the honey. You've earned it — eleven years of keeping me alive deserves honey."

At the door, Ovelin's hand touched her shoulder, light as a falling leaf.

"Taster Dunmore. If you should ever learn anything — anything at all — about who was in that vestry, you'll come to me first. Before the guards, before the priests, before anyone. Because I protect what's mine." The smile was warm bread again. "And you have been mine for eleven years."

* * *

She walked back through the corridors with the honey jar cold in her hands and did not remember the stairs. In the kitchen, the morning rush swallowed her — orders shouted, loaves split open, Marta bellowing about the state of the fish — and Isandro moved through it like a ghost through a wedding, present and unseen, doing her work with hands that knew it without her.

It was only at noon, tasting the regent's midday soup in the little stone alcove that served as her office, that she understood the shape of what had happened. She held the spoon halfway to her mouth and stopped.

The broth that morning had been laced. Wolfsbane, coaxed and diluted — a dose calibrated, she now saw, not to harm but to *observe*. A dose that would make an ordinary person's tongue tingle, their lips numb, their eyes water. A dose that would leave no mark at all on a woman whose body simply refused such things. Ovelin had not been testing whether Isandro could detect the poison. Any taster could detect wolfsbane. She had been testing whether the poison could detect *Isandro* — whether the valley's great silence would hold her the way it held everyone, or whether something in her answered anyway.

And Isandro had drunk it and felt nothing, and reported the bitterness honestly, and lied about everything else, and the regent had smiled and called her *mine*.

She set down the spoon. Through the alcove's slit window she could see the eastern road, thin as a wire, running away toward Aldric's country, and somewhere on it — days out, weeks out, but coming — grey hoods and humming devices and carts built for burning.

She had lied today for the first time in her life, and it had come easily, and that frightened her more than the candle, more than the broth, more than the regent's warm hand on her shoulder. Because a person who could lie that smoothly was a person who could survive what was coming. And survival, she was beginning to understand, would cost her the self she had spent eleven years building — the honest, invisible, dutiful taster, the woman whose word was iron.

The soup steamed untouched. Below in the yard, a horse was arriving at a hard gallop, foam on its neck, a rider in travel-stained grey swinging down before the beast had fully stopped — and the gate wardens were not barring him but bowing, and the under-cook was already running for the steward, and the whole palace began, note by note, to change its tune.

Grey, Isandro thought. He wore grey.

She put down the spoon and went to tell Dame Ovelin that the king's hunters had come to Hollowmere.
