## 5. Hunters at the Gate

They came at dawn, when the mist still lay in the valley like a held breath.

Isandro saw them first from the scullery window — three riders on grey horses, moving at a walk up the mill road, unhurried as men who had never once been told no. Their hoods were the color of wet ash, drawn deep despite the early hour, and something about the way they sat their saddles made her stomach tighten before her mind caught up. Not soldiers. Soldiers announced themselves. These came the way illness comes: quietly, and already inside.

She was kneading bread dough, her hands white to the wrist, and she did not stop kneading. That was the first strange thing she noticed about herself — that terror could arrive and the body would go on with its work, flour and water and salt, because the body knew that bread waited for no one.

"Morel," she said to the under-cook, keeping her voice level. "Riders on the mill road."

Morel squinted past her, wiping his hands on his apron. "Tax collectors, likely. Or Aldric's post."

But he said it too quickly, and Isandro heard the same thing underneath his words that she felt underneath her own ribs: they all knew. The edict had been read at the well nine days ago, and since then Hollowmere had been holding its breath, the whole valley one long inhale, everyone waiting to learn whether the cleansing would come here or pass them by.

It had not passed them by.

* * *

The hunters dismounted in the outer courtyard, below the kitchen's high windows, and Isandro watched from behind the leaded glass as the head of the household guard went out to meet them. She could not hear what was said — the window was thick, built to keep the cold out of the ovens — but she could see the guard's posture change, the way a man's spine bends when he reads a seal he cannot refuse.

One of the hunters carried a case. Long, narrow, bound in grey leather, worn at the corners from travel. When he set it down on the cobbles and opened it, the other two turned away slightly, the way men do around things they respect or fear or both.

The device inside was small. That surprised her. She had imagined — though she had not let herself imagine it, exactly; it had arrived unbidden in the nights since the proclamation — she had imagined something grand and terrible, an altar-piece, a machine of iron and ceremony. This was the size of a loaf of bread. Dark metal, unadorned, with a mouth like a bell's mouth but no bell. It made no sound she could hear.

But she could feel it.

That was the second strange thing. A pressure behind her sternum, faint as a finger pressed to a bruise, and it grew as the hunter lifted the device from its case and cradled it against his chest. Not pain. Recognition. The way a tuning fork answers a note struck across a room.

*No,* she thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than any feeling. *Not me. I am nothing. I am the valley's own. Magic cannot follow into Hollowmere.*

The thought repeated itself like a prayer, and like most prayers it was addressed to a god she was no longer sure was listening.

* * *

By midmorning the whole household had been assembled in the outer yard. Cooks and grooms, laundresses and stable-hands, the chamber staff and the gardeners, even the old gatekeeper who had served Dame Ovelin's father before her. Perhaps sixty souls, standing in the thin sunlight while the three hunters walked the lines slowly, the humming device held out before them like a lantern.

Isandro stood among the kitchen women, her hands folded, her face arranged into the mild blankness she wore for tastings. She had spent eleven years perfecting that face. Nothing showed in it. Nothing ever had.

The device hummed.

She heard it now, or heard it in some way deeper than hearing — a low, patient tone, like a wineglass rimmed by a wet finger, and it rose and fell as the hunters moved along the rows. Near the laundresses it was silent. Near the farriers it murmured and died. Near the old gatekeeper it gave one soft pulse, and Isandro's heart stopped, and then the hunter moved on, and she understood: the device answered everything a little, the way a compass trembles near any iron. It was listening for more than a tremble.

It was listening for a voice.

And hers, whatever it was — whatever the candle flame had bent toward, whatever the broth never touched — hers stayed shut. Sealed. The valley had seen to that long ago, or so she told herself, over and over, a rosary of denial counted on flour-white fingers.

Then the hunters reached the stables.

Tam was there because Tam was always there. Sixteen years old, all elbows and freckles, with straw in his hair no matter how often Wren-the-laundress picked it out. He had been the first person in Hollowmere to speak to Isandro without fear, back when she was new and the other servants crossed themselves after she passed. He used to steal honeycakes from the kitchen and blame the cats. He had once told her, gravely, that horses liked her better than people because she didn't pretend.

He was holding a currycomb. He was smiling at the hunter in front of him, that open, guileless smile, because Tam smiled at everyone, because Tam had never in his life met a thing he needed to be afraid of.

The device screamed.

There was no other word for it. The low tone climbed and climbed until it broke into a shriek like a kettle boiling dry, and every head in the yard turned, and the hunter holding it stumbled half a step as if the sound had shoved him.

Tam's smile faded slowly, in stages, like a lamp running out of oil. He looked at the device. He looked at the hunter. He looked, last of all, across the yard, directly at Isandro — and she would carry the look for the rest of her life, though nothing in it accused her. It was only confusion. Only a boy asking the world, with his whole face, why this was happening.

"Take him," the lead hunter said. His voice was quiet, almost gentle, and somehow that was the worst part. "Gently."

* * *

What happened next took less time than the telling of it.

Two hunters took Tam's arms. He did not fight — that was the thing Isandro could not stop seeing afterward, in the dark behind her eyelids, the thing that woke her for years: he did not fight. He let himself be led, turning his head once to call out to the stable-master, "Don't let them saddle Cinder without warming her bit, she'll crack her teeth—" and the stable-master, a broad gray man who had known Tam since he was found abandoned in the tack room one winter, looked at the boy and said nothing, and did nothing, and wept without moving his face.

The kitchen women stood shoulder to shoulder with Isandro. She could feel Morel beside her, trembling. She could feel the laundress Wren's hand find hers, cold and gripping, and neither of them pulled away, and neither of them moved.

Sixty people. Three hunters. One boy.

The arithmetic sat in Isandro's chest like a swallowed stone.

"He's just a child," someone whispered behind her. "He mucks stalls. What gift can a boy who mucks stalls—"

"Horses," another voice answered, barely audible. "His mother was from over the pass. They say her family could gentle anything." A pause. "I always thought it was just that he was good with them."

*I always thought,* Isandro thought, and the words turned to acid. Because she had known. Not known-known, not the way the device knew — but she had noticed things about Tam and filed them away in the place where she kept everything she refused to examine. The way foals followed him into strange water. The way the stallion who bit everyone else stood docile as a dog for him. She had noticed, and she had loved him a little for it, and she had said nothing, because saying something would have meant asking herself the question that lived at the bottom of her own well.

The hunters led Tam toward the gate. And here is what Isandro did:

She stood in the yard of the only home she had ever known, in a crowd of sixty able-bodied souls, and she watched.

She watched them put him on a horse — his own horse, Cinder, taken from the paddock, and wasn't that a cruelty with a certain economy to it, making the animal he loved carry him away. She watched him twist in the saddle to wave, actually wave, because Tam had decided this must be some mistake that would be sorted out by supper, and mistakes deserved good manners. She watched the grey hoods ride him down the mill road, through the mist that was burning off now, thinner and thinner, until the road bent at the willows and the riders were gone and the sound of the hooves was gone and there was only the yard, and the silence, and the sun coming up warm and gold on a morning that had murdered something.

Then the head cook burned something in the hearth — Isandro smelled it from the doorway, juniper and salt, the old rite the valley used for grief, done furtively, illegally, with the flue choked so the smoke wouldn't rise above the roofline. Even mourning had to be smuggled now.

And Isandro went back to work.

* * *

That was the shame of it. Not that she had been afraid — fear was honest, fear was animal, no one could be blamed for fear. But she had gone back to her station, and she had shaped the noon loaves, and she had tasted the regent's soup at the appointed hour and found it clean, and she had done all of it competently, calmly, with the mild blank face, while somewhere down the mill road a sixteen-year-old boy rode toward a pyre or a prison or whatever Aldric's cleansing did with the ones it caught.

She had done nothing.

Worse: she had discovered, in the terrible laboratory of that morning, that doing nothing was easy. That her body knew how. Eleven years of tasting poison had taught her a discipline most soldiers never learned — the discipline of swallowing. Of taking the worst thing offered and letting it pass through you without a flicker, without a face, without a single muscle betraying what it cost.

She had swallowed Tam.

She knelt that night in her narrow cell off the kitchen, in the dark, and pressed her fists against her mouth, and understood that the edict had not come for her today. Today it had come for something else. It had come and found the girl she believed she was — loyal, careful, harmless — and had weighed her in the balance of one boy's confusion, and had found her wanting.

*You watched,* said a voice in her, her own voice, colder than any hunter's. *You stood behind glass and in a crowd and behind your blank face, and you watched. You are a taster. You know exactly what poison looks like. You have spent your whole life learning to recognize it, and this morning you drank it and called it prudence.*

The candle stub on her shelf guttered. Bending toward her, the way it had bent before, toward her finger, toward whatever she was. Even her grief answered her now. Even her shame had a flavor, and the candle leaned into it like a plant toward light.

She pinched the flame out with her bare fingers. It did not burn her. That frightened her more than anything else that day — more than the device, more than the grey hoods, more than the empty stable where Cinder's stall stood swept and waiting for a boy who would not come back to it.

Her skin did not burn.

She sat in the dark a long time, holding her unburned fingers, and the valley lay silent around her, magicless, safe, hollow — and for the first time in her life Isandro Dunmore understood that the safety had never been hers. She had been living in the eye of a storm, calling the stillness peace.

Somewhere out there, past the willows, past the edge of everything she knew, Tam was riding in a grey hood's custody, and he had waved at her, and she had not waved back.

* * *

Dame Ovelin sent for her at moonrise.

The regent's sitting room was warm, firelit, smelling of beeswax and dried lavender, and Dame Ovelin looked older than she had a week ago — smaller inside her robes, her sharp face softened by something that might have been sorrow or might have been calculation wearing sorrow's clothes. Isandro had never learned to tell the two apart in her. She suspected no one had.

"You saw the morning's business," the regent said. It was not a question.

"Yes, my lady."

"And you were not troubled by it?"

Isandro kept her eyes lowered, her hands folded, the picture of a servant properly chastened. "A boy was taken, my lady. Troubled is the least of it."

"Mm." The regent poured two cups of wine — an honor, a small one, but honors had been rare lately and each one rang false. "The hunters will return within the week. They are sweeping the valley house by house. The device, they say, does not lie." She sipped. "Tell me, Isandro. In all your years at my table, tasting for me, has anything ever... lingered? Anything strange? Any broth that behaved oddly? Any flame that leaned?"

The question was soft as down, and barbed all the way through.

"No, my lady," Isandro said. "Nothing. I am the valley's own. There is nothing in me for their device to find."

She lied.

She lied smoothly, instantly, without the stumble or the heat she had always imagined would betray a liar, and the ease of it appalled her — appalled her more than the watching, more than the swallowed morning, because this, too, was a kind of immunity. Apparently she was immune to truth as well.

Dame Ovelin studied her for a long moment over the rim of her cup. Then she set the wine down and smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near her eyes.

"I believe you," the regent said gently. "Go and sleep, child. The hunters come back Thursday."

Isandro bowed and left, walking the cold corridor with her heart hammering a single word against her ribs, over and over, like the hum of a device searching for a voice:

Thursday. Thursday. Thursday.

Three days. She had three days to decide what she was — the girl who watched, or something else entirely — and she already knew, lying awake in her cell with her unburned hand curled against her chest, that the decision had been made for her the moment Tam turned in the saddle and waved.

She had not waved back.

She would not make that mistake twice.

At the bottom of her trunk, beneath eleven years of careful, obedient, poisoned life, Isandro found the things she had never let herself pack: a knife, a coil of rope, the coin purse she'd hidden from herself. She wrapped them in cloth while the valley slept, and listened to the mill-race churning in the dark beyond her window, loud and cold and running out past the willows, out past the edge of everything —

and for the first time, she let herself wonder what was on the other side.
