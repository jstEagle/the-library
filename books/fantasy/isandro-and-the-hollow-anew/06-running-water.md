## 6. Running Water

The mill-race ran behind the tannery like a secret nobody bothered keeping. Isandro had known about it since she was nine years old, when she'd followed a runaway cat along its mossy lip and discovered that the whole eastern wall of Hollowmere could be walked around if you didn't mind wet boots and the smell of rendered fat. Everyone knew. That was the thing — everyone knew, and no one used it, because no one had ever needed to leave.

She crouched in the shadow of the tanning sheds with her bundle pressed to her chest and listened to the water go by, black and quick and indifferent, and she tried to make herself believe that this was still an ordinary night.

It was not working.

Above her, on the hill, the palace windows burned yellow against the dark, and one of them — the narrow one at the end of the west wing, the one that looked out over the kitchens and the well and the whole small world of her life — was lit even now, past midnight. Dame Ovelin did not sleep. Isandro had served the woman broth for eleven years and had never once seen her chamber dark before dawn. She had thought it was diligence. She had thought it was devotion to the regency, to the valley, to the fragile machinery of a kingdom that existed only because everyone agreed not to look too hard at what it had replaced.

Now she knew better. The regent was awake because she was waiting for the grey hoods to come back with their humming box and tell her whether the taster's blood sang.

Isandro's hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a kind of detached irritation, as though they belonged to someone else — some frightened girl who had never drunk hemlock and nightshade and arsenic-laced wine without so much as a sour stomach. *Stop it,* she told them. *You've swallowed worse than fear.*

But she hadn't. That was the truth of it. She had swallowed every poison the palace could brew and felt nothing, and tonight, for the first time, she understood why, and the understanding was worse than any toxin. The candle flame had bent toward her finger. It had *leaned*, like a flower toward sun, like a dog toward its master's hand, and she had stood in her little room above the scullery with her heart slamming and watched fire behave wrongly in her presence until it guttered out, and she had known — the way you know your own name — that the edict read from the well that morning had been written for her.

*All bearers of the gift must be surrendered to the crown for cleansing.*

The head cook, Marta, had burned something in the great hearth after the criers passed — a sprig of dried vervain, old superstition, smoke rising straight up in the dead air of the kitchen while forty people stood silent among the copper pots. No one had spoken. No one had looked at anyone else. And when Isandro had reached for the tasting spoon an hour later, Marta had taken it from her hand gently, the way you take a knife from a child, and said, "I'll do it today, love," and that gentleness had been the loudest alarm in the whole kingdom.

They knew. Or they suspected. And kindness, Isandro had learned in eleven years of palace service, was what people gave you when they'd already decided your fate and wanted to feel clean about it.

A dog barked somewhere up the hill. Isandro flinched, then made herself breathe. Not the hunters' dogs — those were kennelled at the gatehouse; Tam had told her once, in his rambling way, that they were fed at dusk and slept like stones till morning. Tam. Her stomach turned over. They had taken Tam two days ago, walking him out of the stable yard between two grey-hooded men while he looked back over his shoulder at the palace windows, at *her* window, and she had stood behind the glass doing nothing, absolutely nothing, her body refusing to move as though the valley itself had pinned her there.

She would not be pinned again. Whatever waited outside Hollowmere — whatever the wide world did to people like her — it could not be worse than standing at a window while a boy was led away in chains.

She moved.

The tannery yard was ankle-deep in sawdust and stink; she crossed it bent double, bundle clutched tight, and came down the bank to the race where the water ran fast and shallow over its stone bed. Cold hit her ankles through her boots and stole her breath. She waded in anyway, gasping, and began to walk upstream against the current, because Sef Ardenne — the disgraced cartographer she'd found three nights ago drinking alone at the Drowned Ox, the man who had looked at her across a table and said, without being told, *"You're one of the hollowed ones, aren't you? I can always tell"* — had told her one thing she trusted above all the rest of his strange talk:

"Running water confuses their device. Old magic hates a moving current. If they come for you, get into the river and stay in it."

She hadn't believed him then. She believed him now, because she had heard the hum. Yesterday evening, crossing the market square, the grey hoods had been there with their brass-and-bone contraption held out before them like a lantern, and it had made a sound — not loud, more felt than heard, a wasp trapped inside her skull, a tuning fork pressed to her teeth — and she had watched a fishmonger's wife freeze mid-transaction, eyes going wide and guilty, before the hoods closed around her and led her away to the sound of her children crying. The hum had brushed past Isandro like a cold draft. It had paused. It had *hesitated*. And then the mill wheel had groaned into motion down the lane and the sound had slid off her like rain off oiled cloth.

Water. Running water. She put one foot in front of the other against the current and let the race carry her story away behind her.

* * *

The valley narrowed as she went east. Hollowmere was shaped like a cupped hand — she knew this from Sef's forbidden maps, the ones he'd shown her by candlelight, charts of a country called Vessia that appeared on no sanctioned document anywhere in the kingdom — and the walls of it rose on either side of her now, black shoulders of rock blotting out the stars, so that she walked in a corridor of darkness with only the silver ribbon of sky overhead and the water shouting around her knees.

Twice she stopped and listened for the hum. Twice there was nothing but the water and her own pulse.

Her bundle held almost nothing. A change of clothes. The bone-handled tasting spoon she'd stolen from the kitchen — she couldn't have said why, except that it was the only object in the world that knew what she was, the only witness to eleven years of quiet heroism that no one had ever thanked her for. A heel of bread wrapped in cloth. And underneath it all, flat against her ribs where her heartbeat could warm it, the small tin of antidotes Marta had pressed into her hands at the last, saying nothing, only gripping her wrists hard enough to bruise before turning back to her ovens.

Marta knew. Marta had always known, maybe. And Marta had chosen the spoon's side.

Isandro stopped in the middle of the race and let herself cry for exactly thirty seconds — she counted them against the rhythm of the water — and then she wiped her face on her shoulder and kept walking, because grief was a luxury and the current didn't care about it.

That was the thing about running water. It went one direction only. There was no tasting it first, no weighing it, no sending a spoon ahead of yourself into the unknown. You simply went, or you drowned.

* * *

She smelled the edge of the valley before she saw it.

Not a smell exactly — more a thinning, the way air thins near a high window. All her life Hollowmere had tasted faintly of nothing, a plateau flavor under everything, bread and beer and river-mud all slightly muted, as though the whole valley sat under a bell jar. She had never noticed it until this moment, wading toward the eastern gap where the mountains parted, when suddenly the air ahead of her grew *loud* — pine resin and cold stone and distant woodsmoke and a hundred green growing things all shouting their names at once, unfiltered, unmuted, alive.

Sef had called it the hollowing. "The valleys are corked," he'd said, jabbing his pipe at his maps. "Someone, long ago, drove the magic out of these places and stoppered them, and everything inside got... flattened. Safe and small. Your kingdom isn't a kingdom, girl. It's a cellar."

She had thought him mad. She had thought him mad right up until the moment a candle flame leaned toward her finger like a lover.

Now she climbed the last of the race — the water spilling out ahead of her over a lip of rock into the wider dark beyond — and pulled herself up onto the stones, dripping, shaking, and stood at the edge of the only world she had ever known.

The stars were wrong.

That was her first thought, absurd and total: *the stars are wrong.* They weren't wrong, of course — they were simply *more*, hundreds more than she had ever seen, thousands, a spill of white fire across a sky so vast it made her stagger. In Hollowmere the valley walls cropped the heavens to a neat rectangle, a garden plot of stars, tidy as a kitchen herb bed. Here the sky went on forever in every direction, and beneath it the land rolled away black and silver and endless — forest to the north, open moor to the south, a thread of river catching moonlight far below, and somewhere out there, past all of it, cities she had only heard named in market gossip: Callowmark, Vell's Landing, the free coast.

The wind hit her face and it was enormous. It carried things. Rain that had fallen on mountains she'd never see, the breath of forests older than the kingdom, the far-off iron-and-onion smell of some town whose name she didn't know. Eleven years she had lived inside a flavorless jar, and she had called it safety, and she had called it home, and both of those things were true, and both of those things were ending, and she stood on the lip of the world with water running out of her boots and laughed — actually laughed, a wild cracked sound that the wind took and scattered — because it was terrible and it was magnificent and she was free.

Then the laughter died in her throat, because freedom, she discovered, has a taste, and the taste is loss.

The palace would be waking soon. Marta would light the ovens and find the taster's room empty and say nothing to anyone, and Dame Ovelin would send riders, and the grey hoods would come with their humming box, and the valley would go on being safe and small for everyone who stayed, everyone who kept their heads down and their gifts buried deep enough. The baker's boy who could make dough rise with a touch. The midwife who sang fevers down. The fishmonger's wife, already gone. Tam, wherever they were keeping him.

She had left them. That was the arithmetic of this night, plain as a ledger: one girl saved, a valley abandoned to its hunters. She had spent her whole life protecting these people one spoonful at a time — taking the risk at every meal so that no one else had to die for a duke's dinner — and now she was running, and every step east was a step away from the people she'd sworn to keep safe.

"You couldn't have saved them anyway," she said aloud, to the wind, to herself. "One girl with a strong stomach against a king's hunt." The wind didn't answer. The wind just kept coming, huge and indifferent, carrying the smell of a world that had never heard of Hollowmere.

She thought of Tam looking back over his shoulder. She thought of the candle flame leaning toward her finger, hungry and obedient, and wondered what else in her was waiting to lean.

* * *

Dawn caught her on the moor road, or what passed for one — a rutted track winding down through gorse and bracken toward the river country, following the water the way Sef had told her to follow it. "Rivers remember roads," he'd said. "Every settlement since the beginning of time sits on a river. Follow the water downstream and you'll find either help or trouble, and in my experience the two travel together."

Her legs ached. Her feet were ruined — she'd misjudged the distance to the first ford and walked miles in soaked boots, and now each step was a negotiation with blisters. She was hungry in a way she'd never been hungry in her life; in the palace, food arrived tested and seasoned and abundant, and she had never once considered what it meant to walk all day without any. The bread was gone by midnight. She'd eaten it sitting on a milestone in the dark, and here was a strange thing: the milestone bore letters, old ones, half-worn, and beneath the moss she could make out the ghost of a word scratched over roughly, deliberately defaced, the way you'd strike through a debt.

She hadn't been able to read what lay under the scratch. But Sef's voice had been there in her head anyway, dry and certain: *Vessia. Everything out here is Vessia, girl. They renamed the roads and burned the records, but the roads still go where they've always gone.*

She crested a rise as the sun came up behind her, throwing her shadow long and strange across the moor, and stopped dead.

Below her, where the river widened into its first true valley, a town sat smoking its morning fires — real smoke, woodsmoke and bake-oven smoke, dozens of chimneys, a bridge, a mill, carts already creaking on the far road. People. Hundreds of people, none of whom had ever seen her, none of whom would know a taster's stride or a servant's bowed shoulders or the particular way a hunted person holds their bundle too close. Anonymity, vast and golden in the morning light.

And on the road between her and the town, perhaps half a mile off, moving slowly along the riverbank, three figures in grey.

Isandro dropped into the gorse so fast she tore her sleeve and felt blood on her forearm and didn't care. Her heart slammed. She lay among the prickling branches and watched them through the stems — hunters, it had to be hunters, the hoods, the deliberate pace, and one of them carrying something long and pale held out before him like a divining rod.

The hum reached her a moment later. Faint, at this distance, a wasp behind a wall — but unmistakable, and her whole body remembered the market square, the fishmonger's wife's wide guilty eyes, the children crying.

They were sweeping the river. Downstream. Toward the town.

And the river, she realized with a lurch, was the only way forward she had — Sef's advice, her one plan, follow the water — and they were walking it ahead of her, combing it, patient as netters drawing a weir. Sooner or later their device would pass close enough to catch whatever leaked out of her, the candle-bending thing, the sealed vessel Sef had stared at across a tavern table and named without being told. Running water had saved her in the race. But they were *in* the water country now, all of it, everywhere, and there was nowhere left to run that wasn't running toward them.

Unless.

She watched them again, forcing her breathing slow, making herself think like a poisoner instead of a prey animal. Poisons had directions, her old master used to say. Every toxin had a nature — some raced to the heart, some crept to the lungs, some sat in the belly and waited. The trick was never to fight the nature. The trick was to use it.

Three hunters, moving downstream, expecting their quarry to run *away* — east, south, anywhere but toward them. Nobody ran toward the hunt.

Isandro lay in the gorse until the grey figures dropped below the next fold of land, and then she rose, tore a branch of gorse, and rubbed its crushed green reek over her sleeves and hair until she smelled like the moor itself. Then she turned downstream, toward the town, toward the hunters, and started walking — along the high bank, in the open, in plain sight, an ordinary traveler on an ordinary morning, letting the river's noise drown whatever small song the thing in her chest might make.

Halfway down the slope, on the road ahead, she saw a figure sitting on a fallen wall, watching her come. A man, thin and weathered, with a cartographer's ink-stained fingers and a look on his face like a man who has been waiting for someone.

Sef Ardenne raised one hand, not quite a wave, and said, loudly enough to carry:

"Took you long enough. We need to talk about the Door."
