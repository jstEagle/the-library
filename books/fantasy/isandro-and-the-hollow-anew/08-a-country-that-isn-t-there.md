## 8. A Country That Isn't There

Sef Ardenne had learned long ago that the best way to tell a lie of state was to make it boring.

That was the genius of it, really. No one guards against boredom. The erasure of Vessia hadn't been accomplished with fire or massacre — those leave scars, and scars invite questions. It had been accomplished with ledgers. With the slow, patient replacement of one word for another on ten thousand documents until no living clerk could say when the swap had happened. *Vessia* became *the Hollow Territories* became *unincorporated crown land* became nothing at all, a blank margin where a country used to be. Children grew up pointing at the blank and calling it geography.

He had spent eleven years trying to draw it back.

Now he sat across a fire from a runaway food-taster who had crossed half the valley's edge in one night on blistered feet, and he understood that everything he'd drawn was about to matter or get them both killed, and he could not yet tell which.

She was watching him the way he imagined she watched plates meant for kings: cataloguing, weighing, waiting for him to turn out poisoned. Her hands were wrapped in strips torn from her apron. She had refused his offer of the blanket twice, which told him she was either proud or frightened, and her eyes said both.

"You knew," she said. "Back at the crossing. You looked at me like you knew what I was."

"I didn't know. I guessed." Sef turned the map case over in his hands. Oiled leather, scarred at the corners from years of being clutched too hard. "You walked through the mill-race without flinching. Running water kills most traces, but you came out the far side still *humming*. Anyone who's spent time around the hollowed places learns to feel it. Like standing near a struck bell that won't finish ringing."

"I don't hum." She said it flatly, but her hand drifted toward her own sternum, as if checking.

"No," he agreed. "You *contain*. There's a difference. The valley hums because magic pools there like rainwater in a cellar. You're the opposite. You're dry ground walking around in a wet world." He fed a stick to the fire. "I've only felt it twice before. Once in an old woman in Drossholt who could sour milk by looking at it. They took her before I could warn her. And once—" He stopped.

"Once?"

"Once in myself, briefly, when I held something I shouldn't have." He let that sit. She didn't press. Good. Curiosity without greed; another point in her favor.

"So tell me," Isandro said. "What is it you think I am? Because I've spent my whole life being nothing special. A tongue. A tool. I sip what might kill regents, and I go to bed, and I wake up, and nothing happens to me. If there's a word for that, I'd like to hear it."

"There are several words. Most of them are on lists." He opened the map case.

* * *

The first thing she saw when he unrolled the chart was the blankness.

Sef had watched a hundred faces meet this map, and they all did the same thing: their eyes went looking for Vessia and found paper. The chart showed the free cities along the coast — Callowmark's double harbor, Marrowfen's marsh-roads, the salt-towns strung like beads down the shore. It showed the crownlands, Aldric's grey-and-gold, neat and surveyed. And inland, east of the mountains, where every sanctioned atlas printed tidy fields and prosperous market towns under the seal of the Crown, Sef had drawn instead a shape outlined in fine, stubborn ink: borders, rivers, roads, cities. All of it labeled in a dead language. All of it crossed by a single diagonal stroke of red, the cartographer's mark for *disputed*, except that in eleven years no one had ever disputed it, because officially there was nothing there to dispute.

"Vessia," he said.

"That's not possible." Isandro leaned closer, and the firelight moved across her face. "Vessia fell two hundred years ago. Before Hollowmere. Before the valleys. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what they were taught. Show me the battle." He tapped the blank space. "Show me where the last king died. Show me the treaty, the surrender, the occupation. Go on. Name me one date, one field, one grave."

Her mouth opened. Closed. He watched her reach for the story every child in the valleys was raised on — the old kingdom that fell, the dark times, the wise founders who built the hollowed sanctuaries where magic could not follow — and watched her find, beneath the story, nothing holding it up.

"There isn't one," she said slowly. "There's no battle. There's just... after. We always started the story *after*."

"Because there was no before, not anymore. Not anywhere it counted." Sef sat back. This was the part he hated telling, because it was the part no one believed, and the part that was truest. "Conquest leaves evidence, Isandro. Burned granaries. New coins. Bastard children with foreign names. Two hundred years is long enough to bury a lot, but not all of it — not unless someone went through the country like a clerk with a knife, cutting out every mention, every record, every *name*, and then burned the knife."

"And someone did."

"The Unmaking wasn't an army. It was a project. Decades of it. Archivists, surveyors, heralds — my predecessors, if you want to be grim about it. They didn't defeat Vessia. They *edited* it." He traced the red diagonal stroke. "Every charter rewritten. Every gravestone re-cut with new names, or broken and buried. Every song collected and reprinted with the Vessian words replaced. They changed the roads so they no longer met the old ones. They moved boundaries on paper until the paper disagreed with the ground, and then they declared the paper correct, because paper always wins eventually. People believe maps. I should know. I make them."

"But why? What does a conqueror gain by pretending a country never existed? Why not just rule it?"

"Because ruling leaves a wound, and wounds fester. Erasure leaves a smooth surface." He looked into the fire. "And because of what Vessia *was*. Not its armies — those were ordinary. Its magic. Vessia was a country where the gift ran through everything, the way water runs through chalk. Every child born there had some small piece of it. Not wonders. Small things. Bread that rose true. Doors that knew their owners. Candles that bent toward grief. A whole nation of tiny magics, woven together, holding each other up."

Isandro's hand had gone again to her sternum. "The candle," she whispered. "In the pantry. Mine bent toward my finger."

"Yes."

"That's not possible either. Magic can't follow into the valleys. That's the entire — that's what Hollowmere *is*. Nothing gifted can exist there. The edict—"

"The edict is a lie built on top of a bigger lie." Sef kept his voice level, though his pulse had picked up. This was the edge of the cliff, and he was about to walk them both off it. "Magic can't follow into the valleys. True. Do you know why?"

She shook her head.

"Because the valleys were made to hold it. All of it. When the Unmakers finished with the records, they turned to the harder problem: the gift itself. You can burn books, but you can't burn what's in the blood — not cleanly, not quickly. So they built vessels. Great sunk basins of suppressed ground, warded and hollowed, and they drove the gift of an entire nation down into them like driving a herd into a canyon. Hollowmere. The other valleys. Every 'sanctuary' where magic cannot follow." He laughed, and it came out bitter. "They called them mercies. Places the tainted could live safe from themselves. And people wept with gratitude and moved in, and their grandchildren were born there, and the walls of the canyon got taller every generation, and no one asked why a mercy needed wardens."

Isandro had gone very still. Firelight caught the fine tremor in her jaw. "The gift of a whole nation," she said. "Down in the valleys. Under Hollowmere."

"Asleep. Pooled. Suppressed." He nodded. "Which brings us to you."

"I'm a taster."

"You're a taster who should have died nine hundred deaths." He leaned forward. "Think about it properly, the way you've never been allowed to. Poison is chemistry, yes, but in Vessia — in real Vessia — poison was also a *thing*, almost a creature. Malice made material. And your body has been drinking malice since you were a child, and it slides off you like rain off wax. That's not a strong stomach. That's not luck. That is a vessel so perfectly sealed that nothing gets in — including, I'd wager, the pool's slow call. The valley suppresses everyone, but you it *corked*. Somewhere in your line there was Vessian blood, and somewhere in your making, the sealing took deeper than it ever should have. You aren't outside the magic, Isandro. You're a jar it's been poured into and stoppered. And jars—" he hesitated, hating the next words and saying them anyway, because she deserved the truth even if it terrified her, "—jars do not hold forever."

* * *

She didn't speak for a long time. Sef let her. He busied himself with the fire, with the kettle, with the small domestic machinery of a camp, giving her silence the way you give a wounded animal a clear path.

When she finally spoke, her voice was steady in the way of things balanced on a knife's edge. "Say the rest. You've been circling it since the crossing. The Door."

"Ah." He unrolled a second chart — smaller, older, drawn on paper that had gone the color of weak tea. This one he had never shown anyone alive. "This is the part that got me exiled."

The chart showed the mountains east of the blank country, and threaded among them, in ink so fine it was nearly a whisper, a road. It began nowhere and ended nowhere, and along its length Sef had marked points — not towns, not landmarks, but small circles with a word beside each: *surfaced here, 3rd of Harvest, year unnamed. Surfaced here, winter, year unnamed.*

"A road that appears on no other map," he said. "The coronation road of Vessia. The way kings traveled to be crowned, back when crowning meant something the Unmakers wanted dead. And the Door of Anew travels it."

"A door doesn't travel."

"This one does. It's not a door the way your kitchen door is a door. It's a door the way a wound is a door — a place where the erasure didn't take. Think of what they did as a seam sewn shut across a whole country. Every stitch holds, except the Unmakers could never close the last one, because the last one was the coronation itself — the moment a country *agrees to exist*. You can't edit consent out of a people entirely. So the Door remains, moving along the old road like a splinter working its way toward the skin. It surfaces. It opens, briefly. Things come through — light, sometimes voices, once, according to a shepherd's account I paid dearly for, a smell of bread baking in a country that has no ovens. Then it sinks again and moves on."

"And you found this how? An exile with no patron, mapping a road that doesn't exist."

"Badly. Obsessively. By trading everything I had." He touched the tea-colored paper with one finger, gently, the way you touch a scar. "I was the royal cartographer's second son, given a post nobody wanted: surveying the Hollow Territories, confirming the blanks. Confirming emptiness, year after year. And everywhere I went, I found things that shouldn't survive an erasure. A milestone with the numerals chiseled off but the stone too heavy to move. A bridge built for traffic that no sanctioned road ever carried. Villages whose festivals didn't match any calendar the Crown published. Little inconsistencies. *Seams.* I started collecting them. I started drawing what the seams implied." He smiled without humor. "My superiors called it sedition. Drawing a country that legally does not exist is a strange crime — you can't hang a man for making marks on paper, so they took my post, my name from the guild rolls, and my right to carry a commissioned seal, and they let me walk. They thought poverty would finish the job. They were almost right."

"Why do it?" Isandro asked. "Why spend eleven years chasing a ghost country? What is Vessia to you?"

The question landed harder than she could have known. Sef looked at the fire for a while.

"My grandmother sang songs in a language she told me was 'kitchen talk,' nothing official, just old family nonsense. Lullabies. Blessings over bread. When I was grown and reading the archives, I found three of her songs transcribed in a pre-Unmaking collection, attributed to Vessian hearth-customs. She wasn't eccentric. She was a survivor passing contraband to a child who didn't know it was contraband." He rolled the old chart closed. "Half the valleys are full of grandmothers like her. The Unmakers killed a country and left its ghosts in kitchens, and everyone agreed not to notice. I noticed. That's all I am, in the end — a man who noticed, professionally."

Isandro was quiet again. Then: "If the Door opens onto Vessia — onto whatever survived the erasure — then Aldric's hunters aren't just purging the gift. They're clearing the ground. Everyone with the gift gathered up, registered, branded, or burned, before—"

"Before anyone reaches the Door. Yes." He watched her arrive at it, step by step, and hated being her guide to this particular summit. "Aldric's Edict of Cleansing isn't fear. Fear doesn't build devices that hum near hidden gifts and send grey hoods into every valley at once. That's logistics. Someone knows the Door is surfacing soon — soon enough to need the bearer-population culled first. Either they want the Door for themselves, or they're terrified of what walks through it, and either way, we are standing between."

"We?" She raised an eyebrow. It was the first expression she'd worn all night that wasn't fear or calculation, and it suited her. "There's a *we* now? I don't recall agreeing to a we."

"You drank my tea without asking what was in it."

"You handed it to me already tasted. I watched you drink first."

"Exactly. Which means you trusted me for exactly as long as it took to verify, and not one breath longer." He grinned despite himself. "That's the soundest basis for an alliance I've ever heard of. Most partnerships fail because people trust too fast. Ours will fail slower than anyone's."

She almost smiled. Then the smile faded, and she looked east, past the fire, past the dark hills, toward the blank space on his map where a country waited inside its own absence. "The Door of Anew," she said. "It leads back. Into Vessia. Into whatever's left of it."

"It leads *through* the erasure. What's on the other side, no chart can tell you. That's rather the point of charts failing there."

"And Aldric wants it. And his hunters are behind us, and ahead of us, and probably beside us by morning." She flexed her bandaged hands. "So the question is whether I spend the rest of my life running from a lie, or spend it walking into one."

"Not a lie," Sef said quietly. "The opposite of one. Everything else in your life was the lie, Isandro. The valley, the safety, the nothing-special taster with the strong stomach. This—" he laid his palm flat on the blank map, on the stubborn inked outline of the unmade country, "—this is the true thing. It's just been waiting two hundred years for someone to draw it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the slope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

And then Isandro went rigid, her head turning sharply toward the darkness north of their camp, and Sef felt it a heartbeat later — a faint, wrong vibration in the ground, in his teeth, in the water in the kettle. A humming. Like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Grey hoods did not travel at night. But their devices, apparently, did not need to sleep.

"Saddle nothing," Sef breathed, dousing the fire with one kick of dirt. "We walk. Now."
