## 9. The Boy Who Hears Doors

Perrin had been living inside the walls of the Hollow Way station for eleven days, and he had learned its whole song.

That was how he thought of it — not the creaks and groans that ordinary ears heard, but the deeper music underneath, the one only he could hear. Every locked thing hummed. Doors hummed when they were shut and dreaming of being open. Windows hummed when they were latched against the wind. The old strongbox in the station's cellar had a low, stubborn drone, like a wasp asleep in amber, and Perrin had given up trying to crack it because it sang so *satisfied* about being closed that he figured whatever was inside wanted to stay there.

The waystation itself had the saddest song he'd ever heard. It had been empty for years — since before the war with nobody's name, his mam would have said, except she'd never say anything again, and Perrin didn't let himself think about that, not directly, the way you don't look straight at the sun. The station sat on the old road between nowhere and nowhere else, a squat stone building with a stable attached and a well out front, built back when people still traveled this stretch. Now travelers went around, or over the mountains, or they didn't travel at all, because the roads belonged to grey hoods now.

Perrin knew all about grey hoods. He'd seen what they left of the tinker's caravan on the Fen Road. He'd been small enough to hide in the tinker's wagon, under the canvas, among the pots, and he'd heard everything through the wagon's thin iron bands — the questions, the screaming, the smell. The wagon had hummed the whole time, high and frightened, every latch and buckle singing its little terror-song, and Perrin had pressed his hands over his ears and sung louder to himself until it stopped mattering.

After that he traveled alone. Alone was safer. Alone meant nobody to give away, nobody whose breathing might be heard from under a floorboard, nobody to—

He caught himself before the thought finished forming. That was the trick of it. You didn't finish thoughts like that. You let them die halfway, like birds hitting glass.

Eleven days in the waystation. He knew its song by heart now. The front door had a tired, resigned hum, the hum of a door that hadn't been asked to do anything in years. The cellar hatch had a wet, mossy note. The loft ladder — technically not a lock at all, but Perrin swore it hummed anyway, faintly, like it remembered being a drawbridge once — creaked in E-flat when he climbed it at night to sleep where the rats couldn't reach him easily.

And then, on the twelfth day, the road outside started humming too.

Perrin woke before dawn with his heart going like a rabbit's, because the *road* was humming. Roads didn't hum. Roads were dead things, flat things, things that only ever carried other people's songs across them. But this was a real hum, rising up through the stones of the waystation floor, through the straw he'd bedded down in, into the bones of his feet even though he wasn't standing. A walking hum. Two sets of footsteps, still far off, but coming.

He did what he always did. He vanished.

There was a gap behind the cellar wall where the masonry had pulled away from the earth, a space barely wider than his shoulders, dark as the inside of a closed mouth. He'd widened it himself over three nights with a broken slate shard. He folded himself into it, knees to chest, and pulled the loose stone back over the gap, and waited in the black with his hands over his ears — not because it helped, but because pressing hard made the hum go distant, made it someone else's problem for a few seconds at a time.

The footsteps came up the road slow. One set heavy, dragging a little on the left side, like whoever walked it was tired clear down to their name. The other set lighter, careful, the walk of somebody who checked sightlines without thinking about it. They stopped outside. Perrin heard the well bucket — the rope's pulley gave a rusty squeal and a short, startled hum — and then voices, low, just words shaped like sound through two feet of stone.

A man's voice: "Empty. Been empty a while." A pause. "Door's not even locked."

The woman's voice answered, dry as chalk: "Nothing out here is locked anymore. That's rather the point of everything, isn't it?"

Perrin frowned in the dark. He knew that voice. Not the voice itself — he'd never heard it — but the *shape* of it. Sharp. Educated. The kind of voice that came from cities with names, from houses with more than one room. It reminded him of the tax assessor who'd come to the fen villages once, the one who spoke to everyone like they were sums he was doing in his head.

Then the third thing happened, and Perrin forgot all about voices.

The woman walked past the front of the station, close enough that her boots scuffed the threshold stone, and the moment she did, the whole building *changed key*.

Perrin had no word for what it was like. It was as if the waystation had been humming its sad, dusty tune for years and suddenly, in the space of one footstep, every note in it turned toward her — the door, the windows, the cellar hatch, the dead hearth flue, even the well rope — the way sunflowers turn toward light, except these were locks turning toward a person, and the sound they made wasn't fear. It was *recognition*. A long, low chord, deep in the ground, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep and murmuring a name.

And underneath it, far below — below the cellar, below the rock, below anything Perrin had ever heard — something answered.

It was vast. That was the only part his mind could hold onto. Vast the way the sky is vast, the way drowning is vast. A single tone, so deep it wasn't sound so much as pressure, rising from somewhere under the mountains to the east, and it passed through the waystation and through the soil and through Perrin's folded body and rang him like a bell, and for one horrible, wonderful second he could hear *everything* — every lock within miles waking at once, doors rattling softly in their frames in farmhouses he couldn't see, the strongbox in the cellar buzzing awake, the whole world's closed things opening their eyes —

Then it faded. The woman's footsteps moved on around the corner of the building. The chord sank back into the earth. Perrin sat in his hole shaking, and realized his face was wet, and couldn't have said if it was terror or the opposite of terror.

*What was that?*

He'd heard big things before. The dam at Marrowfen had a heavy hum. The prison wagons that sometimes rolled the north road had a grim, clanking chorus. But nothing — nothing — had ever sounded like that. That hadn't been a lock. Locks were made by men. That thing under the mountains was older than making. It had hummed like a door the size of a country, a door that had been shut so long it had forgotten there was an outside.

And it had noticed the woman.

Perrin stayed in his hole a long time after that, doing the arithmetic of survival. Two travelers, one tired, one sharp-voiced. Not soldiers — no jingle of kit, no boot-cadence. Travelers on the old road, which meant either fools or fugitives, and the woman's careful walk said fugitive. Fugitives were dangerous. Fugitives brought grey hoods behind them like gulls behind a fishing boat.

But the hum. The hum had come from *her*, or through her, and Perrin had spent twelve years listening to the world's locked things and had never once heard them sing at a person.

Curiosity was going to get him killed someday. His mam had told him so, back when telling him things was still a thing she did. He climbed out of the hole anyway.

* * *

He watched them from the loft for most of the morning, through the gap where a shutter had rotted away.

The man was forty-something, lean, with ink-stained fingers and the kind of clothes that had been good once and were now mostly patches and argument. He'd spread papers on the station's one table — actual maps, Perrin saw, hand-drawn, covered in writing — and was arguing with them as much as with the woman. The woman sat near the fire she'd built small and smokeless, hood back, and Perrin got his first proper look at her and felt something in his chest go strange.

She was younger than her voice. Twenty maybe, twenty-two, dark-haired, plain-dressed, with the stillness of somebody used to being watched. She held a tin cup of well water and didn't drink it. She kept looking at the cup like it might tell her something, and once she tipped a drop onto her finger and touched it to her tongue, quick and practiced, the way Perrin had seen the tinker test solder heat.

"Still arguing with the map," the woman said.

"The map isn't arguing," the man said. "The map is *wrong*, which is worse. There should be a milestone two miles back. There's a socket for it. Someone took the stone and left the hole, which means it wasn't weather, it was *decree*." He tapped the paper. "They're erasing it as they go, Isandro. The road itself. Which means we're not ahead of the erasure anymore. We're walking along its edge."

Isandro. Perrin filed the name away.

"You said the valley was the worst of it," she said. "You said past the mill-race it would be ordinary country."

"I said it would be *less* extraordinary. I've been wrong before. I was wrong about the shape of the northern coast for six years before anyone bothered to check." The man rubbed his eyes. "Sef Ardenne, disgraced cartographer, wrong about everything except the things that matter. Remember that, if we live."

Perrin almost smiled. Then Isandro turned her head — fast, sudden, a movement with no wasted motion in it — and looked directly at the loft gap, directly at the place where his eye was, and said:

"You've been breathing up there since dawn. Come down or leave. But decide."

Perrin froze. Every instinct he owned screamed at him to fold back into the wall-gap and become a rumor. But she was still looking at the gap, patient, unafraid, and the man had gone very still with his hand drifting toward something under his coat, and Perrin understood with sudden clarity that if he ran now, he'd spend the rest of his life wondering what the world's locks had been singing about.

He came down the ladder. Slowly. Hands visible, the way you did with dogs and armed people.

Up close she was smaller than she'd seemed, and her eyes were the color of strong tea, and they went over him the way the tinker's hands had gone over a broken hinge — cataloguing, assessing, deciding whether he was worth fixing. He knew what she saw: a skinny boy, twelve years old or thereabouts, hair cut raggedly with a knife, dressed in layers of stolen coats, dirt worked so deep into his knuckles it looked like shadow. Feral. He'd heard adults use the word about him once, through a door, and had decided to wear it like armor.

"Where are your people?" Sef asked.

"Gone," Perrin said. It was the whole answer. It was always the whole answer.

Isandro glanced at Sef, and something passed between them — Perrin couldn't hear conversations' meanings the way he heard locks, but he could read faces fine, and what crossed hers was recognition. She knew what *gone* meant. She had one of those faces herself, newly made.

"There's bread," she said. "In the pack. If you want it."

Bread. Real bread, not turnip-tops and stolen eggs. Perrin's stomach made the decision his pride wanted to argue about. He sat down across the fire from them, exactly as far as politeness allowed and not one inch closer, and ate, and watched them watch him.

"What's your name?" Isandro asked.

"Perrin."

"Just Perrin?"

"Just Perrin." Names got people found. He'd buried his family name with his family, somewhere back on the Fen Road, under the smoke.

Sef had gone back to his maps, but Perrin noticed he wasn't really reading them; he was listening the way Perrin listened, sideways, with the whole surface of himself. "How did you survive out here?" Sef asked. "Alone, on the roads, with the edict out. Boys your age end up in cages for less than whatever it is you're hiding."

"I'm not hiding anything."

"Everyone's hiding something," Isandro said quietly. "I hid something from myself for twenty years."

Perrin chewed. Considered. These two were fugitives; fugitives kept secrets the way other people kept coins, close and counted. But they'd fed him. And there was the other thing, the thing he couldn't stop hearing — the way the station's locks still, faintly, hours later, leaned toward Isandro like flowers toward a window.

So he did something he hadn't done since the tinker's wagon. He told the truth.

"I hear them," he said. "Locked things. Doors, boxes, gates, chains. When they're shut, they hum. Different ones hum different. The strongbox downstairs hums like a fat wasp. The well rope hums scared. Your pack buckle hums proud — you fixed it yourself and you're pleased about it." He nodded at Sef's satchel. "Yours hums sad. Like it used to belong to something bigger."

Sef had gone white around the mouth. "That's my old survey case. Royal issue. Before—" He stopped. Started again. "You can hear that? From a buckle?"

"And last night," Perrin said, and his voice came out smaller than he wanted, "last night I heard the biggest thing I ever heard. Under the mountains. East of here. It hummed like a door, but not any door anybody built. It hummed like a door the size of—"

"—of a country," Sef whispered.

The fire popped. Isandro set down her cup very carefully, the way people do when their hands aren't steady and they don't want anyone to know.

"It answered you," Perrin said, looking at her. "When you walked past. All the locks in this building turned toward you. I never heard that before. Things don't notice people. But they noticed you."

Isandro looked into the fire for a long time. When she spoke, it was half to him and half to Sef. "In the valley, a candle flame bent toward me. Magic isn't supposed to follow into Hollowmere. Nothing is supposed to answer me. And yet." Her tea-colored eyes came up and fixed on Perrin, and he had the sudden, skin-prickling sense of being *seen* — fully, the way no one had looked at him since his mam. "You're saying things respond to me. Even here. Even hollowed."

"I'm saying I heard it," Perrin said. "I don't say things I didn't hear."

Sef was already pulling a map across the table, fingers moving fast, tracing the old road east with its scraped-out milestones and its holes where names used to be. "Under the mountains. Northeast, then — the range runs that way. Perrin, boy, think. Can you point? Can you show me where it was deepest?"

Perrin pointed east-southeast, slightly south of where Sef's finger was. "More that way. And lower than you think. It wasn't in the mountains. It was *under* them. Deep. Old. Patient." He shivered. "It hummed like it had been waiting so long it forgot waiting was a thing. Like a door that doesn't remember outside."

Sef and Isandro exchanged a look that lasted three full seconds and contained an entire conversation, and Perrin, who had spent twelve years learning to read the language of faces because words could lie but expressions rarely bothered, read it clearly: hope, terrifying and unwanted, arriving in two people who had trained themselves not to expect it.

"The Door of Anew," Sef breathed. "If it's surfacing — if it moves along the coronation road and it's surfaced this far west — then we're not weeks behind it. We might be days. We might be *walking beside it*."

"Don't say it loud," Isandro said. "Hope loud gets people hunted."

But she was leaning forward now, toward the map, toward the east, and Perrin watched her and understood something that settled into him like a swallowed stone: she wasn't running anymore. She was going *toward*. And everything that went toward that door — grey hoods, kings, boys who heard locks — was going to arrive at the same place eventually, and the place was going to be loud.

* * *

They left the waystation at midday, and Perrin left with them, and nobody actually invited him.

That was the part he'd planned, insofar as he planned anything. He simply fell into step behind them on the old road, ten paces back, hands in his pockets, wearing the expression of a boy who had always been there and always would be. Sef glanced back twice. Isandro glanced back once, longer.

At the third mile marker — the hole where the stone used to be — Sef finally stopped and turned around. "You understand we're being hunted. That following us is the single most dangerous thing you could do with your short life."

"Yes," Perrin said.

"You have no claim on us. We have no obligation to you."

"Yes."

"If the grey hoods come, we may have to run, and we won't slow down for you."

"Yes." Perrin shifted his weight. Somewhere behind them, far back along the road, he thought he heard — faint, so faint it might have been wind — the first thread of a hum he'd learned to dread: metal on the move, buckles and cage-latches and the long cold chain-hum of men who locked things for a living. He didn't mention it yet. He would. But not yet. "You'll slow down anyway," he added. "Both of you. You already feed strays. I've watched you for a day. You gave half your bread to each other when the other wasn't looking. People like that collect strays. It's just what happens."

Isandro made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "He's been profiling us."

"He's not wrong," Sef muttered, "which is irritating."

They stood in the middle of the erased road, the three of them, wind coming down off the mountains carrying the smell of snow and old stone. Perrin waited. Waiting was most of surviving.

Finally Isandro sighed — the sigh of someone adding a weight to a pack already too heavy and finding, annoyingly, that it balanced the load — and jerked her chin at the road ahead. "Walk where I can see you. If I say run, you run. If I say hide, you hide and you don't come out until I call you by name."

"You don't know my whole name."

"Then I suppose," she said, and there was something in her face then, tired and crooked and almost warm, "you'll have to stay close enough to hear it."

Perrin grinned, quick and crooked to match, and fell into step beside her — beside, not behind; he'd negotiated himself upward and they both knew it.

They walked east, toward the mountains and the enormous patient hum beneath them. And a mile on, when the wind shifted, Perrin finally let himself say it, quiet, just to Isandro, watching her face change:

"They're behind us. Grey hoods. Three, maybe four. Their cage-wagon's latches are humming hungry."

Isandro didn't break stride. But her hand found the strap of her pack and tightened, and she said, "How far?"

"A day," Perrin said. "Maybe less. And they're not following your trail." He swallowed. "They're following mine. Somebody's been asking about a boy who hears doors."
