## 10. Grey Hoods on the Road

The river came on them the way bad news always did: quietly, and then all at once.

Isandro heard it before she saw it — a low, constant muttering under the birdsong, the sound of water working at stones. After six days of walking through country that felt stitched together out of other countries' leftovers, that sound was the first thing that had seemed certain about anything.

"Ford's ahead," Sef said. He had his map case slung across his chest like a bandolier, and he kept one hand resting on it the way other men touched a sword. "The Vell. It's shallow here, if my charts are honest."

"Are your charts honest?"

"My charts are the only thing about me that is." He didn't smile when he said it. Sef rarely smiled, but Isandro had learned to read him anyway — the set of his shoulders, the way his eyes moved along the treeline without ever seeming to look at it directly. Right now his eyes were doing a great deal of not-looking. "Something's wrong with the birds."

Perrin stopped dead in the middle of the path. The boy had been chattering all morning — he talked to doors, to fences, to the hinges of abandoned gates they passed, greeting each one by name or by sound — but now he went silent as a held breath. His head tilted, the way it did when he was listening to something no one else could hear.

"The bridge," he said. "There's a bridge back there, half a mile east. It's locked."

"Bridges aren't locked," Wren said.

"This one is. Someone put a lock on it that isn't a lock. It's humming." Perrin's fingers found the hem of Isandro's sleeve and gripped it, an unconscious habit he'd developed somewhere around day three that she had decided not to examine too closely. "It hums like the well-house in Hollowmere. Like something that finds things."

The word *finds* dropped into the group like a stone into still water.

"Hunters," Sef said flatly.

"They'd be ahead of us, not behind," Wren said. Her voice was calm, but her hands were already moving, checking the ties of her herb satchel with quick, practiced tugs. "They'd want the ford. Water breaks a trail; any tracker worth her salt knows you push your quarry toward a crossing, not away from one."

"Then we don't cross," Isandro said.

"We have to cross." Sef unrolled a corner of a chart against his forearm, pinning it with two fingers against the wind. "East of here the Vell cuts through a gorge for twenty miles. There's no other crossing inside a week's walk, and we're already eating the margin I built into this route. We cross at the ford, or we stop being a party traveling somewhere and become four people hiding in woods until winter."

Nobody argued. That was the trouble with Sef's maps — they were right, and being right left very little room for anyone else's preferences.

They came down toward the ford single file, off the path, through alder scrub that scratched at their arms. Isandro went last, which was where she'd begun placing herself without discussion; she was the largest of them, and the one most likely to hear a snapped twig over the river's noise. The water ran wide and brown-green, maybe knee-deep at its center, broken into white riddles around a spine of stones. On the far bank the land rose into open meadow, long grass gone gold, and beyond that a dark line of forest.

It looked peaceful. It looked exactly like the kind of place where nothing would ever happen.

"Perrin," Sef murmured. "What do you hear? Here. Now."

The boy closed his eyes. Isandro watched his face do something complicated — fear and concentration wrestling for the same ground. "Water," he said. "Water doesn't hum. Water's the only thing that goes quiet. But—" His eyes snapped open. "Under the far bank. Under the grass. Two somethings. They're not moving. They're waiting the way a trap waits."

* * *

They might have run. Later, Isandro would turn that moment over and over — the ten seconds in which running had still been possible, back into the alder scrub, back toward the gorge and its impossible twenty miles. She would conclude, every time, that it wouldn't have mattered. Grey hoods did not come alone, and grey hoods did not stake out a ford with only two of their number unless there were more nearby, and running men are easier to find than standing ones.

So instead they stood at the water's edge, four travelers with nothing to declare, and waited to see what the morning wanted from them.

It wanted a parley.

The man rose out of the far grass first — a big shape resolving into a hunter, grey hood thrown back, hands visible and empty, palms out in the universal grammar of *I mean you no harm*. Behind him, a second figure stood up more slowly, and Isandro's stomach tightened, because the second figure carried the device.

She'd seen it once before, from a window in Hollowmere, the day they took Tam. It looked like nothing — a lantern frame of black iron holding what appeared to be a lump of cloudy glass, except that the glass wasn't glass, because it moved. It turned, slowly, of its own accord, like a compass needle made of fog. And even at forty paces, even across running water, Isandro could feel it. Not hear it, exactly. Feel it — a faint pressure behind her breastbone, the way the air feels before a storm, or the way a room feels just before someone says the thing that can't be taken back.

"That's far enough," the big hunter called. His voice was pleasant. That was somehow worse than if it hadn't been. "We're not taking anyone today. Captain's orders. We'd like to talk."

"Talk," Sef repeated, without inflection.

"Parley. There's wine." The hunter lifted a skin from his shoulder and let it swing visibly from his fist. "Road custom. You drink, we drink, nobody's a prisoner. Then you go your way and we go ours, and everyone reports honestly to whoever they report to."

"He's lying," Perrin whispered. "The wine's lying. It's— it tastes wrong. I can't taste things right, but the *lock* on it is wrong, it's got a lock on it like—"

"I know," Isandro said quietly.

She knew better than any of them could. She knew the smell of bitterbark and the green undertone of hemlock water and the almond ghost of distilled peach kernels, knew seven ways poisoners masked their work and nine ways they didn't. She had spent eleven years drinking death in small cups so that a regent could eat dinner in peace. Whatever was in that wineskin, she would know it the way you know your own name.

And that was when the thought arrived — the terrible, clean, cold thought — sliding into place with the same ease as a key into a lock she'd never noticed her own hand fit.

*Let them see.*

"Isandro." Wren's voice, low, at her shoulder. The hedge-witch had gone very still. "Whatever you're thinking —"

"We can't outrun them. We can't outfight them. There are at least two more in those trees — don't look, Sef, I saw the birds avoid the same spot you did." Isandro kept her eyes on the hunters. "But they think they know what we are. They think we're frightened people with something to hide. Frightened people refuse the wine."

"And if you drink it?" Wren asked, though her voice said she had already worked out the answer, and hated it.

"Then they have to decide I'm either not what they're hunting," Isandro said, "or something they don't have a box for. Either way, we walk across this river."

"You don't know what's in it."

"No," Isandro agreed. "I know what's *not* in it. Nothing in that skin can touch me. Nothing has touched me since I was six years old." She heard her own voice and barely recognized it — level, almost bored. Eleven years of swallowing death in silence, and it had been training for this after all. All those quiet mornings in the palace kitchen, sipping laced broth while servants looked through her like she was furniture. Every cup had been a lesson. Every lesson had been leading here, to a brown river and a grey hood and a wineskin swinging from a liar's fist.

She stepped forward, into the shallows.

"Isandro—" Sef started.

"Wine's road custom," she called out, wading. The water pushed cold against her shins, then her knees. "I'll take the parley. But I'll pour for myself — I've seen what pours out of skins in this country."

The big hunter laughed, easy and warm. "Sensible. Come on, then. Bring your friends if you like, or leave them on the bank — their choice."

She crossed. The device tracked her the whole way; she could feel its attention like a finger pressed between her shoulder blades, that fog-glass needle turning, turning, tasting the air for her. Let it taste. Let it find whatever it found. She had spent her whole life being nothing, and nothing, it turned out, was excellent camouflage.

On the far bank, the grass came up past her waist. The big hunter met her at the edge of the meadow, and up close he was younger than his voice — thirty at most, sunburned, with a soldier's neatness to him. The second hunter hung back with the device, hood shadowing her face, and Isandro noted the way she stood: weight balanced, free hand near a knife. These were not bandits playing at soldiers. These were professionals.

"Name?" the young hunter asked.

"Does the parley need one?"

"Custom says it does."

"Then call me Marren. I'm a cook." The lie came out smooth as poured cream, and she hated how easily it came — the same way she'd hated it in Dame Ovelin's sitting room, the first time. Lies used to be hard. Now they were just another flavor she could hold on her tongue without flinching.

"A cook." He smiled. "Long way from any kitchen."

"Good kitchens travel badly. Bad ones travel fine."

He laughed again, genuinely this time, and held out the skin. "Wine, Marren-the-cook. Aldric's own vintage, if you believe the label, which I wouldn't."

She took it. It was heavier than it should have been, and the smell rising from the spout confirmed everything Perrin's strange senses had caught and her own tongue now finished: bitterbark, yes, and under it something subtler — a mineral sharpness, silver-tasting, that she didn't recognize. New. They were developing new compounds for the hunt. Some part of her registered this with a professional's detached interest, the way she'd once noted a new spice at the palace docks, and that part of her frightened her more than the poison did.

The three figures on the far bank had come down to the water's edge. Sef stood with his arms folded. Wren had both hands visible and empty, which Isandro knew meant she was ready to use them. Perrin simply stared across the river with the fixed, unblinking attention of a child watching a fire.

"Your friends don't drink?" the hunter asked.

"They trust me."

"Do you trust you?"

"With wine?" Isandro met his eyes. "Completely."

She drank.

She drank deep, and slow, and deliberately — a long pull, throat working, the way a thirsty traveler drinks, letting them see every swallow. The poison bloomed across her tongue like a struck match: bitterbark first, hot and vegetal, then the silver-mineral note spreading down her throat, cold, searching, looking for something in her blood to grip. She felt it try. She felt it slide off her, the way rain slides off oiled cloth, the way every poison of her life had slid off her, finding nothing to hold, no door to enter. Her sealed vessel took it in and gave back nothing.

Eleven years of practice, spent in one swallow.

She lowered the skin and exhaled, slow and easy, and wiped her mouth with the back of her hand like a drover at a fair.

"Well?" the hunter said. Something in his voice had changed. She couldn't have said what — a half-step of hesitation, a door opened a crack.

"It's a rough vintage," Isandro said. "Too much bark in the finish. Whoever blended it rushed." She held the skin back out to him. "Your turn."

And that was the moment. She watched it happen in his face — watched the pleasantness crack like ice on a pond. Because he couldn't drink it. Of course he couldn't drink it. The wine was not for him; the wine had never been for anyone; the wine was a question, and questions are only safe for the person asking them. He looked at the skin, and then at her, and then at the device, and the woman with the device had gone rigid, the fog-glass needle straining toward Isandro like a dog against a leash, and finding — nothing. Or worse than nothing. Finding something that swallowed everything and reported nothing.

"What are you," the woman said. First words. Flat, quiet, dangerous.

"A cook," Isandro said. "I told you."

"Nobody drinks that."

"Someone does." She let a beat pass. Let them look at her — ordinary, road-worn, a woman with dust on her boots and no weapon showing. "Maybe your blend's weak. Maybe your bark's old. Or maybe—" and here she gave them the gift, the terrible gift, the only coin she had, spending it freely because she understood suddenly that it was the only way across this river — "maybe some people are born wrong. Born hollow. Poison needs something in the blood to bite, and I've got nothing. My mother said I was a hollow child. Milk wouldn't stay down unless it was boiled twice. Doctors gave up on me at six." She shrugged, a peasant shrug, a nothing shrug. "Hollow people make good cooks. We can taste anything without dying of it."

The silence stretched. The river muttered. Somewhere behind her, she heard Sef shift his weight — reading the play, understanding it, hating it.

The young hunter's face had gone through disbelief and come out the other side into something like awe, and awe curdled fast into fear, and fear in a soldier looks a great deal like anger. "That's not possible."

"Clearly it is."

"The captain will want to hear about this."

"The captain," Isandro said pleasantly, "can hear about a cook who didn't die. Write it down. I'm sure it makes a fine story." She turned, unhurried, and raised her voice across the water. "Parley's done! Wine's foul! Coming back!"

And she walked. That was the hardest part — walking, not running, wading back into the brown water with her heart slamming against her ribs so hard she was sure they could see it move her shirt. The device's needle followed her, straining, hungry, blind. The two hunters stood in the grass and did not follow, because the rules of parley bound them — custom was the one law even Aldric's grey hoods kept, the thin thread of order that let men talk before they killed — and because, she suspected, neither of them entirely wanted to get closer to whatever she was.

Halfway across, the woman's voice came over the water, quiet enough that only Isandro truly heard it.

"The hollow child." A pause. "There's no such thing. Whatever you are — we'll find out what you are."

Isandro didn't turn around. "Get in line," she said, and kept wading.

* * *

They walked fast for an hour, off any path, through the golden meadows and into the dark line of forest, and only when the river was well behind them did Wren finally stop, plant her fists on her hips, and round on Isandro with the expression of a woman assembling a lecture she'd been saving.

"You could have died."

"No," Isandro said. "That's rather the point of me."

"You *drank* it. You drank a hunter's poison straight down and then you *criticized the vintage*—"

"It was a fair criticism. The bitterbark was stale."

"Don't you joke at me, girl." But Wren's voice cracked in the middle of it, and Isandro saw then that the fury was wearing grief's clothing, the way it always did with her. The hedge-witch pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment. "I've buried people who were braver than that and luckier than you. Do you understand what you spent back there? They've seen you now. That woman will write it in her report — a bearer who drinks poison and calls it vintage. Every grey hood between here and the coast will know your face by winter."

"I know."

"You *know*—"

"I know," Isandro said, quieter. "And I'd spend it again tomorrow. Wren. Look at me." She waited until the witch's hands came down. "They had us. Two at the ford, more in the trees, a device that hums, and a river at our backs. Refusing the wine bought us a net. Drinking it bought us a road. I chose the road."

"She's right," Sef said. He'd lowered himself onto a fallen log, map case across his knees, and was staring at nothing with the particular intensity of a man re-drawing the last hour in his head. "It was the only play. I've sat across tables from Aldric's people. They respect two things: orders and impossibilities. You handed them an impossibility wrapped in an order — parley observed, no crime committed, nothing to arrest. They had to let you walk." He looked up, and there was something new in his face when he looked at her. Not warmth, exactly. Recalculation. "Though I'll say this plainly, since someone should: whatever you are, Isandro Dunmore, that woman is going to spend the rest of her career finding out. You've made yourself the most interesting riddle in the kingdom."

"The kingdom doesn't exist," Perrin said.

Everyone turned. The boy was crouched at the base of a tree, drawing in the dirt with a stick, and he spoke without looking up, in the matter-of-fact tone he reserved for things he considered obvious.

"The kingdom doesn't exist, Sef says so. So she can't spend her career on you in a kingdom that isn't there." He glanced up, and his eyes — those strange, listening eyes — settled on Isandro with unsettling directness. "Also, you lied. About being hollow. You're not hollow at all. You're the opposite of hollow. You're so full it hurts to stand next to you sometimes. Like a jar with the lid glued on." He went back to his drawing. "You shouldn't lie to hunters. Lying makes locks. Locks can be picked."

The forest was very quiet.

"A jar with the lid glued on," Wren repeated slowly. She exchanged a look with Sef — one of those long, wordless exchanges the two of them had begun having, maps and medicine meeting in the middle. "Isandro. What Perrin hears. When he says you're full—"

"I don't know what I am," Isandro said. And it was true, and it was becoming truer every day, and the truth of it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Magic could not follow into Hollowmere; the valley had been built to make sure of it. She had lived her whole life as proof of that law. And yet the candle flame had bent toward her finger. And yet poison slid off her as if her blood were oiled glass. And yet a child who heard the world's hidden machinery had just told her, in the flat voice he used for facts, that she was not empty but *sealed* — a vessel, corked, pressurized, holding something down that very much wanted to come up.

"Not today," she said aloud, to all of them, and mostly to herself. "Today we put miles behind that river."

Sef unfolded a chart across his knees. "Northwest, then. Three days to the fen country — Marrowfen, if the names on my charts still mean anything. There's a woman there, if she's alive, who owes me a debt and hates me for it. A healer." He glanced at Wren. "No offense intended."

"Some taken anyway," Wren said, and the ghost of a smile passed between them, the first in days.

They moved. Isandro fell into the rear position again, and the forest closed around them, and for a long time the only sounds were footsteps and birdsong cautiously returning and Perrin occasionally informing a gatepost that they were sorry to have disturbed it.

But she kept tasting it. That was the thing nobody understood — you could wash your mouth, and the taste stayed. Bitterbark and silver, blooming and dying and blooming again on her tongue like a flower on a loop. Eleven years she had tasted death professionally, dispassionately, the way a banker counts coins. This was different. This time, death had been aimed at *her*, in *her* mouth, and she had invited it, and it had failed, and its failure had terrified two armed men more than any sword could have.

Her curse, weaponized. Her secret, spent.

And underneath the taste, underneath the fear, something else was stirring — something that had no business feeling like triumph, but did. For eleven years she had drunk poison in silence so that others could live safely. Today she had drunk it loudly, and four people had walked away alive because of it, and one of them was a boy who drew pictures in dirt and heard doors.

Maybe the seal on the jar was failing. Maybe she was becoming something the valley had been built to prevent. She could feel the pressure behind her breastbone growing day by day, patient and enormous, like water building behind a dam.

But the dam had held today. And today, that was enough.

Ahead, Perrin stopped abruptly and cocked his head.

"What is it?" Sef asked.

The boy listened to something none of them could hear. When he answered, his voice had gone careful and small.

"There's a village ahead. Off the paths. And it's humming — but not like a lock." He frowned, trying to translate the sound into words, the way he always had to. "Like a kitchen. Like bread."

Isandro looked at Wren. Wren looked at Sef. And somewhere far behind them, back across the brown water of the Vell, Isandro knew that a grey-hooded woman was writing a report by candlelight, describing a cook who could not be poisoned — and that the hunt, from this day forward, would never again be a search for strangers.

It would be a search for *her*.
