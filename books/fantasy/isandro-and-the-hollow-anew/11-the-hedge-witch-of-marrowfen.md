## 11. The Hedge-Witch of Marrowfen

The marsh gave up its dead slowly, and Wren Ostara had learned to work at the same pace.

She was stitching a miller's hand back together when the knocking started — three soft raps against her doorframe, then silence, then three more. The rhythm of someone who wanted to be let in but didn't want to be heard. She tied off the last knot with her teeth, pressed a poultice of bog-myrtle and spider-silk moss over the ruined palm, and said, without looking up, "Keep it dry for six days. If it goes black, come back before you lose the arm."

The miller — a boy, really, seventeen and gray-faced from blood loss — nodded at her the way people nodded at her: too quickly, eyes sliding away to the jars on her shelves, the bones hanging from the rafters, the things that made Marrowfen's only healer equal parts necessity and superstition.

"Pay me when the reeds come in," Wren said. "Not before."

He left coins anyway, stacked them by the door where she couldn't refuse them politely, and went out into the fog. Wren looked at the money for a long moment. Then she swept it into the chipped bowl on her mantel, the one she never counted, because counting would mean knowing whether she could afford the winter, and not knowing had become its own small comfort.

The knocking came again.

"I heard you the first time," she called. "Door's not locked. Nothing in Marrowfen is locked. That's rather the point of us."

It opened a hand's width. Fog curled through the gap like something alive, and behind the fog stood three figures, and Wren set down her needle very carefully, because she had learned to read silhouettes the way other women read weather.

The first was tall and stooped, carrying a leather tube across his back like a coffin for something flat. A cartographer's case — she knew the shape; she'd stitched up two of them in her day, mapmakers who thought the fens were safe shortcuts. The second was a boy, thin as a heron, standing slightly behind the others in the way of children who had learned that doorways were places people got grabbed. The third —

The third made Wren go still.

A woman, mid-twenties maybe, travel-worn past the point of fashion and into the territory of survival. Mud to the knee. Eyes that moved around the room cataloguing exits. And under all of it, under the exhaustion and the wariness, something Wren recognized the way you recognize a scar on your own body: a wrongness in the air around her, a stillness, like a pond that refused to ripple no matter what fell into it.

*Oh,* Wren thought. *Oh, you poor creature. You don't even know what you are.*

"You're the hedge-witch," the tall man said. It wasn't quite a question. "Sef Ardenne." He didn't offer his hand. People who'd been exiled rarely did; they'd learned hands were things that got held down. "We were told —"

"Told by whom?"

"The ferryman at Cindermoss. He said if we were going toward the free cities, there was a woman in Marrowfen who could get us there alive."

Wren snorted. "Cindermoss will say anything for a copper. Last month he told a tax collector I was a widow who drowned her husband, which is slander, because I never married him." She stood, wiping her hands on her apron out of habit, though they were already clean. "Come in or don't. The fog bites after dark."

They came in. Of course they came in. They had nowhere else, and the boy looked at her hearth the way starving men look at bread.

* * *

She fed them first. This was policy, not kindness — people told the truth faster with warm food in them, and lies sat heavier in a full stomach. Barley soup with fen-onion and a scrap of salt pork she'd been saving. The boy ate like the soup might be taken away, hunched over his bowl, one arm curled around it. The woman ate slowly, methodically, tasting each spoonful before swallowing.

Wren noticed that. Filed it away. *Tastes everything. Even my soup. Even mine.*

"So," she said, ladling herself a bowl and settling onto her stool across the fire. "Cartographer, child, and — what are you?"

"Isandro," the woman said. "I'm — we're traveling to Callowmark."

"That's not an answer to what I asked."

"It's the answer I have."

Wren smiled, showing more teeth than the smile strictly required. "I like her," she said to Sef. "You can stay."

Sef didn't smile back. He set his bowl down with the care of a man handling something explosive. "The ferryman said you guide people. To the free cities. Through the fens."

"I do."

"For money?"

"Sometimes. Sometimes for barter. Once for a promise I'm still waiting on." She sipped her soup. "And sometimes not at all, when the person asking is running from grey hoods, in which case I charge differently."

The temperature of the room changed. The boy stopped chewing. Isandro's hand drifted — casually, badly — toward the knife at her belt, and Sef put two fingers on her wrist, a small motion, *not yet, not here.*

"We're not," Sef began.

"Don't." Wren set her bowl down. "Don't insult both of us. You've been walking the drainage paths for two days — I know because the eels have been spooked since yesterday morning, and eels only spook for strangers off the high road. Nobody takes the drainage paths unless the high road has hunters on it. And nobody travels with a child who flinches at doors and a woman who tastes her food like every bite is a deposition unless they've got a reason the law would find interesting." She leaned forward, firelight catching the lines of her face. "So. Are we doing this honestly, or am I going to spend the evening pretending to believe whatever story you rehearsed in the reeds?"

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a bittern boomed its low, hollow call, the sound that had given the fen its name — *marrow*, the old folk said, because the sound came from inside the earth, from the wet bones of the world.

Then the boy spoke.

"She fixed Tam's leg once."

Everyone turned. The boy had gone pink, as if he'd surprised himself.

"Perrin—" Sef started.

"He means nothing by it," Isandro said quickly.

"He means everything by it," Wren said softly. "Tam. Tam of Hollowmere? Stable-boy, crooked leg from a kick when he was small?" She watched Isandro's face do something complicated and painful. "Yes. I thought so. I patched him four years ago. His mistress paid me in cheese and didn't tip." She sighed, and some of the sharpness went out of her, the way a blade goes back in its sheath. "If you know Tam, then you came from Hollowmere, and if you came from Hollowmere after this season, then you're either smugglers, spies, or the beginning of a very long tragedy. I've housed all three. Only one of them ever thanked me."

"And which were they?" Sef asked quietly.

"The tragedies." She stood, took their bowls, and stacked them. "Sleep tonight. Decide tomorrow. I'll want payment either way — but if you're running from Aldric's purge, we'll discuss what kind."

* * *

Wren did not sleep. She rarely did, the night after new arrivals; instead she sat by her window with a cup of nettle tea going cold and watched the fog move through the reeds like slow white cattle.

Marrowfen had been hers for eleven years. Before that, other places, other names — she'd worn names the way other women wore shawls, putting them on against the cold and taking them off when the season changed. There had been a name before all the names, a girlhood name, spoken in a house with a blue door in a town that now appeared on no map worth trusting. She kept that name in the same place she kept everything else that mattered: buried, waterlogged, preserved by the cold and the dark.

Her sister's name lived there too.

She got the jar down from the top shelf, the one behind the dried angelica, and held it in her lap without opening it. She never opened it. Opening it would mean admitting it was ash and not sleep, ash and not a journey interrupted, ash and not a sister merely waiting somewhere for a better season. The jar was clay, unglazed, sealed with wax she'd renewed every year on the same date. Inside was what remained of Liss Ostara, twenty-two years old, who had hidden a gifted child from the king's men and been given to the pyre for it while Wren stood in the crowd and did nothing, because doing something meant dying too, and Wren had chosen arithmetic over love and had been paying the difference ever since.

That was nine years ago. Long enough that the grief had stopped being a wound and become a trade. That was the trick nobody warned you about: grief doesn't fade, it professionalizes. She'd built a whole practice on the ruins of it. Every refugee she stitched together, every gift she smothered under poultices and misdirection charms so the hunters' humming devices would pass over, every hopeless case she talked into accepting a smaller life instead of a shorter one — all of it was Liss, all of it was the pyre, all of it was the girl with the blue door saying *you should have run with us, Wren, why didn't you run with us.*

*I'm running now,* she told the jar, as she told it most nights. *Slowly. In circles. But running.*

Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear the sounds of her guests settling. The cartographer murmuring to himself over his maps — she could tell by the scratch of charcoal. The boy breathing in the quick shallow rhythm of a child sleeping near strangers, ready to bolt. And the woman, Isandro, silent. Perfectly, unnaturally silent. Wren had listened to hundreds of refugees sleep in this house, and they all made noise — moaning, muttering, crying out for people they'd lost. This one made nothing. Lay down like poured water and went still.

*What are you?* Wren wondered, and then, because she was a healer and healers were professionally curious in ways that got them killed: *No. What are you hiding?*

She put the jar back behind the angelica and went down to check her stores. If she was guiding them to Callowmark — and she already knew she was; she'd known the moment she saw the woman taste her own soup — she'd need six days of food, a punt, and a route along the drowned causeway that the grey hoods' devices hated, all that standing water scrambling their hums into nonsense.

Six days. Five nights. Two river crossings. One stretch of open heath where the fog thinned and you could see a hooded rider at half a mile, if you were lucky, and at ten feet if you weren't.

She was inventorying the dried fish when she felt it.

At first she thought it was the bittern again, that deep pulse from under the earth. But it wasn't coming from below. It was coming from her guest room, faint and rhythmic, a pressure against her awareness the way a struck bell presses against the air. Wren closed her eyes and listened the way she'd been taught — not with ears, with the older listening, the one that had gotten Liss killed and Wren spared, the one she used now only for diagnosis, only ever for diagnosis.

There. Under the silence of the sleeping woman: a hum. No — the opposite of a hum. A *hush*, vast and dense, like the quiet at the bottom of a well. Every living thing Wren had ever listened to rang with some small note of the gift, a flicker, a tremor. This woman rang with absence. A vessel so full it had gone inert. A cup filled to the brim and sealed, holding decades of something that had nowhere to go.

Wren opened her eyes. Her hands were shaking.

*The valley,* she thought. *She's from Hollowmere. The valley that swallows magic whole. She grew up inside the cork.* And the cork, if Wren's listening was true, and it was, it always was — the cork was starting to give.

She thought about the edict. Everyone had heard the edict read aloud in market squares across three provinces, read by criers with government voices: *all bearers of the gift shall be surrendered to the crown's mercy.* And beneath the official words, the unofficial ones everyone understood: *the king is dying, the king needs something only the gifted have, and mercy is a word the crown uses the way a butcher uses "harvest."*

She thought about Liss. About the child Liss had hidden. About the arithmetic.

Then she thought about the woman downstairs, tasting soup like every meal was a trial, traveling with a disgraced mapmaker and a feral child toward a city that would brand her if it caught her — and not knowing. Not knowing what slept inside her. Not knowing that the valley hadn't saved her from the purge, it had *made* her the purge's single greatest prize, a vessel that size, that old, that ripe.

"Oh, you poor creature," Wren said aloud, to the empty kitchen. "You're not running from the storm. You *are* the storm."

She banked the fire, checked the latch on the medicine cabinet out of forty years of habit, and went to bed. She did not sleep. But she decided.

* * *

Morning came up gray-gold through the fog, and the fen birds started their arguing, and Wren made porridge with honey because today was a honey day, one of maybe four a year.

They gathered at her table looking like a jury that expected a verdict they wouldn't like. Sef with his hands flat on the wood, a negotiator's posture. Perrin wedged into the corner seat with his back to the wall. Isandro across from her, watchful, that terrible stillness humming under her skin where she couldn't feel it but Wren could, the way you can feel a storm two counties off.

"Here's how it is," Wren said. "I'll take you to Callowmark. Six days by the drowned causeway, five if the weather holds and the weather won't hold. I know every path the hoods' devices can't follow, every island with a roof, every crossing where the water runs fast enough to hide a trail. Without me, you reach the free cities in maybe one chance in five. With me, call it nine in ten. The tenth is bandits, and bandits I can't fix."

"What does it cost?" Sef asked. Direct. She granted him that.

"Money, I'd say forty crowns, and you don't have forty crowns, so forget money." She folded her hands. "I'll guide you for a price. I'm not going to tell you what it is yet."

Three faces did three different things. Sef narrowed his eyes. Perrin went very still. Isandro — Isandro just looked at her, and for a moment the stillness cracked and something human and exhausted showed through, something that had been lied to enough times to recognize the shape of another lie coming.

"That's not how bargains work," Sef said.

"It's exactly how bargains work. You pay after delivery. Ask any ferryman." She stood, began stacking bowls, talking with her back to them because it was easier. "Here's what I'll tell you, free of charge, so you can decide with your eyes open. The price isn't money. It isn't dangerous. It isn't anything you'll have to steal or kill for. It's a thing I need carried, and I need it carried by someone going where you're going, and I'll tell you what it is when I'm good and ready — which will be before you owe it, not after. I'm a healer, not a merchant. I don't believe in selling surprises."

Silence again. Then Perrin, from his corner, in that blunt way children have:

"She's got a jar."

Wren's hands stopped moving.

"On the shelf," the boy said, apologetic, miserable, unable to stop himself. "Behind the plants. It hums sad. Everything hums. Hers hums the saddest thing I ever heard." He looked down at his porridge. "Sorry."

The kitchen was very quiet. Wren kept her back turned until she was sure her face worked properly, and when she turned around, Isandro was watching her with an expression Wren hadn't seen on her before — not pity, worse than pity, *recognition*. One person who carried something sealed recognizing another.

"Well," Wren said briskly, and her voice only broke a little. "Now you know it's heavy. You don't know whose it is. That part keeps." She pulled her oilskin pack from under the bed and began packing it with the efficiency of long practice: fish, hardtack, the good knife, the birch-bark canisters of poultice. "We leave within the hour, ahead of the fog lifting. Causeway first day, Cindermoss by dark tomorrow if we push. And one rule on the water: whatever you hear singing out in the reeds, you don't answer it. The fen sings to lonely people. It's how it feeds."

Isandro rose. Stood there a moment, awkward, a woman who clearly didn't know how to say thank you and suspected the words might cost extra.

"Why?" she asked finally. "Why help us? You don't know us. By your own account, everyone you've helped like this ended in tragedy."

Wren slung the pack over her shoulder and met her eyes.

"Because tragedy's the only crop that grows reliably these days," she said, "and I'd rather be the one holding the sickle." She pushed open the door onto the pale gold fog, onto the drowned causeway glinting faintly beyond the reeds, onto the long road west. "Besides. You taste your food before you eat it. Anyone that careful has something worth protecting."

She stepped out into the fen, and after a moment — she'd known they would, she'd known since the soup — they followed.
