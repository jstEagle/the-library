## 12. What Wren Buried

The fire had burned down to its red heart by the time Wren Ostara finally spoke, and even then she spoke to the flames rather than to the four people gathered around them.

"You want to know my price." She turned a sprig of dried yarrow between her fingers, not looking up. "I've been paid in chickens, in coin, in one memorable case in a piglet that bit me. I've never once been asked what I charge before I name it. That's either very polite or very stupid of you."

"We're not going to press you," Sef said. He sat with his maps rolled at his feet like sleeping animals, his long fingers laced over one knee. "You said you'd guide us to the free cities. That's enough."

"It isn't, though. Is it?" Wren glanced at him sidelong. "Because I've watched the way you look at me when you think I'm not attending. All of you. You're wondering what a hedge-witch wants with a taster and a mapmaker and a boy who talks to hinges. Nobody walks into Marrowfen asking for me unless they're desperate, and nobody leaves my company without paying something. So let's have it done properly."

Perrin had gone still the way he went still when he was listening to something no one else could hear — but there were no doors out here, only the wind moving through the dead bracken, and after a moment Wren understood he was listening to *her*. Reading her the way he read locks. Children did that. They heard the hum under a person's voice, the thing that didn't match the words.

Isandro sat closest to the fire. The taster. The woman who drank poison like spring water and called it nothing. Wren had known her for three days now, and in three days she had catalogued her the way she catalogued any patient: pulse steady, appetite good, sleep shallow, hands that never stopped moving — folding bandages, sorting herbs, always useful, always slightly apart. A woman built like a wall with no door in it.

Wren knew about walls. She'd built hers brick by brick over eleven years, and every brick had a name.

"My sister's name was Lark," she said.

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"She was two years older than me and better at everything, which is the standard complaint of younger sisters everywhere, so you'll forgive me if I don't elaborate. She could make a fever break just by sitting with it. She could coax a calf from a dead cow with her hands in the dark. When the blight came through the barley fields the year we were girls, she walked the rows singing and half of them came back. Half. Everyone said it was luck." Wren smiled at the fire, and it was not a kind smile. "Everyone always says it's luck. Luck is what they call it when they don't have to burn anyone."

Isandro's hands had gone quiet in her lap.

"The hunters came to our village in the third year of Aldric's cleansing," Wren went on. "Grey hoods, humming boxes on chains, the whole theater of it. We'd had warnings, so Lark and I had done what everyone sensible does — buried our herbs, doused our charms, become two plain women who sold poultices and knew nothing. And it would have worked. It *would* have worked, except for the Osgood boy."

She set the yarrow down. Her fingers wanted something to do, so she made herself fold them.

"Tomas Osgood. Nine years old. His gift came in late and loud — things moved around him when he cried, and he cried a lot because his father beat him, and the more his father beat him the more things moved, and so on, the whole ugly wheel of it. The night before the hunters reached us, his father dragged him to my door half-frozen and said, *fix him or I'll drown him in the mill-pond.* Those were his exact words. I remember them because I was holding them in my mouth for years afterward, turning them over, wondering if I should have said something different."

"What did you do?" Perrin asked. His voice was very small.

"I hid him," Wren said. "That was my trade, before it was amputating hope. There are ways to fold a gift down inside a person — old ways, Vessian ways, midwife's ways. You wrap the gift in layers of itself until it can't hear its own name. It goes quiet. Not gone. Quiet. Like a coal banked under ash. I'd done it twice before, for a girl whose hands healed too fast and a man who dreamed true. Both of them lived out their lives as ordinary as fence posts, and both of them thanked me, and neither of them ever knew what it cost *me* to do it, because doing it means you hold some of their fire in your own hands while you work, and fire remembers where it's been."

She looked up, at last, and found Isandro watching her with an expression Wren recognized and hated: the look of someone assembling a picture they'd been avoiding.

"So I folded Tomas Osgood's gift down small and sent him home, and told his father the boy had grown out of it, children do sometimes, praise the saints. And the next morning the grey hoods came through the village with their humming boxes, and the boxes sang past Tomas like he wasn't there, and I stood in my doorway and thought, *there, it worked, we're safe.*"

The owl asked again. Still no answer.

"Except Lark," Wren said. "Lark had helped me. Held the boy's head while I worked, sang the lowing-song to keep him calm. And the fire I held while folding his gift — some of it passed through her. Just a thread. Just enough. The box on the hunter's chain swung toward her like a compass needle finding north, and the hunter looked at my sister, and my sister looked at me, and I will tell you the exact truth of what happened in that moment, because I have earned the right to tell it exactly."

Her voice did not shake. She had practiced this, in the dark, in the eleven years since. Practiced until the telling was smooth as river stone, because grief you can't say cleanly will cut you forever.

"I looked away," Wren said. "One heartbeat. Maybe two. I looked away because I was afraid, and in that heartbeat the hunter decided, and by the time I looked back there were hands on her arms and the box was singing and the hunter was saying the words — *bearer of the gift, surrendered to the crown's mercy* — and Lark was looking at me over their shoulders, and she wasn't afraid. That's the worst of it. She wasn't afraid. She was *sorry*. Sorry for me. Like I was the one being taken."

Nobody moved. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"They burned her that evening in the market square, along with a widow who cured warts and a shepherd who could call foxes. Three pyres. The village came out to watch because the hoods made them, and most of them looked away, and I learned something important watching them do it: looking away is how ordinary people survive. It's a skill. I had it. I used it. One heartbeat, maybe two."

Isandro spoke for the first time. Her voice was careful, the voice of someone walking on ice. "You couldn't have stopped them. Two women against armed hunters—"

"Oh, I know." Wren waved this away with a hand that was steadier than it felt. "I've had eleven years of knowing it. Knowing changes nothing; that's the first lesson of grief and nobody teaches it. But here's the part I haven't told anyone, the part that matters to you, the part that is my price." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the seams of her face, all the places the years had written their marginalia. "Lark didn't die clean. The hoods took her to the pyre unburned, as they do — the burning is the sentence, but first there's the taking, and the taking has procedure. And in that procedure, in the hour they held her, someone searched her. And found, sewn into her bodice, a little packet of ash."

The wind moved through the bracken. Perrin hugged his knees.

"Our mother died when we were young. Vessian woman, born before the hollowing, married to a cooper, dead of childbed fever with me inside her. Before she died she gave Lark a handful of grave-dirt from the old country and told her: *keep this until you find the way back, and if you never find it, pass it to your sister.* Grave-dirt from a country that isn't on any map anymore. Dirt from Vessia." Wren nodded at Sef. "Your maps, cartographer. The ones that got you exiled. The country you drew — my mother was born in it. My sister carried a piece of it against her heart, and the hunters took it off her body and threw it on the pyre with her, because to them it was evidence of sedition. Ash to ash. They were thorough."

Sef had gone pale in the way scholars go pale — not with fear but with recognition. "There are stories," he said slowly, "among the old families in the borderlands. That anything taken from Vessia's soil belongs to Vessia, and finds its way home when the Door opens."

"Stories." Wren snorted. "Yes. And I'm a hedge-witch who folds children's souls into their ribs, so you'll understand why I keep a professional interest in stories. Here is mine: after they burned her, I raked the pyre. Night, alone, while the village slept behind shutters. I raked it like a woman looking for a coin she'd dropped, and what I found was this."

From inside her coat she drew a small clay jar, no bigger than a fist, sealed with wax and wrapped in oilcloth despite everything, despite eleven years of handling. She set it on the ground between them all, and it sat there in the firelight like a small dark egg.

"Bone ash doesn't burn twice," Wren said. "But my sister's ashes are mixed with my mother's grave-dirt now, because I gathered them together, both of them, everything they took and everything that was left. And I have carried this jar through eleven winters, and I am fifty-one years old, and my knees are shot, and I will tell you plainly what I have never told a living soul."

She looked at each of them in turn. Last of all at Isandro.

"I'm not guiding you to the free cities. The free cities are a detour. I heard what you're actually doing — the Door of Anew, the last threshold, the way through the erasure. Old Marrowfen gossip travels faster than you'd think, and I've been listening for eleven years, because I made a promise at a pyre. Someone must carry my sister home. Through the Door. Into whatever's on the other side of it, whether it's paradise or nothing or a country waiting to be named. Because if the Door is real, then the erasure can be undone, and if the erasure can be undone, then my mother's grave exists again somewhere, and Lark's ashes belong in the ground beside it, and I—" 

For the first time, her voice caught. Just once. She rode through it the way she rode through everything.

"I am tired," she finished, "of carrying my family in a jar."

Silence. The owl had given up. Far off, a wolf said something brief and businesslike to the moon.

It was Perrin who moved first. He crawled forward on his knees, twelve years old and feral and fearless in the way only children and fools are fearless, and put one small dirty hand flat on top of the jar. He cocked his head, listening to it the way he listened to doors.

"It hums," he said wonderingly. "Quiet. Like a door that's almost asleep. Like it's *waiting* for somebody to knock."

Wren closed her eyes. Eleven years of carrying, and a child had heard it in three seconds.

Then Isandro reached out and covered the boy's hand with her own, and looked at Wren across the fire with those steady taster's eyes that had swallowed poison and lies and terror and never yet flinched.

"Then we'll carry her," Isandro said simply. "All of us, if we have to. Sorrow shared is weight halved — that's the oldest law there is, older than kingdoms. Your sister comes with us, Wren. That's not the price. That's just what family does."

And Wren Ostara, who had amputated hope for a living and never once wept at her own table, turned her face to the fire so none of them would see what the flames were doing to her eyes — and missed entirely the way Sef had gone rigid, staring at the jar, because he had seen, in the flicker of the light, the faintest shimmer of a pattern in the ash.

A pattern shaped like a road.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, as Wren insisted — she trusted the grey hours, the time when hunters' humming boxes were coldest and slowest to wake. Frost silvered the bracken. Perrin walked at the front with his head tilted, reading the land's locked things, and Sef walked beside Wren with the jar tucked carefully into the pack between them, wrapped now in one of his spare shirts like a sleeping infant.

"You saw it too," he said quietly. "Last night. In the ash."

"I see a great many things in ash. It's mostly ash."

"A road, Wren. A coronation road. The same alignment I found in the erased milestones outside Hollowmere." He kept his voice low, pitched under Perrin's hearing and above Isandro's, though he suspected — correctly — that Isandro heard everything. "Your sister's ashes aren't mixed with grave-dirt by accident of grief. Bone remembers. Soil remembers. If there's a map anywhere in the world that the usurper's decree couldn't erase, it might be written in that jar."

Wren walked three steps before she answered.

"If you're wrong, cartographer, I'll feed you to the Marrowfen eels."

"And if I'm right?"

She looked ahead, to where the dawn was coming up grey-gold over hills that belonged to no country at all, and where — far off, at the edge of seeing, the way a word sits at the edge of memory — something glimmered that might have been standing stones.

"Then my sister gets to go home," Wren said, "and I get to find out whether the dead forgive." She tightened the pack straps and lengthened her stride. "Either way, we walk fast. Because I've been running from grey hoods for eleven years, and I know their smell, and I'm telling you now —"

She stopped. Turned. Downwind, faint as a lie, came the scent she knew better than her own grief: cold iron and lamp-oil and the particular sourness of men who hunt for wages.

"— they've already found us."
