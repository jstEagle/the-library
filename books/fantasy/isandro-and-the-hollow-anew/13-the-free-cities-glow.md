## 13. The Free Cities Glow

Perrin heard the city before anyone saw it.

Not with his ears — or not only. Doors had a sound, all of them, a low hum like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing, and he'd been listening to that hum since before he could talk. A cottage door was a small note. A keep's gate was a chord. But this — this was ten thousand doors at once, layered over each other like rain on a roof, and it came up through the soles of his boots and settled in his teeth and made him stop dead on the ridge trail with his mouth open.

"What is it?" Isandro asked. She always asked first thing now, ever since the river crossing, ever since he'd heard the hunters' lockboxes humming half a mile off and bought them their lives with a shout. She took him seriously. Nobody had ever taken him seriously before.

"Everything," Perrin said. "It's everything."

They crested the ridge as the sun went down, and there it was.

Callowmark lay in the valley below like a spilled jewel-box. The free city had no walls worth the name — just a sprawl of rooftops and towers and market districts bleeding out into farmland, lit from within by ten thousand lamps and lanterns and witchfires, gold and white and one whole quarter glowing green where, Sef said later, they grew light-moss on the roofs. Smoke rose from a thousand chimneys. Somewhere down there a harbor bell rang, though the sea was still two days east; Perrin could hear the bridges too, old stone ones, humming their long patient notes, and the gates, and the great clock tower whose door sang louder than any door he'd ever heard, a deep bronze voice saying *I am open, I am open, I am open.*

He had never seen so many lights in his life. He had never seen more than forty lights at once, counting a wedding.

"It's real," he said stupidly.

Wren laughed beside him, a dry rustle of a laugh. "What did you think it was, boy? A story?"

Perrin didn't answer, because yes, actually, that was exactly what he'd thought. He'd heard about the free cities the way you hear about the moon — often, and with authority, and without any reason to believe it. Callowmark, Vell's Landing, Osterhale. Places where there was no king, no registry, no Edict of Cleansing. Places where a boy who heard doors wasn't a thing to be surrendered to grey hoods but maybe, possibly, just a boy who heard doors.

Sef stood a little apart, arms crossed, studying the city the way he studied everything — like it might lie to him. "Three days we've been walking," he said. "And I keep waiting for it to be smaller than the maps say. It isn't." There was something raw in his voice. Perrin had learned that Sef got raw whenever the world matched his maps instead of contradicting them. "It's bigger."

Isandro hadn't said anything at all. Perrin glanced over and found her standing very still, her face lit orange by the distant glow, her eyes wet.

"You're crying," he said, because he was twelve and had no tact and nobody had ever taught him any.

"I'm not," she said, and then, "maybe a little," and then she crouched down so they were eye to eye, which she did sometimes, which he pretended to hate. "Have you ever seen anything like it?"

"No."

"Neither have I." She looked back at the city. "I lived my whole life in one valley, Perrin. One valley with one road in and one road out, and I thought that was the world. And that—" she pointed at the sprawl below, at the green-glowing quarter, at the harbor bell he could hear ringing and ringing— "that was out there the whole time. All those people, living under no king at all."

"They'll have prices, though," Wren said grimly. "Free cities are free the way taverns are free. You pay going in and you pay coming out."

But Perrin barely heard her. He was listening to the doors.

* * *

They came down off the ridge in the dark, following a cart road busy even at night — oxen, pilgrims, a family of tinker-folk whose wagon hummed with a hundred little latched boxes that made Perrin's head swim pleasantly. Nobody asked them questions. That alone nearly undid him. In Hollowmere country, every traveler got looked at twice. Here, on the road into Callowmark, nobody cared. He was nobody. It was the most wonderful feeling he had ever had.

The outer gate — the *Weeping Gate*, the tinkers called it, because of the spring that ran through its arch — stood open. Actually open, at night, unguarded except for two bored watchmen playing dice. Perrin stopped under the arch and put his palm flat against the cold stone and listened.

It was singing.

Not the strained hum of a locked thing, not the whine of a door that wanted keeping shut. This was a full-throated, unhurried note, like a monk holding a psalm. *Open. Open. Open.* The gate had been open so long it had forgotten any other song.

"What do you hear?" Isandro asked quietly. She was the only one who asked what he heard. Sef asked what it meant for their safety. Wren asked whether it could be used to detect hunters. Isandro just asked.

"It's happy," Perrin said. "Doors get a kind of — a mood. Locked doors are scared, mostly. Or angry. This one's been open a hundred years and it doesn't remember being anything else." He looked up at her. "I want to live here."

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under water. He couldn't read it yet. Later he would understand it: the moment she stopped thinking of Callowmark as her sanctuary and started thinking of it as *his*.

* * *

Inside, the city swallowed them.

Perrin had no words for it, so mostly he walked with his head craned back and his mouth open, and Wren kept grabbing his collar to steer him away from carts. Noise everywhere — not hunter-noise, not the flat dangerous quiet of roads where grey hoods might be listening, but good noise, thick noise: hawkers crying the price of eels, a fiddler in a doorway, hammering from a cooper's yard, someone laughing so hard and so high that Perrin laughed too without knowing why. Smells stacked on smells — frying onions, tar, horse, bread, something spiced and strange from a stall run by a woman with a shaved head who winked at him. Overhead, laundry lines crossed between buildings like bunting strung for a festival that never ended.

And underneath it all, the doors. Hundreds of them in sight at any moment, thousands out of sight, each one murmuring its own small note. Shop doors that were content. House doors that were sleepy. One alley door that was frightened, and when Perrin steered the party around that alley without explaining why, Isandro didn't ask. She just took his hand and moved him along.

A city, he decided, was a place made entirely of thresholds. Every street was a threshold. Every hour. He had spent his whole life hearing single doors in empty places, and here was the whole choir.

"I can't hear any of it," Sef muttered, watching him tilt his head like a bird. "What's it like?"

Perrin considered. "You know how a map feels?" he said. "All folded up, and every fold is a place?"

"Yes," Sef said, in the careful voice he used when Perrin said something that mattered.

"It's like being a map that's open."

Saf stopped walking. For a moment the disgraced cartographer of nothing looked at the boy who heard doors with an expression close to reverence. Then he cleared his throat and said, gruffly, "Come on, before Wren spends our last coins on eels."

* * *

They found a room above a dye-shop in the tanners' quarter, cheap because it stank, and Perrin fell asleep that first night harder than he had ever slept in his life — no dreams, no listening, just black warm nothing with the city's ten thousand doors humming him down like a lullaby sung by everyone at once.

He woke near dawn to voices below the window, low and urgent, and crept to the sill because creeping and listening were the only skills he owned.

Isandro and Sef stood in the yard among the dye-vats, wrapped against the morning chill. He should have gone back to bed. He didn't.

"The registry," Sef was saying. "I told you. Gifted refugees register, they get a brand on the wrist and a work-assignment, and in exchange the city swears its watch won't sell your name to Aldric's agents. Tolerated labor. Not citizens. Not quite people."

"And if we don't register?"

"Then we're smuggled goods, and every informer in Callowmark has a price, and Veyra has coin." Sef rubbed his jaw. "There's a third option. Passage east, on a grain ship, out of Aldric's reach entirely. But passage costs, and we've got—" he turned out his purse and shook it; something small and copper fell into his palm— "that."

Silence. Perrin watched Isandro look east, toward the harbor he couldn't see, toward the sea she'd never smelled until three days ago.

"You could sell the maps," she said quietly.

Sef went very still. "No."

"Sef—"

"They're all I have. They're the only record that Vessia existed. If I sell them they'll end up on some collector's wall, next to a hunting tapestry, and no one will know what they mean, and the country will be dead twice." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, Perrin could tell, hated it the way Isandro hated lying — visibly, bodily.

Below, Isandro reached out and closed Sef's fist back over the copper coin. Perrin couldn't hear what she said next; she said it too softly, and the dye-yard wind took it. But he saw Sef's shoulders come down from around his ears, and he saw her glance up — right at his window, right at him, though he was sure she couldn't see him in the dark room — and he understood suddenly that whatever she was weighing, she was weighing it for him.

She wanted the ship for herself, maybe. Escape, clean and far. But she wanted the city for *him*. For the boy who'd never seen forty lights at once, who'd slept his whole life in ditches and waystations listening to frightened doors. She wanted him to have a gate that sang *open*.

Perrin backed away from the window, heart thumping, and sat on his straw mattress, and did the only thing he knew how to do.

He listened.

Under the city's ten thousand contented doors, under the harbor bell and the bronze-voiced clock tower, there was another sound. Far off. Faint. East and north, out past the edge of the maps Sef carried in his head. A door so large and so old that its hum wasn't a hum at all but a pulse, slow as a sleeping heart, patient as stone.

He'd heard it once before, half-buried in a dream, the night after the ford. He'd told himself he imagined it.

He hadn't.

"Perrin!" Isandro's voice on the stair, bright, deliberately casual. "There's bread. Real bread, with the crust that fights back. Come fight it."

He went. Bread was important. But at the top of the stairs he paused and looked back east, toward the pulse beneath the mountains, and found Isandro watching him with that sharp taster's attention she never quite turned off.

"You heard something," she said. Not a question.

Perrin opened his mouth — and downstairs, in the street, a watchman's whistle blew, once, twice, and a voice cried the morning proclamation: *"Registry opens at midbell! All bearers presenting for brand and bond, south gate yard! Unregistered persons found within the walls will be remanded to the hunters' liaison!"*

The bread could wait. Everything, Perrin was learning, could wait — except the sound under the mountains, which had begun, very faintly, to speed up.
