## 14. Sanctuary Has a Price List

The gates of Callowmark were made of ship-timber, black with salt, and carved with the faces of drowned sailors whose names no one remembered. Sef Ardenne had drawn them once, thirty years ago, from a merchant's description — a boy with a borrowed compass and more ambition than paper. He had gotten the faces wrong, he saw now. He had given them dignity.

"Keep your hood up," he murmured to Isandro, walking at his left shoulder. Perrin clung to Wren's hand on his other side, eyes enormous, drinking in the noise of the queue like a man dying of thirst. "And whatever they ask you, answer slowly. Stupidity is cheaper than suspicion here."

"What do they ask?" Isandro said.

"How much you're worth."

The line moved in increments. Around them pressed the flotsam of Aldric's cleansing: a family of millers from the Ashwater valleys, their youngest child carrying a hen under one arm like a doll; an old man with burned hands who kept saying *I'm not gifted, I'm not, I only dreamed things before they happened*; two sisters in matching grey who had stopped speaking entirely somewhere on the road and hadn't started again. Sef catalogued them all without meaning to. It was the cartographer's curse — he could not look at a crowd without seeing it as a map, every face a landmark, every grief a border.

At the gate stood a clerk behind a plank table, protected from the wind by nothing but a ledger's worth of self-importance. He had ink-stained fingers and the flat eyes of a man who processed suffering by weight.

"Name?"

Sef gave his. The clerk wrote it, then looked up with the first flicker of interest.

"Sef Ardenne." He said it the way one reads a label on spoiled meat. "The cartographer."

"I've been called that."

"You were Vessia's court mapmaker. Before the Unmaking." The clerk's pen hovered. "There's a standing notice on that name. Reward for information leading to recovery of royal chart materials."

*Of course there is.* Sef felt Wren shift behind him, felt Perrin's small hand find the back of his coat. "The chart materials were confiscated," he said evenly. "Twenty-nine years ago. Everything I carried was burned in the square at Corvane. You may check the records."

"Oh, I intend to." The clerk smiled without warmth and turned to Isandro. "And her? Name, trade, and — " he tapped a box on his form with grim satisfaction — "gifted or ungifted? Bear in mind that lying on a registry is a flogging offense, and we test."

Isandro was quiet a moment too long. Sef watched her weigh it — he could see the scales working behind her eyes, the taster's precision applied to her own skin. She had spent her whole life being measured by what she could swallow. Now the measurement would be written down.

"Ungifted," she said. "Cook. Taster, formerly. Of the palace at Hollowmere."

"Taster." The clerk's pen scratched. "Useless trade here. Cooks are wanted, though. Registered labor, kitchen class." He stamped something. "You'll report to the Labor House on Saltrow within three days for assignment. Next."

"That's it?" Isandro said. "No brand? No — "

"The brand comes with registration, not entry." The clerk was already waving forward the millers and their hen. "Entry means you exist inside the walls. Registration means you're allowed to work, rent, buy bread above the scrap tier, and walk through certain districts after dark. Unregistered gifted are tolerated for seven days. After that, the harbor watch sells your description to whoever's buying." He glanced up, almost kindly. "Mostly it's Aldric's people. They pay in iron."

They passed under the gate into the roar of the city.

* *

Callowmark smelled of fish, tar, frying onions, and money — the last strongest of all. It was a city built on the honest principle that everything could be bought, and its streets arranged themselves accordingly: the fishmongers near the water, the chandlers near the fishmongers, the moneylenders near everything. Sef walked them up from the harbor through lanes that climbed in terraces, each level a little cleaner, a little quieter, a little more expensive, until the poor below looked like a tide line left by some retreating sea.

Perrin had gone silent in the way that frightened Sef more than crying would have. The boy walked with his head tipped, listening — Sef knew by now what that posture meant. Ten thousand locks, Wren had told him the boy had said once, in another city. Here it must be twenty thousand. Doors, gates, strongboxes, chains, the great harbor boom that closed the bay at night. A city was a scream of locked things to someone like Perrin, and no one else could hear it.

"There," Wren said, and pointed.

The inn was called the Drowned Compass, which Sef took as an omen either good or terrible, and it stood on Saltrow between a rope-maker and a pawnshop, sagging slightly to seaward like a tired sailor. Its sign showed a compass rose with the needle pointing down. The landlady, a broad woman named Maren Cotter with forearms like anchor chain, looked them over in the doorway with the frank arithmetic of her profession.

"Five of you. One room, two pallets on the floor, cold hearth rights, and you eat what the kitchen eats. Nine coppers a night."

"We can't — " Isandro began.

"Seven," Maren said, "if two of you work. Kitchen help, or hauling, or mending nets. I don't care what you were before. In here you're hands." Her eyes snagged on Sef's ink-stained fingers, then dismissed them. Ink was not a trade she recognized as labor.

"We'll take it," Sef said, before pride could finish pricing itself.

The room was under the eaves and smelled of salt and old smoke, but it had a window, and the window had a view of the harbor, and for a quarter hour nobody spoke because Perrin was at the glass watching the ships come in with an expression Sef decided privately he would remember on his deathbed. Wonder, unguarded, in a boy who had learned to hide everything else.

Then Wren counted out their purse on the floorboards, and the wonder was over.

"Eleven coppers," she said. "And a button."

"The button's mine," Perrin said instantly.

"The button's yours." Wren sat back on her heels. "Nine a night. We can eat if we work. But we can't travel. Not all five of us, not fed, not equipped, not past the checkpoints on the coast road. And we can't stay, because in seven days the registry comes due, and none of us are registering."

Silence. Below them, the inn roared with evening trade, and the sound of it — laughter, argument, a fiddle badly played — seemed almost obscene against the arithmetic on the floor.

"I could register," Isandro said quietly.

"No," Sef and Wren said together.

"It's just a brand. I've seen worse — "

"You haven't seen what registered labor looks like after five years," Wren said. Her voice was flat, professional, the voice she used for wounds. "I've patched them. The ones who still had skin left to patch. The brand isn't a mark, girl, it's a handle. It gives everyone who isn't branded permission to grip."

Sef turned to the window so they wouldn't see his face. Because he already knew the answer to their problem. He had known since the gate, when the clerk read his name off a notice with reward attached. His life's work sat in a oilcloth tube strapped to his back, and it was worth exactly enough to save them, and the only way to realize its value was to destroy everything it meant.

* * *

He went out alone that night, telling them he needed air, which was true, though air was not what he sought. He walked down through the terraces to the harbor district, where the shops stayed open late for sailors flush with pay, and he found what he was looking for on a street called Quill Lane: a map-seller's shop with a lamp burning in the window and charts hung in the glass like skins in a tannery.

The proprietor was a narrow man named Osk Vell with spectacles thick as portholes and the connoisseur's squint. He took one look at Sef's hands and said, "Cartographer. Copyist work? I pay by the sheet."

"Not copyist."

Vell's squint deepened. Sef set the oilcloth tube on the counter and did not open it. That was the whole negotiation, right there, in the not-opening.

"Royal Vessian survey," Sef said. "Pre-Unmaking. Full kingdom series. Forty-one sheets, complete, with the marginalia — roads, wells, fords, the old coronation way. Drawn from court originals before the erasure decrees. There are perhaps six such sets in existence. Three are in Aldric's vaults. One is in the free cities' archive and has never been shown to anyone. One sank with the *Merrow's Daughter*." He paused. "And this one."

Vell had gone very still, the way men go still around valuable things. "You're Ardenne," he breathed. "The disgraced one. They say you mapped a country that doesn't exist."

"They say that about anyone who remembers Vessia."

"And Aldric's agents — "

"Would pay me nothing and hang me afterward. You'd pay me and sell it quietly to collectors who'll never show it to a soul. Everyone profits except the truth." Sef heard his own voice and hated its steadiness. Thirty years of exile, of memorizing what he could no longer own, of redrawing sheets from memory by candlelight in rooms he could be dragged out of — and it came down to a shop on Quill Lane and a man with porthole spectacles deciding what memory weighed per ounce.

"How much?" Vell said.

"Two hundred crowns."

Vell laughed, genuinely, and took off his spectacles to polish them. "Master Ardenne. Two hundred crowns is what a ship pays for a cargo of timber. What you're holding is — " he caught himself, recalculated, put the spectacles back on — "is priceless, yes, to the right buyer, and worthless to everyone else, because no right buyer walks into my shop. Ninety."

"One hundred sixty. And you never display it. Never copy it. If a single sheet surfaces in a window, I'll know, and I'll tell Aldric's people exactly where their prize ended up."

Something flickered across Vell's face — respect, maybe, or its mercantile cousin, fear. "One hundred forty. Paid tonight. Half now, half when my buyer inspects it tomorrow."

"Tonight. All of it. I leave the city within the week."

Vell studied him for a long moment, and Sef understood that the man was reading him the way he read charts — looking for the forgery, the flaw in the coastline, the place where the ink lied. Whatever he found, he found acceptable.

"One hundred forty," Vell said, and went to his strongbox.

* * *

Sef did not go straight back to the Drowned Compass. He walked instead along the harbor wall, where the wind came raw off the water, and he unwrapped the tube and looked at the topmost sheet by the light of the beacon fire.

The Vale of Hollowmere, as it had been. Before the hollowing. Before the valley was scooped out of the world like pulp from a fruit and filled with forgetting. On his chart the vale had a name — *Hollowmere* was the erasure's name for it, the joke of it, calling the wound by a word that meant empty — and rivers ran through it that no longer ran anywhere, and a road crossed it that led, eventually, to the coronation way, and to places that the Door of Anew might still remember even if no living soul did.

He had drawn these lines when he was nineteen years old, kneeling on the floor of the royal survey office while old Master Corvel dictated elevations. He had drawn them believing the country would last forever, because at nineteen you believe that about everything. Then the usurpers came, and then the Unmaking, and Sef had committed the only crime that mattered anymore: he refused to stop drawing it. *A country that isn't on any chart,* they charged him, *cannot be mapped, and to map it is to claim it exists.* As if existence were a matter of paperwork. As if ink could kill.

He rolled the sheet away and bound the tube, and his hands were steady, and that was the worst part. A man selling his own heart should at least tremble.

But he thought of Perrin at the window, watching ships with his whole face open. He thought of Wren counting eleven coppers and a button. He thought of Isandro saying *it's just a brand* in a voice that pretended to be practical and wasn't. He thought of Tam, left behind in a grey-hooded wagon, and of every debt a coward accumulates.

Dignity, Sef thought, was a fine thing for men who could afford to eat. His had been repossessed decades ago anyway, in a square in Corvane, in front of a crowd. What remained was usefulness, and usefulness could be sold by the sheet.

* * *

They were all awake when he came back, sitting around the cold hearth in the manner of people who have been having a conversation that stops when a door opens. He set the purse on the floorboards between them, and it landed with the heavy, unmistakable sound of real money, and four faces turned to it the way plants turn to light.

"Where did — " Isandro started.

"I sold the maps."

The silence that followed had texture. Wren looked at him with an expression he couldn't parse — pity, maybe, or its harder cousin, recognition. She of all of them knew what it cost to carry a dead thing and call it living.

"All of them?" Perrin asked. "Even the pretty one? With the mountains?"

"Especially the pretty one. It fetched the best price." Sef sat down heavily. "Understand what I bought with it. Passage for five, outfitted, fed, bribed where bribing is required. Perhaps a month of road. Perhaps less, if the coast checkpoints have raised their rates, which they will have, because war is coming down out of the east and everything between here and the sea knows it." He looked around the circle of faces. "What I did *not* buy is a future in which any of us is safe. Money runs out. Aldric doesn't. So before anyone thanks me, understand that we now have exactly one asset left, and it isn't the purse."

"The Door," Isandro said.

"The Door." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and let himself say aloud the thing he had been carrying since the river crossing, the thing he had checked and rechecked against memory a hundred times. "My charts are gone, but my head isn't. The Door of Anew isn't a fixed point — it never was. The old texts describe it as following the coronation road, surfacing where the road meets certain stones. There's a site in the eastern hill country. The Stone Choir, the shepherds called it, before the shepherds were relocated and the name was struck. Standing pillars, dozens of them, older than Vessia herself. If the Door still moves the way it moved, it will surface there." He hesitated. "Within the month. Possibly within the fortnight."

"And Aldric's hunters?" Wren said. "Do they have your head too?"

"Some of it. Enough to be dangerous. Which brings me to the last thing I bought tonight." Sef reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper, bought from a harbor informer for three coppers, and laid it on the floorboards where the firelight could reach it. "This came off a courier boat yesterday. It's a bounty circular. Look at the description."

They bent over it. Isandro read it aloud, slowly, the way she tasted anything uncertain.

"*Wanted alive. Five persons traveling together. A woman, mid-twenties, dark-haired, formerly food-taster to the regent of Hollowmere, believed gifted, believed immune to poison. A man, fifty-ish, disgraced cartographer of the Vessian court.*" Her voice caught. "*A hedge-witch healer. A boy who hears locked things.*" She looked up. "It's us. Every detail. Someone talked."

"Or someone watched," Sef said. "Either way — the price on our heads is forty crowns apiece, alive. Two hundred for the set." He managed something that was almost a smile. "We are, collectively, worth rather more than my life's work. I'd take it as a compliment."

Nobody laughed. Outside, the fiddle in the common room had stopped, and the inn settled into the creaks and sighs of a building full of sleepers, and somewhere below them Perrin's sharp ears would be hearing the thousand small sounds of locks turning for the night.

"So we run," Wren said. "Tomorrow. Tonight, if the tide allows."

"We run east," Sef corrected. "Not to the sea. To the hills. To the Choir." He met Isandro's eyes across the dead hearth, and saw in them the same thing he felt in his own chest — terror wearing the mask of resolve. "Because the maps are sold, the money will run out, and the hunters are coming. All we have left is the one thing in this world that Aldric wants most and cannot buy."

Isandro picked up the bounty circular, folded it along its creases, and tucked it into her bodice like a promise.

"Then we'd better get there first," she said.
