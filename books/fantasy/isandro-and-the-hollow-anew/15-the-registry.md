## 15. The Registry

The line outside the Registry House began forming before dawn, and by the time the sun cleared Callowmark's eastern wall it had grown long enough to wrap the building like a slow grey serpent. Isandro stood in it with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, counting exits out of habit. Two doors at the front — both barred, both guarded. A loading yard behind, where a cart of ledgers had come in an hour ago. A chimney on the east side, wide enough for a child or a very determined cat.

Not for her. She was neither small nor a cat, and she was tired of thinking like someone who could still run.

"You're doing the thing again," Sef murmured beside her. He didn't look at her when he said it. He'd learned that much about her in three days: she could bear being spoken to, but not being watched while she was afraid.

"What thing?"

"The counting."

"Then stop watching me count."

He almost smiled. In the grey morning light his face looked older than it had by firelight, the exile's lines around his mouth cut deeper, and his eyes kept moving over the Registry House with the particular hunger of a man reading a building like a map. "There's a drainage grate," he said quietly. "Under the yard wall. I saw it when they brought the ledgers in. If it comes to that."

"It won't come to that," Wren said from Isandro's other side. She said it the way she said most things — flatly, as though daring the world to contradict her — but her hand rested on the strap of the satchel where her sister's ashes rode against her hip, and her thumb moved once across it, back and forth, like a woman touching a wound to be sure it was still there.

Ahead of them, the line shuffled forward a body-length. Perrin, wedged between Sef and the wall, had gone unnaturally quiet. He'd been quiet since they joined the queue, actually, which for Perrin was its own kind of alarm. Now he tugged Isandro's sleeve and leaned close enough that she felt his breath through her cloak.

"There's iron under the floor," he whispered. "A lot of it. It's humming low, like a struck bell that won't finish ringing." His eyes were too wide. "And there's something else. Under the humming. Something cold."

Isandro's stomach tightened. "Cold how?"

"Like a door that doesn't want to open. Like the opposite of a door." He shuddered and pressed himself against the wall, putting stone at his back the way he always did in crowds, and would say nothing more.

They moved forward another length. The Registry House had been a customs office once, back when Callowmark traded with kingdoms instead of absorbing their refugees; you could see it in the stonework, the carved scales above the lintel worn smooth by two hundred years of rain. Someone had hung a new sign beneath the old carving, fresh-painted, the letters neat and official:

**REGISTRY OF GIFTED PERSONS. COMPLIANCE IS PROTECTION.**

Beneath that, smaller: *Unregistered persons found within city walls after the first bell will be remanded to hunter custody.*

Compliance is protection. Isandro turned the phrase over the way she used to turn a suspect broth over her tongue, looking for what lay beneath the flavor. It tasted of nothing, which was how the worst poisons advertised themselves. Protection was the word Aldric's criers had used in Hollowmere too, before the grey hoods came. Protection meant the king protected himself from you.

The line lurched forward. Through the open doors she could see the interior: a long counter, clerks behind it with ink-stained fingers, and past the counter, through an inner archway, a second room where the actual work happened. She couldn't see into it clearly. But she could see the light — a dull orange flicker on the archway's stones, pulsing slow and steady, like breathing.

Like a forge.

Her mouth went dry. Beside her, Wren saw where she was looking and understood at the same moment. The hedge-witch's jaw set. "Brand," she said, so softly only Isandro could hear. "I told you. They don't register you with ink. Ink washes off."

"I know what I told myself I could bear," Isandro said. "Telling myself and bearing are different countries."

"They usually are." Wren's voice stayed level, but something in it gentled, the way it did when she spoke to the broken people who came to her table. "You can still walk away. There are roads south that don't pass through any registry. Harder roads. Hungrier ones."

"And hunters on all of them, sooner or later." Isandro watched the orange light breathe on the archway stones. "No. We came here to disappear, and this is how disappearing works in a city. You pay for it in flesh." She flexed her hands inside her sleeves. They were steady. She made them stay steady. "I've spent my whole life putting things in my mouth that should have killed me. One more won't change what I am."

Wren studied her for a long moment. "No," she said finally. "It won't. But it might change what you're willing to become. Mind that line, taster. Don't cross it by accident."

* * *

The clerk at the counter had a face like a closed ledger and a voice that had been sanded down by ten thousand identical interviews. Name. Origin. Nature of gift. Evidence thereof. He asked the questions without looking up, his pen moving in quick economical strokes, and Isandro stood before him performing the hardest work of her life: lying badly on purpose.

Because here was the trap within the trap. Give them her true name — Isandro Dunmore, royal food-taster of Hollowmere, fled the palace the night the hunters took Tam — and she might as well paint a target on her own back and mail it to Captain Veyra. But give them a name too polished, too confident, and every liar's instinct in the room would start twitching. The trick, she realized as she stepped up to the counter, was to lie the way frightened people lied: incompletely. With seams showing.

"Name?" the clerk said.

"Mara." Her voice cracked slightly on it, and she let it. "Mara Vess. From—" she hesitated, and the hesitation was real, because she'd rehearsed this part and now found the rehearsal deserting her— "from a village south of the valley. Small place. You wouldn't have it."

"Everyone says that." The pen scratched. "Nature of gift?"

Here it was. The hinge. She'd turned this over all night in the rented room with its thin mattress and its view of the neighbor's laundry line. Register as what she truly was — poison-proof, sealed vessel, the girl whose blood hummed where magic couldn't follow — and she became exactly the kind of prize the hunters' devices were built to find, registered or not. Register as something lesser, something common, and the brand would be lighter, the leash looser, the attention smaller.

But the registry tested. Everyone in the line knew it; everyone pretended not to. Somewhere past that inner archway, there was a device — one of the humming things, or something worse — and whatever gift you declared had better match whatever it found in you, or the discrepancy itself became evidence.

So she gave them the truth, trimmed.

"I taste things," she said. "That aren't there. Spoilage, mostly. Poison sometimes. My tongue knows what my eyes don't." A pause, then, letting a tremor through: "My mother had it too. She died anyway. Of a fever. So it isn't much of a gift."

The clerk's pen paused. For the first time he looked up, and his eyes were pale and incurious and utterly without mercy, and Isandro understood that mercy wasn't his job. "Sensory gift," he said. "Class four. Low value." The words landed on her like coins tossed to a beggar — small, insulting, and exactly what she'd come to collect. "Testing will confirm classification. Refusal of testing voids registration and constitutes confession."

"I'll test," she said.

He slid a wooden token across the counter, stamped with a number. Forty-one. "Wait against the east wall until your number is called. Do not leave the building. Do not attempt to conceal your person from the testers." And then, because the machine required even its smallest cruelties to be procedural: "Next."

She took the token. The wood was warm from the clerk's hand, and that small human warmth in the middle of all this cold machinery nearly undid her more than the forge-light did.

* * *

They waited against the east wall, the four of them spread apart the way strangers wait, not touching, not speaking, bound together only by the frequency of each other's glances. Sef held number forty-two — he'd registered as a "gift of wayfinding," which was true enough and useless enough to be safe, though Isandro caught the flicker of grief in his face as he said it aloud, because wayfinding was the least of what his maps held and he'd just handed a piece of his life's meaning to a clerk with sandpaper eyes. Wren took number forty-three and declared herself what she was — healer, class six — because hiding it would have meant abandoning the one thing keeping her upright. Perrin was the problem.

Perrin was twelve, unregistered, undocumented, and gifted with something no clerk had a category for. Children under fourteen could be entered on a guardian's record, the notice on the wall said, *classification pending*. Which meant Perrin needed a guardian, and a guardian needed a plausible story, and the story they'd built in the rented room last night — Mara Vess, widowed, traveling with her sister and her late husband's brother and the brother's son — was already fraying under the weight of four people trying to remember who they were supposed to be to each other.

When Perrin's turn came at the counter, Isandro stepped up beside him and put her hand on his shoulder, and the boy — bless him, feral, impossible boy — went perfectly still and perfectly blank, the way he did when he was listening hard to something no one else could hear.

"This one's mine," she told the clerk. "Nephew. No gift that we've seen. His father had the hearing, though. Died last winter."

"No gift that we've seen," the clerk repeated, writing it. "Classification pending. He'll be tested with your batch."

Isandro's hand tightened on Perrin's shoulder. "He's a child."

"The edict doesn't distinguish." The clerk's pen didn't pause. "The edict is the edict. Next."

*They'll put the humming device near him,* Isandro thought, walking back to the wall with Perrin pressed against her side, small and rigid as a fence post. *And he hears everything. What if he flinches? What if he tells them what's under the floor?*

She bent as if to straighten his collar and put her lips near his ear. "Whatever you hear in there," she breathed, "you hear nothing. Can you do that? Can you be deaf for five minutes?"

His answer was a whisper so faint she felt it more than heard it. "I can try. But the cold thing under the floor — Mara—" he'd taken to the false name with unsettling ease— "it's not humming like the others. It's *hungry*. If they bring it close to me, I don't know what my face will do."

* * *

Numbers thirty through thirty-five went through the inner archway while they waited. Thirty-one came back out weeping, clutching her registration papers, a fresh black mark on the back of her left hand — a circle bisected by a line, the sigil of the registry, raw-edged and glistening. Thirty-three never came back out at all. A clerk emerged, consulted a list, and crossed something off, and no one in the line asked, because everyone in the line understood that some classifications ended in rooms without windows.

Number forty was a broad-shouldered ferryman with a river-gift, and as he passed through the archway Isandro got her first clear look into the testing chamber, and wished she hadn't.

The forge was real — a squat iron brazier, banked coals, and laid across it in a rack, a row of branding irons like instruments in a surgeon's case, each stamped with its class. Beside the brazier stood the device: a sphere of grey metal on a stand, veined with channels that caught the coal-light, and around it the air had a wrongness she felt in her teeth, a pressure like the moment before a storm breaks. The tester — a grey-hooded man, though his hood bore no hunter's badge, only the registry's seal — gestured the ferryman to place his hand upon the sphere.

The sphere hummed. The hooded man read something in it, nodded, selected an iron from the rack, and returned it to the coals.

The ferryman watched the iron heat. He did not look away. Isandro found herself respecting him enormously and grieving for him in the same breath.

They called forty-one.

* * *

The testing chamber smelled of hot iron and singed hair and, underneath both, something older and stranger — a smell that snagged at Isandro's trained tongue the way an unfamiliar spice does, half-familiar, half-wrong. Ozone, she thought, though she'd never had a word for it before this year. The smell of magic being handled roughly.

"Hand on the sphere," the hooded man said. "Flat. Fingers spread. Do not withdraw it."

She placed her palm against the grey metal. It was cold — colder than iron had any right to be, cold as well-water, and the cold climbed her arm like frost climbing a windowpane. Then the humming started, and with it the pressure, that searching weight passing through her flesh the way a customs officer's hands pass through luggage, looking for what shouldn't be there.

*Come on,* she thought, and it was half prayer and half command. *Find the tongue. Find the little gift, the poor dead mother's gift, the spoilage-and-poison party trick. Find what I told them and nothing else.*

The sphere hummed. The pressure deepened, bored, reached—

—and stopped.

It stopped the way a finger stops at the surface of frozen ice. Isandro felt it happen, felt the searching weight press against something inside her and slide off, unable to grip. The sealed vessel. The cork. Whatever the valley of Hollowmere had built into her blood, it was holding — the device found her gift the way a man finds a locked door: present, undeniable, and impenetrable. It could tell something was there. It could not say what.

The hooded man frowned at the sphere. Turned a dial. The pressure redoubled, sharp enough to make her vision spark at the edges, and still it slid off, off, off, her secret wrapped in its valley-forged silence.

"Anomalous," the tester said at last, and Isandro's heart dropped through the floor. "Sealed signature. Class indeterminate." He looked at her for the first time, and his eyes were the same pale incurious grey as the clerk's, but behind them something had woken up. "What did you declare?"

"Sensory. Class four. Tasting."

"Hm." He wrote something. She watched the pen move and could not read it upside down, and hated him for it. "Indeterminates are branded to class four provisionally. Re-examination in one year." He selected an iron from the rack — a small one, thank every merciful power, a stylus-thin rod with the registry's bisected circle at its tip — and laid it in the coals. "Left hand. Back. Do not move."

The iron heated. The chamber was quiet except for the coals' soft breathing and, faintly, from below the floor, the cold hungry thing Perrin had described, turning over in its sleep.

Isandro looked at the heating iron and made a decision that felt less like a decision than like setting down a heavy load she'd been carrying since the night she fled through the mill-race.

She had spent her whole life tasting other people's poisons and calling it survival. Swallowing what was meant to destroy her, smiling, declaring the broth fit, and going home to eat alone. Every humiliation of Hollowmere had taught her the same lesson: endure, conceal, survive, and call the surviving enough. Well. She was done calling it enough.

This mark would be a leash, yes. It would sit on her hand for the rest of her life, announcing her to every checkpoint and every clerk and every grey hood. But a leash has two ends. And she intended, with everything in her, to be the end that pulled.

Let it be a promise instead. Let the burn write something no clerk could read: *I am not yours. I am marked, not claimed. Everything you do to me from this day forward, I am allowing — and what I allow, I can one day refuse.*

"Ma'am," the tester said. "Your hand."

She gave it to him, palm down, steady as a table.

The iron touched her skin, and the world went white and silent and absolutely simple. Pain, yes — pain like a struck match dragged the full length of her hand, pain that smelled of herself burning. But beneath the pain, strange and unmistakable, something else: the sealed vessel in her blood noticed the brand the way a sleeping giant notices a fly, and stirred, and settled, and the brand's heat sank into her and *stopped hurting*, absorbed, drunk, gone — so fast and so completely that when the tester lifted the iron away, the mark on her hand was already cooling, already closing, healing with a speed that made the hooded man go very still.

He looked at the mark. He looked at her. Neither of them said anything, because saying it would make it real, and real things had to be reported, and reported things brought grey hoods.

"Class four," he said finally, and wrote it, and his pen pressed harder than it needed to. "Provisional. Collect your papers at the counter. Next."

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had turned gold and ordinary, and Callowmark roared on around them — cart wheels, fishmongers, a bell somewhere marking the hour, ten thousand lives that had no idea what had just been written into the skin of four people standing on the Registry steps. Perrin had his mark too, small and provisional on his wrist; he kept rubbing at it as though it were dirt. Sef walked with his papers folded into his map-case, among the confiscated-and-rebought charts of a country that wasn't there, and said nothing, which from Sef was a speech. Wren took Isandro's hand without asking, turned it over, and examined the brand with a healer's eye.

"It should be weeping," she said quietly. "Day-old brands weep. This one's closed like a week-old scar." Her thumb hovered over the bisected circle without touching it. "What happened in there, taster?"

"I don't know." Isandro looked at her own hand — the leash, the promise, the thing that had swallowed its own burning. "It drank it. The heat. Like it was thirsty."

Wren was silent a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped to that register she saved for diagnoses she didn't like. "Everything about you that's supposed to keep magic out keeps taking things *in*. I'd very much like to know what happens, someday, when there's nothing left for it to take."

Isandro didn't answer. She was looking west, down the long avenue toward the harbor, where the evening light was catching the masts of ships bound for freer waters — and beyond them, far beyond them, the faint blue suggestion of mountains. Under those mountains, if Sef's maps were right and Perrin's ears were truer, something patient was waiting. Something with a door in it.

Her brand throbbed once, gently, like a heartbeat learning its rhythm.

"We need to talk," Sef said suddenly. He was staring at his map-case with an expression Isandro had never seen on him — not the cartographer's calm, but something faster, brighter, almost frightened in its intensity. "Tonight. All of us. I found something in the charts this morning, before the registry, and I thought I was imagining it, and now—" he glanced at her bandaged-looking hand, at the provisional mark that had healed wrong, at all of them wearing the registry's leash— "now I think the maps have been trying to tell me something since Callowmark. About where the Door surfaces next. And I think we have less time than anyone believes."

Far down the avenue, at the mouth of a side street, a figure in a grey hood stood watching the Registry steps. Not approaching. Just watching. Taking notes.

Isandro met its gaze for one long second — defiance disguised as compliance, compliance disguised as nothing at all — and then the figure turned and was gone, and she gripped her family's hands, and they walked home into the gold light like four ordinary registered citizens of a city that was never going to be enough.
