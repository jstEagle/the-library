## 16. Aldric's Voice

The interrogation room in the Hollowmere garrison had once been a tithe office. Veyra could tell by the iron rings still bolted to the floor where grain-sacks had been weighed, and by the tall window with its leaded panes, through which the valley light came down soft and green as water. Someone had hung a burlap curtain over it — the previous captain, a man named Doss who had believed fear worked best in the dark. Doss had been reassigned after his first month produced three suicides and no names.

Veyra pulled the curtain back before she brought the boy in.

He was small for twelve, or thirteen; hard to say. They'd taken him off a hay-wain two days ago at the mill bridge, and he'd fought like something wild caught in a snare — biting, kicking, screaming words that weren't words until one of her hoods had cracked him across the ear with a gauntleted hand. Now he sat on the plain stool across the table from her with his wrists bound in front of him and his head down, hair hanging over his face, breathing through his mouth because his nose was still crusted with dried blood.

Veyra set two cups on the table. She poured honeyed milk into both from a jug she'd carried up herself from the kitchens. Then she sat, unhurried, and drank from her own cup first, letting him watch her throat move.

"You'll take yours when you want it," she said. "It's not drugged. I don't work that way."

The boy said nothing. His shoulders were drawn up around his ears like a hedgehog's spines.

"I'm Captain Veyra," she went on. "I answer to King Aldric, who answers to no one, which makes me very nearly free. I'm going to ask you some questions. You can answer them or not. If you don't, nothing happens today. Nothing happens tomorrow either. I have orders to stay in Hollowmere through the season, and I've been told I'm patient." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the wood. "But here is what I've learned about patience: it always wins against silence. Silence runs out of things to protect. Patience doesn't."

She let that sit. Outside the window, the valley did its valley business — a cart rattling over cobbles, someone calling geese in from the stubble fields, the bell of the low chapel marking the hour with its flat, cracked note. The sounds of a place that had spent thirty years pretending the world outside it was a story told to frighten children.

"Your name is Tam," she said. "That much we know. The stable register lists you as Tam of the Mill House ward, bound to the palace stables since age nine. Your mother died in the fever year. There's no father on record." She watched him flinch at *mother* — a small contraction, quickly smothered, but she was watching for exactly that. "So there's no one to ransom you, and no one to miss you if this goes badly. Which means, boy, that the only person whose opinion matters about how this goes is you."

His head came up an inch. Not all the way — just enough that she could see one eye through the curtain of hair. Brown eye. Wet, furious, frightened. Alive in every particular.

"There," Veyra said. "Now we're talking."

* * *

She had been hunting the gifted for six years, and in six years she had learned that everyone broke differently, and that the breaking told you more than the answers did.

The old ones — the ones who'd hidden their gift through the whole of the Erasure, who'd buried it so deep they'd half-forgotten it themselves — those broke slow, and quiet, and often not at all. They'd had decades of practice at being nobody. The young ones broke fast, because they hadn't yet built the walls, only the habit of hiding behind their mothers' skirts. And then there were the ones in between, the ones who'd found out what they were only recently, whose whole self was still reorganizing around the knowledge — those were the dangerous ones, and the interesting ones, because they hadn't decided yet what the gift meant to them. Fear hadn't finished writing itself into them. Sometimes, in the space before the fear set, you could see what they might have become in another country, under another king.

Tam was one of those. She could smell it on him the way you could smell rain coming.

"The device found you at the stables," she said. "You know the one. Grey case, hums like a struck bell. It sang when it passed within ten paces of you." She took another sip of milk. "What I want to know is why it sang. The device doesn't sing for everything. It sings for doors, mostly. Thresholds. Things that open. So either you've got a door in you, boy, or you were standing near one."

The boy's breath changed. Just barely — a hitch, a shallowing. But Veyra had spent six years reading breath the way other people read letters.

"You know something," she said. "Not about yourself. About someone else."

"I don't know anything," Tam said. First words. Voice cracked and small and stubborn as a weed in a wall.

"Everyone knows something. That's the trouble with people." She leaned back. "Here's what I know, and you can check it against your own knowing. Three nights ago, Isandro Dunmore, food-taster to the regent's household, left her rooms in the servants' wing and did not return. Her cot was made. Her apron was folded on the bedpost. Her knife — the little tasting-knife they give them, bone-handled, you've seen them — was left behind, which tasters never do; that knife is their license and their pride. She went out through the mill-race, waded the sluice for a quarter mile where the water would kill any trail, and climbed the north bank past the boundary stone. Past the edge of the valley." She paused. "No one born in Hollowmere has ever crossed that stone. Not in thirty years. Did you know that?"

The boy's eye had gone wide. He wasn't even trying to hide it now.

"So," Veyra said, "either Isandro Dunmore is dead in the mill-race, or she is the first person in thirty years to walk out of the hollowed country, alone, at night, without a map, without provisions, without telling a soul. And I have walked the mill-race myself, boy, twice, with lanterns and four men, and there is no body in it. So." She spread her hands. "Tell me what you know about Isandro Dunmore."

"I don't—"

"She fed you," Veyra said. "In the winter of the bad barley, when the kitchen scraps went thin, someone was slipping bread up to the loft over the tack room. The stable master logged a complaint about crumbs. Crumbs, boy. Somebody was being careful enough to leave nothing but crumbs, and careless enough to leave those." She tilted her head. "Was it her?"

Something moved through the boy's face — grief, and guilt, and underneath both of them a fierce, stubborn loyalty that made Veyra's chest ache in a way she did not permit herself to examine. He looked down at his bound hands.

"Yes," he whispered.

"And what did she talk about, up there in the loft? When she brought you bread?"

"Nothing." A pause. "Everything. She asked questions. About the horses. About whether I'd ever been past the boundary stone. She used to—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening.

"She used to what?"

"Nothing. I'm not saying anything else."

"You already are," Veyra said gently. "You're saying it with your face. But fine. Keep the rest. Keep it like a coin in your fist. We'll come back to it." She rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the green valley while she talked, so that he could watch her back instead of her eyes, which was sometimes easier. "Let me tell you what I know about tasters, Tam. I've taken eleven of them over the years, in five provinces. Do you know what they all had in common? Every single one?" She didn't wait for an answer. "They were lonely. Profoundly, structurally lonely. Think what the work is. You drink poison so your lord doesn't have to. Everyone in the household needs you alive and no one can afford to love you — because the day someone loves you is the day you start lying for them, and a taster who lies is worth less than the spit in the broth. So they keep you close and they keep you apart, and they pay you well, and they never once sit at your table. Eleven tasters, and every one of them had the same look. Like a dog kept in a fine kennel that's never once been let indoors."

She heard the boy shift on the stool.

"Isandro didn't have that look," she said. "I checked the household portraits. I interviewed the under-cook, the scullery girl, the laundress, the regent's own chamberlain. Do you know what they said about her? Competent. Reliable. *Quiet.* And do you know what none of them could tell me? What she liked. What she feared. Whether she had a sweetheart, a friend, a grievance, a dream. Sixteen years in that palace and she is a shape cut out of paper, boy. She taught herself to be a shape cut out of paper." Veyra turned from the window. "And then one night she folded up her apron, left her knife, and walked out through the cold water into a world that wants her dead. That's not flight. Flight is panic. That was a decision she'd been carrying for years, waiting for a reason. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"No," Tam said. But his voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they understand perfectly.

"I'm telling you she's the most interesting person I have hunted in six years, and I have hunted a woman who could breathe underwater and a man who aged backward, and neither of them planned their way out of a wet paper sack."

* * *

That evening she wrote in her field book, as she did every evening, by lamplight, in a hand so small and regular it looked printed. The book was hers alone; Aldric's clerks could demand her reports, but never her book, and she had made sure of that early, in the way you make sure of things with men like Aldric's clerks — quietly, and only once.

*Subject: Dunmore, Isandro. Taster, Hollowmere regency. Age approx. 26–28.*

*Trait one: patience. Sixteen years in post without incident. Either genuinely loyal (unlikely) or performing loyalty with a discipline I have seen in maybe three living people.*

*Trait two: preparation. Left the knife. Took nothing identifiable. Chose the mill-race — the one route in the valley where our devices read nothing, because moving water drowns the hum. She cannot have known that. Unless she tested it. Unless she has been testing things for years, quietly, the way she tested poisons.*

*Trait three: the immunity itself. This is the part I keep turning over.*

She stopped writing and pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, in the garrison yard, her hoods were drinking and arguing about dice, and the sound of it rose up through the floorboards like weather.

The immunity. Every record agreed: tasters were chosen young, dosed incrementally, built up against the common venoms the way a mason builds a wall, course by course. Standard practice across the old kingdoms, sanctioned, mundane. Except that the records also agreed on something else, something the sanctioning clerks had never explained because explaining was not their trade: tasters' immunities faded within two years of leaving service. The body forgot the poisons. The wall crumbled. That was documented, measured, signed.

Isandro Dunmore had been out of contact with the palace doses for — what, three weeks now? Four? By every account she should be growing *more* vulnerable, not less. And yet the report from the river crossing — Veyra pulled it toward her again, though she knew it by heart — the report from the river crossing said otherwise.

Her lieutenant Brannic had written it, and Brannic did not exaggerate; Brannic couldn't lie convincingly enough to exaggerate. *At the parley,* he had written, *the woman drank the offered wine. Full cup. It was laced with nightshade-bitter, enough to drop a horse. She held it, tasted it, named the poison aloud — named it correctly, named the dose — and set the cup down and asked, politely, if we had anything better to serve. Then they ran the ford while we were standing there like fools.*

Veyra had read that paragraph perhaps forty times.

Naming the poison was skill. Naming the dose was genius. But *drinking it without effect*, after weeks away from the dosing regimen — that was not skill, and it was not immunity, and it was not any natural thing. That was the device humming. That was a door.

And doors, in Veyra's long experience, did not open outward onto nothing. They opened onto whatever had been shut behind them.

She thought about the sealed valleys. Everyone thought about the sealed valleys, if they thought at all; it was the founding fact of the age, the great wound the kingdom had agreed to call a cure. Thirty years ago the old country had burned with the gift — hedge-witches and storm-callers and kings who spoke to the land itself, and wars, and plagues of working, and children born with eyes like banked coals. And then the Concord, and the Erasure, and the great working that had scooped the magic out of the world the way you scoop the yolk from an egg, sealing it into the deep places, striking Vessia's name from every chart and tongue and deed. The valleys were the seal-points. Hollowmere was a cork in a bottle. Everyone knew that. Nobody said it.

Nobody asked what happened to the wine when you corked the bottle.

Veyra looked at her field book, at the neat line *the immunity itself*, and understood, with the cold clean click of a lock turning, that she had been asking the wrong question all along. She had been asking *what is Isandro Dunmore*. The right question was *what has Hollowmere been doing to Isandro Dunmore for twenty-six years* — because if the valley suppressed the gift, and the gift pooled anyway, the way water pools against a dam, then the taster's famous tolerance was not tolerance at all. It was capacity. A vessel, filled slowly, drop by drop, year by year, by the pressure of everything the valley refused to hold.

A vessel. Walking north. Toward the free cities. Toward, if Sef Ardenne's heretical maps were to be believed — and Veyra had paid good silver for copies of them — toward the old coronation road, and the Stone Choir, and the thing beneath it that the humming devices had been whispering about for a decade.

"Oh," Veyra said aloud, to the empty room. "Oh, you clever, terrible girl."

She admired her. That was the truth of it, and she did not flinch from the truth of it; flinching was for people with worse aim. She admired the patience, the preparation, the folded apron, the polite request for a better vintage while poison sat in her belly like a swallowed star. In another life, in a country that existed, Veyra would have wanted Isandro Dunmore at her table, at her side, at her back. In this life, she was going to hunt her across half the world, and she intended to do it with respect, which was the only kind of love available to women like the two of them.

She dipped her pen and wrote, in her smallest hand:

*Conclusion: subject is not fleeing the hunt. Subject is walking toward something. Find the something, and we find her. Priority: the coronation road. Send word to Callowmark — discreetly. The free cities sell everything, including their guests. Especially their guests.*

She blotted the page. Then, after a moment, she added one final line, uncharacteristic, almost tender:

*Note: she will not run from us. She will make us come to her. Plan accordingly.*

* * *

Before dawn, Veyra went down to the cells.

Tam lay curled on the straw pallet, asleep or pretending to it, and beside his pallet, where he could reach it, sat the cup of honeyed milk she'd left him — empty. Drunk. He'd waited until he thought she wouldn't come, and then he'd drunk it, because he was twelve and hungry and tired of being afraid, and because somewhere inside him he had decided, without admitting it, that she was not Doss.

Good, Veyra thought. Trust was just a longer leash.

She crouched by the bars. "Boy."

He stirred. One eye opened, wary as a bird's.

"I'm releasing you," she said.

He sat up so fast the straw scattered. "What?"

"Releasing. Letting go. The word is *freeing*, if you prefer it. You're no use to me unconscious in a cell, and you're no use to me broken, and I find I don't want you broken." She unlocked the cell door and swung it open and stood aside. "Here are the terms, and they're simple. You're going to walk out of this garrison, and you're going to go wherever you were always going to go — and we both know where that is, boy, so don't insult us both by lying. North. To her." She watched his face do several things at once. "You're going to find Isandro Dunmore, and you're going to stay near her, because you love her and because she'll feed you, and every day that you're near her, you're going to notice things. Where she sleeps. Who she travels with. Which road she takes. And every week, a letter will find you — I'll leave instructions with the ferryman at Marrowfen, and the chandler at Callowmark, and three other places you'll discover along the way. You'll write me what you've seen. Small things. True things. And in exchange, when this is all over — however it ends — I will personally see to it that a certain stable-boy of the Mill House ward appears in no register, on no list, under no edict. He will simply have never existed. Clean paper, boy. A whole life with nothing written on it."

Tam stood in the open doorway of the cell, trembling. "And if I say no?"

"Then you walk out anyway, because I said you could, and you spend the rest of the time looking over your shoulder wondering when I change my mind." She smiled, and it was not a cruel smile, which somehow made it worse. "But you won't say no. Because you've already done the arithmetic, same as I have. Right now, the hunters are the most dangerous thing in her world. And you, boy — you could be my eyes inside it. Or—" she paused, and gave him the last piece deliberately, the way you give a child the last piece of bread, "—or you could be the warning that keeps her ahead of me. That's your choice too. I only ask that whichever you choose, you choose it honestly. I'll know if you don't. I always know."

He stared at her for a long moment. Behind the fear and the fury, behind the bruise and the blood, Veyra watched something settle in him — a decision taking root, directionless still, but alive.

"You're not what I expected," Tam said.

"Nobody ever is," Veyra said. "That's rather the point of meeting people."

She watched him go, out through the garrison yard, past the sleeping hoods, past the grey device on its shelf that did not hum for him anymore because she had ordered it muffled — a small mercy, or a small manipulation, and by dawn she honestly could not have said which. At the gate he paused once, looking back, a thin silhouette against the greying sky. Then the valley took him, and he was gone, north, toward the roads and the river crossings and the woman who had slipped him bread through a winter.

Veyra climbed back to her room and opened her field book to a fresh page. At the top she wrote the date, and beneath it, in her small printed hand:

*Asset deployed. Profile complete. Subject: Dunmore, Isandro — taster, vessel, walker of closed roads. Estimated destination: the Stone Choir. Estimated time: one moon, perhaps less.*

She sat for a while with the pen poised, listening to the valley wake below her window — the geese, the cart, the cracked chapel bell. Then she wrote the last line of the entry, and underlined it twice:

*She named the poison and asked for a better cup. Find me the person who does that, and I will show you either the death of this kingdom or its second birth. Possibly both. Possibly the same thing.*

Outside, far to the north, beyond the boundary stone, beyond the hills, the road to Callowmark lay open under the fading stars — and somewhere along it, moving at the speed of a guilty conscience, a stable-boy carried her letter's first instruction in his fist like a coal: *Watch the one she trusts most. Watch the mapmaker.*
