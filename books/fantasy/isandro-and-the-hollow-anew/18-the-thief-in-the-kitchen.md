## 18. The Thief in the Kitchen

The Gilded Perch smelled of onions, tallow, and other people's money.

Isandro had learned its smells the way she had once learned the palace kitchens of Hollowmere — by standing very still in the middle of them until they stopped being strangers. Three weeks now she had worked the supper shift under a false name: Marla Vetch, widow of nowhere, hands too rough for a lady's and too careful for a farmwife's. The lie fit her like an old shoe. She hated how well it fit.

"Marla!" Brannick's voice carried over the clatter from the front room, the way it always did, pitched to reach the back of the kitchen without him raising it above the noise of his own dining hall. "Table six wants the pike again. And the magistrate's party is running late, so hold the quail."

Magistrate Corvel. Third time this month. Isandro wiped her hands on her apron and reached for the pike.

She had come to Callowmark with nothing but Sef's rolled maps hidden in the lining of her coat, Wren's grudging protection, Perrin's hand locked in hers at every crossing, and a brand-shaped promise she had made to herself in the registry office: *I will not be small enough to catch.* Registering as unmarked labor had cost her a name she didn't own and a number inked on a ledger page; it had not, thank every quiet god, cost her skin. Registered laborers could work. Unregistered gifts got hunted. So she worked, and she kept her gift folded up inside her like a letter no one was meant to read, and every night she went back to the rented room above the cooper's shop where four people breathed in their sleep and pretended that was safety.

It almost was.

The pike came off the fire gold-crusted, stuffed with sorrel and river onion. She plated it, tasted the sauce — habit, always habit, the taster's tongue moving before thought — and carried it out through the swinging door herself, because table six sat near the kitchens and because Brannick liked her out front when the room was full. A girl who moved like she belonged made customers tip better. He didn't know he'd hired a court skill.

The Gilded Perch was loud tonight. Dice rattling in the corner by the window. A wool merchant arguing happily about freight rates. Two off-duty city watchmen drinking small beer and pretending not to watch the door, which meant there was something worth watching for. Isandro catalogued all of it without looking directly at any of it — another palace habit, the servant's art of seeing everything while appearing to see nothing.

She set the pike down. The merchant thanked her. She turned back toward the kitchen.

And stopped.

Not because of anything in the room. Because of something in her.

It took her a moment to name the sensation, and when she named it her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world. It was the feeling of the valley — Hollowmere's flat, pressed-down silence, the way magic couldn't follow past its rim. She hadn't felt that deadness since the night she'd gone over the mill-race. But here it was, faint as a draft under a door, coming from the direction of the magistrate's reserved table in the alcove by the hearth.

Someone had set a suppression ward in a public dining room.

* * *

She finished the shift the way a drowning woman finishes a sentence — mechanically, correctly, one foot in front of the other. She bussed plates. She smiled at the wool merchant. She did not look at the alcove more than four times, which was three times too many, and she knew it, and she couldn't stop.

The ward itself told her things. That was the trouble with being what she was: even corked, even buried, the vessel answered. Whoever had laid the charm had done it cheaply — hedge-work, market-stall quality, the kind of thing a nervous household bought for three coppers to keep spirits out of the pantry. It hummed against her awareness the way a bad tooth hums against the tongue. And beneath the hum, threaded through it like wire through wax, was something else. Something that tasted, when her attention brushed it, faintly bitter.

Bitter. In a room full of food she hadn't tasted yet.

By the time the magistrate's party arrived — Corvel, grey-bearded and loud, with two clerks and a bodyguard who looked bored — Isandro had already decided two contradictory things. First: she would say nothing. She was Marla Vetch, a widow with a number in a ledger, and widows who noticed wards in dining rooms ended up in ledgers of a different kind. Second: she would taste everything that went to that table before it left the pass, and if the bitterness showed itself anywhere but in her imagination, she would break her first rule so fast it wouldn't have time to hit the floor.

Brannick loaded her tray himself. "Quail for the magistrate. Stuffed with fig and chestnut, the good batch. Go careful, Marla — Corvel tips like a man apologizing."

"I know," she said, and heard how much she knew, and covered it with a smile.

In the kitchen's heat she stood over the platter and did what she had spent twenty years doing and three months pretending she'd never done. She broke off a piece of the breast meat, dipped it in the fig glaze, and set it on her tongue.

Nothing. Rich, sweet, the chestnut's mealiness, salt, fat, a whisper of rosemary. Clean.

She tried the stuffing. Clean. The glaze alone, from the pot. Clean. The bread. The wine she wasn't allowed to touch but touched anyway, a fingertip's worth against her lower lip. Clean, clean, clean.

So it was the ward, then. Just the ward, humming its cheap little hum, and the bitterness was her own fear wearing a flavor. She exhaled, picked up the tray, and pushed through the door—

—and the bitterness bloomed.

Not in the food. In the air between the food and the table. She felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm: the quail steaming gently on its platter, the ward's hum lying over the alcove like a net, and drifting up from somewhere beneath the magistrate's place setting — under the table, under the floor, woven into the wood itself or into the mat beneath the plate — a thread of that same bitter note, thin as spider silk, patient as rot.

Poison that wasn't in the dish. Poison in the *place*.

Her arms nearly buckled under the tray. Not from its weight. From the arithmetic arriving all at once behind her eyes: someone had paid good money for hedge-ward work and laced it with toxin, which meant this wasn't a knife in an alley, wasn't passion or robbery. This was a message, or a purchase, or a policy. Magistrates in free cities were supposed to be untouchable — that was the whole point of free cities. If someone could buy Corvel's death over dinner at the Gilded Perch, then sanctuary had a price list, and someone had just paid it.

Sef's voice in her memory, low and tired, from the night they'd struck their alliance over a fire: *Every map has blank spaces, Isandro. The dangerous ones are the blanks somebody drew on purpose.*

She set the platter down in front of Corvel with steady hands. Her hands were always steady; that had never once been a comfort to her.

"Wine, magister?"

"Red. The good red, not whatever my clerk ordered." He laughed at his own joke. His clerks laughed. The bored bodyguard didn't laugh at anything.

Isandro poured. And stood there one heartbeat longer than a serving girl should, looking at the plate, at the mat beneath it, at the space where steam curled up off the quail and bent — bent, just slightly, the way candle flame had bent toward her finger in a dark kitchen in Hollowmere on the worst night of her life — bending away from the mat, curling aside from the bitter thread like water around a stone.

The poison wasn't in the food. It was rising. Slowly, invisibly, on the warmth of the dish, on the steam — a vapor caught in the ward's net, pooling over the magistrate's place like weather. He would breathe it in across the length of a long supper, a little at a time, and by morning it would look like his heart, and no taster in Callowmark would ever have been able to catch it, because no taster tastes air.

No taster except one whose whole life had been a slow education in exactly this kind of quiet.

*Say nothing,* said the part of her that had watched Tam dragged away in chains from a stable window while she did nothing. *You are a number in a ledger. You have three people sleeping above a cooper's shop who need you unknown.*

Corvel lifted his fork.

*Say nothing,* said the part of her that remembered Dame Ovelin's kind eyes measuring her across a private tasting table, kindness laid out like a trap, and how she had lied for the first time in her life and hated how easily it came — because lies were what survival cost, and she'd paid, and paying changed you.

He brought the fork to his mouth.

"No," Isandro heard herself say.

The word came out wrong — too loud, too raw, a serving girl's voice cracking across a magistrate's dinner like a dropped plate. The dice stopped. The wool merchant stopped. Even the hearth seemed to lean in.

Corvel looked up, fork still raised, eyebrows climbing into the deep furrows of a man accustomed to being obeyed. "I beg your pardon?"

"You'll pardon me, magister." Her heart was going like a mill-race. Her voice, absurdly, had gone calm — the kitchen-calm, the tasting-calm, the voice she used when the broth might kill a regent and everyone was watching her face for permission to eat. "But I've worked tables since before I could count change, and I know what a spoiled dish looks like. That bird's off. The glaze turned. I won't serve it, and I can't let you eat it, and if you want my head for it, take it after you've let me bring you the pike instead."

"It smells magnificent," said the older clerk.

"Aye," said Isandro, "it does. That's rather the problem, isn't it."

A silence. Somewhere in the corner the dice resumed, tentatively, like rain deciding whether to start again. Corvel looked at the quail. Looked at her. And Isandro watched him do the thing that powerful men do — weigh the scene, weigh the witnesses, weigh whether a widow's insolence was worth a moment's theater — and she watched something else move underneath the weighing, some old animal instinct that had kept him alive through however many years of municipal knives.

"Brannick," Corvel called, without taking his eyes off her. "Your girl says my supper's poisoned."

From the kitchen door, wiping his hands, genuinely alarmed: "On my mother's grave, magister, that bird came off my own spit—"

"So it did," Isandro said quietly. "Which is why whoever wanted you dead didn't bother with your supper, magister. They bothered with your chair."

The words were out before she could stop them, and the instant they were out she understood the size of the mistake, the way you understand a broken dam only after the water's through. Corvel went very still. The bored bodyguard was suddenly not bored at all — he'd shifted his weight forward, hand resting easy on his belt, watching her the way hunters watch brush that has just moved.

"The mat," Isandro said, because stopping now would be worse than finishing. "Under the plate. There's a preparation worked into it — cheap warding, the kind sold at market stalls, but it's been soaked with something bitter. Heat from the dish draws it up. You'd breathe it all evening and feel like a fool with indigestion, and in a day or two your heart would stop, and no physician in Callowmark would find a mark on you." She swallowed. "I can't prove any of that. I only know what I know."

"And what is it you know, girl?" Corvel asked softly. "What's your name?"

"Marla Vetch." The lie came easily. She hated how easily it came. Some things followed you out of valleys.

"Marla Vetch." He turned the plate toward himself, studied the mat beneath it, sniffed once — a politician's sniff, tasting the air for danger the ordinary way, the way that killed him. Then he lifted the mat with two fingers, held it to the light, and his face did something complicated. "There's a discoloration here. Faint. Like old wine."

"Bitter," Isandro said. "If you dare."

Corvel dared. He touched the stained spot to the tip of his tongue, and Isandro watched a magistrate of the free city of Callowmark go grey in front of forty witnesses.

* * *

They cleared the dining room. They cleared it badly — rumor moves faster than constables — but by the time the city watch arrived in force, the Gilded Perch's common room held only Corvel, his clerks, the bodyguard, four watchmen, Brannick wringing his apron, and one serving girl who could not stop noticing that the ward's hum had died the moment the mat was bagged in oilcloth, leaving her suddenly, nakedly aware of how loud a room was when it wasn't pressing down on her.

An officer with a ledger asked questions. Isandro gave answers — Marla Vetch, six years a cook, trained in a great house in the country, no family living, no, she'd never seen such a preparation before, yes, sometimes you can just tell. Each lie landed soft as snowfall. Each one weighed a little more than the last.

Corvel watched her give them. He watched the way Dame Ovelin had watched, and the resemblance was the worst part — the intelligent, patient attention of a person adding columns. When the officers finally released her, he beckoned her close with two fingers.

"You saved my life," he said. "I don't enjoy owing debts, so we'll settle it tonight. Name something."

"Nothing, magister."

"Everyone names something."

"Then let me name it later," Isandro said, "when I know what I need."

His eyes narrowed — pleasure and suspicion in equal measure, the exact mixture she'd seen on the regent's face in another life. "Careful, girl. People who know what they need are either very wise or very dangerous, and I haven't decided which you are." He waved her off like smoke. "Go home. Don't leave the city. I may have more questions, and I pay better than anyone you'll ever serve."

She walked back through Callowmark's lamplit streets with her heart still hammering and her mind doing what it always did when terror finished with it: making lists. The mat would be traced to whoever sold it. The seller would be found or wouldn't. Either way, questions would radiate outward from the Gilded Perch like rings from a dropped stone, and somewhere in those rings was a person who had paid for a magistrate's quiet death and would now want to know which serving girl had ruined the purchase. And somewhere beyond the rings, following her own slower circles across the country, was a woman in a grey hood who collected exactly this kind of story — a taster, fled from Hollowmere, turning up where she shouldn't be, knowing things she shouldn't know.

Veyra. Captain Veyra, who hunted with a humming device and a courteous voice.

Isandro climbed the stairs to the cooper's shop and stood outside the door a moment, listening. Four people breathing. Sef's maps rustling as he turned in sleep. Perrin murmuring to the locks in his dreams — he talked to them even asleep, a low contented litany of tumblers and hinges. Wren's silence, which was its own kind of sound.

She could tell them. She should tell them. Tomorrow, she promised herself. Tomorrow, over breakfast, calmly, with the door barred.

She opened the door, and Perrin sat bolt upright in the dark, wide awake, eyes enormous.

"You're loud tonight," he whispered. "You're louder than I've ever heard you. Something happened and it's still stuck to you." He rubbed his eyes. "Also there's a woman downstairs asking the cooper about you. Grey hood. She's being polite." He shivered. "Polite doors are the worst ones. They don't rattle at all."

Down below, through the floorboards, Isandro heard the cooper's sleepy voice, and another voice over it — level, courteous, asking its questions one at a time, each one a small key turning in a lock she couldn't see.

She knew that voice. She had served it soup once, and tested it three times.

"Get Sef up," Isandro whispered, already reaching for the maps in the coat lining. "We're leaving before she finishes her conversation."
