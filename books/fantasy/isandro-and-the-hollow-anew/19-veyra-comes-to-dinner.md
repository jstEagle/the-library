## 19. Veyra Comes to Dinner

The summons came with the evening bread.

Isandro was scrubbing the great kettle when Marta, the innkeeper's wife, appeared in the kitchen doorway holding a folded slip of paper as though it might bite. She was a broad, capable woman who had survived three husbands and one fire, and Isandro had never seen her look uncertain before.

"Table for one," Marta said. "Reserved. Paid in advance." She held out the paper. "He asked for you by name."

Isandro dried her hands slowly. *By name.* In Callowmark she was Maren Cotter, a widow from the river country, registered laborer, brand hidden beneath her sleeve and a story worn like an apron. Nobody asked for Maren Cotter by name because nobody knew Maren Cotter existed.

She unfolded the note. The handwriting was precise, unhurried, each letter placed like a soldier on a parade ground:

*Captain Veyra requests the pleasure of supper. She has heard the kitchen here is honest.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The word *honest* sat on the page like a blade laid flat across a table — not threatening, exactly. Just present. Just waiting to be picked up.

"When?" Isandro asked. Her voice came out steady. She was grateful for that, at least. Ten years of tasting other people's poison had taught her how to keep her face still while everything underneath screamed.

"Sunset," said Marta. "He said—" She stopped, corrected herself. "*She*. The captain's a woman?"

"So I'm told."

"You've met her?"

"No." Isandro took the note back, read it again. *Honest.* "But she's met me. Or she's been asking after me long enough that meeting me is just a formality."

Marta's jaw tightened. She was one of perhaps four people in Callowmark who had guessed what Isandro was — not the brand, not the false name, but the thing underneath, the tongue that knew. She'd guessed and said nothing, which in this city was its own kind of loyalty. "You could run," she said quietly. "Back door, the tanner's yard, the culvert under Tanner's Row. You'd have an hour's start."

"And she'd have my description, my name, and every gate watched by morning." Isandro folded the note into quarters, then eighths, until it was a small hard square she could close her fist around. "No. If Captain Veyra wants supper, Captain Veyra gets supper. Better at my table than hers."

"It isn't your table."

"It will be tonight."

Marta left. Isandro stood alone in the kitchen with the kettle dripping behind her and the light going amber through the window, and she let herself feel it — all of it, the cold drop of it, the way her stomach clenched around nothing. Fear was a flavor. She'd learned that young. It sat on the back of the tongue, metallic, faintly sweet, and no amount of bread or beer washed it away. She had tasted fear in the regent's broth once, years ago, when Dame Ovelin had watched her too closely over the rim of a silver spoon. She had tasted it again in Hollowmere the day the grey hoods took Tam.

This was that taste. Stronger. Closer.

She got to work.

* * *

If she was going to die tonight, she decided, it would not be because of a badly made soup.

That was the joke she told herself while her hands moved through the familiar liturgy: bones roasted until they clicked brown, onions sweated down to translucence, thyme stripped from stems between thumbnail and forefinger. The joke helped. The work helped more. A kitchen was the one country where Isandro Dunmore had never been a stranger, and if she was going to sit across from the woman hunting her family, she would do it standing on her own ground.

Three times she tested the soup.

The first time was habit. She dipped the silver spoon — her own spoon, kept wrapped in cloth in her kit since Hollowmere, the only possession she'd carried out of the valley besides her grandmother's ring — and drew a mouthful across her tongue, and found what she always found: stock, salt, fat, the green whisper of thyme. Clean.

The second time was for the hunters. Grey hoods used certain poisons; Sef had catalogued them from old court records, back when he'd still had access to court records. Nerve-sleepers that slowed the breath. Bitterweed that stopped a heart politely, mid-laugh, so the victim seemed merely tired. She tasted for each. Nothing. The soup was clean.

The third time was for herself, and this was the strange one, because by now she should not have needed it. Since the ford — since the hunter who had died with her knife in him, since the fever nights when Sef burned and Wren worked and Isandro had stood in the doorway feeling something move under her skin like water under ice — her tongue had been changing. Flavors arrived louder now. Salt rang like a struck bell. Bitterness had texture, layers, geography. And underneath everything, faint but growing nightly, there was a new sense altogether: a sense of *intent*, as though food remembered the hands that made it and told her so.

The soup told her about Marta's hands. Steady. Grieving, a little, over the price of saffron. Fond of the boy who swept the hearth. Clean hands. Clean soup.

She tasted it a fourth time anyway, because terror makes ritualists of us all, and then she ladled it into the best bowl the inn owned — white clay glazed blue at the rim, reserved for magistrates and funerals — and carried it out through the door into the common room.

* * *

Captain Veyra sat alone at the corner table with her back to the wall, which told Isandro she was professional, and her hood down, which told Isandro she wanted to be seen.

She was smaller than Isandro had imagined. Reports — Sef's careful intelligence, gathered from dockworkers and frightened refugees — had built her tall and grim, a grey shadow with a humming device and a ledger full of names. The woman at the table was compact, dark-haired, thirty perhaps, with a scar splitting one eyebrow and hands that rested on the tablecloth open and empty, deliberately, the way a duelist shows sheathed steel. Her grey cloak hung on the chair beside her. No device hummed. No soldiers filled the room.

Just her, and a second place set across from her, and a small pot of honeyed wine steaming between them.

"Maren Cotter," she said, and smiled. It was a good smile. That was the worst part. "Sit, please. I ordered for both of us. I hope that's not presumptuous."

"You paid in advance," Isandro said. "Presumptuous would have been paying after."

Something flickered in the captain's eyes — appreciation, quickly banked. She poured the honeyed wine herself, two cups, and pushed one across. Isandro looked at it. Looked at her.

Veyra lifted her own cup and drank first, a long unhurried swallow, then set it down and turned it so Isandro could see the dregs. "You'll want to check it anyway," she said. "I would, in your position."

"In my position," Isandro said, sitting, "I'd want to know how much you know."

"Ah." Veyra folded her hands. "Straight to the stock. Good. I hate a long simmer." She leaned back. "Here's what I know, Maren Cotter — and we can use that name as long as it's useful to you, I'm not cruel. I know a food-taster fled Hollowmere the night my colleagues collected a stable-boy named Tam. I know she crossed the mill-race, which no ordinary runner thinks of, because ordinary runners don't know that running water confuses a resonance device. Someone taught her that, or she learned it, or—" and here the captain paused, delicately, "—she *is* it. I know she traveled with a disgraced cartographer whose maps were confiscated twice and bought back twice, which is a very expensive habit for a poor man. I know she was seen at the Marrowfen crossing with a hedge-witch, and at the west ford, where one of my hunters died."

The room went quiet around them. Not silent — the inn murmured on, dice clicking, someone laughing near the fire — but their table had drawn a border around itself, and inside it the air was different.

"I know," Veyra continued, softer now, "that the magistrate Aldric sent me to profile described his poisoner as someone whose tongue could find a needle of bitterweed in a barrel of honey. And I know that last month, in this inn, a serving woman saved that same magistrate's life by refusing to serve him a dish she claimed smelled off. He ate elsewhere. Three men who ate at the next table spent the night vomiting blood." She tilted her head. "Smell. Interesting word for it, don't you think?"

Isandro kept her face pleasant. Kept her hands loose on the table. Inside, her heart was doing arithmetic at speed: *how much does she actually have, how much is bluff, what does she want.* "You've come a long way to ask a serving woman about her nose," she said.

"I've come a long way to eat your soup." Veyra nodded toward the kitchen door, where the tureen waited. "They tell me it's good. They also tell me you test everything three times. A woman who tests three times is either very careful or very afraid. I'm curious which."

"Both," Isandro said. "Aren't you? Careful and afraid. It's why you're alive."

For a moment — just a moment — the captain's composure shifted, like a card turned face-up and turned back. Then she laughed, low and genuine, and Isandro hated how warm it sounded. "Yes," Veyra said. "Exactly that. Both. Every day since I was nineteen." She gestured at the kitchen. "Serve me, then. I've been looking forward to this all week, and I don't mean the soup."

* * *

Isandro served her.

It was, absurdly, the most careful service of her life. She filled the bowl at the table rather than carrying it full — showing the pour, showing the steam, showing the spoon she used to skim the surface and touch to her own lips before offering it. Every gesture said: *watch me prove it's safe.* Every gesture was also, she knew, a performance, and Veyra watched it the way Sef studied maps: completely, missing nothing, drawing conclusions.

"Beautiful," Veyra said, when the bowl was before her. She did not eat yet. She turned the bowl a quarter-turn, examining the glaze, the surface, the little eddy of fat at the rim. "Do you know what I admire about tasters? Real ones, the old kind, before the registries. Everyone thinks their gift is knowing poison. But that's not it. Anyone can learn arsenic's fingerprint. What a true taster knows is *people*. The poison is just where the person shows up." She picked up her spoon. "So. Tell me about yourself, Maren Cotter. Widow, yes? River country?"

"Husband drowned," Isandro said. "Two years back. I cook because grief needs somewhere to go."

"Mm. And where did you learn to skim a bowl like that? Left hand braced, wrist turned out, testing the rim first for contact poison before ever lifting the spoon?" Veyra raised the spoon, paused. "Because that's palace form. That's Hollowmere. I served a season at the regent's table before I took the grey hood, you see. I've watched tasters work. You move like one."

The trap closed so gently Isandro almost admired it. Not a question about magic, not a demand for confession — just an observation, offered like a compliment, waiting for her to accept it or stumble.

"A dead husband teaches economy," Isandro said. "Every motion costs something. Why waste any?"

Veyra held her gaze a moment longer. Then she ate.

Isandro watched her do it — the first spoonful slow, deliberate, eyes never leaving Isandro's face; the second faster; the third with actual pleasure, shoulders dropping a fraction, the professional vigilance giving way to something human. It was good soup. Isandro knew it was good soup. She had made it three times in her mind before making it once in the pot, and some traitor part of her was pleased — genuinely pleased — to watch it land.

"Well?" Veyra said, after a silence. "Aren't you going to ask?"

"Ask what?"

"What I want. Everyone at this table has been wondering since I walked in. You, the innkeeper pretending to count coins by the fire, the boy at the hearth who keeps looking over." She smiled again, thinner now. "Ask."

"All right." Isandro folded her napkin. "What do you want, Captain?"

"I want to understand you." Veyra set down her spoon. For the first time she looked tired — not performed, Isandro thought, but real, a weariness that lived deep in the bone. "Do you know what my orders are? Bring in the gifted alive. Do you know why? Because the king needs them. Needs their blood, their resonance, whatever it is they carry — he won't say, and I've stopped inventing reasons, because the reasons I invented were kinder than the truth probably is. I've delivered forty-one people to that need. Forty-one." She looked at her hands. "I used to tell myself they were needed. Now I tell myself I'm finding out what 'needed' means before I deliver number forty-two."

"That's a comfortable story," Isandro said quietly. "Comfortable stories are how good people carry bad work."

"Yes," Veyra agreed. "I know. I wrote it myself." She looked up, and her eyes were clear and terrible and honest. "So here is what happens tonight, Maren Cotter, or Isandro, or whoever you are. I am going to finish this excellent soup. Then I am going to leave a coin on this table that overpays by a factor of ten, and walk out that door, and report that the serving woman of this inn is exactly what she appears to be — a widow with a careful nose and no gift worth a device's hum. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless you give me a reason not to." Veyra leaned forward, and her voice dropped to something almost intimate, almost kind. "I watched you pour that bowl. I watched you test the rim, the steam, the spoon. I watched you decide — *decide*, in half a heartbeat — whether to poison me. Don't think I missed it. You weighed my life against yours and chose grace, and grace is rarer than magic, in my experience." She sat back. "So. One question. Answer it honestly, and I'll take my answer to my grave and my report to my king. Lie to me, and I'll know — I always know — and this inn burns by morning with everyone in it."

The common room murmured on. Dice. Laughter. The fire settling. Somewhere upstairs, Sef lay fevered and sleeping, Wren keeping watch with herbs and stubbornness, Perrin — dear, impossible Perrin — pretending to sweep a floor he'd swept four times already, listening with those door-hearing ears to every word that passed beneath him.

One question. Everything hanging on it.

"Ask," Isandro said.

Veyra studied her for a long moment. Outside, the lamplighter was working down the street, and the light through the window went from amber to blue by degrees, and the soup steamed between them like a treaty neither side trusted.

"If I asked you to come with me," the captain said, "tonight, quietly, no chains, no devices — just you, walking beside me to the capital, to stand in front of the king and show him what you are — would the people you're hiding be safer? Yes or no. Think carefully. I'll know a lie."

And Isandro understood, finally, what this dinner was. Not an interrogation. An offer. Veyra had hunted her across half a country, assembled her out of rumors the way Sef assembled coastlines, and somewhere along the road the hunt had become something else — curiosity, respect, maybe even the beginning of that dangerous thing the captain's whole life was built to prevent: belief. She was asking Isandro to trust her. She was asking in the only language either of them spoke fluently — the language of the table, of tested food, of grace performed under threat.

Isandro thought of Tam, dragged from the stables while she watched from a window and did nothing. She thought of Wren's sister on the pyre. Of forty-one names in a grey ledger. Of Sef's maps sold to fund their passage, of the brand under her sleeve still tender as a promise.

She thought of the Door of Anew, patient under the mountains, and the moon rising toward the Stone Choir.

"No," she said.

Just that. No elaboration, no plea, no lie. She held Veyra's eyes and let the word stand there, naked and complete.

Veyra exhaled slowly. Something in her face settled, the way a scale settles when the weights are finally right. "Honest," she said softly. "Of course. You'd taste the lie before I finished serving it." She finished her wine, rose, and left a coin on the table — gold, far too much, gleaming like a small accusation. At the door she paused, cloak over her arm, and looked back.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the soup was the best I've had since the regent's table. And for what it's further worth — the king's men reached the garrison at Harrow Ford yesterday. They're moving on the free cities within the fortnight, not the month your cartographer's charts predict. Whatever you and your friends are planning—" her eyes flicked, briefly, knowingly, toward the stairs, toward the fevered room above, "—plan faster."

Then she was gone, the door swinging shut behind her, and the common room breathed again all at once, noise rushing back like water into a channel.

Isandro sat alone at the corner table with the gold coin and the cold soup and her heart hammering out a rhythm she hadn't felt since Hollowmere. After a moment, Perrin came down the stairs two at a time, wild-eyed, and skidded to a stop beside her.

"She knows," he whispered. "Did she— she knows, doesn't she? About us? About the Door?"

"She knows enough," Isandro said. She picked up the coin. It was warm from the tablecloth, or from Veyra's hand, or from her own fear. "And she gave us a fortnight instead of a net."

Perrin stared at her. "Why?"

"Because I fed her honestly," Isandro said, "and she's starving for something truer than her king." She stood, pressing the coin into his palm. "Go wake Wren. Tell her we leave for the Stone Choir at first light — not in a fortnight. Tonight, if the horses can be ready."

Above them, through the floorboards, she could hear Sef turning in fever, muttering fragments of maps and mileages, and somewhere far north, beneath mountains none of their charts named, she imagined — she *knew* — the Door of Anew beginning, very softly, to sing.
