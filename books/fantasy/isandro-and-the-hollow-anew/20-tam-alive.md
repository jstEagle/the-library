## 20. Tam, Alive

The road taught Tam three things before it taught him how to die slowly.

First: that hunger was a language, and after nine days he spoke it fluently. Second: that the grey hoods did not ride at night, because their humming devices needed stillness and dark made the needles wander. Third — and this was the one that kept him moving, one boot dragging through the mud of the cart-tracks, his ribs a cage of broken laths — that a boy who could hear doors could also hear when they were about to open behind him.

He had heard the stable gate of Hollowmere groan in its frame two full seconds before the hunters kicked it. He had heard the lock of the grain cellar sigh as Veyra's men forced it, and had already slipped out through the loose board behind the flour sacks. And on the ninth night, half-crawling along the drainage ditch outside Callowmark's eastern wall, he heard the city gates begin to wake for morning trade — ten thousand iron teeth yawning open all at once, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat — and he wept into the ditch water without quite knowing why.

Not because of the sound. Because of what the sound meant.

Walls. Walls meant people. People meant someone who might listen.

* * *

They found him at the fish-market steps, or rather he collapsed onto them, and the fishwives stepped around him the way people step around weather. He had learned in Hollowmere that servants were furniture, and furniture that bled was worse than useless. But Callowmark was not Hollowmere. A woman with herring scales on her knuckles crouched beside him, said something sharp in the river-accent he was only beginning to untangle, and pressed a heel of bread into his hand whether he could chew it or not.

"Isandro Dunmore," he rasped. It came out wrong, half-swallowed. He tried again. "The taster. From Hollowmere. She'll be here. I have to — she has to hear —"

"You're raving, lad."

"Aldric." The name cost him everything left in his chest. "Aldric marches early. Not spring. Now. On the free cities. Weeks, maybe less. Someone has to tell her."

The fishwife looked at him a long moment — at the hollows under his eyes, the way his hands shook around the bread, the brand-shaped burn scar on his wrist where a hunter's device had gripped him too long while asking questions he hadn't answered. Then she stood, whistled down a boy with a handcart, and said, "Inn called the Gilded Spoon, by the tannery row. There's a kitchen girl there with valley vowels. Go on, Osric. Before he dies on my steps and I have to explain it to the harbor watch."

Tam remembered very little of the handcart ride. Mostly the smell of fish brine and tar, and the sound of the city opening above him — every shop-gate, every warehouse door, every hundred thousand hinges singing their small iron songs as Callowmark woke up — and thinking, deliriously, that Isandro would hate this noise and love this noise, both at once, the way she'd loved and hated the palace kitchens.

Then the handcart stopped, and a door opened, and he heard her voice.

* * *

She came down the inn stairs two at a time. He knew her footsteps before he saw her face; you spend two years mucking stables beneath a person's window, you learn the weight of their tread. She looked different — thinner, harder, hair cut short and ragged like she'd done it herself with a knife — but when she dropped to her knees on the tannery-row cobblestones and took his face in her hands, her palms were exactly the same. Cool. Steady. Taster's hands.

"Tam." Just his name. Nothing else fit inside it.

"I got out," he said, and started laughing, which hurt so badly it turned into something else, and then he was crying against her shoulder in front of God and the fish carts and the whole waking city, fourteen years old and sobbing like the child he'd been pretending not to be since the grey hoods came. "I got out, Isandro, I got out, I didn't tell them anything, I didn't—"

"I know." Her voice cracked clean through the middle. "I know you didn't. I saw them take you and I did nothing. I stood at a window and did nothing, and you—"

"You ran," he said fiercely, pulling back to look at her, gripping her sleeve with fingers that wouldn't stop shaking. "You ran. That's not nothing. That's the opposite of nothing. Do you know what I thought, all those nights in their wagon? I thought: she got away. That was the whole prayer. Not that I'd live. That you did."

Isandro made a sound he had never heard from her — a small, wounded thing, like a door hinge that had rusted shut for years finally giving way — and held him again, and this time she was crying too, quietly, the way she did everything, as if even grief had to pass her tongue first and be tested safe.

* * *

They put him in a bed upstairs that smelled of lavender and old fever-dreams, and Wren Ostara worked over him for an hour with poultices that burned and teas that tasted like a forest floor had been boiled in anger. Sef Ardenne sat in the corner drawing maps on scraps, because apparently that man drew maps the way other men breathed. Perrin sat cross-legged at the foot of the bed and stared at Tam with enormous unblinking eyes, and finally said:

"Your door hums."

"What?"

"The room door. Everything locked hums. Yours is scared." Perrin shrugged. "I told it you weren't going anywhere."

Tam decided, in his feverish state, that he liked the strange boy immensely.

It was evening before Isandro sent the others out and sat beside him, and the lamplight caught the new hardness in her face, and he understood suddenly that she had been carrying something heavy across a country and had not yet set it down.

"The armies," she said. "Tell me all of it. Every word you heard. Don't soften it."

So he told her. The wagon, the questions, the device that gripped his wrist until the skin charred. The camp at Thornford Crossing — thousands of tents, supply trains stretching past sight, siege engines on ox-carts. The captain, Veyra, coming herself to question him, calm as a winter lake, asking about a taster who fled. And then the night the guards drank bad wine — Tam had smelled it, of course, smelled the rotgut bitterness of something fouled, and said nothing, and let them drink — and slipped their chains in the dark, and ran east with nothing but stolen boots and a boy's knowledge of how to move unseen through places built to hold horses.

"And the marching," Isandro said. "When."

"They were breaking camp when I escaped. Moving north. Toward the free cities." He swallowed. "Veyra said — I heard her say — 'the cities will fold or burn by first snow.' Isandro, they're not waiting for spring. They think the Door is a story. They're just coming to take the cities."

Isandro was quiet a long time. Outside, Callowmark clattered and sang its ten thousand small iron songs. When she spoke again, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone reading an ingredient list.

"How long do you think we have?"

"Weeks. Maybe less if the roads are good." He hesitated. "There's more. The night before I ran, I heard the device-needles singing near the command tent. Perrin says locked things hum. Well — those devices hum too, louder than any lock I ever heard, and that night they were all pointed the same direction. South-west. Into the mountains." He met her eyes. "They know something's out there. They know where to look."

Isandro went very still. Then she rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the city lights with her arms folded tight, and Tam watched her back and understood that whatever she was deciding, it had already begun.

"Get some sleep," she said at last, turning, and her smile was the old kitchen smile, warm and crooked and completely unconvincing. "Tomorrow we wake the others. There's a vote to be had."

"A vote?"

"You'll see." She blew out the lamp. In the dark, her hand found his hair and ruffled it once, the way she used to when he was twelve and afraid of thunder. "You did good, Tam. You saved yourself, and then you saved us. Both times, nobody else did it for you."

He fell asleep smiling, and dreamed of doors — thousands of them, all unlocked, all swinging wide — and underneath their singing, far to the south-west beneath the mountains, something vast and patient beginning, very softly, to hum.
