## 21. The Vote at the Hearth

The safehouse was a tanner's loft above a shuttered dye-works in the low quarter of Marrowfen, and it smelled of oak gall and old smoke — a smell Wren Ostara had loved since she was small enough to sleep under her mother's worktable. It was not a clean smell. It was a working smell, an honest one, and after three days on the road with four other bodies and their assorted wounds and fears, honest smells were worth more than gold.

She had the fire going before full dark, because a hearth did half her work for her. People told the truth to fires. They sat down beside them, and their shoulders dropped, and whatever they'd been carrying all day slid off them into the coals where no one had to look at it.

Sef Ardenne sat closest, which surprised no one; his wound had gone from angry to sullen, the flesh around the stitches hot and tight as a drumhead. He held his maps on his knees like a man holding a sleeping child he wasn't sure would wake. Perrin lay curled against Isandro's side with his head pillowed on her arm, eyes open, listening — always listening — to the locks and latches of the building below them, cataloguing the house's small iron voices the way other children counted sheep. Tam sat apart by the window, as he always did now, watching the street with the particular stillness of someone who had been taken once and meant never to be taken again. And Isandro —

Isandro was stirring a pot of barley and dried mutton with the concentration of a woman defusing something.

"You've tasted it four times," Wren said.

"Five." Isandro lifted the spoon, blew across it, and drank. Her face did nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer. "It's fine. The mutton's older than Perrin."

"I'm twelve," Perrin said without opening his eyes.

"The mutton's older than you," Isandro said, and ladled out bowls, and for a little while there was nothing in the loft but eating and the pop of the fire, and Wren let it last, because she knew what came next. She had known since noon, when Sef had unrolled his map across her kitchen table in Marrowfen proper and put his finger down on the Stone Choir, and she had watched five faces arrive at the same arithmetic at the same moment and go quiet about it.

There were two roads out of this room. Everyone knew it. Nobody wanted to be the first to say it aloud, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things had to be chosen, and chosen things could be regretted.

Wren set down her bowl. "Right," she said. "Vote."

* * *

It went the way she'd expected, which is to say it went badly.

"Coast," Sef said immediately. "Callowmark has a harbor. Harbors have ships. Ships go places Aldric's grey hoods cannot follow, because his writ ends at salt water. We buy passage with what's left of my map money, we sail south, and in a year none of us can point to Hollowmere on a chart." He shifted, and Wren saw him hide the wince — badly. Fever was coming up in him like a tide. "It's not cowardice. It's survival. Survival is not a dirty word."

"No one said it was," Isandro said quietly.

"You were thinking it."

"I was thinking that Callowmark registers people like me." Isandro turned her bowl slowly in her hands. "Branded. Leashed. Working the docks until the registry sells my contract to whoever pays. That's if Veyra doesn't find me first, and she will, because I've seen how she works and she does not stop. The coast isn't an exit, Sef. It's a slower door."

"A door nonetheless."

"A door with Aldric standing behind it."

Perrin sat up. He didn't say anything at first — he rarely led with words — but his whole body had gone taut, the way it did when some distant hinge groaned. "I heard them today," he said. "On the north road. The humming things. Four of them, maybe five, moving toward the river ford. They're not going to the coast. They're going east." He looked around the circle of faces, and his voice dropped to the flat certainty he saved for doors. "They know about the Choir too."

Silence. The fire cracked and settled.

"They can't," Sef said. "The Door is a rumor. A story hedge-witches tell—"

"It's not a rumor," Wren said. "You found it in your own charts, Ardenne. You showed me the pattern yourself. The coronation road, the way the erasure bends around certain stones like water around a keel. You believed it enough to sell your life's work to get us here." She let that sit. "Don't unpick your own stitching just because the thread frightens you."

"My stitching isn't what frightens me." Sef pressed the heel of his hand against his bandaged side. "What frightens me is racing an army to a door that may not exist, with a fevered cartographer, a branded fugitive, a boy who hears hinges, a dead man's sister—" he nodded at Tam, who flinched, "—and a healer who hasn't slept since the ford. And when we lose that race, we lose everything: the coast, the ships, the chance to run. We spend our one escape on a gamble."

"That's exactly right," Wren said. "That's the shape of it. So let's stop pretending it's a choice between safety and danger, because it isn't. There is no safety on either road. There's only the question underneath both roads, and nobody's asked it yet."

"Ask it, then," Isandro said.

Wren looked at her. Of all of them, Isandro Dunmore had changed the most since Marrowfen — or perhaps only become visible. In her kitchen, weeks ago, she had been a woman built entirely of careful motions: tasting, testing, tasting again. Now she sat with the firelight on the brand-scar at her wrist and her chin up, and she looked like someone who had decided, somewhere on the road, to stop being a vessel and start being a person.

So Wren asked it.

"Is a door an exit," she said, "or a beginning?"

* * *

Nobody answered at once. Good. Wren had spent twenty years learning that the answers that came fast were the ones people already owned, and the ones that took time were the ones that changed them.

"Say more," Isandro said finally.

"Sef thinks the Door is a way out. Through the erasure, back past the hollowing, out of Aldric's reach forever. Maybe that's true. Maybe we walk through and find green country and old names and no hunters, and we live out our lives in a Vessia that remembers itself." Wren poked the fire; sparks climbed. "But I've buried a lot of people who were looking for exits. My sister among them. And here's what I learned at their bedsides, all of them, every one: an exit is a place you go when you've already given up on wherever you are. Exits don't make anything. They just end things more politely than knives do."

"And a beginning?" Tam asked. His voice was rough. He hadn't spoken much since the hunters' wagon, and every word seemed to cost him toll.

"A beginning is a thing you walk toward because you want what's on the other side to exist. Not because you want what's on this side to stop." Wren spread her hands. "The Door of Anew. Think about the name. Not the Door of Escape. Not the Door of Return. *Anew.* Somebody named that door knowing what it was for. Doors like that don't lead away from anything. They lead toward."

"You think it leads to a country we can build," Sef said. Not mocking — testing. His cartographer's mind worrying at the claim like a dog at a knot.

"I think it leads to a country somebody has to choose," Wren said. "And I think that's harder and better than any ship."

"The free cities are real," Sef said. "I can draw you Callowmark's harbor right now, every quay, every warehouse. I can't draw you what's behind that Door. I'm a mapmaker, Ostara. I'm being asked to bet five lives on blank parchment."

"Yes," Wren said. "That's precisely what you're being asked." She leaned forward. "And here's the part you keep circling and won't land on, so I'll land it for you. If we run to the coast — if we take the exit — then Vessia dies. Properly, finally, with our blessing. Every gifted soul left inside the valleys gets hunted or leashed, the stones stay silent, the name stays struck, and we live warm and safe and small in a city that tolerates us, and every year the forgetting gets a little deeper, until even we forget what we ran from. The coast doesn't save Vessia, Ardenne. The coast attends its funeral and eats well at the wake."

The words landed hard; she saw them land, in each face, one after another. She didn't soften them. Softening was for people who hadn't watched a pyre.

"And if we go to the Choir," Tam said slowly, "and we win — what is it we win? A door. Just a door."

"A door is never just a door," Perrin said.

Everyone looked at him. He had gone still in the way that meant he was hearing something far off, some lock turning in some distant wall, but his eyes were present, and fierce, and fixed on the fire.

"Every door I ever heard," he said, "hummed different. Cellar doors hum low. Church doors hum high and thin. The big ones — gates, the city doors — they hum like they're full. Like there's more on one side than the other and the door knows it." He swallowed. "The thing under the mountains doesn't hum like a cellar. It hums like a gate. Like the biggest gate there ever was. Gates aren't for running through." He looked up at Isandro. "Gates are for coming home through."

* * *

The argument went on another hour, and Wren let it, because a vote taken too fast was just fear wearing a decision's clothes.

Sef fought for the coast with everything he had, and she gave him credit for it — he laid out tides and distances and the price of passage, the exact number of days between here and the Stone Choir versus here and the sea, and his voice went hoarse and his fever brightened his eyes until Wren wanted to put him to bed and dose him with willow bark and end the whole debate by force. But he wasn't arguing to win. She'd known him long enough now to see it. He was arguing because he needed someone to prove him wrong with more conviction than he could muster himself. He was a man whose king had erased his country and called it peace, and some splintered part of him still needed permission to believe in things that weren't on sanctioned charts.

Tam argued for the coast too, and Wren understood that one completely, and hated herself a little for understanding it. Tam had been in the grey hoods' wagon. Tam knew what losing looked like from the inside. When he spoke for running, he spoke for every soul who had ever decided that staying alive was the whole of virtue, and no one in that loft had the right to shame him for it. Least of all Wren, who had failed to save his namesake — no, not his namesake, she corrected herself, the stable-boy was Tam, there was only one Tam, and he was sitting right there having survived the thing she hadn't saved him from.

It was Isandro who turned it. Near midnight, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and Sef's arguments had worn themselves thin against Perrin's humming certainty, Isandro set her bowl aside and stood.

"When they took Tam," she said, "I watched from a window."

The room went very quiet.

"I stood there and I did nothing, because doing something would have cost me the only life I had, and I chose the life. I've thought about that window every day since. And I'll tell you what I've learned." Her voice didn't shake, though Wren could see the effort it cost, like watching someone carry water across ice. "The coast is that window. The coast is me at the glass, alive, safe, watching. Watching the valleys burn, watching the registries fill, watching Perrin get sold a contract, watching Wren's pyres stack up — alive, safe, watching. I am done being at windows." She looked around the circle. "If we go to the Choir and it kills us, then at least we die facing the thing instead of the wall. But I don't think it kills us. I think it's the first door in my whole life that opens onto something instead of closing onto something. And I want — " she stopped, and started again, quieter. "I want to find out what I am when I'm not corked. Even if it's terrible. Especially if it's terrible. I can't do that from a ship."

Then Tam, of all of them, broke first. "I'm not getting in another wagon," he said hoarsely. "Running got me a wagon. I'd rather walk toward it." And that was the coast's argument dead, because Tam was the reason they'd all been pretending the coast was mercy, and mercy had just refused them.

Sef closed his eyes. For a long moment he sat with his maps on his knees, and Wren watched him grieve — actually grieve — the safe, small, sensible life he'd been arguing for, the way you grieve a road not taken. Then he opened his eyes and said, in a voice scraped raw: "The Choir. Gods help us. The Choir." And began, immediately, compulsively, unrolling his chart to plan the route, because that was how Sef Ardenne said *yes* — he mapped it.

Perrin grinned like a lantern.

Wren let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the ford.

* * *

"So it's settled," Isandro said. "Four for the Door." She glanced at Wren. "You haven't voted."

Haven't I, Wren thought. Aloud she said, "I voted weeks ago, girl, when I agreed to guide you at all. I just hadn't told you my price yet." She rose, joints cracking, and went to her pack by the wall — the one she guarded the way Sef guarded his maps — and brought back a small clay jar, sealed with wax, wrapped in linen gone soft with handling. She set it on the hearthstone where the firelight touched it, and the room stilled around it the way rooms still around graves.

"What's that?" Perrin asked, though Wren suspected he already knew. Boys who heard doors probably heard jars too.

"My sister's ashes." Wren kept her voice level, the healer's voice, the one that reported hard facts gently. "Maren Ostara. Burned eleven years ago this winter, in the square at Hollowmere, for hiding gifted children in my mother's old stillroom. I was meant to be on that pyre beside her. I got out with this jar and nothing else." She crouched by the fire. "I've carried her through two purges and three borders. I told myself I was keeping her safe. That was a lie. I was keeping her waiting. Because there's one place in this world — or under it, or however that Door works — where a name that's been struck can be spoken again. And if the Door of Anew is what Perrin says it is, then Maren goes through it. That's my price. That's why I'm walking to the Stone Choir with a fevered mapmaker and a branded taster and two children the world has already tried to eat once." She looked up at their faces, all of them lit orange and solemn. "Not to escape. To begin. Someone has to carry the dead into the new country, or the new country will be built by people with nothing to remember."

No one spoke. Then Isandro reached over and put her hand flat on the hearthstone, beside the jar — not touching it, just near it, the way you stand near grief to tell it it's not alone. One by one the others did the same, Sef wincing as he leaned, Tam slow and deliberate, Perrin last, his small palm dwarfed by theirs.

"Well," Wren said, and her voice cracked despite twenty years of practice, and she let it. "That's the vote, then. All five."

She banked the fire low and sent them to their bedrolls — Sef dosed with willow bark and ordered flat onto his back, Perrin tucked against Isandro's side where he'd appointed himself, Tam by the window where he needed to be. She took first watch, because her body had forgotten how to sleep before dawn anyway.

Near the black hour, Isandro stirred and came to sit beside her, blanket around her shoulders, and for a while neither of them said anything. The dye-works ticked below. Somewhere east, under mountains, a door hummed its patient gate-hum, and somewhere behind them, grey hoods moved along dark roads toward the same stone.

"Do you really believe it?" Isandro asked at last. "A beginning?"

Wren watched the embers breathe. "I believe doors are like people," she said. "They become what you bring to them. Bring fear, and it's an exit. Bring the dead and the living and a jar of ashes and a boy who hears hinges—" she shrugged. "Bring all that, and it can hardly be anything but a beginning."

Isandro nodded slowly. Then she frowned, tilting her head — and Wren saw Perrin sit bolt upright in the same instant, across the loft, eyes wide in the dark.

"What is it?" Wren whispered. "What do you hear?"

Perrin's voice came out thin and certain as wire. "The road," he said. "Someone's on the coronation road. A lot of someones. And they're not hurrying." He swallowed. "They don't need to hurry. They already know where we're going."

East, beyond the walls and the fens and the sleeping town, the night felt suddenly, enormously thin — like a door about to be knocked on from the wrong side.
