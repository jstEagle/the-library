## 22. Leaving Callowmark

Perrin heard the net closing before anyone else did.

He was lying on the roof of the Gilded Kettle, in the gap between the chimney and the water barrel where the slates held the day's warmth, listening to the city the way other people listened to music. Callowmark at dusk was a thousand conversations happening at once — hinges complaining, bolts sliding home, the great chain on the harbor gate groaning like an old man getting out of bed. Every lock in the city had a voice, and Perrin had spent three weeks learning them the way Isandro had spent three weeks learning which merchants watered their wine.

That was how he knew. The locks were changing their tune.

All day the city's locks had been singing their usual evening song — the small satisfied *click* of ten thousand doors shutting out the dark. But tonight, underneath the clicking, there was something else. A new sound, low and wrong, like a note played on a string that wasn't meant to be plucked. It came from the direction of the Registry, and it was moving. Spreading outward through the streets the way spilled water finds every crack in a floor.

He sat up so fast the slates rattled.

*They're rekeying the locks,* he thought, and the thought tasted like copper. *They're changing every lock in Callowmark, and they're doing it tonight.*

He slid down the drainpipe — he'd learned the Kettle's pipes the first week, the way he'd learned everything about the inn, quietly and without asking — and came through the kitchen window just as Isandro was setting a tray of bread on the board. She looked up. She always knew when he came through a window instead of a door; he'd stopped trying to figure out how.

"They're doing it tonight," he said. "The locks. All of them. There's a new sound coming out of the Registry, and it's walking."

The kitchen went still. Sef looked up from his maps, which were spread across two pushed-together tables and weighted down with teacups, salt cellars, and one very patient cat. Wren set down the pestle she'd been grinding. Tam, who had been asleep in the corner by the oven for two days and would be asleep for two more before his body forgave him for the road, didn't stir — and that, more than anything, made Perrin's chest go tight. Tam couldn't run. Not yet.

"Walking how?" Sef asked, already on his feet.

"Street by street. Like — like someone's going door to door with new keys. Every lock they touch sings different after. I can hear the wave of it. It's past Tanner's Row. It'll be here before midnight." He swallowed. "It's a net, isn't it. That's what a net sounds like."

Nobody answered him right away, and Perrin had learned that the pauses were always worse than the answers.

It was Wren who finally spoke, in her flat way that meant she'd already done the arithmetic nobody wanted done. "A rekeying order. They change the locks on every registered door in the city, and every gifted soul who's registered has to present themselves for the new key. In person. At their registered address." She looked at Isandro. "You registered under a false name at a false address."

"And the new key won't come," Isandro said quietly. "And when it doesn't come, someone comes instead."

"The Grey Hoods," Perrin said, because someone had to say it. "Veyra's hoods. The ones who've been asking about the inn. The ones who asked about the soup."

He saw Isandro's hand go, very briefly, to the place below her collarbone where the registry brand sat under her shirt. She did that sometimes. He'd never asked what it felt like. He wasn't sure he wanted to know.

"Then we're leaving tonight," Isandro said. "All of us."

Sef's hands flattened on his maps. "The roads out of Callowmark — there are three. The coast road south, the river barge north, and the mountain track east. If Veyra's smart, and she is, she'll have watchers on all three by now. A rekeying isn't just a net, it's a *clock*. It tells her exactly how long we have before we know we're caught."

"Then we don't take a road," Perrin said.

Four faces turned to him. He felt his ears go hot. He hated talking in front of all of them at once — words came out of him sideways and wrong when he was looked at — but the city was singing to him right now, ten thousand locks murmuring their fear, and he knew something none of them could know.

"There's a way out that isn't a road," he said. "The old tannery, down by the south wall. It's been shut for thirty years. Nobody's rekeyed it tonight because nobody knows it's a door. But it *is* one. It hums. It goes under the wall — there's a drain, an old one, big enough to walk through, and it comes out in the marsh past the wall where the watchers won't be because the marsh eats boots and nobody's stupid enough to—"

"Perrin." Isandro crouched down to his level, the way she did, which he both loved and resented. "Breathe."

He breathed.

"Show us," she said.

* * *

They went out through the Kettle's cellar, which Perrin had scouted on his second day in the city because a cellar was a door and doors were his business. Marta the innkeeper stood at the top of the steps with a lantern, and she didn't say anything for a long moment. Then she pressed a sack into Isandro's hands — bread, hard cheese, a jar of honey, a flask of the good brandy — and said, "The magistrate you saved drinks here every Thursday. I'll tell him you went north, on the barges."

"Marta—" Isandro started.

"Don't." The old woman's voice cracked like a whip and then went soft. "You fed my city when it would have let you starve. Go. And take the boy's hand in the dark. He'll pretend he doesn't want it. Take it anyway."

Perrin felt his whole face burn. He was twelve, not a child.

But in the dark of the cellar tunnel, when the walls closed in and the city's song faded to a muffle, a hand found his. It was Isandro's, and it was rough from kitchen work, and she didn't say anything about it and neither did he, and they walked that way until the tunnel ended.

The tannery was a ruin of rotten beams and old stone vats that still stank of lime after thirty years, and Perrin loved it immediately, because it hummed. Not the way the Registry hummed — not the new, wrong note — but the old way, the patient way, the way forgotten doors hummed when they'd been waiting a long time to be used. He put his palm against the rusted drain grate at the back of the property and felt the vibration go up his arm and settle somewhere behind his ribs.

"Here," he whispered. "It's been waiting. It wants to be opened."

Sef, hauling his map case through the dark, gave him a look. "Doors *want* things now?"

"Everything wants something," Perrin said, surprised anyone would question it. "Locks want to be locked. Doors want to be opened. Walls want to fall down. You can hear it if you listen."

Sef opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at Wren, who shrugged in a way that said *he's not wrong*.

The grate came away with a scream of rust that Perrin felt in his teeth, and beyond it the drain ran under the wall like a stone throat. They went in single file — Wren first with her lantern shuttered to a coin of light, then Tam, half-carrying him, then Sef, then Isandro, then Perrin last, because he was the one who could hear whether anything was coming up the tunnel behind them.

Nothing was. But the city above them, muffled now through thirty feet of earth and stone, was still singing its new song, and the wave of rekeyed locks was getting closer to the Gilded Kettle with every step. He tried not to think about Marta standing in her kitchen at midnight when the hooded men came with their questions. He tried, and failed, and made himself a promise instead: *when we're safe, when Tam's well, I'll find a way to pay her back.* He didn't know how yet. Promises could be like seeds. You planted them and waited.

The drain let out into the marsh, and the marsh was a country of black water and whispering reeds and a sky suddenly enormous overhead, and Perrin stopped dead the moment his boots hit mud because the sound hit him like a wave.

Out here, away from the city, the locks were gone — and in their absence he could hear everything else. The marsh hummed. The distant wall hummed. Far off to the east, so far it was barely more than a shiver at the edge of his hearing, something enormous and old hummed under the mountains, patient as a heartbeat, the same song it had been singing since the waystation, the song that had started all of this.

The Door.

And threaded through it all, faint and getting fainter, the sound of Callowmark's ten thousand locks — and among them, one by one, the locks of the Gilded Kettle going silent as new keys turned in them, or didn't, and hooded men found an innkeeper who knew nothing and a cellar that was already empty.

"They're there," he said. His voice came out small. "At the Kettle. Right now."

Isandro's hand landed on his shoulder. "Then we're gone in time," she said. "That's what tonight was for. That's the whole of it."

But her voice had a crack in it, and Perrin, who heard everything, heard it.

* * *

They walked the marsh until the city lights were a smudge behind them and the reeds were tall enough to swallow a horse, and then Wren called a halt on a hummock of dry ground and they made a fire the size of a cupped hand, because Tam was shivering and no amount of caution was worth his life. They ate Marta's bread and honey. Sef spread a map on his knees and started muttering about the mountain track east, about the Stone Choir, about one moon and counting. Tam fell asleep against Wren's side mid-sentence, which everyone pretended not to notice so that he wouldn't be embarrassed about it.

And Perrin sat at the edge of the firelight, listening to the marsh, and tried not to think the thought that had been circling him like a hawk all night.

The thought was this: *everyone leaves.*

Marta had stayed, but Marta wasn't his. The waystation folk had left, or died, or sold him — he'd been seven and the details were a locked room even in his own head. The road had given him Sef and Wren and Isandro, and Tam, and for three weeks in Callowmark he had almost — *almost* — believed that this was a thing that lasted. That found was the same as kept.

But found things got lost. That was what *found* meant.

He heard Isandro's footsteps in the reeds before he saw her — he always did, her walk had a rhythm like a particular kind of latch — and she sat down next to him without asking, the way she did, and for a while they both just listened. The marsh sang its wet green song. The Door sang its far stone song. Somewhere behind them, Callowmark's locks were all singing the wrong note, and the Kettle's cellar was dark, and Marta was lying to a man in a grey hood with a straight face, and Perrin's chest hurt with all of it.

"Perrin." Isandro's voice was low. "You've been quiet since the tunnel. Quieter than usual, which for you is saying something."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always *listening*. It's different." She pulled her knees up. "What did you hear tonight that scared you? And don't say the hoods. You weren't scared of the hoods. You were the calmest one in that tunnel."

He picked at a reed, splitting it down the middle with his thumbnail. The question sat in him like a stone in a boot. He could lie — he'd learned lying on the road, everyone did — but lies sounded wrong to him, the way a key that didn't fit sounded wrong, and he'd never been able to make one come out of his mouth whole.

"When Tam came back," he said finally, "half-dead, at the gate. You cried. Everyone saw."

"Yes."

"And before that, when the hunters took him in Hollowmere, and you watched from the window and did nothing — you don't talk about that, but Wren told me, because Wren tells me things she thinks I'm too young to understand and I always understand them." The reed split clean. He stared at the two halves. "You left him. Once. You didn't mean to, but you did. And then he came back and it was like — like the door opening again after you thought it was bricked up."

Isandro said nothing. The fire popped.

"So," Perrin said, and his voice did the thing it did when he was twelve and tired and the marsh was enormous and the night was enormous and he was small inside both, "when you leave. When you go through the Door, or wherever you're going, and everyone always goes — are you going to leave me too?"

The words came out smaller than he meant them to. He hated that. He stared very hard at the fire so she wouldn't see his face do whatever it was doing.

Isandro was quiet for a long time. Long enough that he almost wished she'd just say *yes* and get it over with, the way the waystation woman had, the way everyone eventually did.

"I'm bad at promises," she said at last. "I want you to know that going in. I've spent my whole life being the person who watches from windows. It's — it's the thing I do worst. I watched Tam get taken and I did nothing and it's the worst thing I've ever done, worse than the brand, worse than the killing, worse than all of it, and I will carry it until I die. So when I promise you something, I need you to understand that I'm promising it with that in me. With the window in me. I'm promising you that I will never, ever be at a window again while something happens to you. That if they come for you, they come through me first. That when I walk through a door, you're on the same side of it, or I'm not walking through."

"That's a lot of words," Perrin said, because his voice was doing something embarrassing and he needed to fill the space.

"I told you. I'm bad at it." She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Sef would say it in six words. Wren would say it in two. But here's the thing, Perrin — I mean every single one of them. Every word is true and I will still mean them tomorrow, and the day after, and on the other side of the Door, and in whatever country we find there. I'm not going to leave you. Not because I'm good at keeping promises. Because you're mine now. You climbed through my kitchen window in a city full of hunters and you told me the locks were singing, and that was that. You're mine. Family isn't something that happens to you. It's something you *choose*, over and over, and I'm choosing you, and I'll keep choosing you, and that's the only promise I know how to make that I'm sure I can keep."

Perrin looked at her then, finally, because he had to know if her face was lying even if her voice wasn't.

It wasn't. Her face was doing the thing it did when she talked about Tam — cracked open and terrified and certain all at once, like a door held open against the wind.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"Okay." He leaned, very slightly, so his shoulder touched hers, which was as much as he could manage with his face still hot and the marsh watching. "But you're bad at it. The promising. You should practice."

"I'll practice," she said solemnly. "You can grade me."

"I'm going to be very harsh."

"I'd expect nothing less."

They sat like that while the fire burned down to a red eye and the marsh sang and the Door sang and Tam slept and Sef muttered over his maps and Wren kept her watch with her sister's ashes in their little iron box against her heart. And Perrin listened, because listening was what he did, and what he heard — under the reeds and the water and the far stone heartbeat — was five people breathing in the same rhythm on the same patch of ground, and for the first time in his life the sound of it didn't scare him.

He was almost asleep when Sef's voice cut through the dark, sharp and low.

"Perrin. Wake up. Listen to the east."

He was already awake. He was already listening.

And what he heard, threading through the Door's patient song like a black thread through gold, was the hum of grey devices — many of them, moving fast, on the mountain road.

Coming from the Stone Choir.
