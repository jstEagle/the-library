## 23. The Coronation Road

Sef had spent eleven years learning to read a country that officially did not exist, and the first lesson had been the hardest: absence has a texture.

He felt it under his boots before he saw anything at all. The road they'd found at dawn — a seam of older stone running beneath the turf like a vein of silver in grey rock — was laid in the Vessian manner, courses fitted without mortar, each stone cut to answer its neighbor. No road in Hollowmere was built that way. No road anywhere in Aldric's kingdom was built that way, because no road anywhere in Aldric's kingdom was allowed to remember how.

"Keep to the seam," he said over his shoulder. "The turf's a skin. Underneath it's all still there."

Perrin crouched and pressed his palm flat against the stones, the way he did with doors. "It's humming," he said. "Low. Like it's talking in its sleep."

"Roads don't hum," Wren said.

"This one does."

Sef let the boy be right. Perrin was right about more things than any of them found comfortable, and Sef had learned — from the ruin of his own career, if nothing else — that being right about an inconvenient thing was not a defense against what the truth cost. He had been right about Vessia for eleven years. It had gotten his name struck from the guild rolls, his workshop burned, his charts declared contraband, and one very long night in a cellar in Callowmark where he'd traded the only copies left to a smuggler who paid him in bread and contempt.

Now he walked on the proof of it, and the proof walked back.

* * *

They made nine miles by midday, following the seam northeast through country that looked, at a glance, like nothing at all: moorland going to heather, stands of birch gone gold at the edges, a stream that couldn't decide between two valleys. Isandro ranged ahead the way she always did now, restless, her shadow longer than it should have been in the flat light. Wren kept pace beside Sef with her pack riding high on her shoulders — the pack with the urn in it, though nobody said so, and everybody knew.

"The milestones," Sef said, and stopped.

There were three of them within thirty paces, half-sunk in the heather, and every one had been defaced. Not broken — defaced. Someone had come down this road with chisels, centuries ago or last year, it was impossible to tell, and ground away the inscriptions until the stones were blank as eggs. But chisels are honest tools; they leave the shape of what they remove. Sef knelt and ran his thumb along the ghost-letters, feeling the grooves where strokes had been.

"Names," he said. "These were boundary markers. Every milestone on the coronation road carried the name of the town it pointed home to." He traced a deep vertical stroke, then the curve that had swung away from it. "This one said *Vael*. There—" his finger found a second stone, "—*Osterhale*. And this—" the third, nearly worn smooth, but the first letter survived, a proud doubled arch, "—was *Vessia* itself. The capital marker. Zero miles."

"To where?" Perrin asked.

"To everywhere," Sef said. "That's what a capital is. Every distance in the old kingdom was measured from this stone. You could stand here and know exactly how far you were from your own front door."

Isandro had come back down the line to stand over him. He watched her read the blank stones the way he read them — not as rocks but as wounds.

"They didn't knock them down," she said slowly. "They just... took the words off."

"That's how you unmake a country," Sef said. "You don't burn it. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be named. You take its name off everything it touched. Roads, coins, graves, children's lessons. You leave the stones standing because moving them costs money, and a blank stone is just a stone. Then you wait one hundred years, and the people who could have told you what the letters meant are dead, and the stone is only a stone again." He stood, knees cracking. "Except it isn't. Not out here. Out here the erasure never finished."

"Why not?" Perrin asked.

Sef looked north, toward the mountains they could not yet see but all of them could feel, the way you feel weather coming in a bad tooth. "Because the erasure wasn't done by men alone," he said. "And men are thorough, but magic is patient. Come on. We should reach the first standing stones by dusk."

* * *

They came on the stones an hour before sunset, and Sef made them stop a quarter mile out, because he wanted to watch Isandro see them.

There were seven of them, ranked along the seam of the old road like a choir waiting for a conductor — tall, leaning, furred with lichen, each carved in the shape of a robed figure whose face had been weathered into anonymity. They predated the milestones by a thousand years, maybe more. On no sanctioned chart in the kingdom did they appear. In Sef's confiscated-and-rebought maps they appeared on every sheet, drawn small and stubborn in his own hand, annotated in code: *the Seven Witnesses. Do not mark their faces. The faces change.*

"What are they?" Isandro asked quietly.

"Witnesses," Sef said. "That's not poetry. That's what they're called in the oldest fragments I ever recovered — the ones the guild burned before I could copy them properly. Standing stones raised along the coronation road so that the land itself would attend every crowning. A king of Vessia wasn't crowned by a priest in a hall. He was crowned in the open, on the road, with the country watching." He wet his lips. "Which means when the country was unmade, someone had a problem. You can't erase witnesses. You can only make them forget what they saw."

"And did they?"

Sef started walking again. "Look at their faces," he said, "and tell me."

* * *

They camped in the lee of the third Witness, and that was when the flickering began.

Sef noticed it first as a wrongness in the firelight. The flames leaned north, though there was no wind. Then the light itself began to behave strangely at the edges of the camp — thickening, pooling, and in the pooled places things appeared that were not there. A wall, low and mortared, running off into the heather. A gatepost with a lantern hanging from it, unlit. The corner of a roof, slate-dark, with rain-grooves worn in it from centuries of storms that had fallen on a house that no longer stood.

"Perrin," Sef said carefully. "What do you hear?"

The boy sat cross-legged, staring at the ghost-wall with his whole body, the way he stared at locked doors. "Everything," he whispered. "It's like — you know when you put your ear against a door and there's people talking on the other side? It's like that. But the door is the ground. And there's a *lot* of people."

"There aren't," Wren said flatly. "I've walked this moor. There's nothing out here. Nothing for forty miles."

"No," Sef said. "There isn't. Not anymore." He fed a stick to the fire and watched the ghost-roof shiver and hold. "But there used to be. This was the heart of the kingdom — the coronation road ran through the richest country in the world. Towns every few miles. Vael. Osterhale. Corryn Mere. Nine towns between here and the Stone Choir, and every one of them struck from the record, hollowed, folded down into valleys like Hollowmere where the magic couldn't follow." He looked around the fire at their faces — Isandro's taut and hungry, Wren's guarded, Perrin's lit up like a lamp. "Except the folding was never perfect. The land remembers its shape. And we're walking straight down its spine."

As if the word *spine* had been a summons, the flickering strengthened.

It came in waves after that, timed to nothing Sef could measure — though later, much later, he would realize it was timed to Isandro. When she moved toward the fire, the ghosts receded. When she turned away, or slept, or simply stopped paying attention to the present tense, the past swelled up around the camp like water rising in a lock. A whole street surfaced once, for the length of ten heartbeats: cobbles, shuttered windows, washing strung between eaves, a smell of bread and woodsmoke so vivid that Wren stood up with her hand on her knife. Somewhere in the ghost-street a dog barked twice, and then the street was heather again, and the silence where the bark had been was worse than the sound.

"It's her," Wren said, low, when Isandro had gone to check the picket lines. "The flickering. It follows her attention. Or the lack of it."

"I know."

"You've known since the milestones."

"I've known since the valley," Sef said. "Since the day I met her and realized what a food-taster with total immunity actually is. She's not empty, Wren. She's *full*. Sealed full, like a jar with the lid waxed down. And this road—" he gestured at the dark, at the buried country humming under it, "—this road was built to carry coronations. It's the most consecrated ground left in Vessia. Wax melts near flame. She's walking down a corridor of flame, and her seal is thinning, and every time she stops paying attention, everything the country ever was leaks through the gap she makes."

Wren was quiet a long moment. The urn-shaped weight of her pack sat between her feet like a guest.

"Then we should hurry," she said finally.

"Yes," Sef said. "We should hurry."

* * *

He took the middle watch, as he always did, because he slept badly and saw no reason to inflict it on the others. The moon rose fat and hazy. The Witnesses stood black against it, and Sef got up, as he had wanted to do all evening, and walked down the line of them with his journal open.

He had drawn these stones a hundred times, from memory, from rumor, from the one stolen glimpse he'd had of them at nineteen, before the guild caught him copying restricted charts. Drawing them had been the crime. Drawing them was still the crime; he had no doubt that somewhere in Callowmark a magistrate's clerk kept a file with his name on it and the charge written out in a fair hand: *cartography of the unmade nation.* Eleven years, and he had never once seen the Witnesses with anyone beside him. Now there were four people sleeping in their shadow, and one of them was the key to the door under the mountains, and the irony of it — that the guild had exiled him for making maps of a place that apparently needed finding — was so clean it felt designed.

He reached the seventh Witness and stopped.

The faces changed. His annotation had said so, copied from a fragment he'd never fully trusted, and he had filed it under folklore for eleven years. But moonlight lay along the seventh stone's weathered features, and the features were not weathered. They were *worn smooth*, deliberately, like the milestones — except that where the chisel-work ended, new contours were rising underneath, faint as breath on glass. A brow. The suggestion of a mouth. As he watched, the mouth seemed to open — not move, nothing so vulgar — but to become the kind of mouth that had once spoken, that remembered speaking, that was deciding whether to speak again.

Sef wrote it down. His hand shook, and he wrote it down anyway, because that was the whole of what he was: a man who wrote it down.

Behind him, across the camp, Perrin sat bolt upright in his blankets and screamed.

* * *

By the time Sef reached the fire the boy was on his feet, pointing north, and the others were up, and the night had gone strange in a way Sef had no chart for. The ghost-flickering had stopped entirely — the moor was brutally, perfectly present, every heather-bush sharp as an engraving — and into that terrible clarity came a sound from under the earth. Not the low sleep-talk hum Perrin had described at dawn. Something rhythmic. Deep. Patient as a heartbeat and exactly as regular.

"It woke up," Perrin gasped. Isandro had her arms around him and he was shaking like a snared bird. "Under the ground — it heard us — it heard *her* — it's counting. Why is it counting?"

"Because it knows we're coming," Sef said.

He looked north, where the mountains should have been and weren't — the haze too thick, the horizon holding its breath — and then down at the road beneath his boots, the old fitted stones drinking the moonlight, running straight as a ruled line toward the Stone Choir and whatever waited there. Behind them, far to the south, he thought he saw a light that might have been a campfire and might have been something with a hood on, and he decided, in the professional manner of a man who had survived eleven years by knowing which truths to write down and which to pocket, not to mention it yet.

"One moon," he said aloud, to all of them, to steady himself as much as them. "Maybe less. The Door surfaces at the Choir when the Choir sings, and the Choir sings at moonrise, and the moon will be right soon. We walk fast, we sleep in shifts, and we trust the road." He paused. "It's the only coronation road left in the world. I suspect it wants a procession."

Above them, the seventh Witness wore a face that was almost a face, and its almost-mouth was almost smiling.

And far behind, at the edge of sight, the southern light went out — deliberately, the way a hunter pinches a candle — and the dark it left behind began, very quietly, to move.
