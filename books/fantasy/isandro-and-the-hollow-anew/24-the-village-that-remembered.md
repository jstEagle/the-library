## 24. The Village That Remembered

The village had no name, which was how Isandro knew it was hiding.

Sef had said as much three days ago, when the coronation road had narrowed to a track and the track to a suggestion of a path between birch trees. "Every place on my maps has a name," he'd told them, tapping the worn vellum with one finger. "Even ruins get names. Ruins are permitted. But this—" he'd gestured at the fold of hills ahead, green and unremarkable and utterly blank on his chart, "—this is a hole in the record. And holes in records are dug by hand."

Now they stood at the edge of that hole, and it looked like nothing at all: a scatter of stone houses with slate roofs, smoke rising straight in the windless evening, a flock of geese complaining near a well. Maybe forty people. A church without a bell. Fields climbing the hillside in terraces so old they had softened into curves.

"It's too quiet," Wren said. Her eyes moved over the village the way they moved over a wound — cataloguing, assessing. "Forty souls, no dogs barking at strangers. They've seen us coming for an hour."

"They've seen worse coming," Sef murmured. "And learned not to bark about it."

Perrin had gone still in the way he did when he was listening to something none of them could hear. His head tilted, birdlike, his whole body a cupped ear. "There's locks here," he said slowly. "But they're all... inside. Not on doors." He frowned, struggling with it. "Like everyone locked something up in their chests and threw away the key."

Isandro felt the words settle into her like a stone dropped into still water.

"Then we knock gently," she said, and walked down into the village.

* * *

The first door opened before she reached it.

An old woman stood in the frame, small and square as a hearthstone, her hands dusted with flour to the wrists. She looked at Isandro for a long moment — not at her face but lower, at the way she walked, the set of her shoulders, some geometry of bone and habit that meant more than any face could.

"You're hungry," the old woman said. It was not a question, and it was not quite an offer either. It was a diagnosis.

"We won't trouble you," Isandro began.

"You're already troubling me." The woman stepped back from the doorway. "That's what hunger is. Come in before the light goes, or don't, but stop standing in my yard like a confession."

Her name was Maren, though she didn't offer it easily; Sef coaxed it out over bread while the others arranged themselves around the single room's fire with the careful choreography of people who had slept rough too long. The house smelled of woodsmoke and yeast and something underneath both — a spice Isandro couldn't name, warm and dark, that made her chest ache with a homesickness for somewhere she had never been.

The bread came out of the oven dark-crusted and heavy. Maren set it on the table and cut it with a long knife, and Isandro saw the blade catch on something hard halfway through the loaf.

She saw Maren see her see it.

For a heartbeat the kitchen went very quiet. Perrin's head tilted. Wren's hands stilled on her bowl. And then Maren finished the cut, laid open the loaf, and there in the pale crumb sat a coin — old silver, worn nearly smooth, stamped with a face no king currently minted.

"The baker hides a coin in every loaf," Maren said, watching Isandro. "Whoever gets the piece with the coin in it has luck for a year." She paused. "That's what we tell the tax collectors' children, when we feed them on the road and they carry stories home."

"And what do you tell yourselves?" Isandro asked quietly.

Maren turned the coin over with one floured finger. On its reverse was a word in a script Isandro had never been taught and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize your own reflection in a window at night.

"That bread is body," the old woman said. "And the country is a body too. And even if they burn the skin off it, the bones remember where they were."

* * *

They stayed the night because Maren made staying impossible to refuse, and because Sef, examining the coin by candlelight with hands that trembled slightly, kept saying "this is real, this is actually real" in a voice that made Wren threaten to take it away from him.

After the others slept, Isandro couldn't.

She sat by the banked fire turning her own thoughts over like stones, and that was how she heard it: singing. Faint, from somewhere up the hill, in a language that wasn't any language spoken in Hollowmere or the free cities or anywhere she'd ever traded gossip in a kitchen. It rose and fell like breathing. Like grief that had learned to keep time.

She followed it barefoot through the cold, past the terraced fields, to a low stone building half-sunk into the hillside — older than the church, older than anything, its doorway so short she had to stoop. Inside, a dozen villagers stood around candles, and their song filled the earth-floored room the way water fills a well.

No one stopped singing when she appeared in the doorway. No one seemed surprised. An old man with a voice like gravel simply shifted, making room for her in the circle, and the song folded her in.

She didn't know the words. She sang anyway, humming the shape of them, and felt the melody move through her chest like something drinking. When the last note settled, the silence afterward was full rather than empty.

"What is it?" she asked. "What were you singing?"

"A naming," the old man said. "We sing the names of things the decree struck out. Every night, one thing. Tonight it was the river." He nodded toward the east. "You can't hear it anymore, you know. The river that runs past this village. Officially it has never run here. So we sing it back."

Isandro thought of Hollowmere, where magic could not follow, where the valley drank everything strange and gave back only silence. She thought of herself, sealed like a jar, and the cork beginning to fail.

"How long?" she asked. "How long have you done this?"

"Since before I was born. Since after." He shrugged, an entire history compressed into the motion. "A country doesn't die because someone crosses out its name. It dies when no one says it anymore. We say it. Quietly. Badly. Our grandparents' grandparents spoke it properly, and we only have the songs, and the songs have holes in them now." His eyes, deep-set and unhurried, found hers. "But we keep saying it."

"Why show me? You don't know me."

"No," he agreed. "But I know your grandmother."

* * *

The world went very still and very loud at once.

"My grandmother died before I was born," Isandro managed.

"Yes," the old man said. "They usually do. Sit down, child. This takes longer than a song."

He led her to a bench against the curved stone wall, and Maren appeared — Isandro never saw her arrive, she was simply there, the way grandmothers are — and pressed a cup of something hot into her hands. The old man lowered himself beside her with the care of a body long past arguing with.

"My name is Edric," he said. "And sixty years ago, a woman came through this village heading south, heavy with child, running the same road you're running. She had Dunmore hands." He reached out and took Isandro's right hand, turned it palm-up, and traced one callused finger along the base of her thumb. "See? The taster's callus builds here, in a line, like a plow furrow. Her mother had it. She had it. You have it."

"She was a taster?"

"She was a keeper." Edric smiled, and there was sorrow in it, worn smooth as the coin in the bread. "In the old tongue, *velthari*. One who holds. Your family held things for Vessia, girl. Oaths. Names. Small magics that needed keeping safe. When the erasure came, the Dunmores scattered like everyone else, but they carried what they carried." He tapped her palm once, gently, and let go. "Your grandmother left something here. She said she'd come back for it, or send her blood back for it. Sixty years is a long time to keep a promise warm, but we're good at that. It's most of what we do."

Maren crossed the room to a niche in the stone wall, hidden behind a loose slab, and drew out a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age. She set it in Isandro's lap with both hands, the way you set down something sleeping.

Inside the cloth lay a ring.

It was plain at first glance — a band of dark metal, unadorned, sized for a woman's finger. But the metal wasn't any metal Isandro knew. It drank the candlelight instead of reflecting it, and deep in its curve, when she tilted it, something glowed: a seed of light, small as a grain of wheat, patient as a buried ember.

"She said it was a door-seed," Edric said. "From before the erasure. The Dunmores kept such things. Held them. *Velthari.*" He watched Isandro's face. "You didn't know any of this."

"I didn't know any of this," she whispered. Her whole life she had believed herself an accident — a kitchen orphan with a strange tongue, immune by luck or fluke, useful and disposable. And all along there had been a lineage, a purpose, a word for what she was, spoken in a dead language by an old man in a hole in the map.

"I spent my life tasting poison," she said, and heard her own voice crack. "I thought that was all I was. A tool with a good mouth."

"Child," Maren said, sitting down heavily on her other side, "you think a seed isn't a tree? Everything that grows was first something small that held on." She nodded at the ring. "Put it on. It's yours. It was always going to be yours. Blood remembers blood."

Isandro slid the ring onto her finger. It fit perfectly, of course it did, and the little light within it pulsed once — twice — like a heart remembering its rhythm, and warmth ran up her arm and settled behind her sternum, in the exact place where the sealed pressure of her magic lived.

The two lights answered each other. She felt it clearly: the ring's small flame leaning toward the vast corked ocean inside her, recognizing it. Calling to it, very softly, across whatever distance there was between a spark and a sea.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, it knows me."

"Everything here knows you," Edric said. "You're just the last one to be told."

* * *

She woke at dawn with the ring on her finger and the others gathered around the fire looking at her with expressions she couldn't fully read.

"You were singing in your sleep," Perrin said. He sounded awed and slightly alarmed. "In the dead tongue. You don't know the dead tongue."

"I don't," Isandro agreed. She flexed her fingers and watched the ring's inner light breathe. "Apparently I do."

Sef was already awake, already working, his maps spread across Maren's table weighted with stones and a heel of bread. He looked up at her, and there was something new in his face — hope sharpened into urgency.

"This changes our timing," he said. "If the Dunmores were keepers, if your family held pieces of the old country, then the Door may answer you faster than I calculated. Or slower. Or sideways. Either way—" he flattened his hand on the map, on the faint line of the coronation road climbing toward the mountains, "—the Stone Choir is four days' walk. And Isandro—" he hesitated, and she saw him choose honesty over comfort, as he always did, eventually, "—last night, while you slept, I checked the horizon. There's dust on the southern road. A column. Moving fast."

Wren's jaw tightened. "Hunters?"

"At this speed? Hunters don't march in columns." Sef met her eyes. "That's an army. Aldric isn't sending anyone to find us anymore. He's coming himself, or close enough to make no difference."

Silence around the fire. Perrin's hand found Isandro's sleeve and gripped it.

Edric and Maren exchanged a look across the room — a whole conversation in a glance, conducted in the language of people who had survived six decades of erasure by reading each other perfectly.

"Then you leave by the shepherd's path," Maren said briskly, already moving, pulling a sack from under her bed and filling it with the efficiency of a woman who had packed fugitives before. "North through the high pasture. It joins the coronation road above the ford, past where anyone watching would think to look. And you take bread." She thrust the sack at Isandro, and inside, wrapped separately, were three loaves — each one, Isandro now understood, holding its hidden coin. "Bones remember," Maren said. "Feed them."

At the door, Edric caught Isandro's hand one last time and pressed it closed around something small. She opened it: another coin, unwrapped, ancient, the erased face staring up at her.

"When you reach wherever you're going," he said, "say the name. Out loud. However badly. Say it anyway."

"I will," Isandro promised. "I don't even know it yet."

"You will," Edric said, smiling. "It's in the ring. It's in you. It always was."

They climbed out of the nameless village as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, five figures on a shepherd's path with bread in their packs and a dead country's name waiting in a ring of dark metal. Behind them, faint on the wind, Isandro thought she heard singing start up again — the river's name, maybe, or the mountain's, or theirs.

Ahead, far to the south, the dust of Aldric's army rose against the sky like a second, darker dawn.
