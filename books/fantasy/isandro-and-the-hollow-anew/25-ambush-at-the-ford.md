## 25. Ambush at the Ford

Veyra had learned long ago that rivers were honest in a way people were not. A river showed you its depth by the color of its water, its temper by the sound it made over stone. People lied with their faces and their voices and even their silences. So when she came down through the birch trees to the ford at Cael's Crossing and saw the water running shallow and quicksilver-bright over its gravel bed, she trusted what it told her: this was where they would try to cross, because there was nowhere else for thirty miles, and desperate people always chose the shortest grief.

She had been hunting Isandro Dunmore for eleven days now, and in that time she had assembled a portrait of her the way a jeweler assembles a setting — one small fact at a time, each one fitted against the last until the whole thing caught light.

Fact: the taster had fled Hollowmere through the mill-race, which meant she knew the valley's water better than most people knew their own hands. Fact: she traveled with Sef Ardenne, the disgraced cartographer, which meant she was looking for something on a map — or looking for a map of something. Fact: at the river parley outside Thornhollow, Veyra had offered her a cup of wine laced with enough widow's-bane to stop a horse's heart, and Isandro had drunk it like well-water, held her gaze across the fire, and said, *That was rude, Captain,* without so much as a tremor.

Veyra had poured out the rest of that bottle afterward and sat looking at the empty clay for a long time. Not because she was afraid. Because she was fascinated, and fascination was a luxury a hunter could not afford, and she had allowed herself to have it anyway.

Now she crouched in the fern-shadow at the treeline with six of her grey hoods fanned out behind her, and she watched the five of them come down to the water through the morning mist, and she let herself be fascinated one last time.

They looked wrong together, and right together, all at once. The cartographer walked with a stick he'd cut two days ago, favoring an old knee, his satchel of maps strapped across his chest like armor. The hedge-witch moved the way healers did, scanning everything — the treeline, the water, Veyra's own hidden position, though she couldn't have known exactly where it was; she just knew someone was watching, the way animals knew. The boy Perrin walked in the middle with his eyes shut half the time, listening to whatever locked things sang to him. And Tam — the stable-boy from Hollowmere, the one who'd escaped the holding cells in Callowmark's undercity and somehow crossed forty miles of hunted country to reach them — stayed close to Isandro's side like a burr.

And Isandro. Veyra studied her the way she'd study a blade before buying it. The woman walked like a servant still, shoulders slightly stooped from years of standing at walls while nobles ate, hands quiet. But there was something else underneath now, something that hadn't been there in the inn at Callowmark. A stillness that wasn't humility. The stillness of a vessel filling.

*What are you?* Veyra thought, not for the first time. *The edict says you're a bearer. My instruments say you're something the edict doesn't have a word for.*

She raised two fingers. Behind her, six hooded figures moved through the ferns like smoke, taking up positions along both banks. The device at her belt — the humming thing, brass and bone and a sliver of glass that Aldric's artificers had made from no material she could name — began to shiver against her hip. It hummed loudest near gifts. Near Isandro, she suspected, it would do more than hum.

She waited until they were ankle-deep in the ford. Then she stood up out of the ferns and said, in a voice pitched to carry over the water:

"Taster."

* * *

Isandro stopped mid-stream. Veyra watched her weight settle, watched her hand find Tam's shoulder and move him half a step behind her — instinctive, unthinking, the gesture of someone who had decided, somewhere in the last eleven days, that these people were hers to protect. That was the fact Veyra kept failing to fit into her portrait. Servants didn't protect. Servants survived. Everything Veyra knew about the machinery of palaces said that a royal taster was a creature trained to be expendable, and yet here was this one planting herself between danger and other people like she'd been doing it all her life.

"Captain," Isandro said. Her voice was steady. "You're a long way from your warrant."

"My warrant goes wherever the king's enemies go." Veyra stepped down onto the gravel bar at the ford's edge, boots dry, deliberately unhurried. She let her hood fall back so Isandro could see her face — courtesy cost nothing, and courtesy disarmed. "Five of you. Six of me, plus four on the far bank you haven't spotted yet. The math is poor, taster. Come back with me. Talk to the king. That's all I'm authorized to promise, but I'll keep even that."

"You'll drag us to Aldric," Wren said from the shallows, "and Aldric will open your veins into a bowl."

"Aldric will do what kings do," Veyra said, not looking at the witch. She kept her eyes on Isandro. "I'm offering you the version of this day where everyone walks away. There aren't many versions left. Take the one on the table."

And Isandro — Veyra would remember this, would turn it over in the dark watches for weeks afterward — Isandro actually considered it. Veyra saw the consideration happen: the flicker of arithmetic behind her eyes, the glance at Tam, at Perrin, at Sef leaning on his stick in the shallows. She saw a woman weigh her own freedom against five other lives and find the scale already tipped.

"No," Isandro said quietly. "But thank you for asking. Nobody else has."

Something in Veyra's chest turned over, unpleasantly. She crushed it.

"Then we do the arithmetic my way." She drew her sword. Around the ford, ten grey shapes rose out of fern and reed and stone, and the morning filled with the sound of steel leaving scabbards.

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way violence always did — too fast for the part of the mind that wanted narrative, too slow for the part that wanted mercy.

Perrin screamed first. Not a scream of fear — Veyra realized later, replaying it — a scream of warning, because the boy heard things, and what he must have heard was the humming device at her belt spiking as Isandro's sealed gift answered it, and he understood before anyone what that meant: they know exactly where she is, they've always known, the whole ambush is built around her.

"Run the middle!" Perrin shrieked. "The middle stones! They're hollow!"

Sef Ardenne, to his credit, didn't hesitate. The old cartographer bellowed "MIDDLE!" and drove forward into the current, stick and all, and the others followed him toward the line of mid-river boulders — and Veyra saw why a moment later, when two of her hunters waded in from the far bank and the gravel shelf they'd counted on gave way beneath them, dropping both men chest-deep into the channel. The boy heard doors. Apparently he also heard floors.

"Take the witch alive if you can," Veyra snapped, wading in after them. "The taster—"

She never finished the order, because Isandro came at her.

Not with a weapon. With a broken oar-blade she must have torn off the pack-raft they'd abandoned upstream, and with a fury that Veyra had genuinely not prepared for, because every report, every profile, every careful note in her journal described Isandro Dunmore as quiet, watchful, self-effacing. No one had written down that she fought like someone who had spent her whole life swallowing and had finally, finally run out of throat.

Their blades met over the water. Veyra was stronger, faster, trained at the Crown school where they taught you to end fights in three moves. But Isandro fought close and low and without any style at all, taking a slice along her forearm rather than give ground, and Veyra understood with cold clarity that this woman would trade blood for inches all day.

"Yield!" Veyra snarled, pressing her. "You're not a killer, taster. I've read you. You're not—"

"I tasted for the man who killed my valley," Isandro said, and shoved her back into the current.

Veyra went down to one knee in the shallows, and the fight broke apart around her into its separate disasters. On the near bank, Wren Ostara had done something Veyra had no name for — the hedge-witch had her hands out and one of Veyra's hunters was simply *stopping*, mid-lunge, his body locking up like a door slammed shut inside his own joints, and he went into the reeds rigid and breathing. On the far bank, Sef Ardenne had put himself between two hunters and the children, swinging his stick like a man threshing grain, and one of them — Halloway, young Halloway, who'd joined the hunt because his village owed taxes — lost patience with the arithmetic of *alive if you can* and drove his sword through the old man's side.

Veyra heard Sef's cry across the whole ford. She heard it and hated that she heard it, hated that some traitor part of her had started keeping score of these people as *people*.

And then she saw Isandro see it.

She saw the exact moment. Later, writing in her journal by candlelight, Veyra would try to describe it and fail, and try again, and fail better: it was like watching a lock turn in a face. Isandro looked at Sef folding into the water, at Tam screaming his name, at the hunter — Halloway — raising his blade to finish, and everything soft in her went somewhere else. Somewhere with no furniture.

"Halloway!" Veyra shouted. "Don't—"

He didn't hear her, or the river ate her voice. He lunged. Isandro moved — not fast, nothing about it was fast, it was almost gentle, the way you'd reach to catch a falling cup — and drove the broken oar-blade up under his jaw.

Halloway stopped being alive between one heartbeat and the next. He sat down in the shallows as if tired, and the river took an interest in him, and Isandro stood over him with red water running off her hands and a look on her face that Veyra recognized from funerals.

The ford went quiet. Even the fighting stopped — Wren frozen with her hands still raised, the rigid hunter gasping back to life in the reeds, Tam kneeling in the current holding Sef's head above the water, Perrin standing on the mid-stones with his fists pressed over his ears as if the whole world had become a scream he couldn't shut out.

Ten hunters. One dead. One wounded old man bleeding out in a river. And a food-taster standing in the wreckage of her own innocence, looking at her hands like they belonged to a stranger.

Veyra should have pressed. Every doctrine she'd ever been taught said press — the party was broken, their guide was dying, their protector was gut-shocked, and ten blades were still standing. Press, take them all, be home by dark, collect the king's gratitude.

She looked at Isandro's hands. She looked at the humming device at her own belt, which had gone strange — not spiking anymore but *singing*, a thin rising tone like wet glass rubbed at the rim, as if whatever was corked inside the taster were pressing outward against the seal, testing it, tasting the crack.

And Veyra, who had killed nine men and never once hesitated, hesitated.

"Fall back," she heard herself say.

Her second, Dree, stared at her through her hood. "Captain?"

"Fall *back*. All of you. Now." She sheathed her sword with a motion that cost her more than the drawing of it had. "No pursuit. Mark it: no pursuit until I understand what she is. Anyone who chases on their own answerable to me."

"She killed Halloway—"

"And I'll write the letter to his mother myself," Veyra said, and the flatness of her voice closed the argument like a lid.

They withdrew up the near bank, gray shapes dissolving into gray birches, and Veyra went last. At the treeline she stopped and looked back once.

On the gravel bar, Wren Ostara had Sef Ardenne laid out flat and her hands buried in the wound to the wrist, working, muttering, her face gray as ash. Tam held the old man's hand and talked to him, nonsense words, steady as a mill-wheel. Perrin had come down off the stones and stood beside Isandro, and the boy — twelve years old, feral, afraid of everything — put his small hand on her bloody arm and left it there.

And Isandro knelt in the shallows washing Halloway's face. Washing the dead hunter's face with river water, gently, closing his eyes with two fingers, arranging his hood over his hair. Giving him the only dignity left to give. Veyra watched her do it and felt the ground shift under everything she believed about the woman she'd been hunting.

*Innocence spent,* Veyra thought. *Irrevocably. She'll never be the person she was an hour ago, and she knows it, and she's burying the man who made her know it.*

It was the most terrible thing Veyra had ever seen, and she had seen pyres.

She turned and walked into the birches, and the device at her belt sang its thin glass song all the way, and she did not silence it. She wanted to listen. Whatever was sealed in Isandro Dunmore was waking up, and waking angry, and King Aldric — dying, desperate, hungry for gifted blood — had ordered her to bring the taster in alive without ever telling her *why*, and Veyra was beginning to suspect that the why was bigger than the king, and older than the hunt, and that she had just chosen, at a shallow ford in the middle of an unmade country, to stand aside and let it arrive.

Behind her, faint over the water, she heard the hedge-witch begin to sing — an old Vessian mourning-song, in the tongue that officially did not exist — and the stones of the ford seemed almost to hum along.

Veyra walked faster, and told herself the chill down her spine was the morning air.

It was not the morning air.
