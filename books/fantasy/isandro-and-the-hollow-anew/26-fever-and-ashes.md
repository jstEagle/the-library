## 26. Fever and Ashes

The wound had been clean when Wren closed it. That was the thing she kept returning to, the thing she could not stop turning over like a coin worn smooth: it had been *clean*. Ford water, yes, but she'd washed it with boiled willow-bark and salted wine, packed it with moss from the high ground, stitched it with gut she'd cured herself. She had done everything right. She had done everything right for thirty years, and the wound had festered anyway, as if Sef's body had decided to argue with her.

"His fever's climbing," Isandro said from the doorway of the shepherd's hut.

"I can smell that from here," Wren said, and immediately regretted it, because Isandro flinched the way she always did when Wren was sharp — a small withdrawal, a door easing shut. Wren made herself look up from the basin. "I'm sorry. It's not you. Sit with him."

Isandro sat. She'd been sitting with him for two days, holding his arm down when the fever made him thrash, murmuring fragments of kitchen-talk at him — broth temperatures, the correct way to test for nightshade, nonsense about the regent's table in Hollowmere — as if the sound of ordinary things could anchor him to the world. It was more nursing than Wren had managed to give anyone since Marrowfen. She hated how much it moved her.

Sef lay on a pallet of straw and sheepskins, grey as old tallow, his lips cracked and moving around words no one could catch. The wound in his side had gone the color of a storm horizon, weeping thin yellow fluid that smelled wrong — sweetish, rotten, like fruit left too long in a hot room. Blood-poison. In Marrowfen they'd called it *the red road*, because once it started walking you could only follow.

"You said you'd fixed it," Perrin said from the corner. He hadn't left the hut either. He sat with his knees drawn up, watching Sef with the flat, unblinking attention of a boy who had learned that watching was survival. "You said he'd be fine."

"I said I'd done my best." Wren wrung out a cloth in cool well-water and laid it across Sef's forehead. His skin burned through the linen like a stone left in the sun. "Best isn't always enough. That's the first lesson of healing, and nobody believes it until they have to."

Perrin's jaw tightened. He didn't answer. He turned toward the wall instead, and Wren saw him do the thing he did when something hurt — press his ear against the stone, listening. Listening for what, she couldn't imagine. Doors, he'd said once. Everything locked hums if you know how to hear it. She wondered what a dying man sounded like to him. She decided she didn't want to ask.

* * *

Night came down over the hills like a lid. They were three days off the coronation road now, holed up in an abandoned shepherd's hut on the shoulder of a mountain whose name had been scratched from every map Sef owned — which meant, these days, that it had been scratched from existence. Veyra's hunters were somewhere behind them; the Door of Anew was somewhere ahead, surfacing at the Stone Choir within the moon, if Sef's maps were true, if Sef lived long enough to read them, if any of it was real.

Wren worked by candlelight because her gift worked better in the dark, or because she liked to pretend it did. She laid her hands over the wound and let the warmth come — the slow, green warmth, the hedge-witch warmth, the only magic she'd ever been allowed to keep because it looked so much like ordinary kindness. She felt the infection the way you feel a splinter: a wrongness under the skin, branching, patient. She pushed heat into it. She pulled poison out of it. She sang the low wordless thing her mother had taught her, the one that wasn't quite a spell and wasn't quite a prayer.

The fever broke a little past midnight. By the second bell of morning it had climbed again, higher than before.

"That's not right," she said aloud, to no one.

"What's not right?" Isandro's voice came from the dark near the pallet. She hadn't slept. Of course she hadn't slept. None of them slept anymore; they took turns being awake the way soldiers take turns standing watch.

"The fever should stay broken. Even blood-poison yields, for a while, when you draw the rot out. This is—" Wren stopped. Her hands were still on Sef's side, and under her palms she felt something she had never felt in thirty years of hedge-work: a resistance. Not the dull resistance of a sick body fighting its own war. A *directed* resistance. Something in the wound was pushing back against her gift specifically, the way a hand pushes back against a hand.

She sat back on her heels. Her heart was doing something strange and slow.

"Wren?" Isandro again.

"There's craft in this," Wren said. "Someone poisoned the blade. Or the water. Or—" She thought of the ford, the reeds, the grey shape of movement upstream that she'd taken for a heron. "Or someone followed us to the ford and dosed the crossing. Aldric's hunters don't need to catch us if they can slow us. A festering wound will do their work for them."

Isandro went very still. Wren had learned to read that stillness over the weeks of travel: it was the taster's stillness, the moment before she named what was in the cup. "Can you counter it?"

"I can try. I've been trying." Wren wiped her hands on her apron, though there was nothing on them. "Whatever it is, it's woven deep. It's not a simple toxin. Simple toxins I could pull out in my sleep." She heard her own voice going flat and professional, the voice she used when grief got too close, and she let it, because the alternative was letting the other voice out, the one that wanted to say *I cannot lose another one, I buried my sister, I am not burying a cartographer too.*

"Then we'll need more of whatever you use," Isandro said. Practical. Steady. "Tell me what to gather. I'll go tonight."

"You'll go nowhere at night on this mountain."

"Then tell me what to gather at dawn."

Wren almost smiled. There it was — the stubbornness that had carried a food-taster out of Hollowmere and across half an unmade country. "Yarrow. More willow bark. And comfrey root, if the slopes above us still grow it. And Isandro—" she caught the girl at the door, silhouetted against the grey pre-dawn, "—if you find anything with a milky sap, leave it alone. Some poisons wear flowers."

"I know," Isandro said quietly. "I used to taste them for a living."

* * *

The day passed in gathering and grinding and boiling. Perrin came back from the stream with his arms full of yarrow and a report delivered in his flat, careful way: he'd heard humming downstream, faint, wrong, the sound a lock makes when someone is picking it. Hunters' devices, he thought. Far off. A day behind them, maybe less.

"So we can't move him," Wren said.

"He can't be moved anyway," Isandro said. "Look at him."

They looked at him together. Sef had surfaced from the fever once, briefly, around noon — long enough to grip Wren's wrist with shocking strength and say, "The Choir. Don't wait for me. The map case, under my head, the third fold, the timing—" and then gone under again before she could answer. The map case stayed under his head. Nobody touched it. It had become, without anyone deciding, a kind of altar.

By dusk the wound had spread its bruise-color halfway to his ribs. Wren changed the packing, drew what poison she could, watched the fever climb. She was running out of tricks and she knew it, and the knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

It was full dark when Isandro came to sit beside her at the fire and asked the question Wren had been waiting days for someone to ask.

"How bad?"

Wren fed a stick to the flames. Outside, wind moved through the bare trees with a sound like distant singing — the mountain's own choir, mocking her. "If he were in Marrowfen, with my whole stores and my sister beside me, I'd give him four chances in ten."

"And here?"

"Two."

Isandro absorbed that. She had a way of absorbing terrible arithmetic without flinching that Wren both admired and found slightly frightening. "What would change the odds?"

"Nothing I can gather on this mountain." Wren stared into the fire. "There's a thing I could try. It's not herb-craft. It's the other kind. The kind that gets you a pyre if Aldric's people see it, and gets you dead if you do it wrong even if they don't."

"Then do it," Isandro said simply.

"It's not that simple."

"Why not?"

And there it was — the question, the real one, the one Wren had spent three weeks outrunning since the ford, since before the ford, since the day she'd agreed to guide five strangers toward a door she'd only ever known as a story. She looked at Isandro's face in the firelight, young and scorched and certain, and she understood that she was going to have to say it out loud at last, because this girl deserved better than a healer who rationed her courage.

"Because I'm afraid it won't work," Wren said. "Not the working. The working might work. I'm afraid the Door won't."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, Perrin shifted in his sleep and murmured numbers — he often counted locks in his dreams, he'd told them once, thousands of them, all humming.

"Sef says the Door of Anew moves along the coronation road," Wren went on, and her voice came out lower than she intended. "He says it surfaces at the Stone Choir within the moon. He says it's the last threshold out of the erasure, the last way through to — to whatever Vessia was before Aldric's kind unmade it. And I have walked with you all this way on the strength of his maps and his certainty, and I have let myself believe it, because believing it was the only thing that kept me walking." She turned her hands over, palm up, the way she did when she was reading a body. "But I'm a hedge-witch, Isandro. I deal in what's in front of me. Fever, pus, broken bones, babies that won't turn. I fix what's real. And every mile closer to that mountain, some part of me has been whispering that I've traded my whole life's discipline for a legend — that when we reach the Stone Choir, there will be nothing there but stones and wind and hunters, and my sister's ashes will still be in this satchel, and I will have led all of you to die on a mountainside for a story I needed to be true."

The confession hung in the smoke. Wren felt lighter and sicker for having said it, the way a lanced wound feels.

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "May I tell you something? About tasting."

Wren blinked. "If you like."

"The first year, Dame Ovelin told me that immunity was a discipline. That if I kept my mind ordered, kept my tongue clean, kept my fears small, the poisons wouldn't take. I believed her for years. I built my whole self around it — every morning, the ritual, the order, the control." Isandro's eyes were on the fire. "And then I learned it was never discipline at all. It was magic, sealed in me before I could walk, and everything I thought was true about myself was a story someone needed me to believe." She looked up. "The story was false. But the woman the story built is real. She walked out of Hollowmere. She crossed the ford. She's sitting next to you." A pause. "Maybe the Door is like that. Maybe it doesn't matter whether Sef's maps are scripture. What matters is what the walking built."

Wren laughed — a short, wet, surprised sound. "That's a very fine speech for a girl who claims she lies badly."

"I do lie badly. That wasn't a lie." Isandro reached over and took Wren's hand, her grip warm and strong, a cook's grip, a survivor's grip. "Do the working. Whatever it costs. If the Door is a story, then let's make it a true one. We're good at making things true. We've been doing it since Hollowmere."

* * *

The working took until dawn.

Wren sent Isandro and Perrin outside — not for secrecy, but because what she did next was ugly, and children shouldn't watch, and neither should women who had already seen enough ugliness to fill a kingdom. She built the fire up. She opened her satchel and took out the small clay jar she had carried for eleven years and never once opened: her sister's ashes, or most of them, the portion she'd kept back from the river where the rest had gone.

Ashes of a gifted woman, burned by Aldric's purge, gathered by the sister who failed to save her. Old craft said such ash held residue — not power exactly, but *insistence*, the stubborn echo of a gift that had refused to be extinguished politely. Hedge-witches weren't supposed to touch it. Hedge-witches were supposed to heal, not traffic in the dead.

"Haleth," Wren said to the jar, using the name she hadn't spoken aloud in eleven years. "You were always the reckless one. Lend me your recklessness."

She opened the jar and tipped a measure of ash into the boiling water, and began the working — the deep one, the forbidden-adjacent one, the one her mother had described once and never demonstrated, drawing the infection up and out through Sef's skin the way you draw a splinter with a needle and a steady breath. It hurt him even unconscious; he arched against the pallet, and Wren put her whole weight on his chest and sang the low wordless song and did not stop, even when the sweat ran into her eyes, even when the ash-water turned black, even when her own vision started to swim and she tasted copper and realized the working was taking payment from her too, as the deep ones always did.

Somewhere in the smallest hour, Sef stopped thrashing.

Somewhere in the smallest hour, the fever broke — truly broke, the way it should have days ago, the wrongness under his skin finally, grudgingly, yielding.

Wren sat back on the floor of the hut, shaking, empty, older than she'd been at sunset. The fire had burned to embers. Through the cracks in the shutter, the sky was going grey at the edges, and far away down the valley she could hear — or imagined she could hear — a faint hum, like a lock the size of a mountain beginning, very slowly, to be picked.

She crawled to the pallet and pressed her ear near Sef's mouth. Breath. Slow, deep, even. Alive.

"Well," she whispered, to the jar, to her sister, to the grey light coming through the shutter. "Either your recklessness just saved him, or I've finally lost my mind entirely." She closed the jar with trembling hands. "Either way, Haleth — we're going to make it a true story. All of it. I promise you."

Behind her, on the pallet, Sef stirred, and his cracked lips moved, and this time the words were clear enough to catch:

"The third fold," he breathed. "Check the third fold. We have less time than I told them."
