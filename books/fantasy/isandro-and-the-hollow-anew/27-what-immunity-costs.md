## 27. What Immunity Costs

Sef's fever broke on the third night, and Isandro slept for the first time in what felt like a season — a thin, grey sleep full of running water and humming metal, the kind of rest that left her more tired than waking.

She rose before dawn out of habit. In Hollowmere she had risen before dawn because the regent's first meal was served at six, and a taster who arrived late was a taster who had already decided which dishes were safe. Here there was no regent, no kitchen bell, no tray to meet with her lips. There was only the ruined waystation Wren had chosen for their camp, its roof half-collapsed, its hearth still holding last night's coals, and Sef breathing slow and even under two blankets in the corner.

She knelt beside him without meaning to. Old instinct. In the palace she had checked the breathing of servants after every banquet, counting the ones who'd eaten from trays she'd cleared. It was not tenderness, exactly. It was bookkeeping.

His breath came steady. His color was better. The wound in his side, where the hunter's blade had opened him at the ford, was angry red but no longer the black of corruption. Wren had worked three nights on it, burning through her stores of moss and marrow-salve and something bitter she called graveleaf, and when Isandro had asked if he would live, Wren had said only: *He'll live. Whether he forgives himself is another matter.* Isandro hadn't asked what that meant. She suspected she knew. Sef had been reading his maps when the hunters came, maps spread open like a man showing his chest to a knife.

She fed the fire, put water on, and sat back on her heels.

And felt it again.

The thing she had been feeling for days now, the thing she had been very carefully not naming. It started behind her sternum, a pressure like a hand pressed flat against her breastbone from the inside. Not pain. Fullness. As though she were a cup being filled by someone patient, drop by drop, and the drops were coming faster.

She held up her hand in the firelight and watched the flame lean toward her fingers.

Not flicker. *Lean* — the whole tongue of fire bending like wheat in wind, straining toward her palm as if she were the sun and it a foolish moth. When she curled her fingers closed, the flame righted itself, sulking. When she opened them, it leaned again.

In Hollowmere, magic could not follow. That was the first truth she had ever learned, older than her own name. The valley was hollowed ground; the gift died at its borders like a flower cut from its stem. She had tested herself once, weeks ago, in the dark of her old room — a candle flame bending toward her finger, terror and wonder at war in her chest — and she had buried the evidence beneath the only explanation that let her sleep: *it isn't real, or it isn't mine, or the valley is leaking, or I am imagining it.*

She was done imagining explanations.

"Stop that," Wren said from the doorway.

Isandro closed her fist. The hedge-witch stood silhouetted against the pre-dawn blue, a bundle of kindling under one arm, her grey-streaked hair tied back with a strip of leather. She looked tired in the way stone looks tired — worn smooth, still standing.

"I wasn't doing anything," Isandro said.

"You were sitting very still and letting it happen to you." Wren dropped the kindling by the hearth and crouched across the fire from her. "There's a difference. Sit still long enough around a thing like yours and it starts making decisions."

"What do you know about it?"

Wren's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "I hid gifts for eleven years. Tucked them down inside people like stones into riverbeds, so the hoods' devices would sing past them. You learn what a gift feels like when it's sleeping, and what it feels like when it's dreaming." She nodded at Isandro's closed fist. "Yours isn't sleeping. Yours is drowning."

*

Wren made her sit for it after breakfast, while Perrin was out in the treeline listening to whatever twelve-year-olds who hear doors listen to, and Sef slept on. She built a small fire, set a copper bowl of water between them, and told Isandro to hold her hands over it and think of nothing.

The water began to move.

Not boil — *move*, turning in the bowl in a slow, deliberate spiral, climbing a finger's width up the sides and holding there, defying the bowl's curve, as though the water had remembered it was once rain and wanted to be rain again. Isandro snatched her hands back and the spiral collapsed.

"That's not possible here," Isandro said. Her voice sounded far away to her. "We're days past the valley. Magic works out here. But that's not — I don't have magic. I'm a taster. My whole life, poisons pass through me and nothing happens. Nothing has ever happened. I'm the emptiest vessel in Vessia. Was in Vessia." She heard herself stumbling over the country's name and hated it. "Whatever this is, it's new. It started when we crossed the border."

"Did it?" Wren asked quietly.

Isandro opened her mouth. Closed it.

"The candle," Wren said. "In your room in Hollowmere. You flinch every time you see an open flame. You've been doing it since I met you — you stand with your hands behind your back near fires, like a soldier at attention. I thought it was palace training. It isn't, is it."

"No," Isandro said. "It isn't."

"And the broth. Tell me about the broth."

So Isandro told her. About the kitchens of Hollowmere, the laced broth meant for Dame Ovelin, the hundred small poisons she had sipped over nine years — hemlock in the wine, arsenic in the sweetmeats, the slow metals they never told her the names of — and how each one passed through her like water through a net. How she had been proud of it. How she had thought it was discipline, a tongue trained to iron, a body honed by routine into something poison couldn't argue with.

"I thought I was immune because I was strong," she finished. "That was the story. Nine years of that story."

"And now?"

"Now I think—" She stopped. The pressure behind her sternum pulsed, patient as a second heartbeat. "Now I think I was never empty at all."

Wren stirred the fire. For a long moment the only sound was Sef's breathing and the wind worrying the broken roof.

"Where I grew up," Wren said at last, "there was a woman who kept bees. Not honeybees — the wild kind, the mountain kind, that sting like sin and die anyway. She wore no veil. Never got stung. Everyone said she was blessed, or touched, or lying. She told me once, when I was young enough to ask questions strangers answered: *They don't sting me because I am already full of their poison. A bee knows when there's no room.*" She looked up. "You understand what I'm telling you."

Isandro's hands had gone cold. "The valley didn't suppress my gift."

"It corked it." Wren's voice was gentle and merciless, the voice she used for delivering prognoses. "Hollowmere doesn't kill magic, girl. It holds it. Every valley like it is a stoppered jar — that's what the unmaking was, that's what Aldric's masters built. They didn't destroy the gift. You can't destroy it. They *bottled* it, sealed it into the low places, and called the seal peace. And you—" she pointed, and her finger did not quite tremble, but it wanted to— "you were born inside the jar. Born with a gift, in a place designed to hold gifts shut. So it never left you. It never burned off the way it does in children outside, the little sparks that fade by seven. It just stayed. Nine-and-twenty years of staying. Every poison you drank didn't pass through you. It *fed* you. Or your gift ate it, or it went into the store — I don't have the word for it, I'm a hedge-witch, not a philosopher. But nothing about you is empty, Isandro Dunmore. You are the fullest vessel I have ever stood next to. And a full vessel—"

"—breaks," Isandro said.

"A full vessel cracks," Wren corrected. "Breaking is fast. Cracking takes years. You've been cracking since the day you were born. The question is what happens when the cork comes out."

*

Perrin came back mid-morning and found them still sitting by the dead fire, and read the silence between them the way he read everything — instantly, completely, and out loud.

"You're scared," he said, dropping cross-legged beside Isandro. He smelled of pine sap and cold air. "Both of you. But you're scared different. Wren's scared-of-herself scared. You're scared-for-everyone scared."

"Perrin—"

"Sef does that too," he went on, relentless as weather. "Scared-for-everyone. That's why he hides in his maps. Tam used to do it. He'd get scared and start fixing things that weren't broke." He tilted his head at her, and his eyes — those strange pale eyes that saw doors everywhere — narrowed. "Something's wrong with you. Inside-wrong. I can hear it."

"Hear it," Isandro repeated.

"There's a sound everything makes. Locked things hum. Doors hum loudest. People hum too, most people, quiet, like a house at night." He frowned, and reached out and laid two fingers, feather-light, against her wrist. Then he pulled them back as if scalded. "Oh," he said softly. "You're loud."

"Loud."

"Like a door that's locked from both sides. Like a door somebody nailed shut a long time ago, and the nails are—" he searched, twelve years old and fumbling for the shape of it— "*pulling*. The wood wants to open and the nails want to hold and you can hear both of them fighting." He looked up at her with terrible simplicity. "It's going to be loud when it opens, isn't it."

"Perrin," Isandro said, and her voice cracked in the middle, "what happens to a door when the nails come out?"

He considered this with the seriousness he gave all questions, which was the seriousness other people gave funerals.

"Doors don't break," he said finally. "Doors just become openings. It's the wall around them you have to worry about."

*

That evening Sef woke properly for the first time, gray-faced and furious at his own weakness, and demanded his maps. Wren pretended not to hear. Isandro fetched them.

He propped himself against the wall with the leather tube across his knees and drew out the coronation road chart — the one with the moving Door, the one that said the Stone Choir within the moon — and studied it with the fierce concentration of a man proving he still existed. Isandro sat nearby, mending a strap, and watched him, and felt the pressure behind her sternum swell another fraction, the way it swelled now every few hours, tide-like, inevitable.

"Sef," she said. "When you mapped Vessia. Before. What did the gifted feel like? Around you. Did you ever notice?"

He didn't look up. "Everyone felt like everyone. I'm a cartographer, not a diviner. Distances, heights, boundaries. People were just terrain." A pause. His quill stopped. "Why?"

"Because I need to know if I'm becoming terrain," she said. "Or weather."

He did look up then. Whatever he saw in her face made him set the quill down. "Tell me."

So she told him that too — the flame leaning, the water spiraling, Perrin's fingers jerking back from her wrist, the pressure that came now in waves, and the other things, the things she had been hoarding like a miser hoards evidence of her own ruin. Three nights ago she had caught a falling kettle one-handed from across the room without deciding to move; her arm had simply already been there. Two nights ago she had tasted the stream water and known, absolutely and without reasoning, that a dead ewe lay upstream beyond the bend — and it had. Last night, half-asleep, she had heard the camp's surroundings the way Perrin described hearing locks: every tree, every stone, every closed thing humming its own note, and hers underneath them all, vast and nailed-shut and *straining*.

"And today," she said, "at noon. I was angry. About the ford, about Sef bleeding, about all of it. And for a moment I wanted the hunters to come back. All of them. Because I think—" her voice dropped, ashamed— "I think I could do something to them now. Something large. And I *wanted* to want it. Do you understand? The wanting wanted me."

The fire popped. Sef rolled his map closed with careful hands.

"In the old records," he said slowly, "the ones that survived the erasure — the ones I copied before I understood what I was copying — there's a phrase that recurs. *Vessel-born*. Children born inside the hollowed valleys, whose gifts never escaped. The texts call them the kingdom's savings. Its stored treasury." He met her eyes. "Aldric needs gifted blood to hold his spell together. We've assumed he means ordinary bearers. But Isandro — if he learned what you are—"

"He'd stop hunting me," Isandro said, "and start harvesting me."

Nobody spoke. Outside, dusk was coming down blue and cold, and somewhere in the treeline Perrin was singing to himself in the dead tongue he'd picked up from no one, a tuneless child's song about a door that opens from the inside.

*

She took the middle watch, as she always did, because sleep had become a country she visited less and less.

The camp breathed around her: Sef's fever-cool sleep, Wren's light healer's doze, Perrin sprawled like a dropped puppet. The fire was a red eye. Beyond it the mountains shouldered up against the stars, and somewhere past them, two days maybe three, the Stone Choir waited with its patient song.

Isandro sat with her palms open on her knees and let herself look at it directly for the first time. Not the flame-bending, not the water-turning — the thing itself, the pressure, the fullness. She breathed toward it the way you breathe toward a locked door in a dream, knowing the lock is yours.

And it turned.

Just a hair. Just enough. Warmth flooded up through her chest like the first swallow of soup on a winter day, and for one impossible moment she could feel the whole camp — the exact shape of Sef's hurt, Wren's banked grief, Perrin's dreaming hum — written on her skin like heat from a hearth. She could feel the mountains. She could feel the *road*, the old erased coronation road beneath the mud and moss, still humming its dead kingdom's note, still pointing, patient as a compass needle, toward the Choir.

Then the warmth hit the walls of her, the nine-and-twenty-year walls, the cork and the nails and the seal, and stopped. Held. The pressure spiked — a band of white-hot tightness around her ribs — and faded, and she was sitting alone by a dying fire with frost forming on her eyelashes and tears she had no memory of starting.

Her hands were glowing. Faintly, gutteringly, the way embers glow under ash — a soft gold light rising from her palms, from the lines of them, from the old burn scars of nine years of tasting. She watched it pulse twice, three times, in time with her heart.

Then it guttered out, and the cold rushed in where it had been, and Isandro understood, with the clarity of the fully damned, what Wren had been trying to tell her all day.

She was not becoming stronger. She was becoming *less* — less the woman who counted servants' breaths, who lied badly and hated it, who carried guilt like a stone in her shoe. Every day the vessel strained, some part of her went into the mortar of the walls, and what would be left when the cork finally gave was anyone's guess. Power as erosion. The sea does not defeat the cliff by attacking. It only keeps arriving.

From the darkness at the treeline, Perrin's voice drifted into the camp, still singing, and then stopped mid-verse.

A moment later he padded into the firelight, wide awake, barefoot in the frost, and Isandro saw his expression and was on her feet before she knew it.

"They changed their song," Perrin said. "The stones. Under the mountains. They were singing slow, like sleeping. Now they're singing like—" he swallowed— "like someone lit a lamp inside them. They know you're coming, Isandro. The Door knows." He grabbed her glowing-faded, frost-cold hand in both of his small ones. "But there's another sound too. Far off. Metal and boots and humming machines. Lots of them. And they're not following our trail anymore."

He looked up at her, pale eyes enormous.

"They're following *yours*."
