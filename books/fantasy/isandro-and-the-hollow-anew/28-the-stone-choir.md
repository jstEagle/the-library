## 28. The Stone Choir

Perrin heard the Door three days before anyone else did.

It came to him first as a change in the wind. Not the wind itself — Perrin had stopped trusting wind years ago, back when he'd learned that a door could be locked from either side — but in what the wind carried. Every place had a sound underneath it, if you knew how to listen. Farmhouses hummed low and contented, like sleeping cattle. Inns buzzed with a hundred small locks and latches, restless as wasps in a jar. The free city of Callowmark had roared so loud behind its gates that he'd stuffed wax in his ears for a week and still dreamed of hinges.

But this sound, drifting down off the high moor where the coronation road climbed into the old country, was different from anything he'd ever heard.

It was patient.

That was the only word for it, and Perrin hated that he needed a word at all, because words were Sef's territory and Isandro's and Wren's, and his was the hearing itself, the thing that lived under his skin like a second heartbeat. But there was no other way to say it. Doors were never patient. Doors wanted things — to be opened, to be shut, to be knocked upon. This sound beneath the mountains didn't want. It waited. It had been waiting so long that waiting had become its whole shape, the way a river's shape becomes its bed.

"It's close," Perrin said, on the morning they crested the ridge and saw nothing but grey stone and greyer sky. "Really close. Under us. Or ahead of us. It's hard to tell because it's so big."

"How big?" Isandro asked. She always asked him that, and she always listened to the answer like it mattered, which was why Perrin had decided — without asking anyone's permission, because you didn't ask permission for family — that he would follow her anywhere.

"Big," he said. "Like the city. Like all of Callowmark folded up small and buried."

Sef pulled up short on the road ahead, one hand shading his eyes against the flat white light. The cartographer had gone strange over the last two days of climbing — quieter even than usual, touching the standing stones they passed like a man checking the pulse of an old friend who ought to be dead. "The Stone Choir should be over this rise," he said. "If my maps are right."

"Your maps are always right," Wren said. She walked with her sister's ashes in a clay urn slung at her hip, and she touched it now, the way she touched it a dozen times an hour, a small unconscious motion like worrying a tooth. "That's what got them confiscated."

"My maps being right is the only reason any of us are on this road at all." Sef said it mildly, but Perrin heard the tightness under it, the same tightness that lived in all of them now, wound tighter with every mile. They had left Callowmark nine days ago, ahead of Veyra's net, and every night since, Perrin had lain awake listening for the hunters' devices — those humming brass things that found the gift the way his own ears found doors. He hadn't heard them yet. That didn't mean they weren't there. It meant they were far enough away that the world swallowed their song before it reached him.

He kept that to himself. Some knowledge was a weight you carried so others didn't have to.

They crested the rise together, and the Stone Choir opened below them like a held breath.

* * *

There were more than a hundred pillars, maybe more than two hundred, standing in long curving rows across a shallow bowl of moorland, and no two of them were alike. Some were tall as watchtowers, some no higher than Perrin's shoulder. Some stood straight; others leaned together in pairs and clusters, the way people lean toward each other when they're sharing a secret. The stone was veined with something pale that caught the light and gave it back slow, like the glow lived inside the rock rather than on it. Snow lay in the hollows between them, untouched, unmarked by any track.

And when the wind moved through them, they sang.

Not whistled. Not hummed. *Sang.* Each pillar gave out a single deep note as the air passed over it, and the notes braided together into chords that rolled across the bowl and up the slopes and out over the moor, and Perrin felt the sound go through his chest and his teeth and the soles of his feet, and behind his eyes something enormous turned over in its sleep.

"There it is," he whispered. "Underneath. Under the singing. That's it. That's the Door."

Isandro crouched beside him and put her palm flat on the frozen ground, the way she did when she wanted to listen with her body instead of her ears. "I can't hear it," she said. "But I can feel it. Like a drumbeat under the floor of a house."

"That's exactly what it's like!" Perrin said, delighted despite everything. "You're getting good at hearing without ears."

"I had a good teacher." She ruffled his hair, and he ducked away, too old for that, but he let her do it anyway, because she meant it kindly and kindness was scarce on the coronation road.

Sef had already spread his maps on a flat-topped stone, weighting the corners with pebbles, though the wind kept trying to take them. "The Choir sits at the crossing of every ley the old kingdom measured," he said, tracing lines with a cracked fingernail. "Every coronation road in Vessia ends here. Kings were made on this ground — or near it. The Door surfaces where the roads converge, and according to the pattern I found, it will surface here within—" he glanced at the sky, measuring, "—a moonrise or two. Tonight, most likely. The moon is nearly full."

"And Aldric knows the same thing we know," Wren said. Her voice was flat, the healer's voice she used when she was delivering news nobody wanted. "Because Veyra has the same maps. Because we sold them to buy our way out of Callowmark, and Sef stole them back, and somewhere between the theft and the gate, someone counted what was missing."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing to answer. They all knew. They had known since the night in Callowmark when Sef had come back to the inn with the recovered maps and a look on his face like a man who'd realized the ladder he was climbing was also the rope around his neck. Their escape had been quiet, but not silent. And Captain Veyra did not strike Perrin as a woman who missed much.

"So we have until moonrise," Isandro said. "Maybe less. We find the Door's exact spot, we prepare, and we—"

"We wait," Wren finished. "We wait, and we hope the hunters don't come, and if they come, we run, or we fight, or we—" She stopped. Her hand was on the urn again. "Or whatever we decide at the hearth. We already decided."

Perrin wasn't listening to the argument. He was listening to the Choir.

The pillars sang, yes, but they sang differently depending on where you stood, and Perrin had begun walking the rows while the others talked, head tilted, following the sound the way water follows a slope. Near the edge of the bowl, the notes were thin and scattered. Toward the center, they thickened, layered, harmonized — and underneath them, deeper than stone-deep, the Door's patience pulsed like a heart in a sleeping giant.

Except.

Perrin stopped between two leaning pillars, one tall and one broken, and frowned.

There was another sound. Faint. Far. Coming from the east, from the direction of the road they'd climbed, and it was not patient at all.

It was brass. It was humming. It was many voices in unison, moving fast.

* * *

He ran back to them across the snow, boots punching through the crust, and grabbed Isandro's sleeve before he remembered he was twelve and supposed to be too proud for grabbing sleeves. "They're coming," he said. "The hunters. A lot of them. East of us, past the ridge — I can hear the devices, dozens of them, all humming the same tune. They're not searching. They're coming straight here."

The argument died. Four faces turned to him, and Perrin watched the fear move through each of them in a different way: Sef went still and calculating, eyes dropping to his maps; Wren's hand closed white-knuckled around the urn; Isandro's jaw set in that line it made whenever she decided to be brave instead of sensible, which was most of the time lately.

"How far?" Sef asked.

"A day? Half a day? They're moving fast. Faster than us." Perrin pressed his palms over his ears, then dropped them; blocking the sound never helped anyway, it just made the world feel muffled and wrong. "They'll be here by moonrise. Maybe sooner. They're not even trying to hide."

"They don't need to hide," Sef said quietly. "They have the same map we do. They know where the Door surfaces. They don't need to search for us — they only need to stand between us and it." He began rolling the maps with quick, precise hands. "We've been walking toward the same destination as an army. I keep telling myself the geography bought us time. It may have bought them instead."

"Then we find the Door now," Isandro said. "Before moonrise. Before they arrive. If it opens early—"

"It won't." Sef shook his head. "The Choir sings it partway open at moonrise — that's what the records say, what little survived the erasure. Partway. Enough to show the threshold. Full passage comes later, at the true conjunction, and I don't know when that is, because the people who wrote it down were very thorough about being erased." He looked around at them, and for once the disgraced cartographer looked less like a man with answers than a man holding a candle in a very large dark room. "We have hours, perhaps. And an army at our backs."

Perrin tugged Isandro's sleeve again. "Can I show you something? While they're still far?"

She followed him into the rows of stones, and the others trailed after, because they always did, because Perrin's ears had become the party's compass and everyone knew it. He led them toward the center of the bowl, weaving between pillars, following the pulse of the Door as it grew louder and deeper and more impossibly calm. The singing swelled around them until speech felt rude, until Wren mouthed something that might have been a prayer or a curse.

Then the ground changed.

It was subtle — a softness underfoot, a snowdrift that gave differently — but Perrin felt it like a shout. He knelt and swept the snow aside with both mittens, and beneath it the stone was warm. Not slightly warm. Warm as bread. Warm as a hearthstone at midnight. In a bowl of frozen moorland under a white sky, a circle of ground perhaps ten paces wide was warm as living flesh, and faintly, faintly, veins of that pale inner light pulsed beneath it in rhythm.

Rhythm. Patient. Heartbeat.

"Here," Perrin said. "This is where it sleeps. Right here. It's been sleeping so long it forgot how to stop."

Isandro knelt beside him and laid her hand on the warm stone. Perrin watched her face do something complicated — wonder and fear and recognition all fighting for the same space. "It feels like me," she said softly. "Sealed. Waiting. Full of something it isn't allowed to be yet."

Wren crouched on her other side, and her breath caught audibly. "The warmth. The pulse. Isandro, this is what your magic feels like when I heal near you. The same signature. The Door and you—" She stopped herself, but the thought finished itself in the silence, and everyone heard it.

The valley had corked Isandro. The Door had been corked by a whole kingdom's erasure. Two sealed vessels, one small and one vast, and both of them waiting on this hilltop for the same moonrise.

"Well," Sef said at last, into the singing. "That's either the best omen I've ever encountered or the worst."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, because preparing was the only thing that kept the fear from eating them alive.

Wren walked the perimeter of the bowl and marked the paths a runner could take — north to the fells, west along the stream-cut, south down the scree. Sef studied his maps and the pillars together, matching stone to ink, muttering names in the dead tongue that the Choir seemed almost to answer, each syllable drawing a new chord from the rocks. Isandro built a fire in the lee of the tallest pillar and cooked, because cooking was what she did when the world tilted, and the smell of onions and barley in the middle of the haunted moor was so absurdly domestic that Perrin laughed out loud, alone, and then pretended he hadn't.

Perrin himself did the only useful thing he knew how to do. He listened.

He walked the eastern rim of the bowl and lay down in the snow with his ear against a fallen pillar, and he tracked the hunters' devices as they came. Dozens of them now, their brass hum braided into a single droning chord, and threaded through the chord — footsteps. Hundreds of footsteps. Wagons. The jingle of harness. And once, briefly, cutting through everything else like a blade through cloth, a human voice giving orders in a tone that was calm and precise and utterly without mercy.

He knew that voice. Everyone who'd fled Callowmark knew that voice.

Veyra.

Perrin lay in the snow a long moment, listening to her army climb toward them, and he thought about how Isandro talked about her sometimes at night, when she thought he was asleep — not with hatred, which would have been simple, but with a strange reluctant respect, the way you talk about a storm that spared your house. *She hunts people, not prizes,* Isandro had said once. *I saw it in her eyes at the inn. That's worse, somehow.*

The sun went down behind the western fells and the sky burned orange and then bruised purple, and the temperature fell so fast the snow squeaked underfoot, and the Choir's singing changed. It deepened. The scattered notes of daylight drew together, aligned, began to move toward a single chord like a crowd turning to face a stage.

Moonrise was coming. So was the army.

Perrin ran back to the fire. "They're in the pass," he said, breathless. "The last pass before the moor. An hour, maybe less. And Isandro — the big device is with them. The huge one, the one that hums so low it feels like sickness. It's coming too."

Isandro rose slowly from the fire. The light of it moved on her face, and Perrin saw her make the decision before she said it, saw it settle over her like armor being buckled on.

"Then we don't run," she said. "Running was always the plan, and running was always going to fail, because they have the same map and better horses. We stand at the threshold. All of us. Whatever comes up the pass tonight, it finds us already there."

"The Door opens partway at moonrise," Sef said. "Partway. We cannot cross fully, and we cannot hide, and we will be standing in a ring of singing stones lit up like a beacon when they arrive."

"Yes," Isandro said simply.

Wren laughed — a short, sharp, unhinged sound that had grief inside it. "Of course. Of course this is how it goes. We finally reach it, and the whole hunt arrives with us." She lifted the urn from her hip and held it against her chest with both arms, the way you hold a child. "My sister crossed half of Vessia to get away from men like these. I will not step aside for them now."

Perrin stood between them, twelve years old, ears full of the coming thunder of hooves and the rising song of stones and the vast patient heartbeat underneath everything, and he understood suddenly, completely, what the adults kept forgetting:

Doors didn't only open outward.

Whatever happened tonight, whatever came through that threshold or broke against it, none of them would ever be the people who had walked up the coronation road. The Door was listening. He could feel it listening. And so, he realized with a cold slide in his stomach, could something else — because far to the east, beneath the droning chord of the hunters' devices, there was a new sound now, a sound he had no name for, vast and wrong and hungry, like the erasure itself had grown ears.

The moon cleared the fells.

The Stone Choir took a breath, every pillar at once, and began to sing the Door of Anew awake — and down in the pass, torchlight bloomed like a wound in the dark, hundreds strong, converging.
