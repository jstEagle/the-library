## 29. Aldric's Herald

The cough came at dusk, as it always did.

King Aldric Vane felt it building in his chest an hour before it arrived — a slow gathering of wet heat behind the sternum, like a tide pulling back before it broke. He had learned to read its approach the way sailors read weather, and so he set down his pen, capped the ink, and turned in his chair to face the window of the Grey Keep's highest chamber, where he could cough in private and pretend, for a few moments, that he was not dying.

Below him, Hollowmere lay in its bowl of hills, lamplight kindling street by street, the palace kitchens already breathing woodsmoke into the cold air. A pretty valley. A quiet one. He had made it quiet himself, eleven years ago, when the last of Vessia's name was struck from the rolls and its magic was poured out of the world like wine from a cracked cask. The valleys were where the pouring went. Hollowmere held more of it than most — he could feel it sometimes on still nights, a pressure against his skin like the silence inside a bell.

He coughed then, long and deep, and pressed a folded cloth to his mouth, and when he drew it away there was blood on the linen. There was always blood now. It had been six years since the first fleck; four since the physicians stopped meeting his eyes; two since he dismissed them all and kept only the apothecary who asked no questions and the alchemist who could not answer them.

*Poison,* they had whispered, in the early years, before whispering became dangerous. *Slow poison, administered over months, perhaps years.* They had gone back through every taster's ledger in the keep, every record of every dish that had passed his lips in the year the sickness took root, and found nothing. No taster had fallen ill. No dish had been flagged. Whatever was killing him had slipped past the finest tongue in the valley as if it had not been there at all — or as if the tongue itself had been part of the recipe.

He had executed three tasters anyway. It had changed nothing, except that afterward no one would serve him food he had not watched being carried from a sealed kitchen by soldiers he trusted, and even then he tasted copper on everything, real or imagined, and some nights he simply did not eat.

Aldric folded the bloody cloth into quarters and set it beneath a leaden box on the side table, where the others waited. Forty-one of them now. He had counted this morning. He tried not to count them anymore.

"Your Majesty."

The voice came from the doorway without a knock — only one man in the Grey Keep entered unannounced, and he was dead, or nearly, which amounted to the same courtesy. Aldric did not turn. "You're late, Corvess."

"The stairs are long," said the alchemist, "and I am old." Old was generous. Corvess had been old when Aldric found him, twenty years ago, in a cellar beneath a burned library in what had once been northern Vessia, surrounded by books whose titles dissolved when you looked at them directly. He had been old when the unmaking was still a theory scrawled in a dead language, and he had grown no older since, which was its own kind of horror. His skin had the grey translucence of parchment held to a flame. "And the work required me below. The lattice is thinning again."

"How thin?"

"Thin enough that I felt it in my teeth." Corvess crossed the room with a sound like dry leaves and lowered himself into the chair across the desk, uninvited, unhurried. Between them, on the desk's scarred oak, sat the thing they both pretended not to look at: a shallow bowl of black glass, and within it, suspended in nothing, a lattice of light no thicker than a spider's thread — a web of faint gold filaments strung between seven points of darker gold, like a crown seen through fog.

It was beautiful. That was the cruelest part. Eleven years ago, when Aldric had stood in the ruins of the Vessian court and spoken the words Corvess had taught him — words that unmade a country the way fire unmakes a letter, leaving ash and shape but no name — the spell had needed something to hold its edges. Erasure is not destruction; destruction leaves a wound, and wounds heal. Erasure must be *maintained*. Every day, the world tries to remember what you have taken from it. Every day, the name of Vessia presses up against the lie like grass under a paving stone, and every day the lattice presses back.

And every day, the lattice spent itself doing so.

"We have a moon," Corvess said. "Perhaps less. The filaments are down to hair-width at the anchor points. When the last one parts—" He made a small gesture, fingers opening like a bird released. "Everything comes back. All at once. Every erased milestone, every scratched-out name, every grave we filled with someone else's history. And the magic with it. All of it, everywhere, in a single breath."

"And me with it," Aldric said.

"You especially," Corvess agreed, with the mildness of a man discussing rainfall. "You are the keystone, Majesty. The words were spoken in your voice. When the lattice fails, the unmaking does not simply stop — it *reverses*, and it reverses through the one who spoke it. You will be the first thing Vessia remembers."

Outside, the valley darkened another shade. Aldric rose, walked to the window, put his back to the black-glass bowl. He had learned, over eleven years, to govern a kingdom from a room he could not bear to face.

"There is a remedy," he said. Not a question. He had asked it a thousand times, in a thousand tones — pleading once, early on, which shamed him now; imperious after that; finally just tired. Corvess gave the same answer he always gave, because it was the only answer there was.

"Gifted blood. Freely given, freely flowing, drawn at the moment of transfer from a vessel strong enough to hold what the lattice has spent." The old man's voice dropped into the cadence of recitation; he had said these words so often they had worn smooth as river stones. "The lattice is made of stolen magic, Majesty. Stolen things mend only with willing payment. One bearer, unsealed into the lattice, and the web renews itself for another lifetime. Perhaps longer. Perhaps forever, if the vessel is rich enough."

"And the vessels we have taken are not rich enough."

Corvess was silent a moment. In the silence, Aldric heard the lattice humming — or imagined he did; the physicians had suggested his hearing was going the way of everything else. Seven points of gold, seven anchors sunk into seven valleys, and threaded through them all, the drained gift of every bearer his hunters had brought him in eleven years. Farmers' daughters. Hedge-priests. A fiddler from the coast roads. Children. He had told himself, in the beginning, that each one bought a decade of order, that a kingdom saved was worth any arithmetic. He had stopped telling himself anything, somewhere around the fifteenth anchor. Now the transfers simply happened, in a chamber below the keep, and he signed the ledgers, and the lattice brightened for a season, and the coughing continued, because the stolen magic was thin — thinned by distance from its owners, thinned by fear, thinned by death — and thin magic bought thin time.

"They are not enough," Corvess said. "Nor will the next hundred be. The lattice needs a sealed vessel. Magic that has never been spent. Magic that has had nowhere to go for a lifetime." He paused, and Aldric heard the old man's dry pleasure at what came next. "Magic like a corked bottle. Like a taster's tongue."

Aldric turned.

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the lattice hummed between them like a held note.

"A taster," Aldric said slowly. "You think the poison—"

"I think nothing. I know less than I pretend, which is how I have survived every king I have served." Corvess's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "But consider it, Majesty. A poison that kills no taster. A poison that slips past the finest guarded tongue in the valley as though it were invited. Either your kitchens failed you — and I have read those ledgers, and they did not fail — or the poison never touched the taster at all. Or rather—" the dry pleasure again, sharper now, "—it touched him and found nothing to touch. A body so thoroughly closed to outside influence that even death poured into it found no door. That is not immunity, Majesty. Immunity is a wall. What your tasters have is a *seal*. And a seal means something sealed *inside*."

The cough chose that moment to return, and Aldric let it take him, bending over the window ledge while his chest tore itself in long wet waves. When he straightened, the cloth came away soaked red, and Corvess watched him with the detached interest of a man observing a reaction nearing completion.

"Find them," Aldric said, when he could speak. "Every taster who served me in that year. Every one still living."

"They are being found. Two died of ordinary causes. Three remain — one in the eastern provinces, one too feeble to matter, and one—" Corvess tilted his head. "One who left Hollowmere nine days ago, by night, through the mill-race, past a hunter's device that should have sung her name to the rooftops and instead recorded nothing at all. A woman called Isandro Dunmore. Royal food-taster, twelve years' service, spotless record, no friends among the staff, no vices, no debts. A ghost in her own household until the night she ran."

Nine days. Nine days she had been in the world, and he had not known. The thought sat in him like a swallowed stone. "Why did no one report it?"

"Because she registered as nothing. Because the device read her as empty. Because, Majesty, she is exactly what I described: a vessel so well corked that our instruments mistake her for a jug with nothing in it." Corvess leaned forward, and the lamplight went through his parchment skin and lit him faintly from within, like a lantern made of paper. "She is the richest vessel in the world. She has been storing her whole life without knowing it. If we bring her to the lattice — if we unseal her into it, willingly or otherwise — the web will not merely renew. It will become permanent. Unbreakable. Vessia will stay unmade until the sun burns out, and you will stay alive to see it."

*Until the sun burns out.* Aldric looked at his own reflection in the dark glass, a grey-faced king with blood at the corner of his mouth, and felt the old hunger rise in him, the one that had started all of this — not ambition, nothing so clean, but the animal refusal of ending, the fist-clench of a drowning man. He had told himself once that he unmade Vessia for order, for peace, for the good of realms tired of magical wars. Lies age well. He had believed them so long they fit like tailored clothes.

Now he knew the truth of himself, and it was smaller and colder: he had done it because he was afraid to die, and he would do anything — had done everything — because he was still afraid.

"Veyra," he said.

"Captain Veyra reports from the field tonight. Her message arrived an hour ago." Corvess produced a slip of paper, thin as onion skin, and laid it on the desk beside the black-glass bowl. "I took the liberty of reading it."

Aldric crossed the room and picked it up. Veyra's hand was small and exact, each letter placed like a soldier on parade. He read it twice.

*Found the waystation boy. Interrogated. Confirms party of five: the fugitive taster Dunmore; the cartographer Ardenne; a hedge-witch of Marrowfen, name uncertain; the boy Perrin, hearing-gift, unregistered. They travel the coronation road. Subject displays anomalous readings — devices register absence, not presence. Request guidance.*

Beneath it, in the same precise hand, a second line:

*They seek the Door of Anew. I believe they will find it. Recommend immediate decision, Majesty. The Choir sings within the month.*

The Door of Anew. Even the name of it had survived the erasure only as rumor — a threshold out of hollowed Vessia, a way through the lie, the last unfinished business of a country he had killed. Corvess had told him about it years ago, in one of their midnight conferences: that the unmaking had a seam, that erasure on such a scale must leave one place where the old name still pressed through, one door the spell could not fully close. He had sent hunters to find it and they had come back with nothing, and eventually he had ordered it struck from even the unofficial records, because a door he could not close was a fear he preferred not to carry.

And now a taster with a sealed soul was walking toward it, with a disgraced mapmaker who had charted the unmade country, a witch, and a boy who could hear doors.

"She recommends a decision," Aldric said.

"She does. She is a careful officer. She knows the Door changes the arithmetic." Corvess steepled his grey fingers. "If they open it, the erasure begins to fail from the seam outward. If they merely pass through it, the country beyond remains raw, unnamed — dangerous, but containable. But if they open it and *name* what they find—" the old man's voice went very quiet, very dry, "—then Vessia returns with founders. With witnesses. With a story people can believe. Belief is mortar, Majesty. The lattice cannot hold against a nation that remembers itself on purpose."

"So the Door must not open."

"The Door must not open *for them*." Corvess unfolded his hands. "Consider, Majesty. A moving threshold, surfacing at the Stone Choir within the month. A vessel of unprecedented depth walking straight toward it, guided by the only mapmaker alive who understands the road. Do you see what she is? She is not merely a cure. She is a *keystone for a new arch*. Bring her to the Choir. Open the Door ourselves. Seal her into the threshold — her unspent lifetime poured into the seam — and the Door closes forever, the lattice drinks deeper than it has ever drunk, and the unmaking becomes eternal. Two birds, Majesty. One stone. One girl."

Aldric stood at the window a long time. Below, the valley had gone fully dark except for the keep's own torches, and somewhere down in the kitchens a door banged, and he thought — absurdly, uselessly — of soup, of the twelve years of dishes that had passed his lips untouched by harm, of the tongue that had tasted them all and kept him safe while the poison bloomed in him like frost in a wall. Had she known? Could a taster taste a thing that never touched her? Was there a morning, somewhere in those twelve years, when Isandro Dunmore had sipped his broth and felt, for half a heartbeat, something answering in her blood?

It didn't matter. Nothing mattered except the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was simple at last, after eleven years of variables: one girl's lifetime against his death; one sealed vessel against the return of everything he had buried. He had never flinched from the count before. He would not flinch now. Flinching was for men with time to regret.

He took up a pen. The ink was slow in the cold, and he had to shake the nib twice before it flowed.

"To Captain Veyra," he said, writing as he spoke, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "The subjects are to be taken alive. All of them. The taster above all — she is not to be marked, not to be bled, not to be frightened into spending whatever she carries. Fear spends magic, Corvess tells me. She is to be delivered to the Stone Choir calm, whole, and sealed. Tell Veyra—" he paused, pen hovering, and then wrote the line he knew would be obeyed, because Veyra was the best hunter he had ever owned and the best hunters understand economy, "—that the woman Dunmore is worth more than every other prize of this hunt combined, and that any hood who spills so much as a drop of her blood will answer to me personally."

He signed it. The wax was the grey of the keep, the grey of the hoods, the grey of the lattice's light. He pressed his seal into it and felt, distantly, the effort of even that small grip in his stiffening hand.

"She will want to know why," Corvess said. "Veyra. She asks questions. It is her flaw."

"Then tell her why." Aldric looked out at the dark valley, at the small lights of the town where ten thousand people slept believing the world was finished being made, and he put his hand flat against the cold glass as if he could press the cough back down into the tide. "Tell her their king is dying, and that the girl who fed him is the only medicine left in the world. Tell her to bring me my taster."

Corvess gathered the message and rose, leaves rustling, and shuffled toward the door. At the threshold he paused, head cocked, listening to something far below — the lattice, maybe, or the mountain, or the future.

"Majesty," he said, "one caution. A vessel that full, disturbed too roughly, may break rather than pour. And a broken seal releases everything at once. All of it. In one place." His parchment face was unreadable. "Pray your captain is gentle."

The door closed behind him.

Aldric stood alone with the humming bowl and the forty-one folded cloths and the dark, and after a while he began, quietly, to laugh — a dry, rusted sound with blood in it, the laughter of a man who has bet everything he has left on the gentleness of a hunter and the obedience of a girl with poison-proof veins.

Far to the south, under singing stones, a door waited to hear his name for it.
