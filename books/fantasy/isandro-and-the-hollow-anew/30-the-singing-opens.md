## 30. The Singing Opens

The moon rose the color of a fresh scar.

Isandro felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her sternum, the same sensation she'd carried since the ford, since the fever, since the night she'd finally understood that the valley had not emptied her but filled her to the brim and stoppered the neck. Now, standing among the pillars of the Stone Choir with that pressure swelling like water against a dam, she understood something else: the Choir was calling to her. Or perhaps she was calling to it. The distinction had stopped mattering somewhere on the coronation road.

There were thirty-one pillars. Sef had counted them twice, walking their circumference in the blue hour before dusk, muttering measurements to himself like prayers. They stood in no pattern any eye could fix — lean this way, lean that, some fallen, some snapped at the waist by some violence older than memory — and yet when the wind moved through them they sang in unison, a low chord that Isandro felt in her teeth rather than heard with her ears. Not music exactly. More like a name being pronounced very slowly, over and over, by a mouth made of stone.

"It's been singing since we arrived," Perrin said. He sat cross-legged at the base of the tallest pillar, his ear pressed flat against it, his whole body trembling the way it did when he'd found something enormous. "But it's getting louder. It knows the moon's coming."

"Can you hear the Door?" Wren asked. She was laying out her kit on a spread cloak — needle, gut-thread, the brown glass vials, the small clay jar of ash-and-honey paste — because Wren prepared for wounds the way other people prepared for weather.

Perrin nodded without lifting his ear from the stone. "It's under us. Deep. It's not humming like locks do. It's more like—" His brow furrowed; twelve years old and feral as a fox, and still the only one of them honest enough to admit when words failed him. "Like breathing. Like something asleep that's about to wake up and be very glad about it."

Sef stood at the Choir's edge with his maps spread across a flat-topped pillar, weighted at the corners with stones, though there was no wind worth weighing them against. He'd been quiet all day — quieter than usual, which for Sef meant nearly silent — and Isandro knew why. The wound in his side was three days closed now, knitted by Wren's craft, but the fever had left him thinner, grayer, older around the eyes. And he had spent those three days doing what Sef always did with fear: turning it into geography.

"The moonrise is within the hour," he said now, tapping the map without looking up. "Veyra's devices converge from the south and east. If she kept the pace I'd have kept, she camps at the tree line tonight and takes us at dawn." A pause. "If Aldric gave her leave to hurry, she takes us tonight."

"She won't attack during the opening," Wren said. It wasn't conviction; it was hope wearing conviction's clothes. "Not if she wants the Door. You don't burn down the only bridge while you're standing on it."

"You don't," Sef agreed. "But Veyra doesn't want the Door. She wants *us*. The Door is Aldric's want, and Aldric is a day behind her with an army." He rolled the map closed with careful hands, the way he handled everything he loved. "We have until moonrise. After that, we have whatever the Door gives us and nothing else."

Isandro looked up at the pillars. The chord deepened as the sky darkened, and beneath it — fainter, patient, vast — she felt the breathing Perrin had described. Something under the mountain of stone, waiting to be asked.

"And if it opens," she said, "and we go through — what then? We step into a country that might exist again, and Aldric's army arrives to find an empty choir." She shook her head slowly. "He'll dig. He'll tear these stones apart stone by stone. He has to. His own spell is eating him alive." She thought of Tam's account, delivered half-dead into Callowmark: the king coughing blood into a silk cloth, the court physicians burning their herbs by the cartload, the grey hoods doubling their levies of gifted children. *Aldric needs us*, Tam had said. *We're the mortar in his wall.* "He follows us through, or he dies here trying. Either way, the hunt doesn't end at the threshold."

"No," Sef said quietly. "It ends where you decide it ends, Isandro. That's what the Door is *for*."

She didn't answer. There was nothing to answer yet. Some choices you could only make standing in front of them.

Wren finished lashing her kit shut and came to stand beside her, shoulder almost touching shoulder. Up close, in the last gray light, Isandro could see how much the hedge-witch had spent on the road — the hollows under her eyes, the tremor in her hands she hid by clasping them. Healing was Wren's gift, and gifts, out here beyond the valleys, cost their bearers like coin costs a miser. Every mended bone, every drawn poison, every fever broken had taken a little more of her.

"When it opens," Wren said, low, so the others wouldn't hear, "I want you to know what I'm going to do."

"Do?"

"I'm going to look at it. Just look. My sister walked eleven years in my head, Isandro, and every one of those years she was walking toward this door." Her voice didn't break. Wren's voice never broke; it just went thin and hard, like wire. "Whatever comes through it, whatever it asks — I've already paid worse than anything it can charge."

Isandro put her hand over Wren's clasped ones. Neither of them said anything else. Out here, past the erasure, that was its own kind of vow.

* * *

Moonrise came the way a wave comes — you saw it building for a long time and were still unprepared when it broke.

The full moon cleared the eastern ridge and the Choir *answered*. The chord that had been a murmur all evening rose through registers Isandro hadn't known stone possessed, and the thirty-one pillars began to shine — not lit, not glowing, but *remembering* light, the way old cloth remembers dye. Veins of silver woke in the granite and ran upward, and the wind died entirely, and in the windless silence the song became articulate. Isandro could almost catch words in it. Old words. Dead-tongue words, the kind the village off every map had sung over their secret bread.

And beneath their feet, the breathing stopped — and became a heartbeat.

"Down!" Sef shouted, though there was nothing to duck. "Everyone away from the center — the ground—"

The ground at the Choir's heart began to glow.

It started as a seam, a hairline of white light splitting the packed earth between four leaning pillars, and it widened with a sound like ice releasing a lake — groaning, cracking, joyous. Light poured up and outward, and Isandro felt the pressure behind her sternum surge in answer, her sealed vessel straining toward that radiance like a drowning thing toward air. She staggered. Perrin caught her arm, his eyes huge, reflecting silver.

"It's happy," he whispered. "Isandro, it's *happy*."

The seam became a line became an arch — light drawing its own doorway in the air above the split earth, taller than two men, framed in nothing, held up by nothing, and through it—

Through it was not the free cities. Through it was not darkness or death or the blank page Sef's maps promised. Through it was *country*. Rolling green under a younger sky, hills unscarred by roads, a river running bright as spilled mercury, and everywhere — everywhere — that quality of rawness, of waiting, of a world holding its breath before its first word. Isandro's grandmother's ring, warm on her finger since the elder at the remembering village had pressed it there, grew hot enough to sting.

"That's Vessia," Sef breathed. He had dropped to his knees at the threshold's edge, his maps forgotten, tears cutting clean lines through the road-dust on his face. "That's — oh, gods, that's *before*. That's the country before we unmade it. It kept itself. All this time, it kept *itself*."

"It's not open all the way," Perrin said. His ear was turned toward the arch now rather than the stones, and his face had gone serious in the way that made him look twenty years older. "It's like a door on a chain. It can't open further unless—" He stopped. Looked at Isandro. Looked at the ring.

"Unless what?" Wren demanded.

"Unless someone holds it," Perrin said. "Someone has to stand in it. On the line. Or it swings shut again and goes back to sleep for another hundred years."

Later — in the days after, which none of them yet knew were coming — Isandro would remember that moment as the last quiet one: five people and one impossible door, the moon high, the stones singing, the whole world narrowed to a rectangle of green light and the question of who would stand in it.

Then the first crossbow bolt took Sef's map-pillar six inches from his hand, and the night broke open.

* * *

They came from the south and east at once, exactly as Sef had read it — grey hoods flowing down the slopes like spilloil, thirty of them, forty, crossbows leveled, the humming devices held high on poles so their resonance swept the Choir like lantern-light. Veyra's voice carried across the stones, calm as a dinner invitation:

"Isandro Dunmore! King's business! Stand away from the threshold and no one bleeds tonight!"

"Sef, the maps—" Isandro was already moving, dragging Perrin by the collar toward the fallen pillar that formed their one piece of cover. "Wren, get him down!"

"I'm *fine*—" Sef began, and a second bolt answered him, splintering stone where his shoulder had been. He flattened. Around them the Choir went on singing, indifferent, glorious, the arch of light burning steady as if armies were weather.

Isandro pressed her back to the pillar, Perrin tucked against her side, and did the arithmetic she'd been dreading since Callowmark. Forty hunters. Five of them — and of the five, one healer who had never fought, one mapmaker with a three-day-old wound, one boy, and herself. Herself, whose gift was *not dying of poisons*, which was magnificent at a banquet table and useless at forty yards against steel.

Except.

The pressure behind her sternum had not eased when the Door opened. It had *grown*. Standing this close to the threshold, her sealed vessel strained against its cork like a river against a weir, and for the first time she could feel the shape of what was stored inside her — decades of it, the valley's slow accumulation, magic that had nowhere to go and so had gone *into her*, layer upon layer, year upon year. The taster's tongue. The bending candleflame. The way hunters' devices screamed near her instead of humming.

It wasn't absence. It had never been absence. It was a cask, and the hoops were groaning.

"Isandro." Perrin's hand found hers in the dark. "Your hands are doing the thing."

She looked down. Silver light leaked between her fingers, thin as smoke, pulsing in time with the Door's heartbeat. The nearest humming device swung toward them and shrieked — a sound like a nail dragged across the inside of the world — and the grey hood holding it shouted something, and bolts came again, three of them, hammering the pillar.

"They know where I am," Isandro said. "Good. Then they're not looking at the others."

"What are you going to do?" Perrin asked, and there was no fear in it, only the terrible trust of a boy who had decided she was the fixed point of his universe.

"I don't know yet," she told him honestly. "Stay down. Count bolts for me — tell me when they reload."

Across the Choir, Wren had reached Sef, and Isandro saw the hedge-witch's hands move over his wounded side with the quick economy of long practice — and then stop. Saw Wren look up at the advancing line of grey hoods, at the hunter dragging a screaming villager's son toward the devices, at the bolt-nocked ranks closing on the only door out of a dead country. Saw her close her eyes.

"Wren," Isandro said. "No. Whatever you're thinking — *no*, that's not what your gift is *for*—"

"Healing is just knowing where the life is," Wren said, and her voice carried across the singing stones, thin and hard as wire. "Turns out knowing where the life is means knowing where it *isn't*."

She stood up out of cover, spread her arms, and burned.

Isandro had seen Wren heal: the soft gold warmth of it, candle-gentle, thread-through-flesh. This was not that. This was the same gift run backward through a heart that had buried a sister and eleven years of refugees and had finally, *finally* been given a target worthy of the grief. Light poured off her in a wave — not gold now but white, searing, the color of the Door itself — and where it touched the hunters' devices they burst like blisters. Where it touched the grey hoods, they fell — not dead, Isandro would understand later, not killed, but *emptied*, their strength poured out of them into the stony ground, sleeping the sleep of the utterly spent. The advance line folded like a dropped puppet show.

Wren swayed. Stayed up. Burned again.

A bolt took her through the meat of her left shoulder and she didn't even cry out, just turned toward the shooter with an expression Isandro had seen once before, on the night Wren described the pyre, and the second wave of light took the man's device and his bow-arm's strength together.

"NOW," Sef roared, up on his feet despite everything, pointing at the gap Wren had torn in the southern line. "The arch — while she's got them blind — *move*!"

Isandro moved. She pulled Perrup up — pulled *Perrin* up — and ran for the light, across open ground, bolts hissing past, and halfway there the world tilted because the cork in her chest didn't just strain anymore, it *cracked*, and everything the valley had ever poured into her woke up at once and wanted OUT.

Silver fire sheeted off her skin. The nearest humming device melted. Two hunters dove aside from a woman who had never thrown a punch in her life, screaming, because whatever they saw coming at them across the Stone Choir was not a food-taster.

The arch stood before her, green and vast and breathing. Behind her, Wren burned and bled. Beside her, Perrin ran with his hand welded to hers. Ahead, through the threshold, the unmade country waited under its young sky, patient as seed.

Isandro crossed the line of light —

— and felt the Door take hold of her, gentle as a mother, and understood in one white instant what Perrin had heard in the stones: it could not open fully alone. Someone had to stand on the threshold. Someone had to *hold*.

Behind her, Veyra's voice cut through the battle, no longer calm: "*Hold your fire! HOLD — do not hit the arch, you idiots, the king wants them ALIVE—*"

And through the ringing, singing dark, from the southern ridge, came the sound Isandro had dreaded across nine chapters of road: drums. Many drums. An army keeping pace with its king.

Aldric had come to the Choir after all.

The Door pulsed beneath her feet, half-open, hungry, waiting — and Isandro Dunmore, royal taster, sealed vessel, keeper of a dead country's ring, turned to face the army with silver still streaming from her hands and made the only promise that mattered:

"Not them. *Never* them."

The first rank of grey hoods crested the ridge.
