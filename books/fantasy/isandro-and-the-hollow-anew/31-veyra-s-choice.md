## 31. Veyra's Choice

The stones were singing.

Veyra had heard the reports — shepherds' rumors, a miller's drunk confession, the trembling testimony of a hedge-priest who claimed the Choir sang only when something old woke beneath it. She had dismissed all of it as the fever-talk of a superstitious countryside. Now she stood in the shadow of the first pillar with her hood down and her sword drawn, and the sound went through her like cold water through cloth.

It was not music, exactly. It was closer to memory — the way a name feels in the mouth before you say it aloud. The pillars rose around the hollow like the ribs of some enormous patient animal, and between them hung a seam of light that had no business existing: vertical, thin as a knife's edge, widening by inches as she watched.

*The Door of Anew.* Twenty years of hunting, and here it was, glowing like a wound in the world.

"Captain." Dain's voice, low, at her shoulder. He was her best tracker and her worst listener. "The device is screaming. Whatever's under these stones — it's bigger than anything we've logged."

"I can hear it without your toy," Veyra said.

She could. That was the thing she would never write in a report. Since Hollowmere, since the taster who drank poisoned wine at a river crossing and smiled at her over the cup, Veyra had been able to feel the hum herself — faintly, like a second pulse behind her teeth. She told herself it was the devices. She told herself a great many things.

"Positions," she said. "Archers on the eastern rise. Dain, take six and close the western gap. Nobody moves until I give the word."

"And the taster?"

Veyra looked across the hollow to where Isandro Dunmore stood at the center of the singing stones, one hand raised toward the seam of light, her face lit gold from within. Around her: the cartographer, gray-faced and bandaged at the ribs; the boy pressed against a pillar with his hands over his ears; the hedge-witch standing in front of them all with her arms out like a shepherd defending a flock from wolves.

"Alive," Veyra said. "All of them, if it can be managed."

Dain blinked. "The edict says—"

"The edict says surrender or the pyre. The king's orders to me say *alive*. Which of us do you imagine he meant?" She did not wait for an answer. "Go."

He went. The grey hoods melted into the dark between the pillars, and Veyra stayed where she was, alone in the open, watching the light widen, and admitted to herself what she had been refusing to admit since Callowmark:

She did not want to catch this woman.

* * *

It had started, if she was honest, with soup.

Veyra had eaten in a hundred inns across a hundred miles of hunt, and the meal at the Gilded Kettle in Callowmark had been unremarkable except for one detail: the serving girl — dark-eyed, careful-handed, with a scar on her forearm she kept turning away from the light — had watched Veyra eat with an attention that had nothing to do with service. Not fear. Not flirtation. *Assessment.*

Later, reviewing the registry rolls, Veyra had found the name: Marla Fen, registered laborer, brand on the left wrist, arrived from nowhere in particular three weeks prior. The sketch attached to the entry was poor. The posture in it was not. She had held the page beside her memory of the inn and felt the click of it, quiet and certain, the way a lock answers the right key.

She had gone back to the Gilded Kettle the next evening. Ordered soup. Watched the girl serve it — watched her taste the steam above the bowl, a flicker of tongue, so fast most people would have missed it, and then watch Veyra with those grave dark eyes while she ate.

Veyra had eaten every spoonful. It was excellent soup. And somewhere between the third spoonful and the last, sitting across from a woman she had crossed half a kingdom to destroy, Captain Veyra of the King's Hunt had understood two things simultaneously.

First: Isandro Dunmore knew exactly who she was, and had served her anyway.

Second: Veyra did not want to win.

That second thing had frightened her more than the first. She had built her life on wanting to win — on the clean arithmetic of the hunt, quarry found, quarry taken, ledger balanced. Her father had been a butcher. He used to say you could tell a good blade by how little it hesitated. She had spent thirty years being a good blade.

But a blade cuts what is put beneath it. It does not ask what the thing is. And Veyra had spent months now asking what Isandro Dunmore was, assembling her profile piece by piece the way she'd been trained: the taster's discipline, the flight through the mill-race, the poisoned wine drunk smiling, the false name in the registry taken with a brand rather than run from. Every fact pointed the same direction. This was not a fugitive. This was not even, properly, a criminal. This was a woman who had spent her whole life swallowing poison so that others wouldn't have to, and who had run not to save herself but because the world had decided that the shape of her loyalty was a crime.

And the king wanted her blood. Literally. Veyra had read the sealed orders twice before believing them — the ritual requirements, the vessel of stored gift, the way Aldric's physicians spoke of the usurper's wasting illness in the hushed tones usually reserved for plague. Take them alive, the orders said. All bearers. The Door especially. His Majesty requires the threshold intact.

His Majesty required a great many things lately. None of them, Veyra was beginning to suspect, would survive contact with the truth.

The seam of light widened another foot. The singing deepened. Somewhere beneath the stones, patient as a heartbeat, something enormous turned over in its sleep.

* * *

They struck at moonrise, when the Door stood half-open and the light poured across the hollow like spilled honey.

Veyra gave the signal herself. She watched her own hand do it — two fingers dropping, the archers rising, the whistle cutting the night — and felt strangely outside her body, a woman watching another woman command a thing she no longer believed in.

Arrows hissed. The hedge-witch moved faster than any healer had a right to — her hands flared white, and the arrows nearest her simply stopped, hanging in the air like insects in amber, then dropped. Veyra heard Dain shout. Heard steel ring off stone. Saw the cartographer go down behind a pillar with the boy dragging him, saw the witch stand her ground in a corona of light that made Veyra's eyes water and her teeth ache.

And Isandro Dunmore turned toward the attack, and Veyra saw her face clearly for the first time since the inn: not afraid. *Resolved.* The look of someone who has already done the arithmetic and paid the cost and stands on the far side of the bill.

Veyra walked into the hollow.

She did not run. Running was for soldiers who still believed in the mission. She walked, sword drawn but low, past the fighting at the western gap, past a grey hood sprawled unconscious with frost blooming strange on his armor, past the boy Perrin who looked up at her with wide eyes and said, in a voice pitched only for her, "You're the one who eats the soup."

"I am," Veyra said, and kept walking.

Isandro met her at the center of the Choir, between the two tallest pillars, with the Door's light at her back. She had picked up a short blade from somewhere — a cook's weapon, a kitchen knife with a honed edge, held the way a taster holds nothing, awkwardly, honestly.

"You should run," Isandro said.

"You should lower that. You'll cut yourself."

"I've cut myself before. I heal slowly. It's one of my few ordinary qualities."

Despite everything — despite the arrows, the shouting, the witch's light burning at the edge of vision — Veyra almost laughed. Almost. Instead she raised her sword, because there were forty grey hoods in this hollow and half of them had eyes, and a captain who lowered her blade at the moment of contact would be relieved of command by sundown, or worse.

"Isandro Dunmore," she said formally, loudly enough to carry. "By order of King Aldric, I—"

"I know the words," Isandro said quietly. "You said them at the ford. You said them at the inn. You always say them like they taste bad."

"They do."

"Then why?"

The question hung between them, absurdly intimate, in the middle of a battle. Behind Isandro the Door blazed; around them the stones sang; somewhere Dain was screaming orders; and Veyra stood with her sword leveled at a woman's throat and answered her honestly, because she was tired, and because the night was too large for lies.

"Because I took the king's coin twenty years ago and never asked what it bought. Because the hunt was the only thing I was ever good at. Because until four months ago I believed the edicts were justice." She tightened her grip. "And because if I don't bring you in, someone else will, and they won't offer parley first."

"So this is mercy with a point."

"This is the only kind I have left."

They came together. It was not really a fight — Veyra was stronger, faster, armored where Isandro was not — but Isandro fought like someone who had spent a lifetime surviving poison: patiently, economically, giving ground only when ground had to be given, looking always for the single opening. Twice she nearly took Veyra's wrist. Once the kitchen knife whispered close enough to part the laces of Veyra's gorget. The stones sang louder with every exchange, and the Door's light pulsed like a quickening heart, and Veyra realized with distant clarity that the Choir itself was watching them, whatever that meant, weighing them.

Then the opening came. Isandro overcommitted on a parry — exhaustion, or desperation — and Veyra's blade slid inside her guard and stopped, flat against the side of her neck, the edge a finger's width from the artery.

The hollow seemed to hold its breath.

"Yield," Veyra said.

Isandro did not yield. She stood very still, chin lifted, dark eyes steady, and said, "Do it, then. But know what you're killing. Not a bearer. A person who tasted every meal in Hollowmere for eleven years so Dame Ovelin could sleep at night. Who ran not to save her own skin but because Tam — a boy, twelve years old — was dragged out of a stable in chains while I watched from a window and did nothing. I've spent my whole life being the vessel other people pour their safety into, Captain. If you cut my throat, the pouring stops. That's all. That's the entire crime."

The words went into Veyra like the wine at the ford must have gone into Isandro — swallowed whole, feeling nothing, and then, later, the slow dawning of what had actually been taken in.

*A person, not a prize.*

Her father's voice, from thirty years ago, in the butcher's shop: *a good blade doesn't hesitate.* And her own voice, older than that, a child's voice, asking: *but Papa, doesn't the lamb mind?*

She had never asked again. She was asking now.

"Dain!" she called, without moving the blade. "To me!"

He came at a run, six hoods behind him, swords out, faces eager in the Door's light. "Captain — the witch is pinned, the cartographer's down, we have them, we—"

"Stand down."

The words came out of her calm and absolute, the voice she had used at sieges and executions, the voice that brooked nothing. Dain stopped so abruptly the man behind him walked into his back.

"...What?"

"Stand down. All of you. Sheathe your blades."

Dain stared at her. Then at Isandro. Then back, and Veyra watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes — the numbers every soldier runs when a commander gives an impossible order — and she watched him arrive at the wrong sum.

"Captain," he said carefully, "the king's orders are explicit. Bearers alive. The ritual requires—"

"I know what the ritual requires." Her voice did not waver. "I've read the sealed dispatches, Dain. Have you? Do you know what they're harvesting her *for*? Do you know what Aldric's physicians are doing with the blood of everyone we've brought in since winter?" She let the silence stretch. "No. Of course you don't. You weren't meant to. None of us were."

"She's lying," someone said from the back. "The taster's beguiled her. It's a gift-trick—"

"It's soup," Veyra said. "She served me soup and didn't poison it when poisoning me would have saved her life. That's the trick. It's called being better than we are, and I find I've lost my appetite for anything else."

Dain's jaw worked. "If you won't take her, Captain, I will. For the king."

"For a dying man who'd feed you to the ritual as soon as look at you, if your blood ran true." Veyra stepped sideways — deliberately, slowly — putting herself between Dain and Isandro, her sword still level, its point now aimed at her own lieutenant's chest. "Last chance. Stand down."

The hollow went very quiet. Even the stones seemed to lean in.

Dain looked at her sword. At the Door. At the woman behind her. And Veyra saw the exact moment he chose — saw it in the set of his shoulders, the small tightening around his eyes, the way his blade came up not in salute but in guard.

"I'm sorry, Captain," he said, and meant it, which was somehow worse. "For the king."

"For the king," Veyra repeated softly. "Yes. I remember that prayer."

He attacked her.

It was brief. Dain was good — she had trained him, sharpened him, taught him everything he knew — and that was precisely why it was brief. She knew his openings the way she knew her own. She caught his first cut on the forte, turned the second, and on the third — the high lunge he always overcommitted, the flaw she'd corrected a hundred times and he'd never once fixed — she stepped inside it and drove her sword through the gap beneath his arm, up and in, and held him as he sagged, and lowered him to the stones instead of letting him fall.

"For the king," she said again, to the body, to herself, to the child who had once asked about the lamb. "May he choke on it."

The remaining hoods had frozen. Six men, swords half-raised, staring at their captain kneeling over their lieutenant's corpse with blood to her elbows.

"There are two ways this goes," Veyra said, rising. She did not wipe the blood. She wanted them to see it. "The first is that you remember every order I've ever given you, and you follow this one: we were ambushed at the ford. We lost Dain. The bearers escaped through the Door before we could close it. You will swear it on your mothers, and it will be true enough, because after tonight none of us will ever speak of what actually happened here."

"And the other way?" one of them asked hoarsely.

"The other way is that you explain to His Majesty's inquisitors why his hunt captain turned traitor, and you spend the rest of your short careers wondering which of your comrades sold you first." She looked at each of them in turn. "I know which way I'd choose. I chose it thirty seconds ago, and it cost me everything, and I have never in my life felt lighter."

A long silence. Then the youngest of them — Rell, a farm boy from the Marches, a boy who still wrote letters home — sheathed his sword with a click that sounded, in the singing hollow, like a door closing.

One by one, the rest followed.

* * *

Afterward, the hollow was very still. The witch's light had dimmed to embers; the cartographer sat propped against a pillar, breathing shallow but breathing; the boy had come out from behind his stone and was regarding the grey hoods with the frank curiosity of a cat inspecting new furniture.

Veyra cleaned her sword with mechanical care, not looking at anyone, because if she looked at anyone she would have to account for what she had just done, and she was not yet sure she could. Thirty years of service, ended in ten seconds of steel. No going back. No garrison, no pension, no name worth having. She had traded all of it for a woman she had hunted across a kingdom, and the terrible, laughing truth of it was that her hands had never shaken less.

"You didn't have to do that."

Isandro stood beside her. The kitchen knife was gone; her hands were empty; there was a thin line of blood at her neck where the flat of Veyra's blade had kissed her, already drying.

"Yes, I did," Veyra said. "Eventually. I was just late about it. I'm told that's a habit of mine."

"Why?" Isandro asked. "Truly. You had me. Blade to throat. One motion and the hunt ends, the king's favor, everything you've worked for—"

"You want the truth?"

"I've spent my life around poison, Captain. I can survive the truth."

Veyra almost smiled. She slid her sword home in its scabbard and finally turned to face her — this woman she had profiled and pursued and served soup to and nearly killed, whose file Veyra could recite from memory and whose soul she had, apparently, misread entirely.

"The truth is that I've known what Aldric needs you for since midwinter, and I told myself it was strategy — keep the quarry alive, deliver the prize intact, that's all. The truth is that I sat in your inn and ate your soup and went back to my quarters and wrote in my report that the trail had gone cold, and I didn't know why until tonight." She looked up at the singing pillars, at the seam of light burning patient and enormous between them. "The truth is that a good blade doesn't hesitate, and I have spent thirty years being a very good blade, and tonight, for the first time, I minded what was underneath it."

Isandro studied her for a long moment. Then, absurdly, she held out her hand — the taster's handshake, palm open, the gesture of someone offering to drink first.

"Isandro Dunmore," she said. "Formerly of Hollowmere. Currently of nowhere."

"Veyra Sarn." Veyra took the hand. It was warm, and steady, and did not flinch from the blood on hers. "Formerly of the King's Hunt. Currently of considerably less than nowhere."

Behind them, the Door's light surged — sudden, urgent, the singing rising to a pitch that set Veyra's teeth on edge and made the youngest hood, Rell, clap his hands over his ears. The witch was on her feet, staring at the threshold, and her face had gone the color of ash.

"What is it?" Isandro demanded. "Wren — what's happening?"

The hedge-witch's voice came out thin. "It's not the Door. It's what's coming *through the valley behind us*." She turned, and in the light Veyra saw tears cutting tracks through the grime on her cheeks. "Bells. I hear bells, and horns, and — that's an army. That's the whole cursed army."

Far to the south, beyond the hills, a sound reached them at last: the deep, rolling voice of war-drums, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a single horn blown three times — the royal summons, the signal Veyra had heard at the head of a hundred columns.

Three blasts. *Come forth. All is commanded. Nothing is refused.*

Veyra closed her eyes. "He knows," she said. "Somehow, he already knows. Aldric isn't sending hunters anymore."

She opened them and looked at the Door — half-open, blazing, waiting — and at the ragged handful of people she had just burned her life down to protect.

"He's coming himself. And we have until moonset."
