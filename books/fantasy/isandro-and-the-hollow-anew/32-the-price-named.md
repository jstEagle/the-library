## 32. The Price Named

The Door did not open all the way. That was the first thing Wren understood, and the last thing she wanted to say aloud.

She had spent thirty years learning to read wounds — the way a body spoke before it screamed, the small dishonesties of healing flesh, the truth that lived in what was missing rather than what was present. So she read the threshold the way she read a patient. The Door of Anew stood open in the heart of the Stone Choir, a seam of light hanging in the air between two pillars, and it was *straining*. The light shivered at its edges like a held breath. Like a suture pulled too tight across a wound too deep.

It wanted something. Everything alive wanted something.

Behind her, Isandro stood with her hand pressed flat against the air, and the light bent toward her palm the way a flame bends toward a draft — hungry, leaning, almost tender. Perrin had his ear against a pillar, eyes shut, his lips moving. Sef sat propped against a fallen stone with his bandaged side and his maps spread across his knees, because Sef would die with his maps in his lap and they all knew it. And Veyra stood apart from all of them, grey hood down, her sword still in its sheath, watching the treeline the way a woman watches a debt come due.

The hunters had fallen back after the battle among the stones. Fallen back, not fled. Wren had seen the difference in the way they'd dragged their wounded — orderly, deliberate, like surgeons clearing a table between operations. They would come again. Everyone standing in this ring of singing stone knew it.

But first, the Door.

"It's asking," Perrin said suddenly. His voice cracked on the second word, the way it did when the things he heard were too large for a boy's throat. "It's been asking since we got here. Nobody was listening."

Wren had been listening. That was the trouble.

She had heard it from the moment they'd crested the ridge and the Choir had begun to sing — a note beneath the note, a second pulse under the pillars' wind-song, patient as a heartbeat in a sickroom. She had heard it and she had known, the way she'd known when her sister Maren first started coughing blood, that the answer would cost more than anyone wanted to pay.

"Say it, then," Sef said. He didn't look up from his maps. His hands, she noticed, had gone very still, and Sef's hands were never still. "Whatever it's asking. Say it out loud, because I've been watching your face for an hour and you've got the look of a woman reading a deathbed will."

*You've got the look of a woman writing one,* Wren thought, and almost smiled.

Isandro turned. The light from the threshold caught the side of her face, and Wren saw what the girl had become in the weeks since Marrowfen — the sealed vessel cracking open day by day, something vast moving behind her eyes like a flood behind a dam. She had uncorked a little of it in the battle. Wren had felt it pass through her like a hot wind, and she had thought, absurdly, of her sister's hands on a loom.

"The Door needs a keeper," Wren said.

The Choir's song filled the silence after, wind through stone, a hundred pillars humming their dead coronation hymn.

"It can't open all the way on its own," she went on, because once you started a hard sentence the only mercy was to finish it. "It's been unmade too long. Erased. A door that's been struck from the world doesn't remember how to be a door — it needs someone to stand on the threshold and *hold* it. Bear it open from the inside. One bearer stays, and the Door stays open long enough for the rest to pass through." She looked down at the satchel at her hip. The satchel she had carried through marsh and mountain and the whole long grey length of the coronation road. "And if no one stays, it seals. Forever. There won't be a second time. There won't be a Vessia again, not ever, not anywhere."

"How long?" Isandro asked. Her voice was very quiet. "If someone stays. How long do they hold it?"

Wren met her eyes and did not lie, because lying to Isandro Dunmore felt like lying to a mirror.

"Long enough," she said. "That's all it promises. Long enough."

*Not forever. Not coming back. Long enough.*

Perrin made a small sound, and Wren looked at him and saw that the boy had understood it completely, the way children understood the things adults spent sentences trying not to say. His face had gone white around the mouth.

"No," he said. "No, it doesn't get to — doors open. That's what doors *do*. I've heard a thousand doors and none of them ever —"

"This one's been broken a hundred years," Wren said gently. "Broken things ask for more."

*

She had known it would be her. She had known since Marrowfen, since the night she'd told them her price — *someone carries my sister through the Door* — and had watched them agree without understanding what they were agreeing to. She had known even before that, maybe. Since the pyre. Since the moment she'd stood in the snow outside a hunter's garrison with a box of ash in her arms and made a bargain with a God she didn't believe in: *let there be a door somewhere in this broken country, and I will carry her to it, and I will not complain about the toll.*

Gods, in Wren's experience, always took you at your word.

She unslung the satchel now, working the buckle with fingers that wanted to shake and did not, because her hands had learned long ago that shaking was a luxury for afterward. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and then in linen and then in the small embroidered cloth Maren had made at fourteen, terrible at needlework, proud of it anyway: the ashes. All that the pyre had left. All that Wren had saved, which was to say, all that Wren had been allowed.

"Maren was the gifted one," she said. She hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, the way the truest things did, sideways, while you were looking at something else. "Everyone thinks — the hedge-witch, the healer, she must have been the one with the light in her. No. I can *mend*. That's plumbing. That's knowing where the pipes run. Maren could make a fever *sing*. She could put her hands on a dying field and it would come up green by morning. She hid other people's gifts for six years — tucked them down inside them like coins in a mattress, so deep even the humming devices passed over. She saved forty people that way. Maybe more. We never counted. Counting felt like bragging."

The light from the threshold moved across the stones, and the Choir sang, and Wren heard her own voice as if it belonged to someone younger.

"The forty-first was a boy who couldn't hide what he was. He glowed. Literally glowed, at night, like a lamp with a bad shade. Maren hid him in our cellar for a winter. The hunters came in spring. They'd traced him through three villages, and when they couldn't find the boy they took the nearest bearer instead, and the law was the law, and my sister stood on a pyre in the market square of Marrowfen and did not scream, because she said screaming would frighten the boy they hadn't found yet." Wren's thumb moved over the satchel's buckle, back and forth. "They never found him. He's in the free cities now, last I heard. A glassblower. His work glows too, but that's just craft."

Isandro had come closer without Wren noticing. The girl did that — moved quiet as a taster, a profession that taught you to occupy a room without disturbing it. She stood at Wren's shoulder now, and she didn't touch her, and Wren was grateful, because she might have come apart at a kind hand and she could not come apart yet. Not yet. There was still the toll to pay.

"You knew," Isandro said. "When you told us your price. You already knew it would be you."

"I suspected," Wren said. "There's a difference. A healer's got to leave room for the patient to surprise her." She looked at the threshold — the seam of light, the straining, the hunger. "But no. I didn't suspect. I knew. I've been walking toward this since Marrowfen and pretending the road was going somewhere else."

"Then we find another way," Sef said. He'd risen, somehow, wound and all, one hand braced on the fallen stone, his maps forgotten. "There's always another way. That's what maps are *for* — there's a way around everything, you just haven't drawn it yet —"

"Sef." Wren said his name the way she said *hold still* to a man with an arrow in him. He stopped. "You've seen the charts. You've read the erasure decrees. There is no other way. There is no second Door. There is this one, on this night, under this moon, and it is asking for one keeper, and I am the one of us who has been preparing to pay a toll for six years." She smiled, and knew it was the smile she used for delivering the worst news, the one she'd practiced in mirrors until it stopped looking like grief. "Besides. You need a cartographer, a taster, a door-hearer, and a hunter to build whatever comes next. You don't need a hedge-witch. My profession is about to be made obsolete — you're all walking into a country where everything is broken and nothing is *wounded*. There's a difference."

"There isn't," Perrin said fiercely. "There isn't a difference. You fixed me. When you found me in the waystation I was — I was all the way broken, and you fixed me, and —" His voice broke and he hated it, she could see him hating it, twelve years old and furious at his own throat. "You can't stay. You're *ours*."

And there it was. The word. Wren felt it go through her like a blade going through cloth — no resistance, just the sudden terrible ease of being cut.

*Ours.*

She had been no one's for six years. She had made a profession of it — the healer at the edge of town, the woman who patched refugees and asked no names, who kept her door open and her heart shut, grief given a profession, that's what she'd called it once and meant it as a joke and it had not been a joke at all. And now a boy who heard doors was standing in front of her saying *ours* like it was a fact, like it was geography, like she was a country on Sef's maps that could not be erased because someone had drawn her in ink.

"Come here," Wren said, and her voice did shake then, and she let it.

Perrin came. He hit her hard, arms around her middle, face buried in her apron, and she held him the way she held the dying — completely, without reservation, as if there were all the time in the world. Over his head she saw Isandro's face, and Sef's, and even Veyra's, the hunter standing at the edge of the firelight with her jaw tight and her eyes wet and refusing, absolutely refusing, to look away.

"You're all being ridiculous," Wren said into Perrin's hair. "I haven't even said it's me. The Door didn't say *Wren Ostara*. It said *one bearer*."

"It said you," Perrin said, muffled. "I heard it. It's been saying you since the ridge. It says you the way my name sounds. Like it's known you a long time."

Wren closed her eyes.

*

They argued. Of course they argued. Wren had expected weeping; she got a war council instead, and she supposed she should have expected that too, because look what she'd gone and collected — a girl who'd lied to a regent to save her own skin and hadn't stopped being brave since, a cartographer who'd traded his life's work for their passage and never once mentioned the cost, a boy who'd survived alone in the wilds on nothing but his ears, a hunter who'd turned her sword on her own grey hoods and could never go home.

They argued around the fire at the Choir's heart while the pillars sang and the threshold strained and the night leaned in.

Sef argued geography: the Door had surfaced at the Stone Choir, it could surface again, doors that moved had patterns, he would find the pattern, give him a year —

"Aldric's armies reach the free cities in a season," Veyra said flatly. "There is no year."

Sef argued that they could hold the Choir, fortify it, the pillars were defensible —

"There are four of us who can fight," Veyra said, "and a hundred of them, and their captain is me, and I know exactly how I would take this position."

Sef argued that Wren was being sentimental, that her sister was *ashes*, that the Door wanted a bearer's living strength and not a box of —

"It wants what it wants," Perrin said, "and it wants her, and it wants the ashes too, I think. I think the ashes are —" he frowned, listening to something none of them could hear, "— I think they're the *key part*. It's not just a keeper. It's a keeper who brings something of the old Vessia through. Something that remembers. Maren remembers. That's why it's been saying Wren's name. She's not the toll." His voice dropped. "She's the hinge."

Isandro said almost nothing through all of it. She sat close to the threshold with her hand still raised toward the light, and Wren watched the light lean toward her and understood that the girl was listening to the Door the way Wren listened to wounds — with her whole body, with the part of her that was no longer entirely a girl from Hollowmere. And when the arguing finally wore itself down to embers, Isandro spoke.

"Then I'm staying with her."

The fire cracked. Wren's heart stopped and started.

"The Door said one bearer," Isandro went on, in the reasonable tone of someone proposing to share a chore. "It didn't say the rest of us get to be comfortable about it. I'll stand on the threshold with her. I'll hold her hand. If it takes her, it takes me, and you'll have a Door that opens and four people instead of five, and —"

"No," Wren said.

"You don't get to —"

"*No.*" Wren stood, and the healer's voice came out of her, the one that had ordered kings' messengers to hold still and be quiet, the one that did not negotiate with fever or fear. "You are the sealed vessel. You are the reason Aldric's whole rotten spell is cracking. If you stand on that threshold when it takes its keeper, you'll be standing there when it takes *two*, and then this has all been for nothing, and Maren's ashes will have crossed over to hold open a door for no one." She made herself soften, because the girl deserved softness, deserved it more than anyone Wren had ever patched. "Isandro. My love. You're the future. I'm the toll. That's not tragedy. That's just arithmetic, and I've always been better at arithmetic than the rest of you."

"Then we all stay," Perrin said, "and it can take all of us, and —"

"And Vessia stays erased forever," Sef said quietly, "and Aldric burns the free cities, and everyone we've ever helped dies in the spring." He rubbed his face with both hands. When he took them away he looked ten years older and completely, terribly calm. "Wren. Ask it one question before you step up. That's all I ask. Ask it if the keeper suffers. Ask it what it *feels like*. Because I've read every account of the Door that survived the erasure, and there's one line in the oldest one, in the marginalia of a coronation rite, and it says —" he recited it from memory, of course he did, Sef kept everything, "— *the keeper does not fall. The keeper becomes the opening.*"

Wren looked at the threshold for a long time.

The light had steadied, she realized. Since she'd unslung the satchel, since she'd spoken Maren's name into the singing air, the straining had gentled. The Door was not starving anymore. It was *waiting*. The way a patient waits, she thought, when they know the healer is coming. The way Maren used to wait at the window when Wren came home late from gathering herbs — not anxious, just *certain*, certain as only a sister can be certain, certain that the one who loved her would come.

"I know what it feels like," Wren said. "It feels like going home. I've known that since Marrowfen too. That's why I never told any of you what the price really was." She picked up the satchel and slung it across her chest, bandolier-style, the way a soldier carries the thing that matters. "Now. We've got perhaps an hour before Veyra's grey hoods regroup, and I am not spending it on arguments I've already won. Perrin — you're going to help me with the wrapping. Maren would want to arrive presentable. Sef — I want to see the map. The one with the blank space. I want to see where you're going to put the new country, and I want you to promise me you'll name something after my sister, and I don't care if it's a swamp."

"It's a very good swamp," Sef said, and his voice cracked clean through, and he didn't try to hide it.

"Veyra. Watch the treeline. And Isandro —"

Isandro was already in front of her. The girl took Wren's hands in both of hers, and her grip was strong, and the light under her skin had gone from flicker to glow, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady.

"You don't get to do this alone," Isandro said. "That's the one thing. Whatever happens on that threshold — you don't get to be alone for it. We're carrying you there, all of us, and we're standing at the edge, and you will look back and see us, every step, until the Door takes you. That's not a request. That's the toll *we're* paying."

Wren Ostara, who had amputated hope for a living, who had buried a sister and a country and her own soft heart in the same six years of snow, found that she had nothing left to argue with. She had spent her whole life on the giving side of the bargain, and here, at the end, at the singing stones at the top of the unmade world, she was being given something back — not a rescue, she was past rescue, but a *witnessing*. A family standing at the door so she would not go through it unseen.

"Then it's settled," she said, and was surprised to find she was smiling, really smiling, the first true one since Maren died. "One bearer, one box of ashes, and four people who refuse to let a woman die politely." She turned to face the threshold, and the light rose to meet her like a tide. "Maren, you impossible girl. I'm bringing help."

The Choir's song swelled. And far off in the dark, thin as a knife drawn from a sheath, Wren heard the first horn of the grey hoods coming back.
