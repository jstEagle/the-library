## 34. Aldric at the Choir

**Veyra**

She heard the army before she saw it — not the marching, which was disciplined enough to be nearly silent, but the devices. A hundred humming cages slung from a hundred saddles, each one tuned to the same frequency, and together they made a sound like a hive of wasps drowned in honey. The Stone Choir's own song bent around it. The pillars that had been singing all night went quiet one by one as the grey column came up the coronation road, and Veyra thought: *even the stones know what this is.*

King Aldric rode at the head of it on a white horse that should have been dead. She had noticed that about his mounts for years now — the too-bright eyes, the skin stretched over ribs like parchment over a drum. The unmaking spell kept him alive and everything near him half-alive, a candle burning its own wax. He had not eaten solid food in three years. He took his meals through a silver straw, and he had executed two tasters for failing to find poison in water.

Behind him, roped at the wrists and dragged by a rider's lead like a recalcitrant hound, came Tam.

The boy's face was a ruin of healing bruises. One eye swollen shut, lip split, walking on ankles that had been bound so long the rope had worn through his boot leather to the skin. But his head was up. His good eye swept the standing stones ahead — forty pillars in a broken ring on the mountain shoulder, singing their low patient note — and Veyra watched him find something there. Watched him smile with his split lip.

*He can hear the Door,* she thought. *And Aldric cannot. That is why the boy is still breathing.*

Aldric had given her explicit orders on the road: take them alive. All of them. Especially the taster. He had said the word *taster* with a hunger that had nothing to do with food, and Veyra had understood then what she had been assembling a profile of for all these months. Not a fugitive. An ingredient.

She rode forward to the king's stirrup. "The Choir is held, Majesty. My company has the ring surrounded. The fugitives are inside — I count five."

"Six," Aldric said. His voice was dry paper. "You forget yourself, Captain. You were one of them until this morning."

"I am yours until I stop being useful," Veyra said. It was the only honest thing left between them, and they both knew it.

He smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which had gone the color of old milk since winter. "Then be useful. Bring me the girl."

* * *

**Perrin**

Perrin heard them coming for an hour before anyone else did, because Perrin heard everything that locked or latched or chained itself against the world, and an army was nothing but ten thousand small locked things moving together — buckles, scabbards, cage-doors, the little iron boxes that hummed. They sounded wrong. They sounded like a door being forced, over and over, all down the valley of the morning.

"They're here," he said, and his voice cracked on it, and Wren put her hand on his head without looking away from the east.

They stood among the pillars while the light came up grey and thin. Isandro had not slept. Nobody had slept. Sef sat propped against a stone with his bandaged side and his maps spread out on his knees even now, as if geography could save them, and maybe it had been geography that got them here — the Door under the Choir, patient as a heartbeat, exactly where the old coronation road ended. Perrin could feel it below his feet like a second heart. It had been getting louder all night. It sounded almost ready.

Almost was the problem.

"It wants moonrise," Sef had said, an hour ago, tracing the last line of his map with a finger gone yellow from old ink. "The Choir sings it partway open at moonrise. That's what the erased milestones mean — every coronation began at moonrise, when the Door was walked through. We have to hold until then."

"Hold against what?" Wren had asked.

And now the answer came up the road: banners of grey, no sigil, because Vessia had no sigil anymore because Vessia was not supposed to exist, and wasn't that the whole rotten joke of it. Perrin counted the riders the way he counted locks, automatically, uselessly. Three hundred. Four hundred. More behind the ridge.

Tam was with them.

Perrin saw him first — saw the drag-rope, the ruined face, the stubborn lifted chin — and made a sound he didn't decide to make, some animal noise, and Isandro's hand closed on his shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"I see him," she said. Her voice was very quiet and very flat, the way it went when she was most frightened, which Perrin had learned meant she was also most dangerous. "Don't move. Whatever happens, don't move."

"He's alive," Perrin whispered. "He's alive, he's alive—"

"Yes." Isandro's eyes never left the road. "He's alive, and they brought him alive on purpose, and we are going to be smarter than that."

But her hand on his shoulder was shaking, just slightly, and Perrin pretended not to hear her heart, which was hammering like someone pounding on a locked door from the inside.

* * *

**Isandro**

They came into the ring of stones at the pace of men who owned the morning, and King Aldric dismounted in the center of the Choir as if he had been crowned there.

Up close he was worse than the woodcuts. The portraits on Hollowmere's walls showed a broad, ruddy man with a conqueror's jaw; the thing that stepped down from the white horse had the same jaw but nothing behind it, a face like a mask worn so long it had grown into the skull. His cloak was grey. His crown was iron, plain, deliberately ugly — the crown of a man who wanted history to record that he had replaced gold with honesty. His guards fanned out along the pillar-ring, and the humming cages swung from their saddles, and the stones' song thinned to a thread.

Isandro stood with her family at the Choir's heart and did not run, because Tam was watching her with his one good eye, and running would teach him something she refused to teach him.

"Aldric," Sef said, loud enough to carry. "You're standing on my country."

The king turned his milk-colored eyes on the cartographer with something that might once have been amusement. "Sef Ardenne. The mapmaker of nowhere. I burned your charts myself, you know. In the third year. A satisfying fire — paper screams when it burns, did you ever notice? Very like people."

"You missed one," Sef said.

"So it appears." Aldric looked past him then, and found Isandro, and the hunger in his face was so naked that several of his own soldiers looked away. "And you. The taster."

"Taster," she agreed. "That's all I've ever been."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "I have read Captain Veyra's reports. Every one. You drank poisoned parley-wine before four armed hunters and did not blink. You killed a hunter at the ford with a stone — a stone, girl, your first time, and your hand did not shake afterward. You survived Hollowmere, which eats the gift out of children like acid, and came out whole. You are not a taster." He smiled. "You are a vault. And I have been dying for eleven years, and I intend to rob you."

The words dropped into the silence of the Choir like stones into a well. Isandro heard them hit bottom, one after another. *Dying. Vault. Rob.*

Wren moved half a step in front of her. Small, grey-streaked, smelling of yarrow and smoke, and absolutely immovable. "You want her blood for your spell," the hedge-witch said. "Say it plainly. We don't trade in riddles here."

"Plainly, then." Aldric folded his hands. "My predecessor's court poisoned me — a slow working, woven into the unmaking itself, though it took me years to learn who and how. The spell that hollowed Vessia keeps me alive the way a dam keeps a valley dry: barely, and at cost, and it is failing. Gifted blood renews it. A bearer's vessel, freely given, would renew it for a century." His gaze slid back to Isandro. "Yours — sealed, corked, decades deep — would renew it forever. I could make Vessia's erasure permanent. No Door. No free cities' dreams of the old kingdom. Nothing but Aldric's peace, world without end."

"And if she refuses?" Sef asked.

Aldric gestured, mildly, at the army ringing the stones. At Tam.

"There it is," Isandro said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "There's the arithmetic. One of me for all of us. That's the bargain?"

"That is the bargain." He said it without shame, and that was somehow the worst part — the perfect calm of a man who had done arithmetic like this for eleven years and always found himself worth more. "Your friends walk away. The boy walks away. The witch, the mapmaker, even my wayward captain. You come to the palace at Hollowmere, and you live — comfortably, honored — for as long as the taking takes. Which, done gently, is years."

"Gently," Wren repeated, flat.

"I am not a cruel man," Aldric said. "Cruelty is inefficient. I have simply been robbed of my life by poison, and I intend to collect the debt from the only currency that pays it." He extended one hand toward her, palm up, courteous as a dance partner. "Well, taster? You spent your life tasting other people's cups. Here is mine. What do you find in it?"

And Isandro Dunmore, who had lied exactly twice in her life and hated how easily it came both times, looked at Tam's ruined face, and Sef's grey lips, and Wren's white knuckles, and Perrin's terrified listening stillness, and felt the sealed thing under her breastbone shift and press against its corking like a tide against a harbor gate.

She smiled at the king.

"Give me a moment to taste it," she said.

**Veyra**

Veyra stood at the ring's edge with her sword loose in its scabbard and watched the girl consider damnation, and found herself doing something she had not done since she was a cadet: hoping.

It was an unprofessional sensation. She examined it with clinical interest. Eleven months she had hunted Isandro Dunmore across three provinces; eleven months of reading her like a text written in escapes. The mill-race. The wine at the ford. The inn at Callowmark where a magistrate lived because a serving girl had tasted his soup and screamed the kitchen down. Veyra knew this woman's mind better than she knew her sister's, and the knowledge had curdled somewhere along the road into respect, and the respect had ripened — she could admit it now, alone inside her own skull — into something dangerously like admiration.

And Aldric wanted to crack her open like a cask.

*Take them alive,* the orders said. *Especially the taster.* Veyra had obeyed orders for nineteen years, through the purge, through the pyres, through the registration brands and the labor quotas, telling herself each time that obedience was the price of being permitted to do the job mercifully. She had told herself she was a scalpel, not a fire.

Looking at Tam's dragged feet, she could no longer locate the mercy.

The king waited, hand extended, patient as rot. The stones had gone almost silent. Somewhere beneath the mountain, faintly, through the soles of her boots, Veyra felt — actually felt, which should have been impossible, she carried no gift, the devices had tested her a hundred times — a pulse. Like a heartbeat under a floorboard. Getting faster.

Moonrise was less than an hour off. She could read the sky as well as any cartographer.

*Hold,* she told herself. *Wait. See what the girl does.*

The girl opened her mouth to answer the king.

* * *

**Isandro**

Here was the terrible truth Isandro had arrived at sometime in the black hour before dawn, standing watch over sleeping friends: the arithmetic was correct.

One life against four. Against Tam, who had saved himself and deserved to keep having himself. Against Perrin, who had never seen a city until last month and should see ten thousand more. Against Sef's maps and Wren's hands and Veyra's strange, unasked-for turn at the ford. If the choice were only numbers, she would sign it. She had spent her whole life swallowing bitter things so others wouldn't have to; this was only the largest cup.

But it was not only numbers, because of what Aldric had said without knowing he'd said it: *renew it forever.* Her sealed vessel would make the erasure permanent. The Door would die. Every village that remembered, every song in the dead tongue, every coin baked secretly into bread — all of it would gutter out under a century of Aldric's peace, bought with her consent. She would not be paying with her life. She would be paying with everyone's past, and everyone's possible future, forever.

And underneath that — quieter, stranger — was the thing Wren had told her at the sickbed, when Sef's fever peaked: *the Door is a story I need to be true. Faith is just grief that hasn't given up.* Isandro had faith now too. She had felt it under her feet all night, patient as a heartbeat. The Door was real, and it was waking, and it wanted moonrise.

All she had to do was give it time. All she had to do was stand in front of the hungriest man in the world and keep him talking until the sun went down.

"Well, taster?" Aldric prompted. His patience had edges on it now.

Isandro let her shoulders drop. Let her eyes go wet — that part was easy; she thought of Tam's split lip and the tears were honest. Let her voice come out small.

"If I say yes," she whispered, "you let them walk. All of them. Down the coronation road, unhindered, with horses and provisions. And the boy goes free — truly free, no hunters following, no devices. Swear it."

"On my crown," Aldric said.

*On your iron hat,* Isandro thought. *Which will be worth nothing by midnight.*

"Then—" she began.

And Perrin screamed.

Not fear — warning. The boy's head snapped toward the eastern pillars and his whole body pointed like a compass needle. "It's opening! It's opening *early* — the stones — Isandro, the stones are singing the *name*—"

Every pillar in the Choir began, softly, to sound.

It was not the wind-note they had sung all night. It was deeper, layered, many-voiced, a chord struck on an instrument the size of a mountain, and the air above the ring of stones began to bend and brighten like heat-shimmer filling with light. The humming cages shrieked — every device at once, overloaded, and two of them burst their seams in showers of sparks as the horses screamed and bolted. Soldiers staggered. Aldric's extended hand dropped. For one heartbeat the entire army forgot its discipline and simply stared upward at the place where the air was learning to be a doorway.

In that heartbeat, Isandro moved.

She grabbed Perrin's collar and Wren's wrist and shouted "DOWN!" and hauled them both behind the widest pillar as the first crossbow bolts came out of the confusion, and the battle of the Stone Choir began — early, ragged, and nothing like anyone's plan.

Except, Isandro thought wildly, pressing her back to the singing stone, feeling the Door's heartbeat hammer up through the rock into her spine, except perhaps the Door's plan.

The moon was not yet up. But the sun was finally, finally down.

* * *

**Aldric**

The king stood in the chaos of his own ambush unraveling and felt, for the first time in eleven years, something like fear.

The threshold of light hung above the stones, incomplete, flickering, but *there* — a country showing through it like a face behind frosted glass. His spell shuddered in his marrow. He could taste the erasure straining, the great flat lie of a kingdom unmade suddenly asked to hold its shape against the true name being sung out of the ground.

"Take them!" he rasped, and his voice cracked like his body, paper and milk and rust. "The taster especially — *alive* —"

His grey hoods surged toward the pillars. Above them the incomplete Door hummed, and beneath them something older than his crown answered, and King Aldric, who had unmade a country to save his own life, understood with perfect clarity that he had perhaps one hour left to steal a vessel —

— or die twice.

Around the singing stones, the girl's found family rose to meet his army, and Captain Veyra drew her sword, and Aldric could not tell — even then, even now — which side the blade would fall on.
