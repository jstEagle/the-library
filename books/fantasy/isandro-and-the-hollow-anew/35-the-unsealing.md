## 35. The Unsealing

The bargain hung in the air between them like a blade balanced on its point.

Aldric stood at the edge of the Stone Choir with Tam's wrist locked in his fist, and the boy was so pale he looked already unmade — a sketch of himself, half-erased. Behind the king, his army filled the dark hills in their hundreds, torches guttering in the wind that came down off the singing stones. Grey hoods. Iron devices humming on long poles, their sound like wasps trapped in glass.

"Your vessel," Aldric said again, and his voice was not cruel. That was the worst of it. It was tired. It was the voice of a man who had been dying for twenty years and had finally found someone who could stop it. "You give me what you carry. Your people walk free. The free cities burn or don't burn — I am a man of my word, taster. Ask anyone I haven't killed."

Isandro felt Wren's hand at her back, steady between her shoulder blades. She felt Sef breathing hard beside her, his wounded side seeping through the bandage. She felt Perrin pressed against her leg, small and shaking, hearing something under the earth she couldn't yet hear herself.

And she felt the thing inside her — the corked, sealed, decades-deep vessel of everything Hollowmere had tried to drown — turning over in her chest like something waking.

"Yes," Isandro said.

She heard Wren's breath catch. She heard Sef say her name, low and urgent, and she did not look at him because if she looked at him she would have to explain, and there was no time to explain, and some part of what she meant to do would not survive being spoken aloud.

"I agree," she said clearly, so the whole hillside could hear it. "My gift for their lives. Let the boy go first."

Aldric studied her. Up close, in the light of the Choir's half-opened threshold — that seam of gold hanging in the air between the stones, wide enough now to show a country that might exist again — up close she could see what the poison had left of him. A grayness under the skin. A tremor in the sword-hand he kept deliberately still. A man holding himself together by will alone, because the spell that held Vessia together was also the spell that held *him* together, and both were fraying.

"You understand what you're giving me," he said. "It will hurt."

"Most true things do."

He gestured, and two grey hoods came forward and cut Tam's bonds. The boy stumbled toward them, and Perrin broke from Isandro's side and caught him, and the two of them clung together like driftwood, and Isandro let herself feel one clean second of relief before she turned back to the king.

*One second,* she told herself. *Grieve later. Spend everything now.*

The hunters made a circle. They were afraid of her — she could see it in the way they held their humming devices, angled outward like spears. Good. Fear made men precise, and precision was what she needed, because what she was about to do required every one of them standing exactly where they stood.

Wren stepped up beside her. "Whatever you're doing," the hedge-witch murmured, "do it fast."

"You knew?"

"I've known since the ford." Wren's voice was dry as ash and steady as a hearthstone. "You don't bargain with a man like that unless you mean to cheat. It's the only language he respects." Her hand found Isandro's wrist and gripped once, hard. "My sister cheated too. At the end. They burned her anyway, but she died laughing, and I have thought about that laugh every day for eleven years." Her eyes were bright and terrible. "Laugh, Isandro."

* * *

They brought out the transfer-iron then — an old thing, older than Aldric, older maybe than the erasure itself, a bowl of black metal on a stand, covered in script that Sef had once shown her in a confiscated map: *the writing they used before they struck the names out.* It hummed when they set it between her and the king, and the stones of the Choir answered it, a low sympathetic note rolling around the hilltop like distant thunder deciding whether to be weather.

"Kneel," Aldric said. "Put your hands in the bowl. The iron does the rest. It will feel like drowning, and then it will feel like nothing, and then you will be what I was before — ordinary, and free of it."

"And you'll be whole."

"I'll be alive." He said it without shame, and Isandro understood suddenly that this was the whole of him: not ambition, not cruelty, just a poisoned man's endless arithmetic, twenty years of trading other people's lives against his own and calling the sum justice. He knelt across the bowl from her, and his grey eyes held hers, and for one strange moment she pitied him.

Then she put her hands into the transfer-iron, and the world went white.

It began as he'd promised — drowning. The seal inside her cracked, and the first thin thread of what she carried poured out of her, and it was agony, it was cold water in the lungs, it was every year of Hollowmere at once: the laced broths, the numb tongue, the candle flame bending toward her finger in the dark, the valley pressing down on her like a thumb on a beetle, all of it rising up her arms and out through her palms into the black bowl, and Aldric gasped as it touched him, gasped like a man coming up from deep water, and his face began to change, color flooding back into the gray—

*Now,* said something in her that was not quite Isandro anymore. *Now, while the door is open.*

She stopped holding anything back.

* * *

Later — much later, in another life — she would try to describe it to Perrin, and fail, and settle for: *you know how you hear doors? It was like that, except I was the door.*

The vessel broke.

Not opened. Not poured. *Broke* — the way ice breaks on a spring river, all at once, in a hundred places, with a sound like the sky clearing its throat. Twenty-nine years of stored magic, every drop the valley had ever pressed into her and corked, every ounce her body had quietly absorbed and sealed away while she sipped poisons and felt nothing — it all came loose at once, and it did not go politely into the black bowl.

It went everywhere.

The transfer-iron screamed — actually screamed, a note like a struck bell with a voice — and split down the middle, and the light that came out of it was not white but every color at once, the gold of the threshold and the green of Marrowfen and the deep blue of running water, and it hit the Stone Choir and the Choir *answered.*

The pillars sang.

Not the wind-song they'd heard on the road, not the patient ghost-music of old stone. This was a chord, vast and human and furious, and Isandro knew — the way you know your own name — that the stones were singing words. Old words. Words in the dead tongue, the tongue the village off every map still whispered over its coin-baked bread, and the words had a shape, and the shape was:

*Vessia.*

The true name. Not the erased name, not the struck-out name scratched from every milestone and charter. The name underneath, the one no decree could reach because it wasn't written anywhere — it was sung, it had always been sung, it was the country's own heartbeat, and the Choir had been keeping it all this time, waiting for enough voice to sing it aloud.

The name rolled down the hills like a flood.

And where it went, things happened.

The grey hoods' devices burst, one after another down the slope, little blooms of sparks like festival lights. The torches flared green. And the soldiers — Aldric's soldiers, the men who had marched on the free cities, the men who had burned Wren's sister and hollowed the valleys and struck a country from the memory of the world — the soldiers staggered and put their hands to their ears and *remembered.* Isandro could see it happening to them, could see it land like blows: grandfathers' songs, grandmothers' bread, names their families had been forbidden for a generation. Some of them wept. Some of them dropped their weapons. One man near the front fell to his knees and began, in a cracked and rusty voice, to sing along.

Because that was the thing about unmaking, Isandro realized, standing in the heart of the light with her hands still in the ruined bowl and power pouring through her like a river through a broken dam — that was the thing Aldric had never understood, the thing no usurper understands:

You cannot erase a country. You can only bury it. And buried things wait.

* * *

Aldric was screaming.

He had taken the first thread of her gift gladly, hungrily — and then the rest had come, all of it, far more than any vessel could hold, because she had never been a cup. She had been a cistern. Twenty-nine years deep, and he had asked for all of it, and it was drowning him exactly as he'd promised it would drown her.

But it was worse than drowning, and she watched him understand it. Because the magic pouring into him was not just hers. It was Vessia's. It came wearing the true name like a garment, and the unmaking spell — the great grey lattice of erasure he had built his crown upon, the spell that held Hollowmere hollow and the maps blank and the milestones mute — the unmaking spell recognized the name, and began, thread by thread, to unravel around him.

She saw it happen. She saw the grey light that clung to him — the same grey as the hoods, the same grey as the dead space on Sef's charts — she saw it fray and pull apart like rotten cloth, and underneath it Aldric was just a man, an old sick man kneeling in a ring of singing stones, and the sickness rose up in him all at once, twenty years of deferred poison arriving together like a debt called in.

"No," he said. It was almost a whisper. "No — I saved it. I *saved* it—"

"You didn't save it," Isandro said. Her voice came out strange, doubled, as if the stones were speaking through her, or she through them. "You buried it. There's a difference. Buried things come back."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way Veyra had once looked at her across a supper table, hunter to hunted, person to person — and what was in his face was not hatred. It was recognition. The look of a man meeting, at the very end, the one honest thing he'd ever bargained with.

"The door," he rasped. "What's behind the door. Tell me—"

"Choice," Isandro said. "That's all it is. That's all it ever was."

The last thread of the unmaking came apart. The grey went out of him like a snuffed lamp, and King Aldric of nothing, unmaker of Vessia, dying man and murderer and arithmetic, folded forward into the black bowl that had been meant to hold her gift, and did not move again.

The hilltop rang with silence.

* * *

Isandro came back to herself on her knees in the grass, and the first thing she felt was — nothing.

Not the nothing of immunity. The nothing of *after.* Her mouth tasted of blood and honey. Her hands were scorched pink where the bowl had held them. And inside her chest, where the sealed vessel had sat her whole life — heavy, silent, secret — there was now simply space. Room. An emptiness so clean it felt like the first breath after fever breaks.

"It's gone," she whispered. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "All of it. I gave it away and it — it took the walls with it."

"Good," said Wren, dropping down beside her, soot-streaked and shaking and grinning like a woman at a wedding. "Walls are for prisons. Can you stand?"

"I think so."

"Stand anyway. Look."

Isandro stood. Swayed. Sef was there instantly, one arm under hers, his wound forgotten, his face lit gold by the threshold-light — and beyond him, the hillside was transformed. The army was gone. Not destroyed — *gone*, streaming away down the coronation road in ragged companies, some weeping, some singing, carrying the true name home to villages that had been forbidden to speak it. The grey hoods' machines lay shattered among the grass like the ribs of dead whales. And overhead, where the night had been, the stars seemed closer, brighter, as though a veil had been lifted off the whole sky.

Because it had. The hollowing was ending. Everywhere the spell had touched, she could feel it letting go — valleys remembering their depth, rivers remembering their course, the world exhaling after twenty years of held breath.

Perrin appeared at her elbow, Tam's hand locked in his, both of them filthy and alive and staring at her with identical expressions of awe.

"You exploded," Perrin said reverently.

"I didn't—"

"The stones sang your name too," he said. "Did you know that? Underneath Vessia's name. Yours was in there. *Isandro Dunmore, the vessel, the door that chose.*" He frowned, working at it. "I don't think it means you're a door. I think it means you get to decide what opens next."

Isandro looked at him — this feral boy who had attached himself to her without asking, whom she had promised badly and meant completely — and felt something in her chest that was not magic at all, and had never needed a vessel, and had survived everything intact.

"Perrin," she said. "I'm going to need you to hear something for me."

He tilted his head, listening — to the Choir, to the threshold, to whatever vast patient thing waited beneath the singing stones. His eyes went wide.

"It's awake," he breathed. "The Door. It's fully awake, and—" He grabbed her arm. "Isandro. It's asking for us. All of us. It wants to know where we want to go."

Behind them, the seam of gold between the stones widened, slow as a dawn, until it stood open — a doorway of light, tall as a cathedral, showing not a country but the *possibility* of one, raw and unnamed and waiting.

And the Door of Anew, which had waited a generation to be found, waited now, patiently, for each of them to answer one question:

*Where?*

Wren stepped forward, the urn of her sister's ashes cradled against her chest, and her voice, when it came, was steady as a hearthstone.

"We need to talk about that," she said, "all five of us. Before anyone answers."
