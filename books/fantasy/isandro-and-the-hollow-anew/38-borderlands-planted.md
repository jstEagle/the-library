## 38. Borderlands, Planted

The ground here still remembered frost when Wren Ostara knelt in it, but the frost came up easy, melting against her bare fingers like it had been waiting all winter for permission to go.

She had buried a great many things in her life — sisters, patients, hopes, the occasional reputation — and she knew soil the way other healers knew pulse-points. This soil was good. Not the fat black loam of Marrowfen, which grew anything you asked and several things you didn't, but a thin, honest brown over river stone, the kind that made a plant fight for its living. Things that fought for their living stayed rooted.

"Your hands are going purple," said Perrin, crouched beside her with his chin on his knees, watching her work the way he watched everything: as if the world might hum a secret if you stared hard enough.

"They're going *warm*," Wren corrected. "There's a difference. Purple means dying. Warm means alive and complaining about it." She held one hand out to him, palm up, dirt in every crease. "Feel."

He touched her palm with one finger, solemn as a priest taking an oath, then nodded once, satisfied, and went back to staring at the field.

They were nine days past the thaw, five weeks since the Choir, and the borderlands had begun to make up their mind about spring. The river that marked the edge of Callowmark's jurisdiction ran high and loud with snowmelt, and beyond it — beyond the last surveyor's stone, beyond the last tax ledger, beyond the last place any clerk in any free city could point to and say *this belongs to someone* — the land rolled away south in long grassy shoulders toward mountains nobody had bothered to name because naming them would mean admitting they existed.

That was the whole idea, Wren thought. Existence by omission.

Sef Ardenne stood at the top of the nearest shoulder with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, driving stakes into the earth with a mallet and muttering to himself in the language of men arguing with their own measurements. Every few minutes he'd stop, squint at the horizon, consult the battered leather case of instruments at his hip, and then argue some more. He had been at it since dawn.

"You're staking out a graveyard," Wren called up to him. "At this rate we'll be dead before the walls are done."

"I'm staking out a *town*," Sef called back, affronted. "Graveyards are much easier. Everything's already lying down."

Isandro Dunmore came up the slope behind him carrying two buckets of water from the river, walking with the careful, deliberate stride of a woman who had learned — recently, expensively — exactly how much her body could carry and refused to be caught pretending otherwise. The brand on her forearm had healed to a pale shine under the new sun, the false name *Maren Voss* scabbed over into something almost like ornament. She set the buckets down by the fire-ring they'd built from river stones and looked out at the same view Sef was measuring, and Wren watched her look at it, because watching Isandro look at things had become one of Wren's quiet pleasures, like strong tea or the first honest breath after a fever breaks.

Isandro looked at land the way a taster looked at soup. Cautiously. Thoroughly. Waiting for it to hurt her.

"It's not poisoned," Wren said.

"I know." Isandro pushed a strand of dark hair off her face, leaving a smear of mud across her temple. "I'm checking anyway."

"That," Wren said, "is the first sensible thing anyone's said about this valley all morning."

* * *

The valley — because that was what it was, whatever Sef insisted about towns — sat in a fold of the borderlands where two streams met the river, sheltered on three sides by low wooded hills and open on the fourth to the south, where the light came in long and gold from midmorning until dusk. There was no road to it. That was not an accident; Sef had spent four days finding a place with no road, which in the settled lands was harder than finding a place with gold in it. The nearest village was half a day's walk and had, according to Perrin, exactly forty-one doors, none of which hummed with anything worse than loneliness.

No king. No registry. No brand. Isandro had said those words the first night they camped here, standing in the cold with her arms wrapped around herself, and Wren had thought she sounded less like a woman making a declaration than a woman setting a bone — something that had been broken a long time and needed, finally, to be set straight even though setting it would hurt.

It had hurt. It was still hurting. But bones did that, and Wren knew better than anyone that the pain of setting was not the same as the pain of breaking. One of them meant you were healing.

Veyra arrived at midday, as she arrived everywhere: without warning, without apology, and with something useful.

Wren heard the horse before anyone else — you learned to hear horses in Marrowfen, where riders usually meant either customers or corpses — and by the time the grey mare crested the eastern hill, Wren had already decided not to reach for the knife in her boot. Progress. Two months ago her hand would have moved on its own.

Veyra rode down into the valley with a carcass slung across her saddle, a red deer, shot clean through the neck, and dismounted with the economy of motion that Wren had come to recognize as the only honesty the woman permitted herself. Everything else about Veyra Dray was performance — the courtesy, the menace, the weary competence — but the way she moved was true. A body doesn't lie about how it fights.

"Poacher's meat," Veyra said, by way of greeting. "Technically the Crown of Callowmark owns that deer. Technically I'm supposed to arrest myself."

"Technically," Sef said, coming down the hill with his mallet over his shoulder, "the Crown of Callowmark owns nothing past the river, which is why we're standing here."

"Which is why I'm standing here too." Veyra handed him the deer's hind legs without ceremony. "Pardoned by usefulness rather than law. Your phrase, cartographer. I've decided to live up to it."

She had been coming every week since the thaw. First with warnings — Aldric's remnant captains squabbling over the corpse of his army, the free cities' councils quietly burning every record of the Edict of Cleansing and hoping no one remembered it had ever been written — then with supplies, then with labor, and now with deer. Nobody had voted to let her stay. Nobody had voted to send her away either, and in the absence of a vote, Wren had noticed, people simply kept feeding her, and a person who kept being fed was, whether anyone admitted it or not, staying.

Tam appeared from the woodline where he'd been setting snares — real snares now, not the desperate boy-traps of the road — took one look at the deer, and lit up like a lamp. "Is that—"

"Yours to skin," Veyra said. "I don't do the messy part."

"You absolutely do the messy part," Tam said, already dragging the carcass toward the stream, "you just pretend you don't."

Wren watched the boy go and felt the old ache move through her chest, the one that lived beside her sister's ashes, except that these days the ache had company — a whole crowd of aches, really, Tam's hollow cheeks that first terrible night in Callowmark, Isandro's silence after the ford, Sef's fever, Perrin flinching from a door slamming — and the strange thing, the thing no one warned you about grief given a profession, was that aches could get crowded enough to keep each other warm.

* * *

They broke ground properly on the third day of the week, which was not a day anyone had chosen so much as a day that chose itself: the sky washed clean, the wind out of the south, the river loud and bright, and all six of them awake before dawn for no reason any of them could defend.

Sef laid out his plan on a trestle table made of two doors salvaged from a burned waystation — the plan itself drawn in ink on the back of one of his own confiscated-and-rebought maps, because Sef Ardenne did not waste good paper on dreams. He had drawn the valley the way he drew everything, obsessively, lovingly: the meeting of the streams, the rise of the southern hill, the longhouse marked in careful strokes where the ground leveled, gardens, a well, a wall that was honestly more suggestion than fortification.

"No king," he said, tapping the map. "So no keep. No registry, so no gatehouse. No—" he hesitated over the word, then said it plainly, because Sef always said things plainly once he'd decided to say them at all, "no brand, so no barracks. What there is, is a hall big enough for everyone to eat in, and rooms, and fields, and a wall we build because walls are good neighbors, not because we're afraid."

"And if we're afraid anyway?" Tam asked.

"Then we're afraid together, inside the wall, which is the entire point of walls."

Perrin had been quiet through all of this, sitting cross-legged at the end of the table with his head cocked, listening to the ground the way he listened to everything. Now he raised his hand like a child in a schoolroom, waited for no one to call on him, and said:

"The well shouldn't go there."

Everyone looked at the spot on the map. Everyone looked at Perrin.

"There's a door under it," he said. "Small one. Old. It's not a *door*-door, it's a — a cellar-door kind of humming, but it's deep, and if you dig a well there you'll wake it up and it'll be rude about it." He pointed instead at a blank space near the eastern stream. "There. Nothing's sleeping there. Just water."

Sef stared at him for a long moment, then picked up his pen and moved the well.

That was the whole government of the settlement, Wren thought, biting back a smile: a disgraced cartographer who yielded to a twelve-year-old's ears, a huntress who poached for the common table, a hedge-witch, a taster, a thief-turned-farmer, and a boy who heard doors. If the free cities' councils could have seen it, they'd have called it anarchy. Wren called it the first honest thing she'd seen built in thirty years of watching dishonest things stand.

They dug the footings of the longhouse by hand, all six of them, and Wren worked beside them with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back and blisters rising on palms that had spent decades holding scalpels and poultices rather than spades. Her back complained. Her shoulders complained. She let them complain and kept digging, because there was a particular medicine in this kind of ache too — the kind that came from building instead of mending, from putting your strength into something that wasn't already broken.

Once, pausing to wipe her face, she looked up and saw Isandro across the trench, and Isandro was already looking at her, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying that the trench wasn't already saying. We are here. We are digging. We stayed.

In Marrowfen, Wren had amputated hope for a living. Cut it clean, cauterized the stump, told families the truth because soft lies rotted faster than hard ones. Standing in the cold mud of a country that officially did not exist, sweating over a foundation for a house with no owner, she understood — with the mild embarrassment of a professional proven wrong — that she had never actually cut hope out of anyone. She had only ever cut away the dead tissue around it. Hope, it turned out, was the part that survived.

* * *

Isandro planted the seed on the evening of the fifth day.

Wren knew it was coming — they all did; Isandro had spoken of it only twice since the Choir, both times briefly, both times with her hand closed around the ring at her throat — but knowing did nothing to prepare the air in the valley for the moment it actually happened. Perrin felt it first. He stopped mid-sentence at supper, head snapping toward the southern hill, and said, "Oh," in a small voice, "oh, it's *awake*," and then everyone was moving, following Isandro up the slope in the last copper light of the day.

She walked like a woman going to a graveside and a wedding at once, which, Wren reflected, was precisely what it was.

On the crown of the southern hill — the highest ground, the ground that caught the last light — Isandro stopped and unclasped the thin chain from around her neck. Her grandmother's ring sat in her palm, plain silver, worn thin at the band from generations of hands. And inside it, where a stone should have been, there was light: a seed of it, no bigger than a grain of barley, glowing with the patient gold-white radiance of the Door of Anew, the last unspent fragment of a country that had chosen, through five stubborn people, to begin again.

Isandro knelt. She dug with her hands — no spade, no ceremony anyone had planned — turning the spring-soft earth until there was a hole the size of a fist. Then she paused, and looked up at the five of them standing in a loose half-circle around her, silhouetted against a sky going rose and violet at the edges.

"I want to say something," she said, "but I've never been good at this part. Sef makes speeches. Wren makes diagnoses. I make sure nobody's poisoned." A shaky breath of a laugh went around the circle. "So I'll just say the true thing, which is: I spent my whole life tasting other people's food in a valley built to keep me empty, and I thought that was all I was — a vessel that passed things through without being changed by them. And then I learned I wasn't empty. I was *full*. Sealed shut. And everything I am now, everything I'm standing on—" she pressed her palm flat against the earth, "—is because five people refused to let me stay sealed."

She opened her hand and dropped the ring into the hole.

The light sank into the soil like water into drought-cracked ground — eagerly, hungrily, with a sound Perrin later described as a sigh and Wren privately thought of as a first breath. For a moment nothing. Then the earth on the crown of the hill began, very softly, to glow — not the ring anymore, but the ground itself, a spreading warmth of gold beneath the grass, pulsing slow as a heartbeat, and where the glow touched, the spring came up green. Not gradually. *Immediately.* Grass lengthening, buds swelling open on the hillside hawthorn, the whole slope shivering into a season it had already half-decided on, as if the seed had merely given the land permission to be what it was becoming anyway.

Nobody spoke. Perrin had tears running freely down his face and didn't seem to notice them. Tam had his hand over his mouth. Veyra — Veyra had gone to one knee, quite unconsciously, the way soldiers kneel, and Wren saw her lips moving around words that might have been a prayer to a god she'd never served or an apology to one she had.

And Wren Ostara, hedge-witch of Marrowfen, amputator of hope, keeper of her sister's scattered ashes, stood on a nameless hill in a country with no name yet and let herself feel the thing she had rationed out in thimbles for thirty years.

Joy. Quiet, ruin-surviving, spring-come-at-last joy.

"We should give it a name," Sef said at last, hoarsely. "The valley. Countries need names. Even small ones." He looked around at them. "Especially small ones."

"Not tonight," Isandro said. She was still kneeling, her hand resting on the glowing grass, and she was smiling — really smiling, the rare full one, the one that cost her nothing now. "Tonight it's just ours. Names can wait until morning."

* * *

But morning, when it came, brought something else with the birdsong.

Wren woke first — old habit, the healer's hour — and stepped out of the half-built longhouse into a valley full of gold mist, and stopped.

The seed-light had not gone out with the dawn. It had spread. All along the southern hill, in patterns too regular for root or rhizome, thin lines of gold traced through the soil like veins, like script, like — and Wren's breath caught, because she had seen this before, in Sef's maps, in the erased margins of a country struck from every chart — like the ghost-lines of roads. Old roads. Coronation roads. The light was following them, or they were following the light, running away southward toward the mountains and northward toward the river and westward into hills no surveyor had ever troubled to draw, and at the edge of hearing, at the very floor of perception, the whole valley was humming.

Not a door-hum. Something larger. Something patient.

Perrin came out beside her, barefoot in the frost-wet grass, and stood listening with his eyes wide and his fists clenched at his sides.

"Wren," he whispered. "It's not just the seed. The whole country's waking up." He swallowed. "And it's asking for its name."
