## 39. The Taster's Table

The kitchen at Dunmore Hollow was not a palace kitchen, and that was the whole point of it.

Isandro stood at the hearth with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and flour on her forearms, watching a pot of barley soup breathe. The room was small — small enough that two people bumped elbows at the counter, small enough that the smoke knew its way out the chimney by heart — and it smelled of thyme and roasting onions and the particular sweetness of bread rising in a warm corner. Through the window over the basin she could see the borderlands unrolling eastward: new-plowed fields stitched in green, the river running bright with spring melt, and beyond it all the faint blue suggestion of hills where no king had ever set a boundary stone.

Three years ago she had tasted poison in a palace kitchen and felt nothing. This morning she had dipped a spoon into this soup and felt *everything* — the pepper's bite, the marrow's depth, the salt she'd added a half-grain too generously because her hands still remembered feeding a hundred mouths instead of five.

She tasted every dish first. She supposed she always would.

It was habit now rather than duty, the way some soldiers kept saluting long after their wars ended. Spoon to pot, spoon to mouth, a moment of attention so complete it was almost prayer. But where the old tasting had been an absence — a void where fear should live, a corked vessel swallowing harm without comment — this was different. Warmth spread down her throat and settled behind her sternum like a coal wrapped in wool. Nothing in the soup was poisoned. Nothing in her life was poisoned. And her body, which had spent twenty years learning the shape of danger by its silence, kept checking anyway, the way a tongue returns to a gap where a tooth used to be.

*Safe,* said the warmth. *Safe. Safe.*

She had stopped arguing with it around midwinter.

Outside, Perrin's voice carried across the yard, high and imperious, directing chickens as though they were a regiment. He'd grown a hand-span in the last year and wore his new height like a stolen coat, all elbows and sudden gravity. He could still hear doors — every latch and hinge within a mile announced itself to him each dawn like a town crier with too much news — but he'd learned to sleep through most of it. Some mornings he came down and told them what the settlement's doors had dreamed about overnight. Last week the meetinghouse door had been, in his professional opinion, "lonely."

"Table," he had instructed her at breakfast, with the gravity of a general issuing orders. "I'm setting the table tonight. Properly. With the *good* cloth."

The good cloth. Wren had traded three jars of fever-salve for it at the Callowmark spring market — linen dyed the deep green of old Vessian banners, embroidered along one edge with a pattern neither Wren nor Sef could read until Perrin touched it and hummed. Then Sef had gone very quiet, traced the stitching with one finger, and said: *This is a coronation road.* They ate off it anyway. That was the whole point of everything they'd built here. Sacred things belonged on ordinary tables or they belonged nowhere.

The oven breathed out a gust of heat. Isandro pulled the loaf — dark rye, a coin pressed into the bottom out of a custom she'd learned in a village that remembered — and set it on the sill to cool. Her grandmother's ring caught the light on her right hand. Inside it, invisible unless you knew how to look, a seed of the Door's light slept, patient as a heartbeat. Some nights she took it off and held it to her ear, and if she was very quiet, she could almost hear the Choir singing far away under the mountains, singing the true name of a country that was learning, field by field and table by table, to exist again.

Not a kingdom. They had been very clear about that. A country needed no crown; it needed bread, and someone to taste it first, and people worth feeding.

The soup wanted another minute. Isandro wiped her hands and looked at the kitchen — really looked, the way she hadn't let herself in the palace, where looking too closely at anything invited it to be taken away. Copper pots hung from pegs Sef had hammered in crooked. Dried herbs crowded the rafters, bundled by Wren's precise hands. On the shelf above the basin sat a chipped jug Tam had made in his first month at the wheel, lopsided and glaze-cracked and used for nothing but wildflowers, because Tam had declared it "too beautiful for water." There were four chairs and a bench. There was a fifth chair by the door that nobody mentioned and nobody removed, because Veyra came when she came, usually unannounced, always hungry, and pretending otherwise helped no one.

Isandro tasted the soup again. Warmth. Safe.

She smiled at the pot like a fool, and let herself.

* * *

Perrin set the table the way he did everything: with total commitment and total chaos.

He laid the good cloth with ceremonial slowness, smoothing every wrinkle twice, then ruined the effect entirely by sprinting back and forth from the cupboard with plates clutched in dangerous stacks. He arranged the spoons by size, then by shininess, then — after visible deliberation — by "which ones feel friendliest," a criterion he refused to explain. He placed the salt cellar dead center like a keystone and stepped back with his hands on his hips.

"There," he announced to the room. "Perfect. Nobody touch anything."

"You're going to have to let people touch things," Sef said mildly from the corner, where he sat hunched over the big table with his maps, "or dinner will be extremely theoretical."

"That's different. That's *dinner*. This is the *table*."

Sef laughed — a dry, surprised sound, still slightly rusty even after three years, as though laughter were a language he'd once spoken fluently and was relearning word by word. His shoulder had healed wrong; he favored it when the weather turned, and he drew slower than he used to, but the maps had gotten better anyway. Slower and better. He worked now the way Wren healed: carefully, with full attention, as though each line might matter more than he could know.

What he was drawing tonight was not a map of anywhere that existed yet. Isandro had watched it grow across the winter — a valley they'd walked in autumn, following a stream to its source, where the grass grew in a way Perrin said "hummed friendly" and the light came through the trees at an angle Sef swore matched no compass bearing he trusted. Unmade country. Raw potential, waiting to be named. Sef had surveyed it six times, argued with himself about it in his sleep, and finally begun drafting a chart of a place that was only half real, inked in his careful cartographer's hand, with rivers drawn in confident curves and hills shaded in patient crosshatch.

In the center of the valley there was a blank space. He'd left it blank through eleven drafts.

Tonight, while Perrin guarded his table arrangement and the bread cooled on the sill, Sef picked up his pen, hovered over the blank space for a long moment — Isandro saw him glance up, find her eyes across the kitchen, and look away quickly, embarrassed at being caught feeling something — and wrote.

Small letters. Steady hand. She couldn't read them upside down, and she didn't try. Some gifts you waited for.

Tam arrived first, as he always arrived everywhere: slightly late, slightly breathless, carrying something. Tonight it was a jug of cider from the orchard he'd planted his second spring here, the trees still young enough that the cider was thin and sharp and tasted mostly of promise. He'd filled in since the road — broad through the shoulders now, his hands scarred from kiln-work, a short beard he maintained with more enthusiasm than skill. Sometimes Isandro looked at him and saw the stable-boy trembling in a grey hood's grip, and the guilt rose in her like floodwater. And sometimes — more often now — she looked at him and simply saw Tam, who had saved himself, who had crossed half a country on a broken leg to warn them, who built beautiful useless jugs and terrible useful fences and never once asked her to apologize for the window she'd watched him through without moving.

He didn't need the apology. He'd told her so once, plainly, the way he said most true things. "You were a prisoner figuring out the walls," he'd said. "I was a kid figuring out mine. We both got out. That's the whole story." Then he'd handed her a mug of cider and drunk to their mutual escape, and that had been that, more or less, except for the part that lived in her chest forever, which was fine. Some things you carry. She of all people knew you could carry a great deal and stay standing.

Wren came in smelling of soap and woodsmoke, her healer's hands scrubbed pink, her gray-streaked hair braided back for the occasion — and it was an occasion, Isandro understood, though nobody had named it. Three years to the season since five strangers had stood in a stone circle singing, holding a door open against an army. Wren never marked the date aloud. She just braided her hair, and brought out the good cups, and sat a little closer to the fire than usual, and everyone understood.

She paused at the threshold — a habit none of them had ever broken her of, the hedge-witch's instinct to read a room before entering it — and her gaze went, as it always did, to the mantel. To the small clay urn that sat there between a candle stub and Perrin's collection of interesting rocks.

Liora's ashes had come through the Door in Wren's arms, and Wren had scattered them into the light at the threshold, keeping only a handful — "enough for a grave," she'd said, "but I'm not ready to dig it yet." The urn had traveled with them to the borderlands and sat on every mantel since. Some nights Wren talked to it. Recently, Isandro had noticed, the talking had changed register — less confession, more conversation. Grief becoming companionship. Sorrow served warm instead of raw.

"Something smells criminal," Wren said, hanging her cloak. "Whose funeral are we celebrating?"

"Nobody's," Isandro said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They ate the way they always ate: too loudly, passing dishes before anyone finished serving themselves, three conversations braided into one, Tam telling a story about the kiln collapsing that got funnier with each retelling because he improved the details shamelessly. Perrin narrated the day's door-report between bites. Wren scolded Sef for salting before tasting and Sef defended himself with the observation that he'd spent thirty years trusting other people's charts and had earned the right to trust his own tongue, however unreliable.

And Isandro tasted everything first.

She moved down the table doing it — the soup, the bread torn steaming from the loaf, the honey-glazed carrots, the cider — spoon or sip or crumb to her lips before anyone else, a ritual so woven into the family's rhythm now that they passed things to her automatically, first, without discussion. It wasn't superstition exactly. It wasn't quite blessing either. It was somewhere between: a taster's grace, an old vow translated into a new language. In Hollowmere her tongue had been a wall between the regent and death, and no one had thanked her, and no one had sat with her, and warmth had never once followed the swallow.

Here, every taste was answered. Here, the warmth bloomed and stayed, and the family watched her do it with expressions ranging from Perrin's solemn reverence ("it's like she's knighting the food") to Wren's quiet understanding to Tam's easy grin, and the food became theirs only after it had been hers, and hers only because it would become theirs. A circle. A table. A country of five, expanding by dinner.

When the plates were cleared and the candles burned low, Sef turned his map around and pushed it to the middle of the table.

The blank space had a name now.

*"Ours."*

Just that. One word, in his small steady hand, centered in the unnamed valley where the light came through the trees at impossible angles. No borders around it. No crown above it. No registry, no brand, no edict. A space on a map claimed not by conquest or decree but by the oldest claim there is: someone meant to live there.

Perrin leaned over it, listening with his whole body the way he listened to doors. After a moment he frowned.

"It's humming," he reported. "The blank part. Like a door that hasn't decided to open yet."

"Doors decide?" Isandro asked.

"All the good ones do." He tapped the word *ours* once, gently, as if knocking. "This one's going to be a good one. I can hear it thinking about us."

Silence, then — the good kind, the kind that had taken Isandro years to learn to sit inside without reaching for a task. The candle flames leaned together. Outside, spring rain began, soft on the new fields, and Perrin cocked his head toward the meetinghouse door a mile away and reported, absently, that it had just been closed against the weather and sounded satisfied about it.

Wren reached across the table and covered Sef's hand with hers, over the word. Tam raised his cider jug in a silent toast to the blank-and-named valley. And Isandro looked at the map, and at the faces around it, and felt the warmth behind her sternum — the same warmth that answered every taste, every swallow, every safe meal — swell until it was indistinguishable from joy.

Once, she had been a girl in a valley that magic could not follow, tasting poison in the dark for people who would have burned her, and calling it a life.

Now she was a woman at a table that magic had followed home, tasting bread in the firelight for people who called her family, and *this* was the life, this ordinary evening, this crooked-hung copper, this lopsided jug of wildflowers, this word in wet ink on unfinished land.

"Tomorrow," Sef said quietly, rolling the map closed with the tenderness he reserved for living things, "we should walk out and see if the valley agrees with my survey."

"Tomorrow," Perrin agreed, already planning, already halfway out of his chair, "we take the *good* cloth."

Isandro laughed, and reached for the last of the bread, and tasted it first — warmth, safety, home — and passed it on.
