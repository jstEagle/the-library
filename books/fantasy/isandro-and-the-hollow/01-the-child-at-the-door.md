## 1. The Child at the Door

There are three rules to selling illegal magic in a city that has outlawed it, and I broke all of them the night the child knocked.

Rule one: never open your door after the lamps go out. Rule two: never open it for anyone you can't name. Rule three — and this is the one they carve into your bones if you survive long enough — never, ever let them see that what you're selling is real.

I had kept those rules for three years. Three years of shrinking wonders down into pocket-sized lies: luck-charms disguised as buttons, fever-banishing knots worked into bootlaces, a whole shelf of "decorative" river-stones that would keep a fisherman's nets full so long as nobody asked why. Vessengard called it contraband. I called it rent.

So when the knock came — three small raps, polite as a debt collector — I did what any sensible criminal does. I blew out my lamp and stood very still in the dark, listening.

The knock came again. Same rhythm. Patient. The kind of patience that doesn't expect to be ignored twice.

"I know you're there," said a voice through the wood. Small. Unhurried. "The door knows too. Doors are honest."

Which is when I should have known. Everything about that sentence was wrong for Vessengard. Children here don't talk to doors; they've been taught since the Proclamation that doors have ears and ears have owners. And no one in this city — not the fishwives, not the fence who moved my goods, not even the drunk poet who lived above me — said things like *doors are honest* with that particular flat certainty, as though reporting weather from somewhere very far away.

But I was tired, and it was raining, and I told myself it was probably just some guttersnipe sent by a customer. So I broke rule one, rule two, and — though I didn't know it yet — rule three, all in the space of lifting one latch.

* * *

He was mud to the eyebrows. That's the first thing I remember, before the strangeness, before the cold walking up my spine: a child maybe seven years old, plastered head to foot in the gray silt of the river district, standing on my step like he'd been delivered by floodwater. His clothes were wrong for the city — undyed wool, no seams I recognized, the kind of garment you weave rather than sew. His eyes were the color of shallow water over pale stone.

And he was looking at me the way people look at a landmark.

"You're smaller than the stories," he said.

"I don't know any stories about myself."

"You do." He tilted his head. "You just buried them under the floorboards with the rest."

My hand was already moving toward the knife I kept behind the door frame — old habit, dull blade, mostly for confidence — when he spoke again, and the words were not in any tongue spoken south of the mountains or north of the sea.

They came out liquid and layered, each syllable carrying an echo of itself, the way a bell carries its own note after it's struck. A language of roots and riversides. A language with grammar like weather.

No living scholar knows it. I can say that with authority, because I checked, once, frantically, in a library that no longer exists, in a country that may no longer exist either. When the Crown burned the archives and outlawed the old tongues, that language died on paper everywhere in the world.

Everywhere except inside me.

Because I had charted its homeland, long ago — walked its marshlands with ink-stained fingers, mapped the singing reeds and the door-shaped stones, sat by fires while its speakers taught me the word for *threshold* and the word for *home* and the word, most dangerous of all, for *come back*. I had written it into maps that were seized and burned, and I had thought the language burned with them, the way languages do.

It hadn't. It had been waiting in my skull like a seed under snow. And hearing it now, out of a mud-streaked child's mouth on my own doorstep, was like hearing my own heartbeat played back to me by a stranger.

"*You are the one who draws doors,*" he said in it.

And worse — far worse — I understood him perfectly.

* * *

"Inside," I said, because the street was empty but empty streets in Vessengard are never empty, and because my mouth had apparently decided things without consulting me. "Now. Quickly."

He stepped over my threshold the way other children step into puddles — wholly, delightedly, splashing. The moment his bare feet crossed the boards, every charm in my shop flickered.

I want to be clear about what that means, because it took me a moment to understand it myself. My stock was dead stock. That's the trick of the trade: charms sold openly must be *mostly* nothing, trinkets with a whisper of working left in them, enough to justify the price and not enough to hang you. For three years everything on my shelves had been sleeping. Gray beads. Pretty junk.

When the boy walked in, they woke up.

Not dramatically — no light, no song. Just a shimmer, like heat off summer cobbles, running along the shelves in a wave that followed him. The river-stones turned faintly cool. The knot-charms tightened themselves a half-degree, the way a sleeping dog shifts its paws. And the one thing in the room I should never have kept — a compass with no needle, a compass that pointed at places instead of directions, hidden under my floorboards where I told myself I'd forgotten it — knocked once against the wood, soft as a knuckle.

The boy looked straight at the floorboard.

"That one's loud," he said, in accented but serviceable Vessengard common. "Why do you sleep on it?"

"I don't sleep on anything. Sit down. Don't touch the merchandise."

He sat on my one stool, hands folded, mud dripping onto my floor with the serene entitlement of royalty. Up close he smelled of river water and something older underneath — crushed leaves, hot stone, the smell of a place I could still draw from memory with my eyes shut, which was precisely the problem.

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Isandro."

"Just Isandro? No family name?"

"Names come later where I'm from." He considered. "Yours came early, didn't it? Hazel. It's a plant name. You were named after something that grows along fences."

"And how exactly do you know that?"

"The reeds told us someone would come." He said it the way a child reports that it rained today. "The one who draws doors. Who went away and locked the way behind her. We waited a long time. Then the crown got hungry again, and the elders said, send the smallest one. Smallest ones fit through cracks."

My mouth had gone dry. "*Her* — I'm not— look, whatever the reeds told you, I sell buttons. Charms, technically, but the kind that don't work. Souvenirs. If you're looking for a door-drawer, you want—"

"You understand me," he said simply, switching back to the First Tongue, and the sound of it in my small shop was enormous, a cathedral built inside a matchbox. "Nobody in this city understands me. I've been talking to walls for nine days. Walls are bad listeners. But you heard the word for *hungry* before I finished saying it. Your face knew it first."

Because it had. Because the word for hungry in that language isn't a word so much as an opening — a shape your mouth makes around absence — and my face had made it a half-second before my mind caught up, the way a body flinches from a blow it remembers.

Three years. Three years of being nobody, of being a charm-seller with a fake laugh and a fake stock, of burying Hazel Sandoval, cartographer of the unmapped realms, under the floorboards with the compass. I had gotten good at it. I had gotten *excellent* at it. There were mornings I woke up and genuinely couldn't remember the smell of the singing reeds.

And then a child had knocked on my door speaking the one language that knew where I'd buried myself, and every lock I'd built had opened from the outside.

"I can't help you," I said, in common, firmly, the way you'd slam a shutter. "Whatever you were sent to find, it's not here. Go home."

"I can't." For the first time, something moved under the calm of his face — not fear exactly, more like weight settling. "That's the message, Hazel-who-draws-doors. That's why they sent me instead of a letter." He leaned forward, mud and solemnity, and spoke slowly, in both tongues at once, so I couldn't pretend to misunderstand:

"*The king wears something that isn't him. And the door you closed is opening from the other side.*"

Outside, the rain kept falling, patient as everything else in this city. Somewhere down the street, a watchman's lantern swept past my window, and I caught myself doing the thing I hadn't done in three years — calculating escape routes, mapping the room, mapping the block, mapping the city, my traitor hands twitching toward surfaces that wanted drawing on.

The boy watched me do it. He nodded, satisfied, as if watching a clock finally strike.

"You'll need paper," he said. "And you'll want the compass. It's been calling you for three years. Doors are honest, Hazel. But you haven't been — not to yourself."

I looked at the floorboard. Under it, silent and patient, the needleless compass pointed at a place I had sworn never to map again.

I reached for the crowbar.
