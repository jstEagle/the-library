## 2. Charms for a City That Forbids

The morning after the child arrived, I did what any sensible smuggler of small wonders would do: I tried to pretend he was a dream.

He sat at my kitchen table eating the heel of yesterday's bread with the solemnity of a bishop taking communion, and I told myself, *you drank too much plum wine, you talked to a mud-streaked ghost, and now you will open the shutters and the street will be ordinary and this will all be over.*

I opened the shutters. The street was ordinary. The child was still there.

"You're real," I said, in Vessic, because it seemed rude to keep answering him in a language that had been dead longer than my grandmother's grandmother.

"I am here," he agreed, in the First Tongue, and the words went through me like a needle finding an old scar. Because that was the trouble, you see. I shouldn't have understood him. No living scholar knows that language. The academies call it conjecture, the priests call it blasphemy, and the Crown calls it nothing at all, because the Crown has made very sure no one remembers it exists.

But I had charted its homeland once, back when mapping unmapped things was a profession instead of a crime. And languages, unlike maps, don't stay where you draw them. They follow you home.

"Isandro," I said. "Here is what happens now. You finish your bread. You go back wherever you came from. And you never speak to me again, in any tongue, living or dead."

He looked at me with those eyes — gray as unmarked paper — and said, "You are afraid."

"I'm *practical*. There's a difference. Fear is loud. Practicality whispers." I pulled on my coat, the one with the false seams, and began loading my tray: tin birds that didn't sing, glass beads that were only glass, copper rings polished to look older than they were. "I sell trinkets. That's all anyone in this city needs to know about me."

"Your trinkets are sleeping," Isandro said.

I froze with a tin bird halfway into its slot.

"They are not dead," he went on, with the terrible patience children reserve for stating the obvious. "They are only quiet. Like seeds."

"That," I said, "is exactly the kind of thing that gets people hanged."

* * *

You should understand something about Vessengard before we go any further. It wasn't always like this. There was a time — I know because I've seen the maps, drawn by hands other than mine, showing ley-roads stitched through the districts like bright thread — when magic here was as common as rain. Charm-wrights hung their signs next to bakers. Children learned cantrips alongside their letters. The river itself had been charmed against flooding for four hundred years, gently, the way you'd steady an old friend's elbow.

Then came the Sundering Wars, which is what historians call the twenty years when every realm with a grudge and a spellbook decided to settle it at once. Cities burned. Whole valleys were unwritten. And when the smoke cleared, the man who would become King Aldric stood on a heap of ash and said, in the voice of someone who has buried too many friends: *Never again.*

The Proclamation of Forbidding was read from every bell tower. Magic was outlawed — not regulated, not licensed, *outlawed* — and the Inquisition was founded to make sure the outlawing stuck. Charms were confiscated and drowned in lead. Charm-wrights were given a choice between the gray robes of repentance and the black hoods of the scaffold. Within a decade, Vessengard was the cleanest city in the world, scrubbed of wonder, and everyone agreed it was a small price to pay for peace.

Everyone who survived, anyway.

I arrived two years after the Proclamation, young and arrogant and carrying a satchel full of maps that could get a person killed in seventeen different jurisdictions. I'd charted realms whose names I still can't say aloud without my teeth itching. I thought I was clever enough to outlast a prohibition.

I was not clever. I was *adaptable*, which is a meaner virtue. I learned to shrink wonders down until they fit inside lies. A compass that actually pointed home became a "lucky compass" that pointed north, mostly. A salve that knit bone became "grandmother's liniment, good for aches." I sold them in the Graymarket among the fishwives and the fence-sellers, wrapped in enough mundane chatter that even if an inquisitor stopped to browse, they'd find nothing but a charm-seller's patter over honest junk.

That was the trick, and I want you to appreciate how ugly it was: everything I sold was real, and everything I claimed about it was false. I built a whole life out of mislabeling miracles. Twenty years of it. You'd be amazed what a person can normalize, if they do it slowly enough, one pocket-sized lie at a time.

Some nights I took out my old maps — the true ones, inked in silver and blood — and I looked at the places I'd been, and I felt like a widow reading old letters. Then I put them under the floorboards and went to sleep, because grief is a luxury and I had rent to pay.

* * *

So there we were, the exiled cartographer and the impossible child, walking into the Graymarket on a Tuesday, which in retrospect was the last ordinary thing either of us ever did.

Vessengard's market smells of fish and tar and wet wool, and underneath it all, these days, a faint smell of absence — like a room where a fire used to be. I set up my tray at my usual corner near the fountain, and Isandro planted himself beside it like a weed that had decided to be furniture.

"You can't stand there," I hissed. "You're drawing attention."

"No one is looking," he said. He was right, which annoyed me. Children are invisible in markets; they're part of the scenery, like crates and pigeons. It's adults who attract suspicion, adults with trays of goods and histories. "What is this one?" He picked up a tin bird.

"Decorative. It does nothing."

"It is dreaming," Isandro said, turning it over. "It dreams of flying."

"It's *tin*."

He held it in both palms and looked at it the way you'd look at a sleeping animal, and I swear to every god I've stopped believing in, the wings shivered. Just once. A tiny mechanical flutter, like a heartbeat remembered.

I snatched it back so fast I nearly dropped the tray. An old woman browsing nearby glanced over, and I converted my panic into salesmanship mid-breath: "Finest tinwork in Vessengard, madam, painted to look half-alive! Very lifelike!"

She bought two. Gods help me, she bought two, and I gave her change with hands that wouldn't stop shaking, while Isandro watched me with mild interest, as though I were the strange creature here.

All morning it went like that. Every charm he touched woke a little. The glass beads warmed and threw off colors that weren't in them. The copper rings hummed a note only dogs could hear — three dogs started howling two streets over, and I had to move my stall. A "lucky compass" spun wildly in its case until I clapped a lid on it. Each time, I covered: jostled merchandise, cheap workmanship, wind off the river. I lied faster than I ever had in my life, and beneath the patter a single thought kept time like a drum:

*They will see him. Someone will see him, and then they will see me, and then we will both dangle.*

Because here's what twenty years of concealment teaches you: it isn't the inquisitors you fear most. It's the flicker. The moment when something true shows through the lie, and the crowd goes quiet, and some well-meaning soul says, "Wasn't there light coming out of that bead?" Small concealments survive on silence. Wonder is loud.

By midday I'd made more coin than I usually made in a week and aged approximately a decade. I packed the tray with fingers gone clumsy, and Isandro helped, arranging each item with the care of a boy setting a table for guests.

"Why do you hide them?" he asked quietly, as I latched the case.

"Because the Crown says magic is forbidden."

"Why?"

"Because magic burned the world, and the king promised it would never happen again."

Isandro considered this with his head tipped, the way children do when they've caught an adult in something. "The king is wearing a lie," he said. "You are wearing many small ones. Yours are kinder."

I laughed — a short, ugly sound. "Kid, you don't know what my lies cost."

"No," he agreed. "But I know what yours protect." He touched the edge of my tray, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound. "And I know why I found you. You are the one who draws doors."

"And I'm telling you," I said, shouldering the case, "that door-drawer is retired. Whatever you were sent to find, it isn't me. Go home, Isandro. However you got here — go back."

He didn't argue. That was the worst part. He just fell into step beside me as I headed for the fountain square, small and mud-streaked and patient as winter, and said, in the First Tongue, words I understood against every instinct I owned:

"The door is already opening. I only need you to see it before it opens all the way."

And across the square, past the fishwives and the fountain and the gray-robed inquisitor standing very still by the guardpost — an inquisitor whose eyes had been drifting toward my stall all morning, casual as a fisherman watching a line — the bells of Vessengard began to ring the hour.

Except it wasn't the hour. It was eleven strokes too many.

And every dead charm in my case woke at once, singing.
