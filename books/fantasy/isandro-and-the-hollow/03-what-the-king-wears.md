## 3. What the King Wears

The woman with the map-hands does not sleep right. I know this because I do not sleep right either, and we are in the same small room, and the floorboards tell me everything. Floorboards are honest. They creak when they are unhappy. Most things in this city pretend to be happy and then bite you.

Her name is Hazel. She said it twice, like she was checking whether I would steal it.

I will write down what happened today, because Hazel says writing things down is how you keep them, and I have lost many things already. I had a name before Isandro. The Hollow took it when it took everything else. Names are the first thing hunger eats.

* * *

In the morning, Hazel made tea that tasted like bark pretending to be cinnamon. We sat at her table, which has one short leg and a folded paper under it, and I told her why I came.

"You were sent," she said. "By whom?"

"By the underneath of a crown."

She put down her cup. "Say that again, slowly."

"The underneath of a crown," I said slowly, because she asked. "The part of the king that isn't the king. It is very old and very empty and it wants to be full. It sent me up through the dark places to find the one who draws doors."

"And you think that's me."

I looked at her hands. They have ink in every line of them, even after she scrubs. Ink does not leave people who draw doors. "You drew a door once," I said. "A real one. With a river on one side and a country on the other. The door remembers you. Doors remember their makers longer than mothers remember children, because doors cannot forget on purpose."

Hazel was quiet for a long time. The teacup made a small ring on the table when her hand shook, which it did not want to do, but hands are honest too.

"That was thirty years ago," she said finally. "And it was a map, not a door."

"A map is a door that lies flat," I said. This is true. Everyone in the Hollow knows it. Nobody here knows anything, which is why they outlawed magic — you cannot outlaw what you refuse to see.

* * *

Hazel did not believe me yet. Believing takes some people a long time, like swallowing a fishbone whole. So I showed her.

This is how I show: I hold out my hand, palm up, and I say the word for *look*, and then whatever I am remembering climbs out of my eyes and into yours. It is not magic the way this city means it. It is older than magic's meaning. It is just telling the truth with your whole face.

Hazel took my hand. Her palm was warm and rough as tree bark.

"Ready?" I said.

"No," she said honestly. I liked that. Liars always say yes.

I said *look*.

* * *

Here is what she saw, because I saw it again through her seeing:

A room with high windows and gold everywhere, gold like a sickness, gold growing on the walls the way mold grows on bread left in the rain. A throne. And on the throne, a man.

He was tall once. You can tell tall-once people by their shoulders, which stay wide even when the rest of them folds. His robes fit him the way a coat fits a scarecrow — correctly, and with nothing inside agreeing to it.

"King Aldric," Hazel whispered inside my vision. I felt her voice come from far away, like someone calling down a well.

"Yes," I said. "Look at his face."

His face was a good face. Someone had loved that face; there were lines around the mouth from smiling, old ones, kept ones. But the eyes —

The eyes were wells.

Not empty the way a cup is empty. Empty the way a well is empty: deep, lined with stone, built on purpose to hold nothing until something is poured in. And something was looking out of them. Not a person. A *wearing*. The way a coat looks out of its buttons.

"What is that?" Hazel asked, and her voice cracked down the middle.

"The Regent," I said. "It lives under the crown. Under means beneath, and also before, and also inside. It ate its own country to be born. Then it was still hungry, so it ate the space where the country used to be, and then it was hungry for more, and countries are far away, but kings are close."

"It's wearing him."

"Wearing him," I agreed. "Kings are coats that never get taken off. That is why it outlawed magic here. Magic is loud. Magic glows and hums and tells everyone what it is. The Regent needs quiet, because quiet things can be worn without anyone noticing. It has been wearing your city for thirty years, Hazel. Your laws are its seams."

Inside my vision, the king on the throne moved. He lifted one hand — his hand, the real one, the one that still belonged to him — and pressed two fingers against his own chest, over the heart, twice. Tap-tap. Like knocking.

"He's still in there," Hazel breathed.

"For now," I said. "There is a Rite coming. A big binding, with candles and singing men in gray. When it finishes, the coat gets nailed shut. After that, Aldric is not under the crown anymore. He is *of* the crown. Forever is a long word. The Regent knows all of it."

I let go of her hand. The vision folded itself up like paper and went back into my eyes, where visions live between showings.

* * *

Hazel stood up. Sat down. Stood up again. People who draw doors do not like being still; I have noticed this. Stillness is for things that are finished.

"No," she said. "No, that's — child, that could be anything. A fever dream. A trick. Inquisitors use tricks. Someone could have taught you—"

"Taught me the First Tongue?" I said. "Who? Name the teacher."

She opened her mouth. Closed it. There is no teacher. The First Tongue is not taught; it is *left over*. It is what words were before people bent them into tools. Only the Hollow speaks it now, and the Hollow is not a place you visit. It is a place that happens to you.

"I charted a realm once," Hazel said quietly, mostly to herself. "East of the Salt Meridian. The maps came back wrong. Blank in the middle, like the ink refused. My mentor said the realm had 'closed.' I thought she meant politically." She laughed, and it was an awful laugh, dry as a moth. "It didn't close. It was eaten. Wasn't it."

"Eaten," I said. "Yes."

"And the thing that ate it is sitting on a throne three streets from my shop."

"One street and a hill," I corrected. "I counted."

Hazel put both hands flat on the table, the way people do when the world tilts and they need to check if the table is tilting instead. "Why me? Why does it matter if I know?"

"Because doors open from the maker's side," I said. "The Regent came through a door. Somebody drew that door. And somebody can draw it shut. The underneath of a crown sent me to find the one who draws doors, because it is afraid of exactly one thing in all the world: edges. It has none. Give it edges and it can be closed inside them, the way you close a moth in a book."

"Who drew the door, Isandro?"

I looked at her. She looked at me. Outside, a bell rang the hour, and the sound went through the window glass and lay down on the table between us like a third person.

"I don't know yet," I admitted. "But I think you might. I think you knew them. There is ink on your fear, Hazel. Old ink. Fear with ink on it is memory."

* * *

We went out into the market because Hazel said staying indoors would make us easy to find, and also because she needed to walk, and walking is thinking for people with map-hands.

The market was loud in the way markets are: everyone selling, nobody saying. Hazel sold her trinkets from a tray — little brass birds, painted stones, ribbon charms. "Lucky," she called, flat as a dropped plate. "Luck for sale. Get it while it lasts."

They were charms. Real ones, tiny ones, shrunk down and starved until they only whispered. I could hear them whispering. They sounded like mice under snow.

Then I walked past the tray, and the whispers stopped, and one brass bird opened its wings.

Just a little. Just enough. A flicker, like a candle remembering fire. Hazel grabbed it fast and shoved it in her pocket, and her face went white around the mouth.

"Don't *do* that," she hissed.

"Do what?"

"Make things *true* in public! Do you know what they do to charm-sellers here? Do you know what they did to the last tongue-speaker?" She stopped. Her breath went in and stayed there a while.

"No," I said. "Tell me."

"They burned her," Hazel said. "Twenty years ago. In Coronation Square. She was older than your grandmother and they tied her to a post and the crowd sang the anthem while she—" She stopped again. "The Regent doesn't just outlaw magic. It makes loving magic a crime. Because love is how it gets found."

I thought about that. "So everyone here is lonely on purpose."

Hazel stared at me. "What?"

"If you cannot love the loud true thing," I said, "you get quiet alone. The Regent likes quiet alone. Quiet alone is easy to wear."

She did not answer. She walked faster. I walked faster too. Behind us, on the tray, the other brass birds had all turned their heads to watch me go. Hazel pretended not to see. Pretending is a skill this whole city has mastered, but her pretending had a limp in it.

* * *

Near the fountain, a man in gray watched us. Gray clothes, gray gloves, a gray kindness in his face that did not reach anywhere important. Hazel saw him and her hand found my shoulder and steered me, casual as anything, down a side street.

"Inquisitor," she murmured. "Maren Vale's people. Keep walking. Look bored."

"I am bored," I said truthfully. "Your market sells the same six things forty times."

Despite everything, she huffed something almost like a laugh. Then it died. "Isandro. If what you showed me is real — if the king is really — then who else knows? Who sent you besides the... the underneath?"

"The underneath sends dreams to everyone sometimes," I said. "Most people's dreams are too small to carry the message. Yours weren't. Did you dream of a door lately? A chalk door? A door drawn on a wall that wasn't a wall?"

Hazel stopped walking.

People bumped past us. A fishmonger shouted about prices. Somewhere a dog was having a strong opinion. None of it touched her; she had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere thirty years deep.

"There was a door," she said at last, barely above the noise. "In my dreams. Every night this week. Chalk on stone. And a voice behind it, asking to be let out." Her eyes came to mine, and they were frightened in the specific way of people doing arithmetic they don't want the answer to. "I assumed it was the king. Asking for help."

"It might be," I said. "Or it might be the thing that wears him, asking to be let *in*. Dreams knock from both sides, Hazel. That is the trouble with doors."

Above the rooftops, past the hill, the palace stood with its banners going up for coronation eve — white banners, sewn with a crown, and if you squinted the way I squint, the crown on the cloth seemed to hang slightly lower than the pole it was fixed to. Reaching down. Toward the city. Toward the small bright flickering things it was not supposed to be able to touch anymore.

One banner had been hung wrong. It hung upside down, and nobody was fixing it, and every person who passed beneath it glanced up and then quickly away, the way you glance away from a well.

"Coronation eve is in three days," I said.

"I know," Hazel said. "I know exactly how many days. I've known since the moment you knocked on my door, and I've been hoping I was wrong about why."

She turned toward home, toward the drowned streets below the hill where the old magic still muttered in exile, though she did not say that yet. She only took my hand, which was trust, or surrender, or both.

"Come on," she said. "We need to talk to someone about wells and doors, and I know exactly the wrong person to ask."

That is how we began.
