## 4. The Inquisitor's Kindness

There are three kinds of knocking in Vessengard. The first is the knock of a customer, quick and guilty, twice on the wood and once on the frame, as if the frame might be less watched than the door. The second is the knock of a neighbor who wants sugar and will pretend not to see the chalk marks under your fingernails. And the third is the knock of the Crown, which is no knock at all — it is the sound of boots stopping outside your shop and then a silence so deliberate it might as well be a fist.

I heard the third kind on the morning after Isandro arrived.

I had spent the night not sleeping, which I have found to be an excellent substitute for thinking. The child slept curled against my good chair like a comma, breathing in that deep, unbothered rhythm of the young and the hunted, and every time he exhaled I told myself a story: that he was someone's lost nephew, that his language was a fever dream of my own, that the word he'd spoken at my threshold — the one that had made my dead charms shiver in their jars like insects waking — meant something harmless. *Bread*, perhaps. *Come in out of the rain.*

It did not mean bread. I knew what it meant. I had charted the coastline where that word was born, back when coastlines were still allowed to exist.

The boots outside my door did not move. Neither did I. We regarded each other through the wood with the mutual respect of two people who have done this dance before.

"Hazel Sandoval," said a voice I knew better than my own conscience, "your shutters are crooked."

Maren Vale always led with the shutters. It was her way of announcing that she had already catalogued everything else.

* * *

Inquisitor Maren Vale was gray the way weather is gray — not a color but a condition, something you dressed for. She wore the Crown's slate coat with the ease of a woman who had never once wondered if it fit, and she stood in my shop among my trays of brass compasses and river-stone pendants and "lucky" knots, all of it legal, all of it inert, all of it lies I had sanded smooth over three years, and she looked at me with the patient expression of a cat watching a mouse pay rent.

"Inquisitor," I said. "To what do I owe the honor of being suspected before breakfast?"

"Before breakfast is when the guilty are most honest." She turned a pendant over with one gloved finger. It was genuinely just a stone; I made sure half my stock was. "You look tired, Sandoval."

"I'm a shopkeeper. Shopkeepers are born tired."

"Mm." She set the stone down with a small click that sounded, in the quiet, like a verdict deferred. "A child was seen entering this street last night. Mud to the knees, no coat, speaking — according to the watchman — nonsense. The watchman reported it because nonsense is reportable these days." Her eyes came up to mine. They were pale, unhurried, and missed nothing, which is the professional hazard of pale unhurried eyes. "The child has not been seen leaving."

"Children are slippery."

"They are." She smiled, and it was almost warm, which from Maren Vale was more frightening than any threat could have been. "You know why I've never arrested you, Hazel? In six years?"

"Because you enjoy our conversations."

"Because arresting you would end them." She walked the length of my counter, trailing one finger along the wood like a woman reading braille. "Six years ago a cartographer named Sandoval vanished off every registry in the realm. No death record. No departure. A person does not simply stop existing in Vessengard unless they are very good or very careful, and you, charm-seller, are both. So I waited. I learned your stock. I know which tray holds the warming charms disguised as buttons. I know which knot is a warding and which is only a knot. I have known for four years, and I have done nothing, because you were small. Small crimes, quietly committed, hurting no one." She stopped. "And then last night, something spoke a dead language on your doorstep, and you were no longer small."

My mouth said, "I don't know what you mean." My heart said, *run.* I have learned to let my mouth handle negotiations while the rest of me plans escape routes; it's a division of labor that has kept me alive.

"You don't," she agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. Then you won't mind answering a hypothetical." She drew off one glove, finger by finger, and laid it on the counter like a gauntlet. "Hypothetically, a child of the old blood comes to this city on the eve of a coronation. Hypothetically, that child carries a tongue that predates the Crown's prohibition — a tongue that can *unmake* things, Sandoval. Bindings. Seals. Certain doors the Crown has gone to great expense to keep shut. Hypothetically, there are people above my pay grade who very much want such a child found before certain rites are completed." She flexed her bare hand, watching the fingers as though they belonged to someone more interesting. "And hypothetically, an inquisitor who delivered that child would be owed a debt by those people. Debts buy promotions. Debts buy pardons."

The shop was very quiet. From the back room, faintly, came the sound of a child turning pages in a book he could not read but clearly understood.

"I'm going to ask you plainly," Vale said, "because I think you're tired of pretending, and frankly so am I. Bring me the boy. Tonight. And every crime you've committed in this city — the buttons, the knots, the name you buried — is ash. Forgotten. Forgiven. You walk out of Vessengard a free woman with a clean ledger and a royal stipend, if you want it. Or—" she picked up her glove, slid it back on, smoothed each finger, "—you don't. And then tomorrow I come back with writs instead of hypotheticals, and we discover together how fireproof your little shop is."

I want to tell you I weighed it. I want to tell you there was a long, silver moment where I saw both futures laid out like maps, and chose with wisdom.

What actually happened was faster. What happened was that I heard the page turn again in the back room, small and ordinary and trusting, and something in my chest closed its hand.

"No," I said.

Vale paused, glove half-smoothed. "No?"

"No. He's not merchandise. He's not a debt or a promotion. He's a child who knocked on my door, and I opened it, which — I'm given to understand — is the whole of the law where I grew up." I heard myself and marveled a little. Three years of shrinking wonders into pocket-sized lies, and apparently the original model was still in there somewhere, taking up space, waiting for a reason. "So. No."

For a moment something moved behind Vale's pale eyes — regret, maybe, or its professional cousin, disappointment. "You know," she said quietly, "I offered because I thought you were sensible. Sensible people survive here. I've watched you be sensible for six years, and I confess I'd grown fond of it." She settled her coat around her shoulders. "This is the least sensible thing you've ever done, Sandoval. Do you understand what you've just purchased? By nightfall there will be watchers on this street. By tomorrow, writs. You've traded a quiet life for a hunted one, over a child you met yesterday."

"Yes," I said. "That about sums it up."

She reached the door, and there she stopped, one hand on the frame, and did not look back at me when she spoke next, which somehow made it worse.

"For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I hope the boy is worth it." A beat. "And Hazel? The Regent's inquisitors are not like me. I suspect. They *find*. Pray you learn the difference somewhere other than a cell."

The bell above my door gave its small dishonest chime, and she was gone.

* * *

I stood in the empty shop for the length of ten heartbeats, doing the arithmetic I should have done before opening my mouth. Watchers on the street by nightfall. Writs by tomorrow. An inquisitor who had known everything for four years and needed only a reason — and I had handed her one gift-wrapped, with a bow made of my own stubborn tongue.

"Well," I said to the room. "That went well."

Isandro appeared in the doorway of the back room with my atlas of the Sunken Marches held open upside down. He looked at my face and then at the door, and whatever children's instincts are made of — the ones adults sand away — his read the room perfectly.

"She wanted to take me," he said. Not a question. In the First Tongue it would have been a statement of weather.

"Yes."

"You said no."

"Yes."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then closed the book and held it out to me, right side up, which I chose to interpret as forgiveness. "They will come fast now," he said. "Things that are refused come back with teeth."

"Thank you, yes, I had gathered." I took the atlas and set it down. My hands were not entirely steady, which annoyed me. "Isandro. Last night you asked me to help you reach the palace. I said I'd think about it. I'd like to amend my answer."

He waited.

"I don't think particularly well under threat, and I make terrible decisions quickly, and I have just proven both. So we're leaving. Tonight, before the watchers arrive. Which means I need to find someone who can get us into the palace, and there is exactly one person alive who owes me enough and knows enough, and I have spent three years hoping I would never have to knock on her door." I pulled my travel cloak from behind the counter, blowing dust off it, feeling three years of careful smallness crumble off my shoulders like old plaster.

"Who?" Isandro asked.

I looked at the door Maren Vale had just left through, and past it, across the city, toward the Spirecart district where the towers stood like fingers pressed to lips.

"My mentor," I said. "The woman who taught me to draw doors. Odile Ferran."

Isandro went very still, in the way he did sometimes, as though listening to something underneath the world. When he spoke, his voice had that strange doubled quality, as if the words were arriving slightly ahead of him.

"The one who helped build the crown," he said.

I stared at him. "What did you just say?"

But he only tilted his head, mud-haired and solemn, and looked at me with those too-old eyes, and said nothing at all — which, I was beginning to learn, was its own kind of answer.
