## 5. The Mentor in the Tower

There are three people in Vessengard who know what I used to be. One is dead — I attended the funeral under a false name and wept into a borrowed handkerchief. The second is me, and I have spent three years trying to drown her beneath charm-seller's patter and cheap dye. The third is Odile Ferran.

So when a child speaking the First Tongue shows up at my door with a story about hollow kings, there is only one person left to ask.

The problem with asking Odile Ferran anything is that she answers.

* * *

The Cartographers' Guild tower still stands in the Scholar's Quarter, though the guild itself was dissolved eleven years ago, its instruments confiscated, its maps burned in the square below while the city watched and applauded. Burning maps. That's what passes for triumph in Vessengard. They cheered as the knowledge of a hundred realms went up in smoke, because the Crown told them magic was a disease and they were grateful to be cured of wonder.

I stood in that crowd too. I want that on the record. I did not throw my own work onto the pyre — Odile did that for both of us, feeding our atlas of the Sunken Reaches into the flames page by page, her face perfectly composed — but I stood there and I let it happen, and afterward I went home and shrank what remained of myself into trinkets small enough to palm.

The tower had been sealed since. Sealed things in Vessengard get repurposed, not demolished; the Crown has an instinct for real estate the way sharks have an instinct for blood. So it didn't surprise me, climbing the quarter's crooked lanes with Isandro's hand tucked in mine, to see light burning on the top floor. What surprised me was the banner above the door: gray silk, unmarked, the color of the Inquisition.

Isandro stopped walking. He does that sometimes — plants his feet like a tree deciding it lives here now — and looks at things until they explain themselves.

"That's where she is," he said. Not asked. Said, in that flat, certain way of his, the way he'd said *the king wears something wrong* two nights ago over cold soup.

"You don't know that."

"I know doors." He looked up at me. "She's behind this one."

I could have argued. I've talked my way past customs officers, inquisitors, and one memorable river spirit with a grudge against surveyors. But Isandro has this terrible gift: he makes lying feel like a waste of good breath. So I said nothing, and we climbed.

* * *

The guildhall's great map room had been my cathedral once. Forty feet of vaulted ceiling, windows of colored glass depicting the nine known realms as saints, and the Floor — the great living projection table where master cartographers walked their drafts into being, their boots leaving glowing lines across the stone like signatures.

They'd paved over the Floor. Gray flagstone, mortared down, ugly as a closed eye. In its place sat rows of desks, and at the desks sat clerks copying out inventories of confiscated goods, and at the head of the room, where the Floor's heart had been, stood a woman in gray reading a dispatch by lamplight.

Gray suited her. That was the worst part. Odile Ferran had always dressed like weather about to happen — storm-colored coats, silver at the cuffs — and the inquisitor's uniform was just that same palette, disciplined. Her hair was iron now instead of black. She hadn't bothered to disguise herself when she heard the door; she simply turned, and I watched recognition move across her face like frost across a window.

"Hazel." My old name, spoken plainly, in a room full of clerks. Kind of her. It took them perhaps four seconds to stop pretending to work.

"Master Ferran," I said, because if she got to use mine, I got to use hers, and hers was a knife with a handle she'd handed me years ago. "Lovely office. Terrible carpet."

"Walk with me." She set down the dispatch. To the clerks: "Out." Then, softer, eyes flicking to Isandro: "Both of you."

We climbed the spiral stair to her private study, which had been her private study back when it was a cartographer's study too, and the sameness of it hurt more than the changes would have. Same astrolabe in the corner, seized and re-seized, its brass gone dull. Same lopsided globe of the Reach, missing its southern seas because Odile always said the south was a rumor and rumors didn't belong on maps. Same tea service, chipped on the same side of the same cup.

She poured three cups without asking. Isandro took his in both hands and drank like it was a duty assigned to him.

"You're hard to find," Odile said to him.

"I'm not hiding."

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't be. You'd be *arriving*." And then she looked at me, and everything in her face that had been an inquisitor folded itself away, and what was left was my teacher, seventy years old and tired as a long road. "You've seen the king."

It wasn't a question either. We were a matched pair of terrible questions tonight.

"Through your student's eyes," I said. "Something wearing Aldric like a coat. Something that eats magic and calls the eating law." I set down my untouched cup. "And you knew. Don't tell me you didn't. You heard 'hollow king' in my voice and poured tea."

Odile sat down slowly, in the chair she'd taught from, hands flat on her knees the way she used to do before explaining why my draft of the Veil Coast was garbage.

"I didn't just know, Hazel. I helped build it."

* * *

Here is what they never put in the histories, mostly because the histories are written by people who burn maps:

Forty years ago, the realm-wars were ending the world. Not dramatically — worse than dramatically. Slowly. Every border skirmish between the Nine Realms was fought with bound magic, and every binding leaked, and the leaks were eating the land itself. I was a child then; I remember the refugee columns passing through Vessengard, and I remember thinking the adults all smelled of ash. Odile remembers more. Odile was already the finest cartographer of her generation, and cartographers see what kings won't: that the wars weren't over territory. Territory is a line on paper. The wars were over *doors* — who controlled the passages between realms, who could move armies through the folds of the world.

"There was a faction," she said. "Cartographers, mostly. People who understood the architecture of things. We called ourselves the Edgewalkers, which tells you how young we were, because we thought naming ourselves something grand was the same as being right." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "We found it in the space between realms. A thing that had no shape of its own. A hunger, old as the gaps we charted. And it spoke to us — or rather, it listened, and we mistook listening for agreement."

"It offered peace," Isandro said quietly. His cup was empty. He held it anyway.

"It offered an end to the wars," Odile corrected, with the precision she'd beaten into me over ten years of apprenticeship. "Those aren't the same sentence, child, whatever the priests tell you. We were drowning. It threw us a rope. All it asked was a body. A crown. A kingdom to wear." She looked at her hands. "Aldric's father was dying. Aldric was nineteen and already famous for wanting the wars over more than he wanted anything alive. We thought — gods forgive us, we *thought* — that we could bind it tightly enough. That a crown could be a cage. That we'd built the cage well enough."

"And the price?" I asked, though I already knew. You always know the price after you've paid it. "What did it eat to keep its cage?"

"The magic around it," Odile said. "Slowly at first. Then faster, as it grew comfortable. Every working within its borders fed it. So we outlawed magic. Formally, publicly, with bonfires and edicts. We told ourselves we were protecting people." She finally raised her eyes to mine. "We were setting the table."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a clerk coughed.

I want to say I raged. I want to report that I shouted, that I called her traitor, that loyalty cracked like ice underfoot and I went through it cleanly. But that's not what happened. What happened was smaller and much worse: I thought of every charm I'd ever sold, every wonder I'd shrunk into a pocket-sized lie, every night I'd spent grateful the Crown's ban gave me cover to hide — and I realized I'd been grateful to the cage. Comfortable in it. Fed by the same table.

"You taught me that maps should tell the truth," I said finally. "First lesson. Before scale, before symbols. *The map serves the territory, never the reverse.*"

"I know what I taught you."

"Then how?"

"How does anyone?" She spread her hands. "You draw one line that isn't true, because the truth is unbearable, and then you spend forty years defending that line. I built a cage around a hunger and called it peace, and when it started eating, I burned the maps that proved it, and when students came asking questions, I became the thing that asks them instead." She touched the gray sleeve, lightly, like touching a scar. "The Inquisition found me useful. I found being useful easier than being honest. There. Now you know the whole of it, and you may hate me properly."

Isandro set down his cup. It made no sound against the desk, and somehow that was louder than shouting.

"She's sorry," he said to me, in the First Tongue, and I understood it — understood it the way I understand north, the way I understand the pull of a door — "but sorry is a map of the wound, not the closing of it."

"Yes," I said aloud, in plain Vessengard speech, so she'd hear. "That's exactly it."

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them, the teacher was gone again and the inquisitor was back, and I understood that the inquisitor was the armor she wore so the teacher could survive at all.

"If you go to the palace," she said, "you'll die. If you reach the throne room, you'll fail. And if, by some miracle of stubbornness, you succeed — if you unmake the Regent — you will unmake the peace with it. Forty years of no wars. Gone. Every realm that kept its borders because the Regent's hunger frightened them into treaties — gone. Do you understand what stands on the other side of that door? The wars come back. The leaks come back. The world ends the way it was always going to end, only now it will remember having been saved once, and that memory will make the despair worse."

"Then we'll need new maps," I said.

"Hazel—"

"No." I stood. My legs were steadier than I deserved. "You don't get to finish that sentence. You had forty years to finish it and you spent them paving over the Floor." I took Isandro's hand. "I'm going to draw the truth, Odile. Even if it costs the whole page."

For a moment — one moment — something moved behind her eyes that might have been pride, if pride were allowed to live in a person wearing gray.

Then she rang the little bell on her desk, and the door opened, and four soldiers came in, and she said, with terrible gentleness:

"I'm sorry, Hazel. But you've just confessed to treason in front of witnesses, and I am *still*, despite everything, trying to save your life. Take them downstairs."

* * *

They put us in a cell in the tower's basement, which was, in another life, the archive where apprentice cartographers slept among the rolled charts. I know because I slept there for ten years. The iron brackets that once held map-scrolls still stud the walls. Someone has bolted manacles to one of them.

Isandro sat cross-legged on the floor, entirely calm, watching the torchlight through the barred door.

"They're coming," he said.

"Who? More soldiers?"

He shook his head slowly. "The thing under the crown. It felt me today. Through her." He looked up, and his eyes in the torchlight were very old and very young at once. "It knows its key is loose. It's sending something to collect me before the Rite."

"When?"

He considered the question with the seriousness he gave all questions.

"Soon," he said. "But doors open both ways. And Hazel—" he tapped the floor beneath us, twice, the way you knock on a friend's door, "—there's a map under this tower. An old one. Sleeping. Can you feel it?"

I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone, and far, far below — deeper than cells, deeper than foundations — something answered.
