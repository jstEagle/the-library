## 6. Down Through the Undercity

The soldiers came through the market at dusk, and I learned something useful about fear: it makes an excellent cartographer.

One moment Isandro and I were haggling over a meat pie — he'd insisted on paying with a button, and the pie-seller had, inexplicably, accepted — and the next, the crowd was bending around six gray coats like water around stones. Odile's colors. Odile's training. Moving toward my stall with the unhurried certainty of people who already know how the story ends.

"This way," I said, which was a lie, because *this way* led directly past them.

Isandro took my hand anyway. He had a child's genius for trusting the wrong directions from the right person.

We cut down Tanner's Row, through the tannery itself — apologies to my future self for whatever that smell has done to my coat — and out the sluice-gate where the city drains its sins into the river. Behind us, boots on wet cobbles. Not running yet. Running would come later, when they found the charm I'd left ticking on my doorframe: a little thing that whispered *nothing to see here* in a voice like a bored clerk. It wouldn't hold six trained inquisitors long. It might hold them ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds is a country. You can cross a lot of it if you don't look back.

"The water goes down," Isandro said.

"Yes."

"People live where the water went?"

"Some people," I said. "The water kept the better real estate."

He laughed — that startling, unguarded laugh of his, as if we were on an adventure instead of fleeing arrest by an institution that burns people. Then he said, in the First Tongue, a word I felt in my teeth before I understood it in my head. *Down-home.* The old word for shelter dug beneath disaster.

I hadn't heard it in thirty years. I had charted the deltas where it was born, back when mapping a realm meant walking into it and letting it change you. Back when I still believed maps were promises instead of confessions.

The sluice-gate grate was rusted open — of course it was; Vessengard forgets its doors the way it forgets its laws' original reasons — and beyond it the stairs went down into the drowned quarter, the undercity the Crown pretends is a drainage problem. Officially, nobody lives there. Unofficially, the official version is what keeps them alive.

I lit no lamp. There are stairs your feet remember when your mind is busy screaming, and mine had walked these twice before, once coming and once going, in another life with another name.

Halfway down, the boots above started running.

"Hazel," Isandro said calmly, "they are making the fast kind of noise."

"I noticed."

"What do we do?"

I looked at him, mud-streaked and solemn, holding a button he'd been given change for, and I did the arithmetic every coward does eventually: the stair behind us, the dark ahead, and one small hand in mine that the whole gray machine of the Crown wanted very badly.

"We go where they can't follow," I said. "And then we find out who still remembers me down there."

* * *

The undercity does not have streets. It has suggestions.

Ceilings drip in counterpoint. Walls wear centuries of flood-marks like tree rings. And everywhere, tucked into arches and drowned doorways, the exile communities burn their little heretical lights: a shrine where someone tends a flame that bends toward prayers instead of drafts; a laundry where sheets dry impossibly fast because the woman hanging them remembers a word for wind; children playing a hopping game whose chalk squares rearrange when adults aren't looking.

Magic outlaws ourselves, all of us, keeping wonder alive the way I kept charms alive — shrunk down, disguised, deniable.

They saw us coming before we saw them. Of course they did. A stranger descending the Stair of Suggestions with a First Tongue child in tow is not a subtle arrival.

They stepped out of the walls the way undercity people do — six of them, seven, no weapons visible, which in the undercity means every weapon imaginable. An old woman with river-clay packed under her nails. A man built like a dock crane. A girl of maybe fifteen holding a lantern that burned green, which is either witchcraft or chemistry, and in Vessengard both are illegal.

"No gray coats come down here," the old woman said. "No gray coats' friends either."

"I'm not their friend," I said. "I'm their problem."

"That's what a friend would say."

Fair. I raised my hands, slow. "My name is Hazel Sandoval. I used to be a cartographer. Before the Prohibition, I mapped—" I stopped. Old habit, the stopping. "I mapped places. Real ones. And this child came to my door speaking the First Tongue, and men in gray want him, and I need passage deeper than you're comfortable giving."

Silence, except the drip.

Then Isandro stepped forward — forward, always forward, the boy had no reverse gear — and spoke to the old woman in the language older than kingdoms.

I will render it as best I can, though rendering it is like netting smoke. He said: *We are running from the ones who wear the king. The water remembers the way down. Please, grandmother-of-the-water, we are small and the night is large.*

The old woman made a sound I have only heard once since — a kind of keening, low, involuntary, the sound of a bone setting. She dropped to her knees on wet stone without caring about the wet stone.

"It's true," she whispered. "Saints preserve the discarded, it's *true*. The tongue of making. I thought it was gone. I thought they'd burned it out of every mouth." She looked up at Isandro with wet eyes and terror and joy fighting openly on her face. "Child, do you know what you're saying?"

Isandro considered this with terrible seriousness. "I know what I'm saying," he said. "I don't always know what it means. Hazel knows some of it. Hazel's mouth is rusty."

"My mouth is *fine*," I muttered.

"He speaks it like a foreigner," she told the others, delighted, wiping her face with clay-cracked hands. "Like a pilgrim. But he speaks it." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and gripped my wrist hard enough to leave marks. "Cartographer. Do you know what you've brought us?"

"A great deal of trouble," I said. "It's my chief export."

"You've brought us the key."

* * *

Isandro:

The grandmother-of-the-water took us down further, where the river still lived under the city like a sleeping animal. There were candles made of fat that burned underwater, which Hazel says is impossible and also held my hand tighter, so I think Hazel was scared of the beautiful thing.

There was a priest there. He was old the way trees are old, bent but not broken, sitting in a boat that had not floated in years. His robe had fish embroidered on it, badly, by someone who loved him more than they could sew.

Hazel said, "This is a bad idea," which is what Hazel says before good ideas.

The priest asked my name in the First Tongue. Nobody asks my name in the First Tongue. In Vessengard everyone speaks the gray language, the locked language, the one where words sit in boxes. But the priest's words moved like water moves, and when I answered him — *I am Isandro, sent up from beneath the crown, to find the one who draws doors* — he began to cry.

He cried the way rain starts. Slowly, then all at once.

"Do you know what he said?" the priest asked Hazel, in the gray language, because crying people need plain words.

"Roughly," Hazel said, which was a lie, because Hazel's ears had gone white at the edges, the way they do when the First Tongue gets loud.

"He said he was sent from beneath the crown." The priest wiped his face. "Grandmother, do you know what lies beneath the crown? Nothing. That is what lies beneath the crown. The Hollow. We sang about it, before singing was a crime. A place where a kingdom used to be, until something ate it to make room for itself." He turned back to me, and his eyes were kind and frightened in equal measure, which I am learning is what truth looks like on adult faces. "Messenger, messenger. No. No, child. A messenger carries words. Words can be stopped at any door. You are not carrying the way to unmake the binding."

He touched my forehead, gently, between the eyes.

"You *are* the way. You are the key. The First Tongue doesn't describe the lock, boy. It *is* the lock. Every word you speak is a tooth of it. That is why the Regent hunts you, and that is why the Regent cannot simply kill you — because a key thrown away is a key that might be found again, but a key kept close can be melted into a new crown."

Everyone was quiet. Even the dripping got quieter, or I stopped hearing it.

Hazel crouched down next to me. Hazel's face was doing several things at once, which is normal for Hazel, but underneath all of them was one big thing, and the big thing was afraid *for* me, which is different from being afraid of me. Most grown-ups are afraid of me now. Hazel is afraid for me. I have decided this is my favorite thing about Hazel, even counting the meat pies.

"Isandro," Hazel said quietly. "Did you know this? What you are?"

I thought about it honestly, because lying to Hazel feels like drawing on a map wrong.

"I knew I was sent," I said. "Sent things are letters. Letters get read and then put in drawers." I shrugged. "But the priest says I'm a key. Keys get *used*. Doors open. That's better than a drawer."

Hazel made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost the other thing.

Then the priest stood up in his boat that couldn't float, and his voice changed — it went deep and ringing, the way bells sound underwater, and I understood that he was praying in the First Tongue, the first prayer anyone had spoken in it in thirty years, right there among the candles and the drowned doors and the discarded people.

He prayed: *Maker-of-edges, the key has come home by the water road. Give us one night of courage. Tomorrow they will come with fire, because they always come with fire.*

Hazel translated none of it for me. Hazel didn't need to. Some words you feel in your teeth.

Above us, far away, through fathoms of stone, I heard the fast kind of boots start again.

"They found the sluice-gate," Hazel said, standing, hand already finding mine. "Priest. How many ways out of here?"

"One," the priest said, smiling through the last of his tears, "that they don't know about. It goes up, cartographer. Everything worth reaching goes up."

Hazel sighed the sigh of a person whose night has decided to keep going.

"Naturally," Hazel said. "Up. Toward the palace. Toward the crown. Toward the *thing wearing the king*." A pause. "Lead on, then. And somebody give the boy a real coin — he's been trying to buy our salvation with buttons."
