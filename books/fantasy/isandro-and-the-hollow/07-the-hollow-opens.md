## 7. The Hollow Opens

The river-priest said my tongue was the language of making. He wept when he said it. Grown-ups cry a lot around me lately, and I have decided this is not my fault.

I want to say something about sleep first, because sleep is where it happened, and I think you should know that I did not invite it. Sleep is a door too. Hazel says doors are things you draw, and I am a thing that was drawn, and so maybe I should have known better than to close my eyes in a city with a king-shaped hole in it. But I was tired. Tired is a country I visit more often than I would like, and its borders are badly guarded.

We slept in the undercity, on a pallet that smelled of river and candlewax, under a ceiling painted with fish that had never swum anywhere. Hazel was beside me. Hazel breathes like someone counting. In-out-in-out, like the world might stop if they stopped keeping tally. I fell asleep listening to that. It was a good way to fall asleep. I mention this because afterward I was angry at myself for having enjoyed it, and anger needs somewhere to go, so it goes into sentences.

* * *

Here is what the dream was like.

It began as a market. Not Vessengard's market — a bigger one, a market made of everything anyone had ever wanted. Stalls without sellers. Bells without bells in them. I walked down the middle aisle and every stall knew my name, which is a rude thing for a stall to do.

Then the stalls were gone, and there was only the aisle, and then there was only the walking, and then there was nothing left to walk on except the fact of walking itself, which sounds impossible but is very easy once you stop being a person and start being an appetite.

I know that sounds like I am describing myself. I am describing the other one. Please keep up.

The Hollow does not look like anything. That is the first thing, and the most important thing, and the thing nobody will believe until they see it. People expect a monster to have teeth. Teeth are edges. The Hollow has no edges. The Hollow is what is left after you take everything away and then take the away away too.

But it showed me what used to be there. That is how it hunts. It shows you the shape of the absence by filling it with memory, the way a dead man's coat still holds his shoulders.

So: I saw a realm.

I saw a realm called — it did not have a name I can carry in my mouth; names are small doors and this place was a cathedral — but I saw it. Rivers that ran uphill out of politeness. Mountains that migrated, slowly, seasonally, like enormous gray birds. A sky with weather you could negotiate with. There were people. There were so many people. They built their houses out of sung stone, and the songs were different in every house, and when two houses loved each other they taught each other the songs, and the children grew up bilingual in masonry.

I saw all of this, and I understood, with the terrible clarity dreams give you, that none of it existed anymore. It was not destroyed. Destroyed leaves ruins. Ruins are something. This was *unmade*. There was no rubble because rubble takes up space and space was part of what got eaten.

And then the dream turned around and looked at me.

You want to know what the Regent looks like. Everyone does. Here is the answer, and I am sorry for it: the Regent looks like whatever is missing from you. For the people of Vessengard, it looks like a king, because they are missing a king who is actually alive. For Hazel, I think it would look like a map with all the old countries still on it. For me —

For me it looked like home. My home. The Hollow. Because I am from the Hollow, or near it, or I am the part of it that got sent out with a job to do, and the Regent knows that, and it wore my own missing-ness at me like a face.

"You came back," it said, in my own voice, which is cheating.

"I didn't," I said. "I'm going back. Different direction."

"Everything goes back," it said. "That is what hunger is. Hunger is just remembering forward."

I did not understand that sentence then. I understand it now, which is worse.

It showed me the crown. Not the crown Aldric wears — the crown underneath, the true one, the one that was forged before the gold one, out of the last unbroken piece of the eaten realm. A ring of nothing, set with stones that were once mountains. And it showed me how it feeds, and this is the part I will tell you plainly because someone has to:

Magic is not outlawed in Vessengard because the Regent fears magic. Magic is outlawed because outlawed magic *leaks*. Every charm crushed under an inquisitor's boot, every spell smothered in its cradle, every child told their gift is a crime — all of that power has to go somewhere, and the Regent built the law to be a funnel. Three hundred years of forbidden wonders, poured down into one hungry ring. The prohibition is not a wall. It is a mouth.

I asked it — I remember asking, I remember my dream-mouth moving — "Why did you show me your own kingdom? Why show me what you ate?"

And it said, "Because you are the last bite."

* * *

I woke up screaming.

I want to be honest about the screaming. It was not brave screaming, the kind heroes do to warn people. It was small and wet and it came out of me in the First Tongue, because the First Tongue is the language of making and I had just spent a whole night inside the opposite of making, and languages come out the way they went in.

Hazel was up before I finished the first word. I heard them hit the floor — both feet, no hesitation, a cartographer's reflexes, always ready to survey new terrain — and then their hands were on my shoulders and they were saying my name, my market-name, the plain one, the safe one, over and over like a charm.

Which is funny, if you think about it. Which I did not, at the time. At the time I was busy burning them.

Because here is what Hazel will not say yet, and I will say it now while it is still my secret to give: my words hurt them. I could feel it happening. Every syllable of the First Tongue that came out of me landed on Hazel like a hot coal, and Hazel held on anyway, and did not let go, and did not even flinch in a way they thought I could see. But I could see. I see everything lately. Being a key means all the locks look at you.

"Hazel," I said, in the plain tongue, forcing it, dragging each word across the border. "Don't touch my mouth. Whatever comes out. Don't listen with your whole self. Listen with half."

"That's not how listening works," Hazel said, wry even at three in the morning with a burning child in their arms, because Hazel would make jokes during the end of the world and probably bill someone for it.

"It is how listening works tonight."

They listened with half. Good. Half was enough for them to hear the important part, which I told them while my hands were still shaking: the Rite. The Binding Rite. It is sooner than we thought. The Regent showed me its calendar the way a host shows off a garden. Coronation eve is not a ceremony. It is a feeding. When the Rite finishes, the door I am closes forever, and the key melts into the lock, and Vessengard becomes a farm. A quiet, lawful, well-fed farm.

"The throne room," Hazel said. Their face did the thing faces do when they are doing arithmetic they hate. "We need a way in that doesn't exist."

"Yes," I said.

"And the only person who makes ways in that don't exist is me," Hazel said, "and the price of that is —"

"Don't say the price," I said. "Prices get heavier when you say them out loud. That's not magic. That's just true."

Hazel almost smiled. Almost smiles are Hazel's native currency. Then they looked at me properly, and their eyes went to my mouth, and I watched them decide not to ask the question, and then ask it anyway, because Hazel's curiosity has never once lost a fight.

"What did it show you?"

And I thought about lying. I am good at lying; I learned from watching smugglers hide charms in loaves of bread. But the First Tongue does not like lies — it is the language of making, and a lie is a small unmaking, and my mouth has been arguing with me ever since.

So I told the truth, in the plain tongue, as gently as I could:

"It showed me a whole world that isn't there anymore. And then it showed me the space where it used to be. And then it said I'm dessert."

Hazel went very still. Stillness, in Hazel, is loud.

"Isandro," they said. "When you speak that language now — does it hurt you, or does it hurt the room?"

I considered this. Honest answer: both. The words come out of me already lit.

"Ask me again after we win," I said.

Outside, far above us, through forty feet of drowned stone and older dark, the palace bells began to ring. Not the hour. Not the alarm. The Rite-bells, the ones the undercity folk call the dinner bell, though they don't laugh when they say it anymore.

Coronation eve had begun.

Hazel stood up, and I watched them reach — without thinking, without looking — for the satchel where they kept their ink and their chalk and the rolled blank paper they had been not-looking-at for three days, the way you don't look at a debt.

"Get some water," Hazel said. "Eat something. Tomorrow I'm going to draw us a door into hell, and I'll need you wide awake to say the words."

"And if the words eat me?" I asked.

Hazel paused at the ladder down to the river-priest's chapel. In the candlelight their face was all sharp planes and bad plans.

"Then I'll draw you a way back out," they said. "I've mapped places that didn't exist. One boy won't be hard."

It was a lie. We both knew it was a lie. It was also the kindest thing anyone had said to me since the Hollow sent me out, and I have decided that kindness does not have to be true to work. Sometimes it just has to be drawn well enough to walk on.

I drank the water. I ate the bread. Somewhere above us, the bells kept ringing, and beneath the bells, beneath the city, beneath everything, something with no edges waited for its last bite to walk willingly into its mouth.

I picked up my shoes and followed Hazel toward the drawing.
