## 9. The Unbinding

The map began to eat me at the palace gates.

I want to be precise about that, because later — when I had nothing left but the shape of having lost something — precision was all I could hold onto. A living map does not consume you all at once, the way fire consumes a letter. It takes you in the order you drew yourself in. The first thing to go was the memory of drawing it.

I had drawn the palace of Vessengard a hundred times in my old life: its seven towers, its drowned foundations, the throne room set like a heart at the center of the body of the building. But those were dead maps, honest maps, maps that described a place without daring to *be* it. Tonight I drew it alive, on vellum made from my own oath — Odile's word for it, and she would know — and every line I laid down became a corridor I could walk, and every corridor I walked took back a piece of me as toll.

The gates were the first cost. I remembered learning to draw battlements as a girl, my tongue between my teeth, Odile laughing over my shoulder. The memory went into the ink like salt into water. Gone. I felt the hole where it had been, smooth-edged, already healing over with nothing.

"Does it hurt?" Isandro asked, walking beside me with his hand in mine. He had asked this four times now. He always asked the same way, as though pain were a weather he needed reported accurately.

"Not yet," I said.

"That is not true," he said. "You are limping inside."

Leave it to a child from the Hollow to see through skin. "Then yes," I said. "It hurts."

"Good," said Isandro, with the terrible simplicity of the First Tongue behind him. "Things that are given away should cost. Otherwise they are not given. They are dropped."

We came up out of the sewers through a grate beneath the aviary, because even a living map has to start somewhere real, and the real Vessengard smelled of pigeon filth and tallow and, underneath everything, the cold mineral breath of the Hollow leaking up through the stones. Above us, the palace blazed with coronation-eve light. Every window gold. Every bell getting ready to lie about joy.

Inside, they were singing the Binding Rite.

* * *

Isandro

The singing is wrong. That is the first thing I think, standing under the stairs while Hazel counts guards. The song is a door-song. My people sang door-songs when we closed things, and we sang them sad, because closing is a kind of small death and you should feel it. These singers do not feel it. They sing it happy, fast, like a market tune. They do not know what they are singing. They only know the crown told them to.

Hazel says, "Two guards at the great doors, four in the gallery, and Maren Vale somewhere being inevitable." Hazel talks like that when scared. Words stacked high so no one can see over them into the fear.

"The Regent knows we are here," I say.

"I know."

"It stopped pretending an hour ago. It is listening through the king's ears. Through the walls' ears. It heard us come out of the river." I touch the wall. The stone remembers us. Stone always remembers; it just doesn't usually get asked. "It wants us to come. A key is only useful if it is put in the lock."

Hazel looks down at me. Hazel's face does the thing where it tries to be brave and mostly manages to be tired, which is braver, if you ask me, which nobody does, because children aren't asked things here. In the Hollow children were asked everything. There wasn't anyone else left to ask.

"You don't have to do this," Hazel says. "I could still find another way."

"No," I say. "You couldn't. And also that is a lie you are saying to be kind, which is a strange custom. Kindness made of lies." I squeeze Hazel's hand. "When I speak, you will hear the words trying to take me apart. Hold on to the part of me that is only a boy. The rest of me will be busy."

"What part is only a boy?"

"The part that is hungry," I say. "And scared. And wants to go home, except home is gone, so I suppose the part that wants a new one." I consider. "Also the part that likes honey bread. Guard the honey-bread part especially. If I lose that, I have lost more than the Regent can ever give back."

Hazel laughs. It comes out broken in the middle, but it comes out. "Honey bread," Hazel agrees. "Non-negotiable."

Above us, the singing swells toward its ending. Doors open ahead, unbarred, waiting, exactly as promised.

* * *

Hazel

The throne room of Vessengard was built to make you feel small before you'd done anything to deserve it. Vaulted ceiling painted with a sky that never rained. Banners of every conquered realm hanging like flayed skins. And at the far end, on a dais of white stone, King Aldric — what was left of him — kneeling before his own crown while a hundred robed voices finished the Rite around him.

He looked worse than the vision had shown me. The vision had shown empty eyes. This was emptier. Aldric knelt the way furniture stands: present, purposeful, unoccupied. His mouth moved with the hymn. Nothing behind it did.

And above him, coiled through the crown like smoke poured into a glass, the Regent.

I had expected a monster. Monsters are easy; I've mapped monsters. What I saw instead was *absence* wearing majesty — a shape defined entirely by what wasn't there, the way a footprint is defined by the foot that left. It had eaten a realm once. It had eaten a king slowly, over decades, and called the eating law and order and peace.

Maren Vale stood at the foot of the dais, gray-robed, sword sheathed, hands folded. She saw us enter — of course she did; the doors had been opened for us — and she didn't reach for her weapon. She reached for her chains of office instead, and lifted them over her head, and set them down on the floor.

"Get out," she said quietly. "That's the last kindness I have in me, Sandoval. Take the child and go, and in twenty years tell yourself a story about why."

"The Rite finishes in minutes," I said. "After that, Aldric isn't a man anymore. He's a glove."

"He's been a glove for thirty years and the realm has known peace for thirty years." Her voice cracked on *peace*, just slightly, and I understood — she believed it. She had built her whole gray life on it. "You'll plunge us back into the realm-wars for one man's soul?"

"One man's soul," Isandro said, stepping forward, "is worth every war it costs. That is what your songs used to say, before you forgot them. I read it in the stones."

Vale looked at the child for a long moment. Then she stepped aside. I will never know if it was mercy or arithmetic or simply that some part of her had been waiting thirty years for someone to make the choice she couldn't. It doesn't matter now. She stepped aside.

The Regent turned its attention on us, and the temperature of the room dropped, and every candle flame leaned toward the dais like grass in wind.

*You brought my key,* it said, in a voice that wasn't sound but subtraction — each word taking something away from the air. *How thoughtful. How very like a cartographer. You cannot resist delivering things to their proper places.*

Isandro raised his chin. "I am not yours."

*Everything is mine,* said the Regent, with the calm of a thing that had never once been contradicted successfully. *I ate a kingdom to wear this crown. Do you imagine a boy frightens me?*

"Yes," said Isandro, pleasantly. "Or you wouldn't have outlawed magic. Hungry things bar their doors."

And then he spoke.

* * *

Isandro

The words come up from somewhere below my ribs, below my feet, below the world. The First Tongue is not learned. It is remembered by the body, the way the body remembers falling. I open my mouth and the language opens me.

*"Aldric-of-the-broken-name. Come out of the borrowed house. The house is not yours. The name is not yours. The hunger is not welcome."*

The Regent screams. Not with a mouth. With everything.

It tears at me. I feel it pulling — my memories first, then my names, then the honey-bread part, oh no, the honey-bread part — and then there are hands on my shoulders, warm, human, shaking, and Hazel's voice saying words I half understand: *"I've got you. I've got the boy. Whatever else you are right now, the boy is held. Speak."*

So I speak.

* * *

Hazel

Anchoring Isandro was like holding a kite string in a hurricane, except the kite was a child and the hurricane was the birth-language of reality. Every word he spoke rearranged the room. The banners unraveled into thread. The painted sky rippled like actual weather. And the Regent came out of Aldric the way a splinter comes out — screaming, reluctant, leaving the wound open and clean.

Aldric collapsed. An old man, gray-faced, breathing. Alive. Weeping without knowing why. I allowed myself half a second to feel that, and then the Regent gathered itself in the air above the throne and turned all of its vast attention on me.

*Cartographer,* it said. Almost gently. That was the worst part. It had learned gentleness the way it learned everything: by consuming it. *I know what you have already paid tonight. I know what the map is still eating. Stop drawing, and I will return it all. Every realm you charted. Every shore you walked. Your mentor's laugh, your mother's kitchen, the first map you ever loved. I keep what I take, cartographer. I am the only thing in creation that keeps what it takes.*

My hand was full of pen. The living map lay across the throne room floor, half-finished, hungry, waiting for its last edges.

And here is the truth I spoke plainly, because it was the only coin I had left: "No."

*Why?* Genuine puzzlement. Hunger cannot comprehend refusal; it can only price it. *Name your reason.*

"Because a map without edges isn't a map," I said. "It's a hunger. I've spent three years selling charms in a city that forbids wonder, and I know exactly what you are now. You're what's left when something refuses to end. Everything needs borders, Regent. Even grief. Especially you."

I knelt and drew the final line.

The map took the rest. I felt it go in installments — the realms first, the Ashwind Coast, the Ninefold Delta, the sky-roads of wherever-I-can-no-longer-name — then the years, then the names, then the ache of missing them, which was somehow the last thing to leave and the hardest to surrender. I drew the Regent's edge with a hand that no longer remembered why it was crying.

The Regent, given boundaries at last, discovered it had an inside. It fell into itself like water finding a basin. The map drank it down, rolled itself closed with a sound like a book shutting, and was ash before it hit the floor.

Silence. Real silence, the kind with edges.

Isandro let go of my hand, walked to the fallen king, and sat beside him, and did the most ordinary thing in the world: he waited for the old man to wake up.

"Hazel?" the child said, after a while. "Your eyes are doing the thing where they look for something that isn't there anymore."

"Yeah," I said. "They do that."

"Do you remember the Hollow?"

"No."

"Do you remember me?"

I looked at this mud-streaked child who had walked into my shop out of nowhere, whom I had apparently crossed a burning city and unmade a god to protect, and found — nothing. A blank page where our whole story should have been.

"No," I said honestly. "But I'm told I liked honey bread."

Isandro smiled, wet-eyed and certain. "Then we begin there," he said. "Once upon a time, there was a door drawn in chalk—"

And outside, for the first time in thirty years, dawn came up over Vessengard without asking anyone's permission.
