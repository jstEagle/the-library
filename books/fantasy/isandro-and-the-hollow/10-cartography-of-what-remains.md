## 10. Cartography of What Remains

The trouble with giving away every memory you own is that afterward, people keep handing them back to you like coins they found in your coat.

"You drew the Saltmarsh Reaches," a woman told me in the market, three weeks after the Rite, gripping my sleeve with the fervor of the recently converted. "You mapped it when no one else would go past the drowned causeway. My grandmother had your chart hanging above her hearth."

"Did she," I said, and smiled the smile I'd spent three years perfecting for customers who wanted charms that didn't work. "I'm afraid I mostly draw trinkets now."

She looked at me the way people look at a house that's burned down — grief and fascination in equal measure — and I went home and put my head on the table for a while.

That was the arithmetic of it, the part nobody warns you about when you're trading your past to close a door on a hungry god. You don't just lose the realms. You lose the *having been there*. Somewhere in my skull there used to be a library of everything I'd ever charted: the glass forests of Veyl, the river-countries where the water ran backward on feast days, the Saltmarsh, the Hollow's edges before the Hollow was an absence. All of it gone, replaced by a smooth white nothing that ached like a pulled tooth whenever anyone said the word *map*.

What remained was this: a small shop above a chandler's in Vessengard, a stock of trinkets that were now merely trinkets, and a child who ate all my bread and slept curled against my door like a cat who had decided the household was his.

Isandro. Who was not, as it turned out, a messenger. Who was a key, which meant he was also a lock, which meant that when the Regent tore free of Aldric and I finished the living map around it — gave the thing edges at last, the one mercy and the one cruelty I had left in me — the door it had come through closed *through him*, or partly through him, and what was left standing in the throne room afterward was a boy of perhaps eight years with mud still dried in the creases of his knuckles and no kingdom underneath the crown anymore at all.

He didn't remember the Hollow either. I checked, gently, over the first few weeks. He remembered being sent. He remembered the finding — *the one who draws doors* — and he remembered me, with a fidelity that occasionally made my chest hurt. But the space beneath the crown, the realm-sized hunger, the language that burned: those were sealed behind the same door I'd paid to shut. When I asked him about it, he'd go quiet in a particular way, the way you go quiet pressing on a bruise to see if it still hurts, and then he'd say, "It's flat now," and ask for more bread.

Flat. The whole devouring absence, flattened into a memory he could carry in one hand. Children are better cartographers than we give them credit for.

* * *

King Aldric abolished the magical prohibition on the first day of spring, which everyone agreed was very poetic and which Aldric himself, I suspect, did not notice, because Aldric was a man walking around inside his own life like a tenant reading someone else's mail.

Thirty years the Regent had worn him. Thirty years of outlawing magic so the crown could feed on the fear of it, and Aldric remembered none of it from the inside — only waking, hollowed, on the floor of his own throne room, with a dead god folded up in a map at his feet and a cartographer and a child standing over him. They say he wept when he understood what had been done in his name. They say he asked Isandro's forgiveness, and Isandro, literal to the last, said, "You weren't wearing yourself. It wasn't your hands." Which is either absolution or the most devastating thing ever spoken in a throne room, depending on how much thought you give it. I try not to give it much.

The proclamation went up on every gate: *Let the practice of magic be lawful within the bounds of Vessengard and its territories.* Underneath, in smaller letters, because even freed kings are still kings: *subject to such reasonable regulations as the Crown shall determine.* Old habits. At least these were his own.

My little illegal trade became legal overnight, which should have felt like vindication and instead felt like being handed back a stolen coat that no longer fits. Dead charms flickering alive in the market; hedge-witches coming out of cellars blinking in the daylight; the undercity communities rising, blinking, into legitimacy. The old river-priest — Oren, who had wept over Isandro's tongue and called it the language of making — was given a chapel on the surface, actual walls and a roof, and he preached there every seventh day about doors and keys and the difference between a lock that keeps things out and a lock that keeps things in.

I attended once. He saw me at the back and stopped mid-sermon, and the whole congregation turned, and I realized with horror that I was going to be *thanked*, publicly, by a room full of people whose exile I hadn't personally ended but had at least not gotten killed. So I left through a side door, which Oren later told me was itself a kind of sermon, and which I choose to interpret as dignity.

* * *

Odile turned herself in.

Of course she did. Odile Ferran never did anything halfway except kindness, which she rationed like a woman crossing a desert. She walked into the reconstructed court on the second day of the trials, laid her gray inquisitor's coat on the bench like a shed skin, and confessed to everything: the binding of the Regent, the forging of the prohibition, thirty years of feeding a realm-sized hunger on the fear of her own city because she believed — she *still believed*, she said, voice steady as a plumb line — that it had ended the realm-wars. That peace purchased with a hollowed king was cheaper than another century of burning borders.

They offered her a tribunal. She requested one. And they sent me an invitation, wax-sealed, formal, asking the celebrated cartographer Hazel Sandoval to attend and give testimony regarding the events of the Rite.

I burned it in the chandler's fireplace, and then I felt bad about the waste of wax, and then I sat with the feeling for a long time, because it was the only honest thing I'd produced all week.

Here is what I couldn't have explained to the court, and can barely explain to myself. It wasn't anger. Anger would have been easy; anger would have gotten me dressed and out the door. It was that every question the tribunal would ask her, I had already asked her myself, on the night she intercepted us with soldiers at her back and begged instead — begged *me*, on her knees in the rain outside the palace wall, to let the binding stand, because unmaking the Regent unmade the peace. And I had answered her with a finished map and a boy's voice speaking the First Tongue, and I had watched her face do something I had no memory left to recognize but recognized anyway, the way you know a language you've forgotten you speak.

She built the cage. I closed the door. Between us we had broken a king and saved a city and neither of those facts cancels the other, and I did not trust myself to sit in a courtroom and say any of that without it curdling into something smaller.

So I sent nothing. And Odile, who knew me better than I currently knew myself, sent back — through channels, unsigned — a single sheet of paper with a hand-drawn compass rose in the corner and one line beneath it:

*A map is not a territory. Neither is a person. Go be both.*

I keep it pinned above my workbench. I don't know if it's forgiveness. I don't know if it needs to be. Some debts you pay by refusing to attend the accounting.

* * *

Which leaves the mornings.

Mornings are Isandro's. He rises before I do — children are solar-powered, it turns out, regardless of what kingdoms they were keys to — and by the time I come down he has arranged himself at my workbench with the gravity of a scholar, chalk in hand, ready for the day's lesson.

Because here is the arrangement we fell into, without ever discussing it, the way you fall into most true things. I had lost my past. He had lost his. And it occurred to him — or to whatever survives in a child when you take away the god-shaped hole he was carved from — that he remembered things about me that I no longer remembered about myself. Not the realms. He'd never seen the realms. But the stories. The way I worked. The person I had apparently been before I shrank myself down to fit inside Vessengard's prohibitions.

So every morning he teaches me myself, one story at a time, delivered in his strange literal cadence, like reports from a country I emigrated from.

"This morning's story," he announced today, chalk already moving, "is the door."

"The door," I repeated, settling onto the stool across from him with my tea.

"The first one you ever drew." He was drawing it as he spoke: a rectangle, slightly crooked, with a lopsided handle. "You were small. Smaller than me. You lived in a house with a real door but you wanted a door that went somewhere the real door didn't go. So you drew one on the wall in chalk, and you knocked on it, and — "

"Nothing happened," I said. "Obviously. Chalk doesn't open."

He looked up at me with mild reproach, the look of a teacher whose student has interrupted the lesson to display ignorance. "It opened a little," he said. "Not enough to walk through. Enough that you saw what was on the other side. Enough that you spent the rest of your life trying to draw it properly." He tapped the crooked rectangle. "That's why your maps were good. Everyone else was drawing places. You were drawing the knock."

I sat with that. Outside, the market was waking up — legal now, loud about it, a hedge-witch two stalls down arguing cheerfully with the chandler about the price of tallow versus the price of blessings. A city rebuilding itself out loud. Somewhere across town, a broken king was learning to wear his own face. Somewhere in a cell, a woman who taught me everything was paying for it with the only coin she respected.

And here, at a scarred workbench, a child finished drawing a crooked chalk door, blew the dust off his fingers, and pushed it across the table to me.

"Your turn," he said. "Draw the other side."

I picked up the chalk. My hand, which had drawn coastlines I can no longer name and borders that no longer exist and one map with no edges that cost me everything, hesitated over the blank paper — and then, because that is apparently who I am, whoever exactly that turns out to be each morning, I began.

I don't know yet what's on the other side of the door. Neither does he. We're mapping it together, one story at a time, and for the first time since I opened my shop door to a mud-streaked child speaking a dead language, the not-knowing feels less like loss than like coastline: the beginning of a chart, waiting to be drawn.
