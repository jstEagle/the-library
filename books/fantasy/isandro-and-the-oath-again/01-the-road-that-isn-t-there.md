## 1. The Road That Isn't There

The road didn't exist, and Gideon Okafor walked it anyway.

That was the first thing they taught you in the Wayfarers' Guild—before knots, before stars, before the long recitations of passes and fords and the names of every dead guide worth remembering. They taught you that a road was not a thing in the world. It was an agreement between the walker and the land, and like all agreements, it could be made or unmade by whoever held the terms.

Gideon had been holding the terms of Thornfell for thirty-one years, and she was tired.

Not tired enough to stop. She climbed the last pitch of the Shepherd's Spine with a hundredweight of untaxed ironbark resin on her back and her breath coming in white ribbons, and she thought, as she did on every run: *one more season*. She'd been saying it for eleven seasons now. The saying had become a kind of prayer, and prayers, her grandmother used to say, were just promises you made to yourself when nobody else would take them.

Below her, the official road wound along the valley floor—a broad, graveled thing, patrolled twice daily by Magistrate Corven's riders, lined with toll-stones and watch-fires and the little shrines travelers built to whatever god kept them from being robbed. It was a good road. It went where it said it went. Any fool with a mule could follow it.

Which was exactly why Gideon hadn't touched it in nine years.

She crested the ridge where the spine narrowed to a knife-edge of black stone, and paused—not because she needed to rest, but because this was the part of the walk she allowed herself to love. From here, Thornfell opened up below her like a book only half the pages of which had ever been printed. The valley roads, yes. But also the ghost-roads: the switchback that existed only in fog, the ford under the falls that appeared when the river ran low in late summer, the hollow way through the hills that the old guild maps called the Sleeper's Throat because it closed behind you like a snoring mouth. None of them appeared on any chart drawn in the last century. None of them could be found by anyone who hadn't been taught them—walked through them once, hand on a teacher's shoulder, while the teacher named each turn aloud so the path would learn your name back.

That was how it worked. Nobody believed it until they'd done it. Roads in Thornfell weren't geography. They were relationships.

And Gideon was, as far as she knew, the last person alive who was on speaking terms with all of them.

* * *

Three days earlier, she'd taken the job at the Drowned Lamb, the smugglers' tavern wedged into the cliffside above Lowmarsh where the tax men never came because the tax men couldn't find the door unless someone wanted them to.

The buyer was a resin-factor named Aldous Vey, fat rings on his fingers, sweat on his upper lip even in the cold. He counted out coin on the table with the reluctance of a man paying for something he suspected might be stolen—which it was, technically, though Gideon preferred *reclaimed*. Ironbark resin fetched triple its weight in silver downriver, and Corven's new tariffs had priced every honest harvester out of the trade, which meant the harvesters sold to Gideon, and Gideon moved it, and everyone ate except the magistrate's ledger.

"You'll take the valley road," Vey said. "It's faster."

"The valley road has four checkpoints," Gideon said. "You know this. You're testing me, or you're stupid, and I've watched you count money, so I know it's not stupidity."

He flushed. "I meant—"

"I know what you meant." She swept the coin into her coat. "I take my own roads. Your cargo goes over the Spine and down through the Hollows. Three days. If I'm not back in three days, don't come looking. Looking is how people get found."

"And if the cargo doesn't arrive?"

"Then the cargo doesn't arrive, and you're out the cost of a hundredweight of resin." She stood, and let him see, briefly, the thing about her face that made factors stop arguing—the flat patience of someone who had buried more friends than he'd ever made. "It won't happen. But if it does, you'll live. That's the warranty."

He didn't ask what she meant by that either. Smart, for a factor.

* * *

Now she was on the Spine, and the wind up here had teeth.

She moved the way her grandmother had drilled into her before Gideon was old enough to understand why the lessons mattered: weight on the balls of the feet, eyes reading the ground two strides ahead, hands free. Never hurry. Hurrying was how walkers became fallers, and fallers became stories, and stories became warnings told to children who then stayed off the mountain entirely, which was its own kind of death for a road.

Because that was the other truth of the hidden ways, the one the guild had never written down: a road no one walked stopped being a road. Not all at once—it faded the way a friendship fades, through neglect, through years of unsent letters. The Sleeper's Throat had been sulking for a decade because Gideon was the only one who still visited. The fog-switchback above Ravenmere had gone strange and slippery since old Maren died; paths remembered their dead, and grieved them badly.

There had been forty sworn guides in the Thornfell chapter when Gideon was born. Her grandmother used to say the number out loud on festival nights, forty voices, forty sets of boots by the hall door, the whole mountain threaded with people who knew its secrets the way priests knew scripture.

Now the hall stood empty except for her. Now the number was one, and one was a number that couldn't survive a bad winter.

She thought about that more than she wanted to. She thought about it most on the high stretches, where the wind stripped everything else away and left a woman alone with the arithmetic of extinction. Forty to one. And after her—nothing. No apprentice. The last two candidates had taken one look at the life she led—invisible, unpaid in anything but barter, married to the mountain—and gone down to the valley towns to become cartographers' assistants and toll-clerks. Honest work. Warm work. Work where you could tell people what you did at a festival without watching their faces go carefully blank.

Gideon had chosen the mountain. She reminded herself of that on nights when the loneliness got loud. She had chosen it, the way you choose a spouse or a wound.

Mostly she believed it.

* * *

She made the descent into the Hollows by mid-afternoon of the second day, dropping through a seam in the rock face that no surveyor had ever mapped because the seam didn't want to be mapped. Inside, the Hollows ran for six miles under the shoulder of the mountain—a limestone warren of galleries and chimneys, dry-floored, invisible from above, and utterly unfindable unless you knew that the entrance was keyed to a particular quality of afternoon light falling across a particular split boulder. Get the hour wrong and you walked past solid wall. Get it right and the mountain opened its mouth.

Her grandmother had brought her here at seven years old, in exactly such a light, and made her stand at the threshold and say the words: *"The road is not there. Say it. The road is not there until we agree otherwise."*

Seven-year-old Gideon had said it, and the wall had opened, and she had screamed with delight, and her grandmother had cuffed her gently and said, *Never scream underground. Sound travels where light can't.* Then, softer: *One day you'll be the only one who knows the words. Don't let that make you cruel.*

Gideon moved through the Hollows now with a shuttered lantern and the easy stride of long habit, checking the marker-cairns as she passed—small stacks of stone, rebuilt whenever they slumped, each one a sentence in a conversation the guild had been having with the mountain for three hundred years. Most were fine. One, near the mid-gallery, had collapsed, and she stopped and rebuilt it in the dark, stone by stone, murmuring the restoration under her breath the way you'd straighten a fallen picture frame in a dead relative's house.

"Still here," she told the cairn. "Still walking you."

The mountain, as always, said nothing. But the air in the gallery warmed by a degree, the way a house warms when someone comes home, and Gideon chose to believe that too.

* * *

She delivered the resin on schedule, took her payment in silver and salt-pork and news, and started home on the fourth morning with the particular lightness of a run completed clean.

The news, as it happened, had been bad. A courier had gone missing on the high passes—some official runner out of the vale country, east of Thornfell, carrying dispatches no one would name. Riders had been asking questions in Lowmarsh. Corven's riders, asking them politely, which was worse than rude, because politeness meant they already knew the answers and were measuring whether you'd lie well enough to be worth arresting.

Gideon had filed it away the way she filed everything: quietly, in the deep cabinet of the mind where she kept things that might someday need to kill someone.

Home was a day's walk north, most of it on the ghost-road above the tree line that she privately called the Widow's Stair, because her grandmother had named it that after her grandfather fell from it, and grief, in the guild, got baked right into the geography. She climbed it in the grey of early evening, the peaks around her going rose and then ash, and she felt the familiar settling come over her—the one that wasn't quite peace and wasn't quite resignation, the feeling of a woman returning to an empty house that was also an entire inheritance.

At the top of the Stair, where the path crossed the saddle below Cragwatch, she stopped.

Habit. Always stop at the saddle. Read the sky, read the wind, read the ground.

The sky was clean. The wind carried pine and snowmelt and nothing else.

But the ground—

There was blood on the switchback below her. Fresh, darkening at the edges, smeared in the dragging pattern of a body that had tried to crawl and mostly failed. It led off the path, down toward the scree, and Gideon stood very still in the failing light with her hand already inside her coat on the knife she hoped she wouldn't need, and listened to the mountain hold its breath.

No rider in sight. No sound but wind. Whoever had bled here was either gone or dying, and both possibilities required her to move, and only one of them required her to decide what kind of person she was going to be tonight.

She went down toward the scree, quick and silent, the Widow's Stair forgotten behind her, and the last keeper of the hidden roads followed the blood to find out whose story had ended—or hadn't—on her mountain.
