## 2. Blood on the Switchback

The pass above Kettle Hollow had three names and none of them were true. The shepherds called it the Widow's Shoulder because a woman had frozen there two winters back, though Gideon knew the woman had been no shepherd and had died twenty miles east of where the story put her. The carters called it the Long Climb because they only ever saw it from below, hauling salt up the switchbacks in the summer months. And the Wayfarers' Guild—what was left of it—called it the Third Stitch, because it was the third thread in a seam of paths that ran from the Thornfell lowlands to the high meadows without once touching a road any magistrate could find on a map.

Gideon Okafor knew all three names and trusted none of them. Names were how you got found.

She came over the shoulder at dusk, moving with the particular economy of someone who had walked this ground since before she could name it. Her boots knew where the scree shifted. Her hands knew which rock held warmth after sundown and which gave it back too fast, betraying frost by morning. She carried forty pounds of contraband—feverbark tincture, mostly, and a bolt of Vale-woven silk that would sell for triple its worth in the river towns—and she carried it the way she carried everything: lightly, invisibly, as if the mountain itself had agreed not to notice her.

That was the trick of the guild's roads. Not concealment. Agreement. Her grandmother had tried to explain it once, near the end, when the words came slow and expensive. *The path isn't hidden, child. It's forgotten. There's a difference, and the difference is everything.* Gideon had been nineteen and impatient and hadn't asked what she meant. She'd had time since. Twenty years of time, walking roads that appeared on no chart, keeping them alive by walking them, and watching the guild dwindle from eleven sworn guides to four to—this season—to one, if you didn't count old Maren whose knees had retired before her pride did.

One guide. One set of feet keeping a hundred miles of forgetting alive.

She crested the final switchback below the cairn and stopped.

There was blood on the stone. Fresh enough to still be dark, not yet blackened by cold. A smear along the inner edge of the turn, where a body would drag if it crawled, and then a wider pool ten paces on, half-soaked into the gravel, already stiffening at the edges in the evening air.

Gideon went still in the way the guild taught—not frozen, but *quiet*, weight settling through her boots into the mountain, breath thinned to nothing. She listened. Wind in the scree. A hawk somewhere over the eastern face. Water ticking under the ice lens beneath the trail. No hoofbeats. No voices.

She moved forward off the main line of the path, onto the broken ground where her own tracks would read as nothing but weather, and followed the blood.

He was wedged into a fold of rock below the trail, half-sheltered, as if he'd crawled there deliberately—as if he'd known, even dying, that open ground meant being seen. He was young. That was the first thing, the wrong thing. Couriers who worked the high passes were supposed to be old and gray and unremarkable, men whose faces slid out of memory before they'd finished paying for their tea. This one couldn't have been past thirty, with a farmer's hands and a city man's boots, and he'd been cut nearly to pieces.

Three wounds that she could count in the failing light. One across the thigh that had bled him most of the way empty. One through the meat of his sword arm, defensive, taken facing his attacker. And one low in the belly that she didn't need to touch to know was fatal, because his skin had gone the color of the snowmelt and his breathing made a wet sound like a boot pulling out of mud.

His eyes found her anyway. Dying men always did; the guild said it was because the dying had already stopped caring about anything except being seen, and so they saw everything.

"You're—" His voice cracked apart. He swallowed and tried again. "You're real. I thought—I thought the paths were stories."

"Everything's a story until you're standing on it." She knelt beside him, already pulling her pack open, already knowing the feverbark wouldn't help and the needle and gut-thread in her repair kit was for canvas, not men. "Who did this?"

"Riders." He coughed, and the cough cost him something he couldn't afford. "Gray cloaks. Magistrate's men. They took the—no." His hand shot out and caught her wrist with a strength that surprised them both, grip like a trap sprung shut. "No. They didn't get it. Listen. *Listen.*"

"I'm listening."

"They think I dropped it in the river at Grayford. I let them think it. But it has to go—it can't stop, do you understand? It can't stop moving." He was fumbling at his own chest with his ruined arm, clumsy, weeping with frustration or pain or both, and Gideon found herself helping him, which was the second wrong thing she did that evening, because a guide's first rule was *don't touch what the mountain is already taking*, and she touched him anyway. She got his coat open. Inside the lining, sewn flat against his ribs, was an oilcloth packet the size of a prayer book.

He pressed it into her hands and then kept pressing, as if he could push the whole thing through her palms into her bones.

"Ashenmoor," he said.

The word landed strangely. Not like a name. Like a bell struck in an empty room—a sound she felt in her teeth before she understood it. Somewhere far back in her childhood, in her grandmother's kitchen, in a lullaby hummed over a stove, she almost caught the shape of it. Then it was gone.

"What is it?" she asked. "What's Ashenmoor?"

"The covenant." His eyes had gone glassy, fixed on something well past her shoulder, well past the mountain, maybe well past the world. "It's failing. Thirteen turns. You have to take it to—the tokens—find the—" Blood came up instead of words. He turned his head and spat it into the gravel, and when he looked at her again he was clearer, terribly clear, a man spending his last coin all at once. "Your grandmother. Sera Okafor. She'll have told you something. Anything. Look for it."

Gideon's spine went cold in a way the wind couldn't account for. "My grandmother's been dead nine years."

"I know," the courier said, and smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen all day, and she'd seen a village constable hanged from his own gallows beam last spring. "I know. That's why I climbed."

* * *

He lasted another quarter hour. She spent it doing the things you do: holding his head so he didn't drown in his own blood, feeding him sips of watered spirits from her flask, lying to him about how close the shrine at the top of the pass was and how good the fire would be there. He knew she was lying. He was grateful anyway. Near the end he gripped her hand and said, "Name's Pell. Tell whoever comes after that Pell got it through. Tell them Pell got it through."

"I'll tell them."

And then he spent the rest, and was done, and the mountain took him the way it takes everyone, patiently, without malice.

Gideon sat back on her heels in the blue dark and let herself shake for exactly sixty seconds. She counted them against her pulse, a discipline drilled into every guild child: fear is fuel, but only measured. At sixty she stood, wiped her face with her sleeve, and became practical.

Because that was when she heard the horses.

Far below—two switchbacks down, maybe three, muffled by the wind but unmistakable to anyone raised on these stones—the iron-shod knock of hooves on the causey, and with them the thin carry of voices. Gray cloaks. Magistrate's riders, working the pass in the dark, which told her they hadn't given up on their courier and hadn't found what they wanted at Grayford. They were climbing. They would reach the cairn within the hour, and they had lanterns, and lanterns on the Third Stitch would find blood, and blood would find them following her.

Her first instinct was the oldest one, carved into her before memory: *be gone*. Drop off the trail's shoulder onto the goat-path, vanish into the scree, be in Kettle Hollow by midnight and a different person entirely by morning. Leave the body. Leave the map. Let the dead keep his own secrets; the living had enough.

But the packet was in her hand. Had been since she'd tucked it inside her coat, and she noticed now that she'd done it without deciding to, the way you catch something falling before your mind agrees it's falling.

And Pell's voice was in her ears. *Tell them Pell got it through.*

"Damn you," she said quietly, to him, to the mountain, to whatever arrangement of the world had put a dying stranger across her path on the one night she'd planned to sleep warm. "Damn you for climbing."

Then she got to work.

Hiding a body on the Widow's Shoulder was not easy, and Gideon did not pretend otherwise. The pass was scree and slab and thin air, nothing soft, nowhere deep. But the guild's roads taught you the mountain's private architecture: the hollows under tilted slabs, the screes that swallowed and kept, the places where winter's melt undercut the stone and left rooms beneath the trail that no honest surveyor would ever mark. Two hundred yards below the cairn, behind a fall of rock that looked like every other fall of rock, there was a fissure—an old frost-crack, waist-deep, running back under the hillside. She'd sheltered in it during a whiteout when she was sixteen. It had saved her life once. It would serve again.

Moving Pell was the hardest labor she'd done in years. He was heavier dead than alive, the way the dead always are, and the ground fought her, and twice she nearly went over the edge with him, and once she did go down, hard, driving her knee into stone hard enough that she'd feel it for a week. She didn't cry about it. She swore, comprehensively, in three languages, and kept dragging.

Before she rolled him into the fissure, she went through his pockets. It wasn't theft; it was arithmetic. If the riders found identification, they'd have a name, and a name was a thread, and threads got pulled. She took his papers—a trade pass under a false name, Tomas Beck, which told her he'd been careful—and his purse, twelve coppers and a silver bit, and a small wooden token on a cord around his neck that she might have missed if the moon hadn't come out just then.

It was a wayfarer's knot. Carved oak, worn smooth, the old guild sign—the sign that hung, or had hung, over the door of every guildhouse from here to the coast. Her fingers closed around it and something in her chest clenched like a fist.

Pell had been carrying a wayfarer's token. Pell had known to look for a guide on a road that wasn't there. Pell had known her grandmother's name.

*That's why I climbed.*

She put the cord over her own head, next to her skin, and told herself she was keeping it safe for his people. She was almost sure that was even true.

She rolled him into the fissure, packed loose stone until the shape of him was only a shape of stone, swept the blood-smeared gravel with a bundle of brush until the trail read clean, and scattered fresh scree across the whole stretch of it. Then she stood in the dark, breathing hard, knee screaming, and listened to the riders come.

They reached the cairn while she watched from the shadow of the rockfall, fifty feet away and utterly invisible, because that was the one thing she had never failed at. Four horses. Lanterns swinging. Gray cloaks with the magistrate's badge stitched at the shoulder—a black tower on a field of ash. Their leader dismounted at the bloodstain and crouched over it, and even at that distance Gideon saw how he moved: economical, unhurried, a man reading a page rather than searching a trail.

He studied the ground for a long time. Then he stood and looked—not at the trail ahead, not back the way the courier must have come, but directly at the rockfall. Directly, it seemed to Gideon, at her.

"Blood stops here," he said, to his men, in a voice that carried up the pass like it had been sent. "Which means either the river took him, or somebody helped him along. Sweep the shoulder. Be thorough."

They swept. Gideon stood in her pocket of dark and did not breathe more than the mountain breathed, and after a lifetime of perhaps ten minutes, the riders remounted and continued up toward the cairn, lanterns bobbing away into the night like slow, malevolent stars.

She waited until the sound of them was a rumor, and then a memory, and then nothing. Then she took out the oilcloth packet and, with numb fingers, unwrapped one corner—just enough to see the edge of what lay inside.

Vellum. Old vellum, stained dark at one corner in a way she was beginning to understand was blood, and marked across its face in faded ink with lines that were not roads—or rather, were roads, but roads drawn in a hand and a scheme she had never seen, converging on a point in the mountains' heart where no map she'd ever memorized showed anything but blank stone.

At the center of the convergence, small and precise, someone had written a single word:

**Ashenmoor.**

And beneath it, in a different ink, older, in a hand that made her stomach drop through the mountain into some colder place—because she knew that hand, had watched it write recipes and letters and the guild's ledgers, had watched it go unsteady at the very end—

Beneath the word, her grandmother had written:

*If you are reading this, the thirteenth moon has begun to turn. Come home, Gideon. Bring the oath.*

Gideon wrapped the packet closed, tucked it against her ribs where Pell had carried it, and started down the pass in the dark, alone, moving fast, no longer invisible to herself.

Behind her, high on the Widow's Shoulder, the magistrate's lanterns paused at the cairn—and then, unhurried, began to descend.
