## 3. What the Guild Swore

The map smelled of blood and old tallow.

Gideon had washed her hands three times since the pass—scrubbed until her knuckles cracked in the cold water of a stream that wasn't on any honest chart—but the smell seemed to have gotten into her skin, into the creases of her palms where she'd gripped the courier's wrist and felt his pulse stutter and stop. She kept flexing her fingers as she walked, like she could shake it loose.

She couldn't shake loose what he'd said either. *Ashenmoor.* He'd said it twice, the second time barely a breath, and then the magistrate's riders had come over the ridge and there had been no time for anything but the body, the scree slope, the loose stone she'd rolled down over him with hands that shook so badly she'd left half her fingerprints in the dirt.

Three days later she was still walking. Not toward anywhere. Away from the pass, away from the road, following the kind of route that didn't exist—a deer trail that became a goat track that became nothing at all, then a seam in the rock face that only opened if you pressed your shoulder against the right stone and trusted it would give. It gave. It always gave. Her grandmother had taught her that: *the mountain remembers who asks politely.*

By the third evening the trees thinned and the air changed, took on the smell of cold ash and older stone, and Gideon stopped pretending to herself about where she was going.

The guild hall stood in a fold of the mountains where no valley should be—a hollow the maps called a surveyor's error, because the surveyors who found it never surveyed anything again. It had been grand once, she supposed. She'd seen the engravings: a long hall of black timber and pale stone, lanterns burning in every window, the doors thrown open to any traveler who knew the knock. Now the doors hung crooked on leather hinges gone stiff as bark, and moss had eaten the eastern wall whole, and the lanterns were just iron cages full of bird nests.

She'd been coming here since she was six years old. It had never once felt like coming home. It felt like visiting a grave that still expected you to do chores.

Inside, dust lay thick enough to write in. Someone had written in it, years ago—*Mara Okafor was here, and so was the rain,* scratched by her grandmother's own hand near the door, and Gideon stepped around it without looking, the way you step around a wound.

She built a fire in the great hearth because the chimney still drew, one of the few mercies the hall allowed, and set the courier's map on a table that had been a door once, laid flat across two barrels. Then she sat down across from it and made herself look at it properly for the first time.

It was not a map of roads.

That was the thing she'd been avoiding for three days. A smuggler's map showed roads—passes, fords, the gaps in patrols, the farms that sold eggs and asked no questions. This showed none of that. The parchment was covered in fine, dense linework that resolved, when she tilted it toward the firelight, into mountain ranges she half-recognized and ranges she did not recognize at all. Two territories, drawn overlapping each other like two hands laced together. Thornfell she knew—the ridgelines were wrong in the way a portrait is wrong, stylized, but she could feel her own country under the ink. And threaded through it, interwoven with it, a second land she had no name for, all deep vales and standing stones and rivers that ran silver instead of blue.

Between them, binding them like stitching through cloth, ran lines. Hundreds of them. Roads—but not roads. They connected points marked with small careful symbols: a bell, an eye, a hand, a flame, a key.

And at thirteen points along the border between the two lands, drawn larger than the rest, were seals. Circles within circles, ringed in script too small and too old to read by firelight.

Thirteen. The number snagged in her mind like a fishhook. She didn't know why yet. She only knew that when she counted them—and she counted them twice, three times—her heart began to beat faster, and not from fear exactly. From recognition. The way you recognize a face across a crowded market before you can place the name.

There was a seal at the top corner of the map, larger than the others, half worn away by handling. Around its rim ran a script she *could* read, because she'd seen it a thousand times without ever seeing it.

It was guild-script. The old wayfarer's hand, the one carved above the doors of this hall, the one stamped into the lead tokens her grandmother had kept in a box and never explained. The seal read:

*We walked first. We swore first. What binds the realms was bound by our feet.*

Gideon sat back from the table so fast her chair scraped.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty hall, because saying it out loud made it more deniable. "No, we were smugglers. We were guides. We carried wool and lied to tax collectors."

The hall said nothing back. Halls like this never did. But the fire popped, and somewhere up in the black rafters a bird shifted its weight, and Gideon found she couldn't take her eyes off the seal, off those seven words in her own guild's hand, worn soft by thumbs that had touched them over and over and over. Generations of thumbs. People who had known what this was. People who had died knowing, and passed it down, and passed it down, until the passing-down reached a woman who carried brandy past excise men and thought the family trade was hiding things.

Maybe it was. Maybe hiding things was always the surface of it.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with a strange detachment, as though the hands belonged to someone else—someone who hadn't spent thirty years being the steadiest person on any mountain she stood on.

*You need to sleep,* she told herself. *You're grieving a man you met dying and reading ghosts into ink.*

But she didn't sleep. She fed the fire and turned the map by degrees under the light, and the longer she looked, the more the guild-script surfaced in it—not just in the seals but along the roads themselves, tiny annotations in margins, tiny words tucked into river bends. *Toll paid. Toll paid. Toll unpaid—avoid.* As if the roads themselves kept accounts. As if they were alive, or had been, or had been treated as though they were.

And one annotation, near the largest of the thirteen seals, in a different hand—older, deeper, cut into the parchment the way you score a line before you fold it:

*The vale sleeps behind the veil. The veil is woven of oaths. When the last oathkeeper's line ends, the weaving fails.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, almost human:

*God forgive us for what we agreed to.*

* * *

Her grandmother had died in this hall, eleven years ago, in the month the rowan berries came early.

Gideon had been twenty-four and already the best guide in Thornfell, though she'd have denied it with her fists if anyone said so. She remembered everything about those last days with the terrible fidelity memory reserves for things you'd rather forget: the smell of the sickroom herbs Mara Okafor insisted on brewing herself even when she couldn't stand at the hearth; the way her grandmother's hands, which had been strong enough to pull a grown man out of a white-water ford, had gone thin as winter branches; the way the old woman had refused, absolutely refused, to die anywhere but the guild hall, in the room behind the great hearth where generations of guildmasters had done their dying.

On the last night, her grandmother had sent everyone else away. The other guild kin—there had still been nine of them then, nine, when now there were four and one of those was a boy of sixteen who'd never walked a high pass alone—had grumbled and obeyed, because nobody disobeyed Mara Okafor, living or dying.

"Sit," her grandmother said, and Gideon sat on the edge of the bed, and the old woman took her hand. Her grip was still surprising. That was the thing Gideon remembered most: how much strength was left in it, held in reserve like coin, spent all at once on this.

"You think I taught you the roads," Mara said. Her voice was a ruin, gravel and ash, but her eyes were clear as meltwater. "I taught you the roads so you'd survive. I never taught you what the roads are for. That was a mercy. I'm going to take it away now, because I don't have time to be kind."

"Grandmother—"

"Hush. Listen." She pulled Gideon closer, close enough that Gideon could see the map of veins in her temple, the pulse fluttering there like a trapped moth. "The guild is older than its ledgers. Older than the crown charters, older than the Vale Council, older than every border ever drawn in these mountains. We were not founded to smuggle. Smuggling is what we became when everyone forgot. We were founded to *keep.*"

"Keep what?"

"The oath." Her grandmother's eyes went to the ceiling, to the rafters, to something beyond both. "Before your mother's mother's mother, there was a war between Thornfell and the vale beyond the veil—Ashenmoor, they called it, though the name's been burned out of every record the lowlands keep. The war ended not with a battle but with a bargain, and the bargain needed keepers. People who could walk between. Who could carry the tolls and tend the seals and make sure neither realm forgot it owed the other. Thirteen seals, girl. Thirteen turns of the hidden moon. Our people swore to hold them."

Gideon had laughed. She remembered that with shame, the small disbelieving laugh, and the way her grandmother hadn't smiled back.

"Mara," she'd started—

"Don't." The grip tightened, painful now. "I've watched the guild shrink my whole life. Watched the seals go untended because the tolls stopped coming and the keepers stopped believing and the Council in the vale stopped answering. I tended what I could alone. I've walked to four of the thirteen in my time, and I'll tell you something that will freeze your blood: the ones I tended answered. The stone warmed. The water rose to meet my hand. And the ones I couldn't reach—" her breath caught, rattled, steadied— "the ones I couldn't reach have gone quiet. Cold. You understand? It's not that the magic leaves. It's that the *agreement* frays. And when it goes entirely, the veil goes with it, and everything Ashenmoor locked behind that veil comes through into your mountains, and everything of yours bleeds into theirs, and there will be no border left for anyone to fight over because there will be no *sides.*"

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you're the last one who can carry it." Her grandmother's voice dropped, went almost tender, which was worse than the gravel. "I hoped it would skip you. I hoped the guild would end clean, with me, and the oath would fail gently, over centuries, and no one would ever pay the bill. But the moon's turning faster than it should. I've felt it in the passes. Things moving that shouldn't move. And you—" she lifted Gideon's hand, turned it over, pressed her thumb hard into the palm, into the callus earned from ten thousand miles of rope and rock— "you walk the old ways like you invented them. You ask the mountain politely and it answers. It's never answered me like that. Not once in sixty years."

"I don't want it," Gideon had said. Stupidly, plainly, the truth of a twenty-four-year-old. "I didn't ask for any of it. I just want the roads."

"I know." Her grandmother had almost smiled then. "So did I. So did every keeper back to the first. That's how you know it's real work and not ambition. Ambition wants. Inheritance doesn't want—it *waits.*" She let go of Gideon's hand and fumbled beneath her pillow, and pressed something into it: a lead token on a rotted cord, stamped with the eye-and-bell sigil. "First seal is under the drowned chapel, west fork of the Graywater. You'll know the door when the water shows you. Go at low ebb, never at flood. And Gideon—" the last strength went into it, all of it, her voice cracking like ice underfoot— "when someone finally comes asking about Ashenmoor, and someone always does, remember whose feet bound it. Remember we weren't their servants. We were their *equals.* Whatever they offer you, whatever they threaten, we signed as equals, and any terms that say otherwise are lies wearing our ancestors' faces."

She died before dawn. Gideon sat with her until the light came, holding a hand that no longer held back, and then she had buried her grandmother in the hall's small yard, under the rowan tree, and put the token in a box, and not opened the box for eleven years.

Eleven years of carrying brandy and lying to tax collectors. Eleven years of telling herself the deathbed talk was fever, was legend, was an old woman's way of making her small life feel large.

* * *

Now she sat in the same hall, in the same firelight, with a dead stranger's map unrolled in front of her and her grandmother's words standing up in her memory like the dead woman herself had risen from the rowan's roots to point at it.

*Thirteen seals.* Thirteen on the map. *The moon's turning faster than it should.*

Gideon got up, crossed the hall on legs that felt borrowed, and pried open the floorboard behind the master's chair—the one her grandmother had shown her at fifteen, *in case I die stupid and sudden*, though she'd never said what was under it. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle, lay the guild's last relics: three lead tokens, a stub of black candle, and a book no bigger than a prayer volume, bound in hide that might have been deer and might not.

She brought it to the fire. The cover bore the eye-and-bell, and beneath it, in guild-script: *Ledger of Tolls, Being the True Account of the Covenant of Ashenmoor, Kept by the Order of the Open Road.*

The first page was a list of names. Keepers, she understood at once—guildmasters, generation after generation, each with a date of swearing and a date of death, the columns marching back further than any charter she'd ever heard claimed. Further than the kingdom itself. The last entry was *Mara Okafor, sworn 3rd of Deepwinter, died —*, the death date blank, waiting.

Waiting for hers.

Gideon closed the book. Pressed it flat between her palms. Breathed.

Outside, far below the hollow, a nightbird called, and another answered, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, indifferent, unaware that its borders were stitches and the stitches were pulling. She thought of the courier's blood drying on her sleeve before she'd scrubbed it. She thought of the riders on the ridge, and how they hadn't been chasing a smuggler. Nobody sent armed men over a pass at dawn for a cargo of wool. They'd been chasing *this*—and whoever sent them knew what this was, which meant someone out there believed in the covenant enough to kill for it, or feared it enough to.

Either way, the invisible life was over. She'd known it on the pass, really; she'd just been walking away from the knowledge for three days, and the hollow had run out of distance.

"All right," she said to the hall, to the ledger, to the rowan tree and the woman under it. "Grandmother. Fine. I'm listening."

She opened the ledger to the middle pages, where the toll records lived, and found what she was looking for: a hand-drawn key to the seals, each symbol matched to a location, each location annotated in scripts spanning centuries. The first seal—the drowned chapel, west fork of the Graywater, exactly where her grandmother's token pointed—was marked with a single word in Mara Okafor's handwriting, fresh and sharp compared to the entries around it, added in the last decade of her life:

*Still warm. Hurry.*

Hurry. Eleven years ago, hurry. Gideon looked at the word a long time, and then at the map, and then at the blank space beside her grandmother's name in the ledger, and felt the vertigo of inheritance open under her like a crevasse—clean-sided, bottomless, patient. A legacy she had never asked for, sitting quietly in the dark for eleven years, waiting for her to stop running.

She was reaching for the candle stub to mark the page when she heard it.

A sound from the treeline below the hollow. Soft. Deliberate. The particular crunch of a boot placed carefully on frozen ground by someone who knew she'd hear it anyway—who wanted her to hear it.

Not a rider. Riders didn't come here; there was no path that would admit them. One traveler, on foot, in the dark, walking straight toward a hall that did not exist on any map, in a hollow that killed surveyors.

Gideon snuffed the candle with her fingers, moved the map into shadow, and slid her knife free of its sheath without a whisper.

Whoever it was, they had found the road that wasn't there. Which meant they were guild—or they were something worse.

In eleven years, no one had come looking for her here.

Tonight, someone had.
