## 4. The Warden's Summons

The summons came at dawn, the way the Council liked its orders delivered: early enough to feel like a kindness, formal enough to remind you it wasn't.

Isandro Vael was already awake. He had been awake since the third bell, as he had been every morning for six years, because exile had taught him that sleep was a country he no longer held citizenship in. He sat in the narrow room above the ferry house in Lowmark—the last habitable structure before the Vale's border stones—and watched the light come up gray over the Ashenmoor peaks, and when the knock came he knew what it would be before the messenger opened his mouth.

"Vael." The boy couldn't have been more than nineteen, Vale-born, wearing the gray sash of a Council courier with the self-consciousness of someone who'd only just earned it. He wouldn't meet Isandro's eyes. They never did, anymore. "You're to come down to the ferry landing. There's a writ."

"A writ," Isandro said.

The boy swallowed. "From the Council of Quiet Rooms."

Of course it was. Isandro rose, rolled his shoulders once the way an old soldier does—loosening something that had nothing to do with muscle—and followed him down the stairs into the cold.

* * *

The ferry landing at Lowmark was a plank platform jutting over water so still it looked poured rather than fallen. Beyond it, the river ran out of the Vale through a gap in the mountains called the Warden's Door—a name that had once been his family's pride and was now, apparently, still useful enough that they'd sent for him instead of someone else.

Three Council wardens waited under a canopy of oiled canvas. Not colleagues. Colleagues was a word for people who stood beside you. These three stood apart, arranged with the careful geometry of men who had rehearsed this moment and didn't want to admit it.

Warden-Principal Ondrell Voss stood at the center. He had been Isandro's commander once, back when Isandro had been the youngest warden ever raised to full rank, back when the word *exile* was something that happened to other people, in stories, to traitors. Voss had aged well. His beard was silver now instead of brown, and he wore it like a decoration.

"Isandro." Voss said his name gently, which was worse than saying it coldly. "Thank you for coming."

"You didn't leave me much choice."

"There's always a choice." Voss produced a scroll sealed with the Council's mark—three interlocking rings, pressed deep in gray wax. "But we hoped you'd make the right one without being compelled."

Isandro didn't take the scroll. "Say it plainly. You've crossed half the Vale to find me. Don't dress it up now."

Voss glanced at the two wardens flanking him—a small motion, but Isandro caught it, and understood: *this part isn't for them.* "Walk with me," Voss said.

They walked to the end of the landing, where the planks groaned under their boots and the water below held the mountains upside down, perfect and unreachable. Somewhere upstream, a bell rang the hour in a village Isandro hadn't been allowed to enter in six years.

"A courier left the Vale eleven days ago," Voss said. "Carrying documents concerning the old covenant. The border covenant."

Isandro kept his face still. Every child in the Vale learned about the covenant the way they learned about the flood myths: ancient, half-legendary, the bargain by which Ashenmoor and Thornfell had been bound together—or separated, depending on which teacher you got. The vale kept its magic; the mountains kept their roads; neither realm bled into the other. A story with a moral at the bottom of it.

"The covenant is a dead thing," Isandro said. "It's been failing for generations. Everyone knows."

"Everyone knows it's *failing*," Voss agreed. "What nobody outside these walls knows is how close it is to failing *entirely*. Thirteen moons, Isandro. That's what our astronomers give it. When the thirteenth moon completes its turn, the binding goes slack, and everything the covenant holds apart—" he gestured, a small, elegant motion that took in the mountains, the river, the whole ordered world, "—comes together. Uninvited."

"And the courier?"

"Went rogue. Took the documents off the approved route. We believe he intended to deliver them to someone in Thornfell." Voss paused. "We believe he may have been intercepted. Our riders found blood on the high passes two days ago. No body."

"No body," Isandro repeated.

"The documents were maps. Sealed maps. Covenant-maps, marked with the tokens of the original oathkeepers." Voss finally held out the scroll again, and this time Isandro took it, because not taking it had stopped being a position and started being a performance. "Whoever holds that map holds the terms of the covenant's renewal. And whoever renews the covenant—"

"—controls both realms," Isandro finished quietly.

"The Council requires the map recovered. Intact. And it requires the bearer silenced." Voss's voice stayed level, reasonable, the voice of a man discussing grain tariffs. "That's the writ. Recover and silence."

Isandro looked down at the seal. Three interlocking rings. He had sworn on those rings at nineteen, kneeling on marble, certain that duty was the same thing as honor. He had learned otherwise at thirty-one, on a border road, holding a writ that ordered him to let a starving village fend for itself because feeding it would set a precedent. He had refused. The village had starved anyway—he'd been relieved of command before he could act—and Isandro had been stripped of rank, title, and home, and turned out through the very door his grandfather had built.

Six years. And now they wanted him back.

"Why me?" he asked. "You have a hundred wardens who never fell from grace. Send one of them."

"Because the map will run through Thornfell," Voss said, "and Thornfell is smuggler country. Hidden roads. Roads that don't appear on any chart made inside the Vale. There is exactly one order of people alive who know those roads, and they're nearly extinct." He met Isandro's eyes at last. "And because you know what it costs to be cast out, Isandro. Whoever holds that map is going to be frightened, and proud, and alone. You'll recognize them. That's not a weakness here. That's the qualification."

*Silence them,* the writ said, in words Isandro hadn't read yet but could already hear. *By whatever means necessary.*

"And if I refuse?"

Voss smiled, and it was almost kind. "Then you stay exiled, and someone less gentle does it, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what they did with your gentleness. I've read your file, Isandro. I know what you did at Grayford. I know why." He turned toward the canopy, then paused. "There's a horse saddled at the border stone. Consider it a down payment."

* * *

He rode out through the Warden's Door at midday, and the Door closed behind him—not literally, it was only a gap in the rock, but he felt it close all the same, the way a man feels a cell door even when it's the horizon swinging shut.

Thornfell announced itself gradually. First the trees changed—Vale pines gave way to blackthorn and ironwood, gnarled and leaning, growing at angles that suggested they'd grown in a hurry or in defiance. Then the roads changed. The broad warded highways of the Vale narrowed to pack trails, then to suggestions, then to nothing at all, and Isandro realized he was navigating by moss and sun-angle and the memory of maps he'd studied twenty years ago as a cadet, maps that were already wrong.

Good. Wrong maps meant the roads were hidden. Hidden roads meant the Wayfarers' Guild still worked them, or what remained of it.

He made camp that first night in the hollow beneath a fallen ironwood, built no fire, ate cold salt-pork, and lay looking up at stars that seemed slightly wrong after a lifetime of Vale skies—shifted, as if the heavens themselves had been hung by a different hand. In the dark, unobserved, he let himself do the thing he had forbidden himself on the ride: he thought about home.

Not the Vale. Not the marble halls or the Council of Quiet Rooms with their soft voices and hard arithmetic. Home, smaller than that. His mother's kitchen with its copper pots. His sister Maren, who had written to him twice in six years and then stopped, because their father had told her that correspondence with a disgraced warden reflected poorly on the family, and Maren had always been better at obeying than Isandro was. The little garden behind the warden's residence where he'd learned to fight with wooden swords, where his grandfather had told him, *A door exists to be opened for the right people, lad. Everything else is just carpentry.*

His grandfather had died while Isandro was in disgrace. He had not been permitted to attend the funeral. He had stood outside the wall of the family plot, in the rain, close enough to hear the rite but not to see it, and a groundskeeper had looked through him as though he were weather.

That was what exile cost. Not the title. Not the house. It cost you the right to be seen by the people whose seeing had made you real.

And tomorrow he would go hunting a stranger through these hills and take from them the last thing they had, and if the writ's language meant what he knew it meant, he would take their life along with it, and the Council would call it restoration, and somewhere Maren would hear the news and perhaps write again, and he would be permitted to stand inside walls again.

Isandro found that the thought did not warm him. It sat in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed years ago and never passed—his disgrace, carried inward, worn smooth by time but no lighter for it.

*Duty,* he told himself, in the voice of the young warden he had been. *Duty is the door. Walk through it.*

But the young warden's voice sounded thinner every year, and the hollows around it wider, and Isandro slept badly and woke before the bells and rode north into Thornfell with the writ against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

* * *

On the fourth day he found the first proof that the map had a bearer.

It was at a waystation—a Thornfell roadside shelter, low-roofed, built of dry-stone and turf, the kind of place smugglers used to sleep between runs. Isandro approached it the way he'd been trained: downwind, slow, hands empty. Inside, the hearth was cold, but not long cold. Ash still held its shape.

Someone had slept here within a day. Someone traveling fast, alone, and carefully—carefully, because they'd banked the fire to look dead and swept their own tracks with a branch, and the branch was still leaning against the wall where they'd forgotten it in the dark.

On the hearthstone, half-buried in ash, someone had left a coin. Not payment. A guild-mark: a stamped token showing a road splitting around a mountain, the old sign of the Wayfarers. Left deliberately. Left as a message—to anyone of the guild who might pass, or perhaps to anyone hunting them. *I know you're behind me. I know what you are.*

Isandro picked it up and turned it in the gray light. The stamp was worn nearly smooth. Old. Older than the person who'd carried it, probably—an inheritance, passed hand to hand down a line that was running out.

So. The bearer was guild. Or guild-adjacent. Which meant the bearer knew these roads the way fish knew water, and Isandro—with his wrong maps and his Vale instincts—was a hunter trying to run down a deer on the deer's own ground.

He should have felt discouraged. Instead, standing in the cold waystation with the little worn token in his palm, Isandro felt something he hadn't felt in six years: interest. Purpose, even, though purpose and duty had stopped being the same thing for him a long time ago.

Somewhere ahead of him was a person carrying a map that could remake two realms, hunted by his own former masters, protected by roads that didn't exist. Frightened, and proud, and alone.

*You'll recognize them,* Voss had said. Voss had meant it as tactics.

Standing there, Isandro understood it as prophecy.

He slipped the token into his pocket—evidence, he told himself, though some quieter part of him noted that he'd taken it gently, the way you take something from a shrine—and stepped back out into the Thornfell gray, and turned north, following a woman he hadn't met yet through a country that had already decided not to remember him.

Behind him, far to the south, at the edge of hearing or perhaps only at the edge of imagination, he thought he heard riders. Many riders. Moving fast, and not bothering to hide it.

The Council, it seemed, had sent more than one hunter after all.

Isandro lengthened his stride. Whatever else happened, he intended to reach the bearer first—because the writ said *silence*, and Isandro Vael had spent six years learning exactly how much silence cost, and he would be damned before he let anyone else set the price.
