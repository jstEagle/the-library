## 5. Two Travelers, One Fire

**Isandro**

The waystation sat where no road should have allowed it—a squat stone shelter wedged into a fold of the Thornfell hills, its roof furred with moss, its door hanging open like a slack jaw. Isandro had been following Gideon Okafor for six days, and in six days he had learned exactly one thing: she did not travel through Thornfell. She traveled *through* it, the way a needle travels through cloth, and the country closed behind her without a mark.

He'd lost her twice. The first time at a river crossing that had three banks, as far as he could tell. The second time in a birch wood where every tree looked like every other tree until it didn't, and then he was walking back the way he'd come without ever having turned around.

Tonight he'd stopped trying to follow and started trying to predict. She needed sleep eventually. Everyone did. And there were only so many places in these hills where a fire could be lit without the smoke announcing itself to the whole valley—the old Wayfarer stations, built into hollows, vented through stone chimneys that scattered the heat before it rose. His grandmother's people had used them too, once, before his family chose the Vale over the wilds.

He found the third station by smell: woodsmoke with a thread of juniper, burned to hide the scent of meat.

Isandro stood in the dark outside the door for a long moment, hand on his sword hilt, and made himself honest about what he was about to do. The Council's orders were folded against his heart like a second rib. *Recover the map. Silence whoever holds it.* Silence was a word they'd chosen carefully. Not arrest. Not question. Silence.

And the woman inside had held that map for eleven days now, and the world hadn't ended, and she hadn't sold it, and by every report from Corven's riders she'd buried a stranger on a high pass rather than leave him for the crows. That was not the behavior of a thief or a spy. That was the behavior of someone who took dying men seriously.

It changed nothing. He told himself it changed nothing.

He pushed the door open.

* * *

**Gideon**

She heard him on the gravel four steps out—too heavy for a guild walker, too deliberate for a bandit—and had her knife off her belt and her body low behind the fire before the door moved. When it opened she saw him silhouetted against the stars: tall, broad-shouldered, wearing a traveler's cloak over armor she could hear when he shifted. A soldier pretending to be a pilgrim. The worst kind.

"Sit down slow," she said, "or stand up fast and die tired."

He stood very still. "Gideon Okafor."

"You have me at a disadvantage. You know my name and I don't know yours, which means one of us has been careless and I don't think it's me."

"Isandro Vael." He said it like a man paying out rope he might need to hang himself with. "Warden of the Third March. Formerly."

"Warden." She kept the knife level. Wardens were Vale creatures—oath-keepers, border-judges, the mountain realms' idea of law with a sword attached. "Wardens don't usually skulk after smugglers for six days. They send letters."

"I'm formerly a warden. Exiled. Which means I do my own skulking now." He lowered himself to the ground across the fire from her, slow, hands visible, and she hated how much she appreciated it. "I've come for the map."

Of course he had. She let the silence stretch until it got uncomfortable for him, which took longer than she expected. He wasn't uncomfortable easily. That was worth knowing.

"There is no map," she said.

"There's a map. It was carried by a courier named Dannel Ferro, who left Ashenmoor on the ninth day of the waning moon and died on the Kestrel Switchback with your name spoken into a dead man's ear by the rider who found him." He said it flatly, without triumph, and that frightened her more than a threat would have. "You buried him under a cairn you built yourself. You stacked the stones wrong. Guild cairns go counterclockwise, oldest stone first. Yours went clockwise."

Her stomach dropped. "Who told you that?"

"A Wayfarer. Forty years ago, when I was a boy and the guild still had members." Something crossed his face—grief, maybe, moving fast behind the duty like a fish under ice. "I'm not here for Corven. If I were, you'd be ash already; he burns what he can't buy. I'm here because the Council sent me, and the Council wants the map, and I want—" He stopped. Started again. "I want to know why a dying man spent his last breath giving it to a stranger instead of burning it."

"That's between him and me."

"He gave it to *you*, Okafor. Out of everyone on that pass. Don't you want to know why?"

She wanted to throw the knife at him for asking. Because yes—every night since the switchback, banked under the fear and the fury, that question glowed like a coal. Why her. Why the last guide of a dead guild. What Dannel Ferro had known about her that she didn't know about herself.

Outside, the wind moved through the pines, and somewhere far off something howled—not wolf. Wolves sounded like hunger. This sounded like grief.

"Here's how this goes," Gideon said. "You're going to tell me what the covenant is. All of it. What it binds, what breaks it, what happens if it fails. And if I believe you, I'll take you to the first token."

His eyebrows lifted. "The tokens are real."

"You knew that already, or you wouldn't have flinched." He hadn't flinched, exactly. But his breathing had gone shallow for half a heartbeat, and she'd spent thirty years reading travelers' bodies in bad light. "Don't insult us both. Here's the rest: you don't take the map, you don't take the tokens, and you don't touch me. You walk where I walk, stop when I stop, and if you draw steel on anything but what I point you at, I will leave you somewhere in Thornfell that even I would have trouble finding again. Are we agreed?"

"And if I say no?"

"Then one of us dies tonight, and I promise you it won't be the one holding the knife in the dark with the fire at her back."

He looked at her across the flames for a long moment. Firelight did strange things to faces—took away the armor and the office and left just the person underneath, and the person underneath Isandro Vael looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with six days of tracking. Tired the way people get tired of carrying something heavy up a hill that never ends.

"You're desperate," he said quietly. "You know what the covenant does, or you suspect, and you need me to confirm it. That's why you're negotiating instead of running. You could lose me in these hills tonight and be gone by dawn."

"And you're desperate," she returned, "because an exiled warden doesn't come alone unless he can't trust whoever he'd bring. Your Council sent you to silence me and you haven't. So either you're disobedient or you're doubtful, and either way you need this truce more than I do, because I can survive not knowing. Can you?"

The wind gusted. Sparks climbed. Neither of them moved.

"Agreed," Isandro said at last.

* * *

**Isandro**

She fed him last, which he respected. She ate first, watching him the whole time, and only when she'd finished did she push the dried venison and the hardtack across the fire. He ate. The food was better than it had any right to be—juniper salt, he guessed, a guild trick—and he hated how much comfort a handful of salted meat could give a man who'd eaten Vale rations for two years.

"The covenant," he said, when the plates were empty. "You want all of it."

"All of it."

He turned his hands toward the fire. Old habit. His father had done the same when telling hard truths, as if warmth excused them. "Before the Vale had a Council, the mountains were nine realms at war. Not wars of armies—wars of weather, of blight, of roads that led soldiers into ravines. Each realm had its own magic, its own deep workings, and each used them against the others. Then came the Sundering Winter. You'll have heard stories."

"My grandmother called it the Year the Rivers Forgot Which Way Was Down."

"She'd have been close enough. Nine realms starving, magic turning on its makers, and no one able to stop because stopping meant trusting the enemy realm not to strike while you knelt." He paused. "So someone built the trust. Eight keepers—one for each of the great realms, plus a ninth to hold the center. They swore an oath at Ashenmoor, in the vale at the heart of everything, and bound their magics together so that no realm could turn its workings to war without all eight feeling the pull. The covenant. Peace by shared consequence."

"And the ninth keeper?"

"The ninth held the oath itself. The words, the terms, the seals." He watched her face. "The Wayfarers' Guild, Okafor. Your founders weren't smugglers who happened to know hidden roads. They were the ninth keeper's line. The roads exist because the covenant needs paths between realms that no army can use. Your guild didn't find those roads. It *made* them."

The fire cracked. Gideon didn't move, but he saw the change in her anyway—the small settling of the shoulders, like a house taking weight onto a beam that wasn't built for it.

"You're lying," she said, without conviction.

"I'm exiled and freezing and eating your food. Lying to you buys me nothing I can carry." He leaned forward. "Here's what matters. The eight seals are failing. One by one, over generations—the Council calls it decay, natural erosion. But the thirteenth moon completes its turn soon, and when it does, whatever's left of the binding comes apart entirely. Nine realms' worth of unbound magic, all waking at once, none of them remembering how to be neighbors. My masters believe the renewal requires blood from the ninth keeper's line."

"Your masters believe," she repeated. "And you?"

He should have given the answer he'd rehearsed on the road. Duty. Necessity. The alternative was catastrophe and the price was one life against millions.

"I think," Isandro said slowly, "that the Council has known the seals were failing for sixty years and done nothing, and I have never once heard them say why. I think they sent me to retrieve a map they could have copied in Ashenmoor if they'd ever let the courier arrive. And I think a magistrate who burns villages to flush out a piece of parchment is not working for the peace the covenant was built to keep." He met her eyes. "I think I was sent to fetch something for people who intend to use it badly, and I found out too late to refuse cleanly. So here I am. Eating your venison. Asking you to trust the man who was sent to kill you."

Gideon was quiet for a long time. Outside, the grieving thing howled again, closer.

"Why exile?" she asked finally. "What did you do?"

"That's not part of the bargain."

"You asked me to trust you. I'm pricing it."

Something almost like a smile touched his mouth and died young. "There was a village on the border. Coldmere. Bad harvest, worse winter, and the Council ordered the granaries sealed—rebellion risk, they said. Let hunger sort the loyal from the rest. I was the warden on that march. I opened the granaries." He shrugged, a small motion that cost him visibly. "They couldn't execute a Vael. Too much history. So they unmade me instead. Took the title, the lands, the name's standing. Left me the sword and the shame."

"Coldmere," Gideon said. "I ran grain through Coldmere that winter. Three loads, down the Hollow Road." She stared at him. "That was you. The granary doors were unlocked from inside. We could never work out how."

"From inside," he agreed. "Where no one checks."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the fire filled the silence, and Isandro understood that something had just happened that he didn't have a word for yet. Two people who had spent their lives being lied to by institutions had just caught each other telling the truth.

* * *

**Gideon**

She didn't sleep. She sat with her back to the wall and the knife loose in her lap and watched Isandro Vael sleep like a man who'd decided, somewhere on the road, that vigilance was a luxury he could no longer afford—or no longer wanted. Every hour or so the wind shifted and she checked the door, and every hour or so he stirred and checked it too, and once their eyes met in the dark across the dead fire and neither of them pretended otherwise.

Near dawn she got up and rebuilt the blaze, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"The courier," she said, when he woke. "Dannel Ferro. You knew him."

"He trained under me. Best map-reader the Vale produced in a generation." Isandro sat up, hair wild, dignity abandoned somewhere in the night. "Why?"

"Because before he died, he said a word I thought was delirium. He said *heir*. He pressed the map into my hands and said 'the heir will know,' and then he said Ashenmoor, and then he stopped." She fed a stick to the flames. "I assumed he was raving. But you said the ninth keeper's line. The guild's line. And my grandmother, before she died, made me swear an oath I never understood. She said, 'When the roads call you home, pay what they ask, and forgive them for asking.'" Gideon looked up. "I thought she meant the smuggling routes. I think she meant this."

Isandro had gone very still. "Your grandmother. Her name."

"Maren Okafor."

He breathed out slowly, and it sounded almost like a prayer, or the end of one. "Maren Okafor taught my grandfather the counterclockwise cairns. He wrote about her in his journals. The last keeper he ever trusted." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Do you understand what you're saying? Dannel didn't give the map to a random smuggler. He gave it to the heir. The actual heir. He walked out of Ashenmoor carrying the covenant's future and he carried it straight to you."

"Yes," Gideon said. "I'd worked that out around midnight, thank you."

"And you're still offering to lead me to the first token."

"I'm offering because I can't read the seals alone, and you clearly know more than you've said, and because—" She stopped. Started over, quieter. "Because if the Council wants this thing badly enough to burn villages for it, then it's bigger than me, and I've spent my whole life being small on purpose. I'm tired of it. Aren't you?"

He looked at her across the new fire, and whatever he saw made him nod once, slow, like a man signing something.

"One condition," he said. "We reach the drowned chapel before the next full moon. After that, the passes start snowing, and Corven's riders don't stop for weather."

"Drowned chapel," Gideon repeated. "Under the Grayfangs. Flooded caverns." She felt her grandmother's voice move through her, faint as water under stone: *when the roads call you home.* "I know the way in. Nobody living knows the way in."

"Then we leave within the hour."

"We leave when I say." But she was already rolling her blanket, and he was already kicking dirt over the fire, and they moved around each other in the small station with the ease of people who had done it for years, which was absurd, which was dangerous, which she refused to think about.

At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at the ashes of their fire. Somewhere south of here, a magistrate was burning his way through the hills looking for her. Somewhere north, a Council was waiting for a map that was never coming home. And somewhere beneath the Grayfangs, in water and darkness, the first seal of a broken peace was waiting to see whose blood would wake it.

"Gideon," Isandro said behind her. He'd used her first name for the first time, and they both noticed. "Whatever we find down there—it changes things. Once you touch a token, there's no walking away from it."

"I know." She stepped out into the grey morning light, into the hills that had never once betrayed her, and found, to her surprise, that she wasn't afraid. "That's rather the point."
