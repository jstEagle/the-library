## 6. Magistrate Corven

The village was called Lowmere, which meant nothing to Corven except that it sat where two roads met, and roads were how things moved, and things that moved could be caught.

He arrived at dusk with forty riders and a wagon of pitch-barrels, and he did not bother with the pretense of a warrant. Warrants were for men who needed the law to be on their side. Corven had learned, over thirty years of wearing the magistrate's gray, that the law did not care whose side it was on. It was a lever. You found the fulcrum and you pressed.

"Names," he said to the headman, a stooped man with flour to his elbows who had come out of his mill still wiping his hands, as if cleanliness might count as innocence.

"Lowmere's got ninety-one souls, lord magistrate. Farmers, mostly. A smith. We've done nothing—"

"Everyone's done nothing." Corven dismounted, unhurried, and let the silence do its work while his riders fanned out along the lane with the ease of men who had done this before. "That's what makes my work so tedious. Everyone's innocent. And yet here we are."

He walked the village the way he always did: slowly, hands clasped behind his back, looking at everything and letting everyone see him look. The mill. The shrine with its rain-faded saint. The well. The inn, shuttered against the cold but not against him; nothing shut against Corven for long.

"Three nights ago," he said, loud enough for the gathering crowd, "a courier came through here. Tall. Gray cloak. Riding hard enough to lather two horses in a day. He slept under your roof—" he nodded at the innkeeper, a woman with a bruised jaw and steady eyes, "—and by morning he was gone, and so was whatever he carried. I want to know who met him. I want to know which way he went. And I want to know why a mill-village with no secrets suddenly can't remember its own week."

"We don't know any courier," the headman said.

Corven smiled. It was not, strictly speaking, a hostile smile. That was the worst part, the villagers would say later, if anyone asked them, which no one would, because there would be almost none of them left to ask. He smiled like a man doing sums.

"Ninety-one souls," he repeated. "Good number. Round."

* * *

He had them counted into the square by torchlight—not because counting helped, but because being counted reminded people they were countable. His sergeant, Vess, a broad woman with a burn-scarred forearm and no imagination whatsoever, read the names off the tax rolls while Corven stood at the well and listened to the sound a village makes when it understands what is happening. It is a specific sound. Not screaming. Screaming comes later, or never. It is quieter than that: the sound of ninety-one people all deciding at once not to breathe too loudly.

"The courier," Corven said again, pleasantly. "Gray cloak. Which way?"

Silence. The good kind, from his perspective. Silence meant there was something to protect, and things that were protected could be threatened, and threats were just promises with better manners.

"You know," he said to the headman, conversationally, "I burned a village very like this one eleven years ago. Harrow Fen. Do you know it? No, of course you don't. There's nothing left to know. They also knew nothing. Also had done nothing. Also had a courier pass through—a different one, a smuggler, carrying letters for people who fancied themselves patriots. I asked my questions. They gave me their silence. So I gave them fire, and by morning the truth was lying in the ashes where I could read it plainly." He tilted his head. "Do you know what the truth looked like? It looked like a man named Aldous Renn, the schoolteacher, who broke at the first smell of smoke and told me everything, and who lived, incidentally, and lives there still, keeping the school, teaching the children of the families that replaced the ones I burned. Truth keeps. People don't. That's the whole lesson of my profession, if you're interested."

The headman's face did something complicated. Corven watched it happen with genuine interest—the flicker toward the schoolteacher among them, whoever it was, the arithmetic of survival beginning behind the eyes. He did not interrupt it. Interrupting arithmetic was amateur work.

But then the headman closed his mouth, and set his feet, and said, "We know nothing, lord magistrate," and Corven felt something almost like respect, and something much more like disappointment.

"Well," he said. "Vess. The barrels."

* * *

They fired the mill first. It was the largest structure and it burned with a satisfying thoroughness, the old dry grain going up in a roar that pushed heat across the square like an open hand. Then the inn, over the innkeeper's flat stare—she did not scream either, this one, and Corven made a note of her face, because faces that didn't scream were usually faces that had already decided something, and decisions were information.

By full dark half of Lowmere was alight and the other half was lit by it, and the villagers stood in their square in the orange glare like figures in a painting of a place that no longer existed.

And then, as Corven had known it would, the arithmetic finished.

A boy came forward. Sixteen, maybe. Soot on his cheek that wasn't from the fire. "He went north," the boy said, voice cracking. "The courier. North up the shepherd's track. And there was a woman met him at the crossroads—an hour before dawn—and she took something from him and rode west, into Thornfell, and he went on north alone."

Corven crouched to the boy's level, which he knew unsettled people; magistrates were supposed to loom. "Describe the woman."

"Dark-skinned. Older. Riding like she was born on the horse. She took the road that isn't on the maps."

There it was.

Corven straightened slowly, and for a moment—for a moment only—he forgot the boy, the village, the performance. The road that isn't on the maps. The Wayfarers' paths. He had spent four months and a small fortune in bribes pulling threads out of old archives, and every thread ended the same way: *the guild kept the hidden roads, and the hidden roads led to Ashenmoor, and Ashenmoor was not supposed to exist.*

Any lawman should have stopped at *smuggler*. Smugglers were a problem with procedures. But Corven had gone past procedure years ago, back when he'd found the first document sealed with a sigil he'd been told since childhood was only a heraldic curiosity: a road drawn as a circle, open at neither end. The sigil of the Wayfarers' Guild. The sigil that also appeared, worn nearly smooth, on the oldest cornerstone of the Vale Council's own hall.

He knew what the courier had carried. He knew, better than the courier had, probably. Better than the woman. Better, perhaps, than anyone still living outside a single room in the vale where old men spoke in whispers about a covenant failing and a thirteenth moon turning.

Which raised the question that Corven had been turning over like a coin for four months now, feeling its weight, testing whether it was gold or gilt:

*Who had sent* him?

The warrant in his saddlebag bore the Council's seal, routed through three layers of provincial bureaucracy so that no name ever touched paper. Officially, he was suppressing sedition. Unofficially—unofficially, in the archive-cellar where he did his real reading—the Council wanted that map found and destroyed, and wanted the bearer silenced, and wanted no record of any of it. That was the story he'd been given.

But Corven had burned enough villages to know that the person who sends you to make a thing disappear is never telling you the whole reason the thing frightens them. And the documents he'd found hadn't read like the papers of men protecting order. They'd read like the papers of men protecting *property*. The covenant, whatever it was, whatever it bound—the Council's founders had signed it. Had built it. And somewhere in the intervening centuries, the keepers of the law had become the beneficiaries of the secret, and the secret had become the source of the benefit, and now the secret was dying, and the dying of it terrified them in a way that no mere sedition ever had.

So: either he served justice, or he served fear, or—most likely, most usefully—the distinction no longer mattered, and the only question was who reached the map-bearer first, and what they did when they found her.

"A woman," he said aloud, to Vess, to the burning village, to himself. "Riding into Thornfell on a road that isn't on the maps. Carrying something a courier bled for."

"You know her?" Vess asked.

"I know *of* her," Corven said. "Or of her kind. The last of a guild that officially died thirty years ago and unofficially never did." He looked around the square—at the mill collapsing inward in a gout of sparks, at the headman standing very straight beside his ruined livelihood, at the boy who had talked and who was now learning, in real time, what his talking had bought. "Leave them the shrine and the well," he said. "A village needs water and somewhere to pray. Everything else goes."

"That's—" Vess hesitated. "Generous, sir."

"It's efficient," Corven said. "Dead survivors breed martyrs. Ruined ones breed caution." He swung back into the saddle. "Send riders north after the courier's trail anyway. Dead ends tell us where the dead ends are. And get me every map of Thornfell in the province—I want copies, originals stay in my possession, and I want the cartographers who drew them to understand that if a single road appears on those copies that isn't on the originals, I will know, and I will visit."

"And the woman, sir? Into Thornfell? The hidden roads?" Vess said it carefully, the way you'd say *ghosts*.

Corven looked east, toward the black shoulder of the mountains, where Thornfell's forests swallowed the starlight. Somewhere in that green dark was a network of paths that existed by not existing, kept alive by a handful of dying men and women who had made invisibility into a sacrament. He almost admired it. Almost. Admiration was a luxury he permitted himself exactly as often as it cost nothing.

"She'll do what her kind always does," he said. "She'll hide. She'll run the old roads. She'll trust the ground under her feet because her grandmother trusted it." He gathered the reins. "And the ground doesn't know it's dying, Vess. That's the tragedy of inheritances. The heirs always believe the house will stand."

He put spurs to his horse and rode out of Lowmere without looking back, though he listened—listened the way he always listened—to the sound of the village burning behind him, cataloguing it, filing it, adding it to the long ledger of costs that someone, someday, might present to him for payment.

Let them present it. He had read the old documents. He knew what was coming down out of the mountains when the thirteenth moon turned, and he knew what the Council intended to do about it, and he had decided—quietly, without ceremony, the way he decided everything—that when the reckoning came, he intended to be the one holding the map.

Not the Council. Not the rebels. Not some invisible woman with her grandmother's roads.

Him.

The firelight fell away behind him, and the dark of the road ahead received him like a signature on a contract.
