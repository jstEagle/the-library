## 7. The Drowned Chapel

The cavern mouth opened in the hillside like a held breath, and Gideon stood before it the way she'd stood before every door her grandmother had ever described: with respect, and a hand on her knife.

"Down there," she said, "the water does what it wants. We go when it allows."

Isandro looked at the black slot in the rock, then at her. "You've been inside?"

"No." She said it plainly, because lying to him about small things made the large lies harder to carry. "My grandmother told me of it. The Wayfarers kept the approach maps in their heads—never written, never spoken whole. She gave me the pieces over twenty years, one at a time, like medicine dosed out so I wouldn't overdose on knowing."

"And you trusted her?"

"I trusted that she loved me," Gideon said. "It's not the same thing, but it holds weight better."

She knelt at the entrance and did what had to be done first. The guild's oldest rule was also its simplest: *ask the road before you take it.* She pressed her palm flat to the wet stone and spoke the opening words of the Road-Prayer, the one every Wayfarer learned before they learned to walk.

*Stone above, stone below, stone that remembers my name.*

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered anymore. But the words settled something in her chest, the way they always did—a small order imposed on a large dark—and she rose and began to unlace her boots.

Isandro watched her strip to her undertunic and bind her hair back with a cord. "How deep?"

"Chest-high in places. Deeper where the ceiling drops. There are three air-chambers between here and the chapel. If we get separated—" she looked at him until he met her eyes, "—you do not go forward. You go back. You climb, you breathe, you live, and you come back up and wait for me. Swear it."

"You know I can't swear that."

"You can. I've heard you lie beautifully to the ferryman at Grayford about your business. This is easier than that."

Something moved behind his eyes—not quite a smile, but the shape of one, held under the surface like a fish under ice. "Sworn," he said. "If we separate, I retreat."

"Good." She handed him a shuttered lantern and took the second for herself. "Keep it low. The air down there is old. It doesn't like fire much."

They went in.

* * *

The first hundred paces were easy, almost gentle—the kind of passage a child could walk, sloping down through stone worn smooth by centuries of water. Gideon counted her steps without meaning to; her grandmother's voice lived in the counting now, an inheritance she'd never asked for and couldn't put down. *Twelve steps to the bend. At the bend, look up. If the ceiling shines, the flood is high. If it's dry, you may pass.*

The ceiling shone.

She stopped, raised her lantern, and felt her stomach drop half an inch. Water marks gleamed overhead like old scars, a hand's width above her head.

"What is it?" Isandro murmured.

"Flood line's higher than she said it would be." Gideon turned slowly, reading the chamber the way other people read letters. "Either the rains came wrong this year, or the mountain's shifted. Both mean the same thing. The deep channel will be running fast."

"Then we turn back."

"We didn't come this far to turn back at the porch." She was already moving, testing the water with her boot—cold as a grave, moving harder than she liked. "Stay on my left. The current pulls right. And whatever you hear singing, it isn't singing."

He caught her arm. "What do you mean, singing?"

"The water through the narrows makes a sound. Grandmother called it the choir. It's just pressure and hollow stone." She pulled free gently. "But men have followed it off ledges. So: whatever you hear singing, it isn't singing."

The narrows took them an hour.

It should have taken twenty minutes. But the water ran thigh-deep then waist-deep, and twice Gideon had to wedge herself against the current and haul Isandro past a lip of rock where the floor fell away into nothing she could see. The choir started at the third bend—a high, thin thread of sound threading through the roar, almost sweet, almost like a woman's voice calling a name. Gideon fixed her eyes on the wall and walked. Beside her, she heard Isandro's breathing change, quicken, and once he drifted a half-step toward the sound before she caught his sleeve and hauled him back without a word.

Afterward, in the first air-chamber—a low dome of stone where they sat gasping in the lantern light, water streaming off them—he said, "I heard my sister."

Gideon wrung out her hair. "Dead?"

"Exiled with me. Worse, in a way. She chose to stay in the Vale when I refused the order. We haven't spoken since." He stared at the black water below them. "Her voice was very clear."

"That's what it does. It doesn't show you things. It just takes the voice you most want to hear and lends it to the water." She checked the lantern oil, hands steady, heart not. "Grandmother said the chapel was built here on purpose. That the founders wanted anyone coming to the token to arrive already grieving. Said it sorted the worthy from the greedy."

"Did she believe that?"

"She believed the founders were cleverer than us." Gideon stood. "Come on. Two more chambers."

* * *

The second air-chamber was smaller, and getting to it meant diving.

Gideon crouched at the edge of the flooded sump, lantern held high, and pointed down into water so black it looked solid. "Four body-lengths. There's a rope groove in the left wall—Wayfarers cut it, generations back. Follow it with your hand, don't let go, and come up slow. There'll be air. There'd better be air." She glanced at him. "When did you last swim?"

"In the Vale, we don't."

"Lovely. Then watch me first."

She went under without ceremony. The cold hit her like a fist, closing on her chest, and she hauled herself along the groove hand over hand, counting—one, two, three, four—and kicked upward into air that tasted of stone and centuries. She dragged herself onto a shelf barely big enough for two, coughing, and held the lantern high until Isandro's head broke the surface below her, spluttering, furious, alive.

He climbed up beside her and sat dripping, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. The silence down here was different from any silence above—thick, total, pressing on the ears. When he finally talked, he talked quietly, as if the dark might be offended by volume.

"My grandmother died when I was nine," he said. "I barely remember her voice. Yours—you said she taught you for twenty years."

"She talked until the end. Talked at me, mostly. Rules, routes, warnings." Gideon fed a little oil to the lantern flame. "The last thing she said wasn't any of those."

"What was it?"

Gideon hesitated. She hadn't told anyone this. Hadn't told Sefa, who'd earned it more than most. But the dark was enormous and patient, and something about it loosened the lock she kept on her own history.

"She said, 'When the road asks for blood, girl, make sure it tells you why.'" Isandro turned to look at her. "I thought it was just an old woman's grumbling. Now I'm standing in a drowned church under a mountain, going to fetch a thing that drank my grandmother's generation dry, and I keep hearing it."

"Maybe it was a warning."

"Everything she said was a warning. That's what made the ones that weren't stand out." She closed the lantern's shutter partway. "One more dive. Then the chapel."

* * *

The third chamber opened all at once, and even Isandro—who had crossed half the known world as a warden and claimed to be beyond astonishment—stopped walking.

They stood on a ledge of natural stone at the rim of a vast flooded hall. Stalactites hung from a ceiling lost in darkness above, and the water below lay perfectly still, mirror-flat, holding their lantern light like a bowl holds stars. And rising out of that stillness, pale and broken, were columns.

Seven of them, in a crescent. Chapel columns, carved with figures worn nearly smooth—processions of small people, walking, always walking, around and around the stone. Between two of the columns, half-submerged, an altar showed its edge above the waterline like the prow of a sunken ship.

"There," Gideon said, and her voice came out hushed without her deciding it. "That's where she knelt. She told me—she said she could put her hand flat on the altar stone, and the water came to her elbows, and she was sixteen, and she was terrified, and she did it anyway."

"Where's the token?"

"Under the altar. In a socket beneath it. The founders built it so you couldn't take the token without kneeling—without putting yourself lower than the thing you're asking for." She began to work off her boots again, though she'd never really dried. "Grandmother said that was the whole theology of the guild in one piece of masonry. You serve the road. The road doesn't serve you."

Isandro set his lantern on the ledge, and its light reached only so far before the dark took it back. "I'll come with you."

"No." She said it before she'd decided to. "You stay. Not because I don't trust you." She looked at him, and found, to her irritation, that she meant it—that somewhere between the waystation and the narrows, trust had grown in her like moss grows in a cellar, silently and against all sense. "Because if something goes wrong down there, someone has to carry word out. That's not sentiment. That's tactics."

"It's both," he said quietly. "Go."

She slid into the water.

The cold was total now, a whole-body fact, and the depth was worse than she'd guessed—her feet found no bottom for three terrible strokes, and she swam blind through blackness that pressed on her from every side, toward the pale shape of the altar growing in her lantern's glow. Her grandmother had been sixteen. Gideon was thirty-one, and terrified, and doing it anyway. Some inheritances skip nothing.

Her feet found stone. Shallow here, blessedly—the altar platform rose from the depths, and she stood on it gasping, water to her ribs, the columns rising around her like witnesses. Up close she could see the carvings better: the small processional figures, and above them, worn but legible, larger shapes. Hands. Dozens of carved hands, reaching down from the capitals of the columns, open-palmed, giving or taking—it was impossible to say which.

*Ask the road,* she thought, absurdly, and put her hand flat on the altar.

The stone was warm.

Not sun-warm, not body-warm. Warm like a living thing, like the flank of a horse, and Gideon snatched her hand back with a sound she was not proud of. The warmth stayed. And beneath it, faint as a heartbeat under a bandage, she felt a pulse.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, you're awake."

The socket was where her grandmother had said—beneath the altar's lip, reachable only by kneeling, by putting her head below the waterline, by making herself small in front of seven carved columns and whatever slept between them. Gideon knelt. The water closed over her ears and the world went silent except for her own heartbeat, which suddenly sounded less alone than it should have.

She felt along the underside of the altar. Stone, stone, stone—and then metal. A ring, cold despite everything, set into a bronze plate. She braced, filled her lungs, and pulled.

The token came free with a sound like a struck bell, muffled by the water, felt more than heard—a single deep note that went through the stone, through the water, through her bones. She surfaced gasping and held it up into the lantern light.

It was small. A disc of grey metal, no bigger than a coin, stamped with the sign of the Wayfarers' Guild: the forked road. She'd seen the mark a thousand times—branded on crates, tattooed on her grandmother's wrist, burned into the guildhall door. She had never once wondered why a smugglers' guild carried a sigil old enough to be worn smooth by veneration rather than use.

Blood ran down her forearm—from a scrape she didn't remember taking, a thin red line drawn by the altar's edge—and dripped onto the token.

The token drank it.

There was no other word. The drop touched the grey metal and vanished, and the disc warmed in her palm, and the forked-road sigil began to move—the two roads of the mark separating, unwinding, becoming a map. Lines spread across the metal's face like frost blooming on glass: roads, passes, fords, a web of hidden ways spanning two realms, drawn in light that came from nowhere and needed no lantern. And at the center of the web, where all lines converged, a point of darkness pulsed in time with her own heart.

Ashenmoor. She knew it without being told. She knew it the way she knew her own name.

"Gideon." Isandro's voice, tight, from the ledge. "The water."

She looked up. The flooded hall had begun to move—not waves, nothing so crude. The entire surface had begun to turn, slowly, a vast wheel of black water rotating around the altar platform, and the carved hands on the columns seemed—she would swear this later, under oath if anyone had asked—to have shifted. Lower. Closer. Open palms now angled toward her like a dozen patient beggars.

And the pulse in the stone beneath her knees had quickened to match her own.

*When the road asks for blood, girl, make sure it tells you why.*

The token cooled in her hand. The map faded back to plain grey metal, the sigil settling into its familiar forked shape, innocent as a coin. The water stilled. The hands were just carvings again, worn by time, meaning nothing.

But her blood was still on the altar, a small dark smear, and as she watched, the stone drew that in too—slowly, thoroughly, leaving the surface clean as a tomb.

Gideon swam back to the ledge on arms that shook, and let Isandro haul her out, and sat dripping and shivering with the token clenched in her fist.

"Well?" he said.

"It knows me." She opened her hand and looked at the little disc, at the forked road, at twenty years of her grandmother's warnings rearranging themselves around it into a shape she didn't like at all. "It knows my blood. Isandro—the guild didn't guard the covenant. We're *part* of it. The tokens aren't keys we carry."

She looked up at him, and the lantern light caught the water still streaming down her face, so that he might have thought she was crying, and she let him think it.

"They're pieces of us. And something down here just took its first payment."

Behind them, far back through the flooded narrows, faint and impossible, the choir began to sing again—and this time, Gideon could have sworn, it was singing her name.
