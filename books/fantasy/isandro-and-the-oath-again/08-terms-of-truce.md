## 8. Terms of Truce

The fire was a mistake. Isandro knew it the moment Gideon built it—small, shielded in a hollow of stone, smoke funneled through a crack in the rock like she'd done it a thousand times, which she probably had—but he let her do it anyway. Two days out from the drowned chapel, with the token wrapped in oilcloth against his chest and her blood still dried under her fingernails where the thing had bitten her, they were both too tired to be careful.

"You first," she said.

They'd been circling it since the chapel. The questions neither asked. He'd watched her carry them the way she carried everything—lightly, on the outside, like weight was a rumor. Now she sat across the fire with her boots off and her knife across her knees, sharpening it in slow strokes, and looked at him with those gray eyes that gave away nothing except when she decided to give away everything.

"First what?"

"Whatever it is you're not saying." She turned the blade. "You've been carrying something since before we met. I can see it in how you sit. Like a man with a stone in his boot who's decided the stone is part of him now."

Isandro fed a twig into the fire. Watched it catch. There were answers he could give that were true and useless—the Council's orders, the map's recovery, the terms of his commission—all of it paper, all of it armor. She'd already seen through all of it. That was the trouble with guides. They read terrain, and people were terrain.

"The Vale Council exiled me," he said. "Four years ago."

The whetstone paused. Resumed.

"For what?"

"Disobedience." He almost laughed at how small it sounded. Four years of cold, four years of watching Thornfell from the outside like a ghost pressed against a window, and the whole architecture of his ruin came down to one word. "There's a village called Merrow's Bend. On the vale side of the border mountains. Poor place. Fishing, some terraced grain, a shrine nobody important ever visited. When the last famine came, the Council calculated the supply routes and decided the bend was a rounding error. They ordered the granaries sealed. Officially it was quarantine. Unofficially—" He shrugged. "Unofficially there was no unofficial version. There never is. Just an order, signed, with a seal on it, and a line of arithmetic underneath explaining why two hundred souls didn't fit in the column."

"What did you do?"

"I opened the granaries." He said it flatly, because anything else would have been worse. "I was warden of the eastern marches. I had the authority. I used it, and I wrote my name on the requisition so no one else could be blamed, and I fed two hundred people through a winter the Council had decided they wouldn't survive. And in the spring, when the roads cleared, they took my title, my house, my name off the rolls, and they put me on a boat going nowhere, and they told me I could come back when I'd remembered what obedience cost."

Gideon had stopped sharpening the knife. She was just looking at him now, the blade forgotten across her knees, and the firelight did something complicated to her face that he couldn't read and didn't want to guess at.

"And now they've taken you back," she said slowly. "To fetch this map. To silence whoever holds it."

"To retrieve the covenant's property," he corrected, and heard how thin it sounded even as he said it. "Silence was always discretionary."

"Was it." Not a question. She picked the whetstone back up, but gently now, like the knife had stopped being the point. "So they exiled you for feeding starving people, and then they handed you a warrant to kill a stranger, and you took it. Because coming home was worth that."

"Yes," Isandro said. Because lying to her had stopped being possible somewhere on the switchback, or in the water, or maybe the first moment she'd gotten the drop on him at the waystation and offered him tea instead of a throat. "That's the truth of it. I told myself it was duty. I'm good at telling myself things. It's the warden's whole craft—you take an order apart until you find the shape of it you can live inside, and then you live inside it, and eventually you forget the order had any other shape."

"And did you? Forget?"

He thought about Merrow's Bend. About a fisherman's wife pressing a heel of dark bread into his hands at the dock, weeping without sound, and how he'd eaten it in front of her because refusing would have been cruelty dressed up as modesty. About the boat going nowhere. About four years of learning to sleep on the ground.

"No," he said. "I remember every day. That's why I'm sitting at your fire instead of cutting your throat in your sleep, which is what the warrant actually says. I keep waiting for the part of me that obeys to wake up and do its job. It hasn't. I'm starting to suspect it won't."

* * *

She didn't answer right away. She banked the fire instead, coaxing it down to a red murmur, and Isandro understood that whatever came next was going to cost her, because everything about the way she moved said she was arranging herself for it—turning her body toward him, setting the knife down within reach but not in hand, choosing the ground before the fight.

"My grandmother raised me," Gideon said. "In the guild hall at Hollowmere, the one you saw the seal of. My mother died on the roads—guide's death, flash flood, very ordinary, very final. My father I never met. So it was the two of us, and the hall, and the other wayfarers drifting through between runs, fewer every year." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "You want to know why I stay invisible. Why the guild kept itself secret even after it stopped mattering to anyone."

"If you're willing."

"It wasn't secrecy for safety. Not at first." Her voice went quiet, even, the voice of someone reciting something learned by heart long ago. "My grandmother told me once—we were mending nets on the porch, I was maybe ten—that the Wayfarers weren't hidden because we were weak. We were hidden because we were *useful*. Every realm, every magistrate, every council and crown and rebel cell in these mountains has needed, at some point, to move something or someone without being seen. Armies. Fugitives. Plague doctors. Money. Messages that start wars if they're read by the wrong eyes. And every one of them, sooner or later, tried to own us. Because whoever owns the invisible roads owns the mountains."

The fire ticked. Somewhere down the slope, a night bird called and got no answer.

"So the founders made a choice," she went on. "Better to be no one's weapon than everyone's. They buried the roads. They taught their children to walk unseen, to build fires like mine, to leave no tracks worth reading. And they paid for it. The guild shrank. Clients forgot we existed. Kin married out or died out. By the time I was born there were eleven of us. Now—" she held up her hand, folded fingers down one by one, "six. Maybe five. I haven't heard from Tamsin since midwinter and Tamsin doesn't go quiet."

"That's why the courier found you," Isandro said. "Not despite the hiding. Because of it."

"He was guild-trained. Old marks on the map, old hand-signs. He was dying and he needed someone the realms had forgotten, because someone the realms have forgotten can't be owned." She looked at him across the embers. "You understand what that makes you, warden? You're the first person in twenty years who's stood where I could see you and known what I am. And you came with a warrant."

"I know."

"Do you?" Something moved behind her eyes—not anger, or not only anger. "Because here's my problem, Isandro Vael. Everything in me says you're the trap. Exiled warden, convenient conscience, sad story about bread—what better bait than a man whose shame matches my loneliness? I feed you my trust and one morning I wake up bound and gagged and the map's gone back to the Council, and they'll burn it, and whatever my grandmother spent her life keeping will end in a brazier in some quiet room."

"Yes," he said again. "That's exactly what I'd do, if I were them. It's a good plan. I'd believe me too."

"But?"

"But I opened the granaries." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough to the coals that he felt their breath on his face. "And here is the thing I've worked out in four years of exile, Gideon, which is that obedience and duty are not the same animal. The Council taught me they were. My grandmother—" he caught himself; she huffed something that was almost a laugh, "—my commanding officer, rather, taught me otherwise, the year before I lost everything. Duty is what you owe. Obedience is what you're told you owe. The distance between them is where every honest person lives or dies." He held her gaze. "I don't know what the covenant does. I don't know why the Council wants it dead. But I know they sent me with a silence clause, and I know what they did to Merrow's Bend, and I know that institutions which round people down to arithmetic don't stop at two hundred souls. Whatever they're afraid of in that map—I'm starting to want to know it more than I want my house back."

Gideon was silent a long time. The embers settled. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—lower, stripped of the guide's easy competence, just a tired woman on a mountain talking to the only other soul in reach.

"When my grandmother was dying," she said, "she held my hand and she said, *keep the roads, little bird, and the roads will keep you.* And then she said the second part, the part I've never told anyone. She said, *and forgive us, because we hid so well we hid the reason too.*" Gideon's jaw tightened. "I've spent my whole life thinking that meant the guild failed. Lost the plot. Kept the walls and threw away the house. But standing in that chapel with the token burning my blood, I felt it—the reason. It's still down there. Under everything. And I think the Council knows what it is, and I think that's why they want it burned."

"Then we want the same thing," Isandro said.

"We want the same thing *today*." But she was smiling, faintly, crookedly, the first smile he'd seen from her that wasn't armor. "Tomorrow you might get new orders."

"I'm four years past taking orders."

"Everyone says that right up until they do." She reached over and nudged his boot with hers—a small touch, deliberate, the guide's equivalent of a handshake. "Truce, then. You lead me to what the Council knows, I lead you to what the guild kept. Equal weights. And if either of us breaks it—"

"I know how the sentence goes. I've written enough of them."

"Good." She lay back on her bedroll, arms behind her head, studying the strip of stars above the hollow. "Sleep, warden. Dawn's early and the road ahead doesn't exist yet. I have to go make it."

He should have slept. Instead he watched the fire die and listened to her breathing even out, and understood, with the calm horror of a man watching floodwater rise past a mark cut into a bridge piling, that something in him had shifted and was not going to shift back. He'd crossed into Thornfell carrying his disgrace like a stone, certain it was the heaviest thing he owned.

It wasn't anymore. The heavy thing was sitting three feet away, sleeping with her knife in reach, trusting him exactly as far as she could throw him and no farther—and he wanted, with a ferocity that had nothing to do with warrants or covenants, to deserve it.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold. Isandro woke to the smell of tea and found Gideon already packed, standing at the mouth of the hollow, looking east.

"There's a road," she said, without turning. "Well—there isn't. That's the point of it. An old guild run, three days, ends at a market town called Grayford. There's a bridge there, and beyond the bridge, country where the Council's writ runs thin and rebels run thick." She glanced back at him. "If we're going to learn what your masters broke, we'll need help doing it. And the only people in these mountains who hate the Council more than I do are the people who've been fighting it in the open."

Isandro felt the warning prickle along his spine before his mind caught up to it. "Rebels."

"Sefa runs the eastern cells." Gideon stepped out of the hollow into the pale light, and for a moment she was silhouetted against the sunrise, all edges and motion, a woman made of roads. "She taught me half of what I know. She also thinks I've been dead for six years, which gives us a day's grace before the questions start." She smiled that crooked smile again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Come on, warden. Let's go introduce you to my oldest friend—and pray to whatever's left of the covenant that she never learns what you're carrying."

She started down the slope, quick and sure, walking a path Isandro's eyes insisted was bare rock and scree. He followed. Behind them the fire's ashes were already cold, and ahead the mountain opened onto a road that wasn't there, going somewhere neither of them had agreed to yet.
