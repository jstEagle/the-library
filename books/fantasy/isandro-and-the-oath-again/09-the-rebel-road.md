## 9. The Rebel Road

The trouble with old friends is that they know your habits.

Gideon had taken the low road through the Kestrel Woods precisely because it was the one road she never took—too soft underfoot, too many sightlines, too much sky. But Sefa had known her for eleven years, had drunk her tea and slept in her lofts and learned to read her the way Gideon read terrain. And so when the net dropped out of the alder canopy thirty miles from Grayford, weighted with river stones and strung on smuggler's line, Gideon understood, even as she threw herself sideways, that this was not an ambush.

It was a letter.

"Down!" Isandro shouted, but he was already down, flat against the moss with his blade half-drawn, and the second net took him across the shoulders like a striking hawk. He went into the ferns hard. Gideon rolled, came up with her knife out, and found the woods full of people.

Not magistrate riders. These moved like she moved—quiet, distributed, dressed in browns that weren't quite brown. She counted eight before she stopped counting, which was six more than she should have missed.

"Sefa," she said to the trees. "You're getting sloppy. I heard the third one breathing."

A laugh from the ridge above, warm and rough as ever. Then Sefa came down the slope the way she always did, like the mountain owed her passage: long stride, gray-streaked braid, a scar through one eyebrow that Gideon herself had stitched shut with fishing line in a waystation outside Marrow's Ford, back when the world was simpler and the two of them were only running salt and secrets.

"Gideon Okafor." Sefa spread her arms. "Last of the Wayfarers. Do you know what people pay me for news of you?"

"Less than you paid me for that coat you're wearing."

"It kept me alive through two winters." Sefa stopped an arm's length away, and the warmth in her face banked itself down to something harder. "Your friend there is Vale-made. I can see it in how he falls—trained falling, expensive boots. So before we get comfortable, you're going to tell me why the last Wayfarer guide is walking my woods with a warden of Ashenmoor."

* * *

They made camp in a hollow the rebels had clearly used before—fire ring already built, smoke channeled through a split log so it came out cold and thin. Efficient. Gideon sat with her hands around a tin cup she didn't drink from and watched Sefa's people watch Isandro, who sat bound at the wrists with a patience that frightened her more than struggle would have. He was listening. Cataloguing. She could see him doing it—the count of guards, the weight of the fire, the angle of the treeline—and she knew that if he decided to move, some of these people would die, and then everything would come apart at once.

"So," Sefa said, settling beside her. "Talk."

"You first. Since when do you run forty fighters?"

"Since Corven burned Hollowmere." The name landed between them like a stone in still water. Sefa's voice stayed level, but her thumb pressed white against the tin cup. "Sixty-one souls. He gave them until dawn to hand over a courier who'd already passed through, and when they told the truth—that he was gone—he made the truth expensive. I buried children, Gid. I stopped being a smuggler that day."

Gideon looked into the fire. She had heard about Hollowmere. Everyone on the roads had heard about Hollowmere. She had also heard the rumor underneath the rumor: that the courier Corven wanted had been carrying something, and that something had ended up in the hands of a dying man on a high pass who'd pressed it into hers with blood-slick fingers.

"The map," Sefa said.

Gideon's spine went cold. "What map?"

"Don't." Sefa turned and looked at her, and eleven years of friendship looked back, steady and unblinking. "The courier who died on the pass. The one whose body you hid in a cairn under the Widow's Tooth—I know, because I have people on every pass, and one of them saw you coming down with blood on your sleeve and something wrapped in oilcloth. Word travels the roads, Gid. You taught us that. You just forgot you taught it to everyone."

The fire popped. Across the hollow, Isandro's eyes were on her—not warning, not pleading. Just watching. Letting her choose.

"What do you want with it?" Gideon asked.

"The same thing you want. An end to Corven. An end to the Council that gives men like him their seal and calls it law." Sefa leaned closer. "That map leads to Ashenmoor's heart. To whatever the Council has been hoarding behind their quiet doors while Thornfell starves and burns. If I put that in the open—if every village, every road-family, every hungry soul sees where the wealth is locked away—we don't need to beg the Vale for justice. We take it."

"And then what? Every ambitious fool with a sword marches on the vale?"

"Then the people own it instead of a council of ghosts. Yes."

Gideon looked at her oldest friend and felt the lie assembling itself in her throat like a stone she'd have to swallow whole. Because Sefa was wrong, but not stupid-wrong—she was wrong the way good people are wrong, believing the world is a lock and freedom is the key. Gideon had knelt in a drowned chapel and watched a token answer her blood. She had read enough of the map's shifting seals to understand that the covenant wasn't treasure. It was a dam. And Sefa wanted to breach it in front of a crowd.

She thought of her grandmother's voice: *Some oaths aren't chains, child. Some are the only thing holding two wild things apart.*

"I don't have it anymore," Gideon said.

Sefa went very still.

"I sold it," Gideon continued, and hated how easily the words came, how smooth eleven years of smuggling had made her tongue. "In Marrow's Ford. To a broker working for—" she flicked her eyes toward Isandro— "his people. That's why he's here. Not hunting me. Confirming delivery. The Council has the map now, Sefa. I'm just the guide they kept on retainer to walk them in when they're ready."

Silence. One of the rebels by the treeline shifted. Sefa stared at her, and Gideon held the stare the way she held a switchback in fog—one careful step at a time, no looking down.

"That's not nothing," Sefa said slowly. "That's everything. If the Council has it—"

"Then taking it back means going into the vale. Which means you need someone who knows the ways in." Gideon set down the cup. "I can get your people to the vale's edge, Sefa. But I won't hand you a weapon you don't understand. Not yet. First I want to see what we're walking toward. Give me a week. Meet me at Grayford Bridge with twenty of your steadiest, and if what I find is what you think it is, I'll open every door I know."

It was a good lie. It had a true shape—a meeting place, a timeline, a promise of partnership. It was built exactly the way she'd been taught to build cover stories: mostly true, generous where it mattered, impossible to verify quickly. Her grandmother would have been proud. Her grandmother would also have broken her wrist for it.

Sefa studied her for a long moment. Then she laughed, short and without much humor. "Still the best liar on the mountain."

"Still the worst at enjoying it."

"Mm." Sefa rose, nodded to her people, and Isandro's bindings came off—though she noticed they left his blade confiscated, and noticed he let them, filing that away too. "Grayford Bridge. Seven days. Bring your warden if you must, but Gideon—if you're lying to me, if you've gone over to the Vale—"

"I haven't gone anywhere," Gideon said. "I'm still standing on the same ground I've always stood on. It's the ground that keeps moving."

Sefa smiled at that, the old smile, and for one heartbeat Gideon almost told her everything. Almost. The word *Ashenmoor* rose in her throat and she swallowed it with the rest.

* * *

They walked out of the rebel camp at dusk, escorted to the treeline by four armed women who were polite the way drawn bows are polite. When the woods finally released them onto a sheep track heading south, Isandro waited until the last of them was out of earshot.

"You lied well," he said.

"Don't."

"I mean it as craft, not compliment." He flexed his hands, working feeling back into his fingers. "But you should know the flaw in it. You told her the Council has the map. If Sefa believes that, she won't wait seven days. She'll move against the vale immediately—or worse, she'll go looking for proof, and she'll find the broker in Marrow's Ford who never bought anything."

Gideon stopped walking. The sheep track wound down ahead of them through bracken gone gold, and somewhere below, a river ran cold and dark toward Grayford.

"I know," she said quietly.

"Then why—"

"Because the truth was worse." She started walking again, faster, arms tight around herself against the evening chill. "If I'd told her what the covenant actually is, she'd have called it Vale propaganda—warden's talk, designed to keep the commons from the vale's door. Or she'd have believed me, and then she'd have understood that the only thing standing between two realms tearing each other apart is a bloodline, and she'd have looked at me differently forever. Either way I lose her. This way I might only lose her later."

Isandro was silent for several steps. "And if she attacks the vale with twenty fighters against a Council that wants the covenant dead anyway?"

"Then I'll have bought us a week with a lie that costs lives." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, and pushed through. "Welcome to the roads, warden. This is what invisibility costs. Somebody pays, and it's never the invisible one."

He caught her arm, gentle, and she stopped. In the failing light his face was unreadable, but his grip was careful, and that undid her more than anger would have.

"My order once asked me to let a village starve," he said, "because feeding it would have revealed a supply route. I refused, and it cost me everything. So believe me when I tell you I know the arithmetic you're doing. And I'm telling you it doesn't balance. It never balances. You just choose which debt you'll carry."

"Then help me carry this one," she said, "and stop lecturing."

They walked on as the dark came up out of the valley floor, two liars and a stolen secret, and behind them the Kestrel Woods held its breath.

It was near midnight, making cold camp in the ruins of a shepherd's bothy, when Gideon unrolled the map by ember-light to check their route to Grayford—and went rigid.

The third seal, which had shown a closed gate since the pass, had changed. The ink had crawled overnight into a new shape: a bridge, rendered in fine black lines, spanning a river. Beneath it, in her grandmother's cramped hand, three words that had not been there yesterday:

*Do not trust.*
