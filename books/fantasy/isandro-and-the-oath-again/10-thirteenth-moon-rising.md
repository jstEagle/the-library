## 10. Thirteenth Moon Rising

The hermit lived at the top of the world, or close enough to it that the air tasted like iron and the stars came down to argue.

Isandro climbed the last stretch of the Stair of Crows with Gideon a half-step ahead of him, which was how she always walked—never behind, never beside, always slightly ahead, as if the mountain might forget she'd earned the right to lead if she ever let him pass her. Three days out of Grayford, two days since the bridge, and neither of them had said much. Grief had its own pace. He knew that from experience. You didn't hurry it. You just kept walking until it stopped walking with you.

The hermit's tower wasn't a tower so much as a stone finger someone had dared to stand upright against the wind. It leaned. It groaned. A light burned in the window at the top, and below it, scratched into the rock in letters older than any alphabet Isandro knew, ran a single line:

*We count so they don't have to.*

Gideon read it twice. "Charming hospitality," she said, but her voice was thin. She hadn't slept. Neither of them had. The dead didn't sleep well in the company of the living.

Isandro knocked on the door with the flat of his fist, the way you knocked when you wanted whoever was inside to know you weren't afraid of them but were willing to pretend otherwise.

The door opened on a woman who looked like winter had been using her as a whetstone for sixty years. Her eyes were pale as frost glass, and they went first to Gideon, then to Isandro's warden's sigil—the one he still wore because taking it off felt like admitting something he wasn't ready to admit—and then back to Gideon.

"You're the last one," the hermit said. Not a question.

"I'm the last *guide*," Gideon said. "There's a difference."

"There used to be." The hermit stepped aside. "Come in before the wind decides it owns you. It usually wins."

* * *

Inside, the tower was one room stacked with instruments that made no sense to Isandro: brass rings nested inside brass rings, lenses ground so thin he could see his own distorted face in them, charts pinned to every wall covered in ink lines that spiraled inward like things drowning. And everywhere, everywhere, moons. Drawn moons. Carved moons. Moons cut from tin and bone and slate and hung from the ceiling on wires, thirteen of them, turning slowly in some draft he couldn't find, casting small restless shadows across the floor.

"Vessia," the hermit said, tapping her own sternum. "I was an astronomer at the Vale Observatory, before I asked one question too many."

"What question?" Gideon asked.

"*Why thirteen?*" Vessia said. "Everyone else was content to count twelve and call the thirteenth a calendar error. A rounding mistake in the old reckoning. But the thirteenth moon doesn't make mistakes. It makes *appointments*." She crossed to a chart, pulled a pin, and let a length of paper unroll down the wall to the floor. It was a sky map, decades deep, moon-tracks inked in overlapping generations of hand. "You came about the covenant. Don't bother denying it. Nobody climbs the Stair of Crows for my conversation."

"We need to know what happens," Isandro said. "When the thirteenth moon completes its turn."

Vessia looked at him for a long moment. Something in her expression shifted—not softer, exactly. More precise. The way a blade gets more precise.

"So you *do* know what it is," she said. "The Council sent you with a map and a silence order, and somewhere between there and here somebody told you enough to be frightened. Good. Frightened men ask better questions." She turned to Gideon. "And you. Do you know what your guild built?"

"I'm learning," Gideon said. "Slowly. Against my will."

"That's how all real knowledge arrives." Vessia took down one of the hanging moons—a slate disc, chipped at the edge—and held it up to the lamplight. "Then listen, both of you, because I'll only say this once and I'm old enough to mean it.

"The realms were not always separate. Thornfell and Ashenmoor were one country once—one land, one people, one sky. The Sundering wasn't a war or a plague. It was a *wound*. Someone tore the vale out of the world and folded it away, and the tearing left edges, and edges bleed. The covenant isn't a law. It's a *stitch*. The oathkeepers' blood is the thread. As long as a keeper renews the oath each cycle, the seam holds and the two realms stay strangers who share a border without knowing it.

"But the thirteenth moon—" she set the slate disc spinning on her palm, "—is the counterweight. It doesn't belong to either realm. It belongs to the *seam itself*. Once every great age, it completes a full turn through both skies, and for one night the stitch loosens on purpose. That night is the renewal. That night, the keeper stands at the heart of Ashenmoor and pays the price and ties the knot again. If the keeper comes, the worlds stay apart another age. If the keeper doesn't come—"

She let the disc fall. It hit the floor and spun, wobbling, slowing, and Isandro found himself watching it with his heart going hard against his ribs, because he already knew how it ended. Everything in this story ended the same way lately.

"—the seam opens," Vessia finished. "Not all at once. First it leaks. Magic bleeds out of the vale into Thornfell, wild and unbound, and Thornfell bleeds back—its forests, its dead, its hungry old gods that everyone politely pretends are folklore. The borderlands go first. Then everything within a hundred miles of them. Within a generation, there aren't two realms anymore. There's one roiling mess where nothing that lives in either world survives for long." She picked up the disc. "The thirteenth moon completes its turn in nineteen days."

Silence. The little tin moons turned overhead on their wires, patient as vultures.

"Nineteen days," Gideon repeated. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut against something enormous. "And the tokens? The ones on the map?"

"The keys to the covenant's heart. Without them, even a willing keeper can't reach the rite." Vessia's frost-pale eyes moved between them. "How many do you have?"

"One," Gideon said. "We lost the second at Grayford Bridge."

"Then you have nineteen days, one key, and half a map." Vessia almost smiled. "Your grandmother would have been proud. She worked with less."

Isandro's head came around. "You knew Maren Okafor?"

"I counted stars with her, forty years ago, the last time the thirteenth moon began its turn." Vessia's smile faded entirely. "She stood in this room and asked me the same question you just asked. What happens if the keeper doesn't come. And then she went down the mountain and spent the rest of her life making sure her granddaughter would be able to come. Ask yourself why, warden. Ask yourself why a guild that spent three centuries hiding its roads suddenly started teaching one child *everything*."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the tower's base, because descending the Stair of Crows in the dark was the kind of decision that made other decisions irrelevant. Gideon took first watch. Isandro lay on his back on bare stone and did not sleep, and after an hour he gave up pretending and sat up, and found Gideon watching him across the cold ashes of their fire.

"You figured it out on the stairs," she said quietly. "Before Vessia even said it. I saw your face change."

He should have lied. Duty said lie. The Council said lie. Every rule he'd carried across the border like a stone in his chest said lie, keep the mission clean, retrieve the map, manage the asset.

But the bridge was two days behind him. Two of Gideon's kin were under cairns on the ridge above Grayford, and he'd helped stack the stones, and the youngest of them—Dara, who'd laughed at his city boots—had died holding a rebel's knife away from Gideon's throat. Duty had been very quiet since the bridge. Duty had nothing useful to say.

"The Council knew the date," he said. "They've known for years. The observatory records are sealed—I assumed it was routine classification. It wasn't. They knew exactly when the turn completes, and they've known long enough to prepare, and they've done—" he heard his own voice go strange, "—nothing. No evacuation of the borderlands. No contingency. No succession of keepers. Nothing."

Gideon was silent. Waiting. She was good at that; she'd told him once that a road taught you patience whether you wanted to learn it or not.

"And then I thought about the orders themselves," Isandro went on. His hands were cold. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather. "*Recover the map. Silence the bearer.* I thought it was about secrecy. Controlling the covenant. But it isn't, Gideon. Think it through. If the Council wanted the covenant renewed, they'd need a keeper—and the only keeper left is the woman carrying the map. Instead of escorting her to Ashenmoor, they ordered me to erase her. They're not trying to control the renewal." He made himself say it plainly, because saying it plainly was the only way to make himself believe it. "They're not trying to prevent it either. They're just... clearing the board. Removing every variable. So that when the moon finishes its turn, whatever happens happens, and no one can trace it back to them."

"Why?" Gideon's voice was very soft. "Why would they want the seam to open? Vessia said nothing survives it."

"Nothing in the *borderlands*, maybe. But think about what bleeds *out* first. Wild magic. Unbound. The vale has been hoarding it for generations—that's what the Council does, that's what they've always done, they ration magic like grain and call it stewardship. An open seam means the hoard spills. Whoever's positioned closest to the breach—" He stopped. Shook his head. "No. Even that's too generous. Maybe they're not positioned at all. Maybe they've simply decided that the vale's survival and theirs aren't the same thing, and they'd rather rule a dying realm than share a living one. I don't know. I've sat in those quiet rooms my whole life and I genuinely don't know which it is, and that might be the worst part."

"The worst part," Gideon said, "is that you're surprised."

That landed harder than it should have. He looked at her. In the starlight her face was all angles and patience, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse.

"You grew up outside the walls," he said. "You learned this early. I was born inside. I was raised to believe the Council was the reason the vale survived at all. Discipline, secrecy, sacrifice—they *taught* us it was sacrifice. Every hard order was a burden someone had to carry for the common good." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I believed it so completely that when they exiled me, my first thought wasn't anger. It was shame. I'd failed *them*. I refused to let Karthen Vale starve, and I lay awake for a year feeling like the failure was mine."

"It wasn't."

"No," he said. "It wasn't. I know that now. It took me eleven years and a bridge and two graves to know it, but I know it." He stared at the dead fire. "Here's what I can't get past, Gideon. My whole life, duty was the ground I stood on. It didn't matter if it hurt. It mattered that it was *true*—that serving the Council meant serving the vale. And tonight I found out the ground was never ground. It was a floor they built over a hole, and they've known about the hole the entire time, and they let me—"

His voice failed him. He was vaguely horrified. He was a warden; wardens did not crack open on mountainsides in front of smugglers. But Gideon didn't look away, and she didn't offer comfort either, and somehow that was better. She just sat with him in it, the way you sit with someone at a graveside. Present. Unhurried. Letting the thing be as large as it actually was.

"So what's under the ground?" she asked finally. "If the duty was never real. What's left when you take it away?"

Isandro considered the question honestly, which was more than he'd managed in eleven years.

"A man," he said. "Just a man, with a sword he knows how to use and a door-key ring he stole off a corpse and—" he glanced at her, "—a growing suspicion that the only orders worth following are the ones I give myself."

"That's not nothing," Gideon said. "That's actually the first honest thing anyone from the vale has ever said to me."

"Give me time. I'll say worse."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through her, brief and exhausted, and then it was gone, and she was looking east toward the black line of the mountains, toward Ashenmoor, nineteen days and one token short of everything.

"They'll send someone else after me," she said. "Now that you've gone soft. Corven doesn't stop. Sefa has my second token and a grudge. And in nineteen days the sky finishes counting." She flexed her bandaged hand—the one that had bled on the drowned chapel's altar, the one the token had *answered*—and Isandro watched her do it and understood, finally, why Vessia had looked at her the way she had. Not like a guide. Like a keystone.

"Gideon," he said. "What did the token feel like? When it responded to your blood. Tell me the truth."

She was quiet long enough that the wind filled the space. When she answered, her voice had gone somewhere he hadn't heard before—past fear, past defiance, into a flat clear country beyond both.

"Like being *recognized*," she said. "Like something enormous opened one eye, looked at me, and said, 'Ah. There you are. We've been waiting.' " She closed her fist. "My grandmother knew. Vessia's right—Maren taught me everything because she knew I'd have to walk into that heart someday and pay whatever it asks. She spent her whole life aiming me at it like an arrow." A breath. "And the Council spent theirs making sure the arrow would never arrive."

Below them, far down the mountain, a light moved along the valley road. Small. Steady. Wrong for this hour, wrong for this country, moving with the unhurried confidence of someone who owned every road it touched.

Isandro was on his feet before he'd decided to stand.

"That's not a patrol," he said. "Patrols travel in pairs. That's one rider, and he's coming up the Stair."

Gideon rose beside him, and the exhaustion fell off her like a cloak, and underneath it was the woman who had out-negotiated a warden over a single fire.

"Corven?" she asked.

"Corven doesn't ride alone," Isandro said. "Corven sends people ahead of him." He watched the light climb, patient as the tide. "Which means whoever that is, they got here first—and they knew we'd come to the astronomer."

Gideon's hand went to the map at her breast, and the map—Isandro saw it clearly in the starlight, and his stomach dropped—was glowing faintly through the leather. A new line of ink, seeping up from beneath the old paths like blood through a bandage, drawing itself toward the tower door.

Toward the hermit's stair.

Toward them.

"She sealed a warning into it," Gideon whispered. "Grandmother. 'A path only I can walk.'" Her eyes lifted to his, wide and lit by the map's pale rising glow. "Isandro—it's not showing me a way *out*."
