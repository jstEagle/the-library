## 11. Betrayal at Grayford Bridge

**Gideon**

The message came by sparrow, which should have been the first warning. Sefa's people didn't use sparrows—they used crows, loud and obvious, because Sefa believed fear worked better than subtlety. A sparrow meant someone was trying to be quiet about it.

*Grayford Bridge, moonrise. Bring the warden. We have the second token.*

Gideon read it three times by the light of a shuttered lantern in the loft of a sheep barn two valleys east of where they'd slept. Isandro watched her from the hay, arms crossed over his knees, his face doing that thing it did when he was thinking hard and pretending not to—going very still, very flat, like a pond freezing over.

"You don't like it," he said.

"I don't like anything before dawn." She folded the note into her boot. "But no. I don't like it."

"Because?"

"Because Sefa doesn't send sparrows." She swung down from the loft, landing soft on packed straw. "And because we already *have* a route to Grayford. Three of them. She knows at least one. If she wanted to meet us, she'd have named one of mine."

Isandro came down after her, heavier, less graceful—a man built for garrison floors, not barn lofts. "Maybe she's being careful."

"Sefa has never been careful in her life. I once watched her drink a customs officer under the table and then pick his pocket for the bribe he'd already taken." Gideon pulled her cloak tight. "Careful is new. New is wrong."

"So we don't go."

"We go." She said it before she'd decided to say it, and heard the truth of it land between them. "If it's real, the second token is sitting on a bridge with my oldest friend, and I'm not leaving it there. If it's not real—" She looked at him. "Then I need to know who sold us."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Somewhere below them, the sheep shifted and bleated, stupid and warm and unaware.

"If it's a trap," Isandro said quietly, "we walk into it together. That's the arrangement?"

"That's the arrangement."

They left an hour later, taking the shepherd's cut along the ridge—not because it was fast but because it was theirs, a path worn into Gideon's memory so deep she could walk it blindfolded, could walk it dead. Her grandmother had shown it to her at nine years old, pointing out the false scree slope, the spring that only ran in autumn, the hollow tree where guild runners had cached messages for four generations.

The hollow tree was empty. It shouldn't have been. There was always something in it—a chalk mark, a knot of cord, some small sign that the road remembered its keepers even if no one else did.

Gideon stood in front of it for too long. Isandro waited without asking, which was either kindness or patience or the particular cruelty of a man who already knew what she was going to find out.

"It's just a tree," she said finally.

"Yes."

"It's never been empty." She turned away from it. "Come on. Moonrise won't wait, and neither will whatever's waiting for us."

---

**Isandro**

He saw the ambush before they reached the bridge, and hated himself for how long it took him to see it.

Grayford Bridge spanned the Rillwater in a single gray arch, old imperial work, wide enough for two carts. On the far bank, torchlight moved among the trees—too many torches, spaced too evenly. Soldiers' spacing. And on the near bank, where Sefa was supposed to be waiting with a handful of rebels and a stolen token, there were exactly three figures by a cookfire, visible from half a mile off, which Sefa would never allow because Sefa had survived twenty years of rebellion by being invisible until the moment she chose not to be.

"They're lit up like a festival," Isandro murmured.

"I see it." Gideon had gone still beside him in the bracken, and he watched her do the thing he'd seen her do a dozen times now: read a landscape the way he read a garrison roster. "Three fires. One cook. Nobody on the bridge itself—if you're meeting friends, you stand on the bridge. If you're bait, you stand where the trap wants you to look."

"The far bank."

"The far bank." Her jaw tightened. "That's Corven's drill. He ran sweeps through Thornfell's river country six years back—I lost a runner to it. He lights the decoy camp bright and puts his crossbows in the dark across the water."

"And Sefa?"

Gideon didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice had gone somewhere flat and terrible. "Sefa's either in that decoy camp knowing exactly what it is, or she isn't in it at all."

They lay in the bracken while the moon climbed, and Isandro did the arithmetic he didn't want to do. The Council had wanted the map. Corven wanted the map. The rebels wanted the map. Everyone wanted the map, and everyone knew that Gideon Okafor was the map's keeper now, and the fastest way to catch a guide was to make her walk a road she hadn't chosen.

"There," Gideon breathed.

Two figures had detached from the decoy fire and started across the bridge. Even at this distance, in this light, Isandro saw Gideon recognize them—the way her whole body leaned forward, the way her breath caught and held.

"Who?"

"Maren. And Tam." Her voice cracked on the second name. "Tam Voss. He was twelve when I found him sleeping in the guild hall. I taught him every switchback in the eastern range. He's—" She stopped. Started again. "They're walking into the kill zone. They're walking *slowly*, like they want to be seen."

"Or like they were told to be seen."

"By whom?" But he watched the answer arrive in her face, watch it hit her like a blow she'd been bracing against for days. "No," she said. "No. Sefa wouldn't."

Isandro said nothing, because he had served institutions his whole life, and he knew exactly what they wouldn't do, right up until they did it.

Below them, Maren and Tam reached the middle of the bridge. The torches on the far bank went out—all of them, at once—and the night went black as a closed eye.

"Gideon." He put his hand on her arm. "We can't reach them."

"I know."

"If we go down there, we die with them."

"*I know.*"

The first bolt came out of the darkness with a sound like a wet branch snapping. Then the others, a dozen, a score, a hail of them, and the bridge that had stood for eight hundred years suddenly rang with the sound of bodies falling on old stone.

Gideon made a noise he would hear for the rest of his life. Not a scream. Something smaller and worse—a single syllable, bitten in half, the sound a person makes when grief arrives faster than the breath to carry it.

She was moving before he could stop her, down the slope, through the bracken, toward the bridge, and Isandro did the only thing he could do, which was go with her.

---

**Gideon**

Later, she would not be able to reconstruct those minutes. They existed in her memory as fragments: the cold spray off the Rillwater. Tam's face, young and astonished, a black-feathered shaft standing in his chest like a horrible flowering reed. Maren crawling, dragging one leg, reaching the near end of the bridge where Gideon caught her under the arms and hauled her behind the abutment while bolts hissed past overhead and buried themselves in the wood with a sound like rain on a roof.

"Hold on. Hold on, you hold on—"

Maren laughed, which was worse than crying. Blood on her teeth. "Knew you'd come," she said. "Told Tam. Told him, she'll come, she always comes."

"Shut up. Save your breath. We're going to get you out, we're going up the shepherd's cut, it's ten minutes, Maren, ten minutes—"

"Gid." Maren caught her wrist. Her grip was already failing, and Gideon felt it fail, felt the exact moment the strength went out of it, and hated herself for feeling it, for cataloguing it, for the guide's instinct that measured everything even now. "It was Sefa. She sold the route. Tam heard her talking to a rider in gray. He wouldn't believe it. He came tonight to prove it wasn't true."

The world went very quiet around those words, the way a room goes quiet after a door slams.

"Why?" Gideon heard herself ask. "Why would she—"

"Because you chose the warden." Maren's eyes were going glassy, but her mouth kept working, stubborn to the last, a rebel's mouth, a guild-kin's mouth. "Because you're walking with *him* instead of us. Because she thinks you've turned. Oh—Gid, don't make that face. Don't you dare. You did what you thought was right. We all—" A cough took her, and brought blood with it. "We all follow you. That was the problem. We followed you into something none of us understood."

"Maren—"

"Listen. Listen to me." The grip tightened again, a last flare. "The token. Sefa has it. She showed Tam, she was proud of it, she's going to use it to buy terms with the Council, she thinks it makes her legitimate—" Another cough. "Don't let her. Don't let her trade our dead for a seat at their table. Promise me. Promise me you'll take it back and do it *right*, whatever right is, because I never knew and you—"

"I promise." Gideon pressed her forehead to Maren's brow, cold and damp. "I promise. I promise. I promise."

Maren smiled. It was the smile from a hundred camps, a hundred bad roads, crooked and unimpressed and certain of her. Then it faded into nothing in particular, and her grip slid from Gideon's wrist, and the Rillwater went on talking to itself beneath the bridge as though nothing at all had happened.

Bolts still hissed overhead. Voices on the far bank, ordering a crossing. Gideon knelt there holding her dead friend and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that the soldiers would cross within minutes, and that Maren and Tam deserved better than to be found here, displayed, made into a message.

"Invisibility," she said aloud. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "I kept us invisible. Twenty years. I kept the roads hidden and the names secret and nobody knew we existed, and it didn't save a single one of them."

Isandro crouched beside her. He didn't touch her. He had learned, somewhere in the last weeks, when not to.

"They crossed the bridge in the open," he said quietly. "In the moonlight. On a route you didn't choose. This isn't your invisibility failing, Gideon. This is someone selling it."

"It's the same thing." She laid Maren down gently, closed her eyes, arranged her hands. "A secret is only safe while everyone keeping it is alive and loyal. I built everything on secrets. I built *us* on secrets. And Sefa—" Her throat closed. She forced the words through it. "Sefa was the first person I ever told a secret to voluntarily."

Above them, boots on stone. The vanguard of the crossing party, moving onto the bridge.

Isandro stood, drew his blade, and stepped between her and the bridge with the easy finality of a man who had done this math many times. "Back way?"

"Under the abutment. There's a ledge along the gorge." She rose. Her legs held. She was almost surprised. "We leave them covered. There's brush by the north pier."

"Gideon—"

"*Covered.*" She was already moving. "Corven doesn't get to make banners out of my dead."

---

**Isandro**

They went down the ledge with the river roaring below and crossbowmen fumbling in the dark above, and Isandro learned something about grief on that climb: it made Gideon faster. Not careless—she never missed a handhold, never slipped—but stripped, the way ore looks after the furnace. Everything decorative burned away. What remained was purpose with a pulse.

They reached the shepherd's cut as the soldiers reached the near bank and found the brush-covered bodies and, presumably, reported back to a magistrate who would add two more entries to a ledger he probably kept very neatly.

At the top of the ridge, in the first gray suggestion of dawn, Gideon finally stopped. She stood looking back down at the valley, at the bridge, at the smoke of the abandoned decoy fire, and Isandro watched her hands open and close at her sides.

"When I find her," Gideon said, "I'm going to ask her why. Once. I'm owed that much—she owes me that much. And then—"

"And then?"

"I don't know." It seemed to cost her enormously to say it. "That's the worst part, Isandro. I've spent my whole life knowing exactly what I'd do to anyone who betrayed the roads. I had answers ready since I was fifteen years old. And now I'm standing here and I don't know, because she gave me the second token's location in a sparrow note and I *knew* it was wrong and I went anyway, because part of me—" Her voice broke and reformed. "Part of me believed she'd never sell me. So maybe I'm the fool, and maybe the fool doesn't get to choose the punishment."

Isandro looked at this woman who had out-negotiated him, out-walked him, out-endured him, standing gutted on a ridge with ash-colored light on her face, and he understood that he had passed some line he couldn't name and didn't want to find his way back over.

"Then we don't decide tonight," he said. "Tonight we decide the next thing. Which is—"

"The token." She turned to him, and her eyes were dry now, and that was somehow more frightening than the tears would have been. "Sefa has the second token, and she means to trade it to the Council for legitimacy. Except the Council doesn't want legitimacy. They want the covenant to fail—they *need* it to fail, you told me yourself, you figured it out on the mountain. So Sefa is carrying the one thing that could save both realms straight into the hands of the people who will bury it."

"Where would she trade it? If she's going to the Council—"

"She wouldn't go to Ashenmoor. She hates the vale; she'd sooner swim the Rillwater in January." Gideon's mind was visibly running ahead of her mouth now, laying routes, discarding them. "But there's a neutral ground. Traders' ground. Corven holds court at Grayford twice a season, and the next session is—"

She stopped.

Isandro watched the color leave her face.

"Three days," she whispered. "The session is in three days. At Grayford. Where she just killed my people. She's not trading with the Council at all." A breath, ragged. "She's trading with *Corven*. She sold us to Corven, and now she's going to sell the token to him too, and he'll burn it, and then there's nothing—no map, no tokens, no oath—nothing left but the thirteenth moon coming up over two realms tearing each other apart."

The wind came up the valley, smelling of river water and woodsmoke and the faint iron tang of blood still on their clothes.

Isandro drew a slow breath. "How fast can you get us to Grayford?"

Gideon looked down at the bridge one last time—at the place where Maren and Tam lay covered in brush, unseen, unnamed, the way she had kept everything unseen and unnamed—and when she turned back, there was nothing left in her face but direction.

"Faster than anyone thinks possible," she said. "Watch me."
