## 13. Into the Vale

The chains were real. That was the first thing Isandro had insisted on, and the last thing Gideon had argued about.

"Forged iron," he'd said, turning the manacles over in his hands at the mouth of the pass, three nights ago now. "Not the theater kind. If a guard tests them, they test true."

"And if I need to run?"

"You won't." He'd said it without looking at her, which was how he said the things that mattered most. "You'll be exactly where you chose to be."

She hadn't argued after that. She'd only held out her wrists.

Now, four days later, those same wrists were raw beneath the iron, and Isandro walked three paces ahead of her through the Warden's Gate with his heart trying to climb out of his throat.

He had prepared for this crossing the way he prepared for everything—by memorizing it until it lost its power to surprise him. The gate itself: black basalt, forty feet of it, carved with the sigils of the thirteen wards. The guards: six at the outer post, two at the inner, all of them men he had trained or trained beside. The protocol: prisoner presented by a warden of standing, chains verified, escort required beyond the second threshold.

What he had not prepared for was the smell.

Ashenmoor smelled like home. That was the cruelty of it. Cold stone and pine resin and the mineral tang of the deep springs, the same air he'd breathed every day of his first thirty years, and his body responded to it before his mind could intervene—a loosening in the chest, an involuntary softening, the animal recognition of *den*. He hated his body for it.

"Name," said the outer guard.

"Warden Isandro Vael."

The guard's eyes flicked up from the ledger. Everyone at this gate knew what had happened to Isandro Vael. Everyone knew the word *exile* the way children knew the word *fire*—abstractly, until it was close.

"You're listed as removed from the rolls."

"I'm listed as restored." He let the lie sit there, smooth and cold. He had spent two days composing the letter that made it plausible: the Council's summons, disguised as one, ordering him to bring in the map-thief alive. A warden who returned bearing both the fugitive and the covenant map would not be questioned at the gate. Questioning happened above, in the quiet rooms, where it could be done properly. "Check the third seal. The Council's own."

The guard checked. The forgery was good—Gideon's work, actually, copied from a genuine writ they'd taken off a dead courier's backup satchel, and she had a forger's patience he'd never have guessed at. The seal held up under lamplight.

"Prisoner's name?"

"She hasn't given one." Isandro let a flicker of contempt cross his face, the look of a man embarrassed by his catch. "Road-rat. Guild remnant, if the tattoos mean anything."

That did it. He watched the word travel through the guard's posture—*guild*—and watched the contempt curdle into something more careful. The Wayfarers were a story here, mostly. A story about invisible roads and people who could walk through Thornfell like ghosts. Stories made guards nervous. Nervous guards followed protocol.

"Chains?"

"Forged iron. Verify if you want."

The guard didn't verify. That was the thing about disgrace—it bought a strange kind of credibility. No one suspected the fallen man of cleverness. They only pitied him, and pity never checked the locks.

* * *

They took her weapons at the second threshold, which she had expected, and took her boots, which she had not.

"They'll give them back," Isandro said, in the flat tone prisoners were meant to hear and dismiss. Underneath it, pitched for her alone: *Count the turns. Left at the third arch. Don't look at the murals.*

She looked at the murals anyway. Of course she did. He heard her breath change behind him—the small hitch of someone seeing their own history rendered in gold leaf by people who had no right to it. The founding of the vale. The thirteen wards raised. And there, in the fourth panel, half-worn: figures walking mountain roads, staffs in hand, faces turned toward Ashenmoor. The Wayfarers. Painted as servants. Painted as *theirs*.

Gideon said nothing. But when the escort prodded her forward, she moved with a deliberateness that Isandro recognized, because he'd seen it on the switchbacks and at the waystation fire: she was counting. Doors, guards, turns, distances. Filing the vale away the way she filed everything—not as a place but as a map, and maps were things you could use.

Two guards walked them down the long gallery toward the holding cells. Isandro kept his eyes forward and his gait even, and inside himself he conducted the argument he'd been conducting since Grayford Bridge.

*You could still turn back.* No. There was no back to turn to; the bridge had burned that road, along with two of Gideon's kin, whose names he'd learned only afterward—Maren and Odile, sisters, keepers of the eastern paths—and whose deaths sat in him like swallowed stones. *You could hand her over. Take your pardon. Walk back into the life they exiled you from.* He tested the thought honestly, the way he tested ice. It held nothing. It had held nothing since the night he'd refused to let Callowmere starve, and everything since was just the long working-out of a decision already made.

The truth was simpler and worse: he had crossed into Thornfell carrying his duty like a stone, and somewhere between the drowned chapel and the fire where she'd told him why she stayed invisible—why a woman built for roads had chosen to become a ghost—he had set the stone down. What he carried now weighed less and cost more.

"Holding cells are full," the younger guard said. "Council's sitting tonight. They want the gallery clear."

"The prisoner goes to the deep cell, then," Isandro said. "Warden's order. She's guild-trained. I don't put guild-trained anywhere with a window."

It was a good line. It was also, he realized a half-second too late, a mistake—because the deep cell was his old jurisdiction, and the younger guard knew it, and something flickered across the man's face. Not suspicion. Recognition. The look of a man remembering that Isandro Vael had once been the person you brought problems to.

"Didn't know they'd given you standing again," the guard said carefully.

"They gave me a writ." Isandro met his eyes. "Ask the Council whether my standing matters more than their paper."

Silence. Then the older guard shrugged—the shrug of a man who had survived twenty years of Council politics by never standing between two claims of authority—and waved them down the stair.

* * *

The deep cells were below the spring-line, where the stone wept constantly and the air tasted of iron. Isandro dismissed the escort at the top of the stairs—"I'll process her myself; the writ names me"—and the sound of their boots faded up through the dark like a tide going out.

Gideon waited until it was gone. Then she lifted her chained hands, and in the gloom he saw her flex her fingers, testing.

"The left manacle's pinned," she said. "Has been since the gate. You're getting better at this."

"You noticed at the gate and said nothing."

"Noticed at the forge." Her teeth flashed white in the dark. "Three days, Vael. I've been carrying a key I couldn't use for three days."

He took the pin from his sleeve and worked the lock properly this time, and the manacle fell open, and she rubbed her wrist and looked around the cell block with the expression of a woman appraising a house she intended to buy.

"So this is Ashenmoor's underbelly."

"This is the part they pretend doesn't exist. Which is why it's the part I know best." He lit the lamp he'd cached behind the third cell's loose stone—his stone, his cache, from a life that felt like it belonged to someone else. "The Council sits at moonrise. Thirteen of them, in the quiet rooms, where the walls carry voice and nothing is written down. They'll expect me to present the map and the thief together."

"And instead?"

"Instead we go up through the warden's galleries. Old passages. Sealed after the Schism, but the seals answer to warden's blood, and they never revoked mine—they just revoked me." He allowed himself the smallest grimace. "Bureaucracy. Even tyrants drown in their own paperwork eventually."

"And when we're up there? In their pretty rooms?" She stepped closer, and the lamplight caught the bruise the manacle had left, and her voice dropped to the register she saved for truths. "Isandro. Whatever we find up there—I need you to hear it before they say it. Your Council broke the covenant on purpose. The map says so. My grandmother's warnings say so. These people hoarded the vale's magic while the outer realms thinned and starved, and they've been doing it for generations, dressed up as stewardship." Her eyes held his. "So when they offer you your seat back—and they will, because that's what men like this do, they buy the useful knife back—remember which side of the wall the starving villages were on."

"They already offered," Isandro said quietly.

Her stillness was total. Even her breathing stopped.

"The summons wasn't forged," he went on. "Not entirely. There was a real letter underneath Gideon's copy—sealed, private, addressed to me. Full restoration. Warden of the First Gate. All charges struck." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "They knew I'd find the courier. They've known where the map was for months. They let me walk into Thornfell because a disgraced warden is the one hunter a guild-keeper won't see coming. And the price of coming home was always going to be her."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the deep stairs. Somewhere far above, a bell began to mark the hour—slow, bronze, the sitting-bell.

"Gideon." He said her name the way he'd wanted to say it for weeks and had rationed like water. "I'm not presenting you to anyone. I'm not taking any pardon. I'm opening doors that can't be closed again, and when the Council understands what I've done, there will be no exile left to return from. This isn't the plan where we get away clean. There is no version of tonight where I go back to being what I was."

"I know," she said.

"You should also know—" and here his voice finally failed him, briefly, and he pushed through it because she deserved the whole weight of it, unsoftened, "—that I would burn every ward on this mountain before I let them put their oath on you. Whatever the covenant costs. Whoever it's supposed to bind. They don't get to write it. Neither do I. *You* do, or no one does."

For a moment she just looked at him, and he saw something in her face he'd only seen once before—at the drowned chapel, when the token had answered her blood: awe, and fear, and underneath both, the terrible calm of a woman who has already counted the price and found it payable.

"Vael," she said, using the name like a hand on his shoulder, "when this is done, remind me to tell you what my grandmother said about men who burn things for other people. It wasn't flattering."

"It rarely is."

"But—" she took the lamp from him, turned toward the deep stairs, and the light threw her shadow huge against the weeping stone, "—it's time. Moonrise is in two hours. Show me your sealed doors, warden."

He drew the key of his old office from around his neck—had carried it through exile like a relic, like a wound—and pressed it to the lock at the bottom of the stair, the one that hadn't opened in nine years.

The mechanism turned like it had been waiting.

Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the sitting-bell finished its thirteenth stroke, and Isandro heard—no, felt—something else beneath it. A vibration in the rock, low and patient, like a heartbeat that had been slowing for a hundred years and was now, tonight, beginning to count down.

The thirteenth moon was rising.

And in the quiet rooms above, thirteen voices were about to learn what their exiled warden had brought home.
