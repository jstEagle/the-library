## 16. Sefa's Bargain

They took us at the mouth of the Hollow Stair, where the mountain pinches down to a throat of stone and there is nowhere to run that isn't straight up or straight down.

I should have seen it. That's the worst part—the part I keep turning over like a stone with something crawling under it. I taught Sefa every trick I know about reading a road, and she used the first one against me: never trust a path because it's empty. Empty means someone cleared it.

Six rebels dropped from the ledges above, quiet as snowfall, and by the time Isandro's hand found his blade they had crossbows leveled at the space between our hearts. Sefa came last, walking down the switchback like she was visiting an old friend's grave.

In a way, she was.

"Gideon." She said my name the way you'd say it over a coffin. "You made this harder than it needed to be."

"Sefa." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Your sentries are standing on a hollow shelf. One more step back each and the whole ledge goes."

"They know. I trained them."

"You trained them on my roads."

"On *our* roads," she said, and there it was—the old wound, opened fresh. "That was always the difference between us, Gid. You thought the guild owned the paths. I thought the paths belonged to everyone."

"The paths belong to no one. That's what keeps people alive on them."

"Keep your lessons." She nodded, and two rebels closed in, relieving Isandro of his sword with the practiced efficiency of people who'd done it before. He let them—mostly. His eyes never left Sefa, cataloging her the way he cataloged everything: exits, numbers, the angle of her knife. "The map, Gideon. And the token you pulled out of the drowned chapel. Both. Now."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Corven gets you instead. He's three valleys back and closing. You've seen what he does to villages that shelter you." Her jaw tightened. "I didn't sell you to him. I want that understood. But I won't bleed for your secrets either."

"You already did," I said. "Grayford Bridge."

The words landed like a slap. Two of my guild kin dead on the stones—Brenna and old Tammas, who'd carried me across the Thornfell fords when I was nine and too proud to ask. Sefa flinched. Good. I wanted her to feel it. I wanted her to feel it *because* it would hurt me too.

"That wasn't supposed to happen," she said quietly. "I sold the route, not the blood. Corven moved faster than—"

"He always does. That's what he is." I stepped forward, close enough that the nearest crossbow twitched toward me, and Sefa raised a hand to hold it. "So here's where we are. You want the map. Corven wants the map. The Vale Council wants the map. Everyone wants a piece of paper none of them can read, and they're willing to kill whoever's holding it. Doesn't that strike you as strange? Doesn't that strike you as the single strangest thing in these mountains right now?"

Sefa's eyes narrowed. "What are you doing?"

"Telling you the truth." I laughed, and it came out ragged. "Finally. It seems to be the only currency left that anyone's afraid of."

* * *

Here is what I'd learned since Grayford Bridge, carrying grief up the high passes like ballast: secrecy had failed. Not because secrets are wrong—some things need hiding—but because I'd hidden the wrong things from the wrong people. I'd kept the covenant secret from Sefa while trusting her with my life, and she'd kept her bargain with Corven secret from me while trusting me with hers. We'd spent years trading silences and calling it loyalty.

Isandro had said it around the fire, weeks ago now, in his flat warden's voice: *"A law nobody can see is just a trap with better manners."*

He was right. Damn him, he was right.

So I stood in the throat of the Hollow Stair with six crossbows on me and told Sefa everything.

About the covenant. About Ashenmoor and Thornfell bound together by an oath older than either realm's government, sworn by the founders of my guild—road-keepers, oathkeepers, the same thing once. About how the Council broke it deliberately, generations ago, to hoard the vale's magic behind their Quiet Rooms. About the thirteenth moon, and what happens when it completes its turn: the realms tearing into each other, forest bleeding into hall, valley bleeding into vale, until neither place is habitable.

About the price. I even told her that. The guild-line ends; the last heir forgets every road, every name, every ancestor's voice. I watched her face when I said it, and I saw her understand what I was offering to pay, and I saw that understanding hurt her worse than any accusation could have.

"Why tell me this?" she said finally. Her voice had gone rough. "You could have lied. You're good at it. You taught me."

"Because lying to you got Brenna killed." I held her gaze. "Because I'm done paying for secrets with other people's blood. And because—" I breathed. This was the part I'd been building toward, the part I'd rehearsed on the switchbacks and immediately abandoned as insufficient. "Because you wanted to tear down the Council. So do I. But not with fire, Sefa. With terms."

"Terms."

"A new covenant. Written openly. Signed by both realms—not imposed by one on the other, not hoarded, not hidden in a chapel under water. The vale's magic shared or at least accounted for. The Council answerable. Every clause published where any road-keeper, any farmer, any rebel with a grudge can read it and argue with it." I spread my hands. "You want justice outside the law? Fine. Then we write the law outside the justice—before the moon turns, before Corven burns another village, before the realms come apart at the seams. I can't do it without you."

Silence. Wind through the stair. Somewhere below us, faint as a headache, the sound of many riders moving fast.

Sefa looked at me for a long time. Then at Isandro. "And him? The warden who came to silence you?"

"He turned his authority against his own Council to get me out of Ashenmoor," I said. "He burned his last chance at home. Ask him yourself."

Isandro met her stare without blinking. "I refused one order to let a border village starve," he said. "It cost me everything. I'm given to understand that's the entry fee now."

Something moved in Sefa's face—something old, from before the guild fractured, from the days when the three of us weren't yet enemies of each other's silences.

"You always were a terrible liar, Gid," she said at last. "That was the whole problem. You hid behind the truth so carefully no one could ever find it."

"I'm learning to walk it in daylight."

* * *

She didn't give me the token immediately. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the mountains keep: Sefa made me wait, made herself wait, walked ten paces away and stared at the valley floor for the length of a hundred heartbeats while her rebels watched her decide.

When she turned back, she pulled the second token from inside her coat—a disc of dark metal on a cord, cold-looking even at a distance, etched with the same spiral sigil that had glowed under my blood in the drowned chapel. She'd taken it off Brenna's body at Grayford Bridge. I hadn't known that until that moment. I don't know how I knew it then. Some things the covenant tells you sideways.

"This belonged to your dead," she said. "I took it to use it. Against the Council. As a weapon." Her hand shook, just slightly, as she held it out. "But weapons are for people who think the war is the point."

"And it isn't?"

"It was. Once." She pressed the token into my palm and closed my fingers over it, and her grip stayed a moment longer than it needed to. "My brother died in the Council's purges, Gideon. Everything I built, I built so no one else's sibling would vanish into a Quiet Room. If your new covenant does that—if it actually does that—I don't need the map. I need it to be *real*. Swear it."

"You have my word. In front of witnesses." I glanced at the rebels, at Isandro. "Both kinds."

Sefa almost smiled. It was a battered thing, that almost-smile, but it was real. "Corven is half a day behind you, maybe less. He has the route to the covenant's heart—I sold him that much before Grayford, gods forgive me. You can't outrun him to the threshold."

"No," I agreed. "But you can slow him down."

Her eyebrows rose. "Asking a lot."

"Asking the only person I know who's meaner in a rearguard action than I am."

This time she did smile, briefly, fiercely. "One condition. When the new terms are written—when it's done—I read them first. Before the Councils, before the wardens, before anyone. The rebels get to argue with the law before it binds them. That was always the point of us, Gid. That was why I left."

"That was always the point of the guild too," I said. "We just forgot it. Deal."

We clasped forearms, rebel-style, and it cost her something visible to do it, and it cost me something invisible, and both debts felt honest for the first time in years.

* * *

We went down the far side of the Hollow Stair as the light failed, Sefa's people peeling off to raise rockfalls and false trails behind us, buying hours with landslides and misdirection. Isandro walked beside me in silence for a long while, and then, without looking over:

"You told her the price."

"Yes."

"You didn't tell me until the threshold. Until it was nearly too late to matter."

"I know."

He was quiet another stretch of trail. Snow had begun to fall, fine and dry, catching in his hair. "Why her and not me?"

"Because she asked the right question," I said. "And because I was still trying to protect you, and protecting people with silence is how I got here." I stopped on the path and faced him, the token heavy against my chest, the thirteenth moon a sliver above the eastern peaks, fat with its last turn. "There's no more time for that. Whatever happens at the heart—whatever I pay—you'll hear it from me first. All of it. That's the new term, Isandro. Mine. Between us."

He looked at me for a long moment, snow gathering on his shoulders, and something in his face unclenched that I hadn't realized was clenched.

"All right," he said. "Then hear this one now. When the rite asks for your blood, it will ask for all of it. Not a cupful. The well. And I intend to stand at that door and argue with a god if I have to."

"There's no god. Just an oath."

"Worse," he said. "An oath remembers."

Below us, far down the valley, a red glow bloomed against the dark—Corven's torches, or his fires, moving along the route Sefa had sold him. Half a day, she'd said. Maybe less.

I touched the second token through my coat, felt it pulse once against my heartbeat like something recognizing kin, and started down toward the light.

Two tokens. One moon. Half a day.

The mountain, at last, was running out of places to hide—and so, I realized, was I.
