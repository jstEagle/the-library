## 17. The Price Named Aloud

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain three miles below the covenant's heart.

Isandro felt it first in his boots—a wrongness in the stone, like walking on the roof of something that had opinions. The passage narrowed, then opened without warning into a gallery of black glass, and the walls were not walls anymore. They were windows. Through them he could see Thornfell's pines growing sideways out of Ashenmoor's marble, roots splitting council floors that hadn't existed for two hundred years. The realms were already bleeding through each other at the seams, and they weren't even at the threshold yet.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," he said.

Gideon didn't slow down. "Because you asked me to."

"I asked you to *listen* to it. There's a difference."

"There always is." She glanced back at him, and in the light of her hand-lamp her face was all edges—cheekbone still bruised from Grayford Bridge, a cut across her brow she refused to let him dress properly. "You want me to walk up to the door of everything my family died carrying and then turn around because it might cost me something. You've met me, Isandro. What did you think I'd say?"

He had no answer for that. He'd had no answer for six days, since the astronomer-hermit's tower, since the moment he understood his own Council had been waiting out the covenant like creditors waiting out a debtor. He'd traded every card he had to get her here—his title, his name, his last thread back to the vale that raised him—and now that they stood close enough to touch the thing itself, all he could feel was how much he wanted her to stop.

That was the problem with duty, he thought. It never told you which duty.

* * *

The threshold announced itself by silence.

Not quiet—silence. The kind that pressed on the ears like deep water. One moment they were climbing a stair cut into living rock, Gideon's breath ragged, his shoulder screaming where a Council bolt had grazed him in the Warden's Door; the next, every sound in the world had been switched off except their heartbeats and the wet rasp of their breathing, which suddenly seemed obscene.

The chamber beyond was round, and old, and lit by nothing. No torches. No lamps. Light simply *was*, rising out of the floor in slow veins of gold, and in the center of the room stood a table of black stone worn smooth as river glass, and on the table lay a book.

Gideon stopped so hard Isandro nearly walked into her.

"That's not possible," she whispered. Even her whisper came out flattened, as if the room rationed sound. "That's my grandmother's hand. That's the guild ledger. I burned it. I burned it myself when the hall fell."

"It was never yours to burn," said the room.

The voice didn't come from anywhere. It came from everywhere—the gold veins, the glass walls, the marrow of Isandro's own bones. It wasn't cruel. That was the worst part. It had the flat patience of arithmetic.

"The oathkeeper stands," it said. "The thirteenth moon rises in four hours. The covenant may be reforged. Name yourself."

Gideon lifted her chin. "Gideon Okafor. Last sworn guide of the Wayfarers' Guild."

"Lineage confirmed. Terms follow."

And the room showed them.

It didn't speak the price—it *showed* it, the way a fever shows you things, direct behind the eyes. Isandro cried out despite himself. He saw roads. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Every smuggler path and shepherd track and dead man's trail in Thornfell, unspooling like thread pulled from cloth, and woven through every one of them a woman's figure—Gideon, younger, older, hooded, laughing, swearing—being unpicked stitch by stitch. He saw her grandmother's face dissolve out of a memory like ink in rain. He saw the guild hall whole and golden and then *blank*, a hole shaped like a building in a landscape that had forgotten why anyone would build there.

He saw Gideon standing in a world with no roads in it, holding a map she could no longer read, unable to remember why her hands knew knots.

"No," he said aloud, to the room, to the vision, to whatever was listening. "No. Take something else. Take me. I'm warden-blood; I'm worth ten of her—"

"The price is fixed," said the room. "The covenant was built on an oathkeeper's line. To reforge it, the line must end—not in death. In forgetting. The guild must become nothing, wholly, or the seal will not hold. This is what your founders agreed. This is what has always been owed."

*This is what has always been owed.* Isandro heard those words and wanted to break something. His Council had known. They had sat in their quiet rooms for generations knowing the final bill would come due, and they had hoarded their magic and exiled their wardens and waited for some last heir to wander in and pay it for them.

Beside him, Gideon had gone very still.

Stillness, with Gideon, was never peace. He'd learned that on the drowned chapel, when the token had drunk her blood and she'd smiled with her teeth. Stillness was her loading a weapon.

"Gideon." He turned to face her fully, and the room let him, and he hated it for its courtesy. "Look at me. Not the book. Me."

She looked at him. Her eyes were dry, which frightened him more than tears would have.

"You heard it," she said. "Four hours. If I walk away, the realms tear open. Thornfell bleeds into Ashenmoor until there's no Thornfell and no Ashenmoor, just a wound with villages on both sides of it. Corven's riders. Sefa's people. The hermit. Children, Isandro. I've seen what happens to children when borders fail. So have you."

"And if you do this, there is no *you*. Not the you standing here. Some woman walks out of this mountain who doesn't know her grandmother's name, who can't find her way to water in a drought, who looks at you like a stranger—" His voice cracked and he let it. He was done pretending to be stone. "I have lost a homeland. I know exactly what it costs to survive it. I would not wish it on you. I will not stand here and watch you pay it."

"Then don't watch."

"Gideon—"

"*Don't watch.*" But her voice broke too, finally, on the second word, and that small crack undid something in both of them.

He reached for her and she let him, and they stood in the gold light holding each other like two people clinging to the same spar, and Isandro understood with terrible clarity that this was the real threshold. Not the book. Not the moon. This: the moment where love and duty stopped being abstractions and became a bill with one name on it.

"Walk away," he said into her hair. "I'm begging you. I have never begged anything in my life. Let the realms burn. Let the Council choke on what they broke. We'll save who we can save—we're good at that, we've been doing it the whole way here—one village, one road, one person at a time. It isn't nothing. It's the only honest work there is."

She pulled back far enough to look at him. Her hands came up and framed his face, thumbs tracing the scar along his jaw, and she smiled, and it was the worst smile he had ever seen—tender and resolved and already saying goodbye.

"You know that's not true," she said softly. "You know I'd be choosing *my* comfort over everyone else's survival, and you'd be helping me do it, and we'd spend the rest of our lives being kind to strangers to make up for it. That's not the life you want either. You refused to let a border village starve. That's why they exiled you. Don't you dare become smaller than that now, just because it's *me*."

"That's exactly why it should be different. Because it's you."

"No." Her thumb pressed his lips shut. "Because it's me, it has to be *more* true, not less. My whole life I kept the roads invisible because invisible roads keep people safe. But invisibility has a price too, Isandro. I paid it at Grayford Bridge. Two of my kin died on a path no one else could see, and no one will ever even know they died defending something. Secrecy failed them. Duty-as-chain failed everyone. So if the covenant wants a price, fine. But I'm not paying *their* price. I'm going to write new terms. You taught me that—you, the warden who turned his keys against his own Council. You don't get to teach a woman to break chains and then object when she breaks hers."

"The price is fixed," the room repeated, patient as tide.

"Then we'll see," Gideon said, "about fixed."

* * *

They had four hours, and Isandro spent them the only way he knew how: working the problem.

The room tolerated movement but not departure—he tested it once, walking back down the stair, and found himself rounding a bend into the same chamber, breathless, Gideon frowning at him like he'd been gone a year. A trap with velvet walls. Of course. The founders had built a cage for their heirs and called it sacred duty.

So he did what he did best. He read the lock.

The book on the table was the covenant itself—he was sure of it now, sure in the way of a man who had spent his life reading seals and wards and the small print underneath them. Old terms, written in blood that had gone brown as rust. And around the terms, fainter, almost erased: marginalia. Amendments. His fingers hovered above the page and the room did not stop him, and that interested him enormously.

"Terms can be amended," he said slowly. "There are amendments here. Someone tried before."

"My grandmother," Gideon said quietly. She was kneeling beside the table, lamp held low. "Look at the hand. It's hers. She got as far as drafting." Her finger traced lines that faded mid-sentence, unfinished, abandoned. "*...the oath shall bind realms, not persons... the keeper shall give what she chooses, not what is demanded...*" The writing stopped there. Below it, in a shakier script: *No. They will never accept. The price is the point. The chain is the point.*

"She figured it out," Isandro breathed. "Generations ago. The Council didn't want a treaty—they wanted a slave-oath, and the price was designed to produce obedient keepers. A keeper with no past, no guild, no ancestors whispering in her blood—she'd be clay. They could shape her."

"And my grandmother couldn't finish the fight." Gideon closed her eyes. "So she left it to whoever came after. She left it to me."

"She left you a draft. Not a victory. You'd be improvising a rewrite of a founding covenant while the thirteenth moon rises, with half the Council's guard somewhere behind us and Corven—"

"Corven is coming." She said it flatly, factually, the way she called out switchbacks. "You feel it too. The mountain's been humming since the chapel. He'll strike at the rite, when the power's exposed. And the Council with him."

"Then we have less than four hours and one chance." Isandro looked down at the brown-blooded terms, at his exile's hands hovering over them, and felt something settle in him that felt almost like calm. "All right. All right. Then we do what we've done since the waystation fire. You lead. I hold the door. And when they come—and they will come—I need you to promise me one thing."

"Name it."

"When you write your terms—in your blood, in front of all of them—write yourself *out* of the price. Whatever it costs, however the magic haggles, you keep something. Your memories. Your roads. Something. Promise me, or I swear I will drag you out of this mountain over my shoulder and damn the realms."

Gideon studied him for a long moment. The gold light moved in her eyes like water over stones.

"You know I can't promise that," she said gently. "New terms mean new risks. Maybe the magic won't take a partial payment. Maybe it takes more. I won't lie to you under this roof. Lying is what Corven does. Lying is what the Council does."

"Then lie to *me*. Just once. Just tonight."

"No." She took his hand and pressed it flat against the ledger's cover, against the cold black stone, over the place where her grandmother's amendment died unfinished. "You once told me trust rebuilt on honesty is the only kind that holds. Sefa believed you. I believe you. So here's my honesty, Isandro Vael, and you can carry it into whatever comes: I am going to finish my grandmother's fight. I am going to write terms that bind realms and free people. And I am going to come out of this rite knowing your name, because I choose to, because I will put it in the terms themselves if I have to—*the keeper shall keep what she loves*—and if the magic refuses, then it refuses, and you'll still have done the only thing that matters."

"Which is?"

"You held the door long enough for someone to try."

Somewhere far above them—or perhaps far below; distance had stopped meaning anything in this place—a horn sounded. Long, low, unmistakable. Council bronze, warden-forged. Isandro knew its voice the way he knew his own heartbeat.

They were coming. Corven, the Council guard, all of it, drawn up the mountain by the covenant's waking like moths to a flame they intended to own.

Gideon drew her knife. Isandro drew his, and turned to face the stair, and set his feet the way he'd learned to set them in twenty years of holding doors: planted, angled, unimpressed by odds.

"One door," he said.

"One door," she agreed.

Above them, the thirteenth moon began, at last, to rise.
