## 18. Thirteen Turns Full

The thirteenth moon did not rise so much as arrive.

Gideon felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her eyes, a hum in the fillings of her teeth, the particular wrongness of air that has been held too long in a closed room. She stood at the mouth of the deep mountain with Isandro's blood still tacky on her sleeve and the map pressed flat against her ribs, and she watched the sky above Ashenmoor's shattered crown turn the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

"Tell me again," she said.

Isandro leaned against the broken archway, one arm cradled to his chest where the Council's guard had caught him with a pike on the way out of the Quiet Rooms. He'd stopped bleeding. He hadn't stopped looking at her like a man doing arithmetic he didn't want finished. "When the thirteenth moon completes its turn—when it sets—the covenant fails. The wards go slack. Thornfell and Ashenmoor stop being two places and start being one bad idea."

"And how long until it sets?"

He looked up. The moon hung low over the eastern peaks, swollen and silver-white, ringed in a halo that no honest moon should wear. "Four hours. Maybe five."

* * *

They climbed together as far as they could.

The path into the mountain's heart was not a path—that was the first thing the map had taught her, weeks ago now, when it had rewritten itself in her blood and shown her a road that existed only for someone carrying Wayfarer blood and Wayfarer debt. It was a seam. A fold in the stone that opened when she put her hand against it and closed behind her like water healing over a dropped stone.

Isandro couldn't follow. She'd known it since the drowned chapel, since the token had sung to her blood and ignored his entirely. But knowing it and standing at the threshold watching him understand it were different animals entirely.

"The rite needs an oathkeeper," he said. "Not necessarily you."

"It needs the guild-line." She checked her knife, her flint, the vial of her own blood she'd been carrying since Grayford Bridge like a talisman that had never once worked. "The guild-line is me. I've done this arithmetic, Isandro. I'm better at it than you are."

"You could walk away." His voice was low, stripped of everything but the words themselves. "You said it yourself—the covenant was a con. The Council built it to hoard magic, to chain the vale to their own pockets. Let it fail. Let both realms go free. That was always an option on the table."

"That was an option before I watched Thornfell start bleeding into these halls." She nodded upward, toward the high galleries where, even now, the tearing had begun. She'd seen it an hour ago from a window: pine trees growing through mosaic floors, moss swallowing marble, the smell of loam and sap rolling through corridors that had been sealed stone for three hundred years. And worse—in the other direction, she'd wager. Ashenmoor's pale light leaking into Thornfell's forests, its old hungry magic pooling in hollows where deer came to drink. Two realms grinding against each other like millstones with nothing between them but air.

"Free," she said. "That's what Corven called it too. Freedom. He burned Marn's Hollow for freedom."

"Marn's Hollow wasn't your debt."

"No," she agreed quietly. "It was my inheritance. There's a difference, but it isn't the difference you think it is."

She stepped into the seam. The stone folded around her, and the last thing she saw of him was his face—wrecked and furious and full of something he'd never once said aloud in all these weeks of shared fires and shared roads—and the last thing she heard was his voice, cracking on the edge of the closing rock:

"Gideon. Whatever it asks. Don't pay more than you owe."

The dark took her, and she thought: *too late for that. I've been paying since the pass.*

* * *

The way down was every road she had ever walked, all at once.

That was the truth of the hidden path her grandmother had sealed—it didn't lead through the mountain. It led through her. Each step forward was also a step backward: the switchback where the courier had died with Ashenmoor's name foaming on his lips; the flooded chapel where the first token had risen to meet her hand like a child recognizing its mother; the guild hall with its crumbling rafters and its ledger of names, each one crossed out except hers; her grandmother's deathbed, candlelight and the smell of yarrow tea, the old woman's fingers tight around hers.

*"We built the roads, little wren. But we built them on top of something older. One day the mountain will ask you what you're standing on. Don't lie to it."*

Gideon walked and did not lie.

The passage narrowed. The walls began to show carvings—old ones, pre-guild, pre-covenant—spirals and open hands and figures walking between two trees that grew from opposite sides of the stone and met overhead. Oathkeepers, she realized. The founders. Her people, carved here before anyone had written the word *Wayfarer*, before anyone had thought to make a guild of it, back when keeping a road between realms had been a sacred act instead of a smuggling trade.

Her grandmother had never told her that part. Had maybe never known it. The guild had kept its secrets so long it had forgotten which ones were shame and which were glory.

At the bottom of the descent, the passage opened.

* * *

The heart of the covenant was a room the size of a cathedral and shaped like nothing built by human hands. The ceiling was living rock veined with quartz that caught the thirteenth moon's light from some impossible angle and scattered it into slow-moving constellations across the floor. In the center stood a table—not stone, not wood, something between, something grown—and on the table lay the tokens.

All four of them. The chapel bell, green with age. The bridge-coin she'd won back from Sefa at the cost of telling the truth for the first time in her life. The gray ribbon from Grayford Bridge, stiff with her kinsmen's blood. And the map itself, unrolled, glowing faintly, its ink moving like something alive that had finally stopped pretending otherwise.

Around the table, the air was already tearing.

It looked like heat-shimmer off summer stone, except it ran vertical, and through it Gideon could see—Thornfell. Actual Thornfell, actual pines, actual rain-slick granite, superimposed over the covenant chamber like a second exposure on a photographer's plate. A bird flew through the gallery above her, or through a forest a hundred miles away, or through both. The seams were failing. The moon was setting somewhere far above, patient as arithmetic.

She approached the table. The map rippled at her approach, and words surfaced in its margins—her grandmother's handwriting, though her grandmother had been dead eleven years and had never touched this map:

*If you are reading this, wren, then the guild is done hiding and so are you.*

*I sealed this path because I could not finish what our founders started. They made a covenant to protect two peoples. Others made it a chain. If you stand here, you can break the chain—but a broken oath is still an oath, and the mountain will want its price named aloud. Name it yourself. Never let the mountain choose for you.*

*And whatever else you do—write new terms. Old oaths keep old wounds alive.*

Gideon stood very still for a long moment, listening to the constellations turn.

Then she laughed—one short, cracked sound that echoed strangely in the vastness. "You knew," she said to the empty air, to the dead woman, to the whole long line of dead women stretching back to the carvings on the stair. "You knew it would end with me, and you left me instructions anyway. You absolute tyrant. Thank you."

She drew her knife.

* * *

The rite, when it came, was simpler than she'd feared and harder than she'd imagined.

Simple, because the tokens wanted only arrangement—bell at north, coin at south, ribbon at east, map spread beneath them all like soil. Hard, because the moment her blood touched the table, the mountain woke up and started talking.

It didn't use words. It used memory. It poured itself into her the way night pours into a valley, and suddenly she was every Wayfarer who had ever lived: she was hauling contraband salt through passes that weren't there; she was kneeling in the drowned chapel three centuries ago, swearing the first oath with her sisters' hands stacked under hers; she was dying in a magistrate's cellar with her tongue cut out rather than give up a client's route; she was teaching a granddaughter to read stars by feel because maps could be confiscated but knowledge couldn't.

Every road. Every keeper. Every secret the guild had ever carried, offered up to her like coins spilled from a torn purse.

*This,* the mountain said, in the voice of all of them at once. *This is the price. The line ends; the memory remains. Give us the guild entire—its roads, its names, its dead—and we will hold the realms apart until such time as free hands rewrite the terms.*

Gideon looked down at her own blood spreading across the ancient table, and she understood, with terrible calm, exactly what was being asked.

Not her life. Her life would have been easy. Her life was the cheapest thing she owned.

They wanted the invisibility. The whole inheritance of it—the hidden paths burned out of her mind, her grandmother's voice erased from her memory, the guild's names gone from her tongue. Everything that had made her the last keeper of roads no one remembered. They wanted her to pay with the only thing she had ever loved without reservation.

And beneath the offer, faint but unmistakable, she felt the other thing the mountain was not saying: that if she refused, it would take the price anyway, along with everything else, when the moon set and the seams tore wide. The offer was a courtesy. The bill was coming regardless.

She thought about running. Honestly, she did. She thought about Isandro's face at the threshold, about Sefa waiting somewhere in the dark with a stolen token and a fragile new trust, about the recruits who didn't exist yet, about a road walked in daylight beside someone who—

She stopped.

No. That was the old thinking. The invisible thinking. The guild's whole sin, the whole rot at the center of three hundred years of secrecy, was believing that survival meant holding things back—holding back routes, holding back truths, holding back herself. Her grandmother had died whispering secrets to a granddaughter in a dark room. The Council had hoarded magic in quiet rooms. Corven wielded law like a blade kept deliberately dull. Every cruelty she had ever witnessed had secrecy at its root.

She would not pay this price in the dark, alone, silently, the way every Wayfarer before her had paid every price.

"Here are my terms," Gideon said aloud, to the mountain, to the moon, to whoever came after.

She pressed the knife deeper and began to write.

* * *

Above her, far above, the thirteenth moon touched the western peaks and began, inch by inevitable inch, to set.

In the galleries of ruined Ashenmoor, the tearing accelerated. Pine forest surged through the Council's marble halls; a river that belonged in Thornfell's high country carved itself a bed through the Vale's oldest archive. Somewhere below, in a chamber shaped like nothing human, a woman stood over a table of tokens with her own blood on her hands, writing terms into the fabric of two worlds.

And somewhere on the stairs between—because of course he had followed, because he had never once in his life obeyed an order he believed was wrong, and because the seam had admitted him the moment her blood began to write—Isandro Vael heard her voice rising up through the stone, steady and clear and utterly unafraid:

"I'll pay," she was saying. "But I'm naming the price myself. And I'm adding a clause."

The mountain listened.

For the first time in three hundred years, the mountain listened.
