## 1. The Charm Seller

The bells of Corvessa rang the ninth hour, and Odile Sandoval opened her stall the way she always did: slowly, with her back to the wind, so that no one passing could see what she laid out on the cloth.

Not that there was much to see. A bolt of grey wool. Spools of thread in colors that had faded toward honesty — rust, moss, river-mud. A wooden box with a loose lid. And, arranged in careful rows like a widow's keepsakes, a few dozen knots.

To anyone walking the night market of Saltrow Lane, she was a knot-seller. That was all. Corvessa had no charm-sellers anymore; it had had none for three years, not since the guildhalls were sealed and their masters marched to the pyres in Triumph Square. What Corvessa had instead was a woman who sold string, and customers who bought string, and a city too tired to ask why a luck-knot worked better than a prayer.

Odile untied the box and counted. Twelve fever-charms, braided in red wool with a drop of beeswax worked into the core. Nine sleep-knots, soft as moth wings. One traveler's bond, which she'd made in the spring and hadn't found brave enough a buyer yet. She set them out with the economy of long practice — never more than one of each kind visible at a time, never two charms touching, because charms that touched could wake each other, and waking things drew eyes.

"Evening, mistress."

She didn't flinch. Three years of practice had burned the flinch out of her, mostly. She looked up into the round, worried face of Tam Wicker, the cooper's son, who came every market-night to buy something his mother needed and never quite met her eye while doing it.

"Tam." She smiled the shopkeeper's smile, warm and empty as a tapped keg. "How's your mother's cough?"

"Worse when the fog comes off the river." He said it quickly, looking at the knots, then away, then at the knots again, the way a man looks at a pretty girl across a chapel.

Odile reached under the table without seeming to reach at all, and when her hand came back up it held a red braid no longer than her thumb. "Fever-charm," she said. "Wool from a lamb born in a hard winter. Tie it above the bed, out of sight. When the wax goes soft, bring it back to me."

"What happens when the wax goes soft?"

"It means it's done its work." It meant the magic was thinning, actually — three days, maybe four, before the charm went dead and she had to re-braid it over a candle flame and a word she hadn't spoken aloud since the pyres. But Tam didn't need to know that. Nobody needed to know anything. Ignorance was the thickest wall in Corvessa, and Odile had built hers brick by brick.

He paid her four coppers, which was robbery by any honest measure, and he paid it gladly, and he walked away holding the little braid cupped in both hands like a bird he was afraid to crush. Odile watched him go and felt the old feeling rise in her chest — the one that was half pride and half grief and entirely against her survival.

*You're keeping a dead thing alive,* Ferren used to say, back when he was Master Ferren of the Weavers' Guild and she was eleven years old with singed fingers and a head full of cantrips. *A tradition isn't a fire, Odile. It's an ember. Your whole job is to keep it small enough to survive.*

He'd said it like a blessing. Lately, when she remembered it, it sounded more like a curse.

* * *

The night market filled the way fog fills a harbor: gradually, then all at once. Lamplighters worked down Saltrow Lane with their long poles, and the stalls bloomed out of the dark — fishwives with silver-bellied catch, a spice man whose saffron cost more than Odile earned in a month, a knife-grinder whose wheel threw orange sparks that everyone pretended not to find beautiful. Somewhere a fiddler tuned up. Somewhere else, someone was already drunk enough to sing.

And through it all, moving in pairs, the hunters.

They wore the Crown's grey and the Crown's ash-leaf badge, and they didn't hurry. That was the worst of it, Odile thought, watching two of them drift along the far side of the lane. They didn't need to hurry. The war was over; they were just sweeping up. One of them stopped at the spice stall, turned a jar over in his gloved hand, asked the spice man something that made him laugh too loudly. The other stood with his back to the crowd, watching faces.

Odile lowered her eyes to her thread and began winding a skein. It was the oldest trick in the weaver's book: be boring. Boring was armor. A woman winding wool was furniture; a woman watching hunters was a confession.

But her fingers, winding, kept count of things her eyes weren't supposed to notice. Four hunters tonight, not the usual pair. Grey cloaks, ash-leaf badges, and — this was new — a third man in black walking behind them with a ledger under his arm. Not a hunter. Hunters carried swords and torches. Men with ledgers carried lists.

*A census,* she thought, and her stomach went cold around the supper she hadn't eaten. *They're counting us.*

Counting what, though? Every soul in Saltrow Lane? Or every soul who bought from the knot-woman? She thought of Tam Wicker walking away with a fever-charm cupped in his hands, and had to make herself breathe slowly, the way Ferren had taught her for steadying a spell. In for the length of a knot. Hold for the length of a knot. Out.

The fiddler started up a jig. The market breathed and went on.

* * *

Her first real customer of the night came at moonrise, and she was the last person Odile expected: Maren Ostrey, the chandler's widow, who had sworn after the pyres that she wanted nothing ever again that smelled of magic, who had crossed the street for two years to avoid Odile's stall.

"Mistress Sandoval." Maren stood at the edge of the cloth, hands knotted together, wearing the expression of a woman about to step into cold water. "I — they say you sell knots."

"I sell knots."

"My daughter can't sleep." The words came out fast, ashamed, like contraband. "Six nights now. The physick does nothing. The priest says it's a melancholy of the blood and charges me a tithe to say it." Her chin came up, defiant and terrified at once. "I don't believe in charms. I want you to know that. I don't believe in any of it. But I'll pay for a knot, if you have one that —"

"Maren." Odile kept her voice gentle and pitched low, under the fiddle. "It's wool. It's a pattern my grandmother taught me. If it helps your daughter sleep, that's the wool's business, not mine."

Something in the widow's face unclenched. This was the liturgy, and they both knew it, and the lie was the sacrament: neither of them believed it, and both of them needed it said.

Odile chose a sleep-knot — grey-green wool, braided in the pattern called *stillwater*, seven crossings and a hidden eighth — and pressed it into the widow's palm, folding her fingers closed over it. Under cover of the touch, barely a breath of voice, she spoke the words that lived behind her teeth:

*Still water, still child, till morning mild.*

Three syllables of Old Guildwork, the tongue of a guild that officially did not exist, spoken so softly that even the lamplight couldn't have caught them. The knot warmed against Maren's palm — a degree, no more, the warmth of a hand rather than a hearth — and the widow blinked, and decided not to notice, because noticing was dangerous and hope was already expensive enough.

"One copper," Odile said aloud. "Bring it back if it doesn't work."

Maren paid, and hesitated, and said, very quietly, without looking at her: "My husband thought the world of the guilds. He said the city would learn what it lost." Then she was gone into the crowd, and Odile sat with the copper in her hand and the words sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal.

*The city is learning,* she thought. *That's the trouble.*

* * *

Past midnight the crowd thinned to stragglers and thieves, and Odile let herself do the thing she only did when the lane was empty: she took out the traveler's bond and held it.

It was a beautiful piece of work, if she said so herself, and she did, silently, because there was no one left to say it for her. Two cords, one spun by her own hand, one bought from a Vael-touched loom that had come out of the mountains before the ban — though she'd tell no one that part. Braided together in the pattern called *two roads*, it would keep two people bound across any distance: whatever one felt, the other would feel an echo of, faint as a bell heard through a wall. Soldiers' wives had once bought them by the dozen. Now the pattern itself was treason, and the loom that made the second cord had been burned with everything else.

She was turning it over in her fingers, feeling the faint pulse of it — alive, patient, waiting for a second wrist — when the hair on her arms stood up.

Not magic. Instinct. The animal kind, older than any guild.

She looked up. The man in black with the ledger was standing ten feet away, at the edge of her lamplight, reading her stall the way a magistrate reads a confession.

"Good evening, mistress." His voice was pleasant. That was somehow worse than if it hadn't been. "Late hours, for a wool-seller."

"People buy knots at night or not at all," Odile said. She put the traveler's bond down among the skeins, casually, one piece of string among many. "Can I help you?"

"You can help the Crown." He produced the ledger and opened it, and she saw columns of neat handwriting, names and numbers, streets and tallies. "A survey. Household goods, trade goods, unusual inventory. The city fathers want a picture of Corvessa's commerce." His eyes moved over her rows of knots with the flat patience of a man counting sheep. "Luck-knots. Fever-charms. Sleep-knots." He read the labels she'd stitched on them herself, years ago, in her own innocent-looking script. "Charming names."

"Folk names," Odile said. "Grandmother's talk. Superstition sells better than sewing."

"Does it?" He smiled. He had a young face and old eyes, and the combination sat wrong, like a child wearing its father's coat. "My grandmother talked a great deal. About the Weavers' Guild, mostly. About how the knots they sold in this very market could cool a fever in an hour." He tilted his head. "Of course, that was superstition too. The Crown was very clear on that point. There's no such thing as magic. Which means—" he tapped the ledger with one finger— "there's nothing here worth burning. Is there, Mistress Sandoval?"

The name landed between them like a dropped knife.

Odile had prepared for this moment for three years. She had a story: she was Odile Sandoval, seamstress, daughter of a seamstress, no guild, no master, no history; the name was common enough in the river districts, there were a dozen of her in the parish rolls. She had rehearsed the shrug, the blank smile, the bored blink.

What she did instead was worse than any of it, because it was true: she said nothing. One heartbeat. Two. Long enough for a man with a ledger to write down.

"No," she said finally. "Nothing worth burning."

"Good." He closed the book. "Keep it that way." And he inclined his head, courteous as a dancing master, and walked away down Saltrow Lane, and the lamplight took him in pieces until the dark had all of him.

Odile sat very still. Around her the market packed up and guttered out — the fishwives stacking their trays, the fiddler sawing his last tune, the spice man snuffing his lamps one by one until the lane belonged to her and the rats. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had gone cold as river stones.

*He knows the name.* Or he suspected, and her silence had fed the suspicion, and either way the clock that had been ticking quietly for three years had just gotten louder. They would come back. Men with ledgers always came back, and next time they wouldn't come alone, and they wouldn't come to buy knots.

She should run. That was the sensible arithmetic. Pack the box, burn what couldn't be packed, take the road east and be a seamstress in some town where nobody knew the word *guild*. Ferren himself had taught her the escape routes, back when he still cared whether she lived.

Instead she found herself doing what she always did, the thing that would get her killed someday, the thing that was the whole of her stubborn foolish heart.

She began to pack the charms carefully, so none of them touched.

Because tomorrow night there would be a market, and Tam Wicker's mother would still be coughing, and Maren Ostrey's daughter would still be staring at the ceiling, and some other frightened soul would stand at the edge of her cloth with shame all over their face, asking for string. The guilds were ash. The masters were ash. The Old Tongue was a whisper behind the teeth. But the fever-charm worked, and the sleep-knot worked, and as long as they worked, somebody had to make them.

*An ember,* Ferren had said. *Keep it small enough to survive.*

She blew out her lamp and climbed the stairs to her attic with the box under her arm, and told herself the cold in her stomach was just the river fog.

She was halfway through locking her door when she heard it: a knock. Soft. Twice. From the inside of the landing below — where, at this hour, there should have been nothing but dark and dust and the smell of old rope.

Then, through the wood, thin and clear, a child's voice speaking words Odile had not heard in fifteen years — not grandmother's talk, not market superstition, but true Old Guildwork, the ceremonial tongue, the language of oaths.

And every charm in her box woke at once, humming like struck glass.
