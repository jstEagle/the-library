## 2. A Knock at the Door

The knock came three hours past midnight, and it was wrong in every way a knock could be wrong.

Odile knew the sounds of her building the way she knew her own pulse: the widow Brannic's cough through the floorboards, the rats in the walls with their industrious little feet, the groan of the roof beam when the wind came off the harbor. She had lived three years in this attic above the dyer's shop and no one had ever knocked on her door, because no one knew she existed. That was the entire architecture of her survival. The charm-seller of the night market was a rumor wearing a shawl; Odile Sandoval, who had once been the youngest oath-keeper the Guild of the Bound Word had ever raised, was supposed to be ash with the rest of them.

So when the knock came — soft, deliberate, twice and then again — she lay still under her blanket and told herself it was the beam settling.

It knocked again.

She rose without lighting the lamp. Her charms hung from the rafters in rows, forty-odd small works of thread and bone and river-glass, each one knotted with a whisper of intent that any guild-trained eye would have recognized as magic and any hunter would have recognized as a death sentence. In the dark they were only shapes, pale clusters like moths at rest. She moved between them by memory, took down the iron needle she kept hidden inside the false seam of her mattress, and put her eye to the peephole she'd drilled behind a hanging charm of dried lavender.

A child stood on the landing.

Nine years old, maybe ten. Barefoot on boards that were cold enough to crack skin this time of year. A shift of undyed linen that had never been cut by any tailor Odile knew of — no seams, no hem, as if it had been grown rather than made. Hair the color of deep water. And she was standing perfectly, unnaturally still, the way nothing alive stands still, not even in sleep.

Odile's first thought was *hunter's trick*. They'd used children before, or so the stories said — glamoured things, or worse, children coerced with threats against their families, sent to knock on doors that opened onto witches. Her second thought was that the stories were mostly invented by hunters to excuse what they did to actual children.

Her third thought arrived when the child spoke.

"*Vaelen dor atha sulen.*"

Three words in a language Odile did not know, and yet her body knew it before her mind could object. Every charm in the attic hummed.

Not rattled. Not stirred by draft. *Hummed* — a low, resonant chord, forty threads singing one note, the sound a tuning fork makes when you strike it against something true. Odile felt it in her back teeth, in the bones of her wrists, in the scar tissue over her heart where she'd burned her oath-mark off with a coal iron three years ago rather than wear something the hunters could find. The scar woke up. It had been numb since the night the guildhouse fell. Now it itched like a healing wound, like something remembering how to be skin.

"*Vaelen dor atha sulen,*" the child said again, patient, and pressed one small palm flat against the door.

Through the wood, Odile felt it: warmth, spreading outward from the child's hand in rings, like a stone dropped in still water. The lavender charm swayed though there was no wind.

Every instinct Odile owned said *run*. Out the window, down the drainpipe, into the maze of Corvessa's rooftops where she'd rehearsed her escape a hundred times in the dark hours after nightmares. But her feet didn't move. Because the humming hadn't stopped, and beneath it — faint, absurd, impossible — she heard something else. The charms weren't just answering the child. They were answering each other. Forty separate spells, knotted over three years by a woman who had sworn never to let them touch, waking up and reaching toward one another like plants toward light.

"Oh," Odile said aloud, to no one. "Oh, that's not good."

She unbarred the door.

* * *

Up close, the child was stranger and more ordinary at once. Mud to the knee. Eyes too old for the face they sat in, the way certain old dogs' eyes are old. Chapped lips, cracked heels, a smudge of soot along one jaw — a real body, then, a body that had walked here from somewhere. Hunters' glamours didn't get chapped lips.

"You shouldn't be here," Odile said, keeping her voice low. Sound carried in the stairwells of Corvessa tenements the way water carries blood. "Whatever you want, whoever sent you—"

"*Vaelen dor atha sulen,*" the child said, and this time Odile heard the structure of it, the grammar underneath the strangeness. It wasn't random. It was a question. Or an address. Something formal, something with rules.

"I don't speak it," Odile whispered. "Whatever it is. I don't."

But that was a lie, and the child seemed to know it was a lie, because she tilted her head, and the humming changed key.

Because Odile *did* speak it. Not fluently — barely. Three years was a long time, and even before the fall, the old tongue had been taught in fragments, guarded syllable by guarded syllable, because language was power and the guilds had learned the hard way what happened when power leaked. But she'd been the best student Master Ferren had ever had, and Ferren had been careless with her precisely because she was the best, teaching her things he should have locked away. She knew perhaps two hundred words of the guild-tongue. She had spent three years not saying any of them out loud.

She looked down the dark stairwell. She listened to the sleeping city. Then she said, in a voice she barely recognized as her own:

"*Sulen vaelen. Atha dor?*"

Roughly: *I hear you. What do you want?* Her accent was probably atrocious. It didn't matter.

The child's whole face changed. It was like watching ice become water — the stillness breaking into motion, relief flooding in so fast and so total that the girl actually staggered, caught herself on the doorframe, and then answered in fluent, rapid, unmistakable Common:

"You *do* know it. Oh, thank the — yes. Yes. I've walked for six days. Everyone else, when I speak, they hear birdsong, or they hear nothing, or they get angry and don't know why. One man threw a boot at me." She said this without any grievance, as if reporting weather. "You heard me. You *answered* me."

"How did you find me?" Odile asked. "Who sent you?"

The child looked at her with those old eyes, and Odile watched her decide something — watched the decision move through her face like weather.

"The river sent me," she said. "And the bells. And a dead woman whose name I can't say properly because it's in the other tongue. You're the keeper. The last one who keeps without being told to."

"I'm a charm-seller," Odile said. "I sell luck-knots. Fever-charms. Little things. Harmless things."

"They're not harmless," the child said, with mild certainty, glancing past Odile into the humming attic. "Nothing that sings is harmless."

* * *

Odile got her inside because the alternative was leaving a barefoot child on a landing at three in the morning in a city where the Crown's writ ran however it pleased, and whatever else Odile Sandoval had become in three years of hiding, she had not become the kind of person who did that. She told herself that was the whole reason. She was aware, distantly, that the humming charms argued otherwise.

She fed her bread and honey and the last of the winter apples, and the child ate with the mechanical thoroughness of someone who had genuinely walked six days, and while she ate, Odile asked questions the way you defuse a trap: slowly, from a distance.

"What's your name?"

The child considered this for longer than the question deserved. "I had one," she said finally. "Before. It doesn't fit anymore. Like a shoe." She wiggled her bare foot as if to demonstrate. "You can call me what the river called me. It called me *the remainder*."

"That's a terrible name."

"It's accurate," the child said, and bit into her apple.

"Where are your parents?"

"I don't have parents. I have *origins*." She chewed. Swallowed. "There's a difference. Parents make you out of love or accident. Origins make you out of purpose. I'm all purpose, unfortunately. It's very tiring."

Odile set down the knife she'd been using to cut bread, mostly so she wouldn't be holding a knife. "Child. Look at me. Who taught you to talk like that?"

"No one taught me. That's the point of me." The girl licked honey off her thumb. "You're frightened," she observed. "Your charms have gone quiet again. They do that when you're frightened. They're very loyal to your moods. It's sweet."

Odile glanced up. The charms hung motionless, and she realized with a lurch that the girl was right — they'd been quiet since the moment fear had tightened in her chest, forty spells holding their breath on their mistress's behalf. Three years she'd knotted those threads, and she had never once noticed they were listening to her.

"Six days ago," the girl went on, "the river in the Vellish Quarter started running backward. Did you see? Everyone said it was the tide. It wasn't the tide. Six days ago something broke, far away, and everything with a seam in it started coming loose, and I was sent to find the people who could sew." She looked up. "You sew."

It was such a strange way to put it that Odile laughed — one short, involuntary bark of a laugh, the kind that escapes before fear can catch it. The girl smiled, quick and luminous, and for a moment she looked exactly her age, nine or ten, a mud-streaked kid eating stolen apples in a witch's attic, and Odile felt something in her chest crack open along an old seam.

Then the girl said, "They'll come tomorrow. Maybe the day after. The ones who hunt. They followed my trail as far as the river, and the river hid me, but rivers can't hide anyone forever, especially now, when they're confused about which way is downstream." She said it the way other children announced that school started Monday. "So we have to go somewhere. You know somewhere. Your face says you know somewhere."

"My face says no such thing."

"Your face says many things. You should sell fewer charms to it." The girl finished her apple and set the core neatly on the table, aligned with the table's edge, precise as a signature. "Master Ferren," she said. "That's the somewhere. Isn't it?"

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Odile had not spoken that name aloud in three years. Had not written it, had not let herself think it fully on most days, because thinking it led to the guildhouse and the guildhouse led to the fire and the fire led to the screaming, and she had built her whole quiet life on the principle that some doors stay shut. And here was a barefoot child of no particular origin saying it like an address.

"I don't know any Master Ferren," Odile said.

"Liar," the girl said, without heat, almost fondly. "Your heartbeat said his name before your mouth denied it. Everything in you is honest except your mouth. It must be exhausting."

Odile stood up. Walked to the window. Stood there looking out at Corvessa at four in the morning — the crooked roofs, the harbor fog, the palace hill with its watch-fires burning steady and mean against the dark — and tried to think like a survivor instead of like whatever she was becoming.

Here was what she knew, laid out cold: a child appears speaking a dead language, and her outlawed magic answers it like a struck bell. The child knows a name she should not know. The child claims something broke six days ago, and Odile had seen the river running backward with her own eyes and told herself the same lie everyone else had. And the child said hunters were following her trail, which meant that wherever this trail ended, it ended in fire, because that was the only ending the Crown knew how to write.

Here was what she felt, which was worse: that the humming had been the first time in three years her magic had sounded *glad*.

"You can sleep here tonight," Odile said at last, without turning around. "In the corner. On the good blanket. Tomorrow I'll figure out what you are and where you go, and it will not involve me, and it will not involve Ferren, because Ferren is dead, as far as I'm concerned, and as far as the Crown is concerned, and that's the safest thing either of us can be."

"All right," the girl said agreeably, curling up on the good blanket like a cat. "You'll change your mind. You keep things alive. It's what you're for. You won't be able to help it."

"Go to sleep."

"Will you sing?" the girl asked, already half-gone, eyes closing. "When you think I can't hear. Your charms like it when you sing. They lean toward you."

"I don't sing."

"Mm," said the child, and slept — instantly, completely, the way the drowned sleep and the dead sleep, as if sleep were simply a place she stepped into and shut the door behind her.

* * *

Odile did not sleep.

She sat by the banked stove with the iron needle across her knees and watched the child breathe, and turned the night over and over like a coin she couldn't stop biting.

Twice, near dawn, the girl murmured in her sleep — fragments in that other language, the one that made the rafters hum. And both times, Odile found that she understood the fragments without wanting to. Not translated; *recognized*, the way you recognize a face across a crowded market, someone you haven't seen since childhood. *The door is thin. The door is thin and the wolves remember their names.*

And once — just once, in the grey hour before sunrise — the child said clearly, in Common, eyes still closed:

"He didn't send me, you know. The old man. He doesn't even know I exist yet. I was sent ahead of the news." A pause. Breathing. Then, softer, almost pitying: "You should decide soon whether you're going to forgive him. It takes longer than you'd think."

Odile sat very still until full daylight came, and the bells of Corvessa rang the hour, and every bell in the city rang slightly, unmistakably, out of tune.
