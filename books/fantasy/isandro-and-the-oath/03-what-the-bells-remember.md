## 3. What the Bells Remember

The bells of Corvessa had not rung in three years, and Odile had learned to miss them the way you miss a tooth — a small absence that ached every time your tongue found the gap.

They'd been pulled down on the Edict's first morning. The Crown's men had come with ropes and oxen and a writ signed in vermilion ink, and they'd taken the bells from the Cathedral of Saint Vessa, from the Guildhall tower, from the little chapel by the fish market where Odile's mother had once been married. Melted some of them for cannon. Sold others to merchants in the southern ports who didn't care what songs a bell had sung so long as it rang true over their warehouses.

But the guild-taught among them knew what the bells had really been. Not clocks. Not calls to prayer. Anchors. Each one cast with a thread of oath-silver in its heart, each one humming at the frequency the old covenant had been sworn in, holding the city's thin places shut the way a stitch holds a wound.

Without them, Corvessa leaked.

Odile felt it now as she climbed the switchback stairs out of the Undermarket, the child's hand cold in hers. The air had a wrongness to it, like a room where someone has just been crying. Lamps burned green at their edges. A cat watched them pass from a windowsill with eyes too large and too gold, and did not blink, and Odile did not look back at it, because looking was an invitation, and she could not afford invitations tonight.

The child came willingly. That was the worst part. She walked when Odile walked, stopped when Odile stopped, and never once tried to pull away — as if being led through the dark by a stranger were simply the next expected thing, the way water expects to run downhill. When Odile had asked her name, back in the attic, the girl had answered in that other tongue, the old one, the one that made the luck-knots on Odile's shelf hum like struck glass. Then she had looked at Odile with those grey eyes — eyes the color of rain about to decide something — and waited.

*Sent*, Odile thought again. *Not lost. Sent.*

She took the long way, the way a hunted animal takes, doubling back twice along the canal road and once through the tannery yard where the stink covered all other smells, including, she hoped, the smell of magic. Three years of hiding taught you habits like scar tissue. You learned which cobbles sat hollow over the smugglers' runs. You learned which watchmen took bribes and which took trophies. You learned to walk like a woman carrying nothing but bread.

Except tonight she was carrying everything. Tonight she was carrying a child who spoke in a language that had been illegal to *write down*, let alone speak aloud, since before the child was born.

* * *

Master Ferren lived, when he lived anywhere, above a chandler's shop in Saltrow, in rooms that smelled of tallow and old paper. Getting to him meant crossing the Ropebridge district at an hour when honest people were asleep and dishonest ones were awake, and Odile moved through it with her hood up and her heart going like a drum in a procession.

She had not seen him in four months. Four months since the last time he'd summoned her — a chalk mark on the third step of the Tanners' Stair, a sign only guild-trained eyes would read — and she'd found him thinner, grayer, more bent over his books, as if the years since the fall were pressing him flatter into the world. He had been the Master of Knots and Bindings, first chair of the Weavers' Guild, and he had taught her everything: how to tie a luck-knot so the threads remembered their purpose, how to sing a fever-charm down to a whisper, how to be small. *Survival is also a craft*, he used to say. *Learn it well, because you will practice it longer than anything else.*

He had been right. He was usually right. It was one of the things she loved about him and one of the things, lately, she had begun to fear.

The chandler's boy let her up without a word; he'd been paid to see nothing. Ferren opened the door before she knocked, which meant he'd felt her coming, which meant he still had enough craft left to feel things coming, which steadied her more than she wanted to admit.

"Odile." His eyes went past her shoulder to the child, and Odile watched forty years of composure fail him all at once — watched his face do something complicated and terrible, like a map tearing along its fold lines. "Saints' teeth. Get inside. Get inside now."

* * *

The room was small and crammed with the wreckage of a dead profession. Books in stacks that leaned like drunks. Bundles of thread sorted by color and, beneath the color, by something else — by weight, by memory, by the old names. A loom stood against one wall under a dust sheet, and Odile had never once seen him touch it.

Ferren lit two lamps instead of one, then a third, until the room blazed, and Odile understood: light was armor tonight. He knelt in front of the child, slowly, joints cracking, and put himself below her eye level the way you'd kneel to a queen or a very dangerous animal.

"What's your name?" he asked, gently, in Vessian, the common tongue.

The child looked at him and said nothing.

"Try the other way," Odile said quietly. "She answers the old tongue."

Ferren went very still. "Odile," he said, without turning around, "what exactly are you telling me?"

"She came to my door at moonset. Barefoot. Alone. She speaks—" Odile heard her own voice go thin. "She speaks the guild-tongue, Master. The deep one. The one we weren't allowed to write after the third year. And when she speaks it, my charms answer her. All of them. At once."

Ferren rose. It took him a while. He crossed to his desk with the deliberation of a man walking toward something he'd spent three years pretending wasn't coming, and from the bottom drawer — the locked one, the one Odile had been told at nineteen never to open and had never opened — he took a book.

It was small. Black leather gone soft as cloth with age. No title. The lock on its clasp had been broken long ago, or perhaps never fastened; perhaps the book simply trusted no one enough to matter.

"The last time I heard that language spoken," Ferren said, "I was younger than you are now, and the man speaking it was Master Aldous Vey, First Chair of Chairs, and he was standing in the Guildhall tower telling us that the bells would keep us safe forever." He set the book on the desk between them like a man laying down a card he can't take back. "Three weeks later the Edict came. Three weeks later the bells came down. Draw your own conclusions about forever."

"Master—"

"Speak to her," he said. "In the old tongue. Ask her name. I need to hear it."

So Odile knelt where Ferren had knelt, and took the child's cold hands in hers, and said, in the deep guild-tongue, the words every apprentice learned first and was forbidden to say last: *"Who walks? Who asks?"*

The child smiled. It transformed her face entirely — from eerie to heartbreakingly ordinary, a nine-year-old pleased to be understood at last.

And she answered.

Her voice was still small, still a child's voice, but the room changed around the sound of it. The lamps leaned their flames toward her. The bundles of thread stirred on their shelves like sleeping birds. And Odile felt every charm she carried — the fever-song braided into her left wrist, the warding knot sewn into her hem, the little luck-threads she sold by the dozen — wake up and turn toward the child like flowers toward sun.

*"I am the part that was promised,"* the child said. *"I am what remains when a promise forgets itself. I have come to find the hands that will remember me."*

Silence. The lamps straightened slowly, flames remembering themselves.

Ferren sat down. Not gracefully. He missed the stool's edge by an inch and caught himself on the desk, and for a moment Odile thought he might actually weep, and realized with a lurch that she had never once, in all her years of knowing him, seen him close to tears.

"Oathscript," he said finally. His voice sounded scraped. "That was Oathscript, Odile. Not the guild-tongue. Older. The guild-tongue is a — a descendant, a grandchild. What she speaks is the parent. The tongue the covenant was sworn in, back when the realms were—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you know why the bells had oath-silver in them? Do you know what the Weavers actually wove, before we were reduced to luck-knots and fever-charms?"

"We kept the peace," Odile said. "Between here and the hidden world. The Vael. Everyone knows the stories."

"Stories." Ferren laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "Yes. Stories. The Crown tells stories now. But the truth is this: there was a covenant. Sworn at a place called the Sunken Altar, in the first age of this city, between humankind and the Vael — the people of the other side, the hidden world, call them what you like. The Oath bound both realms together. It kept their borders gentle. It let magic flow between worlds the way water flows between connected rivers. And it required keepers. Sworn hands on both sides, maintaining it, renewing it, generation after generation." He looked at his own hands, folded on the desk, as if checking they were still his. "We were the keepers, Odile. The guilds. That was always what the guilds were. Everything else — the charters, the trade rights, the feasts — that was dressing. We were the hands that held the promise closed."

"And the Edict—"

"The Edict didn't outlaw magic, child. Magic can't be outlawed any more than gravity can. The Edict killed the *keepers*. Cut the hands off the covenant and called it law and order." He rubbed his face. "An Oath without keepers doesn't die cleanly. It frays. It has been fraying for three years, and the fraying is accelerating, and the things coming through the thin places — the monsters your customers whisper about, the lights in the drowned quarter, the animals born wrong — that isn't invasion. That's a wound going septic."

The child had climbed onto the bench beside Odile and was watching Ferren with an expression far too old for her face. Patient. Almost sorrowful. As if she had heard this lecture before, in some older classroom, from some older mouth.

"So she's—" Odile couldn't finish it. She tried again. "You're saying she's a piece of the Oath. Made flesh."

"I'm saying she may be the last living piece of it." Ferren opened the black book at last, turning pages of script so old it predated the alphabet Odile knew, though she recognized its bones the way you recognize a grandmother's face in a stranger's. "There were fragments. The old texts speak of them. When a covenant weakens, it... sheds. Casts off pieces of itself the way a dying fire casts sparks, hoping one will land somewhere it can burn. If the Oath is failing — truly failing, not fraying but *failing* — then it would send out its last fragment to find keeper's hands. To find someone who could carry it to the place it was first sworn." He looked up, and his eyes were terrible with certainty. "The Sunken Altar."

"Where is that?"

"Underneath us. Beneath the city. Beneath the drowned quarter, deeper than the smugglers' runs, deeper than anything the Crown has mapped. And getting there requires things we no longer have — roads, keys, names. It requires the surviving keepers, and there are perhaps five of us left in this whole city, and two of us are in this room." He closed the book. "And it requires time, and the fraying will not give us time. I've felt it, these last months. Haven't you? The charms costing more. The wards thinning. The nights getting longer in ways that have nothing to do with the season."

Odile had felt it. She'd told herself it was age, or fear, or imagination. She thought of the cat with the too-gold eyes. The green-edged lamps. The way the river had smelled wrong last week, sweetish, like something blooming underwater that shouldn't bloom.

"Why her?" she asked, and hated how small her voice came out. "Why me? Master, I sell luck-knots to people who don't know what they're buying. I hide. I'm *good* at hiding. I have spent three years making myself invisible, and it worked, and I wanted—" Her voice cracked and she pressed on, furious at herself. "I wanted to be forgotten. I wanted history to walk right past my door. And now history has knocked on it barefoot at moonset, and you're telling me it's mine to carry."

Ferren regarded her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something in his.

"You wanted to be forgotten," he said, "because you remember what happened to everyone who was remembered." He came around the desk and put his hands on her shoulders — the old gesture, the teaching gesture, the one that had guided her fingers through ten thousand knots. "I know. Believe me, I know. But hear me, Odile, and hear me clearly: the Oath did not choose you because you are brave or pure or destined. Forget the ballads. It chose you because you kept working. Because when every other keeper ran or burned or bent the knee, you stayed at your little stall in the night market selling fever-charms to people who needed them, and you never stopped, not for one single night. Keeping is a habit. You built the habit. The fragment came looking for habit's hands."

"That's not choosing. That's just—"

"It's the only kind of choosing that matters." He squeezed her shoulders once, hard, and let go. "Now. There are others we must gather. Maren the cartographer, if she lives — blind as a mole now, but she mapped the undercity from memory and her memory is better than any living eyes. Brother Callum, defrocked, drinking, but he was ordained in the old rite and the old rite matters more every day. And we'll need luck besides, because the hunters—"

A sound from the window cut him off.

Not a knock. A scraping — faint, rhythmic, deliberate. Like a fingernail drawn along glass.

All three of them turned. The window over the chandler's yard showed nothing but night, and the night showed nothing back. But across the street, on the roofline of the cooper's shop, a lantern was moving. Not swinging. *Sweeping.* Side to side, patient, methodical, the way a man searches a room he already knows contains what he wants.

"Hunters don't sweep rooftops," Ferren breathed. "They search doorways. That's a pattern-search. Someone gave them a location."

Odile's blood went cold. "Nobody knew I was coming here. Nobody *could* know. I doubled back twice, I—"

"Not you." Ferren was already moving, snuffing lamps, his hands steady in a way his voice was not. "Them. They're not searching for you specifically. They're searching for *it*." He nodded at the child, who had risen silently to her feet and was looking at the sweeping lantern with an expression Odile would remember for the rest of her life: recognition. Not fear. Recognition, like someone greeting an old acquaintance across a crowded room.

"They can feel her," Ferren said. "God help us, the fraying cuts both ways — the thin places leak inward too, and the Crown's witch-finders have instruments now, ugly ones, and something that radiates this much raw oath-magic cannot be hidden. Not forever. Not even by me." He thrust the black book into Odile's satchel, shoved a bundle of thread-cords after it, and pushed them both toward the back stair. "Saltrow tunnel, then the Ropebridge, then get to Maren's — corner of Quill Lane and the canal, blue door, tell her Ferren sent you and show her your wrists. Go now. I'll follow by the long way. Odile—"

He caught her arm at the top of the stair. In the dark his face was mostly shadow, but his grip was the old grip, sure as a knot.

"Whatever happens," he said, "remember what you are. Not what they called us. Not criminals, not witches. *Keepers.*"

Then he pushed her into the dark, and behind them, faint and far off across the sleeping city, Odile heard — or thought she heard, or maybe the fraying world simply let her hear it — the ghost of a bell that no longer existed, tolling once, low and slow, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The child's hand found hers in the darkness. Small. Cold. Certain.

And somewhere behind them, boots were starting to move.
