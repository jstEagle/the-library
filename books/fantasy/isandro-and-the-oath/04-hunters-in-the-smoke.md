## 4. Hunters in the Smoke

The tip-off came as a smell.

Odile had worked the night market long enough to know its weather by scent — frying oil and wet wool on a good night, river rot when the tide turned against the drains. But this was something else: tallow smoke with an iron edge underneath, the smell of hunter's torches burning pitch cut with consecrated ash. The Crown bought that stink by the barrel. Three years ago it had come down her street every night for a week, and she still woke some mornings tasting it.

She was mid-sale, tying off a luck-knot for a fishwife who wanted safe passage past the harbor customs men, when the smell reached her stall between one breath and the next.

"Take it," she said, pressing the knot into the woman's palm before the final word of the blessing was out. "Go home the long way. Don't look back."

The fishwife frowned — she'd paid for a proper charm, not a rushed one — but Odile was already kicking the leg-fold of her table, letting it collapse flat, charms spilling into their padded tray in a cascade of thread and bone and knotted cord. Around her the market did what the market always did when hunters came: nothing, loudly. Vendors kept selling. Laughter kept up its brittle music. A hundred people pretending very hard that they hadn't heard the boots.

It never worked. It wasn't meant to work. It was meant to buy seconds, and seconds were all anyone ever got.

They came in from the Tanner's Gate end, six of them in grey-and-white, torches high, and behind them the local watch doing its ashamed best to look official. Odile marked the leader by his stride — young, eager, boots striking cobbles like he was owed them. Not Renn's patrol. Renn's patrols had a rhythm she'd learned to read across three years of near-misses, a certain weary economy. These were new blood. New blood didn't check stalls. New blood burned them.

She swept the last of her stock into the tray, folded it under her arm, and turned straight into the child.

The girl stood at the mouth of the alley behind her stall, barefoot as always, wearing the grey shift Odile had given her four nights ago. She was looking past Odile at the torches with an expression that wasn't fear. That was the worst part. Odile had spent days trying to teach her to hide — *when I say duck, you duck; when I say run, you don't ask where* — and the child absorbed the words the way stone absorbs rain, politely, without any sign they'd sunk in. She spoke now only when spoken to, in that language that made Odile's teeth ache and her charms hum, and sometimes, when she thought Odile wasn't listening, in a whisper that sounded like two voices agreeing.

"Behind me," Odile breathed. "Don't argue."

For once, the child obeyed.

The first scream came from the far side of the square — a charm-seller named Bess whose luck-bells were about as magical as kitchenware, which would save neither her nor her stall. Torches began finding awnings. The brittle laughter died all at once, the way a candle dies, and then the market was just noise and running and the smell of smoke getting serious.

Odile went the other way, into the alley, with the tray under one arm and the child's cold hand locked in the other.

* * *

The undercrofts beneath Corvessa were older than Corvessa, which everyone said and no one thought about. They had been cisterns once, then granaries, then smugglers' cellars, then nothing at all after the flood of the old king's reign drowned half of them and the city decided forgetting was cheaper than draining. The guilds had remembered, though. The guilds had always remembered. There were doors in Corvessa that opened onto stairs going down, and if you knew the knock, the stairs went somewhere useful.

Odile knew three doors. She ran for the nearest, down Candlemaker's Row, past shuttered windows and one old man who watched her pass without seeing her, because that was the arrangement the whole city lived by.

The door was green with rot and locked with a lock that hadn't been a real lock in a century. She set her palm against it.

Three years. Three years of knots and thread and sleight of hand, of charging honest coin for honest superstition, of keeping the smallest possible flame cupped in both hands so the wind of the world wouldn't find it. Her magic had gone quiet the way a held breath goes quiet. She had told herself it was safety. Standing there, with torchlight beginning to lick down the Row behind her and a child's fingers tightening around hers, she understood it had also been grief.

"*Open,*" she said, in the guild-tongue, and put her will behind the word.

The lock remembered being a key. The door swung inward on air that smelled of deep water and older stone, and Odile felt her own power move through her arm like blood returning to a limb that had slept too long — painful, flooding, almost obscene in its relief. She stumbled onto the stairs and hauled the door shut behind them, and the dark took them whole.

Down. The steps were slick and narrow and the child moved through the black without hesitation, which Odile noted and refused to think about. Water rose over their ankles, then their shins. Somewhere ahead, drips counted time in a voice too slow to be a clock.

"Can you see?" Odile whispered.

The child answered in Oathscript, soft, and every charm in Odile's tray hummed in answer, a faint chord through the wicker, like struck glass. In the dark, the sound was enormous.

"I'll take that as a yes," Odile said, and kept moving.

* * *

They had gone perhaps a quarter mile through the drowned galleries when the light found them.

It came around a bend ahead — a single lamp, hooded, held low — and Odile pulled the child back against the wall and killed her own breath. Grey-and-white. Hunter's coat, though the white had gone grey with wear and the insignia had been unpicked, leaving pale ghost-threads where the Crown's sunburst used to sit. The man holding the lamp was broad, bearded, moving with the careful tread of someone who knew these tunnels better than he wanted to.

Renn.

Odile's stomach dropped through the water. Of all the hunters in Corvessa, Renn was the one she could least afford to meet, because Renn was the one who *looked*. He'd nearly caught her twice in the market's early days — once by following the hum of a fever-charm through a crowd like a hound following heat. He'd been demoted since; the whole market knew the story, though not its cause. He'd refused an order. Nobody said what order. He walked the low districts alone now, hunting without a warrant, which was either devotion or penance, and Odile had never wanted to learn which.

He stopped ten feet away. Raised the lamp.

Light climbed them: water, hem, tray of charms, Odile's face, the child's face. The girl looked back at him with that terrible calm, and Odile saw the exact moment he recognized what she was — or recognized that she was *something*, something the manuals didn't have a woodcut for. His breath changed. His grip shifted on the lamp.

"Don't," Odile said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to. "Whatever you're deciding, decide it slower."

"You're Halloran." Not a question. "The knot-woman."

"I sell thread."

"You sell thread that sings." He lifted the lamp higher, and the light caught the child fully — the eyes too old for the face, the stillness that wasn't human stillness. Something moved across Renn's features that Odile had never seen on a hunter's face. Recognition, maybe. Or grief. "There's a description out. Since last night. A child, nine years or so, mute or near enough, seen at the house of a charm-seller in the Weavers' Quarter." His jaw worked. "The tip said witch."

"The tip said witch," Odile agreed, "and you're standing in a flooded cellar instead of collecting your share of the fire above us. So let's not pretend we're having the same conversation."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the market screamed on. Somewhere a roof beam must have gone, because a sound rolled down through the city's bones like a struck bell, and the water around their legs shivered with it.

Renn looked at the ceiling. Then at the child. Then he did the thing that Odile would turn over in her mind for years afterward, looking for the seam in it, the trick, the trap: he lowered the lamp.

"They're not checking the undercrofts yet," he said. "Captain Veyse thinks the drowned quarter's beneath her dignity. You've got until dawn, maybe less." He stepped aside, clearing the passage, and the gesture cost him something visible — she watched it cost him, watched his shoulders set against whatever he'd been trained to do. "The second gallery forks. Left goes to the old temple cistern and a ladder up by the tannery. Right goes down into water nobody's measured. Take the left."

"Why?" Odile asked. It was the only question left. "Why let us go?"

Renn was quiet long enough that the drips filled the silence, counting.

"Two years ago I burned a woman's house in Saltrow," he said finally. "On a tip. Like tonight." His voice didn't shake, but it had gone flat, sanded down. "She had a daughter. The daughter wasn't in the house. I've told myself for two years that means she got out." He raised his eyes to the child, and Odile saw that he wasn't really seeing her — he was seeing someone else, someone two years gone. "I'm tired of telling myself things."

Then he turned his lamp away from them, deliberately, so its light fell on blank wall, and stood like a man standing guard over nothing while two fugitives waded past him through the dark.

Odile paused at his shoulder. Every instinct she owned said keep walking. But her mouth, which had never once in her life obeyed her instincts, said: "Renn. When they ask where you were—"

"They won't ask. I'm not worth asking about anymore." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "That's the one gift disgrace gives you. Go."

She went.

* * *

The ladder by the tannery came up inside a drying shed reeking of lime and hides, and beyond it the Weavers' Quarter lay under curfew, streets empty, windows dark except where fires still glowed in the direction of the market. Odile moved through the back lanes with the child's hand in hers and her heart going like a trip-hammer, and it wasn't until they were three turns from the shed that she realized she was still holding power in her free hand like a drawn knife — a thread of working light wrapped around her fingers, ready, unconcealed.

She had used magic openly. Twice in one night. After three years of being no one, of being a woman who sold thread.

And it had felt — she made herself admit it, climbing the attic stair, barring the door, setting the tray down among its fellows — it had felt like breathing.

The child sat down on the floor by the cold hearth, folded her hands, and looked up at Odile with those eyes that had watched torches burn a market without blinking.

"They will come again," she said. In trade-tongue, this time. She saved her own language for important things, Odile had noticed, the way other children saved sweets. "Not for the market. For me."

"I know."

"The one who sent me said you would know." The child tilted her head. "He said you were the kind who keeps things alive even when keeping is forbidden. He said—" and here she switched to Oathscript, and every charm in the attic rang softly, a roomful of small bells stirred by no wind, "—he said the last keeper is always the one who never wanted to be one."

Odile stood in her own attic, among her outlawed little flames, and felt the words settle over her like snow.

"Who sent you?" she asked. "Tell me his name."

The child considered her for a long moment. Outside, far off, a bell began to ring the alarm that meant the hunters had found something — or someone — in the ashes of the market.

"He said you would come ask Master Ferren," the child said. "And that Ferren would tell you the truth, or most of it." She unfolded her hands. In her palm lay a knot — a luck-knot, tied in Odile's own style, in thread Odile recognized, thread she had sold at her own stall not a week ago. "But he said to tell you this first, so you would go with your eyes open: *the man who taught you to hide taught himself to hunt.*"

The bell rang on. Odile looked at the knot in the child's small hand, and thought of Ferren in his hiding-place beyond the city walls, patient and kind and full of old knowledge, and felt the first cold thread of doubt work itself into everything she believed.

"We leave at first light," she said.
