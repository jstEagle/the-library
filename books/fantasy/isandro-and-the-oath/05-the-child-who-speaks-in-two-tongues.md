## 5. The Child Who Speaks in Two Tongues

She had been a promise before she was a person.

She remembered it the way other children remembered the warmth of a mother's arms — not as an event but as a condition, the whole of what existence was. She had been the space between two hands reaching for each other. She had been the word *yes* spoken across a border that had not yet learned to be a wall.

She had no name then. Names were things that belonged to people, and she had not been a person. She had been the Oath.

*We will remember you,* they had said, on both sides of the world, in voices like bells and voices like wind through hollow reeds. *As long as you are kept, we are one people.*

And she had been kept. For three hundred years she had been kept — carried in the mouths of oath-keepers who rose at dawn to speak her, carved into standing stones, sung into the marriage rites of families whose blood ran half in one world and half in the other. She had lived in every vow freely made between a human hand and a Vael one. She had been vast without having edges. She had been everywhere without going anywhere.

Then someone broke her.

She did not remember the breaking clearly. That was the cruelest part — that the moment of her shattering was also the moment she began to need memory, and memory came too late, arriving broken herself, in pieces, like everything else. What she had instead were fragments: a keeper's face wet with tears. A name spoken as a farewell instead of a greeting. The sound of a covenant tearing, which was not a sound at all but a silence so complete that both realms flinched from it.

After that there had been falling. Long falling, through years the way rain falls through air — slowly enough to see each one, fast enough that none could be held. She fell out of the stones first; the carvings went dark letter by letter. Then out of the songs. Then out of the mouths of the last keepers, one by one, until only a handful remained, whispering her into the dark like people blowing on embers that would not catch.

And at the bottom of the falling, she had gathered herself into the smallest shape that could still hold her — which was the shape of a child, because a promise is born small, and dies small, and is reborn small, and only lives large in between.

She had been sent. That much she knew with certainty, though she could not have said by whom. Perhaps by what remained of herself, scattered thin across two worlds. Perhaps by the worlds themselves, which were dying of the same wound and did not know it. Someone, or something, had taken the fragment that was left and given it feet, and pointed those feet toward the one city where a keeper still kept without knowing she kept.

Toward Odile Sandoval.

* * *

The woman was strange.

The child watched her now from the narrow bed beneath the slanted attic window, watching the way she had once watched centuries pass — with patience, with hunger, with the particular attention of something that has lost everything except noticing.

Odile moved through her small rooms the way water moves through cracks: thoroughly, quietly, without wasting anything. She mended a frayed luck-knot by lamplight, her fingers quick and certain, and as she worked she murmured under her breath — not words exactly, more the shape words leave behind when they've been said in one place so long they've soaked into the walls.

But the child heard them anyway. She heard everything now. Her ears were new and sharp and greedy, starved for language after her long fall, and Odile's half-voice murmurs rang in her like struck bronze.

*Keep the knot true. Keep the fever down. Keep her safe, whoever she is, the girl in the river district who bought this for her sister.*

That was the thing. That was the thing the child turned over and over in herself like a stone found in a streambed, smooth and strange and heavier than it looked.

Odile kept.

She kept without law, without guild, without oath — she didn't even know she was doing it. She tied luck into thread for strangers who thought they were buying trinkets. She spoke the old shapes into her work because her hands remembered what her mind had been made to forget. And when the child had come barefoot to her door speaking the oldest tongue in either world, Odile had not asked what the child was worth, or what she owed, or what she'd sworn.

She had asked if she was cold.

No keeper had ever asked the Oath if it was cold.

The child pulled the rough blanket higher and let the strangeness of it wash through her again. In the old days — in the days when she was everywhere — she had known ten thousand keepers. She had lived in their mouths and their morning rituals and their careful, reverent hands. They had loved her, she supposed, the way priests love a god: from below, from a distance, with fear folded into the love like salt into bread.

They had kept her because keeping was their duty. Because the alternative was ruin, and ruin was a fine teacher of devotion.

Odile kept because a girl in the river district had a sick sister, and Odile wanted the sister to live.

It was such a small reason. It should have been nothing. Instead it burned in the child's chest like the first coal of a fire that might, given tending, become the warmth of the whole world again.

*This one,* said something deep in her, in the voice she had before she had a voice. *If any can hold what must be held, this one.*

* * *

In the night she dreamed — or remembered; the two had the same texture now — of the breaking.

She stood in a hall of white stone that no longer existed, in a kingdom that had since eaten itself, and she watched a keeper named Ilvaine stand before the Altar with her hands full of everything she loved.

Ilvaine had been the greatest of them. First Voice of the covenant, the one whose tongue the Oath had been first spoken in, the one all others copied. And Ilvaine had loved — the child could never hold the beloved's face steady in memory; it slid away like fish through fingers — but she had loved someone on the far side of the line that was about to be drawn, and the law of the covenant said the line must be drawn, and Ilvaine had gone to the Altar to swear it anyway.

*Why?* the child always asked the dream. *Why swear the severing if you loved across it?*

And the dream-Ilvaine, whose face was rain, always answered the same way: *Because I believed duty would protect what love could not. Because I thought if I gave up my own heart to the covenant, the covenant would spare everyone else's.*

The child knew how that ended. Everyone knew how that ended, though almost no one alive remembered it. Ilvaine had sworn the Oath's darkest clause — the clause of separation, the great closing of the door — and the Oath, being made of promises, had tried to hold a promise that was a lie, because Ilvaine's heart had gone across the line even as her mouth swore it shut. A covenant cannot be built on a divided heart. It had cracked along the fault of her, and the crack had widened year by year, century by century, until three hundred years later it split entirely, and the fragment that was left had fallen into the shape of a child and landed on a doorstep in Corvessa.

Duty that costs love is already broken. The child had not had words for this truth when she was vast and edgeless. Now she had a body, and a body is where truths go to wait.

She woke before dawn with the taste of white stone in her mouth and found Odile already awake, sitting by the window with her knees drawn up, watching the city turn from grey to gold.

"You dream loudly," Odile said, without turning around. "Or you remember loudly. I couldn't tell which."

"Both," the child said.

Odile looked back at her then, and the child studied her face openly, greedily, the way she studied everything — because faces were new, and this one especially. There were lines around Odile's eyes that the child understood, now, were made by years of looking over her shoulder. There was a wariness in her that never fully slept. But underneath it, in the place where the child could see because the child had spent three hundred years living inside people's vows and knew where the true ones sat — underneath it there was a steadiness that no hunter and no fallen guild and no broken world had managed to shake loose.

"You're afraid of me," the child said. It wasn't an accusation. She was cataloguing.

"Yes," Odile said. "I'm also not going to send you away. Both things are true."

"I know." The child hugged her knees. "Both things are usually true. That's why promises are hard."

Odile huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You talk like a very old grandmother in a very small body. Has anyone told you that?"

"The last person who could have told me anything died before your grandmother's grandmother was born."

Silence. The gold light climbed the wall. Below them Corvessa woke — carts rattling, gulls arguing over the fish market, the bells of the low quarter ringing the hour in a city that had outlawed the magic its own bells were built to celebrate.

"Tell me what you were," Odile said at last, quietly, the way people ask questions they're afraid of the answers to. "Before. If you can."

The child considered lying, and found she didn't know how anymore; bodies made lying expensive. So she said, "Everything. I was everything that was promised between your people and mine. Every vow. Every handclasp across the veil. Every treaty and every wedding and every shared harvest. When your kind and the Vael swore anything to each other, they swore it *through* me. I was the road."

"And now?"

"Now I'm what's left of the road after the bridge fell." She looked at her own small hands, turned them over. "I'm a piece. I was sent to find the rest."

"Sent by whom?"

"That's the question, isn't it." The child smiled, and saw Odile flinch slightly — she was learning that her smiles unsettled adults, that they were too old for her face, and she filed this away with everything else. "I don't know. Maybe me. Maybe the world. Maybe the promise itself doesn't want to die. Promises are stubborn. I would know."

* * *

They went out at dusk, because Odile said the market was safer at dusk, and the child learned that in Corvessa even safety had a schedule.

She walked half a step behind Odile through lanes that smelled of tar and frying onions and river-mud, and she listened. Listening was what she was made of, fundamentally. Underneath the ordinary noise of the city she could hear the other thing — the sound of the wound. A wrongness under the cobbles, like a bone grinding in a joint that no longer fit. Magic leaking where it shouldn't. Somewhere north, a dog barking at a shadow that had no owner. Somewhere east, a well whose water had begun running faintly warm, which meant the veil was thin there, which meant the seal was failing there, which meant—

Which meant they had less time than Odile thought. Possibly less time than anyone thought.

"Odile," she said.

"Hm?"

"There are keepers left. Besides you. You know that, don't you?"

Odile's shoulders tightened, a small motion, but the child saw it the way she saw everything now, with eyes that had once seen across two worlds. "I know a man who might know some," Odile said carefully. "A teacher. He—" Something crossed her face, quick and complicated. "He taught me everything I know about the old ways. And then he let the guild burn rather than stand with it. Or that's what I've always believed. I haven't seen him in three years."

"You doubt him."

"I doubt everyone. It's kept me alive." Odile stopped at a stall, bought two skewers of charred fish with coins counted out one at a time, handed one back to the child. "Eat. You're too thin, and whatever you were before, you're nine years old now, and nine-year-olds need feeding."

The child took the skewer and ate, because bodies insisted, and because — this was the strange part, the part she kept returning to — because Odile had handed it to her. Not as tribute. Not as offering to the Oath, the way kings and priestesses had once fed her symbolically with golden grain and sacrificial wine. As food. For a hungry kid.

*I was a god,* she thought, licking grease off her thumb. *Now I am a child eating fish off a stick in a market that forbids me, and this is the first meal anyone has ever given me just because I was hungry.*

Alien tenderness, she thought. That was what Odile was made of. Tenderness so ordinary it was revolutionary.

"You're staring at me again," Odile said.

"I'm deciding something."

"Don't decide anything ominous. I've had a long week."

The child laughed — her first laugh in this body, and it startled her, a bright small sound that several nearby heads turned toward. She hadn't known she could do that. Bodies were full of surprises.

But the decision was made anyway, had perhaps been made the moment Odile opened the door and asked if she was cold. This woman — wary, doubting, magic-starved, hiding in plain sight among luck-knots and fever-charms — this woman was the hinge. The fragment could feel it in herself the way a compass feels north. Whatever remained of the Oath, scattered and dying across two realms, would gather or fail based on what happened next, and what happened next required the man Odile doubted, and the keepers he might know, and a road to a place the child remembered drowning.

"Take me to him," she said. "Your teacher. Tomorrow."

Odile looked down at her for a long moment. The market noise swelled and receded around them like a tide.

"He'll want to know what you are," Odile said. "He'll want it badly. And Master Ferren has never wanted anything mildly in his life."

"Good," said the child, who had been a promise for three hundred years and knew precisely what wanting did to the people who carried it. "Then we'll see what his wanting is worth."

Above the rooftops, unseen by either of them, the evening bell began to ring — and for the first time in three centuries, one note of it came out in Oathscript, clear as a struck glass, before the bellwarden could stop it and stare at his own rope in horror.
