## 6. The Last Guildhouse

The safehouse was a tannery once, before the river turned against that trade, and it still smelled of salt and old blood and the particular rot of things soaked too long. Odile climbed down into it through a trapdoor behind a shuttered chandlery, the child on her back like a small warm weight she was already afraid to put down.

Ferren waited below with a lamp turned low.

Three years since she'd seen him. He had gone gray at the temples in a way that looked deliberate, as if he'd decided aging was another disguise. His hands were the same — broad, scarred at the knuckles from a lifetime of knotwork — and when he reached for her she let him hold her shoulders a moment longer than comfort allowed.

"You brought her," he said. Not a greeting. An assessment.

"She brought herself."

"Even better." He crouched to the child's level, and Odile watched him do the thing he'd always done: make himself smaller, gentler, until you forgot he could fill a room. "Do you remember me?"

The child studied him with those eyes that were never quite the same color twice. Then she spoke — not Oathscript this time but the guild-tongue, the low murmuring language Ferren had taught Odile across a workbench when she was younger than this child was now.

*"The knot-tier. The one who taught the keeper to tie."*

Ferren's face did something complicated. "Close enough," he said softly, and stood, and did not look at Odile while he did it.

* * *

They came after dark, one by one, the way people had learned to move in Corvessa.

First was Maren Voss, the cartographer. She arrived leaning on the arm of a boy Odile took for a guide until Maren dismissed him with two coins and a word, and then she stood alone in the tannery's center with her white eyes half-lidded, mapping the room by sound. She was perhaps sixty, thin as a compass needle, and she wore a blindfold not over her eyes but around her throat, which Odile found herself staring at until Maren caught her at it.

"It keeps the echoes honest," Maren said, by way of explanation or warning. "Rooms lie to the sighted. Sound only lies if you ask it badly."

"You're the one who mapped the undercrofts," Odile said. "Before the Purge."

"I mapped everything." Maren smiled without much humor. "That was my crime. The Crown doesn't mind blindness. It minds a woman who can walk any city in the realm with her eyes shut and know where she is."

Second came Brother Callow, though he flinched at the title. He was defrocked within a season of the guilds' fall — too slow to burn his vestments, too stubborn to deny them — and he arrived carrying a satchel of books wrapped in oilcloth like a man transporting infants. He was round, soft-spoken, and his hands shook until they touched paper, at which point they went perfectly still.

"The liturgies survived," he told Ferren, unwrapping them with reverence. "Most of them. The Sunken Altar rite is fragmentary, but I have three independent copies of the opening invocation, and—"

"Callow." Ferren's voice was gentle. "Breathe. We'll get there."

And third — third came the knock nobody expected, three sharp raps in hunter's cadence, and Renn slipped inside before anyone could decide whether to be afraid.

He looked worse than the night in the undercrofts. Someone had worked him over since; there was a fresh split in his lip and his left eye had gone the color of a plum. He stood with his back to the wall out of habit, hands empty and visible, and said, "I know how this looks."

"How does it look?" Odile asked.

"Like a hunter walking into a room full of oath-keepers." He held her gaze. "Because that's what happened. I reported the raid on the market as a false tip. They'll figure that out by morning. So either you trust me now, or in about six hours I'm dead anyway, and dead men are terrible liars."

Silence. The child crossed the room — everyone watched her do it, even Ferren, especially Ferren — and stood in front of Renn, looking up at him with her shifting eyes.

She said nothing. She simply took his hand, turned it palm-up, and pressed her own palm against it, and held it there, and whatever passed between them made Renn go very still and then very quietly sit down on a crate like a man whose legs had been taken out from under him.

"Well," said Maren Voss dryly, "if the Oath itself vouches for him, I suppose the rest of us can manage."

* * *

They worked by lamplight while rain started somewhere above them, drumming on the tannery roof.

Callow laid out his fragments. Maren, astonishingly, drew — her fingers flying across wax tablet and stylus, producing lines that were straighter than any sighted hand had a right to make, correcting herself by tapping the stylus and listening to the pitch of the scratch. And Ferren stood over all of it, directing, connecting, being for one night exactly what he had been before the fall: a guildmaster.

Odile watched him and felt the old warmth and something newer underneath it, something she couldn't name yet.

"The Altar," Ferren said, "is beneath the city. Everyone assumes the old temples were built above ground because that's where ruins are found. But the first shrine wasn't a building. It was a hollow. A place where the boundary between realms wore thin naturally, before anyone swore anything there. The city grew over it the way a tree grows over a nail."

Maren's stylus stopped. "The drowned quarter."

"The drowned quarter," Ferren agreed. "When the river shifted two hundred years ago, it swallowed nine streets whole. The Crown called it a disaster. The guilds knew better. Water remembers what stone forgets — the flood didn't destroy the road to the Altar. It *hid* it."

"And the route?" Odile asked.

Maren began to draw. What emerged over the next hour was less a map than a descent: streets that no longer existed surfacing in her lines like drowned faces, stairways inside chimneys, a chapel whose crypt connected to a cistern that connected to — here her stylus hesitated, tapped, listened — "a door that isn't a door. There's a gap in my knowledge. One stretch, maybe half a mile, where every sound goes wrong. Even for me. I've never been able to map it. I call it the Hush."

"The Hush is the threshold," Callow said eagerly, cross-referencing his fragments. "Look — 'the last approach is walked unlit, unnamed, and alone.' That matches three sources. The final half-mile strips away everything you carry that isn't *you*. Charms fail. Names fail. If you go in carrying a false self, the Hush takes it."

Odile thought of her luck-knots, her fever-charms, three years of careful small magic sewn into thread. "Everything?"

"Everything," Callow said gently. "It's not cruelty. It's honesty. You can't swear an Oath wearing armor."

Renn, from his crate: "And hunters? Does it strip their warrants too?"

Maren almost smiled. "The Hush doesn't care what the Crown thinks you are."

* * *

It was past midnight when Callow reached the part he'd been dreading. Odile saw it coming — saw him smooth the same page three times, saw Ferren watching him do it — and still it landed like a dropped lantern.

"The ritual requires a life," Callow said. "Freely given."

The rain filled the pause.

"Not taken," he went on, quickly, as if speed could soften it. "Not coerced, not bargained, not traded. Every source agrees on the phrase — *vita data libera*, a life given free. The original covenant was sealed by a keeper who gave their life willingly at the Altar, and the Oath has run on that gift ever since. Three centuries of magic in both realms, bought once, paid once." He looked up. "If we reforge it, someone pays again."

"No," Odile said.

"We don't know the details of—"

"*No.*" Her voice cracked against the walls. The child, dozing against her side, stirred. Odile lowered it. "We are not building a new covenant on top of an old corpse. We're not trading one person's life for everyone else's safety. That's not an Oath, that's an arithmetic problem, and I refuse to solve it."

"Then what would you offer?" Ferren asked. It was a fair question and he asked it fairly, and that was somehow worse.

"I don't know. Not that."

Maren set down her stylus. "For what it's worth, I've known since Maren Voss became a blind woman that I'd pay for my art eventually. I always assumed it would be the Crown collecting." She shrugged. "I'm not volunteering. I'm noting that the price doesn't frighten me as much as the arithmetic does. The keeper who swore the first Oath chose it. Nobody chose for her. Whatever we rebuild has to keep that — the choosing."

"Aye," Renn said quietly. "Or it's just a law with better lighting."

Callow closed his books. "Then we have a route, a rite, and a price none of us will name tonight. I suggest we sleep, such as it is, and argue again at dawn."

But Odile was watching Ferren. He had turned toward the lamp, and the light caught his face in profile, and there was something moving behind his expression that she'd seen only once before — the night the guild fell, when he'd stood in the burning scriptorium and watched thirty years of work go up, and Odile had thought then that the look was grief.

She wasn't sure anymore.

"Master," she said quietly, when the others had settled. "What aren't you telling me?"

Ferren looked at her for a long moment. The lamp flickered between them.

"When the time comes," he said, "you'll understand why some prices are paid in private."

Above them, the rain thickened. And far off, at the edge of hearing — so faint Odile might have invented it — the bells of Corvessa began to ring, though it was nowhere near the hour, and every bell in the city was ringing slightly out of tune, as if the metal itself had begun to forget what it was for.
