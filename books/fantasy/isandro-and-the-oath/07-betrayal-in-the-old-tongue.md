## 7. Betrayal in the Old Tongue

The last guildhouse smelled of tallow and old paper, and for three days it had almost felt like home.

Odile learned the danger of that feeling later. Home was a thing Ferren had always known how to build — a warm room, a shared task, the sense that you were one of the chosen few who mattered. He had built it for her once before, in the years when the guild still had a name, and he was building it again now, brick by careful brick, around six frightened people in a cellar beneath a shuttered chandlery in the Saltrow.

She should have seen it. She would spend the rest of her life not seeing it, and then seeing it all at once, over and over, the way you taste a poison after it's too late to spit it out.

But that night she only knew that the candles were lit, that the blind cartographer Maren had finished her map, and that Master Ferren stood at the head of the table with his hands spread wide like a man blessing a harvest.

"The route is settled," he said. "Three days to the standing stones, two more through the Vael side if the gate will open at all. We move tomorrow, an hour past dusk, while the hunters change their watch."

The child sat beside Odile with her feet tucked under her on the bench, drawing shapes in spilled flour. Oathscript, Odile realized — little spirals and broken lines that hummed faintly against her teeth when she looked at them too long. The girl had been doing it all evening, patient as water, filling the table's edge with a language no living mouth should have known.

Renn leaned against the far wall, arms crossed, hunter's grey gone dusty at the shoulders. "The watch change is real," he said. "I wrote half those rotations myself, two years back. They haven't updated them." A pause. "They don't expect anyone to come out this way anymore."

"No," Ferren said softly. "They don't. Which is our whole advantage, Renn. Invisibility is the last gift the guild has left us."

Maren's fingers moved across her map without her eyes, reading the ink like braille. "The drowned quarter first," she said. "Then the weaver's stair. Then the stones." Her voice was dry as the paper she loved. "I've walked this road in my head four hundred times since yesterday. It holds."

Odile looked around the table: the defrocked priest Anselm sharpening a knife he'd sworn never to use; Maren with her ruined eyes and perfect memory; Renn pretending he wasn't already committed; the child, flour-dusted, humming. And Ferren above them all, silver-bearded, certain, lit from below by candleflame so that he seemed carved rather than born.

*We can do this,* she thought, and the thought itself was warm, and warmth, she would learn, was exactly how he caught you.

* * *

It was Anselm who found the first wrong thing, because priests are trained to notice what's missing from a room.

"Where's the ledger?" he asked, near midnight, when most of them had bedded down on pallets between the shelves of old candle molds.

"What ledger?" Odile said.

"Ferren kept one. Guild accounts, contacts, safehouses — I saw him writing in it the first night. Brown leather, brass corners." Anselm frowned at the dark. "He guarded it like a relic. Now I can't find it anywhere."

"He'll have hidden it," Odile said. "He hides everything. He hid me for three years."

But the words sat strangely in her chest, and she lay awake on her pallet listening to the child breathe, turning the missing book over in her mind the way you turn a stone to see what's underneath. Ferren did hide everything. That was the lesson of her whole apprenticeship: *never write what can be spoken, never speak what can be implied.* So why keep a ledger at all? Why keep a record — names, places, dates — unless somewhere, in some corner of himself he never showed her, he still believed the guild would rise again? Still believed there would be someone to *show*?

Or unless he believed there would be someone to sell it to.

No. She shut the thought away like a drawer. This was Ferren. This was the man who had carried her out of the plague-quarter at nine years old, who had taught her knotwork letter by letter, who had burned his own guild seal rather than let the hunters take it off his neck. Ambition didn't look like that. Ambition didn't bleed for people.

She fell asleep telling herself stories, which is what the loyal always do, right up until the moment they stop being able to.

* * *

The knock came at dawn. Three slow strikes, spaced like a heartbeat — the guild's old signal, the one that meant *friend, but be careful.*

Ferren answered it himself. Odile heard the bolt slide, heard low voices, and came up the stairs with the child's hand in hers to find a stranger in the doorway: a broad man in a dyer's apron, rain-wet, holding a sealed packet the way you hold something hot.

"From the Tallowman," the stranger said. "He said you'd want it before the others woke."

"I'll want nothing before the others wake," Ferren said pleasantly, and took the packet, and closed the door.

But Odile had seen his face in that half-second. Not surprise. Not fear. *Recognition* — the look of a man receiving something he had been promised.

"Who's the Tallowman?" she asked.

"An old friend. A supplier." Ferren tucked the packet into his coat without breaking the seal. "Nothing that concerns the road ahead. Go back to sleep, Odile. Tomorrow will ask everything of you."

She didn't go back to sleep. She sat by the cold hearth with the child curled against her side and watched the light come up grey through the chandlery's high windows, and she made herself name the thing she'd seen in his face, because naming was the first discipline he'd ever taught her. *You cannot fight what you refuse to call.*

Recognition. Relief. The face of a debt coming due.

* * *

They left at dusk, as planned, and the city swallowed them gratefully. Corvessa at night was all shadow and dripping eaves, the failing Oath making small wrongnesses everywhere — gutters that ran uphill for a foot before remembering themselves, a cat with too many joints in its spine watching them from a wall, streetlamps that burned green instead of gold. The child rode on Renn's back through the drowned quarter, silent, her chin resting on his shoulder, her eyes open the whole way.

At the weaver's stair — a collapsed spiral of stone climbing the inside of a dead bell tower — they stopped to rest, and it was there, in the tower's hollow throat, that Odile finally understood.

Not because of anything dramatic. Because of a bird.

A messenger pigeon beat down through the broken roof, circled twice, and landed on Ferren's shoulder as calmly as if it had been trained to do exactly that. Which, Odile realized with a cold spreading under her ribs, it had been. Guild-birds answered to guild-whistles, and Ferren had whistled, low and brief, an hour back on the road — a sound she'd taken for him clearing his throat.

The bird wore a message tube. Ferren removed it, and for one moment he held it in his cupped hands, and Odile watched him weigh it — actually *weigh* it, his thumb moving across the wax seal like a man testing the ripeness of fruit.

"News?" Maren asked, tilting her blind face toward him.

"Word from a friend," Ferren said. "Confirming the route is clear."

And he smiled, and it was the same smile he'd worn over every warm hearth of her childhood, and Odile felt the floor of her whole life tilt.

Because she knew the seal on that tube. Everyone in Corvessa knew it, the way you know a scar on your own city: the crowned eye of the Office of Hunters. She had seen it pressed into warning bills nailed to doors. She had seen it on the warrant that burned the guildhouse with eleven of her teachers inside.

"You're lying," she said. Her voice came out very quiet, which surprised her. She had expected shouting. "That's a hunter's seal. That bird came from the Crown."

The tower went still. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath in the broken belfry above them.

"Odile." Ferren's voice was gentle, the voice for correcting students. "Lower your voice. You're tired and afraid, and fear makes—"

"*Show me the message.*"

He didn't. That was the answer, really. Everything else was theater, and she watched the theater happen — watched him sigh, watched him produce the tube at last as if conceding graciously, watched him crack the seal with his thumbnail — and she read the slip of paper upside down before he could angle it away, because Ferren had taught her to read upside down too, in case she ever needed to know what her betters were signing.

It wasn't a warning. It was terms.

*Restoration of the Guild of Knotwrights and Charmers, full charter, lands and privileges restored per pre-Proscription grant. Full pardon, Master Ferren Aldous and named associates. In consideration of which: delivery of the asset known as the Oath-fragment, alive and intact, to the Office of Hunters, within six days.*

Below it, in a different hand, a single line: *The Crown thanks you for your service. The Crown remembers its friends.*

"Named associates," Anselm read aloud over her shoulder, in the flat voice of a priest pronouncing a death rite. "He sold all of us. He sold the *child*."

"She is not a child," Ferren said, and his voice changed — dropped its warmth like a coat shrugged off, and underneath it was something Odile had never heard from him, something bright and hard and *hungry*. "She is a fragment of the covenant that got our brothers burned and our craft outlawed. She is the reason hunters kick in doors in the night. And she is also, Odile, the single most valuable object in the world — do you understand? Not person. *Object.* The Crown knows it now. And the Crown is offering me back everything we lost — everything *you* lost — in exchange for one delivery."

"So that's what we were," Odile said. "Collateral. Leverage. Something to trade."

"You were my family," Ferren said, and the terrible thing was that he sounded like he meant it, sounded like both things could live in the same mouth at once. "Which is why I chose you for this. Anyone else would have flinched. You understand necessity. I taught you necessity."

"You taught me knots," Odile said. "You taught me the old tongue, the safehouses, the signs, how to read upside down, how to whistle a guild-bird — saints, you taught me *that*, didn't you, so I'd recognize it someday—" Her breath was shaking now. "You taught me everything except how to see ambition wearing duty's face. Or maybe you did teach me that, and I just refused to learn it, because I couldn't believe it of you."

"Odile—" Maren started.

"No. Let me finish. Let me say it while I still can." She turned to face him fully, and the child slid off Renn's back and pressed close against her hip, and Odile put a hand on the small head without looking away from her master. "Eleven people died in the guildhouse fire. I helped bury their charms in the river because we couldn't risk graves. You wept at that river, Ferren. I *watched* you weep. Was that real?"

"It was real," he said quietly.

"And this is real too. Both at once. That's what frightens me. Not that you're a monster — monsters I could run from. But that you're just a man who wanted his life back, and we were the price, and you could stand there and call it *necessity* with tears still dried on your face."

For a moment — one long moment — something moved behind Ferren's eyes that might have been shame. Odile reached toward it, the way you reach toward a drowning man, because three years of love doesn't die in a sentence.

Then he stepped back, and whistled.

Two notes. High, sharp, repeated. Not a guild-signal. A hunter's flare.

The night above the broken tower lit up with answering lights.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do — too fast for heroism, fast enough only for instinct.

Torches bloomed along the rooftops ringing the bell tower. Boots on gravel, the creak of crossbows being spanned. Renn swore in three dialects and grabbed Maren's arm. Anselm threw his knife — the knife he'd sworn never to use — and bought them three seconds of chaos as a torchbearer went down screaming.

"Stair's blocked!" Renn shouted. "They came up the weaver's road — there's a drop on the north side, into the cistern channel—"

"Take them," Ferren called out, and horribly, he wasn't even commanding the hunters. He was commanding *her*, in the old master's voice, the voice that had steered her whole life. "Odile. Give her up and walk out of here and I swear on the guild's ashes no harm comes to any of you. This is the mercy I can still offer. Take it."

"The mercy," Odile said, "of handing a nine-year-old girl to the people who burn children's charms and call it cleansing."

"The mercy of *survival*. The guild died because we loved magic more than we loved living. I won't make that mistake twice."

"Then you've already made it twice," Odile said, "because that's exactly what you're doing right now."

And the child spoke.

Not in Oathscript. In Corvessan, clear and small and absolutely certain, the first full sentence Odile had ever heard from her in the common tongue: *"He broke it first. Long ago. The one who loved across the wall. He is doing it again."*

The words went through the tower like a bell-strike. Even the hunters hesitated. Even Ferren.

Odile didn't hesitate. She swept the child up, turned to the north edge of the stair where the old stonework crumbled over the black slot of the cistern channel, and jumped.

* * *

The fall was short and the water was filthy and freezing, and it saved their lives. By the time the hunters reached the drop-off, Odile had the child on her hip and Renn hauling Maren down the channel's flooded throat, Anselm covering their trail with a charm of misdirection that cost him a nosebleed and three years off his conscience. Behind them, faint through the stone, she heard Ferren's voice, raised now, stripped of all its warmth at last:

"Seal the quarter! Six days! I want the fragment *alive!*"

Alive and intact. Object. Asset. Delivery.

They ran through water that remembered being something else — the failing Oath was worse tonight, the channel walls sweating a pale light like moonrise from the wrong direction, and once Odile was almost sure the stones under her bare hands pulsed, once, like a heartbeat. She didn't stop. She held the child tighter and ran.

Only much later, huddled in a smuggler's alcove with the others, wrung out and shaking, did she let herself feel it properly. Not the fear. The other thing. The grief, sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal.

He had carried her out of the plague-quarter. He had taught her every good thing she knew. And he had sold her — sold all of them, sold the child most of all — for a charter and a pardon, and he had done it wearing duty's face, speaking duty's words, and some part of him had probably believed, right up to the whistle, that he was the hero of the story.

That was the part that made her furious enough to shake. Not evil. Evil she could have fought. It was *conviction* — ambition so deep in the shape of duty that even the man wearing it couldn't tell them apart anymore.

"We can't go back for supplies," Renn was saying, low and urgent, counting arrows with numb fingers. "We can't go back anywhere. Every safehouse on Maren's map goes through Ferren. Every contact is his contact. As of tonight, we have nothing but each other and whatever we're carrying."

"Then that's what we have," Odile said.

The child tugged her sleeve. In the dark of the alcove, the girl's eyes caught what little light there was and gave it back doubled, and when she lifted her hand, the flour-spirals she'd drawn on the guildhouse table rose faintly in the air between them — Oathscript, hanging written on nothing, glowing soft as dawn under a door.

*Three days,* the script said, in letters none of them had written. *The stones remember you. Come.*

Odile looked at it for a long moment. Then at the child. Then at the ragged, hunted, half-drowned remnant of the last guildhouse, gathered in the dark around a piece of floating light.

"Six days," she said, "before Ferren delivers his report and the Crown comes for us in force. The stones are three days' travel." She stood, and her legs held, and her voice held, barely. "So we don't sleep twice. We walk tonight, and tomorrow, and the day after, and we get there before he can sell the world twice."

Renn snorted — exhaustion or admiration, impossible to say. Maren turned her blind face toward the light on the air and smiled like a woman reading a map at last. Anselm murmured something that might have been a prayer or an apology to his knife.

And the child took Odile's hand, and together, wet and hunted and betrayed, they climbed out of the drowned dark toward the standing stones — while behind them, far off in the sleeping city, a guild-bird beat its wings and carried the last of Master Ferren's letters home.
