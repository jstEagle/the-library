## 8. Flight Through the Drowned Quarter

Renn had spent eleven years learning to read a city by its sounds, and now every sound was wrong.

Behind them, the night market was burning. Not with fire — with light. He'd seen hunter-lanterns deployed before, the white flare that stripped shadow from every corner and made concealment a confession. Captain Vess had ordered three of them dropped into the market square, and from this distance the glow looked like a second moon rising out of the cobbles, turning the smoke above the rooftops the color of old milk.

"Left," he said, and took it himself, shouldering through a gap between a dyer's shed and a wall that smelled of urine and mordant. Behind him, Odile followed without question — which unsettled him more than the pursuit did. He was, as of roughly six minutes ago, a traitor to the Crown. He'd expected argument. He'd expected her to look at him the way his old sergeant had looked at men who broke under questioning: with pity and arithmetic, calculating how much trouble he'd be.

Instead she'd said, "Can you get us out?" and that had been the whole of it.

The child rode on Odile's hip like she weighed nothing, though Renn had lifted her once, briefly, in the market's chaos, and she'd been dense as wet sandstone. She had her face pressed into Odile's shoulder, but her eyes were open, watching him over the fabric of Odile's coat. Watching him the way you'd watch weather.

"This way," he said again, because saying things kept the fear at a manageable volume.

* * *

The Drowned Quarter had been Corvessa's river district once, before the engineers of the last century decided the Graywash was an enemy and built the levees that killed it. Now it was a maze of half-submerged streets where the water sat green and still in the lower stories of abandoned houses, and the people who lived there — the ones nobody counted, the mudlarks and net-menders and eel-catchers — moved through it on plank walkways and poling skiffs.

Renn knew it because he'd hunted in it. Three years ago, his first year under Vess, they'd flushed a charm-maker out of a boathouse near Tanner's Stair. The man hadn't run. He'd stood in the doorway of his workshop with his hands open at his sides and said, "You're doing this for the city," in a tone that wasn't a question, and then he'd gone quietly, and Renn had told himself for two years afterward that the man's calm meant guilt.

He didn't tell himself that anymore.

"Stop." He put his arm across Odile's chest and listened.

Water. The slap of it against stone, everywhere, constant. And beneath it — a hum. Low, arrhythmic, coming up through the walkway planks into the soles of his boots. The same hum he'd felt in the market when the child had spoken, when every charm in every stall had lit up like a struck bell.

Odile felt it too. He could see it in the set of her jaw. The child lifted her head.

"It's louder here," Odile whispered.

The child answered — not in Oathscript, not in trade-tongue, but in something between, a sound like a word being remembered rather than said. The water around them shivered. Rings spread outward from the pilings, though there was no wind and nothing had touched the surface.

Then the rings reversed, and flowed inward.

Renn watched the current run backward up the flooded street, against the slope, against everything he understood about how water behaved, gathering itself toward the child in slow silver threads. His skin crawled. Eleven years of doctrine said this was corruption, this was the thing the guilds had loosed on the world before the Crown burned them out, this was why the lanterns and the iron and the pyres existed.

Eleven years of doctrine also said charm-sellers were monsters, and he'd just watched a woman spend her own blood to hold a door shut against four armed hunters so a child could live.

"The river," the child said, in trade-tongue, clear as a bell. "It remembers which way it used to go."

"What does that mean?" Renn asked.

Neither of them answered. Ahead, down the drowned length of Rope Walk, the water was moving. All of it. A slow, vast reversal, the Graywash pulling back against its own bed, and along the exposed banks things were emerging that Renn's mind refused at first to name — shapes like roots, like ribs, like the frames of boats that had never been built, glistening in the lantern-glow bleeding over the rooftops.

"We can't take the main channel," Odile said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, he noticed, were not. "If the water's doing *that*, we'll be seen from a mile off."

"There's another way." He heard himself say it before he'd decided to say it. "Under. The culverts. They drain the Quarter into the old cisterns beneath the Hill."

"Culverts." Odile looked at him. "You want us to swim."

"I want us to wade. There's air in the upper galleries if the maps are right."

"And if the maps are wrong?"

"Then we drown somewhere dry," he said, "instead of burning somewhere wet."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her — short, sharp, gone. She shifted the child higher on her hip. "Lead on, hunter."

That word again. He flinched from it and didn't let her see.

* * *

The culvert mouth was exactly where he remembered it, behind a collapsed net-shed on Tanner's Stair, a black arch half-drowned in weed-choked water. He went in first because it was his plan and because if the maps were wrong he wanted to be the one who found out.

The dark inside was total. He unhooded his hand-lantern — a hunter's lantern, iron-cased, the kind designed to burn white and merciless — and turned the shutter down until it gave only a coin of amber light. It felt strange in his hand, this instrument of his old life, dialed down small enough to be gentle.

The tunnel swallowed sound. Their footsteps became something distant, someone else's. Water slopped at their knees, then their thighs. The air tasted of iron and old stone and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath — loam, almost, like a forest floor, which no sewer had any right to smell like.

"Renn." Odile's voice came low from behind him. "Your lantern."

He raised it. The flame was burning green.

Not flickering green, not reflecting green off the water — burning green, steady as a chapel lamp, and where the light touched the tunnel walls the old brick wasn't brick anymore. It was bone. He saw it clearly, horribly: the great curved struts of ribs, fused and grown together into vaulting, a ribcage miles long that some ancient thing had died inside and the city had been quietly built over ever since.

"Stone remembering being bone," the child said softly, from Odile's arms. "The Oath is thinning. Things forget what they became."

"That's—" Renn stopped. Started again. "That's not possible."

"You've been telling yourself that all night," Odile said. "How's it working?"

He had no answer, so he walked. The green light made their shadows climb the rib-vaults ahead of them, huge and swaying, and Renn thought about the catechism they'd drilled into every recruit at the Hunter's Hall: *Magic is debt. Every wonder is borrowed against the world, and the world always collects.* He'd believed it the way you believe a floor will hold. You don't examine it. You just stand on it.

But standing here, knee-deep in a dead god's ribcage while a lantern burned the color of spring, he understood that the catechism had a hole in it big enough to drive a cathedral through. Because this — the humming, the reversed water, the bones waking up — this wasn't a debt being collected. This was a wound. He'd seen wounds. He'd dressed enough of them on enough raids. A wound doesn't collect. A wound *leaks*. And everything leaking out of the city tonight was leaking through a break that somebody, three centuries ago, had made on purpose.

The Oath. Ferren had said the word like a man naming a tumor. Renn had heard it secondhand, in the market, in the chaos. He turned it over now as he walked, testing it. A covenant. A promise between realms. Broken.

Promises broken leaked. He knew that too, from the inside.

"Here." The tunnel forked, and he took the left branch without slowing, because slowing would give the doubt room to breathe. "There's a gallery ahead that comes up under the tannery ruins. From there it's two streets to the gate road."

"Two streets," Odile repeated. "In a city full of hunters who have my description and hers."

"They'll be looking at the river," Renn said. "Vess thinks in channels and chokepoints. She'll seal the waterfront and work inward. She won't think under."

"Why not?"

"Because I wouldn't have," he said, "six hours ago."

* * *

They came up through a collapsed grate into the tannery yard, and the sky over Corvessa was the wrong color.

Not dawn — hours yet till dawn. But the east held a glow that wasn't firelight or lamplight, a deep auroral shimmer lying along the horizon like a bruise going green at the edges, and above it the stars had begun to move. Not fall. *Move*, sliding in slow arcs toward a point over the northern hills, as if the whole sky were draining toward somewhere else.

The child stared up at it and made a sound Renn had not heard from her before — grief, plain and unarmored, a small animal sound.

"He's falling," she said. "The other side is falling."

Odile knelt in the nettles and held her close. "Who is?"

But the child only pressed her face into Odile's neck, and the aurora pulsed, and down in the tannery vats the stagnant water began, very quietly, to sing.

Renn scanned the walls. Two streets to the gate road, he'd promised, and the gate road was there — he could see the tollhouse roofline — but between them and it the street ran straight and open and lit at both ends, and somewhere in this city right now Captain Vess was assembling everything she had.

"Odile." He kept his voice flat, professional, the voice of briefings. "I need to know what I'm escorting. Not who. What. The Crown calls her a weapon. Ferren called her a fragment. You've been closer to her than anyone. When you look at her — what do you see?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. The child had gone still against her shoulder, listening with her whole body.

"A person," Odile said finally. "A frightened nine-year-old who talks to rivers. Who likes honey on bread and hides behind doors when strangers come. Whatever she was before — whatever she's made of — she's a person *now*, and the Crown wants to cut her open to find out what makes her shine." Her eyes came up to his, hard and bright in the auroral light. "So decide, hunter. Quickly. Which law are you standing in?"

And Renn, who had spent eleven years believing the law was a wall you leaned your whole weight against, found that the wall had a door in it after all, and that he'd been standing in front of it all night without seeing, and that the door had been open the entire time.

"The one that keeps her safe," he said.

Down the street, at the far end, torches rounded the corner of the tollhouse. Six of them. Then ten. Then a dozen more, fanning out with the crisp terrible discipline of a sweep line, and at their center a figure in a gray cloak whose bearing even at this distance Renn recognized the way you recognize a blade coming at you.

Captain Vess had not sealed the waterfront.

She had guessed under.

"Gate road's dead," Renn breathed. "New route. There's an old pilgrim stair behind the chandlery — it climbs the Hill from the inside, comes out by the standing stones above the tannery cliffs. If we can reach the stones before she reaches us—"

"The standing stones," the child said suddenly, lifting her head. Her eyes had gone wide and luminous, reflecting the green aurora, and when she spoke again her voice carried harmonics that had no business in a human throat. "*The door in the hill is opening.*"

The singing in the tannery vats rose to a chord. The water in the flooded street below them turned over all at once, a single silver muscle flexing, and began to flow uphill toward the northern hills — toward the shimmer, toward the moving stars, toward whatever was waiting where the drowned quarter ended and the old world began.

Renn pulled his hood up, took one last look at the sweep line closing through the torchlit street, and made his choice with his feet before his fear could file an objection.

"Then we'd better get there first," he said. "Run."
