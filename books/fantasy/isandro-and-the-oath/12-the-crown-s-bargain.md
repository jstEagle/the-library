## 12. The Crown's Bargain

The pardon arrived at dawn, sealed in green wax, and Ferren read it three times before he let himself believe it.

*By order of the Crown, Master Ferren of the Guild of Oath-Keepers is restored to rank, title, and property...*

Property. That word alone was worth the price. The guildhouse on Vellum Street had been stripped bare eleven years ago — its library burned in the courtyard, its charter nailed to the door with a hunter's knife through it. He had watched from across the square, an old man in a borrowed coat, while soldiers fed the oath-books to the flames one by one. He remembered the smell. Parchment burning has a sweetness to it, like sugar left too long in a pan. He had never been able to eat anything sweet since.

Now the knife-hole in the door would be planed away. The charter rehung. His name spoken aloud again by people who mattered, without the word *outlaw* stitched to it like a brand.

He stood in the restored guildhall — they had given him back the building itself, dust-sheeted but intact, the great round table where twelve masters had once sat still standing in its place — and he ran his thumb along the green wax seal and thought: *I did what any keeper would do.*

That was the sentence he had built for himself, brick by brick, on the ride down from the hills. *Any keeper would have done the same.* The guild existed to preserve the Oath. The Oath was dying; he had seen it dying for years, in the thinning of his own craft, in charms that held for a week instead of a year, in the slow gray fade of the wards along the city walls. A dying covenant could not be preserved by hiding in attics and selling luck-knots to fishwives. It needed power behind it. It needed the Crown.

So he had sold the child's location. Not betrayed her — *transferred custody*. There was a difference, he told himself, and the difference was everything. Odile would have kept the child hidden until the Oath failed entirely and both realms went dark. Ferren intended to put her under the Crown's protection, at the center of things, where a fragment of the living covenant belonged. Where she could be *studied*. Where the ritual could be prepared properly, with proper resources, instead of scrounged together in drowned cellars by a blind mapmaker and a defrocked priest.

Odile would call it betrayal. Odile had always loved the romance of smallness — the charm-seller in the night market, the little kindnesses, the tradition kept alive one knot at a time. It was beautiful. It was also how traditions died. Beauty did not hold a wall against the dark. You needed institutions. You needed law. You needed, occasionally, the willingness to spend one child's freedom to save two worlds.

*Any keeper,* he thought again, and this time the sentence held its shape all the way through, the way a well-made ward holds.

A knock at the hall doors. Two knocks, precise, unhurried.

"Enter."

The man who came in wore the grey-and-gold of the Crown's Inquisition, but not the hunter's leathers — a court coat, high-collared, cut close. He was younger than Ferren expected, perhaps forty, with the smooth unlined face of a man who had never stood in weather. His eyes were pale and very still, like water over something deep.

"Master Ferren." He inclined his head exactly the correct degree. "Lord Chancellor's office. My name is Corvane." He set a leather case on the round table and did not open it. "Congratulations on your restoration."

"I have the Crown to thank."

"The Crown thanks you in turn. Your service has been noted." Corvane drew out a chair and sat, folding his hands. "We should discuss the next phase. Time is short."

"The child—"

"Is being tracked as we speak." No apology in it, no softening. "Your information was accurate. She is with Halloran, moving south through the old districts. Captain Vess has the pursuit."

Ferren sat down slowly. Something in the flatness of Corvane's voice snagged on him, small as a splinter. *Moving south.* Toward the Sunken Altar, then. They knew about the Altar. Of course they knew — he had told them. He had told them everything: the Altar, the ritual, the requirement. A life freely given. He had laid it all out in the Chancellor's office with the green wax already drying on his pardon, and no one in that room had blinked.

"A life freely given," Corvane said now, as if reading the thought off him. "You raised that point in your briefing. I'm told you were emphatic."

"It is the central condition. The Oath cannot be reforged otherwise. The ritual will accept nothing less than a life surrendered willingly — coerced blood fails, stolen blood fails. Every text agrees."

"And you believe the texts."

"I *am* the texts," Ferren said, with more edge than he intended. "There are perhaps four men alive who can read the deep script. I am one of them."

"Yes." Corvane smiled faintly. "Which is why you're here."

He opened the leather case.

Inside, nested in velvet, lay a collar of black iron worked with gold — fine gold, thread-fine, tracing patterns Ferren's eye followed before his mind could stop it. Oathscript. Old Oathscript, the deep kind, the binding-kind. He knew those characters. He had taught them to Odile when she was nineteen, her ink-stained fingers copying the sigils onto slate. They were words of *holding*. Words used once, long ago, to bind things that could not be killed and must not be freed.

"That is not a restraint for a child," Ferren said quietly. "That is a leash for a god."

"It is an instrument of statecraft," Corvane said. "The Chancellor prefers the second term."

"The child is nine years old."

"The child is a fragment of the oldest covenant in existence, currently loose in my capital in the company of a convicted outlaw." Corvane's tone did not change at all. That was the terrible thing about him, Ferren realized — the absolute evenness, the way horror simply failed to register on that smooth face, like rain failing to dent stone. "The Crown's position is simple. The Oath is an asset. Assets are secured, studied, and deployed."

"*Deployed.*"

"You've spent eleven years in the hills, Master Ferren, so allow me to summarize recent history. Three border provinces in open revolt. Two foreign powers testing our passes. Grain riots in the lower city last spring that took four hundred soldiers to put down. And beneath all of it—" he tapped the case, lightly, almost fondly, "—a leaking wound between worlds that lets monsters through whenever it pleases. The Crown has watched magic be outlawed, hunted, and burned for a generation, and what has it bought us? Weakness. We outlawed the guilds because they answered to no one. Very well. Now we will have the power *and* answer to no one. The child goes in the collar. The collar goes in a vault. And when the realm needs a miracle — a river turned, a plague ended, an enemy army shown the inside of their own nightmares — the vault opens, and the Oath performs."

"She isn't an instrument. She's a person. She speaks. She remembers being the covenant itself—"

"She remembers being an *asset*," Corvane said, "before it was mismanaged. You see the distinction. The previous administration allowed a divine artifact to wander unattended for three centuries. We intend to correct that."

Ferren looked at the collar. The gold script caught the morning light coming through the tall windows, and for a moment — only a moment — the characters seemed to move, crawling slowly along the iron like insects waking. He had seen that once before, in the guild's last years, on a binding meant for something in the deep cells beneath the old temple. A thing that had screamed in Oathscript when they chained it. He had been young then. He had believed the screaming was just noise.

"The ritual requires a life freely given," he said. His voice came out strange, distant, someone else's voice. "If you collar her, if you *hold* her — nothing she gives will ever be free. The reforging will fail. You'll have gone to all this trouble to build a lock around a door that will never open."

Corvane regarded him with something that might, in a more expressive face, have been pity.

"Master Ferren. May I speak plainly?"

"I wish you would."

"We have no intention of reforging the Oath."

The words went into Ferren like a blade going into water — no resistance, just cold spreading outward from the entry point.

"The covenant is dying regardless," Corvane continued, in the tone of a man reviewing inventory. "Your own assessment. The realms are separating, the magic is thinning, the whole arrangement is winding down like a cheap clock. Reforging it restores the *old* system — guilds, wardens, a shared world, every hedge-witch in the country suddenly legitimate again. Do you imagine the Crown fought a twenty-year war against the guilds to hand the keys back?" He shook his head, gently, as if correcting a promising student. "No. We don't want the door repaired. We want the *hinge*. The child is the hinge. Whatever power remains in the covenant runs through her — you said so yourself, in writing, with commendable precision. Extract that power, house it in the collar, and the Crown holds the last magic in the world in a box it can open at will. The rest of the covenant can rot. Frankly, the rest of the covenant rotting is *convenient*. Fewer practitioners. Fewer rivals. One source. One hand on the lever."

"One hand," Ferren repeated.

"The Chancellor's."

Outside, somewhere over the rooftops, a bell began to ring — the ninth-hour bell, ordinary, municipal, marking nothing. Ferren heard it as if from underwater.

He thought of the child's hands. Small, ink-stained — no, that had been Odile's memory bleeding into his own. The child's hands were small and bare and they had written words in the air that made the room remember being older. He had watched her speak the deep tongue and felt the floor hum under his feet like a struck bell, and he had thought, even then, even in the Chancellor's office with the pardon warm in his pocket: *this is the most precious thing the world has made in three hundred years.*

And he had sold her. Not transferred custody. Sold.

"You gave us the location," Corvane said, watching him with those still pale eyes. "You gave us the Altar, the ritual, the conditions, the names of the surviving keepers. The cartographer. The priest. The hunter Renn — yes, we know about Renn; he'll hang with the others. In exchange you received a pardon, a building, and a title." He closed the case over the collar, softly, like closing a coffin lid. "All quite legal. All quite final. Is there a problem?"

Ferren found that his hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the old round table, against wood that twelve masters had touched, that had borne the weight of the charter and the candles and the dead guild's last honest work, and he tried to assemble the sentence he had carried up from the hills. *Any keeper would have done the same.*

It would not assemble. The bricks were there but the mortar was gone, and underneath the sentence he could finally see what he had been building over all this time: not preservation. Not duty. Ambition — his own, old and patient and dressed for decades in duty's clothes, waiting for a door to open. He had wanted his name back. He had wanted the guildhouse, the title, the sweet smell of parchment that wasn't burning. He had wanted to matter again, and he had found the one currency that would buy it.

"There is a problem," Ferren said.

"Name it."

"They'll reach the Altar." His voice steadied as he said it, and he understood, distantly, that he was watching himself cross a line he could never cross back — that some part of him had already chosen, and the rest was catching up. "Vess is good, but the drowned quarter will slow him, and the girl knows roads that aren't on any map. If they reach the Altar before you secure the child—"

"Then the asset is lost, and we begin again with whatever fragments remain." Corvane rose, buttoning his coat. "You seem distressed, Master Ferren. I'd counsel perspective. Empires are not built by sentimental men." At the doors he paused, hand on the handle, and glanced back. "Oh — one more item. The Chancellor asks that you prepare a full translation of the binding script on the collar. You are, after all, one of four men alive who can read the deep tongue." The faint smile again. "Consider it your first official duty. Restored guildmasters serve the Crown. That is rather the entire point of restoration."

The doors closed behind him.

Ferren stood alone in the guildhall a long time. Dust motes turned in the light from the tall windows. On the far wall, freshly hung, the new charter gleamed — the Crown's seal, the Crown's terms, the guild reborn as a department.

He walked to the table and sat in the master's chair, which fit him perfectly, because it had always been meant for him, and he had known it since he was a young man, and everyone had always told him he'd have to wait, and wait, and wait.

Then he pulled the case toward him and opened it, and looked at the collar, and began — because his hands knew the work, because the work was all he had ever been good for — to translate.

The first line read: *What is bound shall not be asked.*

The second line read: *What is bound shall not answer.*

The third line stopped him. He read it four times. Then he set down the pen, because his hand would not hold it, and read it a fifth time aloud in the empty hall, in the deep tongue, so that the words filled the room the way they must have filled the room three centuries ago when they were first cut into iron:

*What is bound shall serve until the binder is bound.*

Old law. Binding-law. The kind that cuts both ways and does not care whose hand holds the hilt.

Ferren sat very still in his perfect chair in his perfect guildhouse, listening to the dust settle, and understood at last what he had sold. Not a child. Not a covenant.

A key that turns in every lock — including the one around the hand that holds it.

And somewhere south, through the drowned streets, a nine-year-old girl was running toward the only door in the world that might still be shut in time.

He was on his feet before he knew he'd moved, coat half-on, the pardon crumpled forgotten in his fist — and then he stopped, because the doors opened, and two hunters stepped in, and one of them said, politely:

"Master Ferren. The Chancellor requests your presence. You'll come with us."

* * *

They walked him through corridors he had once owned, past clerks who did not meet his eye, down stairs he had never noticed though he had climbed this building a hundred times — down, always down, into the older city beneath the newer one, where the stones sweated and the torchlight went orange and wrong. At the bottom was a door banded in iron, and beside the door, waiting, Lord Corvane.

"You translated quickly," Corvane said. "Good. Then you'll understand why we're in a hurry." He gestured at the door. "The Chancellor thought you should see it before the work begins. A courtesy. Restored guildmasters are entitled to courtesies."

Beyond the door was a vault, and in the vault, on a plinth of black stone, lay a second collar — twin to the first, same iron, same thread-fine gold.

But empty. Waiting. Sized, Ferren saw with a cold that started in his spine and finished in his teeth, not for a child.

For a man.

"For whom?" he asked, though he already knew, and Corvane's smile, for the first time, reached all the way to his still pale eyes.

"Why, for whoever proves *disloyal*, Master Ferren. The Chancellor believes in symmetry." He turned to the door. "Come. The translation won't write itself — and neither, I'm afraid, will yours."

Behind them, as the door swung shut, the empty collar seemed to shift on its plinth — settling, patient, like something that had all the time in the world, and knew exactly whose throat it was measured for.
