## 13. Seryth's Testimony

Seryth had not spoken of the breaking in two hundred years, and she did not intend to speak of it now.

She stood at the edge of the warden's terrace with her back to the human woman and the child, watching the light fail over the Vael lands. It was a slow failure, the way all their failures were — the sun sinking through water that was not water, the color draining out of the hills one shade at a time until the whole world was the gray-green of a drowned thing. Once, this hour had been called *the gilding*, and the hills had burned gold and copper and the deep violet of nightshade wine. She remembered it. That was her curse among curses: she remembered everything.

"You came to my gate," she said at last, without turning. "You crossed the threshold of a dying realm and you asked me for the Altar as if I were a ferryman. As if three centuries of silence could be walked past because you knocked politely."

Behind her, the human — Odile — said nothing. Seryth had expected pleading. Pleading she could have refused cleanly, the way a blade refuses bone. But the woman simply waited, and the child stood beside her with those eyes that were not a child's eyes, and the waiting was worse.

"The Oath was broken by your kind," Seryth said. "That is the testimony I am bound to give, so hear it. Hear all of it, since you will not leave."

* * *

"Three hundred and eleven years ago," she began, "there was no wall. There was a door, and the door was open, and both realms breathed through it like two lungs in one chest."

She turned then, because some things must be said facing the listener, the way oaths must be sworn facing the sun.

"In those days the Vael walked your cities openly. We taught your healers which roots sang and which only whispered. Your masons built our standing stones into the foundations of your guildhalls, and our wardens walked your markets without a second glance. There were marriages. There were children of both bloods." Her voice caught on that, and she let it catch; grief unshown is grief multiplied. "The Oath held us together. Not law — covenant. A promise made at the Sunken Altar by the first keepers of both realms, each voice answering the other, each bound to the other's flourishing. *Your magic for our memory. Our memory for your magic.* Do you understand what that means, charm-seller? Neither realm could fall while the other stood witness."

"And then?" Odile said quietly.

"Then a keeper broke it." Seryth's hands curled at her sides. "The histories name him Aldous Vane, first keeper of the human line, and they say he grew proud, and they say he wanted power for his realm alone, and they say he severed the covenant at its root with a knife of cold iron and a word of unbinding. That is what the histories say. The histories are wrong."

The child made a small sound. Not a word — the child rarely used words — but a sound like a bell struck softly, and Seryth felt it move through her the way truth moves through a body before the mind consents to it.

"You know," Seryth said to her. "Of course you know. You were there. You are what was left when it ended."

* * *

She told it properly then, because half-truths had rotted long enough in her keeping.

"Aldous Vane loved a Vael woman," she said. "Her name was Ilvaen, and she was a singer of the deep groves, and I knew her — I trained beside her, I shared her fire, I called her sister. When the councils of both realms began to speak of closing the door — your kings feared us, our elders feared you, and fear makes excellent policy — Aldous and Ilvaen went to the Altar to renew the Oath instead. To rebind it tighter. To make the separation impossible."

Seryth looked at her own hands, long and gray-green, steady as stone and shaking anyway.

"But the ritual demanded a life freely given. The same price it demands now. And Ilvaen — who loved him, who loved both worlds, who believed with her whole soul that one death was a small coin against two living realms — gave herself. Freely. Gladly. She stepped onto the Altar singing, and Aldous—" His name would not come out level. Seryth let it break. "Aldous refused the gift. He tore the covenant apart rather than let it be sealed with her death. He thought he was choosing love. He unmade everything love had built."

The wind off the failing hills carried a smell like rain on old books, like pages going soft.

"He did not sever the Oath out of pride," she finished. "He severed it out of grief. And the realms have been bleeding out ever since, because a covenant built to be renewed by sacrifice cannot survive being broken by it either. The wound went both directions. Your magic guttered. Our lands began to drown — not in water, charm-seller. In forgetting. This world is made of memory, and the Oath was our anchor to yours, and without it we are coming loose."

* * *

Odile had gone very still. "Then sealing the gate—"

"—would save nothing," Seryth said, and hated the words even as her duty shaped them. "I know what it would do. I have always known. Seal the gate and the Vael realm finishes its drowning in a century, perhaps two, quiet and complete, and your side forgets us entirely, which might be a mercy. My order has kept this testimony locked for three hundred years because the alternative was unbearable. If the gate stays open while the Oath lies broken, the bleed goes both ways — your monsters, our fading, the walls between moments thinning until neither realm can hold its shape. Sealing is not salvation. It is amputation. It is choosing which body loses the limb."

"Then why—" Odile began.

"Because it is my office!" The words came out of her like something torn. "Four hundred years I have kept the gate. Four hundred years of watching the gilding dim a little more each season, and telling myself it was weather, it was cycles, it was anything but the slow death of my world while I stood guard over the door and did *nothing*. Duty is the last thing I have, human. Take away the duty and I am only an old woman watching her home die. So yes — I will seal the gate. I will perform the amputation. Because the oath I swore to my order demands it, and because the alternative requires me to believe that after three centuries of ruin, two realms can be mended by a charm-seller and a child in a single night."

She heard how it sounded. She heard the crack running through it, the same crack that ran through everything she had said, and she turned away again because she could not bear to have it seen.

* * *

But the child came to her.

Small bare feet on the terrace stones — Seryth heard them, felt them, the way one feels a change in pressure before a storm. The child stopped an arm's length away and looked up, and her eyes held the deep-water light of the Altar itself, and when she spoke it was in the old tongue, the true tongue, the one in which promises were still binding:

*"You kept the door. Three hundred years, alone, while everyone else forgot. That was love too."*

Seryth's breath went out of her.

"I was not loving," she managed. "I was obeying."

*"Is there a difference? My mother obeyed the ritual. The man who loved her broke it. One of them was wrong."* The child tilted her head, and there was something ancient and sorrowful and very young in the motion all at once. *"You think duty is what's holding you together. But duty is just love that got scared. I remember. I was there when it got scared."*

"I don't—" Seryth's voice failed entirely. She knelt — four hundred years of wardenship folding down onto wet stone — because the child's eyes were the eyes of the covenant itself, and one did not lie upward at the Oath from a standing height. "I don't know how to stop keeping the gate. It is all I am."

*"Then keep it,"* the child said gently. *"Keep it open."*

* * *

Afterward — Seryth could not have said how long after — Odile crouched beside her, and the human woman's face was tired and fierce and kind in a way that made Seryth think, absurdly, of Ilvaen.

"The evidence," Odile said. "You said your duty wars with the evidence. Look at it, warden. Look at me. I've spent three years selling luck-knots to people who think magic is a fairy story, and every knot I tied was a prayer that the old world wasn't dead. Your realm is drowning. Mine is suffocating. Sealing the gate kills us both slowly, and leaving it broken kills us both quickly, and there is exactly one door out of that room and it's behind the Altar." She held out her hand — a work-roughened hand, a charm-seller's hand, nothing like a hero's hand. "Come with us. Testify at the Altar instead of at the grave. If the ritual demands a life freely given, then let it hear from someone who understands what freely given cost the first time."

Seryth looked at the offered hand for a long moment.

Everything in her training said *no*. Everything in her four hundred years said *the gate is yours, the silence is yours, the slow dignified death is yours*. And beneath all of it, faint as the last of the gilding, something older than her office said: *Ilvaen did not die so the door would close. She died believing it would stay open.*

"If I come," Seryth said hoarsely, "and the Altar demands what it has always demanded — I will not watch another singer step onto that stone. I will seal the gate myself, with the child inside it if I must, and you will not be able to stop me."

"Fair enough," Odile said. "Ask me tomorrow whether I'd make the same bargain."

"That is not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be honest. Honesty's the only currency I have left."

And that — the weariness in it, the refusal to promise what could not be promised — was, Seryth thought, the first honest thing anyone had offered her in three centuries. She took the hand. It was warm. Human hands were always too warm, and she had forgotten, and remembering hurt.

They set out across the drowning hills toward the Sunken Altar as the false sun went down, and behind them the gate stood open for the first time in four hundred years without a warden at its post.

Seryth did not look back. But far to the east, where the human realm's smoke stained the horizon, she saw lights moving in formation — torches, many torches, marching with the terrible discipline of an army that had been sold a map.

She quickened her pace, and said nothing, and hoped she was wrong.
