## 14. The Siege of the Standing Stones

The bells woke us an hour before dawn.

Not the cathedral bells — those had been silent for three years, since the Crown melted down their bronze to pay for witch-hunter steel. These were older. The standing stones themselves, ringing like struck glass in a wind that wasn't blowing.

Odile was on her feet before her eyes opened, one hand already reaching for the child.

"They're coming," she said. Not a question. The stones were singing an alarm older than any kingdom, and the child sat up in the dark with her eyes wide and full of light that didn't belong to a nine-year-old face.

"I know," the girl said. In Oathscript first, then — because she'd learned Odile's fear as easily as her language — in Corvessan. "They are many. They carry iron that hates."

"Renn," Odile called into the dark of the ruined shepherd's hut they'd sheltered in. "Get up. It's happening."

He was already up. He'd slept in his boots every night since the drowned quarter, sitting against the wall with his sword across his knees like a man who expected to be arrested by his own reflection. He looked at the stones through the broken window, and his jaw did something complicated.

"That's a hunter's approach formation," he said. "Torches spaced at intervals. Flankers on both ridges. That's not a raid party. That's a company."

"How many?"

"Fifty. Sixty." He paused. "And someone taught them where the pass narrows. Someone who knew this ground."

Nobody said Ferren's name. Nobody had to. His absence had been saying it for two days.

* * *

They'd known, of course. Since the drowned quarter, since Renn had come back from checking his old contacts with a face like wet ash and told them: *Ferren walked into the Citadel with the Crown seal on his arm and walked out with a pardon and a key to the old guildhouse.* Since then they'd been running on borrowed hours, and everyone knew whose signature was on the loan.

But knowing a thing and hearing its bells are different animals.

Ivet was already awake when they reached her — the blind cartographer, sitting cross-legged among her maps with her head tilted toward the singing stones, listening the way other people read. She was seventy if she was a day, small and dry as a winter leaf, with milky eyes that somehow always seemed to be looking directly at you.

"Well," she said. "It took them long enough. I've had the route planned since Tuesday."

"Ivet—" Odile started.

"Don't." The old woman raised one finger. "Don't waste my morning with speeches. Tell me what you need."

"The gate," Odile said. "Time to get her through it. And then time enough that no one follows."

Ivet smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent forty years mapping coastlines that eroded and borders that bled, who understood better than anyone alive that all maps end somewhere.

"There's a place in the pass," she said. "Where the road pinches between the stones and the drop. A cart can't pass two abreast. If someone stood there—"

"No," Odile said.

"—and knew the ground," Ivet went on, serenely, over her, "they could hold it for a very long while. Especially if they'd spent forty years memorizing every loose stone on this mountain, which, as it happens, I have. I drew this pass before your mother was born, girl. I know it better than I know my own hands, which is fortunate, because I can't see those anymore either."

"You're blind," Renn said helplessly. "You can't fight sixty hunters."

"I don't need to fight them. I need to inconvenience them." She began rolling her maps with brisk, terrible efficiency. "There's a scree slope above the pinch. One good shove and half the mountainside comes down on their vanguard. There's a false path I marked on the survey copies I let the Crown buy twenty years ago — they'll have it memorized, and it ends at a cliff. And there's fog off the tarn below that rolls in at dawn, every dawn, because mountains keep their promises even when kingdoms don't." She tied the roll closed. "I can make that pass cost them an hour. Maybe two. An hour is a country, at a gate like ours."

"Ivet." Odile crouched in front of her, took her small dry hands. "Come with us. Through the gate. Seryth will—"

"Seryth would feed me to her own hounds rather than harbor a human," Ivet said mildly. "No. My legs stopped agreeing to mountains a decade ago, and my work is here. Every map I ever made is in libraries and survey offices across this kingdom, and every one of them has something hidden in it — a guild mark, a safehouse, a route. When magic comes back, someone will need to find all of it." Her blind eyes found Odile's with unnerving precision. "That's my oath-keeping. This is yours. Don't you dare make me die listening to a speech."

* * *

The priest, Doss, went with Ivet to the pass — not to fight, he insisted, but because "someone must carry her down afterward, and God forgives the practical." He kissed Odile's forehead like she was his daughter and pressed his old cracked rosary into the child's hand, whispering something in Latin that might have been a blessing or might have been an apology. Then the two of them climbed into the pre-dawn dark, the tiny blind woman leading the big frightened priest by the elbow, and Odile watched them go and made herself not think about it.

Renn led the rest of them the back way — a goat track along the tarn, fog rising around their knees like something breathing. The child rode on Odile's hip, too heavy for carrying now, too light to put down. Behind them, faint through the fog, came the first sounds of the morning: a shout, cut short. The crack of a thrown stone finding armor. Doss's voice, high and cracking, calling out words that were half scripture and half pure Corvessan profanity.

Then, louder than all of it, a voice amplified by a speaking-trumpet, cold and clear:

**"By order of the Crown! Surrender the abomination and the witch! You are promised mercy!"**

Renn snorted. "That's Captain Crane. He doesn't promise mercy. He promises the *word* mercy." He glanced back. "Keep moving. The gate's another mile."

They moved. The fog thickened. Somewhere behind them the trumpet voice rose again, angrier now, and beneath it a sound Odile would hear in her sleep for years afterward: the long grinding roar of a scree slope giving way, and men screaming, and then — briefly, terribly — silence.

Then Doss, alone now, singing. An old hymn, badly, at the top of his lungs, to cover the noise of Ivet working, to tell the hunters exactly where he was so they'd look at him and not the ridge.

It stopped mid-verse.

The child made a small sound against Odile's neck, like a bell cracking.

"We're almost there," Odile whispered, to her, to herself. "Almost there. Almost there."

* * *

The standing stones stood in a ring on the shoulder of the mountain, nine of them, taller than the cathedral spire had been, wearing three thousand years of lichen like vestments. In their center the air was wrong — a shimmer, a held breath, the place where Seryth had opened the way and where, if the child's memories served, it could open again.

The child walked to the center stone and laid both palms against it and spoke, and the Oathscript came out of her not as language but as weather — pressure, gravity, the sense of something vast turning over in its sleep.

The air split. Beyond it was not landscape so much as *color* — Vael light, green-gold and slow, the smell of rain on roots, a world holding its breath.

"It's open," the child said. Her voice shook. "But it's hard. It won't stay. Hurry."

"Go," Odile said. "Through. Now."

"Not without—"

"*Now.*"

The girl went, one step, swallowed by the green-gold light, and Odile turned to Renn.

"You next."

"I'm next?" He actually laughed. "I'm the one holding the sword, Halloran. I'm last. That's the whole point of me."

Behind them, down the pass, the fog was thinning. Dawn was burning it off, and through the burning came torchlight, and boots, and the disciplined clatter of a company that had lost its vanguard to a landslide and its patience to a captain who considered retreat a form of blasphemy.

"Renn—"

"Halloran." He gripped her shoulder, once, hard. "Three years ago I dragged a charm-seller out of a burning market and let her go, and it was the only honest night's sleep I've had since I put on this uniform. Everything since — the drowned quarter, the gate, all of it — I finally understand which side of the law I'm standing on, and it turns out it's the same side as you." He pushed her, gently, toward the shimmering gap. "Go. Keep her alive. Reforge the damn thing. And when the world changes — send word. I want to see it."

She went through.

She turned in the green-gold light, in the doorway between worlds, and looked back — and that was how she saw it.

Captain Crane himself reached the ring first, ahead of his men, a lean fanatic with a drawn blade and frost-white eyes, and he saw Renn standing alone among the stones, and he understood everything in a single heartbeat.

"Traitor," Crane said, and it carried across the whole mountainside.

"Captain," Renn answered, drawing his sword. "About time you caught up."

What happened next happened fast, and Odile saw all of it and could do none of it. Renn met the charge at the ring's edge, and he was good — he'd always been good — and for ten seconds it was a duel, steel ringing off stone, the child screaming from inside the gate, Odile lunging back toward the opening only to feel the way closing, the Vael light pulling, the door between worlds narrowing like a closing eye.

Renn went down under four hunters. But he went down slow, and loud, and backward — dragging them with him into the ring itself, into the space between the stones where the Oath's old power still pooled like oil — and the moment their bodies crossed that boundary the gate convulsed.

The light flared white.

The last thing Odile saw of her own world was the standing stones folding inward like hands closing in prayer, and Renn's sword spinning away across the grass, and the sky going suddenly, absolutely ordinary.

Then there was no gate. There was no ring. There was only green-gold country stretching to a horizon with two moons in it, and the child standing beside her, gripping her hand hard enough to hurt, and the silence of a door that would never open the same way twice.

Odile sank to her knees on soil that hummed faintly, like a plucked string still vibrating.

"They're dead," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "Ivet. Doss. Renn. All of them. For us. For—" She couldn't finish it.

The child knelt beside her and did something no fragment of a broken covenant should have known how to do: she put her arms around Odile and held on, and after a moment Odile held back.

When she finally lifted her head, the horizon had changed. Where the green-gold light of the near lands faded, farther out, the color drained from the world — hills gone gray, forests standing bare and silver like bones, rivers lying motionless in their beds. The dying lands. The Vael realm, starving behind its own sealed heart.

And on a ridge between the living color and the dead, watching them with eyes like winter water, stood a tall figure in gray — Seryth, the warden, spear in hand, flanked by others of her kind.

"So," the warden said, in Corvessan, in a voice like ice moving over deep water. "Humans. At the Altar's threshold. With the gate destroyed behind you." Her gaze flicked to the child, and something in her face flickered — grief, or rage, or both wearing the same mask. "You bring her *here*. After everything. Explain yourselves quickly, keeper. My patience died with the gate."

Odile rose, legs shaking, hands empty, three friends buried behind a sealed door and nothing left to offer any world but the truth.

"Then listen fast," she said, "because I've come to tell you that sealing the gate kills us both."
