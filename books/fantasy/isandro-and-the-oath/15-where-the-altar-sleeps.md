## 15. Where the Altar Sleeps

The Vael lands were dying the way a fire dies — not all at once, but in patches, in colors going out.

Odile had expected ruin to look like Corvessa's drowned quarter: black water, collapsed stone, the smell of things rotting where no one could reach them. Instead it looked like autumn that had forgotten how to end. The forest they walked through was still beautiful, which made it worse. Trees stood silver-barked and tall, but their leaves had gone glassy, translucent as old ice, and when the wind moved through them they chimed instead of rustled. Underfoot, moss glowed faintly blue where the child stepped and dimmed again behind her, like the land remembering light only where someone still loved it.

"Is it always like this?" Renn asked quietly. He walked with his hand on his sword hilt out of habit, though there had been nothing to fight for two days. "Or is this new?"

"It's the Oath," Odile said. She'd stopped trying to explain it in words she fully understood herself. "It was the wall between their world and ours. And it was also the... plumbing." Ferren would have winced at the comparison. Ferren wasn't there. That thought still caught like a fishhook every time she reached for him.

The child walked ahead of them all, barefoot on ground that would have cut human feet, and she did not hesitate. She couldn't speak Vaelish here — or rather, she could, but the land answered her before she finished asking. Paths straightened when she looked at them. Streams that ran backward, uphill, groaning with the wrongness of it, went quiet as she passed, as if ashamed.

"She remembers this place," Odile said.

"She *is* this place," Renn said. "Or part of it."

They crossed a meadow where the grass had turned to fine gray wire, and through a stand of trees whose roots arched over the path like the ribs of something enormous and long-dead. Twice the child stopped, tilted her head, listened to something none of them could hear, and changed direction. Once she wept — silently, the way she did everything — standing before a pool of water so clear it seemed to have no bottom, in which dead fish floated like coins dropped by a careless god. She touched the surface. The ripples spread outward and then, gently, flowed back inward, gathering against her fingers, as if even water wanted to be near her.

"We're close," Odile said, because the child's pace had changed. Quicker now. Almost running.

Renn glanced back the way they'd come, at the horizon behind them where the standing stones had fallen. Two days since the gate collapsed. Two days of not knowing whether Captain Vess and his hunters were digging through the rubble, following, or giving up. "How much farther?"

The child answered by pointing.

Through the trees ahead, light was coming from below.

* * *

The Sunken Altar sat in a valley that was not quite a valley — more like a bowl pressed into the earth by something patient and vast. From the rim they looked down into a cathedral built of living roots: trees grown together over centuries into arches and vaults and pillars, their trunks fused into walls wide as houses. Light came up from beneath it, gold-green and slow, rising through gaps in the root-floor like breath through water. It pooled in the hollows and dripped upward in places, defying its own nature, and the whole structure hummed — not a sound exactly, but a pressure Odile felt in her teeth, in the scar tissue of her palms, in the place behind her sternum where her magic lived.

Had lived. She flexed her hands and felt nothing there but ache and cold. Whatever she'd spent fleeing Corvessa, whatever the road of endings had taken from her, it didn't matter anymore. She'd known since the cartographer died that she might arrive at this place empty-handed.

Empty-handed except for the truth. Which, she was beginning to suspect, was the only currency this altar accepted.

"There," Renn breathed.

At the base of the great root-stair that wound down into the cathedral stood Seryth.

She had not changed since the gate. Tall past human measure, gray-skinned, crowned in antlers of pale wood that grew and shed and regrew as Odile watched, like a tree deciding season after season what shape to be. Her eyes were the color of deep water under ice. She held a spear of white thorn, and she planted it point-down between herself and them with a sound like a bell being struck underground.

"You are not welcome here," Seryth said. Her voice carried without effort across the whole bowl of the valley. "Turn back, keeper. What remains of my realm will not be your graveyard too."

"Seryth—" Odile started down the stair.

"*Stop.*"

The word hit like a wall. Odile felt it physically — a pressure across her chest, the air itself refusing to carry her forward. Renn grabbed her arm. The child, remarkably, kept walking, and the pressure parted around her like water around a stone.

Seryth's expression flickered — the first crack Odile had ever seen in it. Grief, maybe. Or recognition.

"You should not be able to walk here," the warden said softly. "This is the heart of the covenant. Nothing broken may enter."

"I'm not broken," the child said. "I'm what's left."

* * *

They came down the stair together, slowly, Seryth's spear tracking them the whole way, and Odile used every step to think. Because she had rehearsed this. On the road, in the dark, while Renn slept and the glass leaves chimed overhead, she had rehearsed arguing with Seryth — and every version she'd practiced had been wrong. You couldn't argue someone out of grief with logic. Seryth had watched three centuries of slow death and built an ideology out of it: seal the gate, save what remains, let the human realm choke on its own severed magic. Duty as a fortress. Odile knew that fortress intimately. She'd lived in one herself for three years, selling fever-charms and telling herself that small kindnesses were enough.

She stopped ten feet away. Close enough to see that Seryth's bark-pale skin was cracking at the joints, fine dark fissures like drought-earth. The warden was dying too. They all were.

"You want to seal the gate," Odile said.

"I do not *want* anything." Seryth's voice was flat. "Wanting is how your kind broke the world. I will do what must be done."

"The gate is already sealed. You know that. It fell two days ago with half the Crown's army digging at its roots. There's nothing left to close." Odile spread her hands. "What you're proposing isn't defense anymore. It's triage. Cut off the limb to save the body."

"And if the body is already lost?" For the first time, something moved behind those ice-water eyes. "You have seen my lands, keeper. You walked through them. Did they look savable to you? Glass leaves. Backward rivers. My people are fewer than the fingers on one hand, and each year the veil grows thinner and our strength bleeds through it into a world that hunted us, outlawed us, burned our names out of its books." The spear trembled — barely, but Odile saw it. "Sealing the gate stops the bleeding. That is all. But it is *something*, and something is more than your kind has ever offered us."

"That's not true," Odile said. "And I can prove it."

"Prove it."

Odile took a breath. This was the moment she'd rehearsed, and every rehearsal had failed, and so she did the thing she'd finally learned on the road of endings: she stopped performing courage and simply told the truth.

"Three years ago I watched the guilds burn. I watched Master Ferren — the man who raised me, who taught me the old tongues — stand in a square and renounce magic while his students were dragged away in chains. I survived by making myself small. Luck-knots. Fever-charms. Little illegal mercies sold one at a time to people who never knew what they were buying." She heard her own voice go rough. "I told myself that was enough. Keeping the tradition alive one knot at a time. It wasn't. It was hiding with better lighting."

Seryth said nothing. But the spear lowered an inch.

"Then a child knocked on my door," Odile went on, "and spoke a language older than any kingdom, and my charms sang back at her, and I learned that the covenant I thought was dead was *bleeding out* — and that I had three years left to be brave instead of thirty." She gestured at the child, standing between them, watching with those ancient, unchildlike eyes. "You say humans broke the Oath. We did. A keeper chose love over duty and unmade everything — I know the story now, all of it. But look at her, Seryth. Look at what the breaking produced. Not a weapon. Not a curse. A *child*. The Oath's own heart, cast out of the covenant, and the first thing it did with its freedom was walk across two worlds barefoot to find a charm-seller in an attic."

"Why her?" Seryth asked. It was almost a whisper. "Why a woman who hides?"

"Because I keep out of love, not law." Odile said it plainly, without pride. "That's what she told me, once, in her way. Your covenant was sworn in duty and sealed in blood, and duty fails and blood runs out. Every chain breaks eventually. But a promise freely renewed—" She stopped. Steadied. "You don't seal a dying realm shut, Seryth. You give it somewhere to live."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, glass leaves chimed in a wind that smelled of rain that would never fall.

"You are asking me," Seryth said at last, very carefully, "to abandon three hundred years of vigilance on the word of a human charm-seller and a fragment of a broken promise."

"I'm asking you to look at the evidence," Odile said. "You told me once the Vael are dying as surely as human magic. Sealing the gate doesn't change that — it just means you die alone in the dark instead of together in the light. Reforging the Oath—" she nodded at the altar behind Seryth, at the drowned gold-green light breathing up through the roots, "—might actually save both worlds. One of those choices has a future in it. The other is just a slower way of losing."

"And the ritual demands a life." Seryth's voice hardened again. "Freely given. That law is older than either of our griefs. Will you pay it? Can you? You arrive with empty hands, keeper. I can see it. Whatever fire you carried across the veil, the road took it."

"Maybe," Odile said. "Maybe not. Maybe the price isn't what we think it is. The last keeper paid a life and the Oath broke anyway — did you ever ask yourself why? If death could hold the covenant together, it would have held. It didn't. Because a covenant built on sacrifice was doomed from the first dawn." She stepped closer, close enough that the spear's point nearly touched her, and did not flinch. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to let us try. If we fail, seal whatever's left. No one will stop you. But if we succeed—"

"If you succeed," Seryth said, "the wall comes down. All of it. Humans in the Vael lands. My people under human laws. Hunters with iron and fire walking roads my children's children will play in." Her knuckles whitened on the shaft. "You ask me to gamble my entire world on the hope that your kind has changed."

"No," Odile said. "I'm asking you to gamble it on the fact that *she* exists."

She stepped aside.

The child looked up at Seryth. And then, deliberately, she held out her hand — the same gesture, Odile realized, with a jolt, that she herself had made on the first night, kneeling in an attic doorway, offering a frightened stranger a cup of tea.

Seryth stared at the small offered hand for a long, long moment.

Then the spear sank point-first into the root-floor, and stayed there, standing alone.

"You understand," the warden said, her voice cracking along its own fault lines, "that if this fails, I will spend what remains of my life making certain the gate stays closed. Even if I must build the wall with my own body."

"I know," Odile said. "That's why I'm glad you're here. Someone has to hold us to it."

Seryth looked down at the child's hand. When she finally took it, her gray fingers wrapped all the way around the small ones twice, gentle as a bird closing its wings.

Behind them, deep beneath the roots, the drowned light of the altar brightened — once, twice — like a heartbeat answering a knock at a door.

And then, from far above the valley rim, thin and wrong and unmistakably human, came the sound of horns.

Renn was already running for the stair. "Vess found a way through," he called back, face gone gray. "Odile — he found a way through."
