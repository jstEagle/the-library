## 16. Ferren's Return

The gate was dying when they came through it.

Ferren felt it go under his boots — a shudder in the standing stones like the last breath of a great animal, a sound too low to hear and too large to ignore. Behind him, the Crown's column poured through the breach: forty hunters in grey lacquer, crossbows slung, lanterns hooded against whatever light this place made. Ahead of him, the ruined arch leaned into the dark like a drunkard against a wall, its Oathscript characters guttering one by one.

He had led them here. He had drawn the route on the captain's own map, in his own hand, with the steady script of a man signing his name to something.

*Master Ferren,* the pardon had said. *Guildmaster Ferren, restored.* The seal had been warm under his thumb, wax still soft from the Chancellor's ring. He had read it eleven times that first night. He had read it again every night since, and each reading it had grown smaller, until now it weighed nothing at all and he carried it anyway, folded against his heart like a coin he could not spend.

"Master Ferren." Captain Vessane's voice, low, at his shoulder. She was younger than he'd expected when they finally met — hard-faced, precise, a woman who sharpened her orders before she obeyed them. "How far?"

"The Altar lies below," Ferren said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, in a distant, sickened way. "Follow the root-road down. The child will be there. So will Odile."

"And the others?"

"The hunter Renn is wounded. The cartographer is dead." He had not seen her die. He had heard it, secondhand, from a soldier's report delivered with the flat satisfaction of a man reading a ledger: *one hostile neutralized at the pass.* Marta, who had mapped half the drowned quarter from memory after her eyes went, who used to tap his knuckles with her cane when he lied. *Neutralized.*

He did not let himself think about it. That was the trick he had learned, three years in hiding and three weeks in the Crown's employ: you did not let yourself think. Thinking was a door, and behind the door were all the things he had done, and if you opened it even a crack they would come out and never go back in.

"Then we take the child," Vessane said, "and the woman if she resists."

"She won't resist." It came out of him before he could stop it, and Vessane looked at him — a quick, cold flick of the eyes, cataloguing. "She'll try to protect the girl. But she isn't a fighter."

"No," Vessane agreed. "Witches rarely are. That's what makes the work simple."

They went down.

* * *

The road to the Altar was not a road. It was roots — vast, pale, braided into stairs and causeways, descending through a dark that glowed faintly from no source Ferren could name. The air tasted of rain that hadn't fallen yet. And everywhere, growing worse with every step, was the wrongness: stone that flexed slightly underfoot, like muscle; water in the channels beside the path flowing uphill, patient and unhurried; a tree whose leaves turned, all together, to watch them pass.

His soldiers felt it too. He heard their breathing shorten, heard someone whisper a prayer to saints who had never heard of this place. One man vomited quietly against a root and was marched on.

Ferren walked at the front because walking at the front meant he did not have to look at their faces.

He had been guildmaster of Corvessa for nine years before the fall. Nine years of keeping the old tongues alive in cellars and attics, of teaching children letters that could wake a charm, of burying masters who died badly so the rest could live hidden. He had told himself, in those years, that survival was the highest duty. That a tradition kept small was better than a tradition kept loud and killed. When the hunters came for the guildhouse, he had scattered his students across the city like seeds and called it wisdom.

And then Odile had come to him with the child — the child who spoke the tongue the covenant was sworn in, the child who hummed in answer to charms, the child who was, by every sign Ferren knew how to read, the Oath itself wearing skin — and he had looked at her and thought not *what is she* but *what am I, if I keep her safe? A dead man in an attic. A footnote.*

The Chancellor's men had found him within the week. He still didn't know how. Perhaps Odile had been followed longer than any of them guessed; perhaps the Crown simply smelled ambition the way sharks smell blood. It didn't matter. What mattered was the moment the offer was laid on the table — pardon, restoration, the guild reborn under royal charter — and Ferren had heard himself say, in the reasonable voice he used for teaching, that the child would be safer in the Crown's keeping than loose in a dying city.

He had believed it, too. That was the part he could not forgive. Not the lie he told them — the lie he told *himself*, that handing a living fragment of the oldest covenant to a government that burned witches was an act of stewardship.

"You're quiet, Master Ferren," Vessane observed.

"I'm counting steps."

"To what end?"

"Habit." He had counted steps once, as a young keeper, to time a ritual precisely. Now he counted because numbers were clean and everything else was not.

The root-road bent, and light rose ahead of them — drowned gold, green-shot, moving slowly like light through deep water though there was no water here. The Altar.

Ferren stopped at the edge of the last turn and looked down at it, and something in his chest that had been clenched for three weeks finally tore.

* * *

It was a cathedral built by no human hand. Roots vast as towers arched overhead and knit themselves into vaulting; between them hung sheets of light like curtains of slow rain. At the center, sunk in a floor of living stone, lay the Altar itself — a basin of dark glassy material, older than the language carved around its rim, and above it the air shimmered with the residue of something enormous. Three centuries of absence. A promise that had been broken here, and had never stopped bleeding.

And there, small at the edge of all that vastness, stood the child.

She was facing the Altar, her back to them, her bare feet planted on the stone. Beside her — of course beside her, where else would she be — stood Odile Sandoval, one hand resting on the girl's shoulder, the other raised, and even from this distance Ferren could see the light gathering in her palm like a held breath. Seryth the warden stood apart, silver and still, watching the humans come down the road with an expression Ferren could not read and did not want to.

"They're here," Vessane breathed. "All of them. Master Ferren — your part is done. Fall back to the column."

Her part. His part. As if he were a tool to be used and set down.

"Wait," Ferren said.

Vessane's hand came up, and the column halted. Forty hunters in the mouth of a cathedral of roots, and the whole place seemed to lean toward them, listening.

"Odile!" Ferren called, and his voice cracked on her name like ice on a pond.

She turned. Even across that distance he saw her face do several things at once — recognition, grief, fury — and settle, finally, on something worse than any of them: pity. She had known. Of course she had known, since the betrayal, since the raid, since the moment she'd escaped the undercrofts and understood exactly which door had been left open for the hunters. She had known for weeks that the man who taught her everything had sold her.

"Ferren," she said. Her voice carried impossibly well in that space, the way voices carry in places built for oaths. "You brought them to the Altar. You brought *soldiers* here."

"I brought an escort—"

"You brought a weapon to a christening." The light in her hand brightened. "There's nothing here for the Crown, Ferren. Do you know what this place is? Do you even understand what you've sold?"

"The child," Vessane said, stepping forward, and her voice was the voice of the state, flat and total. "By order of the Chancellor, surrender the asset known as the Oath-fragment. Resist, and you will be taken by force. This is your only warning."

*Asset.* Ferren watched the word land on the child. She turned fully now, and looked past the soldiers, past the crossbows, past Vessane — and looked at him.

She had eyes the color of the space between two thoughts. She looked at him the way the Oath must look at everyone: without hatred, without mercy, only with perfect knowledge. And in that gaze Ferren saw himself entire — not the guildmaster, not the patriot, not the man who had told himself stories about stewardship — but a small, frightened creature in an attic full of dead traditions, who had been offered a pardon and had mistaken it for a purpose.

Three hundred years ago, another keeper had stood somewhere like this and chosen love over duty, and unmade the world. Ferren had chosen neither love nor duty. He had chosen *himself*, dressed up in duty's clothes, and the world was no more unmade than it already was — but he had helped put a knife in the hands of people who intended to finish the job.

"Take them," Vessane said.

The column moved. Crossbows leveled. Odile stepped in front of the child, and Seryth's blade came free of its sheath with a sound like a bell struck underwater, and Ferren understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that in ninety seconds everyone he had ever loved or failed would be dead on this floor, and the Crown would drag the child back through the ruin of the gate and learn, far too late, that some things cannot be wielded — only kept, or lost.

He moved before he decided to move.

Later he would not be able to reconstruct the decision. There was no decision. There was only the tearing-open of the door he had kept shut for three weeks, everything behind it coming out at once, and his body doing the only thing left that the things behind the door demanded.

He stepped off the root-road, down onto the Altar floor, directly into the line of fire.

"Stand down!" he shouted, arms wide, back to the child. "In the name of the guild charter — in the name of the pardon — Captain, stand your people *down!*"

"Master Ferren—" Vessane's voice went sharp with contempt. "You are done. You were a means to a gate, and the gate is open. Seize him."

Two hunters broke from the column. Ferren did not run. He walked toward them, absurdly, hands open, an old man in a travel-stained coat advancing on armed soldiers of the Crown, and he heard himself say, clearly, in the old guild-tongue — the tongue outlawed, the tongue he had kept alive in attics, the tongue the child had spoken at Odile's door:

"*I was keeper of this law, and I broke it. Let the record show I know my crime.*"

The words rang strangely in the cathedral air. The roots overhead shivered. And the child — the child made a sound he had never heard from her, a low note like a struck string, and every charm, every ward, every scrap of working Ferren carried in his pockets and sewn into his hem lit at once, blue-white, answering her, answering the place, answering the confession whether it wanted to or not.

The hunters faltered. Superstition is drilled out of witch-hunters, but it grows back.

"Enough," Vessane snarled. "Fire on the woman. Take the child alive. Kill anyone in the way — *anyone*."

A crossbow snapped. Ferren was already turning, already throwing himself sideways, and the bolt meant for Odile's heart took him high in the chest instead, punching through, spinning him, laying him out on the living stone with a force that emptied the world of sound.

* * *

Pain came later, and with it, strangely, peace.

He was on his back. Above him the root-vaulting arched away into darkness shot with gold, and the light-curtains swayed, and faces crowded over him — Odile's, fierce and wet-eyed; the child's, grave beyond her years; even Seryth's, remote and unreadable, looking down at him the way winter looks at a late flower.

"Ferren." Odile's hands pressed hard against his chest, trying to hold what was leaving. "You stupid, stubborn old man. Why—"

"Couldn't let you—" Blood made his voice bubble. He laughed, or tried to. "Couldn't let you have all the foolish ideas. Someone had to—outdo you."

"Don't talk."

"Listen." He caught her wrist. His fingers were already cold. "The ritual. I know what it costs. I've always known — I read the old accounts, I never told you, I thought—" *I thought I might need the knowledge for leverage,* he did not say. "A life freely given. That's the price. That was always the price."

"We know," Odile said quietly.

"Then hear me." He dragged in a breath that cost him everything and spent it all at once. "I can't undo it. Nothing undoes it. I sold you, I sold the girl, I sold Marta and Renn and every student I ever swore to protect, and the pardon is in my coat and it isn't worth the wax it's sealed with — they'll burn us all, Odile, the Crown doesn't want the Oath restored, it wants it *owned*—"

"We know that too," Odile said. "Hush."

"—but this," and his grip tightened, and he turned his head, and found the child's eyes, "this I can give. Freely. No bargain, no pardon, no charter. Take it. Take my life for the ritual. It's the only thing I have left that isn't stolen goods."

The child regarded him for a long moment. Around them the battle was building — Vessane shouting her column into order, Seryth moving to meet them, the light-curtains trembling — and inside that circle of noise the child was perfectly still.

"You broke the Oath's keeping," the child said. Her voice was the voice of a promise remembering itself. "Not the Oath. Keepers break. The Oath waits."

"Then let it wait no longer," Ferren whispered. "Let me pay. Let me be the last thing the old covenant takes — and let what comes after cost no one anything."

The child looked at Odile. Odile looked at Ferren — at the man who had raised her, taught her, sheltered her, and sold her, all with the same reasonable voice — and Ferren watched her arrive, visibly, at the truth he had traveled three weeks underground to reach: that repentance is not undoing. That some debts can only be paid forward, in a currency you will not live to see spent.

"He's offering freely," the child said. "The Altar hears him."

Above them, at the head of the descending column, Captain Vessane raised her sword, and the light of the Altar began, very softly, to rise.

"It hears him," the child said again, "and it is *waiting* —"

The first arrow fell among the roots like the opening of a sentence no one wanted finished.
