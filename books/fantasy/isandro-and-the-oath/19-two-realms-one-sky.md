## 19. Two Realms, One Sky

### Odile

The light came first.

Not the grey half-light of dawn that Corvessa knew, but something older — a gold that rose out of the ground itself, out of the roots of the Sunken Altar, out of every crack in every stone where the old magic had been hiding for three hundred years like embers under ash.

Odile felt it leave her.

It did not hurt. That was the strange part. She had braced for pain, for the tearing-out feeling she'd imagined when she made her vow — *I give my magic, freely, wholly* — and instead it was like exhaling after holding her breath through a drowning. The power she had hoarded and hidden and sold in scraps of knotted thread for three years poured out of her in one long silver stream and went into the child standing before her, and the child caught it the way the earth catches rain.

Then the world remembered itself.

Odile heard it before she saw it — a sound like a great bell struck underwater, rolling up through the Altar's drowned cathedral of roots, and the water around them lit from below, and the dead river-grass on the banks turned green as far as her eyes could follow it. Above them, through the canopy of intertwined branches, the sky changed color. Not blue. Something between blue and green and gold, a sky that had never quite decided what it wanted to be, because it belonged to two worlds at once.

"It's working," Renn said behind her, and his voice cracked on the second word.

The child — Isandro, though she was not only Isandro anymore, though perhaps she never had been only anything — opened her eyes. They were the same brown eyes Odile had seen across an attic threshold on a rainy night, barefoot and silent. But now they held light the way wells hold stars.

"Odile," the child said, in Oathscript, in guild-tongue, in plain Corvessan all at once, three voices braided into one small body. "Look."

Odile looked.

The wall between the worlds did not fall. Walls falling were violent things, and this was not violence. It thinned — like fog burning off a morning river, like a curtain drawn back by someone who had decided, finally, to let the daylight in. And through it Odile could see Corvessa.

She saw the night market square, empty at this hour except for the stall-keepers beginning their mornings. She saw the fountain in Tanner's Row run backward for one astonished heartbeat and then forward again, its water glittering with motes of light that no one had put there. She saw the bells of the Cathedral of the Chain — the church that had condemned her kind — begin to ring, though no hand pulled any rope, ringing not the alarm-peal but something older, something the bells had been built to say and had forgotten how.

"What are they saying?" Renn asked.

"The bells remember," Odile whispered. "They're saying *it is unbroken*. They've been waiting three centuries to say it."

And then, everywhere at once, the city woke up.

She felt it even without her magic — that was the miracle of it, that you didn't need magic anymore to feel it. A warmth moving through Corvessa like blood through a cold limb. In the guildhalls that had stood sealed and dark since the Purges, dust lifted off abandoned workbenches. In the tenements, fevered children slept easy as their mothers' old grandmother-charms, knotted thread and dried herbs and whispered words, woke up and started quietly doing their work. In the witch-hunters' barracks, a hundred men and women trained to smell sorcery in the air suddenly smelled it everywhere — in the stones, in the water, in their own breath — and understood, all at once and forever, that they had been trying to arrest the sunrise.

"They'll come here," Renn said. "The captain. He won't stop because the sky changed color."

"He might," Odile said. "If what he sees is big enough."

### Captain Aldric Vane

Captain Aldric Vane had taken the hunter's oath at nineteen, and in twenty-two years of service he had broken it exactly once — the night he let a disgraced hunter and a child slip through his fingers in the undercrofts — and he had hated himself for it every day since, because a man who bends the law once has already bent it entirely.

He came down into the Vael lands at the head of forty hunters, through the ruined gate Ferren had led them to, with orders written in the Crown's own hand: *Secure the child. Kill anyone who resists. Burn what cannot be carried.*

He found a cathedral of roots full of light.

His torches guttered and died in his men's hands — not snuffed, but *answered*, each flame bowing toward the center of the Altar like courtiers toward a throne. His boots sank into grass that had been grey rot when Ferren described this place and was now so green it seemed lit from within. The air tasted the way he imagined the first morning of the world had tasted.

And in the center of it stood a woman with no weapon, a hunter who had betrayed him, and a child.

Vane raised his sword anyway. It was the only language he had left. "In the name of the Crown of Corvessa, I claim—"

"You claim nothing," said the child.

Her voice was not loud. It did not need to be. It arrived inside him the way his mother's voice had lived inside him, the way the oath-words lived inside him, spoken in a register beneath his ears and directly into whatever part of a man decides who he is.

"You have spent your whole life guarding a door," the child said, "and you never once asked what was behind it. Look behind it, Captain."

And the Oath — because that is what spoke through her, the reforged covenant, the promise renewed — showed him.

It did not show him visions. It showed him *truths*, which are worse. It showed him that the magic he had hunted was not a disease in the world's flesh but the world's pulse. It showed him the faces of every man and woman he had burned or broken or driven into the dark, and beside each face, patiently, the ordinary human griefs that had driven them to charm and conjure: a sick daughter, a dying husband, a fear of the dark so large it needed a lamp no tallow-chandler could make. It showed him that his order had been founded not to protect the realm from magic but to protect the Crown from everything the Crown could not control.

Vane's sword arm shook.

Forty hunters stood behind him, and every one of them was feeling the same thing, and every one of them knew it, and no one moved.

"I have orders," Vane said, and heard how small it sounded.

"Yes," the child said gently. "You have always had orders. This is the first morning no one does." She took a step toward him, barefoot on the living grass. "The Oath asks nothing of you now, Captain. No duty. No obedience. Only a question, and you may answer it however you like, and no one will punish you for either answer. That is what freedom is. It feels exactly like this — like the ground dropping away and finding out you can fly."

Vane looked at his sword. Twenty-two years. His father's sword, his father's oath, his father's certainty passed down like an heirloom he had never been allowed to examine.

He looked at the child.

Aldric Vane lowered his sword until the point touched the flowering grass, and then, because he could think of nothing else that was honest, he knelt.

Behind him, one by one, in the gold-green light of a dawn that belonged to two worlds, forty hunters did the same.

### Renn

Renn had spent the last hour pretending he wasn't crying, and had failed completely, and had decided he didn't care.

He watched Vane kneel and thought about the day they'd sworn their oaths together, side by side in the white hall, young men certain the world was simple. He thought about the undercroft, the moment he'd stepped aside and let them pass, and how he'd told himself it was weakness, sentiment, the first crack in a broken man. It hadn't been. It had been the truest thing he'd ever done, and it had just taken the whole sky changing color to prove it.

The child came to him last. Of course she did. She saved him for last the way you save the last bite of something sweet.

"Renn," she said. "Hunter of the old law. The old law is dead. Will you swear the new one?"

"What's the new one?" His voice was wrecked. He didn't care about that either.

"It's shorter," she said. "The old oath was: *obey, pursue, destroy.* The new one is just: *keep the door open.*"

Renn thought about it for approximately no time at all.

"I swear it," he said. "In Oathscript?"

"In whatever language you love best," the child said. "That's the point."

So Renn swore it in his mother's dialect, the rough border-tongue of the fishing villages, the language nobody had ever considered important enough to outlaw, and the Oath took it gladly, and light ran up his arms like warm water, and somewhere behind him a fisherman's son named Aldric Vane laughed — actually laughed, wet and disbelieving — at the sight of it.

### Seryth

Seryth stood apart, as she had stood apart for three hundred years, and watched her duty die beautifully.

She had come to the Altar to bar the way. She had argued, in the end, against sealing the gate — Odile's truth had cracked her open too wide for that — but she had still believed, right up until this dawn, that the best the two realms could hope for was a truce, a scar tissue between worlds, a polite distance. Separation as mercy. Distance as survival.

Instead the veil hung thin as morning mist, and through it she could see both worlds at once, and both worlds were *blooming*.

On the Vael side, the blight that had been creeping through the deep woods for a century — the slow grey death she had spent her wardenship cataloguing, tree by tree, spring by spring — was reversing. She could see it happening. Bark regreening. Rivers remembering their courses. The old songs rising out of the ground like heat off summer stone. Her people, scattered and dwindling, were walking out of their hidden hollows and standing in the open with their faces turned toward the Altar, weeping without shame.

On the human side, Corvessa glittered like a city made of struck flint, every window catching a light that had no source in any sun.

Three centuries of grief, and the cure had been a woman who refused the price everyone said was necessary, and a child who was a promise, and a surrender instead of a sacrifice.

Seryth the warden had been wrong about the most important thing in the world, and the world had forgiven her anyway. That was somehow the hardest part.

The child found her there at the edge of the light, as she had found everyone, as if the Oath knew precisely where each person stood.

"Seryth." The child took her hand. Warden's hands, warden's calluses, and the child held them like they were precious. "The gate needs a keeper. Not a wall. A keeper — someone who knows both sides, who can teach the ones who cross to be gentle with what they find. There is no one alive better suited."

Seryth looked out at the thinning veil, at the human city shining beyond it, at the road — an actual road now, visible, walkable — that ran between the worlds.

"I spent three hundred years learning to hate that direction," she said.

"And now?" the child asked.

"Now I suppose I'll learn the other thing."

### Isandro

Isandro walked through the morning light and felt everything.

That was the change no one else could see: she had always felt everything — she *had been* everything, the whole Oath, every promise ever sworn between the realms, a vastness wearing the shape of a small girl — but she had felt it alone, nameless, scattered, a covenant with no one left to keep it. Now the vastness had edges. Now it had a shape with a name inside it. Now when she felt the joy running through Corvessa like a bright river, she also felt her own feet on the grass, and her own breath, and Odile's hand resting on her shoulder like an anchor dropped into happy water.

She was the Oath, and she was Isandro, and for the first time in the history of either, those were not different sizes.

She reached out along the new-thin veil and touched the minds of the waking city — lightly, the way you touch a sleeping person's shoulder — and felt the charm-sellers opening their stalls without fear for the first time in their lives. Felt a blind cartographer, wounded and grieving and gloriously alive, lying in a healer's tent in the pass while the map in his mind redrew itself with roads that led everywhere. Felt a traitor guildmaster in a cell beneath the Citadel, staring at the ceiling, feeling the pardon in his pocket turn to ash, hearing — because the Oath made sure he heard it — the bells saying *unbroken, unbroken, unbroken.*

Felt a thousand children wake up curious.

That was the part that undid her, in the end. Not the blooming lands, not the kneeling hunters, not the sky the color of a promise kept. The children. All across both realms, children were waking up on this first morning of the remade world, and some of them were humming in languages their parents had never taught them, and none of them would ever have to learn those languages in cellars, in whispers, in fear.

"Odile," she said. "Come see."

Odile came and stood beside her, and Isandro felt the absence in her — the place where her magic had been, smooth and quiet as a healed scar — and loved her more for it, if that was possible. The woman had given away the last of the old world's hoarded treasure and asked for nothing back, and the new world had been built on that empty space like a house on cleared ground.

"It's beautiful," Odile said. Her voice was thick. "What happens now?"

Isandro smiled, and the smile was nine years old, and the smile was three centuries old, and both of them were true.

"Now," she said, "someone knocks on your door."

### Odile

They came back through the veil at midmorning, into a Corvessa that had spent six hours deciding to be brave.

The streets were full. Not rioting — celebrating, mostly, arguing loudly in the way Corvessa argued about everything, market women and scholars and dockworkers all talking at once about the lights in the fountain and the bells and the green fire that had danced on the river at dawn. Someone had already strung luck-knots across the entire width of Tanner's Row, hundreds of them, in colors Odile had never dared sell. Children chased motes of drifting light through the alleys the way other generations had chased paper kites.

At the mouth of the lane below her attic, a crowd had gathered outside her building — she saw them from the end of the street and her stomach dropped, hunter-reflexes, three years of terror — but they weren't armed. They were waiting. An old woman held a feverish grandchild. A young man held a bundle of knotted thread and a look of desperate hope. A dozen others held questions.

Word traveled fast in Corvessa. Word had apparently traveled all the way to the Sunken Altar and back: *the charm seller of the night market. The one who ended it. The one who gave it all away.*

"Later," Odile told them, raising a hand, heart hammering, voice steadier than she felt. "Tomorrow. There will be a school. I'll teach anyone who wants to learn — properly, openly, all of it. Come tomorrow."

The crowd murmured, dispersed slowly, reluctantly, the way crowds do when they've glimpsed something worth waiting for.

Renn walked her home — their home, she supposed, now; the attic had gained a hunter somewhere in the last terrible week and neither of them had discussed it and neither of them intended to. At the foot of the stairs he stopped.

"You should rest," he said. "After all of it. The world can wait one day."

"The world waited three hundred years," Odile said. "It can't wait much longer. Besides—" she glanced up the stairwell, toward the attic door, and something in her chest went tight and warm and terrified and glad, all the old feelings, all at once, "—I have a feeling about today."

"You always have feelings about days."

"This one's different."

They climbed. The attic door stood closed. Morning light came through the window at the end of the hall, gold and ordinary and utterly transformed.

And as Odile reached for the latch, from the other side of the door — faint, unmistakable, patient — came a knock.

She stood very still. Renn's hand found her shoulder.

Through the wood, in a voice like wind through standing stones, in a language older than any kingdom, something on the other side said her name.

Odile Halloran, who had spent three years keeping a dead tradition alive one small charm at a time, who had given away her magic to remake the world, who had opened every door this story had asked her to open —

— opened this one too.

And found the future standing on her doorstep, asking to be let in.
