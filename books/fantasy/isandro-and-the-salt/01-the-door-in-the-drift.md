## 1. The Door in the Drift

The storm came down the Thornfell like a wolf coming down on a wounded stag, and Orsen Coldharbour heard it coming three days before it arrived.

She had learned to read the mountains the way other people read letters—by the ache in her old break at the wrist, by the way the ravens abandoned the scree slopes for the shelter of the pines, by the particular iron smell the wind carried off the high snowfields when it meant to bury everything beneath them. On the morning the blizzard was born, she split a full cord of wood and stacked it under the eaves until her shoulders burned. She hauled water from the spring before it froze. She checked the stores twice: salted goat, dried beans, flour gone a little grey but sound, a jar of honey she rationed like medicine because it was the only sweetness left in her life and she refused to spend it carelessly.

Then she sat down in the chair by the hearth, laid her whetstone across her knees, and waited for the world to end for a while.

It always did, up here. That was the point.

* * *

The waystation had been a smugglers' rest once, back when the Thornfell passes ran with contraband and the king's writ stopped at the treeline. Someone had built it into the shoulder of the mountain itself, half stone, half hollowed rock, with walls two feet thick and a single door banded in iron that had rusted to the color of old blood. Whoever they were, they had built it to keep things out. Orsen had come to it twenty years ago for exactly that reason, and she had kept nothing in it since that could be called a life. A bed. A stove. A rack of blades she never touched except to clean them, because neglect was a kind of disrespect and she had disrespected enough in her time.

There was one thing in the house she did not clean, did not touch, did not look at longer than she had to. It hung above the lintel of the inner doorway, where the heat of the hearth reached it and no more: a strip of grey linen, folded seven times, tied with a cord gone stiff as wire. Her oath-cord. Every swordspeaker wore one, once. Every swordspeaker's oath lived in it—the words spoken over it, the blood pressed into its weave—and when the order fell, the cords were supposed to burn with them.

Hers hadn't burned. That was the whole shape of her exile, folded into a strip of cloth she could not bring herself to destroy and could not bring herself to wear.

Outside, the wind rose through the afternoon from a moan to a scream. Snow came not falling but flying, horizontal, a white flood scouring the mountain. By dusk the world beyond the shuttered window had ceased to exist. There was the firelight, and there was the roar, and there was Orsen between them, sharpening a knife she would not need against anything the storm could send.

She told herself the same thing she told herself every winter: *no one is coming. No one knows you're here.*

For nineteen years, this had been true.

* * *

She woke past midnight to a sound beneath the wind—not louder than it, but wrong inside it, the way a false note is wrong inside a song. A knocking. Three strikes, spaced evenly, patient as a debt collector.

Orsen was on her feet with a blade in her hand before her mind finished waking, body moving on rails laid down two decades ago and never torn up. She stood in the dark of the main room, listening. The fire had burned to a red murmur. The knocking came again—three strikes—and then, fainter, a fourth that dragged, that slurred, that hit the door low, near the bottom, as though the arm making it could barely lift.

No traveler walked into a Thornfell whiteout. Nothing human crossed the high passes after first snowfall; even the smugglers of legend had wintered in the low valleys or died trying otherwise. And nothing that knocked four times, the last one failing, was standing comfortably.

She should not open the door. This was not superstition; it was arithmetic. The waystation was hidden. Its existence was known to perhaps five living people, and she had outlived or outrun three of them. Anything that found it had either followed her—which meant someone had finally looked hard enough—or been driven blindly by the storm, which made it harmless, or been sent, which made it the most dangerous thing to cross her threshold in twenty years.

The fourth knock didn't come again. Instead there was a sound she knew with terrible intimacy: the soft collapse of a body sliding down a door.

Orsen swore, once, quietly, at herself—for the softness she'd sworn was dead in her, for the way her hand was already lifting the bar.

Cold hit her like a wall of glass. The storm poured through the gap, snuffing candles, flinging snow across the floorboards in a hissing sheet. She got the door wide, braced it, and looked down.

A child lay crumpled against the threshold, half-buried already, snow gathering on him like the mountain trying to take him back. He was small—seven years old, maybe eight, starved thin enough that his wrists floated loose in sleeves that might have belonged to a grown man. His skin had gone past pale into a terrible blue-white, frost furring his lashes and the fine hairs of his cheeks. One bare foot had lost its boot somewhere behind him; the other foot's wrapping was frozen into a stiff grey shell. His lips were the color of slate.

He was looking at her. That was the worst of it. Children that far gone usually had their eyes rolled back or shut, sunk into the animal dark of dying. But this boy's eyes were open, black and enormous and unbearably clear, fixed on her face with an expression she had no armor for. Not pleading. Not fear.

*Recognition*, almost. As if he had been looking for her specifically, all the way up the mountain, through the teeth of the storm.

"Inside," Orsen said, and her voice came out rusty, a hinge unused. "Can you—"

Of course he couldn't. She hauled him in under the arms—he weighed nothing, a bundle of sticks wrapped in a dead man's coat—and kicked the door shut behind them on the howl of the wind. For a moment the silence rang. Then the storm resumed its siege, muffled now, furious and far, and Orsen knelt on her own floor holding a freezing child, having no idea what to do next, and knowing, with a dread that settled into her bones colder than the snow in his hair, that whatever she did next, nothing would ever be the same.

* * *

She worked the way she'd been trained to work, hands remembering what her mind had tried to forget. Fire built high. Wet clothes stripped away—she paused only a heartbeat at the sight of the brand on his collarbone, a small closed circle bisected by a line, healed long ago but deliberate, a mark made by someone who knew what they were marking—and the boy wrapped in every blanket she owned, tucked against the stones near the hearth where the radiant heat pooled. Snowmelt, warmed, not hot. Rubbing the limbs, working blood back toward the heart. She talked the whole time, low and constant, not because he could answer but because she had read once, in another life, that the dying held on better to a voice.

"You're a fool," she told him. "You're a dead fool and you climbed a mountain in a killing storm, and I want to know who put you up to it." His eyes stayed on her face, unblinking, too bright. "Nobody walks the Thornfell in winter. Nobody walks it in summer without a guide. You're what, seven? Eight? Somebody carried you partway, or somebody's corpse is up there in the drifts, and either way—" Her hands stilled on his forearm, on skin slowly, grudgingly warming. "Either way, someone wanted you here. And I don't believe in coincidence anymore. Coincidence is a story cowards tell."

The boy's mouth moved.

At first she thought it was shivering. Then she saw his cracked lips shaping something deliberate, something with structure to it, and leaned close without meaning to, the way you lean toward a whisper in a dark room.

What came out was barely a breath, hoarse and small, and it was not any language Orsen knew, and it was the only language she had ever been taught to fear and revere in equal measure.

"*Setha-kel. Vora-ith. Ansel.*"

Three syllables. Four. Five. She couldn't have counted; her ears had stopped working properly. Because the words went through her like a blade going through water—no resistance, straight to the bone—and the oath-mark on her inner wrist, the sigil inked into her skin at seventeen and dormant for twenty years, woke up and *burned*.

She cried out. She couldn't help it. She dropped his arm and clutched her wrist with her other hand, and beneath her fingers the mark glowed through her own flesh, lines of cold gold light rising like veins of ore in stone, tracing the interlocking script of the First Tongue in patterns she had spent two decades refusing to memorize and had memorized anyway, helplessly, completely, the way you memorize your own name.

The First Tongue was dead. That was not belief; that was fact, carved into history in the blood of everyone who had ever spoken it. Two centuries ago the last sanctioned speakers had died sealing the drowned gate, and the language had passed out of the world like a candle pinched out—because the tongue was not learned, it was *given*, bound into the covenant, and the covenant had no one left to give it to. Scholars collected fragments. Charlatans sold amulets inscribed with gibberish. Kings' archivists kept dead words in dead books.

And a starving, frostbitten child on her floor had just spoken it as naturally as breathing, in a voice too weak to crackle the fire, and her oath-mark had answered him like a dog answering its master's whistle.

The light faded. The burning eased to an ache, then to a throb, then to a warmth that stayed, unwelcome, like a stranger sitting too close. Orsen knelt there breathing hard, her heart slamming against her ribs, and looked at the boy.

He was watching the fading glow on her wrist with an expression of profound, exhausted satisfaction. Then his eyes lifted to hers, and his cracked lips moved again, and this time the words were slower, deliberate, each one placed like a stone in a wall:

"*Ansel. Vora-ith.*" A pause. His small hand rose from the blankets with tremendous effort and pointed—at her, at the mark, at the space between them. "*Kel-or-sen.*"

*I know what you are,* the gesture said, plainer than any language. *I climbed a mountain to find you.*

"No," Orsen said aloud. Her voice shook, and she hated it, and she made it steady through sheer hatred. "No. Whatever you think I am, whoever sent you, whatever they promised—you've got the wrong woman. There's nothing up here but an old soldier and bad weather. In the morning, when the storm breaks, I'm taking you down to Lowmere and handing you to the priest, and that's the end of it."

The boy blinked at her, slow, unimpressed, the way only children and cats can be unimpressed. Then his eyes drifted shut—not the terrible shutting of the dying, but the heavy, surrendering shut of exhaustion finally allowed—and within moments his breathing had deepened into sleep, there on her floor, in her blankets, beside her fire, in the house she had built to hold no one.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the chair across the room with a blade across her knees and watched him breathe, and the storm screamed against the walls, and the mark on her wrist throbbed in time with her pulse like a second heart, and she thought about the strip of grey linen folded above the doorway.

Twenty years ago, she had made a choice on a field strewn with the bodies of everyone she had loved, and the choice had been silence. Silence as penance. Silence as armor. She had buried her voice, her order, her name in everything but the legal sense, and she had come up the Thornfell to live inside the burial, keeping vigil over a covenant she told herself every morning was already dead, so that her failure to act cost nothing, so that the question of duty never came due.

And now the covenant had knocked on her door. Small. Barefoot. Blue-lipped. Speaking the language of the dead as easily as a bird sings, marked with a harvester's brand, sent—or fleeing—to the one person in the world least fit to receive him.

Orsen looked at the sleeping child and felt the thing she had sealed away for two decades shift under the weight of fresh snow, groan, and begin, very slowly, to crack.

*You're a fool,* she told herself. *Open the door for one stray and the whole winter follows you in.*

Outside, the blizzard howled on, and beneath its roar—if she held her breath, if she listened the way the old training had taught her to listen—she thought she could hear something else. Far off, down the mountain, down in the white dark. A sound like voices. A sound like many mouths, all saying the same wordless word.

Coming up.

She tightened her grip on the blade, and did not sleep, and waited for morning.
