## 2. Words Older Than Salt

The storm had not broken by morning. It had only changed its mind about how it wanted to kill them.

Orsen woke before dawn out of habit rather than hope, and lay a moment in the dark of her loft listening to the wind worry at the waystation's shutters like an animal testing for rot. Below her, in the main room, the fire had been fed — she could hear it breathing, the soft collapse of birch logs into embers. Someone had fed it. The child.

She had put him on the bench by the hearth with every blanket she owned and had lain awake half the night cataloguing the reasons this was a mistake. He was frostbitten in both feet; she had rubbed them with rendered goose fat and wrapped them in wool, and he had not made a sound through any of it. Not when she'd cut his boots away. Not when the skin had come up blistered and gray-white along his toes. Not even when she'd pressed salt-water to the worst of it, which would have wrung a cry from a grown man. He had simply watched her with those dark, patient eyes, as though pain were a language he had already finished learning and found wanting.

Now she came down the ladder into warmth and woodsmoke and the smell of porridge, and stopped dead on the bottom rung.

He was standing at her table. Standing — barefoot on bandaged feet, swaying slightly, one hand braced against the wood — and on the table before him lay her oath-blade, unsheathed.

"Put that down." Her voice came out flat and hard, the voice she used on wolves. She crossed the room in four strides and took the hilt from under his fingers, and the moment her skin touched the steel the mark on her forearm *woke*.

It was not pain, exactly. It was older than pain — a heat like a coal breathed upon, a pressure behind the scarred lines of the oath-script that ran from her wrist to her elbow, the brand every swordspeaker carried and none could remove. She had not felt it stir in twenty years. She had done everything a body could do to make certain she would never feel it again: no oaths spoken, no blade named, no tongue used but the common speech of traders and shepherds, worn smooth and meaningless as river stone.

And the boy was looking at the mark. Not at her face. At the mark.

He said something.

It was three words, no more, pitched low, almost apologetic, and they went through Orsen Coldharbour like a spear through water. The fire guttered. The wind outside seemed to hold its breath. Every hair on her arms rose beneath the old scars, and the oath-mark burned now in earnest, a slow bright agony, as if the script itself were straining toward the sound of his voice the way a starving thing strains toward bread.

She knew the words. That was the horror of it. She knew them the way you know a face from a dream — not learned, *remembered*, some fragment of her fragmented training surfacing from twenty years of deliberate drowning. *Vessel. Anchor. Living.* The boy had looked at her arm and said, gently, in a language two centuries dead: *You carry the writing too.*

"No," she said aloud, to him, to the room, to whatever had arranged this. "No. Whatever you are, whoever sent you — I don't speak it. I don't want to speak it. Sit down before you fall down."

He sat. Obediently, instantly, folding himself back onto the bench with the strange liquid grace of the very young, and watched her sheathe the blade with hands that were not quite steady.

* * *

She tested him all that day, and hated herself for how carefully she did it.

It began as denial dressed up as practicality. He needed feeding; she fed him. His feet needed tending; she tended them. But between the porridge and the second change of bandages, she set a slate on the table and wrote on it in chalk, slowly, the first verse of the Lamentation — the opening formula of the covenant liturgy, the words every initiate of her order had chanted at dawn until the words wore grooves in their souls. She had not written it in twenty years. Her hand remembered anyway. The letters came out clean and severe, the old angular script that predated the alphabet of every living realm, and when she turned the slate toward him she kept her expression blank and bored, a woman idling over children's games.

His whole face changed.

It was not recognition — recognition is small, a flicker, a nod. This was *relief*. This was a man lost in a desert seeing, not water, but the map that promised water existed somewhere. He took the chalk from her fingers with a delicacy that undid her more than any weeping would have, and beneath her verse he wrote a continuation.

Not the second verse. Something else — something that flowed from hers the way a river flows from a spring, answering it, completing it, the way a lock answers a key. She stared at it and felt the floor tilt gently under her certainties.

Because there was no second verse. There had never been a second verse. The Lamentation ended where it ended; her order had possessed perhaps a third of the old script entire, the rest shattered across forbidden books and broken monuments, and every master she had ever trained under had said the same thing with the same grim certainty: *the language is dead, girl. What we keep is a corpse's bones. We rattle them so the realms remember they once had a spine.*

The boy had just grown flesh on the bones.

She wrote the word for *oath*. He answered with the word for *voice*, and then, after a pause, with a compound she had never seen but understood instantly, the way you understand a chord: *oath-voice-bound*, the grammar knotting three meanings into one, and the mark on her arm throbbed in time with her heartbeat.

She wrote the word for *gate*. He went very still. Then he wrote the word for *door*, and beneath it, in smaller strokes, almost shy: *open*.

Orsen set down the chalk. She walked to the window, because she could not let him see her face, and looked out at the white roaring world, and thought: *I am going to send him away tomorrow.*

* * *

By afternoon the storm had settled into a long gray sulk, and she had settled into a colder resolve, and the two of them sat across the table from each other like opposing generals while she laid out, in charcoal on the tabletop itself, the geography of her decision.

She drew the mountains first — Thornfell's spine, the waystation a small square in its shoulder. Then the valley road south, then the river, then, far to the west past the river, a cluster of marks: Highmoor, the last town of any size, three days' walk for a grown man in good weather. She tapped it twice.

"You go there," she said, in common speech, slow and clear, the way one speaks to the very young or the very foreign. "There are people. A temple, a magistrate, wagons going east in spring. You find someone who speaks your tongue — someone will, someone always does, the world is full of strangers — and you go wherever they came from. You do not come back here. You do not tell anyone where you came from. You do not—" she searched for the shape of it, and drew instead, beside the road, a small jagged figure, and made her hand loom over it, and pointed at him, and shook her head.

He considered this for a long time. Then he leaned forward, took the charcoal, and drew — beside her looming figure — another figure, smaller, standing in front of the jagged one with its arms out. Guarding it. And above the pair of them he wrote a single word in the First Tongue.

She knew this one without any fragmentary training at all. It was carved into the lintel of every ruined gatehouse in the mountains; she had slept under it a hundred nights in her wandering years. *Ward.*

"I'm not your ward," she said. "I'm nobody's. And you're not mine, so stop—"

He touched her arm. Lightly. Two fingers on the oath-mark, the way you'd touch a wound to see if it still hurt.

The burning flared — and then, impossibly, eased. Not gone. Soothed. Like a hand laid on a fevered brow, and Orsen snatched her arm back and stood up so fast the bench scraped and the charcoal sticks rolled off the table, and for a moment neither of them moved, and the wind filled the silence with its long gray howl.

"You don't understand what you're touching," she said finally, quietly. "That mark means I made a promise once. To people who are dead. In a language that should be dead. And promises like that—" she pressed her lips together. "They eat the people who carry them. Do you understand? They eat you from the inside, and what's left at the end isn't a person, it's just the promise, walking around wearing your face."

He didn't understand. Of course he didn't. But he looked at her — really looked, the way he had looked at the slate, with that terrible unguarded attention — and then he reached across the table and, deliberately, turned her own drawing around to face her. The mountains. The road. Highmoor far away.

And next to the little square of the waystation, he drew a third figure. Small. Alone. Standing in the snow.

Then he looked up at her and raised his eyebrows, and the question needed no translation in any tongue: *You would send me out there alone?*

"Yes," said Orsen Coldharbour, last of the oath-bound, to a mute child in an empty room at the top of the world. "Yes, I would. Because out there, alone, you're only a lost boy. And here—"

Here, you're a keystone. Here, you're a living anchor of a covenant I buried with my own hands. Here, everything that hungers for the old gates will smell you on the wind, and I am one woman with one sword and twenty years of rust, and I have already failed everyone I ever swore to protect.

She didn't say any of it. She gathered the charcoal sticks back into their box with great care, as though they were the most important things in the room, and put water on for tea, and did not look at him again until the light went blue with evening.

* * *

Night came early, as it does in the high passes in deep winter, and with it the argument she was having with herself arrived at its inevitable dishonest conclusion.

Because here was the truth she kept turning over like a coin she couldn't spend: she had decided to send him away before she ever confirmed what he was. She had decided it in the first hour, watching him not cry over his frostbitten feet. Sending him away was not mercy. It was the oldest trick in her order's book — the one they'd taught initiates alongside the first verses of the Lamentation: *when the covenant calls, look busy doing something else.* She had spent twenty years looking busy. Chopping wood. Mending roof. Guarding, she told herself, a covenant that was already dust, which required precisely nothing, which was exactly why she'd chosen it.

And now the covenant had walked out of a blizzard and sat down at her table and eaten her porridge.

She banked the fire and spread her bedroll below, near the hearth, within reach of the boy's blankets, telling herself it was so she'd hear him if his fever worsened. He was asleep already — truly asleep, finally, the deep boneless sleep of the rescued — and in sleep his face lost its watchfulness and became simply what it was: young. Younger than she'd guessed. Eight, maybe nine. Old enough to have walked out of somewhere. Too young to have walked out of nowhere.

*Someone harvested his village.* The thought arrived unbidden and she flinched from it as from a hot iron. She knew the word *harvest* in the old context; every swordspeaker had. The rival orders — the quiet ones, the smiling ones, the ones who had called themselves conservators right up until the night they came for her own chapter-house with knives and nets and cages of salt — they had believed the covenant's power could be taken rather than given. Drawn out of speakers like sap from trees. Her order had died proving them wrong, or proving them right; the histories disagreed, and the histories were mostly ash.

If the harvesters still existed — if any of them had survived the purges and the years — then a keystone-child was not a boy to them. He was a well. An inexhaustible one. And wells did not need to walk, or speak, or grow old.

Orsen lay in the dark and listened to the wind and felt the old loneliness shift inside her, the way a foundation shifts when something heavy is placed on the other side of the house. For twenty years her solitude had been a discipline. Tonight, for the first time, it was a choice being weighed against another choice, and both choices had teeth.

*I'll take him to Highmoor myself,* she bargained. *When the storm breaks. Walk him down, hand him to the temple, walk home. Three days. Four. Nothing.*

And underneath the bargain, quieter, in the voice she used for truths she wasn't ready to say even to herself: *and on the road you'll teach him more words, and he'll write more answers, and by Highmoor you won't be able to do it, and you know it, and that's why you're planning the route so carefully already, isn't it, Orsen. Isn't it.*

The boy stirred in his blankets. Murmured — not words, not quite, a sound at the border of dreaming — and the oath-mark on her arm warmed like a stone held near a fire, and settled.

Outside, far off to the south, past the ridge, past the valley, something howled. Not wolf. Wolves did not sound like that — did not sound like wind trying to remember having a throat. Orsen was on her feet with the oath-blade drawn before she was fully awake, heart slamming, listening.

Nothing followed. The howl did not come again. The storm went on worrying the shutters, ordinary and blind.

But she did not sleep again, and in the gray beginnings of dawn, sitting by the dead fire with the blade across her knees, Orsen Coldharbour admitted the thing she had traveled all night to avoid:

Whatever had cried out in the dark had sounded hungry. And it had sounded as though it were searching for a voice.

She looked at the sleeping boy, and tightened her grip on the hilt, and began — silently, in the dead language, for the first time in twenty years — to practice.
