## 3. The Wraith Tide

The storm broke three days after the boy arrived, and Orsen spent those days pretending she was not afraid.

She was good at pretending. She had made a discipline of it, the way other women made a discipline of prayer. For twenty years the waystation had been her cloister: one room of stone above a cellar of older stone, a hearth that ate peat and gave back thin heat, a single window glazed with horn because glass would have been an extravagance and extravagance was a thing that got people found. She had arranged her life like a defensive formation—water drawn before dawn, wood split in silence, her sword wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards where it could neither gleam nor sing. She had taught herself to move through the world the way snow moves through the world: without weight, without sound, without leaving anything behind for the sun to find.

And then the child had come through her door speaking a language two centuries dead, and every careful wall she had built stood revealed as what it had always been—not a fortress but a hiding place, and hiding places were only ever fortresses until something came looking.

Isandro slept now in the alcove behind the curtain, in the cot she'd dragged from the cellar and stuffed with dry bracken. His frostbite was healing; the black had retreated from his fingertips to his knuckles and then to nothing at all, which should not have been possible in three days and which Orsen chose not to think about too closely, because everything about the boy declined to behave according to the rules of flesh. He ate enormously and silently. He watched her with eyes the color of wet slate, patient as a stone in a river, and when he dreamed—and he dreamed often, thrashing, mouthing words—he spoke in that other tongue, the old one, the First Tongue, and each time he did, the oath-mark on her left forearm woke like a coal breathed upon.

She kept her sleeve down over it. She kept her back to him when it burned. These were small dishonesties, and she told herself they were for his sake, though she knew in the honest hour past midnight that they were for hers. Because if the mark still lived—if it answered him—then she was not finished. And if she was not finished, then everything she had buried was not buried either. It was only waiting, the way she had waited, the way the mountain waited, patient and cold and full of things that had been put there on purpose.

On the third night the wind turned.

She knew it before she knew it, the way animals know. The blizzard had screamed from the northwest for a week, a blunt honest violence, but past midnight the scream changed pitch—dropped, thinned, curved—and began to come out of the south. Out of the lowlands. Out of the direction of the drowned coast, though the sea was forty leagues away and no wind carried salt that far.

No ordinary wind did.

Orsen sat up in the dark with her heart going hard and slow, like a fist knocking on a door from the inside. The mark on her arm was burning now, fully awake, and beneath the burn she felt something else, something she had not felt in twenty years and had hoped never to feel again: a pulling. A tugging at the root of her tongue, as if the language sleeping in her were seaweed and the tide had taken hold of its far end.

In the alcove, Isandro whimpered in his sleep.

She crossed the room in four strides and pulled the curtain back. The boy lay curled on his side, fists at his chin, and even asleep his lips moved, shaping syllables she could almost—but not quite—parse. *Stay. Stay. Stay,* or something near it, some word whose meaning she felt in her sternum rather than understood in her mind. And with every syllable the pulling strengthened, and with every syllable the wind outside climbed another register, until it was no longer wind at all but something with intention in it, something casting itself against the walls of the waystation the way a beggar casts himself against a locked door.

*It can hear him,* she thought, and the thought arrived already knowing it was true. *Whatever is out there, it heard him the moment he opened his mouth, and it has been walking ever since.*

She went to the floorboards and pried them up.

The oilcloth had stiffened around the blade. She unwrapped it by feel, in the dark, the way you unwrap a wound you are afraid to look at, and when the steel came free the firelight caught it and ran along it and Orsen felt her breath go strange in her chest. The sword was called Meridian, and it had been forged for oath-speaking, its fuller inlaid with a thread of pale metal that was not silver and not iron and that drank light rather than reflecting it. It had hung silent for twenty years. It did not hang silent now. As her hand closed around the grip, the thread in the fuller kindled—a faint gray luminescence, like moonlight seen through deep water—and the mark on her arm flared in answer, and the two of them, blade and flesh, recognized each other across the long divorce with a joy so naked it was indistinguishable from grief.

*No,* she told it. *Not yet. Not unless.*

*Unless* lasted until the third hour before dawn.

* * *

They came with the tide-turn, though there was no tide within forty leagues, and they came up out of the ground.

She heard them first in the cellar—the waystation's oldest stones, laid by hands that predated the smugglers who had used the tunnels below, hands that had perhaps built the tunnels for reasons worse than smuggling. A sound like wet sand shifting. Like a beach exhaling. She stood at the top of the cellar stairs with Meridian bare in her fist and listened to the dark below her begin to fill with a soft, granular whispering, and she understood with perfect clarity that the dead gate's hunger had finally sent out feelers long enough to touch her mountain.

The first one came up through the floor.

Not through a door or a hatch—through the stone itself, the way water comes up through sand, except that what welled up was gray-white and roughly the shape of a man, if a man were rendered in driftwood and brine-crust and old rope. It had no face. Where a face should be there was a hollow, and in the hollow was a sound: a long, tidal sighing, in and out, in and out, and Orsen realized with a lurch that the thing was breathing in the shape of words. That it was trying, with lungs it did not have, to say something in the First Tongue. That it had heard the child and come all this way on the shape of his voice alone, the way a moth comes to a lamp, and that it wanted—not to kill him, not exactly. It wanted to *keep* him. To carry him back down into the cold water where the covenant pooled in the dark and starved, and hold him there forever, a living lantern in a drowned house.

Its hollow face turned toward the alcove. Toward the curtain. Toward the small, sleeping sound of him.

Something in Orsen went very quiet and very old.

"Here," she said, in common speech, and then, because the thing would not understand that, because some doors only open in the old language, she said it again the other way. "*Hær.*"

Two words. That was all it took. The word for *here*, spoken in the First Tongue by a throat that carried an oath-mark, and the wraith stopped as if hooked through the chest, and every grain of its drifting body shivered, and Orsen felt the words leave her mouth like birds leaving a hand—felt them *work*, felt the air take their meaning and carry it, felt the covenant wake in the walls and the floor and the bones of the mountain like blood returning to a numbed limb.

Gods, it was sweet.

That was the horror of it, she thought distantly, stepping down off the stair with the sword coming up. That was the thing she had never confessed to anyone in twenty years of silence: that the speaking was sweet. That the power had always been sweet. When the words left her, she was not a woman hiding in a waystation; she was a swordspeaker of the Order of the Salted Blade, and the world arranged itself around her voice like iron filings around a lodestone, and every step she took landed on certainty. Twenty years of smallness fell away from her in a single exhalation, and underneath it she was vast, and she had missed it the way the drowned miss air.

The wraith lunged. She spoke.

"*Vetha kaelen doru*"—the first form, the cutting form, the oath her mentor had drilled into her at fourteen until it lived in her wrists instead of her memory—and the thread in Meridian's fuller blazed white, and the blade moved, and where it passed the wraith did not so much die as *remember being water*. It burst apart into a spray of brine and gray sand that hissed against the hearthstones and steamed, and the sighing cut off mid-breath, and the silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

More of them. Of course more of them. They came up through the floor in three places at once now, welling out of the stone like pus from a wound, and the cellar door burst inward off its hinges as a fourth shouldered through from the tunnels, and Orsen stopped thinking altogether, because thinking was for afterward.

What followed was not a battle so much as a recitation. The forms came back to her body the way a road comes back to a pilgrim's feet—*kaelen*, the cutting; *dorun*, the binding; *vessa morath*, the word that unmade, that she had sworn never to speak again and spoke twice before the fight was done. Each oath lit the room like lightning held steady. Each oath tore a wraith apart into salt and shadow. She fought in the old tongue and the old tongue fought in her, pouring up through the mark in her arm, through scars she had forgotten she had, through twenty years of sealed silence cracking open joint by joint like river ice in spring, and part of her stood back and watched herself with cold, appalled clarity—*look at her, look how she loves it*—while the rest of her sang.

The last wraith nearly reached the curtain.

She killed it a hand's breadth from Isandro's sleeping face, the cutting form taking its head-shape off mid-lunge, and the body came apart over the cot in a fall of gray grit that settled on the blanket, on the boy's hair, on his closed eyelids, fine as ash after a funeral. He woke then. He looked up at her—at the sword blazing in her grip, at the salt dust sifting down around her shoulders, at the woman he had known for three days suddenly transfigured into something out of the stories children tell—and he did not cry. He looked at the ruined wraith, and then at her, and then he said, softly, in the First Tongue, a word she did not know and that nonetheless entered her like a splinter and lodged under her heart.

Later, working it over, she thought it might have meant *witness*. Or *belonging*. Or possibly just *you*.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and clean, the storm spent, the sky scoured to a pale enamel blue. The waystation looked like the aftermath of a shipwreck indoors: brine crusted the hearthstones, salt rime furred the window-horn, and the cellar door hung off its hinges like a broken promise. Orsen swept the grit into a bucket with mechanical care while Isandro sat on the cot watching her, and neither of them spoke, because she had tried common speech on him and gotten only that patient slate-gray gaze, and she had refused—refused absolutely—to use the other tongue for anything so domestic as sweeping.

But the silence between them was different now. He had seen her. Whatever she had been performing for three days—the dour, self-sufficient recluse, the woman who owed the world nothing—it had died in the night among the wraiths, and the boy had attended the funeral, and he kept looking at her with something that was not fear and not gratitude either but recognition, the look of someone finding a thing they had lost.

She hated it. She was grateful for it. Both at once, in the same breath, the way grief and relief share a bed.

When the sweeping was done she climbed to the roof and stood looking south, and that was when she saw it, and knew the cost had come due.

Far off, at the edge of vision where the foothills folded down toward the lowlands, a smudge of smoke rose against the morning. Not one column but several, spaced with terrible regularity, moving. Torches or signal fires, a line of them, advancing up the old salt road in loose echelon—the formation of people searching a mountainside methodically, grid by grid, the way you search when you know your quarry is close and you intend to leave nothing overlooked. And behind them, further out, where the land flattened toward the marshes, something else: a pallor on the horizon that was not cloud. A whiteness. A bloom of pale color spreading at the edge of the world like frost climbing a pane.

The wraiths had come from there. More would come. And the men under the torches were not wraiths—they burned wood, they rode horses, they cooked meals—and that was somehow worse, because wraiths only wanted to keep him, and men wanted to *use* him, and Orsen had seen, once, twenty years ago, what the using looked like when it was finished.

She had spoken the oaths in the night. Every oath a swordspeaker speaks goes out into the covenant like a bell struck underwater, audible to everything attuned to it, and she had spoken enough oaths to ring every bell in the realm. Somewhere in the drowned dark, the starving gate had heard her and sent its hungry dead. Somewhere in the waking world, whoever tended the harvesters' interests had heard her too, and marked the mountain, and started walking.

Twenty years of silence, undone in six minutes of speech.

Orsen stood on the roof a long time, the wind pulling at her hair, her sword-arm aching with a sweetness she despised. Below her, through the roof-hatch, she could hear the boy moving about—dragging the bracken cot straight, folding the curtain with the grave diligence of a child imitating order—and the sound of it went through her like a hook.

She could send him away. Down the western paths, toward the valley villages, alone or with the first travelers of spring. He was quick; he had survived a mountain winter alone at—I didn't even ask, she thought, I never asked, I don't know how old he is. He had survived before her. He could survive after her. And she could stay here, and seal the mark, and bury the sword a second time and deeper, and let the tide of torches pass over an empty waystation, and go back to being snow.

The thought presented itself fully formed, reasonable, dressed in all its old virtues. Caution. Duty to the greater survival. The wisdom of the small life.

And then, beneath the roof-hatch, the boy began to hum—an aimless, tuneless child's humming, except that it wasn't aimless at all, because the mark on her arm warmed at it, and the frost on the window-horn melted, and somewhere far to the south the white bloom on the horizon seemed, for a moment, to lean toward the mountain like a listener bending closer.

He could not be sent out into a world that could hear him from forty leagues away. Every road was a corridor; every village, a trap with a roof. There was nowhere the sound of him would not carry.

Which meant, Orsen thought, with a calm that felt suspiciously like the beginning of a decision, that the question was no longer whether she went back to war. The question was only which war, and on whose terms, and how much of herself she would have to spend.

She looked south one last time—at the torch-line crawling up the salt road, at the white bloom waiting beyond it—and then she went down through the hatch, and took up the oilcloth, and began, with the grim economy of a woman packing for a journey she has sworn her whole life not to make, to pack.

Behind her, Isandro watched, and said nothing, and smiled.
