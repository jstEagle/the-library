## 5. What the Mountain Remembers

The smugglers' road began behind a false wall in the waystation's root cellar, and it had not been opened in twenty years.

Orsen knew this because she was the one who had sealed it. She remembered the night clearly—too clearly—the mortar still wet under her fingers, the last of her order's dead stacked in the passage beyond, and her own hands shaking so badly she had to press them flat against the stone to make them stop. *No one comes back this way,* she had told herself. *No one left to come back.*

Now she stood before the wall with a crowbar and a child at her back, and the mountain on the other side of it breathed out air that smelled of old smoke and older grief.

"Cover your ears," she said.

Isandro did not cover his ears. He watched her instead with those dark, unblinking eyes that seemed to take in everything and give nothing back except, lately, small things: a nod, a pointed finger, once—a memory she kept turning over like a coin—a single word in the First Tongue spoken softly into the dark of the cellar, meaning, she thought, *safe*. He had said it to the wall itself, as if reassuring the stone.

She worked the crowbar into the seam. The mortar crumbled like old bread. Beyond, blackness opened its mouth.

*Twenty years,* she thought. *You sealed your dead in here and walked away, and now you're going to walk through them.*

She lit a lantern stub from her kit, took Isandro's hand—it was small and cold even inside his mitten, and she hated how much she noticed that—and stepped through.

Behind them, muffled by stone and distance, something howled against the waystation door. The wraith tide had not finished with them. It would not finish with anything until either the child or the covenant was ended, and Orsen no longer believed those were two separate things.

The false wall groaned shut behind them. She wedged the crowbar across the frame, bought them perhaps an hour, perhaps less, and turned to face the dark.

"All right," she said, to the boy, to herself, to the mountain. "We go through."

* * *

The smugglers' road ran under Thornfell like a vein under skin. In the old days—in the days when there were old days—her order had used it freely. The Salt Road above was for wagons and merchants and the slow traffic of ordinary lives; below, the oath-speakers moved unseen between their holds, carrying words too dangerous for daylight. The covenant had needed couriers then. The covenant had needed everything, and everyone had given, and no one had understood until the end exactly what was being spent.

Orsen walked with the lantern held low and her boots finding the worn hollows in the stone without thinking. Her body remembered this road even if she wished it didn't. Every fifty paces there was a niche cut into the wall where a lamp had once hung; every few hundred, a side gallery branching toward some hold or shrine whose name she refused to say aloud, because saying names down here felt like knocking on doors.

Isandro walked close behind her, one hand hooked in her belt. He made no sound. Children his age should have been chattering, whining, asking questions with their whole bodies. This one moved like a small ghost, and the silence he carried was not emptiness. She had learned that already. It was fullness held very carefully, the way you'd carry a bowl brimming with water over ice.

Twice, in the first hour, he stopped and pressed his palm flat against the tunnel wall, and both times Orsen felt the hair rise on her arms, because the stone *answered* him. Not visibly. Not audibly. But the lantern flame leaned toward the wall like a plant toward sun, and somewhere far off in the rock, water dripped in a rhythm that was almost—but not quite—speech.

"You feel it," she said quietly. "The covenant. Even down here."

He looked up at her. Nodded.

"It's thinner than it used to be," she said, and immediately regretted it, because it wasn't information a child needed, and because saying it aloud made it true in a way it hadn't been a moment before. The covenant was starving. She had known it the way you know a wound has gone bad beneath a clean bandage—by the smell, by the heat, by the refusal of the world around it to heal. Two centuries without renewal. Two centuries of oath-speakers dying off one by one, each death a thread cut from a net, until the net hung by its corners and the realms it held together drifted apart in the dark.

And now a mute child from a harvested village spoke the First Tongue in his sleep, and the wraiths came for him like wolves to a bleeding thing, and Orsen Coldharbour—who had buried her power, her name, her grief, all in the same grave—was walking him south through the bones of her own history.

*You should have sent him away,* whispered the part of her that had kept her alive for twenty years. *You should have put him on the low road with a pouch of silver and never learned his name.*

But the harvesters had found the waystation within a day of his arrival. The wraiths had followed within two. There was no away anymore. There was only through.

They came to the first of the shrines an hour past midnight, if midnight meant anything underground.

* * *

It was a niche, really—no grander than that. A shallow alcove cut where the tunnel widened, its walls carved with the First Tongue in characters worn soft by time and touch. Generations of oath-speakers had paused here to speak a word of binding before continuing on; the stone was polished smooth at hand-height where ten thousand palms had rested. Above the alcove, someone had carved a line Orsen had memorized before she could write her own name in common script:

*What is spoken in love does not die. It only waits for another mouth.*

She stopped when the lantern light reached it. She couldn't help it. Twenty years, and the words still went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor.

Isandro stepped past her, drawn as he always was to the written word, and put his fingers on the carving. His lips moved. Reading. Of course he could read it—he'd probably been reading it since before he could walk—and the sound he made was barely a breath, but the alcove heard it. The whole tunnel heard it. The lantern flame stood up straight and tall, and the cold in the air loosened by a degree, and for three heartbeats Orsen felt something she had not felt in twenty years: the covenant, faint as a pulse in a wrist, beating under the mountain.

Then it faded. Starving. But there.

"Stop doing that," she said, more sharply than she meant.

Isandro flinched. Actually flinched, shoulders drawing up, hand withdrawing from the stone, and the look he gave her was not fear of her—not quite—but the wary watchfulness of a child who had learned that adults' anger arrived without warning and had to be weathered.

Orsen closed her eyes. Breathed out through her nose.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That wasn't—that wasn't at you." She crouched, bringing herself down to his height, which was getting easier to do and harder to explain to herself. "This place remembers things I don't want to remember. That's all. It's not your fault. None of this is your fault."

He studied her face with that unnerving thoroughness, the way he studied everything, as though language were unnecessary when you could simply read a person. Then he reached out and touched her hand—the sword hand, the scarred one—and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse and better than any comfort in any tongue.

They walked on. And because the mountain would not let her alone, the memories began.

* * *

She had been twenty-three the night the order fell.

Not the night of the battle—she had salted that night away so thoroughly that even now she could not raise it, only its edges, the way you can feel the shape of a tooth you've lost. No. What rose in her now, unbidden, filling the dark of the tunnel like water filling a boot, was the night *before*. The last ordinary night. The hall at Greywatch Hold full of firelight and argument and someone—Sera, it had been Sera, Sera with her crooked grin and her voice like a landslide—someone playing a fiddle badly while the others sang worse, and old Master Corvane sitting in the corner pretending to disapprove of the noise while his foot tapped the floor in perfect time.

Forty-one of them, then. Forty-one swordspeakers at Greywatch, and maybe three hundred in all the realms, and they had thought that was enough. They had thought the covenant was a thing maintained, a hearth-fire tended, not a debt coming due.

*What is spoken in love does not die.* They had sung that too, that night, drunk on cheap wine and certainty.

The next dusk, the wraith tide had come up out of the drowned gate in numbers no living speaker had ever seen—not the scattered hunters that prowled the high passes, but a *tide*, a moving wall of salt and hunger, drawn by something. Drawn, Orsen understood now, walking beside a silent boy through a dead order's tunnels, by the same starvation that had finally brought it to her door. The covenant weakening. The gate straining. The dead sea beyond it pressing harder every year against a seal no one had renewed.

Greywatch had held for six hours. She remembered the hours the way you remember a fever—flashes, fragments, the smell of scorched salt, Sera's fiddle lying broken in the courtyard snow. She remembered speaking oaths until her throat bled and her mark burned white and the words started coming out wrong, coming out *hungry*, because that was the secret at the rotten heart of the swordspeaker's art: the power did not come from nowhere. It came from you. From what you loved, rendered into language. Every oath was a piece of yourself spoken aloud, and you could not speak forever.

She remembered the moment she stopped. That, she had never salted away. That she had kept sharp on purpose, the way you keep a knife sharp, though she'd told herself for twenty years it was the opposite of a knife.

The hall burning. Corvane on his knees at the threshold, holding the line alone, roaring the great binding-oaths in a voice gone to gravel, and looking back at her—looking straight at her, across the courtyard, through the smoke—with an expression she had spent two decades refusing to name. Not blame. Worse than blame. *Understanding.* He had seen her standing there with her sword drawn and her oath-mark dark and her mouth shut, and he had known exactly why, and he had let her be a coward anyway, because he was spending his last breaths buying her time to run.

Thirty-nine dead. Two survivors. Orsen, and a novice named Aldric who had frozen entirely and whom she had dragged bodily from the collapsing hold, and who had taken one look at the dawn and the ash and the silence where forty voices used to be, and had walked east and never been heard from again.

Leaving one.

One oath-bound swordspeaker, last of the line, standing in the snow with an unbroken oath-mark and a broken vow, who had sworn on the bodies of her dead that she would never speak again—never spend another piece of herself, never feed another word to a covenant that had eaten her family whole and called it duty—and who had then spent twenty years guarding the empty husk of that same covenant out of a loyalty she could not explain and could not kill.

*You didn't guard it,* said the voice in the dark of the tunnel, and it sounded like Corvane, and it sounded like herself. *You guarded the door to the room where you keep the night it fell. That's all a waystation ever was.*

"Shut up," Orsen said aloud.

Isandro's hand tightened on her belt.

"Not you," she said. "Never you."

* * *

They made camp in a side gallery where an underground stream ran thin and cold over black stone, and Orsen allowed them both a handful of dried plums and half a loaf gone hard as slate, softened in stream water. Isandro ate slowly, watching the dark with the vigilance of a creature that had learned what lived in dark places. When he finished, he did not sleep. He sat with his back against her side—she had stopped being surprised by this, and had started, treacherously, to expect it—and traced shapes on the stone floor with one finger.

After a while she looked down and realized he was writing.

First Tongue characters, small and precise, in the film of damp on the rock. He wrote a phrase, touched it, looked up at her. Wrote it again.

"What is spoken in love does not die," she translated, her voice rough. "It only waits for another mouth." She stared at the wet letters gleaming faintly in the lantern light. "I know. I've known it since I was younger than you. My mother taught me—no. My *master* taught me. Before that, my mother taught me nothing, because my mother sold me to the order when I was six, and the order was kinder, which tells you—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Twenty years of silence and now the words came like blood from a reopened cut, all at once, unstoppable. "Which tells you what it was."

Isandro watched her. Then, deliberately, he wiped the phrase from the stone with his palm and wrote something new.

She read it and went cold.

*I know what you lost.*

"You don't," she said. "You can't. You're eight years old. Whatever happened to your village—and I will find out what happened, I swear it on what's left of my name—but whatever it was, you cannot know what it is to—"

He wrote again, fast now, the damp letters crowding each other:

*You carry it the way I carry the words. Heavy. All the time. Your face does it when you think I sleep.*

Orsen looked at the sentence for a long time.

The lantern hissed. The stream said its one cold syllable over and over. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred yards of mountain, wind moved through the passes, and she thought about the waystation door, and the false wall, and the forty-one voices that had once filled these tunnels, and the fact that she had just confessed her childhood to a child who could not answer her in any language she'd been born knowing.

Grief, she discovered, did not stay salted. Grief was patient. Grief waited twenty years in the dark and then rose all at once, and it did not rise as sorrow. Sorrow was the gentle form. What rose in her, kneeling on cold stone with a boy's careful handwriting shining at her feet, was *anger*—white and total—at Corvane for understanding her, at the order for teaching her that love was fuel, at the covenant for starving, at the harvesters for making orphans and calling it salvation, at the mountain for remembering, at herself, most of all at herself, for sealing a wall over her dead and calling it peace.

Her oath-mark flared. She felt it heat along her forearm like a brand coming alive, and the lantern flame stretched tall, and the stream stuttered in its course, and for one terrible instant the words crowded up her throat—the great oaths, the ruinous ones, the ones that would bring half the mountain down on their heads and announce them to every listening horror between here and the drowned gate—

A small hand closed around her scarred fingers.

Isandro. Looking up at her. Shaking his head, slowly, once.

*Not here,* said his eyes. *Not this.*

The mark cooled. The flame settled. Orsen breathed, long and shaking, and the anger drained out of her and left behind something rawer and quieter and, she suspected, more dangerous: purpose with the fat trimmed off it.

"They harvested your village to get to you," she said. It was not a question anymore. She had known it since the wraiths, since the harvesters, since the shape of the hunt. "Everyone who could have hidden you. Everyone who could have taught you. They cleared the ground around you like farmers clearing a field, and they missed you, and now they want you back." Her jaw tightened. "Over my body. Over my *grave*, do you understand? I have one oath left in me that I chose freely, and it's this: no one harvests that child. Not wraiths. Not orders. Not gods."

Isandro let go of her hand. And then, deliberately, in the damp on the stone, he wrote one word.

*Home.*

He pointed at her. Then at himself. Then at the space between them, at the little ring of lantern light on the black stone, at the whole small warm country the two of them had been building without admitting it since the night of the blizzard.

Orsen did not trust herself to speak. She nodded instead, once, hard, the way you accept terms you can't afford and can't refuse.

* * *

They slept in shifts. Hers came second, and her dreams were of firelight and a fiddle played badly and a voice like a landslide laughing, and she woke with wet cheeks and no memory of crying, which was somehow the worst of it.

Dawn—or the mountain's idea of dawn, a faint gray seepage through vents far overhead—found them moving again, southward, deeper. The tunnel widened. The air changed, taking on a mineral tang and, beneath it, something else: water. A lot of it. Moving.

By midmorning they heard it plainly, rushing somewhere ahead in the dark, and Orsen slowed, hand going to her sword, because she knew this stretch of the road and what lay at the end of it. The smugglers' route crossed the River Sorrel twice, and the second crossing was no ford. It was a ferry—a rope-and-pulley contraption, a flat barge, a landing cut into the rock—and it belonged to a woman named Vess, who owed Orsen a debt measured in blood and who was, the last Orsen had heard, drinking herself blind in a river-town hovel and hating the world with admirable consistency.

"She'll rob us," Orsen murmured to Isandro, guiding him forward. "She'll insult us, she'll charge us double, and she'll complain the entire crossing. And then she'll take us where we need to go, because underneath all of it, she keeps her accounts paid. Remember that. It's the rarest quality in this world."

The tunnel bent. Light flickered ahead—not lantern light, but firelight, orange and restless, dancing on wet stone.

And with it, drifting down the passage, came the sound of a voice singing a river-song, low and rough and badly out of tune, and Orsen stopped dead, because she knew that voice too, and it was the last voice in the mountains she had expected to hear again, and the first, God help her, that in twenty years of silence she had sometimes caught herself missing.
