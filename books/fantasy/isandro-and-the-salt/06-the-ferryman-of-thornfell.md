## 6. The Ferryman of Thornfell

The river appeared before they heard it—a black seam splitting the grey of the gorge, running fast and low between walls of wet stone furred with moss the color of old bruises. Orsen smelled it first, actually: cold water and pine rot and something faintly metallic, like a coin held too long in the mouth. The Greywash. It marked the edge of the smugglers' roads, the place where the mountain's secrets gave way to the valley's, and where every road worth traveling required either wings or a boat.

Orsen had neither. She had a child's hand in hers, a sword on her back she had sworn never to draw again, and three days of wraith-sign behind them that made her walk faster than her knees appreciated.

Isandro had not spoken since the tunnels. Not in the First Tongue, not at all. He had learned—or been taught by something older than learning—that his voice drew things out of the dark, and so he had folded himself into silence like a knife into a sheath. He communicated now in tugs: a tug forward when he wanted to move, a tug back when something felt wrong, a single sharp stop when he wanted her to look at something. He had stopped her twice since dawn. Both times, Orsen had found wraith-tracks in the frost—long scorings in the earth like the drag-marks of a net pulled through snow.

Now he tugged her to a halt at the lip of the gorge and pointed down.

The ferry was there. Of course it was. Vess Marrow never strayed far from her water.

* * *

The ferry itself was less a boat than an argument between a boat and a barge, flat-bottomed, tar-black, patched in a dozen places with timber that had clearly been stolen from at least four other vessels. It sat moored at a rickety landing that jutted into the current like a broken tooth. A rope ran from the landing across the river to a stunted alder on the far bank, sagging under its own weight, and beside the landing stood a hut built of driftwood and stubbornness, smoke curling from its chimney in a thin, defiant line.

And on the landing, one-legged stance braced against the current's pull, stood a woman with a single eye and a face like a cliff that had decided, after long consideration, to stop falling.

"Vess," Orsen said. The name came out of her rougher than she intended, scraped raw by disuse.

The woman did not turn immediately. She finished coiling a length of rope with the unhurried precision of someone who had done it ten thousand times, set it down, spat over the rail into the water—tradition, or habit, or possibly a greeting to whatever lived below—and only then swiveled her head.

Her remaining eye was grey-green, the color of shallow water over slate, and it found Orsen with the accuracy of a thrown knife.

"Well," Vess said. Her voice was gravel rolled in honey, deep and dry. "I'll be damned. I thought you were dead."

"I thought you were dead too."

"No you didn't. You'd have come to the funeral for the free drink." Vess's gaze slid past her, down, and fixed on Isandro. Something moved behind that lone eye—calculation, curiosity, and then, briefly and quickly buried, something softer. "And who's this? Didn't know you'd taken up mothering, Coldharbour. Thought your order considered children a distraction from brooding."

"He's not mine." Orsen heard how it sounded even as she said it—the instinctive denial, the flinch away from attachment—and hated herself a little for it. "He's... in my care. Temporarily."

"Mm." Vess drew the word out long as a mooring line. "Temporary. That's what I called my boat when I won it off a dead man twenty years ago." She jerked her chin toward the water. "You want crossing. Everyone who comes down that road wants crossing, and everyone who comes down that road is either running from something or toward something, and the ones running toward something always lie about it. So which are you?"

Orsen weighed the truth against the lie and found, to her surprise, that the lie wouldn't fit in her mouth anymore. Too many years alone. Too much salt in the wound. "Running from something," she said. "Wraiths. And worse behind them."

Vess's eyebrow—the one above the empty socket, a scarred ridge of skin that had no hair left to raise—crept upward anyway, some ghost of expression surviving the old wound. "Wraiths. On the Thornfell roads. In autumn." She said it the way another woman might say *dragons in my kitchen*. "You've been busy."

"They're following him." Orsen put her hand on Isandro's shoulder. He leaned into it, barely, the way a cat leans—not quite admitting it wanted the touch. "Vess, I need passage. South bank, tonight if we can manage it. And I need you not to ask me why for at least a day."

"A day." Vess laughed, short and hard, like a door slamming. "Coldharbour, you owe me a debt the size of this river, and you want to spend it on a day of silence?"

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll swim."

They looked at each other across ten feet of frost-slick planking, and Orsen watched the old arithmetic run behind Vess's eye: the debt, the danger, the child, the wraiths, the woman standing in front of her who had once dragged her half-drowned body out of the Greywash in the worst night of both their lives.

"You would, too," Vess said finally, disgusted. "You'd swim it in armor, just to spite me. Fine. South bank. But you're paying in stories, Coldharbour. Full price. I want to know what's chasing a swordspeaker through my mountains, and I want to know before we're halfway across, because if it's what I think it is, I'm turning this boat around and you can all drown together."

"That's fair," Orsen said.

"It's more than fair. It's generous. Get on the boat before I change my mind."

* * *

The Greywash did not carry boats so much as bully them. The current took them the moment Vess cast off, shoving the hull sideways, and she fought it with a single oar and a lifetime of grudge, muttering curses at the river in a dialect so old that half the words had probably been insults when the mountain was young. Isandro sat in the bow with his arms around his knees, watching the black water slide beneath them with an expression Orsen couldn't read—wonder, maybe, or recognition. As if the river were saying something to him in a language only he could hear.

She kept her hand near his shoulder without touching it. Ready.

"So," Vess said, rowing. "Talk."

Orsen talked. Not all of it—she kept the covenant, the keystone, the drowned gate locked behind her teeth—but enough: the child at her door, the language that should have been dead, the things in the snow that came when he spoke. She watched Vess's face go through skepticism, disbelief, and then arrive somewhere colder and more certain, because Vess Marrow had spent thirty years on this river and knew better than anyone alive what the water remembered.

"Wraiths," Vess said at last, when Orsen fell silent. "Off the Greywash. My grandmother used to say the drowned gate leaked, back before the wars. Said you could see them walking the shallows on bad nights, pale as fish-bellies, looking for voices to steal." She spat again, over the side. "Never saw one myself. Always figured it was a story to keep children quiet."

"They're real."

"I believe you. That's the problem." Vess shipped the oar and let the current take them through a narrow throat of rock, the walls closing in close enough that Orsen could have touched them. "Because if the wraiths are real, then the thing they're guarding is real, and if the thing they're guarding is real, then whoever's hunting that boy isn't going to stop at the riverbank. They'll come to me. They'll burn my boat. They'll—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "How many, Coldharbour? How many are coming?"

"I don't know. Enough."

"Enough." Vess laughed again, but there was no humor in it this time, only exhaustion wearing humor's clothes. "You know what your kind never understood, swordspeaker? Ordinary people live here. Fishermen. Smugglers. One old woman with one eye and a boat held together with prayer and theft. When your wars come down the mountain, we're the ones who burn."

"I know."

"Do you?" The oar bit the water, hard. "Do you actually? Because twenty years ago you people promised the valleys peace, and what we got was harvesters burning villages and your precious order dying in a hole in the ground, and the rest of us got to sweep up."

The words landed where they were meant to land. Orsen took them without flinching, because flinching would have been a lie, and she had promised herself—had promised Isandro, in the wordless grammar of their shared road—that she was done lying.

"You're right," she said.

Vess blinked. The boat rocked. Somewhere upstream, water slapped stone with a rhythm like slow applause.

"What?"

"You're right. We promised peace and delivered ruin. I was there. I helped deliver it." Orsen met the one grey-green eye and held it. "I'm not asking you to fight for the order. The order is ash. I'm asking you to ferry a child across a river. That's all. If you say no, I'll take him and go, and I will never hold the debt over you, and you can go back to your hut and your rope and your river, and I hope to every god still listening that they leave you alone."

Silence, except the water.

Isandro shifted in the bow. He turned—not all the way, just his head, just enough—and looked at Vess with those strange, depthless eyes, and then he did something he had not done since the waystation: he opened his mouth, and instead of words, he hummed. Two notes. Low, then high. A question, or a greeting, or perhaps simply the sound a child makes when he wants to be liked.

The river answered. Where the current had been bullying them sideways, it eased—just slightly, just for a moment, the water smoothing as if a hand had passed over it. The boat steadied. Vess's knuckles went white on the oar.

"What in the deep dark," she breathed, "was that."

Orsen realized her own heart was hammering. "He likes you," she said, which was inadequate, and true.

"He *likes* me." Vess stared at the boy. The boy stared back, humming his two notes, and slowly—so slowly, like ice deciding to become water—his mouth curved into the ghost of a smile. "Twenty years I've run this river. Twenty years, and the Greywash has tried to drown me eleven times, and now it's—" She shook her head hard, like a dog shedding water. "No. No, I'm not doing this. I'm not getting soft over a mute child who charms rivers. I have a reputation."

"You have a hut full of stolen rope," Orsen said.

"And a reputation attached to it." But the corner of her mouth had twitched, and Orsen saw it, and Isander saw it, and something in the boat changed temperature—some small thawing, some crack in the permafrost that Vess Marrow had laid down over her own heart decades ago and maintained with the same diligence she gave her ropes.

* * *

They made the south bank as the light failed, the sky going the color of a bruise healing. Vess tied off at a landing Orsen didn't recognize—newer, cruder than the northern one—and led them up a switchback path to a shelter cut into the cliff: not a cave exactly, but an overhang walled with stacked stone, floored with old nets, smelling of tar and woodsmoke and fish. A smuggler's cache, once. Now it was Vess's second home, and she guarded it with a crossbow she produced from nowhere and propped against the wall within reach of her hand.

"Sleep," she said. "We move at first light. There's a road south that doesn't show on any map worth printing, and it puts us two days from the lowlands if the weather holds."

"Why help us?" Orsen asked quietly, when Isandro had curled up against the wall and gone under with the sudden totality of the young. "Truly. The debt covers a crossing. This is more."

Vess fed a stick into the small fire. For a long time she didn't answer, and the firelight moved on her scarred face, filling the empty socket with shadow and gold.

"When the harvesters burned Hallowmere," she said at last, "eleven years back, my brother was among them. Did you know that?"

Orsen went very still. "No."

"He wrote to me after. Told me it was necessary work. Told me the realms needed feeding and the order was hoarding." Vess's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I wrote back that he was dead to me. Then I sat on this river for eleven years telling myself I'd chosen right, that family you pick counts more than family you're given, and do you know what I found out, Coldharbour? I found out that choosing is easy. It's the *keeping* that's hard. Keeping faith with the ones you picked, year after year, with nobody to witness it." She looked up, and the firelight caught the wet in her one eye before she blinked it away. "You picked that boy. Out of every door in the mountains, he came to yours, and you picked him back. Somebody ought to witness that. Might as well be me."

Orsen found she had no words. Twenty years of silence, and now twice in one day the world demanded speech of her and offered nothing adequate in return.

"Besides," Vess added, gruff again, the moment sealed back over like water closing above a dropped stone, "if wraiths are walking, somebody's got to keep an eye on you two, and you'll do it badly. You'll do it nobly and tragically and stupidly. I've read about your sort."

"We're not a sort."

"You're absolutely a sort." She lay back against her pack, crossbow across her thighs, and closed her eye. "Sleep, swordspeaker. Tomorrow we find out if the boy's friends in the water can keep the harvesters off my boat, or whether I'm about to lose the only thing I love besides my own bad temper."

Orsen settled beside Isandro, drawing her cloak over them both. The boy murmured in his sleep—two notes, low and high, the same greeting—and far out in the dark, the Greywash seemed to murmur back.

She was almost asleep when Vess spoke again, softly, from across the embers.

"Coldharbour. Those tracks we crossed at the ford today. The scorings in the frost." A pause. "There were more of them on the south bank. Fresh ones. Heading for the lowland road."

Orsen's eyes opened in the dark.

Ahead of them, somewhere south, something had already arrived.
