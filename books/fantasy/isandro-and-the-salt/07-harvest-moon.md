## 7. Harvest Moon

Vess had run the Greywater for thirty years, and she knew its moods the way a wife knows a husband's silences. Tonight it was sulking—low, sullen, dragging its mud-hemmed banks like a child pulled from bed. The harvest moon hung fat and orange over the Thornfell foothills, painting the water the color of old blood, and Vess did not like it. She did not like the stillness in the reeds. She did not like the way the frogs had gone quiet an hour before dusk, all at once, as if something upstream had told them to.

She said none of this aloud. She poled her ferry out into the current with the woman and the boy aboard, and she kept her one eye moving.

The woman—Coldharbour, though Vess suspected it wasn't her true name any more than *Vess* was hers—stood at the bow with her hand resting on the wrapped hilt of a sword she hadn't yet drawn. She'd been that way since they'd pushed off from the south landing: not tense, exactly. *Listening.* The way a deer listens, all ears and stillness, while pretending to graze.

The boy sat on the cargo planks amidships, knees drawn up, watching the moon's reflection break and mend itself in their wake. He hadn't made a sound since they'd boarded. Vess had known him three days now and had heard him laugh once—a small, startled breath of a thing, when Coldharbour had slipped on the icy stones at the ford—and even that had been soundless, more shape than noise. A mute child, Coldharbour had called him, back at the waystation, in the flat voice people use when they've already decided what a thing is.

Vess didn't believe he was mute. She believed he was *quiet*, which was a different animal entirely, and a far more dangerous one. She'd seen quiet men gut a room full of loud ones. And there was something about the boy—the way the river seemed to lean toward him when he leaned over the side, the way the current under her pole went smooth and obedient whenever he looked at the water—that made the hair on her arms stand up under her oilskins.

"Current's wrong," she said, mostly to herself.

Coldharbour turned. "Wrong how?"

"Fast." Vess spat over the side, a habit from her mother, who'd claimed it kept the drowned from taking offense. "River should be half-asleep tonight, moon this low. It's running like it's late for something." She planted the pole and shoved, and the ferry surged forward faster than her arms alone could account for. "Like it wants us somewhere."

"Or someone does," Coldharbour said quietly.

Vess grunted. That was the trouble with swordspeakers—or whatever Coldharbour was, whatever was left of whatever she'd been. They always wanted the omen explained to them neat and tidy. Vess preferred her omens unexplained. You lived longer when you didn't ask the river why it was angry; you just got your cargo across and went home.

Except she didn't have a home to go to anymore, did she. Not since the debt. Not since the night she'd pulled a bleeding stranger out of the shallows at Miller's Bend and sewed her up by lamplight and let her sleep in the loft, and woken to find the woman gone but the debt remaining, written into Vess's bones like a scar. *You owe me nothing,* the stranger had said, and Vess had known even then it was the worst kind of lie, because debts you can't name are the only ones that collect.

So here she was, ferrying a fugitive swordspeaker and a silent boy down a river that was running scared, toward a crossing she'd taken two hundred times without incident, on the night of a harvest moon that looked like a wound.

The boy looked up at her. Just looked. His eyes were dark and enormous in his thin face, and in the moonlight they caught the light strangely—not reflecting it so much as holding it, the way deep water holds a dropped coin.

"Keep your eyes off my eye," Vess muttered. "It's rude."

He blinked slowly. And Vess would have sworn, would have staked her boat and both her names on it, that the river slowed beneath them. Just slightly. Just enough to be polite.

*Well,* she thought, poling on. *That's new.*

* * *

They smelled the smoke before they saw the fire.

Not woodsmoke. Something sweeter, fouler—tallow and brimstone and underneath it a mineral stink Vess knew from her brother's soldiers' camps, from the war she didn't talk about: *burning salt.* The harvesters marked their ground with it. Her brother had told her once, drunk, laughing, that it was so the wraiths could follow the scent home like hounds to a kill. She'd laughed too, then. She wasn't laughing now.

"Coldharbour." Her voice came out low and even, which she was proud of. "Off the water. Now."

The woman was already moving. She'd seen it too—the glow upstream, around the bend where the river narrowed between the old quarry cliffs, where the crossing lay. Orange light flickered on the wet stone, and shadows moved against it. Many shadows. Organized shadows.

"Ambush at the narrows," Coldharbour said. "They want us ashore."

"They want the boy ashore," Vess corrected. "We're just packaging."

Something flickered across the swordspeaker's face—something that might have been a smile on a younger woman. "Then we stay packaged."

"There's a back channel," Vess said, already turning the pole, already working the arithmetic of water and weight and distance. "Left fork past the quarry. Shallow, fast, overhung. Too narrow for anything bigger than a coracle. If we can reach it before they—"

The first arrow took her pole out of her hands.

It punched through the shaft a hand's width below her grip and buried itself in the gunwale, quivering, and the shock of it traveled up through the wood into her palms and her teeth. For a heartbeat nobody moved. Then the night came apart.

Torches bloomed along both cliff-tops like a crop of terrible flowers. Arrows followed, hissing down out of the dark in long arcing volleys, and Vess threw herself flat against the cargo planks as one whispered through the space where her head had been and thunked into the mast-step. Coldharbour moved—there was no other word for it; the woman simply *arrived* somewhere else, crouched over the boy, sword half-drawn, and the air around her made a sound Vess felt in her back teeth, a pressure-change, a held breath.

"The channel," Coldharbour snarled. "Go!"

"I'm going, I'm going—" Vess hauled herself up, grabbed the spare sweep, and dug it into the water with everything she had. The ferry swung. An arrow grazed her shoulder, tearing oilskin, and she felt the burn of it and dismissed it the way you dismiss weather. Thirty years on the Greywater. She'd been shot at by smugglers, revenue men, and one memorable cult of river-god fanatics. This was just Tuesday with better archery.

But the arrows kept coming, and the channel mouth was fifty yards off, and the ferry was heavy, and the boy—

The boy was standing up.

"No, no, get *down*—" Vess reached for him, but Coldharbour's arm barred her, and the swordspeaker's face had gone strange. Not afraid. *Listening* again. Listening the way you listen to a door creak open in an empty house.

Isandro stood amidships in the rain of arrows, and the arrows bent around him. Vess saw it plainly, in the full orange light of the burning cliffs: they curved away as if the air itself had thickened into a hand and cupped itself over the child, deflecting each shaft wide, into the water, into the planks, anywhere but his small standing body. And his mouth was open.

He was making a sound.

Vess had spent three days assuming the boy couldn't speak, and now here he was, speaking—but it wasn't speech, not really. It was deeper than speech. It came up out of him like water coming up out of the earth, unhurried, unstoppable, and it wasn't a language Vess knew except that some animal part of her brain understood every syllable. Grief. Pure, undiluted, childish grief, given grammar. The sound a person makes when the world has been cruel beyond all sense and they are too small to do anything about it, and the world *hears.*

The river rose.

Not gradually. Not politely. The Greywater stood up.

Vess watched thirty years of intimate knowledge become worthless in the space of ten heartbeats as the water on either side of the ferry swelled into walls—smooth, black, moonlit walls eight feet high, ten, higher—and the arrows that had been falling among them struck those walls and stopped, swallowed, gone. The current reversed. The whole river, the entire sullen ancient Greywater, turned in its bed like a great dog hearing its name, and came to the boy.

"What," Vess said. It was not a question. Nothing that came out of her mouth for the next several minutes qualified as a question. "What."

"He's calling it," Coldharbour breathed. She had risen too, and her sword was fully drawn now, and the blade was doing something Vess's eye refused to fully record—drinking the moonlight, or giving it back. "The First Tongue. He doesn't know he's doing it. He's frightened and the river is answering him." Her voice cracked on the last words, and Vess heard what lived underneath the crack: awe, and terror, and something older than both. "Gods below. It's *answering* him."

The walls of water moved.

They didn't crash—they *reached.* Two black arms of the Greywater lifted themselves over the quarry cliffs, over the torches, over the ranks of shadowed men with their bows and their burning-salt braziers, and closed like a book. Vess heard screaming, distant and human, and felt nothing about it she was proud of. The fires went out all at once, drowned, and the darkness rushed back in, and in the darkness the boy's voice went on and on, that low grieving tide of sound, until Coldharbour knelt and gathered him against her and put her mouth close to his ear and spoke to him in that other language—the dead language, the old one—softly, rhythmically, the way you talk a nightmare out of a child.

Slowly, shaking, Isandro's voice wound down. The water lowered. The river settled back into its bed with a long, shuddering sigh, like a beast lying down again, and the ferry rocked in the sudden ordinary current, and the night was just a night again, except for the drowned fires and the floating arrows and the boy sobbing silently into Coldharbour's shoulder, his hands fisted in her coat, his whole body trembling.

Vess realized she was sitting down. She didn't remember sitting.

"Well," she said, to nobody. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "That's that, then."

* * *

They made the back channel and grounded the ferry in the willows a mile downstream, where the harvester ambush couldn't follow—at least not tonight, not across water that had just declared its loyalties. Coldharbour built no fire. They ate cold salt-pork and hard bread in the dark, and the boy slept between them, exhausted down to the marrow, his breathing finally slow and even.

Vess couldn't eat. She sat with her back against the mast-step, her ruined pole across her knees, looking at the child.

"That wasn't a trick," she said quietly. "What he did. That was the river. I've poled this water since before you had that sword, woman. It answered him like I'd answer my own blood."

"Yes." Coldharbour's voice was rough. She was sharpening her blade by feel, whetstone whispering, though the edge needed nothing. Vess understood it for what it was: a woman keeping her hands busy so her mind wouldn't drown. "That's what a keystone is. The covenant runs through him. When he speaks—when he *feels* loudly enough to speak—the world answers."

"And the harvesters know it."

"They've always known. It's why they harvest." The whetstone paused. "A keystone's power, taken whole, could renew the covenant without an oath-speaker. Without anyone having to pay anything. Except the child."

Vess looked at the sleeping boy. In the dark he was just a small shape, thin and ragged, one hand curled near Coldharbour's knee even in sleep, as if making sure she was still there. Her chest did something complicated and unwelcome.

"How much did it cost him? Tonight. All that—calling."

Coldharbour was silent a long moment. "I don't know," she admitted. "That's what frightens me. He spoke without meaning to. Untrained, unguarded. The power moves through him whether he wills it or not, and every time it does..." She looked down at the whetstone in her hand as if surprised to find it there. "Every time, I think, it carves a little deeper. A voice like his isn't meant to be used in pieces. It's meant to be given *whole*. And if the harvesters keep driving him—if they keep frightening him into speaking—"

"They'll bleed him dry without ever laying a hand on him," Vess finished.

Neither of them said anything after that. The river murmured past the willows, gentle now, apologetic almost, and above them the harvest moon climbed, fat and indifferent, gilding the water the color of old wounds.

Then Vess smelled it.

Smoke. Real smoke this time—woodsmoke and pitch and burning tar—and she was on her feet before her mind caught up, turning toward the ferry, and the words died in her throat.

Fire had taken the stern. It crawled up the mooring lines like a living thing, orange and hungry, and by its light she saw them: figures on the far bank, half a dozen, motionless, watching. One of them raised a hand—not threatening. Almost courteous. A salute, or a promise. Then they melted back into the trees, and the fire climbed the mast, and thirty years of Vess's life went up in sparks that drifted out over the water like dying stars.

She didn't scream. She didn't weep. She stood in the shallows with the heat on her face and watched the *Merry Widow*—her father's boat, her father's father's boat, the last thing left of either of them—burn down to the waterline, and she thought, with a strange cold clarity: *they didn't try to take us. They wanted us off the boat. They wanted me with no road home.*

Beside her, the boy had woken. He stood in the shallows too, small hand finding hers, gripping hard. He didn't look at the burning boat. He looked at *her*—at her face, lit orange, wet with river-water or something else—and his grip tightened, and in the silence between them Vess heard, clear as a bell, everything he couldn't say:

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'll carry it with you.*

And Vess, who had lost a boat and an eye and a brother and a country and had never once in thirty years let any of it show, squeezed the child's hand back and heard herself say, in a voice gone low and strange:

"Right. Well. They've burned my road home, boy, so I suppose you're stuck with me now." She turned to Coldharbour, whose face was unreadable in the firelight, whose hand had gone to her sword hilt at the sight of the watchers on the bank. "The drowned gate. That's where you're headed, isn't it? South, down the salt roads." She jerked her chin at the flames, at the retreating dark, at the whole burning mess of her old life. "Then we'd best move. Because whoever they are, they know these waters better than I do now—and I intend to find out why my brother's soldiers' tricks are being used against a child."

Coldharbour went very still. "*Your brother?*"

Vess looked at the fire a moment longer. Then she picked up her pack, settled it on her shoulders, and started walking south along the bank, away from the wreck of everything she'd owned.

"Walk and talk," she said. "Because I've got a story about a man named Corvan Vess, and you're going to want to hear it before we meet his friends again."
