## 8. The Grammar of Grief

The bothy clung to the mountainside like a barnacle to a drowned ship's hull, all dry-stone and turf, its roof weighted down with slabs the color of old iron. Orsen had found it once, years ago, on one of her aimless patrols along the high shepherd tracks—found it, noted it, and forgotten it, because a waystation keeper had no use for places she would never need. Now it was the only roof between them and a sky that had been weeping sleet for two days.

She got the fire going with hands that still remembered how, though they shook more than they used to. The peat smoked sullenly at first, then caught, filling the single round room with a sweetness that cut through the smell of wet wool and old smoke soaked into the walls. Isandro sat where she'd put him, wrapped in her spare blanket like a parcel someone had abandoned, watching the flames with an expression she was beginning to recognize as his thinking face—brows drawn, mouth set, eyes moving as though he were reading something written in the fire.

He hadn't spoken since the river.

Not in the First Tongue, not in anything. Vess had rowed them the last stretch downstream in silence, poling the burning wreck of her ferry behind them until it sank, and then they had climbed, and climbed, and climbed, and through all of it the boy had made no sound at all. Orsen knew why. She had seen what happened when he spoke without meaning to—the river standing up out of its bed like a startled animal, the harvesters' arrows swatted from the air by water that should have been past caring about anything. He had learned, in one terrible night, that his voice was a weapon that fired in every direction, including at himself.

So now he held it behind his teeth, and the silence he kept was so complete, so deliberate, that it seemed louder than speech.

Orsen fed the fire another split of the driftwood stacked by the door—shepherd-leavings, salt-bleached—and settled onto the bench across from him. Her knees cracked. Twenty years of stone floors and cold watches had written themselves into her joints, and she was suddenly, absurdly aware of how she must look to him: a gaunt woman with a sword too big for the room and a face like weathered slate.

*You are afraid of me,* she thought. *And you have nowhere else to be afraid.*

She had tried, on the climb, to explain things to him in gestures. Pointing at the smoke, the path, the sky. He understood faster than any child she'd met—he tracked her finger, nodded or didn't, filed everything away—but understanding gestures wasn't the same as understanding words, and words were the whole of their problem. He spoke a language dead two centuries. She spoke one he'd apparently never learned. Between them lay a gulf exactly the width of everything that mattered.

Well. She had been a swordspeaker once. And before a swordspeaker could bind a blade with an oath, she had been a student, and students began with grammar.

"Right," she said aloud, to fill the bothy, to hear a voice that wasn't the wind. "If we're going to do this, we do it properly."

She took a stick of charred wood from the edge of the hearth—a piece of half-burned root, good and black—and crouched at the smooth flagstone near the fire, where generations of shepherd children had evidently scratched pictures, because the stone was polished in patches and ghosted with old drawings under newer soot.

She drew a circle. Then, inside it, a smaller shape: a rough little figure with a stick-sword.

She pointed at the figure. "Orsen," she said. "Orsen." She tapped her own chest. "Orsen."

Isandro watched. His eyes went from the drawing to her face and back, and then, slowly, he uncurled one hand from the blanket and pointed at the figure, and said—in the First Tongue, in that low, resonant way he had, as though the words came up from somewhere beneath the floor of the world—a word she did not know.

But she heard the shape of it. Two syllables, rounded, the second one softening into the first like water finding a channel. And when he pointed again—at the figure, then at her—it was unmistakable. He was giving her back her own name, translated.

"Orsen," he said again, and touched the drawing.

"Yes," she said, foolishly pleased, and hated herself a little for being pleased. "Yes. That's right."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the sleet ticked against the shutters.

It was slow going, slower than she'd expected, because teaching was not like fighting. Fighting, you committed and corrected. Teaching required patience, and patience was a muscle she had let atrophy for twenty years, along with nearly everything else that involved another person. But the boy was patient enough for both of them. He never grew frustrated. He simply watched, and waited, and offered his strange syllables like coins laid carefully on a table, and waited to see if she could make change.

She built the lesson the way her own teachers had built hers, all those years ago in the halls above Aldmere, before the halls were ruins: name first, then number, then need. *Fire. Water. Bread. One. Two. Cold. Hurt.* She drew each thing, said the common word, tapped the drawing, and waited. When he gave her the First Tongue version—which he almost always did, offering it shyly, like a boy showing treasures he wasn't sure he was allowed to own—she repeated it, working her mouth around sounds that felt less like language than like weather. Some of the words seemed to move through her chest rather than her throat. One of them, a three-note word for *ember*, made the fire lean toward her, just slightly, just for a moment, before it remembered itself.

She stopped using that one after that.

By grey dusk he had eleven common words, and she had perhaps twenty of his, and they had eaten barley porridge with salt pork shaved into it, and he had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-watch, the way children do—as though sleep were a country he'd wandered into without noticing the border.

Orsen sat across from him and studied his face in the firelight.

Frost had scarred his cheeks; the marks were fading but would stay, thin white traceries like the ghosts of feathers. Seven years old, perhaps eight. Small for either. Hands that had done work—chored hands, dockside or fieldside, she guessed. And under all of it, under the exhaustion and the wariness, something she had almost forgotten how to see in people because she had spent so long avoiding them: a stubbornness. A core of *stay*. This was a child who had walked out of a burned village, across the Thornfell passes, in the season that killed grown men, following something he couldn't have named, until he reached the one door in the mountains that opened.

Her door. Of all the doors.

*Because it was the only oath-marked door left,* she told herself. *Because the covenant is starving and its keystone walked toward the last crumb of it. Nothing more. Don't make it mean what it isn't.*

But the thought wouldn't sit still, and neither could she. She rose quietly, took her cloak, and stepped out into the dark to check the perimeter—an old habit, a necessary habit, and also an excuse to be alone with the thing that had been circling her since the river.

The night was clearing. Stars in ragged sheets between the clouds, and below them, far downslope, the valley where Vess had lost her boat glimmered faintly with reflected starlight, a long silver thread stitching mountain to mountain. Somewhere down there, harvesters were regrouping. Somewhere beyond them, the wraith tide was turning over in its sleep, dreaming of a voice it had tasted.

And somewhere south, days away, under a mountain that had drowned twice, the gate was waiting. Starving.

Orsen leaned against the bothy's cold stones and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing.

*His village.*

He had come from somewhere. Children did not condense out of blizzards. And the harvesters wanted him alive and unguarded—that phrase had been in the captain's mouth at the river, spat like a curse: *the keystone comes to us whole and alone.* Whole and alone. Not *whole and protected.* Alone.

Which meant whoever had guarded him was gone.

She stood in the freezing dark and felt the knowledge arrive the way frost arrives—not all at once, but everywhere at once, in every seam.

They hadn't lost him. They had *made* him. They had harvested his village—burned it, culled it, salted it, whatever precise horror the Order of the Quiet Blade called their work—and they had left him deliberately, the way you leave a tree standing when you want its fruit later. A keystone with no keepers. An anchor with no ship. They had orphaned him on purpose, efficiently, as a step in a process, and then they had waited for the mountains to deliver him.

And the mountains had delivered him to her instead.

Orsen discovered she was gripping the doorframe hard enough to crack the wood grain under her thumb. She made herself let go. Made herself breathe. The anger came up in her like floodwater, hot and total, and she let it rise and did not speak it into an oath, did not give it a blade or a shape, because she knew—if she knew anything—what her voice could do now that it was awake again, and this bothy, this boy asleep behind her, deserved better than her rage given architecture.

Instead she went back inside, sat down across from the sleeping child, and began, very softly, to cry.

She hadn't done that in nineteen years. It was undignified and useless and it came anyway, silent, scalding, her forehead nearly touching her knees, her shoulders shaking with a control that was really no control at all. She cried for a village she'd never seen, full of people she'd never known, who had died so that a boy could be collected like rent. She cried for Vess's boat, stupidly, because the boat had been the first warm thing in Orsen's frozen life in two decades and it was ash at the bottom of a river. She cried, though she'd have denied it under torture, for herself—for the girl who had once believed oaths were clean things, that duty had a shape you could hold, that walking away from your order meant walking away from grief rather than carrying it somewhere quieter.

A small hand touched her wrist.

She looked up. Isandro was awake, had been awake for some unknown time, watching her with those grave dark eyes. He didn't say anything. He couldn't, wouldn't. Instead he took her hand—her sword-calloused, scarred, ridiculous hand—and turned it palm-up, and with one finger, in her palm, he traced a word.

She felt it more than read it. Three strokes, deliberate, the First Tongue written in touch the way he sometimes wrote it in air when he thought no one watched. She didn't know the word. But she knew the grammar of it, the rising-falling cadence, because there was only one class of word in the old tongue built on that cadence. Her fragmented training surfaced it reluctantly, like a body coming up from deep water:

*Mourning words. Condolence-forms. The syllables you spoke over the dead.*

He was condoling her. Eight years old, orphaned by design, and he was comforting *her*, because he had understood—across every barrier of language—that her tears were for him.

"Oh," Orsen said. Her voice came out wrecked. "Oh, you foolish, brave—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, in the smallest voice she owned. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry for your village. I'm sorry for everyone they took."

He watched her mouth move. He didn't understand the words. He understood everything else.

Then he did something that undid her completely. He took the charred stick from the hearthstones, pulled her hand gently toward the flagstone, and drew. A cluster of small figures—dozens of them, tiny, careful, each one distinct, some with curls of hair, some with hoods, one holding what was clearly a fishing spear. His village. Every person in it, drawn from memory, because memory was all he had left and he was going to keep every single one of them exact.

At the edge of the cluster he drew one figure apart from the rest, larger, and connected it to the smallest figure with a single line. He tapped the small one. Tapped his own chest. Then he looked at her, and his eyes asked the question his voice would not risk.

*Do you know what they took? Do you know who I lost?*

"No," Orsen whispered. "But I'll learn. Teach me. Teach me their names, if names are all that's left. We've got a language between us somehow—we'll find the words."

And then, because promises were the only currency she had ever truly dealt in, she added the oldest word she knew, the first word of the first oath she'd ever sworn, the word that had cost her everything once and might cost her everything again:

"I swear it."

The fire shifted. Just slightly. Just enough that they both noticed, and Isandro's eyes went wide, and Orsen laughed—a broken, wet, astonished sound—because of course. Of course the old tongue heard her even now. Of course oaths still meant something, even sworn badly, even sworn in the wrong language, even sworn by a woman who'd spent twenty years pretending she was no one.

The covenant wasn't dead. It was listening.

Outside, far down the valley, something answered—a sound too low for human ears to catch cleanly, more pressure than noise, rolling up through the stone underfoot like a knuckle dragged along the underside of the world. The fire guttered. Isandro's hand found hers and gripped it.

Orsen rose, drew her sword, and moved to the shuttered window.

Far below, along the silver thread of the river, lights were moving. Many lights. Climbing.
