## 10. A City That Drowned Twice

The water came first.

Isandro felt it before he saw it—a hush in the stone under his boots, a wide flat silence like a held breath. The mountain had been murmuring to him for days now, ever since they'd left the shepherd's bothy: small gossip of roots splitting rock, of cold seams where meltwater ran, of the deep slow patience of things that had never once been afraid. But here the murmur stopped, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters it, and Isandro's chest tightened around the word he could not say.

*Water*, said the stone. *Old water. Remembered water.*

He tugged Orsen's sleeve. She looked down at him—she always knelt a little when she did it, as if her height were something she was trying to apologize for—and followed his pointing hand to where the tunnel opened out onto a ledge of black basalt, and beyond it, down and down and down, a city lay drowned in air.

That was how his mind made sense of it, anyway. There was no water. There hadn't been water here for two hundred years, Vess said later, spitting over the edge for luck. But the city remembered being drowned, and its remembering was so thick that Isandro's skin prickled with it, the weight of it pressing on him like the pressure at the bottom of the millpond back home, back before home was a word with nothing behind it. Aldmere sat in its vast bowl of mountain dark with streets like riverbeds and rooftops furred with pale mineral bloom, and every street, every wall, every broken archway wore words.

Words in his tongue.

Not the tongue of his mouth—he had no tongue's words, not anymore, not since the night the salt men came and took everything with a voice—but the First Tongue, the one that lived behind his ribs, the one that came out of him only when grief or fear pried it loose. He had never seen it written. No one had ever written it for him. And yet he read it the way other children read their own names, instantly, wholly, without effort, as though the letters were less marks on stone than wounds in it, and he had always known what wounds meant.

*What is it?* Orsen's eyes asked. She had learned to ask with her eyes. Her mouth asked other things—"Are you hungry? Do your feet hurt? Are you cold?"—small practical questions that she offered like gifts, because she understood, somehow, that questions about the inside of him were doors he couldn't open yet.

Isandro pointed down at the city, then at his own chest, then spread his hands helplessly, because there was too much. How did you point at a whole language?

Vess leaned over the ledge and whistled low through the gap in her teeth. "Aldmere," she said. "Thought it was a story smugglers told to keep greenhorns off the south road. A whole city, swallowed." She squinted against the gloom, her one eye catching what little light leaked from the phosphor-lichen crusting the upper arches. "Swallowed twice, by the look of it."

Orsen frowned. "Twice?"

"Look at the waterlines, woman. Two of them. High one up there—" she jabbed a finger at a stain running along the cliff wall far above the rooftops, "—and a lower one there, halfway up the towers. City drowned, drained, drowned again. Somebody wanted it wet, then didn't, then did." She shrugged. "Or the mountain couldn't make up its mind."

But Isandro had already read the answer, because the answer was everywhere, carved into the great gatehouse beside them in letters tall as he was:

*WE GAVE THE WATER WILLINGLY.*

He touched Orsen's arm again and traced the letters with his finger, then pressed his palm flat against them. The stone was warm. Not sun-warm, not body-warm—warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, warm from the inside, and beneath his hand he felt it: a pulse. Slow. Enormous. Once every dozen of his heartbeats, a single vast contraction, like the city itself was a heart that had forgotten how to stop beating even after everything else had died.

Orsen went very still. "You feel that too," she whispered. It wasn't a question. Her hand rose to the oath-mark on her collarbone—the old scar that burned whenever he spoke—and Isandro watched her fingers tremble there. "Two hundred years I was told this covenant was a lock. A seal. Something you bolt shut and walk away from." Her voice had gone strange, thin as wire. "It's not a lock, is it."

Isandro shook his head slowly. He pointed at the inscription again. *WILLINGLY.* Then he pointed down into the dark city, at all those thousands upon thousands of words, and made the only gesture he knew for feeding: he cupped his hands and tipped them, pouring nothing into nothing.

Vess made a sound low in her throat. "Well," she said. "That's grim."

* * *

They went down into Aldmere by a stair cut into the cliff face, a smugglers' stair, Vess said, though the steps were older than any smuggling—each one worn into a shallow bowl by feet that had climbed it when the city still breathed above water. Isandro counted them out of habit and lost count past four hundred, because the words kept pulling his eyes away.

They were on everything. Door lintels and drain grates and the flanks of fountains dry as bone. Names, mostly, at first—thousands of names, ranked in columns up the sides of the great avenue like soldiers on parade, and Isandro understood with a lurch that these were the givers. These were the ones who had walked down into the water, or sent their sons and daughters down, or simply spoken themselves empty, each name a small surrender stacked against the next.

*MAERWEN OF THE THIRD TIDE, WHO GAVE HER SUMMERS.*

*HALLOR, WHO GAVE HIS SLEEPING AND HIS WAKING.*

*THE CHILDREN OF THE LAMPKEEPERS' ROW, WHO GAVE THEIR WORDS BEFORE THEY KNEW THEIR OWN NAMES.*

That last one stopped him so completely that Orsen nearly walked into him. He stood in front of it in the lichen-light, his breath fogging, reading it over and over. Children. Children younger than him, giving their words before they knew their names—which meant giving their names, which meant—

His throat worked. Nothing came out. Nothing ever came out, except when it wasn't him doing the coming-out, except when the First Tongue borrowed his grief for a doorway and spoke through him like wind through a broken shutter. He hated that. He hated that his voice belonged to something older and hungrier than himself.

Orsen crouched beside him. "What does it say?" she asked quietly. She never demanded. She waited, always, with a patience that sometimes frightened him more than the wraiths had, because patience like hers could become a place you lived.

Isandro traced the line about the children. Then he pointed at himself. Then he covered his mouth with both hands.

He felt Orsen understand it the way you feel a floor shift underfoot. Her face didn't change much—her face almost never changed much—but her breath did, one long careful exhale, and her hand came to rest on his shoulder, heavy and warm. "No," she said softly. "No one is asking that of you. Whatever this city asked of its children, that debt is not yours."

But the stone disagreed. The stone sang it, low and constant, the great pulse rolling up through their boots: *the gate is fed, the gate is fed, the gate is fed,* and Isandro thought: *and it is hungry now, and I am the only food left that can walk.*

* * *

They made camp that night in a hall that must have been magnificent once—a vaulted chamber near the top of the lower waterline, its ceiling starred with dead lanterns of colored glass, its floor a mosaic so vast Isandro couldn't see its edges. Waves, he realized. It was a map of waves, thousands of tiles of blue and silver arranged in spirals, all of them flowing inward toward a circle at the center of the room where a round stone table stood like an island.

Words ringed the table. He went to it while Vess rigged their little fire behind a windbreak of fallen masonry, while Orsen checked the sightlines with her sword across her knees, and he put his hands on the rim and read.

This was not a list. This was a conversation—frozen mid-argument, voices carved in stone instead of spoken, generations of them layered over one another until the table's edge was a palimpsest half a finger thick. He read forward and backward through time without knowing how he did it, the way you can find your way through a house in the dark if the house loves you.

The oldest layer was triumphant. *THE GATE IS FED AND THE REALMS ARE HELD. LET EVERY GENERATION ADD ITS VOICE.*

Then, higher, thinner: *HOW MANY MORE? THE TIDE ASKS DEEPER EVERY YEAR.*

Higher still, scratched shallow and fast, like someone carving while looking over their shoulder: *IT IS NOT A SEAL. IT NEVER WAS. WE BUILT A MOUTH AND TAUGHT IT TO EAT.*

And near the top, in letters that shook: *STOP FEEDING IT. SEAL IT WITH SOMETHING ELSE. SEAL IT WITH AN OATH THAT COSTS NOTHING—*

Beneath that, someone else had answered, deep-cut and final: *THERE IS NO SUCH OATH. THERE NEVER WAS. THE CHEAP MAGIC IS A STORY WE TELL CHILDREN.*

Isandro pulled his hands off the table as if it had bitten him.

"Boy?" Vess's voice, sharp with concern. "You've gone white as milk."

He backed away from the table and collided gently with Orsen, who had come to stand behind him without his hearing her—she moved like that, quiet as snowfall. She turned him around and studied his face, and whatever she found there made her jaw tighten. "Show me," she said.

He led her to the table. He showed her the last lines first, because he needed her to see them, needed an adult to stand between him and the meaning of them. He tapped the shaking letters—*seal it with an oath that costs nothing*—then the answer beneath. Then he pointed at the oldest words, the proud ones, the ones about every generation adding its voice. And finally, because he could not not say it, because it had been building in him since the gatehouse, since the stair, since the moment he'd felt the city's heartbeat under his palm—he opened his mouth.

He didn't decide to speak. That was the terrible truth of it, the thing he could never explain to Orsen or Vess: the First Tongue used him, it was not used by him. His grief rose like floodwater finding a crack, and the crack was his open mouth, and out of him came three words in a voice that was his and vastly older than him at once, ringing off the vaulted ceiling, making the dead lanterns hum:

*"The gate eats."*

The pulse beneath the floor stuttered. For one awful instant the whole city seemed to listen—to turn its drowned attention toward the small warm living thing standing in its dead heart—and the lichen-light dimmed, and far away down in the dark, below even the lower waterline, below even the deepest street, something vast exhaled.

Then the light steadied. The pulse resumed. The city went back to its dreaming.

But Orsen had gone the color of ash, and her hand was pressed hard over the oath-mark at her collarbone as if holding a wound closed, and Vess had come around the fire with her knife already drawn, scanning the black doorways.

"What did it say?" Vess demanded. "What did the boy say?"

Orsen didn't answer her. She sank down slowly until she was kneeling in front of Isandro on the mosaic waves, eye level with him, and her hands took his hands, and her grip was not gentle—it was the grip of a woman holding on to a spar in open water.

"The gate isn't starving because it was abandoned," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle like river ice. "It's starving because we stopped feeding it. My order didn't guard a sealed door for two hundred years. We guarded an empty bowl and called it mercy." She laughed—one short, awful note with no humor in it anywhere. "And now the bowl has found a child, and it's patient, and it knows how to wait."

She pulled him close then, suddenly, fiercely, his cheek against the cold rings of her mail, and Isandro let himself be held. Over her shoulder he could see the stone table, the frozen argument of the dead, the proud old words shining faintly in the lichen-light:

*LET EVERY GENERATION ADD ITS VOICE.*

And beneath the floor, steady as tide, patient as hunger, the city pulsed its one long syllable over and over, and for the first time Isandro understood that it was not a heartbeat at all.

It was a knock. From the inside.

Far off in the tunnels behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Vess had been listening for since the ferry: the crunch of many boots on salt-crusted stone, coming down the smugglers' road, following them into the dark.
