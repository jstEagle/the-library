## 11. The Traitor's Toll

The river had a smell for every season, and Vess knew them all the way a widow knows a dead man's shirts.

In spring it was iron and snowmelt, sharp enough to sting the nose. In summer, green rot and fish-slime, honest stink of living water. In autumn—the season they were drowning in now—it was leaf-rot and cold stone, the smell of things settling down to die. But tonight, poling her patched and half-burned ferry through the narrows below Aldmere's drowned spires, she caught a smell that did not belong to any season at all: lamp-oil and tallow smoke, riding the mist off the water like a hand on the back of her neck.

She knew that smell. She'd grown up in it. Her father had been a chandler before the harvesters bought him out and bought him whole, and the whole of Vess's childhood had smelled of tallow and wick-trimmings and the particular sweetness of rendered fat going bad.

Somebody downstream was burning a lot of candles.

"Vess." Orsen's voice came low from the bow, where the swordspeaker crouched with one hand resting—casual as a sleeping adder—on the wrapped hilt of her blade. The boy sat between her boots, wrapped in a wool blanket gone gray from river spray, his dark eyes fixed on the mist. "You smell it too."

"Course I do." Vess put her pole into the current and held it there, letting the boat drift, listening. The Ferryman of Thornfell did not get old by being deaf. "That's not campfire. That's a lot of little flames all lit at once. That's ceremony."

Isandro made a small sound—not a word, never a word, the boy hadn't spoken common speech yet, though Orsen swore he muttered things in his sleep that made the oath-mark on her wrist glow like a coal—and pressed closer to Orsen's knee. He'd taken to doing that since the crossing. Since the river had stood up out of its bed for him like a dog answering a whistle, and Vess's boat had burned to the waterline anyway, and they'd all three of them walked away wet and alive and poorer by one vessel.

Vess missed that boat more than she could say, and said nothing about it ever.

"Mist's wrong," she said instead. "See how it sits? Flat. Somebody's burned something on the banks to make a stillness. Old smuggler trick—my da used it. Kills the wind so sound carries clean across the water." She spat over the side, an old habit, into water she no longer trusted. "They want us to hear them coming."

"Then let's hear them," Orsen said.

* * *

They came out of the mist at the Tollstone, because of course they did. There was only one place on this stretch of the Thornfell where a ferry had to put in—the old tolling shelf where smugglers had paid their passage in coin or blood for two hundred years, a flat shelf of black rock thrust out over the current like a lower lip. Vess had run this crossing since she was fourteen years old. She knew every stone of it the way she knew her own teeth.

There were lanterns on the shelf. A dozen of them, maybe more, set in a neat crescent, each one a fat white candle in a jar of green glass. Harvest-green. And standing among them, with his hands clasped behind his back like a merchant waiting on a delivery, was a man Vess had spent eleven years not thinking about.

He'd gotten fat. That was the first thing, and it shamed her, that it was the first thing—that after everything, after the burning and the blood and the eleven years of silence between them, what her heart registered first was that Tammas Vael had gotten soft around the middle, and his hair had gone gray at the temples, and he wore a harvester captain's coat cut fine enough that it probably cost more than her boat had.

Her boat. Which was ash. She made herself stop thinking about the boat.

"Well," Tammas called across the water, and his voice hadn't changed at all, still that warm barrel-deep voice he'd used to talk their father into anything, "still running the Tollstone, Vess? I heard you'd burned your last hull. I told them you'd have another one inside a month. You always were like a spider—lose a leg, grow another."

"I'm not a spider," Vess said, bringing the boat in against the shelf with a bump she felt in her bones. "I'm the thing spiders are afraid of."

She stepped onto the rock with her boathook in her fist and her knife at her hip, and behind her she heard Orsen step down too, light as snowfall, and the boy's bare feet making almost no sound at all. Twelve harvesters in the candlelight, in their green glass lantern-light, and not one of them moved. They didn't need to. This wasn't an ambush. Ambushes were for people you couldn't buy.

Tammas smiled at her. It was their father's smile, worn loose at the hinges from use.

"Sister."

"Don't." The word came out of her flat and hard, a stone skipped across eleven years of water. "Don't you sister me on this rock, Tam. You've got no claim on the word anymore."

"No?" He spread his hands. Behind him, the harvester rank stood silent, faces shadowed under their hoods, and Vess counted crossbows without meaning to—six, seven, eight, two of them trained not on her but on the small shape half-hidden behind Orsen's cloak. "Eleven years I've let you run your little ferry and nurse your grudges, and I've never once sent anyone to collect the toll you owe. That has to count for something."

"The toll I owe *you*, you mean." Vess laughed, and it came out ugly. "For what, Tam? For turning Da in when the order came through? For signing the family name to the Quiet Blade's charter so we'd eat that winter? For standing in our father's kitchen and telling me—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, hated him for cracking it, "—telling me it was arithmetic. That's what you said. *It's just arithmetic, Ves.* Some lives against some other lives. The realms against the children. As if children weren't the realms."

"And look how that arithmetic turned out." Tammas's smile thinned. She'd hurt him. Good. She wanted the wound to be mutual; it was the only inheritance they had left. "Your precious swordspeakers are dust, Vess. Your covenant is a corpse. The gate starves, the wraiths walk, and the only thing standing between these mountains and the salt tide is an order willing to do the necessary work. We don't enjoy it. Nobody enjoys it. But somebody has to hold the ledger."

"Somebody has to hold the ledger," Vess repeated softly. She looked past him, at the hooded ranks, at the green glass candles guttering in the river wind that shouldn't have reached them but did, here, now, as if the water itself was leaning in to listen. "Do you know what we found under Aldmere, Tam? Do you know what the drowned city says, written in a language older than your charter?"

Something flickered in his face. Curiosity, maybe. Or fear wearing curiosity's coat.

"It says the gate was never sealed," Vess said. "It was fed. Fed by oath-speakers who gave up everything they were, willingly, generation after generation, because that was the price and they paid it with open eyes. And your order takes children who can't even say yes and carves the same payment out of them by force, and calls it arithmetic." She tightened her grip on the boathook. "That's not bookkeeping, Tam. That's theft. From the only people who own what you're taking."

"You don't know what you're talking about." His voice had gone quiet, which was worse than loud. She remembered that quiet from childhood, from the day the harvesters' wagon had come for the Aldmere tithe. "You never read the ledgers. You never saw the tallies. The wraith-tide is rising, Vess. Whole valleys gone quiet this year. And here you are, ferrying the one child in the world who could end it, straight toward the gate, with a broken swordspeaker and a burned-out boatwoman, and you think that's rescue." He shook his head slowly. "It's not rescue. It's just a longer way to drown."

Behind her, Isandro made his small sound again—a breath, a catch, the ghost of a word—and Vess heard one of the crossbowmen shift his weight, and Orsen's hand came to rest on the boy's shoulder, and the temperature of the night changed by a degree Vess couldn't measure but could feel in her back teeth.

"What do you want, Tam?" she said. "You didn't light a dozen candles and stand out here in the cold to argue theology. Say it."

And there it was—the real smile, the merchant's smile, the one that meant the goods were finally on the table.

"Amnesty," Tammas said. "Full amnesty. Yours, and the swordspeaker's. Pardon for the crossing, pardon for the burned patrol boats, pardon for everything back to the day you walked out of Da's kitchen. The order remembers you were one of us once. Your father's name still opens doors." He paused, and Vess watched him choose the next words with the care of a man laying out coins one at a time. "Bring the boy to the gate yourself. Hand him over gentle, no chains, no fear—he'll be treated well, Vess, I swear it on Ma's grave. Better than well. He'll be honored. And you walk away rich, pardoned, and free. Free of the mountains. Free of the war. Free of *her*." His eyes went past her shoulder, to Orsen, and there was eleven years of poison in the look. "You've carried other people's oaths long enough, little sister. Come carry your own life for a change."

* * *

The strange thing, Vess thought later, was how quiet it was inside her, in that moment. No storm. No roaring. Just a kind of clear cold stillness, like the surface of the river on a windless dawn, and underneath it, far down, the shapes of things she could see plainly for the first time in years.

Because here was the truth she'd never said out loud, not even to herself, not once in eleven years: some part of her had been waiting for this offer. Had been dreading it and waiting for it both, the way you dread and wait for the letter that tells you a sick parent is finally dead. Because Tammas was right about one thing. She *had* been carrying other people's burdens since she was fourteen years old—her father's debts, her brother's choices, her passengers' secrets, the whole smuggled weight of the Thornfell passing through her hands season after season while her own life sat unpacked in boxes she never opened. Amnesty. Riches. A door out of the mountains, out of the cold, out of the war. A life with her name on it and nobody else's.

And all it would cost was one mute child who had looked at her across a burning river with absolute trust in his face, and a woman who had stood on her deck with death walking out of the water and spoken an oath in a language two centuries dead, and who afterward, soaked and shaking, had quietly helped Vess bail out the wreckage without being asked.

Amnesty. She turned the word over. It fit her hand like a coin worn smooth by other thumbs.

"You done?" she said.

Tammas blinked. "Vess—"

"I'm not finished. I'm asking if *you're* done, because I want you paying attention for the rest." She planted the boathook on the stone, and the iron rang on the rock, and every crossbow in the crescent twitched, and none of them fired, because Tammas raised one finger and they held. "Here's my toll, brother. Here's what you can carry back to your order.

"I was eight years old when they took the Aldmere tithe. Eight. I remember the wagon, and I remember the children in it, and I remember that one of them was Nella Cotch, who used to braid my hair, and I remember that she was singing when they took her, because she thought she was going somewhere good. And I remember you, sixteen years old, standing in the yard in your new green coat, telling me it was arithmetic." Vess's voice didn't shake now. It was steady as a keel. "Eleven years I told myself you'd been young. Eleven years I told myself the order had you by the throat same as it had Da. Eleven years I kept a door open in my chest with your name on it, because you're my blood, and blood is supposed to mean something.

"But blood doesn't mean a thing, Tam. I learned that on the river. Blood is just what you're born carrying. Family is what you build, plank by plank, in the weather, with your own hands, and you nail it down and you patch it when it burns and you *stay aboard when it's sinking*. That's family. And I've got more of it in one burned boat than your whole order's got in a thousand years of green coats."

She stepped forward, one pace, close enough to see the candlelight reflected twice in his eyes.

"So here's your amnesty, captain. Take it back to Hollowmere. Tell them the Ferryman of Thornfell refuses. Tell them she keeps the toll-gate, and the toll is this: every child your order touches from this day forward, she will hear about it, and she will come, and she will not come alone. Tell them the woman with the sword has remembered her oaths, and the boy with the voice has started learning his, and the river itself knows our names now. Tell them all of it. And then tell them—" and here her voice did break, just slightly, and she let it, because some words deserved to be carried rough, "—tell them my brother died eleven years ago, and I buried him myself, and the man standing on this rock is just the thing wearing his coat."

For a long moment Tammas Vael said nothing at all. The candles guttered. The river ran. Somewhere out in the mist, deep under the water, something vast and patient shifted its weight, and the Tollstone hummed faintly underfoot like a struck bell.

Then Tammas smiled—the merchant's smile, the last one, the one that closed the shop—and took a green glass lantern from the rock at his feet.

"Burn the bridge, then," he said quietly. "We'll see whose side the water's on when the tide comes." He turned the lantern over in his hands, once, like a man weighing it. "You should know, Vess. The order isn't sending patrols anymore. After tonight, it sends the Tidecaller. And the Tidecaller doesn't bargain, and doesn't burn bridges, because it doesn't need bridges." He set the lantern down on the stone between them, upright, unlit, a green glass promise. "Last chance, little sister. It stays unlit until I'm back in the boat."

Vess looked at the lantern. Then at her brother's face, and found, beneath the merchant's calm, a flicker of the boy he'd been—the one who'd taught her to skip stones, who'd cried when their mother died, who'd signed the charter with shaking hands. It was still in there. That was the worst of it. It was still in there, drowned, and no amnesty she could offer would raise it.

"Light it," she said. "I want to watch."

And she watched. The flame caught with a soft whump, green as pond ice, and the stillness-smell of tallow rolled over the rock, and Tammas Vael turned and walked into the mist with his twelve shadows around him, and did not look back. Neither did she. Looking back was for people with a bridge left to burn.

When the last lantern-glow faded, Vess stood alone on the Tollstone a moment, breathing the river air, feeling strangely hollowed and strangely full at once, like a boat emptied of cargo that rides higher and lighter and dangerously near the wind.

Orsen appeared beside her, silent as ever, the boy's hand tucked in hers. The swordspeaker said nothing—she was a woman who understood that some silences were load-bearing—but Isandro pulled free of her grip, crossed the rock, and pressed his small cold hand flat against Vess's forearm. Just that. Just a touch, and his dark eyes lifted to hers, and in them was a question he had no words to ask.

"Aye," Vess said roughly, ruffling his hair with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "I'm sure. Don't you go looking sorry for me, sprat. I've been dead eleven years myself. Tonight I just made it official."

She picked up the green lantern, carried it to the edge of the shelf, and dropped it into the river. The water took it without a ripple, the flame snuffing like a pinched wick, and far below—she would have sworn this later, under oath, though no one asked her to swear anything—a vast pale shape turned in the dark and began, slowly, deliberately, to rise.

"Get in the boat," Vess said. "Both of you. We're not stopping again until the gate."
