## 12. What Orsen Buried

They took her at the ford below Greywater Stair, where the smugglers' road narrowed to a single line of stones and the water ran loud enough to drown the sound of boots.

Orsen had known, in the way she knew weather in her knees, that the harvester column was behind them. She had heard it for two days—not marching, which would have been forgivable, but *riding*, with the loose, patient rhythm of hounds that did not need to hurry because they already knew how the chase would end. She had sent Vess ahead with Isandro up the goat track toward Hollowmere Gap, and Vess had gone, swearing creatively, with the boy's hand locked in hers. That was the bargain Orsen had made with herself at dawn: the child does not see me taken. The child does not learn what I am worth as a hostage until he is too far away for it to matter.

So when the six riders closed the gap at the ford, she turned and met them with her sword drawn, and she made them pay for every step of the crossing.

She remembered little of it afterward in any clean sequence. There was the moment her blade opened a rider's forearm to the bone; the moment a weighted net came out of nowhere and dragged her arm down; the moment the water went red around her boots and someone struck her across the temple with something that was not quite metal and not quite stone. The world tilted. The river took her sideways. Hands—gloved, competent, almost gentle in the manner of men handling something valuable—hauled her onto the shingle.

The last thing she saw before the dark came down was a captain's badge on a grey cloak: a circle bisected by a blade. The mark of the Quiet Blade.

Then nothing, and the nothing had a taste like salt.

* * *

She woke in a room without windows.

That alone told her where she was. Hollowmere was the harvesters' seat in the northern mountains—a fortress carved into the bones of a dead monastery, its halls lit by lamps that burned behind smoked glass so that no light escaped to guide pursuers or deserters. Orsen had never been inside it. Twenty years ago she had stood outside its walls with four hundred swordspeakers and sworn, on the frozen bodies of her order, that she would burn it to its foundations.

Instead she had walked into the Thornfells and let twenty years bury her.

Now the walls she had sworn against held her upright, bound at wrists and ankles to a chair of black iron, in a room whose only furniture besides herself was a table, a lamp, and a woman sitting behind the table with her hands folded like a schoolmistress between lessons.

"You're awake," the woman said. "Good. The draught we use leaves most people sick for an hour. You've been sick for three. Your body remembers being drugged."

"I've been drugged by better," Orsen said. Her voice came out gravelled but level, and she was quietly proud of that.

"I don't doubt it." The woman was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had decided long ago that beauty was a poor investment. At her collar, where the other harvesters wore their badges, she wore a small chain of white beads—salt-pearls, each one cured from the tear-ducts of a drowned man, if the old stories were true. "My name is Magistra Corvane. You may not remember me. I was a novice at Aldmere Abbey the year your order fell."

Orsen said nothing. The name landed somewhere beneath her ribs and sat there, cold.

"You were young," Corvane continued. "Younger than me. Ishild's own student, they said—the last one the First Mother trusted with the deep oaths. And then the abbey burned, and the order died, and one girl walked out of the ashes carrying a sword and a silence, and the mountains swallowed her." She unfolded her hands. "We have wondered for twenty years what you carried out. Now we know. You carried the child."

"He isn't yours."

"Nothing is anyone's," Corvane said mildly. "That is rather the point of our whole philosophy. But we'll come to that." She rose, crossed to the door, and spoke a word through the crack. Two figures entered bearing between them a shallow basin of grey stone, wide as a shield, and set it on the table. Into it they poured water from a jug—and salt, from a pouch of woven hair, in a thin deliberate stream, whispering, until the surface of the water went still and bright as a mirror.

Orsen knew what it was. Every swordspeaker knew, though none of her generation had ever seen one used. A telling-basin. Salt and water and grief, the oldest instruments of the First Tongue, arranged to do what the tongue itself could do: reach into a mind and open the doors a person had sealed.

"No," Orsen said.

"It won't hurt," Corvane said. "That's the cruelty of it, actually. It will feel like remembering. Which, after twenty years of your particular discipline, I imagine is the worst thing anyone could do to you."

"I'll break the chair."

"You'll try. The iron is oath-forged; it was made for people like you, by people who used to be like you." Corvane sat back down, and there was something almost gentle in her posture now, the gentleness of a surgeon before the first cut. "Here is what happens next, Swordspeaker Coldharbour. My people are bringing the boy down from the Gap tonight. He will be treated kindly—he is the keystone, and we are not butchers, whatever your order taught you. But before he arrives, you and I are going to settle a question that has haunted my order since the fall of Aldmere. Because we found something in the abbey's ruins, in the years after. Records. Testimony. And everything we read says that on the night your order died, the covenant did not fail. It was *refused*. By one person. By you."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above, stone settled against stone with a sound like a slow breath.

"So," Corvane said, and touched the rim of the basin, and the water began to turn. "Let us look together at what you buried."

* * *

It felt, as promised, like remembering.

Not like watching. Like *being there*—the basin pulling the sealed thing up through twenty years of salted silence the way a flood pulls a coffin out of a hillside, whole and rotten and unmistakably real.

She was in the undercroft of Aldmere Abbey, and it was the last night of the world.

She was twenty-three years old, and her hands did not shake, though everything else in her wanted to. Around her, in the candle-dark, knelt the remnant of the Order of the Oathbound—eleven of them, of the four hundred who had gathered at the abbey's founding, the rest spent across two centuries of holding the gate against the wraith tide. Eleven left, and the covenant starving, and the drowned gate beneath the mountain groaning like a ship in a storm.

Her teacher knelt at the center. Ishild, the First Mother, ancient and straight-backed, her oath-marks glowing along both arms like veins of fire under frost-pale skin. Before her lay the ritual array: salt, water, eleven blades laid in a wheel.

"The renewal requires a speaker," Ishild was saying, in the calm voice she used for hard truths. "One who gives to the gate the memories that fuel the oath. All of them. Everything that makes the voice. That has always been the price. Our founders paid it gladly, one by one, each surrendering themselves until only echoes remained to hold the seal. We are the last echoes, children. It falls to one of us to go down to the gate and give everything—to walk into the water knowing that the woman who walks in will not be the woman who walks out, because there will be no woman walking out. Only a vessel. Only a voice with no self behind it, holding the door forever."

And Orsen—young Orsen, whole Orsen, Orsen with all her memories still hers—stood up in the circle and said:

"No."

The word rang in the undercroft like a dropped bell.

Ishild looked at her, and Orsen watched her own younger face with the detachment of a ghost attending its own funeral, and she saw that her teacher was not surprised. That was the worst of it. Ishild had *expected* this. Had perhaps, in some chamber of her ancient heart, been counting on it.

"Say what you mean, child."

"I mean that this is murder dressed as duty." Young Orsen's voice shook now, finally, but it did not stop. "Eleven of us left, and you ask one to erase herself—not to die, which would be mercy, but to be *unmade*, to stand in the dark under the mountain for eternity with nothing left of herself, holding a door for realms that have forgotten we exist? The founders chose it because they believed the covenant was worth a soul. Fine. But the covenant is starving because the realms stopped feeding it, stopped *remembering*, and now you want to feed it the last of us like it's owed. It isn't owed. Nothing is owed. If the price of the gate is a person, then the gate is wrong, and I will not be the one who pays it, and I will not stand witness while you choose who does."

"And if the gate fails?" Ishild asked softly. "If the wraith tide comes up out of the deep roads and takes the lowlands, the river towns, the children—"

"Then let the realms that forgot their debts find another way to pay them!"

Silence. Eleven faces in candlelight. And then Ishild rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and did the thing that Orsen had replayed in the dark of the waystation for twenty years without ever letting herself finish the memory.

She smiled.

"Then it must be me," the First Mother said. "I am the eldest. It was always going to be me, Orsen. I asked for a volunteer because I hoped—I foolishly, proudly hoped—that one of you loved the covenant more than you feared the price. But I will not command it. An oath given under compulsion is salt on stone; the gate would spit it back." She lifted her blade from the wheel. "I go down alone, then. And the rest of you will hold the abbey until the tide breaks against the renewed seal—or until it doesn't."

What happened next, Orsen had never once allowed herself to remember completely, and the basin gave her no mercy now.

The gate did not accept Ishild's sacrifice cleanly. Something in the old woman's certainty had curdled over the centuries into something the covenant tasted as *bitterness*—a gift given in resentment of the giving. The renewal half-took. The seal knit crooked. And the backlash came up the mountain like a wave coming up a well-shaft, and it took the abbey, and the ten swordspeakers who had stayed to hold the rites, and it would have taken Orsen too except that she had already run—had already, in the first moments of the quake, done the arithmetic of survival and fled up the stair with her sword and her silence, while the mountain folded itself around everyone she had ever loved.

She had told herself for twenty years that she had fled to carry the truth. That someone had to survive to testify.

The memory showed her the uglier shape of it: she had fled because she was afraid, and she had built the testimony afterward, the way survivors build everything, out of whatever rubble was nearest to hand.

* * *

Orsen came back to herself gasping, and discovered she had torn skin off her wrists against the oath-forged iron, and did not care.

"Well?" Corvane's voice, from very far away, then nearer. "Do you see why we needed you to look?"

"You—" Orsen's throat was raw. "You showed me that to break me."

"I showed you that because you have spent twenty years believing a lie about yourself, and lies make poor soldiers." Corvane leaned forward, and her eyes in the lamplight were not cruel. They were worse than cruel. They were *understanding*. "You think you refused out of cowardice. The basin just showed you the truth you salted away: you refused because you were right. The renewal as your order practiced it was a devouring. One soul fed into the dark, generation after generation, and the covenant gorged and starved and gorged again, and called it holiness. Your order didn't fall because you failed them, Swordspeaker. They fell because the system they served was broken, and Ishild knew it, and went down into the water anyway rather than admit it."

"Stop."

"She half-sealed the gate out of bitterness and killed her own order doing it, and the institution she left behind wrote songs about her sacrifice. And you—the one person who said *no*—you punished yourself for it for two decades." Corvane spread her hands. "We of the Quiet Blade read those same records and reached the opposite conclusion from your grief. Not that the covenant should be renewed by sacrifice—but that it should be *harvested*. Controlled. The keystone's power taken deliberately, rationed, owned, so that no more Ishilds ever have to drown themselves for a door. Is that so monstrous?"

"Yes," Orsen said. "Because you'd take it from a child."

"We would take it from a child who cannot consent either way, to save realms that cannot save themselves. Someone always pays, Coldharbour. The only question history ever asks is whether the paying is honest." She stood, smoothing her grey cloak, and at the door she paused. "The boy arrives within the hour. When he does, you will have a choice to make—the same choice, in fact, that you made twenty-three years ago in that undercroft. Refuse again, and watch. Or find a third path, if your conscience can build one faster than my order can take the gate."

The door closed. The lamp guttered. In the basin, the salt-water had gone perfectly still, and Orsen caught her own reflection in it—gaunt, bleeding at the wrists, twenty years older than the woman in the undercroft and no wiser, she thought, no wiser at all—

Except.

Except that the memory had shown her one more thing, at the very end, something she had never sealed away because she had never known it was there: Ishild's face in the last instant before the water took her. Not bitterness. Not resolve.

*Terror.* And beneath the terror, moving her lips in the old silent speech that students learned before they learned words, a message meant for no one—for the dark, for the future, for whoever lived to read it:

*Forgive me. Find another way.*

Orsen stared at the still water, and the shaking started in her hands and would not stop, and above her, muffled through the smoked glass and the living rock, she heard the fortress gates grind open, and horses, and voices—and one voice among them, high and wordless with fear, that she would have known at the bottom of the sea.

Isandro. They had brought him to Hollowmere after all.

Orsen pulled at the oath-forged iron until her blood ran, and the chair held, and somewhere down the corridor a boy who spoke the language of the gates began, very softly, to cry.
