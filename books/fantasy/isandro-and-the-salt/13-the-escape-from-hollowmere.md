## 13. The Escape from Hollowmere

They kept her in a room without corners.

Orsen understood the design the moment the blindfold came off and she saw it: a chamber built as an oval, walls of pale salt-veined stone curved so smoothly that no edge existed for a sword-oath to catch. The Quiet Blade had learned, over two centuries of butchering swordspeakers, that power needed geometry. A corner to stand in. An angle to speak against. Take those away and even an oath-bound blade was only a woman in a bowl.

She had been in the room for what she judged to be two days, judging by the rhythm of the lamp-oil being refreshed through the slot in the door. Two days of hearing Isandro's voice—his *voice*, that terrible beautiful instrument—echoing down corridors somewhere above her, calling and calling and getting no answer, because they would not let him near her. Two days of hearing him wear himself hoarse against stone that did not care.

They were not torturing him. That was the thing she had to keep reminding herself, pressing it flat like a flower between pages. The harvesters never tortured children. Torture bruised the instrument. They simply isolated him, fed him well, spoke to him gently in a language he could not understand, and waited for the loneliness to do what cruelty could not: make him reach for the only tool he had ever owned. His voice. They wanted him to *speak* into their measuring bowls, their salt-glass resonators, their careful instruments of harvest—and every time he cried out for her in the First Tongue, she knew, somewhere above her a device drank the sound and recorded its depth.

*He is spending himself,* she thought, sitting with her back against the curved wall, her knees drawn up. *They are letting him spend himself coin by coin, and he thinks if he calls loudly enough I will come.*

She had tried everything. She had spoken oaths at the walls until her throat bled and the oath-mark on her collarbone burned black—the curved stone swallowed the words whole, gave back nothing but a dull hum, like a bell wrapped in wool. She had searched the oval for flaws with her fingertips for hours, found none. She had slept, because refusing sleep was just another way of arriving at the harvest weaker than they expected.

On the third day, the door opened, and her old mentor came in wearing the face of a kind grandfather, which was the most frightening thing about Magister Corvane: he had always been able to wear kindness like a borrowed coat and mean none of it.

"You're eating," he observed, pleased. "Good. Grief goes easier on a full stomach."

"Where is he?"

"With the acolytes. Learning his letters." Corvane lowered himself onto the stool across from her with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. He was older than she remembered, thinner, but his eyes were the same—pale, patient, absolutely certain. "You look terrible, Orsen."

"Twenty years in the mountains will do that."

"Twenty years hiding." He folded his hands. "I want to show you something before we go further. Not an argument. A demonstration. You always respected demonstrations more than arguments."

"I respected nothing about you after Aldmere's fall."

"After Aldmere's fall you stopped respecting anyone, including yourself." He said it without heat, and that was the worst of it; there was no cruelty in Corvane, only arithmetic. "The demonstration is this: come and watch him for one hour. One hour, behind glass, touching nothing. Watch what happens when they test his resonance. Then tell me the covenant is worth what it costs him."

Orsen said nothing. But she stood when he stood, because an hour within sight of Isandro was worth any trap.

* * *

The testing chamber was a cathedral of salt-glass, and Isandro stood in the middle of it like a candle flame in a windless room.

Orsen watched from behind a partition of smoked crystal, and what she watched broke something loose in her chest that had been rusted shut for twenty years.

They had given him a bowl of river water. That was all. An acolyte—a girl no older than sixteen, gentle-faced, murmuring encouragement in common speech—tapped the bowl's rim and asked him, in gestures and pictures drawn on slate, to say the word for water. Just the word. And Isandro, who had learned in three days that cooperation bought him small mercies, leaned over the bowl and said it.

The word was not water. It was the First Tongue's name for water, the true name, the name that held rivers inside it the way a seed holds a tree—and the bowl did not ripple. The bowl *remembered being rain*. Water climbed out of it in a slow silver rope, hung in the air, shaped itself into a miniature of some river Isandro had once known—a village stream, Orsen realized, a stream with a specific bend and a specific willow, rendered in perfect detail from a child's memory—and then fell back and was still.

The acolytes wrote numbers. Somewhere a resonator chimed. And Isandro looked at his own hands as though they belonged to someone who had died.

That was what undid her. Not the power. The look. The look of a boy discovering that his grief had weight and volume and could be measured in other people's ledgers.

Corvane spoke softly beside her. "Every word he says in that tongue moves the covenant, Orsen. Do you understand? The gate beneath Thornfell drinks his voice and the wraith tides calm for a hundred miles around. Villages that have flooded every spring for generations had dry fields this year. Because a seven-year-old said a word." He let the silence sit. "Now imagine what a *managed* keystone could do. A lifetime of it. We could renew the covenant without another order ever dying for it. Without another *you* ever having to exist."

"And when the bowl runs dry?" Orsen asked. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "When you've spent him down to silence, the way your order spent mine? What does your arithmetic say then, Magister?"

"That we will have bought eighty good years with one childhood." He met her eyes. "I have made peace with worse sums. So have you—you just refuse to write yours down."

They took her back to the oval room. She sat against the curved wall in the lamplight and did not move for a long time.

Then she began, very quietly, to plan—not an escape, because she had been planning escapes since the first day and the oval permitted none—but a *choice*. There was a difference. Escapes used force, and force required geometry this room denied her. Choices required only a person willing to pay.

She had one coin left. She had been saving it since the day the wraith tide came to her door.

Her oath-mark. Twenty years sealed under salt and scar-tissue, the brand that bound her voice to her blade, still alive beneath her skin like a coal banked in ash. If she cut it free—if she opened the seal herself, unbound the last restraint her dying order had placed on her—she could speak one oath at full breadth. Not a sword-oath. A *structure*-oath. Enough to bring this fortress down around its own foundations.

And enough, probably, to bring her down with it.

She pressed her palm flat over the mark and felt it beat against her hand like a second heart, impatient, starving.

"Not yet," she told it. "Wait for him."

* * *

Isandro could not count days in Hollowmere, because the windows were painted glass and the light never changed. He counted instead in meals, and in the number of times the kind woman with the slate came to draw pictures at him, and in how many different ways he had tried to make them take him to the sword-woman.

They never took him. They took pieces of her instead: her boots, left outside his door one morning so he would know she lived. A strip of her grey cloak. Once, unbearably, a single grey hair tied in a knot, which he understood perfectly—they were telling him she was alive the way you keep a fire alive with one stick at a time, and they were also telling him they could stop whenever they wished.

So he stopped asking with his hands. He started asking with the other thing.

He knew it frightened them. He had known since the first night, when he cried in the white bed and the water in his washing basin had stood up and walked over the rim, and the guards had come running with salt in their fists. The tall ones with the quiet blades flinched when he spoke the old language. They flinched the way people flinch from weather.

So each night, lying in the white bed, he sent his voice down through the floor. Not words exactly. More like the feeling of words—the shape of *where is she*, the shape of *I am here, I am here, I am here*—pressed into the stone the way you press a leaf into soft clay. He didn't know where it went. He only knew that the stone listened. All stone listened; the mountain under his village had listened to him his whole life, and he had never known that was unusual until everyone who could have told him was gone.

Down through the floors. Past the cold rooms where the salt-glass sang. Down to wherever the sword-woman was kept.

*I am here,* he told the stone. *I am here. I am here.*

And far below, in a room without corners, a woman with her palm pressed over a branded oath-mark felt the floor hum against her spine, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, and began to weep without making any sound at all—because the child was not spending himself on their instruments after all. He was spending himself on *her*. Coin by coin, night by night, buying her location the only way he could.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. She took out the sliver of sharpened spoon-handle she had spent three days earning, by trading her rations bit by bit to the guard who brought the oil, a hollow-eyed young man who wanted anything sweet and did not ask questions.

"Now," she told the mark. "Now, now, now."

* * *

The seal came off like tearing a nail from the quick.

Orsen had done it kneeling, in the dark, working the sliver under the rim of scar-tissue where the oath-mark met her collarbone, and the pain was enormous and clean and *hers*, the first honest pain she had felt in twenty years, because every other hurt since the fall of her order had been the dull ache of concealment. She screamed into her own forearm. The mark came free in a curl of skin and light, and lay in her palm glowing like a live ember, and the room without corners—for the first time since they built it—had a direction.

Because an oath-mark, unsealed, is a compass needle made of voice. It pointed, faintly, toward the nearest thing in the world that spoke the First Tongue.

Toward Isandro.

She stood. She put her hand flat against the curved wall, and she did not try to break it. She *addressed* it. That was the lesson twenty years of silence had taught her and no harvester had ever learned: power does not need a corner. It needs a listener. Stone listens. She had forgotten that, hiding in the mountains, talking to no one but a hearth—and here was a fortress built entirely of listening stone, salt-veined, old, quarried from the same bones as the drowned gate itself.

"Older than salt," she whispered, in the First Tongue, in the words Isandro had taught her in the shepherd's bothy, the fragments of grammar a child gives a slow student. "Older than my order. Older than their knives. Hear me."

The wall warmed under her hand.

She spoke the structure-oath then—one oath, full-breadth, the kind that had killed better speakers than her—and gave it a single instruction: *open*. Not destroy. Open. The oval room unfolded like a fist remembering it had fingers. Walls slid, split, sighed apart along seams of salt that had been waiting two hundred years for someone to ask politely. Dust fell like snow. Somewhere above, alarms began to ring, and Orsen Coldharbour walked out of the impossible room into a corridor of shouting and lamplight, with an ember of light cupped in her bleeding hand, following it upward like a pilgrim following a star.

* * *

What happened next, the harvesters' own records would later describe as a structural failure of unprecedented scope, and Vess—who heard the account secondhand and laughed until she cried—would describe more accurately as "the mountain deciding it had opinions."

Isandro felt her coming before he saw her. Everyone in Hollowmere felt her coming. The whole fortress felt it: a rising tone in the stones, a deep hum like a struck bell that would not fade, and the boy in the white bed sat up with his heart hammering because he knew that hum. It was the sound the floor made when his night-words went down. It was the sound of an answer.

The door of his room burst inward off its hinges—stone hinges, torn loose, not broken—and there she stood in a haze of dust and lamplight, grey-cloaked, blood on her collarbone, holding a scrap of burning light in her bare hand, and her face when she saw him was not the guarded face he had lived beside for a season. It was open all the way down.

"Isandro."

He crossed the room faster than thought and hit her hard enough to stagger her, arms locked around her ribs, face buried in the rough wool that smelled of woodsmoke and road-dust and *her*, and he was crying, great silent heaving sobs, because that was the other thing about Isandro—he had arrived mute and the muteness had never been in his throat. It had been in his grief. Sound cost him. Every word in the old tongue cost him something out of the place where his village used to be.

But the alarm bells were ringing and boots were pounding in the corridors and the tall ones with the quiet blades were coming, and Orsen knelt, gripped his shoulders, and looked at him eye to eye—not down at him, the way she had finally learned to, somewhere in the last weeks without knowing she was learning it—and said, in common speech, slowly, so he could follow:

"We have to run. And the doors are going to be in our way. And I can't open them all before they reach us." She swallowed. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry to ask this. But I need your voice, and it isn't fair, and I'm asking anyway."

Not an order. Not a use. A request, from one person to another, and Isandro looked at her for a long moment with tears cutting through the dust on his face.

Then he turned toward the corridor, filled his lungs, and grieved.

* * *

It was not a battle cry. No one who heard it ever mistook it for one. It was the sound of a child's loss given its true size at last—the village, the willow, the stream with the bend in it, the mother whose name he still said to himself at night in the old language because saying it in the new one felt like leaving her behind—all of it pulled up out of him in one long note that was somehow every note at once.

Hollowmere's eastern range did not collapse. It *sat down*. Walls folded along their salt seams with a groan like a whale surfacing, ceilings poured themselves into the corridors as smooth flows of rubble, and the harvester strongpoint that had stood nine hundred years became, in the space of eleven seconds, a hill with rooms in it. The pursuing guards were not crushed—the mountain, Orsen would swear forever after, was careful, the way Isandro was careful, the way grieving things are careful with whatever else still lives—but they were buried to the waist, to the shoulders, stopped dead, cursing, digging.

Orsen ran through falling dust with the boy's hand locked in hers, and the way out was lit by her own unsealed oath-mark burning in her fist, and she was laughing, actually laughing, wild and ragged, because terror and joy had gotten tangled past separating.

They went down. Not out—down. She had seen the maps during her hour behind the glass, Corvane's own maps, spread smugly on his table: Hollowmere was built atop the deep roads, the smugglers' tunnels that ran south under the mountains toward the drowned gate, and the harvesters had sealed every entrance they knew. But the mountain had just shown her entrances no map held. Where Isandro's grief had cracked the fortress, it had also cracked the floor of the world beneath it, and a stair of ancient worked stone showed through the rubble, breathing cold air that smelled of deep water and old salt.

"The deep roads," she breathed. "Straight to the gate. They'll never expect us to go *toward* it."

Isandro tugged her sleeve. He pointed down the stair, then at her, then pressed his fist to his chest—the gesture he used for *yes, I choose, me too*—and Orsen looked at this small, spent, extraordinary person who had just unmade a fortress to answer her call, and she knelt one more time, in the dust, and did the thing she should have done in chapter upon chapter past.

She held out her hand. Not took his. Offered it, palm up, empty.

"Partner," she said. In the First Tongue, clumsily, using his own grammar, the word he'd taught her that meant not follower, not charge, not ward: the word that meant *one who walks with*.

Isandro looked at her open hand for a moment. Then he placed his small fingers in it, and answered in the old tongue, clear as a bell over the settling dust—the first voluntary word he had spoken since the fortress came down, and it cost him, and he paid it gladly:

*Together.*

They descended into the dark together, the oath-light swinging between them, while behind and above them Hollowmere groaned and settled like an old man finding a new chair, and somewhere in its buried halls Magister Corvane stood waist-deep in the ruin of his life's work, listening to the sound of footsteps going down toward the gate, and began, calmly, to give orders.

The deep roads were long, and dark, and older than either realm that claimed the mountains above them. And three days south, at the first junction where the tunnels forked toward the drowned coast, Orsen's oath-light flickered—and caught, reflected in the wet rock ahead, the glint of a lantern, and the silhouette of a boat, and a familiar one-eyed figure leaning against the tunnel wall with her arms crossed, waiting.

"Well," said Vess, into the dark. "Took you long enough."
