## 14. The Last Oath-Speaker's Doubt

The drowned gate did not look like a gate.

It looked like a wound in the mountain's throat—a crescent of black stone set into the cliff face above the sunken terraces of Aldmere, half-swallowed by centuries of meltwater and salt-bloom. Water wept down its arch in slow silver threads, each drop striking a basin carved long before Orsen's grandmother's grandmother was born, and the sound it made was not water-sound at all but something closer to breathing. In. Out. In. The covenant, starving, still breathing.

Orsen stood at the threshold with her sword across her back and twenty years of silence sitting on her chest like a stone, and she could not make herself take the last ten steps.

Behind her, Vess crouched over their small hoard of supplies, feeding strips of dried fish to the little iron brazier they'd carried down from the shepherd's bothy. The firelight turned her one eye amber and left the other—the empty socket, the leather patch Vess wore without shame—lost in shadow. Isandro sat between them, knees drawn up, watching the gate with the unblinking attention he gave to everything that spoke in the First Tongue. And the gate spoke. Orsen could feel it now, this close: a low murmur beneath the dripping, like a congregation murmuring prayers they no longer remembered the words to.

She had imagined this moment for two decades. She had rehearsed it in the dark of the waystation, on the smugglers' roads, in the burning wreck of Vess's ferry's aftermath—what she would do when she finally stood here. Draw the sword. Speak the oath. Pay whatever price the old texts demanded and let the realms breathe again.

She had never once imagined standing here unable to move.

"You're doing the thing," Vess said, without looking up from the brazier.

"What thing."

"The thing where your face goes all shutters-and-locks." Vess fed another strip to the coals. "I've hauled cargo past customs officers for thirty years, Coldharbour. I know a woman deciding whether to declare something or throw it in the sea."

Orsen opened her mouth to deny it and found nothing there worth saying. That was the trouble with Vess. She had a smuggler's ear for omissions.

Isandro looked up at her. He didn't speak—he rarely did unless the world pressed words out of him—but his eyes went from her face to the gate and back again, and in his gaze was a question so patient and so terrible that Orsen felt it land in the hollow place beneath her ribs where she kept the worst of herself.

*Tell them,* said the hollow place. *Or don't. But stop pretending the stone isn't there.*

The wind came up the terraces from the drowned city below, carrying the smell of deep water and old salt, and Orsen heard—in it or in memory, she couldn't tell—the voices of the dead. Her order. The Swordspeakers of the Pale Vigil, forty-one strong on the last morning, singing the renewal-oath as the harvesters' blades came for them, because an oath interrupted is worse than an oath never begun, and they had chosen to finish it dying rather than leave the covenant unfed.

She had been the forty-second. She had run.

"I need to tell you something," Orsen said.

Vess's hands went still over the brazier. "Aye."

"Not the version I've been telling you. Not the version where I'm the last loyal one, the keeper, the poor bastard who got left holding the covenant." Orsen's voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "The true one."

* * *

She told it facing the gate, because she could not tell it facing them.

"The renewal-oath requires a speaker," she said. "One speaker, standing at the threshold, giving voice to the covenant's hunger. That's what I was trained for. That's what all of us were trained for. We were raised from children to be vessels—to memorize the First Tongue until it lived in our bones, so that when our turn came, the oath would pour out of us like blood from a cut."

The water struck the basin. In. Out.

"Twenty-two years ago, my turn came early. The speaker before me—Master Ellery Vane, who taught me the grammar of grief before I knew any other kind—she aged into the threshold faster than expected. The oath burns through its speakers. It always has. She had maybe a season left, and the covenant was already thin; you can see it in the records, the wraith-tides coming more often, the salt creeping further up the rivers every year. So the order gathered at Aldmere to pass the voice to the next in line." Orsen's hand found the hilt of her sword—not to draw it, only to hold something. "Me."

"And you refused." Vess's voice was quiet. Not gentle. Vess did not do gentle. But quiet, the way a person is quiet near a wounded animal.

"I refused." The word tasted like the waystation's cold. "Not out of cowardice. That's what I've told myself for twenty years, and I need to say the whole ugly truth tonight, so hear it: I told myself it was conscience. Because I'd read the deep inscriptions by then, the ones in the undercrofts, and I understood what the renewal actually was. It wasn't sacrifice. It wasn't duty. It was—" Her breath caught. "They fed the gate a person. A whole person. Everything the speaker was—their memories, their loves, their name, the shape of their soul—all of it poured into the covenant to keep the seal fed. Master Vane didn't die at the threshold. She was *eaten*. Slowly, over years, and what walked away afterward was a smiling shell who remembered how to bake bread and nothing else. I watched it happen. I watched them call it holy."

Isandro made a small sound. Not a word. A recognition. Orsen glanced back at him—at the child whose village had been harvested, whose people had been rendered down into fuel by men who called it salvation—and saw him listening with his whole body, the way he listened to the gate.

"So I ran," she said. "And three weeks later the harvesters came for the rest of them, and forty-one swordspeakers died at that threshold singing an oath that would never be finished, and the covenant has been starving ever since, and every death since—the wraith-tides, the villages, your village, Isandro—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and let it crack anyway. "Every death since is downstream of the day I chose my own wholeness over the world's. That's the truth. I didn't refuse out of conscience. Or maybe I did, and conscience doesn't absolve anything, because either way I hid. Twenty years in a waystation guarding a door I never intended to open, telling myself I was keeping the faith when really I was just keeping myself."

The silence after was long. The gate breathed. Somewhere far below, in the drowned streets of Aldmere, ice shifted against stone.

* * *

Vess stood. Orsen braced for fury—for the accusation she deserved, hurled in that flat ferrywoman's drawl—and instead Vess walked over and sat down heavily on the stone beside Isandro, close enough that the boy leaned into her shoulder without thinking about it, the way he'd learned to do over these weeks on the road. Found-family habits, grown fast as weeds.

"Here's what I know about you, Coldharbour," Vess said. "I know you pulled me out of the Siltfen with a rope around your waist and a broken rib, for a debt I owed you that wasn't worth a rope and a rib. I know you taught a mute boy to read common speech by candlelight, night after night, when you could've dumped him at the first shrine and gone back to your mountains. I know you've slept four hours a night since Hollowmere so the rest of us could sleep six." She ticked them off on scarred fingers. "That's the ledger I keep. Now you're telling me there's an older page, and it's ugly. Fine. Ledgers have ugly pages. Anyone who tells you different has never done honest trade."

"It's not a debt, Vess. It's not a matter of balancing—"

"No, it's not." Vess's single eye fixed on her, sharp as a hook. "It's a matter of what you do next. You want my forgiveness? Here it is, free of charge, because I'm not in the business of charging for things people can't pay: you were young, you were right about what the oath cost, and you were wrong about what hiding would buy. All three can be true at once. I spent fifteen years not speaking to my brother because pride is cheaper than apology, and I'll tell you what I learned standing across a river from him with knives drawn—" her jaw tightened, the wound of Toll still fresh— "*hiding is a choice too.* Every year you didn't come down off that mountain, you chose. Same as choosing. Just easier to dress up as waiting."

The words went in like a blade going through mail—not deep enough to kill, deep enough to be felt for a lifetime.

"But here's the other thing," Vess went on, softer now, which from Vess was louder than shouting. "You came down. When it mattered—when the boy needed taking to the gate—you came down. Whatever you were running from, you stopped running. So don't stand there telling us you're a coward. Cowards don't walk into the dark with a child and a ferrywoman and a sword they swore off twenty years ago."

Orsen looked at her. At this woman who had burned her boat, burned her brother, burned her whole old life on a causeway two days ago, and sat here now offering forgiveness like it weighed nothing.

"Why?" Orsen asked, and her voice was smaller than she wanted it to be. "Why is it that easy for you?"

"It's not easy." Vess snorted. "It's the hardest thing I do, forgiving people. Including myself. But I learned it from watching you mope around campfires for a month, if you want the truth of it. You forgave me my smuggling, my debts, my rotten temper, before I'd earned any of it. Seemed rude not to return the favor." She shrugged. "Besides. The boy forgave you before you even started talking."

Orsen turned to Isandro.

He had risen while Vess spoke—risen the way he sometimes did, silently, suddenly, drawn by something the rest of them couldn't hear. He stood between Orsen and the gate now, small against the vast black arch, and the drip-water silvered his hair, and he was looking at her with an expression she had no common word for and only one First Tongue phrase for, one he'd taught her in the bothy, high above the snowline:

*Aen sereveth il.* It means: *I see the whole of you, and I stay.*

"You understand what I did," Orsen said to him. "What I didn't do. Your village. Your mother and father. If someone had renewed the covenant—if I had—"

He crossed the space between them and took her hand. His fingers were cold from the gate-draft, and they were certain. He pressed her palm flat against his own chest, over his heart, and held it there, and Orsen felt the faint vibration in him—that hum he carried, the keystone's resonance, the living note of the covenant itself—and understood that he was showing her something. Not forgiving her in words he didn't have. Showing her.

The covenant was not angry with her.

The covenant was hungry, and patient, and it had sent a child to her door through a blizzard because some thread of the old magic, some starving instinct, had known what she would not admit: that she was the one. Not because she was pure or brave or faithful. Because she was the one who understood the price. Only a speaker who knew exactly what the gate eats could choose it with open eyes—or find another way.

"Hiding was its own betrayal," Orsen said aloud, and the words fell into the gate's breathing like coins into a well. "I see it now. I kept myself whole and called it virtue, and the world starved for it. Whatever happens at this threshold—I won't hide anymore."

Isandro smiled. Small, tired, real.

And then his smile changed. Flickered. His hand tightened on hers—not in comfort now, in warning—and his eyes went wide, and the hum in his chest shifted key, rising toward something urgent.

He pulled free of her and stumbled toward the gate's black arch, and pressed both palms against the stone, and Orsen saw the frost of his breath quicken, saw the drip-water shiver in its fall, saw the ancient carvings along the arch begin—faintly, terribly—to glow.

"What is it?" Vess was on her feet, hand on her knife. "What's it saying?"

Isandro turned back to them. His lips moved, shaping common speech with the careful effort he still gave it, and what came out was barely a whisper, and it was the most frightening thing Orsen had heard in twenty years:

"There's a part the stones don't say plain. About the price. I only just—it's not one speaker anymore. It's—" He swallowed. "It's worse than you think, Orsen. And I think it's supposed to be me."

The gate breathed. The light in the carvings brightened, patient as tide.

And down in the drowned streets of Aldmere, far below, something that had been still for two days began, softly, to sing.
