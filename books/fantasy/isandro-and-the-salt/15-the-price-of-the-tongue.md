## 15. The Price of the Tongue

The bothy above the shepherd's pass had a roof that leaked starlight.

Isandro lay awake beneath it, watching the cold points of it come through the split shingles the way water came through the hull of a boat—except these were going the wrong direction, upward, back to where they belonged. He liked that. Things returning to where they belonged. It was one of the few ideas the common tongue had given him that felt true all the way down.

Beside him, Orsen slept badly. He could tell without looking. Her breathing had a shape he had learned over the long weeks of roads and riverbanks and tunnels—the shape it made when her dreams were made of salt. When she dreamed of the harvest, though she did not know yet that he knew what she dreamed of. Vess snored on the far side of the hearth like something contented and enormous, one hand curled around the hilt of her knife even in sleep, and Isandro found that sound comforting in a way he could not have explained in either language. It was the sound of someone who had decided to stay.

He should have been sleeping too. Tomorrow—or the day after, if the weather turned against them—they would reach the drowned gate. He could feel it already, the way you feel a storm coming through your teeth. The First Tongue lived in him like a second heartbeat, and that heartbeat was quickening, pulling southward, downward, toward the place where the covenant starved.

And he knew what it would ask.

That was why he was not asleep. That was why he had risen, silent as snowfall, from his bedroll, and taken the charcoal stub and the flat piece of slate he'd been carrying since Aldmere, and knelt by the dead hearth where the last coals still breathed their faint orange ghosts.

Because there were things the inscriptions never said plainly. And he had read them all.

* * *

In Aldmere, the drowned city, the walls had spoken to him.

Not aloud. Nothing in the First Tongue ever spoke aloud unless a living voice gave it breath—that was the whole nature of it, the thing Orsen kept circling around without landing on, the thing the harvesters had built their whole cruel order upon. Words older than salt were not sounds. They were *bindings*. A word in the First Tongue was a knot tied between two things, and once tied, it held until something paid for its untying.

The inscriptions in Aldmere had been knots. Thousands of them, layered over centuries, and most had rotted or been cut away, but enough remained for Isandro to read the city the way Vess read water: by what moved beneath the surface.

*Here stood the hall of speakers,* said one wall, in characters like frozen lightning. *Here they gathered to feed the gate.*

Orsen had translated that one for Vess in her rough, halting way—she had only fragments, broken training, a language learned secondhand and half-forgotten—and her voice had gone strange when she said *feed*. Isandro had watched her fingers tremble on the wet stone. She thought *feed* meant sacrifice. She thought it meant blood, bodies, the murder her order had died refusing to commit. He had seen the thought move through her like a fish under ice, dark and swift, and he had said nothing, because he had not yet understood enough himself to say it truly.

Now he understood.

He knelt at the hearth and began to write.

Not in the common letters Vess had been teaching him—those crooked, practical marks, good for tallying cargo and naming towns. He wrote in the old script, the one that came out of his hand the way tears come out of eyes, without decision. And as he wrote, he whispered, because some knots needed breath even to be recorded, and the whisper was so soft that neither sleeper stirred.

*What is given to the gate is memory.*

The charcoal moved. The slate filled. He wrote the structure of it the way he had seen it written in the deepest chamber of Aldmere, the chamber below the waterline where the drowned priests had kept their records dry behind glass and prayer: the grammar of renewal, laid out plain.

An oath-speaker's voice was not a talent. It was a treasury. Every binding ever spoken drew its strength from the speaker's own past—from the memories that had shaped them into someone whose word could hold weight. A woman who had never lost anything could not swear an oath worth anything; grief was the coin, love was the mint, and memory was the metal itself. That was why the harvesters took children. Not for innocence. For *density*—a life unlived but intensely felt, a keystone child who carried the whole covenant's weight in a small body, whose every memory was compounded with interest.

And renewal—the great renewal, the re-knotting of the covenant that would still the wraith tide and close the starving gate—required a speaker to pour the whole treasury out. Every memory that fueled the voice. Given to the water. Gone.

Not death. Worse than death, maybe, or better—it depended on what you believed a person was. The gate did not kill what it took. It *kept* it. Somewhere in the deep places, Isandro suspected, everything the oath-speakers of old had surrendered was still there, preserved in salt, the way the city of Aldmere was preserved: perfectly, terribly, forever out of reach.

That was the price the inscriptions never said plainly. They said *the gate must be fed.* They said *the voice must be poured out.* They never said: *what is poured out is you.*

Isandro looked down at the slate, at the lightning-frozen characters marching across it, and felt the truth of it settle into his chest like a stone dropped into a well. He had known since Aldmere. He had known, probably, since the night the wraiths came to Orsen's door and she had spoken her first sword-oath in twenty years and he had felt, through the floor, through the stone, through the roots of the mountain, the small terrible cost of it—a thread of her past thinning, a color going out of her somewhere he couldn't see. She didn't know. She thought she had gotten away cheap. She hadn't. Each oath she spoke spent her, and she had been spending herself for weeks to keep him safe, and she would keep spending until there was nothing left, because that was who she was.

Which meant he could not let her speak the great oath.

Which meant—

His hand stopped. The charcoal hovered. Below the last line he had written, there was room for one more sentence, and he knew exactly what it would say, and his body resisted writing it the way a drowning man resists opening his mouth.

But he had watched Vess burn her brother's letter without flinching. He had watched Orsen confess her oldest shame with her chin up and her hands shaking. The people he loved kept teaching him the same lesson, over and over, in different languages: *the truth is the toll, and you pay it, and then you walk on.*

He wrote it.

*I will pay instead.*

* * *

He did not have to wait for morning. He never learned whether that was luck or whether Orsen had been awake the whole time, listening to the whisper of charcoal on slate the way she listened to everything now—like a woman standing guard over the last fire in the world.

Her hand closed on his shoulder, gentle, and he startled so badly he nearly snapped the slate.

"Little one." Her voice was rough with sleep and something else. "What are you—"

She saw the slate. He watched her see it. Watched her eyes move across the old script—he could read her reading, the way her lips parted slightly, the way her brow drew down, the way her breath went shallow and stopped altogether at the first line.

*What is given to the gate is memory.*

"Oh," she said. Very quietly. The word fell out of her like something dropped from a great height.

She took the slate from him with both hands, careful, the way you take a wounded bird, and she read the rest kneeling there in the ember-light, and Isandro watched twenty years of hiding rearrange themselves behind her face. He watched her understand, line by line, what she had almost done. What she had been walking toward for the entire length of the salt road, with Vess's knife and her own sword and her stubborn, magnificent, doomed courage: the gate, the threshold, the pouring-out. She had known it would cost. He could see her searching her memory for what she'd thought the cost would be—blood, maybe. Life, maybe. Things a swordspeaker knew how to pay.

Not this. Never this.

"The memories," she said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used when the other voice would break. "All of them. Everything that fuels the voice."

He nodded.

"And you've known this since—" She stopped. Started again. "Since Aldmere."

He nodded again, and then, because nodding was a coward's answer and she deserved better, he reached for the slate and wrote beneath his last line, slowly, so she could watch each character form:

*I will pay instead. I am the keystone. My memories are the strongest. It will be enough.*

Orsen went very still.

It was a stillness Isandro had felt before, in the moment before she drew steel, in the moment before she lied to the harvester captain with a straight face and a dead man's calm. But this stillness was different. This stillness had grief in it, and underneath the grief, something molten.

"No," she said.

He had expected the word. He had prepared arguments for the word. He picked up the charcoal.

She caught his wrist. Not hard. Just stopping him, her callused fingers circling his small bones, and her eyes were bright and terrible in the dying light of the coals.

"No," she said again, and now the flatness cracked and everything underneath came through. "Listen to me. Listen—I know you can understand me, don't you look away from me—no. You are a *child*. You are—" Her breath hitched. "You've lost everything already. Your village. Your mother. Your tongue, your home, your whole world, harvested out from under you before you were old enough to grieve it properly. And you want to give me the rest? You want to walk into that water and pour out whatever's left of you so that I don't have to lose my—"

She stopped. Because saying it made it real, and Isandro watched her realize, in real time, what she was about to admit: that she had decided to pay. That somewhere between Hollowmere and here, between the confession and the forgiveness, between Vess's blunt *you hid because it was easier and it nearly killed everyone we love* and Isandro's simple, unguarded trust, she had chosen. She was going to speak the oath. She was going to give the gate everything that made her Orsen Coldharbour, the last swordspeaker, and she had not told them because telling them would have made them try to stop her, and she had not wanted to be stopped.

"You were going to do it yourself," Isandro wrote on the slate, because his hands needed to do something or they would shake. "Weren't you."

Orsen looked at the words. Looked at him. And for the first time since he had arrived frost-bitten at her door in the drift, the celebrated swordspeaker, the last oath-bound blade of a murdered order, the woman who had stared down wraiths and harvesters and her own buried grief—looked away first.

"Yes," she said.

* * *

They woke Vess.

Later, Isandro would understand why—because secrets were how the harvesters had won, secrets were how orders died, secrets were the salt that preserved a person right up until the person rotted from the inside. No more secrets. That was the law of the last three days, and Orsen honored it even now, shaking Vess awake by the shoulder while the ferrywoman came up swearing and reaching for her knife.

"What in the nine briny hells—"

"Read this," Orsen said, and handed her the slate.

Vess read. Isandro watched her face do what faces did when they met the First Tongue unprepared: confusion, then focus, then a kind of seasick dawning. She couldn't read the script. But Orsen read it aloud to her, translating into the crooked practical common tongue, each line landing like a stone in still water, and by the end Vess was sitting very straight with her jaw set and her one eye fixed on Isandro with an expression he had no name for.

"So let me get this straight," Vess said finally. "To fix the gate. To stop the wraiths. To end all this." She gestured vaguely at the world, at the road behind them, at the burned boat and the drowned city and the dead. "Somebody's got to forget everything. Their whole self. Everything that makes 'em them."

"That's what it says," Orsen said.

"And our keystone here"—Vess's voice gentled in a way that always surprised people who didn't know her—"wants to be the somebody."

"He's a child," Orsen said.

"I can see he's a child, Orsen. I ferried children for thirty years." Vess rubbed her eye. "I also see he's the bravest soul on this mountain, and I hate it, and I hate that he's offering, and I am not for one moment agreeing with him. But I'm not going to pretend he didn't offer, because he'll know, and he'll just do it quieter next time." She looked at Isandro. "Ain't that right?"

Isandro nodded. Then he took up the slate one more time, and wrote, and turned it toward them both:

*If Orsen pays, the realms lose their last sword. If I pay, they lose a boy who was already lost. It is better arithmetic.*

"Arithmetic," Vess repeated, disgusted. "Who taught you arithmetic?"

"You did," Isandro wrote. "Tallying cargo."

"Well, your figures are wrong, boy, because cargo isn't people, and I've told you a hundred times—" Vess stopped. Her voice had gone thick. She got up abruptly and put wood on the dead fire, her back to them both, and the sparks climbed toward the leaky roof and out through the gaps into the dark.

Orsen knelt in front of Isandro. She took the slate gently out of his hands and set it aside, and then she took his face in both her scarred hands, the way his mother used to, and the resemblance struck him so hard his eyes stung.

"Listen to me," she said. "I spent twenty years believing my order's sacrifice was murder. I refused to renew the covenant because I would not be part of feeding children to a gate. That refusal cost everything—my order, my name, my whole life—and I would pay it again. Do you understand? If I let you do this—if I stand at that gate and let a child pour himself out to save me—then everything I believed was a lie. Then I was just a coward after all, and the harvesters were right, and there is no difference between us and them except that they're honest about what they take."

Isandro's throat ached. He pulled her hands away from his face—not pushing her off, never that, just holding them—and pressed his palm flat against his own chest, and then, carefully, against hers.

Then he wrote, one-handed, slow:

*Your memories are not only yours anymore. You gave some to me when you taught me. Vess gave some when she fed me. I gave you the First Tongue. We are not one person paying. We are three people who are partly each other.*

Orsen read it. Read it again. And Isandro saw the idea land in her—not as surrender, not yet, but as a crack in the wall, light coming through.

"That's not how the gate works," she said softly. "The oath takes one speaker. One treasury. It doesn't—"

But her voice trailed off, because she was remembering too. The inscription in Aldmere that Isandro had shown her, the one the drowned priests had carved above the innermost door, the one she had translated with frowning doubt: *the gate was never sealed by one voice, nor shall it be opened by one.* She had thought it poetry. So had he.

Outside, far down the southern slope, where the air tasted of salt and old drowning, something answered them.

Not a word. Not a wraith-cry. Something worse and stranger: a silence, vast and attentive, the silence of a starving thing turning its attention toward the smell of food. The coals guttered. The starlight through the leaking roof dimmed as cloud rolled over it, and beneath their feet, through the bones of the mountain, Isandro felt the drowned gate shift in its sleep like a patient waking.

Vess was at the window, knife drawn, her eye narrowed at the dark. "Storm's coming in off the lowlands," she said. "Salt wind. We've got maybe a day. Maybe less."

Orsen rose. She picked up the slate, and Isandro watched her thumb move over his last written words—*we are three people who are partly each other*—and he watched her make a decision, though he could not yet read what it was. Her face had closed into the swordspeaker's calm, but underneath it, for the first time since he'd known her, something was moving that wasn't dread.

"One day," she said. "Then we go down together. All three of us. And nobody pays anything until we've asked the gate one question nobody's thought to ask it in two hundred years."

Isandro tilted his head. *What question?*

Orsen smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman about to commit heresy against everything she'd ever been taught.

"Whether it's ever actually demanded one voice," she said, "or whether we just assumed—because one voice was cheaper than many."
