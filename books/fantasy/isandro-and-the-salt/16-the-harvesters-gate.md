## 16. The Harvesters' Gate

The drowned gate did not look like a gate. That was the first lie of it.

Orsen had imagined doors her whole life—oak and iron, hinges and bolts, something with a seam to break. What lay at the bottom of the causeway was nothing so honest. It was a wound in the world: a circle of black water perhaps forty paces across, ringed by stones that had been standing when the mountains were young, each one carved in the First Tongue with lines worn shallow by two centuries of rain and neglect. The water did not ripple. It did not reflect the grey sky above them or the torchlight Vess had kindled on the shore. It reflected nothing at all, and Orsen found that looking at it too long made her teeth ache, as though she were biting down on a word she could not finish.

Isandro stood at the ring's edge with his small hands pressed flat against the nearest stone, and where his palms touched, the carvings glowed—a faint, salt-white luminescence, like moonlight caught under ice.

*It's listening,* he signed, then remembered she still fumbled half his signs, and spoke instead in that voice that was not quite a child's voice, that carried the weight of older throats behind it. "It's listening. It's been listening for so long it forgot how to hear."

"Then we'll remind it," Orsen said.

She had spent the climb down from Hollowmere rehearsing this moment—the threshold, the oath, the three voices Vess had sworn to lend her—and now that she stood before it, all her rehearsal felt like a child stacking pebbles against a tide. The covenant was starving. She had known that since the wraiths came. But knowing it here, at the source, was different. The air itself tasted thin, rationed, as if the whole mountain range were breathing through a keyhole. Somewhere beneath the black water, something vast turned over in its sleep and did not wake.

Vess crouched at the causeway's narrow waist, checking the line of caltrops she'd scattered with a ferrywoman's economy—not enough to stop an army, but enough to make an army rude about its approach. Her remaining eye kept flicking south along the road they'd come by, toward the switchbacks below the ruin of Aldmere.

"They'll be here by dark," she said. "Maybe sooner. My brother doesn't march slow when he smells a prize."

"He's not your brother anymore."

"No," Vess agreed, and there was no grief in it, only a flatness like a blade laid on a table. "He's their captain now. Which is worse for him."

Orsen turned back to Isandro. He had not moved from the stone. The glow had spread from his palms up both forearms, tracing veins of light beneath the skin, and his breathing had gone slow and deep, the way he breathed when the First Tongue rose in him unbidden. She knelt beside him and put her hand over his, and the light flared gently at the contact, warm as a hearthstone.

"Isandro. Look at me."

His eyes came up. They were too old again, those eyes, and it frightened her more than any army. A boy of nine winters should not look at her as though he had watched empires drown.

"When they come," she said, "you do not speak. Not one word. Not in their tongue, not in yours. Whatever you feel, whatever you see—you hold it, and you give it to me after. Can you do that?"

He considered her with that terrible patience. Then he lifted one hand from the stone and touched her oath-mark—the faded brand at her throat, cold and dead these twenty years—and though he made no sound, she heard the question anyway, the way she had learned to hear everything he didn't say:

*What if holding it is what breaks me?*

"I know," she said quietly. "I know it hurts. I'm sorry. But if you speak while they're close—if you grieve while they're close—they will use it. They will use *you*. So you hold it, and I will hold you, and when this is done I will listen to every word you've saved until my ears bleed. Do you understand me?"

A long moment. Then he nodded once, withdrew his hand, and stepped back from the ring, folding himself small against the innermost stone with his knees drawn up—the posture of a child making himself a hiding place out of his own body. The glow dimmed but did not die. It never fully died around him anymore. He was a lantern now, and no amount of shuttering would make him dark.

Vess came and stood at her shoulder. "You've got the face on," she observed.

"What face?"

"The one you get before you do something stupid and brave. I've seen it twice. Once on the river, once when you told me you meant to give the gate your memories." She spat over the edge of the causeway, a ferrywoman's benediction. "I don't like the face."

"You volunteered for this."

"I volunteered to *answer* your oath, not to watch you walk into the water and come out someone else." Vess's jaw worked. "Twenty years building who you are, and you mean to hand it to a hole in the ground because a book of dead people said that's the price."

"The book of dead people built the realms, Vess. Every border, every harvest, every spring thaw. If the covenant dies—" She gestured at the thin air, the starved silence, the wraith-tide that had already come once and would come again. "This is what dying looks like. Slowly, then all at once."

"And if the price is wrong? If the old speakers were liars?"

Orsen looked at Isandro, curled against the stone, and thought of hollowed villages, of harvested children, of her own sealed memories waiting behind the gate like coins in a miser's vault. "Then we're about to find out," she said, "and we'll be finding out together, which is more than the old speakers ever had."

That was when the horns began.

* * *

They came up the smugglers' road with lanterns lit, which told Orsen everything about their confidence. No force that expected resistance marched openly by torchlight down a single-file mountain track. The Order of the Quiet Blade had never needed darkness; they had always believed themselves righteous enough to work in full view.

She counted banners as they debouched onto the shelf of rock above the causeway. Sixty, perhaps seventy—more than she had ever seen them field, even at the fall of her own order. Grey-cloaked, salt-sigiled, moving with the drilled silence of people who had cut out the noisy parts of their souls long ago. And at their center, riding a mule because horses feared the drowned places, a figure in white that even at this distance Orsen's body recognized before her mind consented to.

Sister Malen. Her mentor. The woman who had taught her to hold a sword-oath in her mouth like a live coal without swallowing it. The woman whose hands had guided hers through the first forms, whose voice had been the last thing Orsen heard on the night she ran, saying *you will understand when you are older, Orsen, and I will wait.*

Twenty years, and Malen had waited.

The harvesters halted at the head of the causeway, thirty paces from the caltrops, and formed their line with a precision that was almost beautiful and entirely obscene. Malen dismounted. She walked forward alone, past her own front rank, and stopped where the caltrops began, and looked down the narrow stone spine at the three of them—one swordspeaker with a dead mark, one ferrywoman with a borrowed blade, one glowing child pretending to be a stone.

"Orsen Coldharbour." Her voice carried the way it always had, pitched to fill a training yard, warm at the edges and iron underneath. "You look well. The mountains have been kinder to you than you deserved."

"Malen." Orsen did not move from her place before the gate. "You brought seventy blades to take one child."

"I brought seventy blades to save two realms." Malen spread her hands, and Orsen hated how reasonable the gesture was, how much of her own younger self saw the sense in it. "Look at the gate, girl. Truly look. You've felt it starving—I know you have; you couldn't stand within a mile of this place and not feel it. The covenant is failing. The wraiths are already loose. In ten years, twenty at the outside, the seal goes entirely, and everything the old speakers drowned comes up the rivers and into the lowlands. Everything." She took a step closer, boots ringing on wet stone. "Unless it's fed."

"It *is* fed. That's what the inscriptions say. Aldmere fed it willingly—"

"Aldmere fed it *slowly*, one speaker at a time, over centuries, and it starved anyway, because the old ways were wasteful. Sentimental." Malen's voice dropped, went intimate, the register she'd used for every lesson that mattered. "One keystone, given whole, renews the covenant for a thousand years. One child, Orsen. One child against every soul in both realms. You've done the arithmetic. You simply don't like the answer."

Orsen became aware that her hand had found her sword-hilt, and that the hilt was warm, and that the dead oath-mark at her throat was not dead at all tonight—it burned like a coal raked back to life, answering the proximity of the gate, the proximity of the child, the proximity of everything she had buried.

"You taught me the First Tongue," she said. "Every lesson, every form. And in twenty years of teaching, did you ever once tell me what the words were *for*? Because I've learned it since, Malen. From a nine-year-old who speaks it better than either of us ever will. The tongue isn't a tool. It isn't power to be spent. It's a *promise*—the promise the world made to itself that it would keep going. And you want to keep that promise by breaking every other one. By harvesting children. By butchering my order when they said no. By teaching generations of students that the language of keeping is a language of taking."

"The order you mourn," Malen said, "died of exactly this softness."

"The order I mourn died on their feet, refusing you." Fury rose in her then—not the hot flash-anger she'd known all her life, but something older and colder, rising from the same sealed place the memories waited in, and it came up through her like water coming up through stone, unstoppable, ancient, *righteous*. "And I have spent twenty years being ashamed of the wrong things. I was ashamed of running. I should have been ashamed of staying silent. Well. The silence ends tonight."

Malen sighed, and it was the saddest sound Orsen had ever heard her make, because it was entirely sincere. "I hoped it wouldn't come to this. Take the child," she said to her line, without raising her voice. "Kill the others if they resist. Gently, if you can manage gentleness."

The line advanced.

* * *

What happened next, Orsen would remember afterward in fragments, the way one remembers a storm—by its impacts rather than its shape.

Vess's caltrops bought them eight seconds and cost the harvesters four men their footing on the causeway's narrow spine; two went into the black water and the water took them without a ripple, which broke the advance's neat discipline better than any sword could have. Vess herself met the first three across the gap with a boat-hook and a fury Orsen had only glimpsed on the burning river, roaring words that were mostly profanity and partly prayer.

But Orsen was already moving the other way, because six of them had split off, quiet and fast, angling down the rocks toward Isandro, and everything else—the battle, the oath, the fate of realms—narrowed to a single point of purpose.

She met them at the ring of stones.

She had not drawn a sword-oath in twenty years, and the last time she had spoken one, it had terrified her. Now she understood that terror had been a kind of modesty, a young speaker's fear of her own voice. There was no modesty left in her. She planted herself between the six and the child, drew steel, and spoke.

"*Valessa mor en kethra dun.*"

*I am the wall, and the wall does not ask permission.*

The words left her mouth and became the world. Light ran down the blade like dawn running down a valley. The first harvester's blade met hers and shattered—not broke, *shattered*, its metal forgetting what it was—and the man behind the blow sat down hard on the wet stone and did not try again. The second and third came together and Orsen went through their guard the way her teacher had taught her, the way Malen had taught her, every form carrying her mentor's hands inside it, and she used that knowledge like a knife turned in a wound: *this is your art, Malen, this is what you made, and it stands against you now.* The fourth reached the ring of stones, close enough to grab for Isandro, and Orsen's oath-word caught him mid-stride and threw him the length of the causeway, where he lay groaning among Vess's caltrops.

The fifth and sixth looked at one another, and at her burning blade, and at the small glowing child, and made the oldest calculation soldiers know, and retreated.

For one breath, one clean breath, the ring of stones was hers.

Then the horns sounded again—not the advance signal but a single sustained note, high and wrong, and every harvester on the shelf of rock above went still and knelt, and Sister Malen walked forward alone down the causeway with her hands empty and open, and behind her the sky over the southern peaks began to boil with shapes.

Wraiths. Hundreds. Drawn by the sound of an oath spoken at the gate itself, drawn like sharks to blood in water, the drowned dead of Aldmere rising in their hundreds to attend the feeding—or the renewal—whichever came first.

Malen stopped ten paces away, wraith-light silvering her white robes, and looked at Orsen with an expression that was almost tender.

"Now you've called them," she said. "Now there's no walking away, for any of us. Give me the child, Orsen, and I swear the order feeds the gate mercifully, and you can spend whatever years you have left telling yourself it was necessary. That's the only mercy left on this mountain. Take it."

Behind Orsen, small and steady, Isandro rose to his feet against the singing stone, and the light in him flared bright enough to throw all their shadows long across the black water, and he shook his head.

Not at Malen.

At Orsen. *No,* the gesture said, clear as speech. *Don't give me to her. Don't give yourself to the water yet. We do this together, or not at all.*

And Orsen Coldharbour, last of the oath-bound, raised her burning sword between her found family and the whole descending night, and answered her mentor with the only words that had ever been true.

"Come and be refused."
