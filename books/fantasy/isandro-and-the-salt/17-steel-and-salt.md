## 17. Steel and Salt

Vess had counted her life in crossings—rivers forded, debts paid, bridges burned—and she had never once counted on standing at the mouth of a drowned city with a sword in one hand and a burning brand in the other, holding a causeway against the end of the world.

The gate lay below them like a drowned eye.

Aldmere's rooftops broke the black water in jagged rows, and beneath them, far down where the light went to die, the drowned gate glimmered—a seam of pale gold in the deep, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart. The causeway ran out over the water toward it, ancient stone slick with salt-spray, wide enough for three abreast and not an inch more. On either side, the sea waited with the patience of something that had already eaten a city once.

"Vess." Orsen's voice came low behind her, stripped of everything but purpose. "You don't have to hold it. We can find another way."

"There is no other way," Vess said, "and you know it as well as I do. The harvesters come up that road, and the wraiths come up this water, and somebody stands between them and that boy while you two work your miracle down there." She spat over the edge, into the dark. "Somebody's me. I've been ferrying folk across dangerous water my whole life, Coldharbour. This is just the widest river I've ever held."

Orsen looked at her a long moment. Twenty years of mountain silence had taught the woman to say little, but her eyes said plenty now—gratitude, apology, and underneath it all a fierce, stubborn tenderness that Vess had spent half a year pretending not to notice.

"Don't die," Orsen said finally. It was the closest thing to a prayer the swordspeaker had ever offered.

"No promises," Vess said. "But I'll make you this one: nothing crosses."

* * *

They came at moonrise.

Vess heard them before she saw them—the harvester column first, torchlight snaking down the cliff road like a burning vein, fifty strong or more, their white-and-grey livery gone dark with road dust. And then the water began to move against its own tide, and she knew the wraiths were coming too, called by the same hunger that had drawn them to Isandro's voice since the night he'd first spoken at a frozen door.

She planted herself at the narrowest point of the causeway, where a broken parapet left a gap barely wide enough for two men shoulder to shoulder. She'd spent the afternoon preparing it: oil from her stores poured into the cracks between stones, pitch-soaked rope coiled at her feet, her old boarding axe sharp enough to shave with. Behind her, the causeway stretched two hundred yards to the gate-shrine above the water, where Orsen knelt with Isandro, speaking low and urgent, teaching him the shape of an oath.

The boy looked small from here. He always did, until he opened his mouth.

The first harvester reached the gap—a big man with a hooked bill-hook and the flat eyes of someone who'd stopped seeing children as children long ago. Vess put her axe through his knee and shoved him into the sea without ceremony. The second got a boot to the chest. The third was smarter; he came with a shield and a friend, and the real fight began.

She fought the way she'd ferried: reading the current, giving ground only when the water demanded it, never once letting go of the rope. The causeway was hers. She knew every crack in it, every slick patch, every place the old stone had crumbled to a treacherous lip. She used all of it. A harvester slipped on the wet rock she'd oiled at dusk and went screaming into the black. Another swung wide to flank her and found the gap in the parapet gave him nothing but open air and a long fall.

"You're trespassing on my crossing," she told them, spitting blood. "Toll's due."

The toll mounted up around her. Bodies in the water. Her own blood mixing with the spray. Somewhere in the press of it she took a blade along the ribs—shallow, ugly, the kind of wound that would hurt like sin tomorrow if there was a tomorrow—and she laughed, actually laughed, because pain meant she was still standing, and standing was the whole of her job description now.

Behind her, the boy's voice rose.

* * *

Isandro did not scream. That was the thing about him that Vess would carry to whatever grave she earned tonight—he never screamed. What he did was worse, or better, or both: he *spoke*, and the First Tongue came out of him like weather.

The sound rolled down the causeway and out over the water, and Vess felt it in her teeth, in the scar tissue of her missing eye, in the deck-boards of a boat twenty years burned. She didn't understand a word of it. She understood all of it. Grief, the language said. Grief and refusal and a child's absolute, unanswerable *no*.

The sea answered.

It came up in a wall—not a wave, waves had shapes and limits, and this had neither—a standing column of black water crowned with foam, and inside it the wraiths shrieked as it took them. Vess had seen wraiths before, had watched them pour out of the Thornfell passes like smoke given teeth, had seen what they left of the villages they passed through. Now she watched the sea itself close its fist around dozens of them and squeeze. They came apart like salt in rain. The drowned gate's gold light flared beneath the water, bright as dawn, and for one impossible moment Vess could see straight down through the ocean to the ruin of Aldmere, every street and stair, as though the whole drowned city had been lit for a festival.

Then the light guttered, and the column collapsed back into the sea, and Isandro's voice broke off into coughing, and Vess understood with cold clarity what the inscriptions had been too polite to say plainly: the covenant was starving because feeding it cost. The boy had bought them minutes. Maybe less.

The harvesters saw it too. She watched their courage curdle—watched the front rank falter, watched their captain bellow them forward with promises of glory and harvest-share—and she set her feet and grinned at them through the blood on her teeth.

"That's right," she called. "He can do that again. Question is whether any of you live long enough to report it."

They came anyway. Fanatics usually did. But they came ragged now, afraid, and afraid men fought badly, and Vess fought like a woman who had already decided how she wanted to be remembered. She went through them at the gap like a storm through rigging—axe, elbow, boot, the burning brand swept in arcs to keep their shields honest. Two more into the water. One down on the stone, groaning, alive, and she stepped over him because she was a ferrywoman, not a butcher, and there was a difference, and tonight of all nights she needed there to be a difference.

Her brother would not have understood that. Her brother was somewhere down that torchlit road, wearing a captain's grey, and she had told him no with fire, and this—this causeway, this blood, this stand—was what her *yes* looked like.

* * *

They nearly had her at the turn of the hour.

A knot of six came together, disciplined, shields locked, the kind of trained push that no single fighter holds forever. Vess gave ground—had to give ground, foot by foot along the causeway, trading space for time, until her heel found the shrine steps and she knew that the next ten feet of retreat put her within reach of Orsen and the boy, and that she would not allow.

So she did the arithmetic every ferrywoman knows: sometimes you burn the boat.

She cut the pitch-rope loose and swung the brand into the oil she'd laid along the causeway's spine, and the stone went up in a line of blue-gold flame between her and the harvesters, heat washing her face, her braid smoking at the ends. The shield-wall broke apart in the firelight, men stumbling back, and in the chaos she killed two and maimed a third and took a sword-point through the meat of her shoulder for her trouble.

She sat down hard on the shrine steps afterward, because her legs had filed a formal complaint, and pressed her palm against the wound, and looked out at the burning causeway and the black water and the gold pulse of the gate below, and thought: *well. If this is the crossing, it's a fair fare.*

"Vess!"

Isandro's voice—his common-tongue voice, rough and young and terrified—came from beside her, and then his hands were on her arm, small and strong, pressing cloth against her shoulder the way she'd shown him once, weeks ago, around a shepherd's fire, never dreaming he'd need it here. His eyes were wet. His mouth worked around words he couldn't shape fast enough.

"I'm fine," she lied, and ruffled his hair with her bloody hand, because some things mattered more than honesty. "Look at me. Still ugly. Still mean. Nothing's changed."

He didn't believe her. He was learning to read her too well for that. But he stayed pressed against her side, one small warm weight in the cold, and together they watched the fire burn and the harvesters regroup beyond it, and Vess understood that the fire would not hold them forever, and that when it failed, she would stand up again, and stand again after that, as many times as her body allowed and a few times after.

That was the job. That had always been the job. You didn't get people across by being willing to drown—you got them across by refusing, absolutely and repeatedly, to let go of the rope.

* * *

The duel, when it came, she watched from the steps with Isandro's head against her good shoulder.

Orsen met her mentor at the far end of the burning causeway, where the flames thinned to embers and the stone showed through. Master Sarelle—Vess knew the name now, had heard it spat and mourned in equal measure—came alone across the scorched gap, white-haired and unhurried, her blade bare and her face full of a terrible gentle certainty. She believed, Vess realized, watching her walk. Truly believed that cutting the power out of a living child was mercy, was salvation, was the cheap clean path for two realms. There was nothing so dangerous as a kind woman doing arithmetic with other people's souls.

They fought the way mountains argue—slow to start, then total.

Vess had seen Orsen move before, in flashes: the wraith tide at the waystation, the ambush at the river. But those had been moments of necessity, power spent like coins from a hoard. This was different. This was twenty years of buried grief given a shape and a target, and the swordspeaker who emerged from it was terrible and beautiful and utterly silent, oath-marks blazing down her arms like rivers of white fire, meeting Sarelle stroke for stroke in a ringing rhythm that made the drowned gate below pulse faster, brighter, hungry for the violence or fed by it—Vess couldn't tell, and didn't want to know.

Sarelle was better. Vess could see it, and hated seeing it. The older woman had kept her edge honed these twenty years instead of burying it; she fought with economy and certainty, driving Orsen back along the causeway step by step, and each step was a step closer to the shrine, to the boy, to everything.

And Orsen smiled.

Vess saw it even at distance, even through smoke—the flash of teeth, the loosening of the shoulders. Saw her stop retreating. Saw her plant her feet on the exact spot where the parapet broke, the spot Vess herself had chosen hours ago, and speak—not a word Vess knew, but a word the *water* knew, because the sea rose up on either side of the causeway in two long silver walls, and Sarelle's footing turned to glass, and the duel turned all at once, the way rivers turn, slow and then all at once.

The mentor's blade went into the water. Orsen's oath-blade rested at her throat, trembling, and did not fall.

Down the causeway, past the dying fire, the harvester captain—her brother, her brother's grey coat, her brother's face gone strange with horror at what the sea was showing him—lowered his sword. Around him, one by one, the harvesters did the same. Not surrender exactly. Something more like witness.

And then the gate called.

* * *

Vess felt it before she heard it—a pull behind her sternum, like a hook set gently in the bone. The gold light beneath the water swelled, and the pulse of it quickened, quickened, became almost a drumbeat, and every hair on her arms stood up as the air filled with a pressure like the moment before lightning.

Isandro lifted his head from her shoulder.

His eyes had gone wide and fixed, reflecting gold, and his lips parted, and what came out of him was not grief this time. It was invitation. Vess didn't know the words, but she knew a summons when she heard one—had heard it in taverns and temples and once, memorably, from a river goddess who'd owed her money. The gate was calling its speakers home.

Orsen turned from her disarmed mentor. Looked down the causeway—at the shrine, at Vess, at the boy. And Vess watched the last of the swordspeaker's certainty drain out of her face, replaced by something rawer and truer: fear. Not of death. Of price. Everyone knew now what renewal cost. Everyone had read what the inscriptions whispered and Isandro had confirmed: memories. The self. The voice itself, fed into the fire.

Orsen started toward them anyway.

"Vess," she said, and her voice cracked on the name, and Vess understood in one cold flash what the woman intended, and hated her for it, and loved her for it, and was already getting to her feet.

"Not alone," Vess said. She picked up the brand. She picked up the boy's hand. "You don't get to do the noble lone-wolf sacrifice, Coldharbour—I've read that story, it ends stupid. Whatever the gate wants, it asked for speakers. Plural." She grinned, and it hurt, and she didn't care. "I'm not much of a speaker. But I've been talking to rivers my whole life, and one of them talks back."

Isandro squeezed her hand. Once. Firm. Answer enough.

Beyond the embers of the fire, Sarelle sank to her knees on the wet stone, disarmed and undone, watching her student walk toward the gate with a ferrywoman and a child, and made no move to stop them. The sea had gone still as hammered pewter. The gold pulsed below, patient, immense, waiting.

The three of them walked down the causeway together—toward the light, toward the price, toward whatever the water would take.

Vess did not look back at the burning boat behind her. Ferrywomen never do.
