## 18. The Oath Unspoken

The causeway was lost.

Orsen knew it the way a swordspeaker knows a blade's balance—by feel, by the deep arithmetic of weight and ruin. Vess had held it for as long as any one woman could, and longer than any one woman should. Now the harvester line had broken through at the western arch, and their salt-bright lanterns were spilling across the black water like a wound opening in reverse, and behind them came the wraith tide in its full and terrible patience.

She was on her knees on the gate-stone, her sword flat beneath her palm, and she could not rise.

Twenty years of hiding had not weakened her arm. That was the bitter joke of it—the oath-mark still burned true along her forearm, the old words still lived in her mouth like swallowed coals. But a swordspeaker's voice was not made of muscle. It was made of memory. Every word she had ever spoken in the First Tongue had been paid for out of the vault of what she loved: her order's halls, her mentor's lessons, the faces of the dead at Hollowmere, the taste of bread in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but inside her. And she had spent so much of that vault already tonight, hurling oaths against the tide, that when she reached for another word there was almost nothing left to spend.

*Almost.*

What remained was Isandro's face when he first stood at her door, frost in his lashes, speaking a language two centuries dead. What remained was Vess laughing with her one eye against the wind of the ferry crossing. What remained was the boy asleep against her shoulder in the shepherd's bothy, teaching her the word for *stay* with his small hand pressed flat over her heart.

That was all she had left. And it was not enough. It could never be enough, because it was hers alone, hoarded, guarded, a single candle cupped in two shaking hands against an ocean of dark.

The wraiths came up the drowned stair like water remembering its shape. They did not run. They *poured*, gray and glistening, their faces half-formed from the memories they had eaten from the gate's leavings—she saw her own order's dead looking back at her from those faces, saw the harvesters' victims, saw centuries of voices the covenant had starved into silence. Her mentor stood at the head of them, steel drawn, blood on his cheek, his eyes fixed not on Orsen but on the child.

"Give him to us," he called across the water, and his voice carried the terrible gentleness of a man who has made cruelty into arithmetic. "One child, Coldharbour. One small price, and both realms breathe easy for another hundred years. You know the old way works. You watched it work."

*I watched it murder,* she thought. But her throat was salt and ash, and the words would not come.

Isandro was beside her. He had refused to go where Vess had tried to send him—back toward the tunnel mouth, away—and now he knelt too, his small body trembling, his hands pressed over his ears because the wraith-voices were loud, so loud, a thousand drowned people whispering the shapes of forgotten words. He looked at her. His eyes were wet and furious and utterly unafraid in the way that only children and the dying are unafraid, and he said—not aloud, not in any tongue she knew, but in the language underneath all languages—

*You are almost empty. I can hear it. Why didn't you tell me?*

"Because," Orsen rasped, "it was mine to carry."

He shook his head, slow and certain, the way he had shaken his head at her burned supper, at her clumsy knots, at every failure of hers he had ever witnessed without judgment. Then he took her hand off the sword and turned it palm-up, and pressed his own small palm against it, and spoke one word of the First Tongue into her skin.

It was not an oath-word. It was older than oaths. It was the word inscribed above every gate in Aldmere, the word the drowned city had whispered to itself for a thousand years before anyone thought to bind anything with it:

*Together.*

And Orsen Coldharbour, last of the oath-bound, felt the truth of it strike her like dawn striking glass.

Not one speaker. It had never been one speaker. The covenant of old had not been maintained by solitary heroes spending themselves down to nothing at a lonely threshold—it had been maintained by *orders*, by circles, by dozens and hundreds of voices bound to one another by more than duty. By love. By shared bread and shared grief and the daily, unglamorous labor of being known. Her order had fallen not because its members died but because they had died *separate*, each guarding a fragment, each hoarding a candle. And she had spent twenty years perfecting that same solitude, calling it discipline, calling it penance, when it was only fear wearing armor.

The gate did not want a sacrifice. It wanted a chorus.

Her breath came out of her in a sob that was also a laugh. Isandro tilted his head at the sound, wary, and she gripped his hand hard enough that he'd remember it, and turned to find Vess.

Vess was limping up the causeway through the smoke, her coat scorched, her boat's ashes probably still drifting somewhere on the black water behind her, her axe notched and red. She looked like a woman who had already decided how she meant to die and found the prospect mildly inconvenient. When she saw Orsen's face she stopped, and spat blood onto the stones, and said, "Whatever mad thing you're about to do, do it quick. We've got maybe a minute of world left."

"I need your voice," Orsen said.

"You know I can't speak your fancy words."

"They're not my words." She pulled them both close, Isandro's shoulder under one arm, Vess's under the other, three of them kneeling on the gate-stone while the tide poured closer and the harvesters' lanterns swung nearer and the drowned gate beneath them shuddered like something waking hungry. "They belong to whoever loves enough to say them. That's the whole secret. That was always the whole secret, and we buried it under ritual and rank and sacrifice because love is harder than heroism."

Vess stared at her. Then, remarkably, she laughed—one short, rough bark of a sound, entirely without hope and entirely without fear. "Twenty years I've known you, and this is the first time you've ever sounded like someone worth knowing."

"Repeat after me," Orsen said. "Exactly after me. Your voice will make the words true even if you don't understand them. Especially if you don't understand them."

"And the cost?" Vess asked, because Vess always asked about the cost. "You told me once the words take payment out of what you love."

Orsen looked down at Isandro. At the boy who had walked out of a blizzard into her silence and taught her, word by patient word, that silence was not the same as peace. She thought of the memories she had left—the door in the drift, the frost-bit child, the ferry burning, the bothy, the grammar of grief built between them across a language barrier. Everything she had rebuilt her self around since the fall. If the covenant took it all, she would wake tomorrow a stranger to the only family she had ever chosen.

Terror rose in her like the tide itself.

And then Isandro squeezed her hand, and spoke into her bones again, that wordless language of his: *I will still be here. Even if you forget me, I will be here. Say it anyway.*

"Yes," she said aloud, answering both of them, answering herself. "Yes, there's a cost. Pay it with me."

She raised her voice, and the First Tongue came out of her not as a weapon this time but as a thread, fine and bright, and she began the oldest oath in the world—the one no single mouth had spoken in living memory, the one written in fragments across the drowned walls of Aldmere, waiting two centuries for enough voices to make it whole.

*"By the salt that keeps and the water that takes—"*

Vess's rough voice caught the words a half-beat behind hers, mangling them, meaning them completely. The gate-stone warmed beneath their knees.

*"—by the door shut in winter and opened in storm—"*

Isandro's voice joined, high and clear, and where his words touched the air the wraith-tide *stilled*, a thousand drowned faces turning toward the sound like flowers toward sun. The harvesters stopped at the causeway's edge. Even her mentor went quiet, his certainty faltering into something like awe, or grief, or recognition.

*"—by the hand given freely and the name given truly—"*

The gate beneath them opened. Not with violence—with relief, the long groan of a thing held shut too long finally allowed to breathe. Light came up out of the deep water, silver and slow, and Orsen felt it begin to draw from her, gently, terribly, reaching for the memories that fueled her voice, and she did not flinch, because Isandro's hand was in hers and Vess's shoulder was against hers and terror was transfiguring itself, syllable by syllable, into communion.

*"—we are not one voice, we are many; we are not the wall, we are the door—"*

The light rose. The wraiths began, very softly, to sing.

*"—and what we love, we hold together, or not at all."*

The oath closed like a ring of water finding its level. For one heartbeat the whole drowned gate, the whole mountain, the whole night hung balanced on the sound of three ragged voices—one trained, one broken, one small and new—and then the covenant answered.

It answered *yes*.

The light took them. Orsen felt it moving through her like a tide going out, and the last thing she held onto, deliberately, fiercely, with everything left in her, was not a memory at all but a sensation: the weight of a small warm hand in hers, and the knowledge—deeper than memory, deeper than the gate could reach—that whatever else the water took, it could not take the fact that she had been loved, and had finally, at the very end, loved back.

Then the silver rose over her eyes, and the night, and the war, and the woman she had been, all went quiet together.

* * *

Sound returned first. Water, lapping gently at stone—gentle now, tamed, a tide that had remembered its manners. Then cold air, clean of salt and smoke. Then weight: a small body curled against her side, breathing slow and even, asleep.

Orsen opened her eyes.

She was lying on the gate-stone. The wraiths were gone—all of them, everywhere, the water dark and empty and ordinary. Far up the causeway, the surviving harvesters were fleeing into the tunnels, leaderless, their lanterns bobbing away like frightened stars. The drowned gate beneath her had gone silent and deep, humming faintly, contentedly, like a fed animal.

Vess sat slumped nearby, alive, whole, staring at her own hands as though checking they were still hers. When she saw Orsen stir, she managed a crooked smile. "Well," she said. "That worked better than most of your plans."

Orsen opened her mouth to answer—and found nothing there.

Not silence exactly. A room where furniture used to be. She reached for her order's name and found a smooth blank wall. Reached for Hollowmere, for her mentor's face, for the twenty years of exile and the reasons for them, and found only soft gray water where the rooms had been. Panic flickered—and then the small warm weight against her side shifted, and a hand found hers in the dark, familiar as her own pulse, and the panic receded, because some things lived below memory, in the marrow, where the water could not reach.

The boy stirred. Lifted his head. Looked at her with eyes that searched her face carefully, the way one searches a house after a flood, cataloguing what survived.

Then Isandro smiled, and spoke his first word in the First Tongue since the night he arrived at her door—a word she somehow, impossibly, still understood, though she could not have said why:

*Home.*

And far below them, in the deep and dreaming dark, the renewed covenant pulsed once, softly, like a heart beginning to learn its rhythm—waiting, patient and alive, for all the other voices yet to come.
