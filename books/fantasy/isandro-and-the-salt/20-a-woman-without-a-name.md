## 20. A Woman Without a Name

She woke to the smell of woodsmoke and rosemary, and for a long, drifting moment she did not know where she was.

That was not the strange part. She had woken in a hundred unfamiliar places—ditches, waystations, the backs of wagons, once the crook of a tree forty feet above a frozen ravine. Waking lost was an old habit of her body. What was strange was that when she reached for the reasons—the names, the oaths, the long ledger of years that had always been there, heavy as a sword at her hip—she found only smooth water.

She opened her eyes.

A ceiling of dark timber, smoke-stained, crossed by beams she did not recognize. A fire burned low in a hearth of fitted stone. Through a window beside her, small and square and set deep in the wall like a ship's porthole, she could see snow falling in fat, unhurried flakes, and beyond it the grey shoulder of a mountain wearing its white like a shawl.

Her body told her things her mind could not. There was a scar on her left forearm, old and pale, shaped like a fishhook. Her right hand was calloused in a way that spoke of a blade gripped ten thousand times, though when she looked at it, it was just a hand. A woman's hand. Knuckles scarred. A thin white line across the palm she had no story for.

She sat up slowly. Every muscle reported in, one by one, like soldiers answering a roll call: bruised ribs, a stiffness in the left knee that flared when she turned, a deep weariness in the chest as though she had shouted herself hollow. Beneath all of it, stranger still, a kind of quiet. A silence in her bones like the silence after a bell has finished ringing.

On the chair beside the bed someone had folded her clothes. They were not hers—or they were hers, but she could not say why she knew the cut of them, the salt-stiffened wool, the sword-belt coiled on top with its plain steel buckle. The scabbard was empty.

*The scabbard is empty,* she thought, and felt nothing. That frightened her more than anything else. A woman who owned a sword-belt should feel something, looking at an empty scabbard. Grief, or vigilance, or shame. She felt the way she imagined a stranger would feel, picking up another traveler's belongings: *I suppose this belongs to someone.*

There were voices below. Muffled through the floorboards, indistinct—one low and rough, a woman's voice, the kind that carried over water; and one that was not a voice at all so much as a feeling, a warmth without words, rising and falling like breathing.

She dressed because her body knew how, which was more than her mind could claim. The wool fit. The belt settled on her hips at a place worn smooth by years of exactly this. Whoever she had been, she had been her a long time.

The stairs creaked under her weight, each step announcing her, and she let them. At the bottom, through a doorway hung with a curtain of oiled canvas, she saw the kitchen: a long table scarred by knives and heat, a pot steaming on the iron stove, and two figures moving around each other with the easy choreography of people who have shared small spaces for a long time.

One was a broad-shouldered woman with a leather patch over her left eye, hair going iron-grey at the temples, arguing cheerfully with a pot about whether rosemary belonged in a broth. The other—

The other was a boy. Ten years old, perhaps. Dark-haired, thin, with the careful stillness of a wild thing deciding whether you are weather or threat. He was setting bowls on the table, three of them, and he moved like someone who had learned that tables and bowls and warm rooms could be taken away without warning.

He saw her first.

He went absolutely still, the way water goes still before it freezes, and his eyes—dark, old, unchildlike in their attention—fixed on her face with an intensity that should have been frightening and was instead something else. Something like recognition. Something like a door opening after a long winter.

"There she is." The one-eyed woman turned, ladle in hand, and looked her up and down with frank appraisal. "Sit down before you fall down. You've been asleep two days and talking in your sleep for one of them, and I'll thank you never to do either again."

"I—" Her voice came out rough, unused. She cleared her throat. "Where am I?"

The one-eyed woman snorted. "Thornfell. My roof, such as it is. Which makes it yours too, apparently, since you're the one who insisted we rebuild the old waystation instead of finding somewhere sensible." She jerked the ladle toward the window, toward the mountain. "You don't remember any of this."

It was not a question. The woman's eye—her remaining one, brown and sharp and unexpectedly kind—narrowed slightly, studying her the way a ferryman studies a river before committing to the crossing.

"No," she said. "I don't remember. Anything." She heard how it sounded and pressed on anyway. "I know how to walk downstairs. I know the wool of this coat is good against wet cold. I don't know my own name."

The boy made a sound. Small, bitten off immediately—but she caught it, and it was grief, or the shape grief makes when it has nowhere to go.

"Vess," the one-eyed woman said abruptly. "My name's Vess. Ferrywoman, smuggler, reformed criminal, current cook. That's Isandro." She nodded at the boy. "He doesn't talk much. Not won't—*can't*, mostly, though I'm starting to think that's a choice he makes when the world gets loud." Vess set the ladle down and folded her arms, and something in her stance shifted, softened, the way ice shifts before it becomes water. "And you—you were somebody. Somebody important. You gave up—" Her jaw worked. "You gave up whatever it cost to close that gate. All three of us did, in our ways. But you most of all. The gate took your past, Orsen. It took it so the rest of us could keep ours."

*Orsen.*

The name landed in her like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples went out and out and found no shores. It sounded right. It sounded like a key that fit a lock whose door had been bricked over. She repeated it silently, tasting it: *Orsen.* And beneath it, fainter, like a word read underwater: *Coldharbour?* But that name slid off her entirely, meaning nothing, weighing nothing—a title, maybe, a rank, a thing she had been called by people who needed her to be a weapon.

"Orsen," she said aloud, testing it against the air of the kitchen, against the steam and the rosemary and the boy's unwavering gaze. "Isandro. Vess." Three names. She looked at the table set for three, the three bowls, and understood without knowing how she understood that this was the whole of it now. This was what remained. "Thank you. For telling me. For—" She gestured vaguely at everything: the bed upstairs, the broth, the two days.

"Don't thank me yet," Vess said gruffly, turning back to the stove. "Wait till you've tasted my cooking. Then we'll see if you still think saving the realms was worth it."

* * *

The days that followed taught her the shape of her own absence.

It was a peculiar education. She discovered she could mend a net, though there was no net within fifty miles. She discovered she could read—not just letters, but a second script, angular and beautiful, that surfaced in her mind whenever she saw certain marks scratched into the lintel above the hearth, though the words it gave her were few and arrived like fragments of a song remembered from childhood: *bind. keep. breathe.* She discovered she could take apart and reassemble the short sword Vess kept behind the door blindfolded, and that when she held it, her body hummed with a readiness that had no object and no history.

And she discovered the gaps.

They were everywhere, once she started looking. She would reach for the memory of learning to fight and find a hole shaped exactly like a girl being handed her first blade. She would catch herself humming a tune in a language she didn't speak and reach for the person who taught it to her and find nothing—not even the certainty that there had been such a person. Once, sorting through a chest of spare clothes, she found a child's mitten, far too small for anyone here, knitted in blue and white, and she held it for a long time while something in her chest ached with a grief that had lost its address.

She did not tell Vess about the mitten. But Vess saw her holding it, and later that day there was a cup of something hot and bitter pressed into her hands without comment, and that, she was learning, was how Vess said the things she couldn't.

Isandro watched her through all of it. Quietly, patiently, the way he seemed to do everything. He followed her on her slow walks around the waystation's walls, always a few steps behind, always there when she turned. He showed her things without words: which stones in the path iced over first, where the fox denned beneath the woodpile, how to hold her hands when the wind came off the mountain so it didn't find the gaps in her sleeves. He was teaching her the world, she realized. Reintroducing her to it, piece by piece, as if afraid that if he stopped paying attention even for a moment, she might dissolve like the rest of her past had.

One evening she found him sitting on the stairs, turning something over in his hands. It was a flat piece of slate, and on it, in that angular beautiful script, were marks she almost understood.

"What does it say?" she asked, sitting beside him. Their shoulders nearly touched. He was warm, the way children are warm, like a banked coal.

He looked at her. Then he took her hand—his grip surprisingly firm—and pressed her finger to the first mark, guiding it, and made a sound in his throat, a note, half-hummed, and the mark *sang*. Not aloud. In her blood. *Bind,* said the mark, in a voice older than stone.

*Keep,* said the second.

*Breathe,* said the third.

She pulled back, heart hammering. "That's—that's the language. The First Tongue. I know it. I don't remember knowing it, but I know it, the way I know how to walk." She stared at the slate. "Why are you showing me this?"

Isandro's expression did something complicated. He looked down at the slate, and then at her, and there was a wariness in him suddenly, an old wariness, the look of a child who has learned that what he carries makes people want things. He wiped the slate clean against his sleeve.

"It frightens you," she said quietly. "When you use it."

He didn't move. Which was answer enough.

"Listen to me." She turned to face him fully, and took both his hands in hers, and felt him tremble—just once, just slightly, a bird's heartbeat of fear. "Whatever that language did before, whoever used it and for what—I don't remember any of that. Maybe that's why I can say this clean: that language isn't a debt you owe anyone. Not me. Not Vess. Not the mountain. If you never speak another word of it, that's your right. Do you understand me?"

His eyes were bright. His chin dipped, once.

"But," she added, softer, "if you ever *want* to—if there's ever a word in you that needs out—there's no safer place in the world than between me and that woman in the kitchen. Whatever comes out of you, it lands on us first. Understood?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he leaned forward and put his forehead against her shoulder, briefly, hard, the way a door closes, and she understood that too, and put her arm around him and held on.

* * *

Winter deepened. The waystation filled itself around them the way a nest fills with what birds bring.

Runners came, at first one and then more—gaunt, wary men and women with the same angular marks tattooed at their wrists or throats, survivors scattered across twenty years and five hundred miles, drawn south by rumors that traveled faster than any rider: *the gate is closed, the wraiths are stilled, there is a school forming in Thornfell.* Vess met them all at the causeway with her arms crossed and her one eye measuring, and brought them up the mountain only after questions that would have made a magistrate blush. But she brought them. And Orsen found herself standing in front of these strangers, these remnants, these people who looked at her with hunger and hope and recognition she could not return, and saying: *I can't give you back what you lost. I don't have it myself. But I can learn alongside you.*

It was Isandro, in the end, who taught them all.

Not by speaking—he still chose silence most days, communicating in gestures and slate-marks and that half-hummed note that made written words sing in the blood. But he had a way of putting his hand on a student's shoulder and guiding their finger along a line of script until the meaning bloomed, and the students began to call these sessions *listening to the well*, and they came out of them changed, quieter, carrying new words home like embers in cupped hands.

Orsen sat in every session. She learned her own forgotten language at the speed of a beginner, stumbling over grammar her body half-remembered, and found—to her surprise—that she did not mind. There was a freedom in it she had not expected. Every word she learned was genuinely new. No ghost of a former self stood behind her whispering *you used to know this, you used to be more.* She was building herself from the foundation up, and the foundation was this kitchen, this mountain, these people.

But some nights, alone on the wall with the wind coming down off the glacier, she wondered. Who had she been, that a gate full of drowned gods had wanted her memories specifically? What had she done that was worth trading for the world? Was the ache she sometimes felt at sunset—aimless, sourceless, like a hand reaching for a rail that wasn't there—was that grief for something specific, or just the shape of the hole?

She never found answers on the wall. But she stopped needing them, gradually, the way a wound stops needing to be explained and starts needing only to be kept clean.

* * *

It happened in early spring, on a morning of hard blue light, when the snow on the southern slopes was beginning to talk to itself in the voice of thawing water.

She was in the kitchen alone, kneading bread, flour to her elbows, when Isandro came in from the yard with an odd, deliberate gait. She knew that gait. It was the walk of a boy who has decided something and is terrified of it.

He crossed to the hearth, where the great stone lintel bore the old carved marks—*bind, keep, breathe*—and beneath them, newer, rougher, the marks the students had added as they learned: small words, small vows, a growing constellation. He stood before the stone for a long moment.

Then he picked up the cutting stylus from the shelf, and turned, and looked at her.

"Isandro?" she said, wiping her hands. "What is it?"

He pointed at her. Then at the stone. Then at himself. Then he touched his own throat, and shook his head—not *no*, but *not this way*—and pointed at the stone again, and she understood: he wanted to add something, and he wanted her to see it, and he could not or would not say it aloud.

"You want to carve something," she said. "For me?"

He nodded. Then hesitated. Then, with sudden fierce resolution, he took her hand and pressed her flour-dusted finger to the first mark on the lintel, the oldest one, and hummed his low note, and the stone sang: *bind.*

Then the second: *keep.*

Then the third: *breathe.*

And then he guided her finger to a blank space beneath them, a space he must have chosen days ago, and he did not carve. Instead he opened his mouth, and for the first time since she had known him—for the first time, she understood, since the night he had arrived frost-bitten at a stranger's door in a blizzard and spoken words that burned—he *spoke*.

The word was not a word. It was a chord. It was three notes braided together, and it went through her like sunlight through water, and where it passed, things lit up. Not memories—she had none to light. But meanings. The word meant a shelter built against wind. It meant a fire kept burning in a window for someone expected. It meant arms. It meant the place where you are known completely and remain anyway. It meant every kitchen in every house where anyone had ever waited up for anyone else.

It meant *home.*

And it was her name.

Not Coldharbour—that dead title, that weight of salt and steel, gone with everything else. Not Orsen, even, though Orsen lived in it, folded inside it like a seed in snow. The word he spoke was larger than both, and it contained her, and it named her, and it asked nothing.

The sound faded. The kitchen was very quiet. Flour drifted in the light from the window.

Isandro stood with his hand still resting on the stone, looking up at her, his dark eyes wet and steady, waiting—not anxiously, but with the patience of someone who has given a gift and knows it cannot be taken back.

"Oh," Orsen said. Her voice broke on it, and she let it. She knelt down on the cold flagstones, heedless of the dough and the flour and the ache in her bad knee, so that their faces were level. "Oh, *that's* what I am."

He nodded, solemn as a priest, and then—wonderfully, unbearably—he smiled. It transformed his whole face, the careful wildness falling away, and for a moment he was simply a boy of ten in a warm kitchen in spring, and she loved him with a fierceness that required no language at all.

From the doorway came a sniff. Vess stood there with an armful of split birch, pretending very hard to have just arrived, her one eye suspiciously bright.

"Well," she said, dumping the wood by the stove with a clatter. "If we're naming things, someone ought to name the stew, because I refuse to serve it under its current title of *whatever this is.*"

Orsen laughed—it came up from somewhere below memory, from somewhere deeper than the places the gate had stripped, from some bedrock self that had survived everything—and pulled Isandro into her arms, and felt Vess's broad hand land on both their heads like a blessing, like a roof.

Outside, the snow was giving way. Down the mountain, the thaw-water ran silver over stones, running toward the rivers, toward the sea, carrying the last of the salt away. And in the kitchen of the rebuilt waystation, a woman without a past kneaded bread beside a boy who had given her a future, and neither of them was alone, and the stone above the hearth held its small constellation of words, and waited, patient as mountains, for all the voices still to come.
