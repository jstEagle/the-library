## 21. The Ferryman's New Road

The waystation had been a tomb when Vess first saw it, and she'd said so. Orsen had looked at her with that flat grey stare of hers—the one that used to make river smugglers wet themselves—and said nothing, which was how Vess knew the words had landed somewhere deep.

Now, eight months on, the tomb had windows.

Vess stood in the yard with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, hauling a beam of mountain pine into place against the new south wall, and let herself feel the sun on the back of her neck like it was something she'd earned. Below her, the valley opened out in long green folds, the river a bright thread stitching through it all the way down to Thornfell's feet. Her river. Well—not hers anymore, not exactly. But she still knew it the way you know an old lover's breathing, and some mornings she walked down to the bank just to say hello.

"Mind your footing," came Orsen's voice from under the beam's other end. "You're listing."

"I'm *balancing*," Vess said. "There's a difference. One of us has sailed a deck through white water for thirty years."

"One of us is standing on a ladder."

Vess grinned and drove the peg home with three hard strikes of the mallet. The beam settled true. Above them, the old watchtower—Orsen's tower, the one she'd hidden in for twenty years like a hermit crab in a shell too small for it—now bore a fresh-cut sign, its letters burned deep into the wood in two scripts side by side: the common hand, and beneath it, looping and strange as water writing itself, the First Tongue.

*The School of Voices*, Isandro had named it. He'd been eleven days learning enough common speech to argue the point, and he'd argued it with his whole body, hands flying, face fierce, until Orsen threw up her own hands and surrendered. *A school*, not a sanctuary, not a hiding place. A place where people came to learn instead of hide.

Children came first. That was how it always went, Vess supposed. Word traveled down the smugglers' roads the way water finds cracks—a shepherd here, a tinker there, a widow in the lowlands whose boy spoke words in his sleep that made dogs howl. They arrived at the door half-starved and twice-frightened, and Isandro would meet them in the yard, solemn as a little magistrate, and take their hands, and something in their faces would unclench.

Then came others. Not children.

Vess hammered the last peg and sat back on her heels, watching the road that wound up from the valley. Three figures on it today, dusty and slow, walking like people who had spent years being hunted and hadn't yet learned the war was over. She recognized the walk. She'd worn it herself, once, after her brother's men burned her boat and very nearly everything else.

"Visitors," she called down.

Orsen appeared at the corner of the house, wiping sawdust from her forearms. She looked at the road, and Vess watched the old reflex move through her—the tightening around the eyes, the weight shifting to the balls of her feet, the swordspeaker reading threat before she read anything else. Eight months, and it was still there. Maybe it always would be. But then Isandro came around the other corner with an armful of kindling, saw the travelers, and dropped the wood entirely, and Orsen's whole face changed like weather.

"Go on, then," Vess said, waving her off. "I'll mind the wall."

She watched from the ridge as Orsen walked down to meet them. The tallest of the three was a woman with a sword wrapped in oilcloth slung across her back—the wrapping of someone who couldn't decide whether to carry it or bury it—and when she got close enough to see Orsen's face clearly, she stopped dead in the road and made a sound Vess had no name for. Not a word. Something older than words. And then the woman went to one knee in the dust, and the other two followed, and Orsen stood over them looking like a woman being drowned gently.

Vess turned back to her beam before she saw anything else. Some things weren't hers to watch.

* * *

They were oath-speakers. Of course they were. Scattered survivors of the Order of the Salted Blade, come out of holes and hillsides because word had gone round the hidden places of the world like a bell rung underwater: *the gate lives, the covenant breathes, there is a school.*

There were nine of them by midsummer. A stooped old man named Corren who had spent fifteen years posing as a deaf-mute beekeeper and now wouldn't stop talking, ever, about anything, as if language were food and he'd been starving. Twin sisters from the coast who finished each other's oaths. A scarred young man called Dain who flinched at raised voices and could speak stone into stillness. And Maren—the tall one, the one who'd knelt—who had held a fragment of the old covenant alone in the far north, keeping one small shrine alive through sheer stubbornness, and who wept the night she arrived, silently, hugely, into a bowl of Vess's fish stew.

"They think I'm the last," Orsen told Vess later, on the porch, in the voice she used for things that cost her. "They've thought that for twenty years. Each of them thought *they* might be the last."

"And now?"

Orsen was quiet a while. Down in the yard, lamplight spilled from the schoolhouse where Isandro was teaching the younger ones their letters—common letters first, though he'd already begun slipping the First Tongue in sideways, the way you sweeten medicine. His voice carried faintly up the hill, patient and clear.

"Now they're finding each other," Orsen said. "That's the thing I never understood, Vess. All those years I kept the covenant alone, I thought solitude was the price. It wasn't the price. It was the wound." She turned her cup in her hands. "The gate doesn't want one voice. It never did. Look at what happened at the threshold—it took three of us, bound by—" She stopped. She still stumbled on that word sometimes. *Love.* Like a step missing in a staircase she'd climbed ten thousand times.

"*Bound by love*," Vess supplied, dry. "Say it enough times and it stops biting."

"It hasn't stopped yet."

"No," Vess admitted. "But it bites softer." She knocked her cup against Orsen's. "Drink your tea, swordspeaker. Tomorrow we raise the east roof and Maren wants to spar with you at dawn, and if you keep brooding I'll tell Corren you need a lecture on apiculture."

Orsen laughed.

It still startled Vess every time—that sound coming out of that woman, rough and rusty as a hinge but real, real as rain. Twenty years of silence, and now the laughter came easier each month, like a river cutting a new channel. Vess hoarded every one.

* * *

The trouble with hope, Vess thought one evening in early autumn, was that it made you careless. She caught herself humming at the ferry landing she'd built downstream—a small boat now, a teaching boat, because Isandro had declared with great gravity that any proper school needed a ferrywoman and Vess had discovered, to her private horror, that she liked being needed.

She was mending netting on the dock when the boy came down the path and sat beside her without asking, the way he did. Twelve now, or near enough. He'd shot up that summer, all wrists and elbows, his dark hair forever in his eyes. He picked up a length of spare twine and began mimicking her knots, watching her hands, adjusting his own.

"You knot wrong," Vess said.

He stuck his tongue out at her. Then he signed—his common speech was fluent now, though he still preferred his hands when he was tired—*Show me again.*

She showed him again. For a while they worked in the easy quiet of two people who didn't need words to fill a silence, which was, Vess reflected, its own kind of language. The river muttered past. Somewhere up the hill, Corren was lecturing somebody about bees; the twins were singing a work-song in two-part harmony that was half common tongue and half First, and the blend of it did something strange and lovely to the air, like light through moving water.

"Can I ask you something, salt-mouth?" Isandro signed. He'd given her the name himself, months ago, delighted with his own wit, and she'd pretended to hate it so thoroughly that now only he was allowed to use it.

"Aye."

*When you had nothing—no boat, no brother, no road—what made you keep going?*

Vess's hands stilled on the netting. Direct question, no warning. He'd learned that from Orsen, who asked direct questions the way other people swung axes.

She considered lying, or deflecting, or telling him a funny story instead. Then she looked at his face—open, waiting, not pitying, just wanting to know—and decided he'd earned better than deflection. The boy had collapsed a fortress with his grief. He could hold an honest answer.

"My brother offered me my old life back," she said. "Amnesty, a warm berth, all of it, if I handed you over. And I said no, and I burned the bridge behind me, and for about a month afterward I woke up every morning thinking, well, Vess, you stupid woman, you've got nothing left, you chose nothing." She tied off a knot. "And then one morning I woke up and realized the nothing had gotten rather crowded. There was a brooding swordspeaker in it, and a mute child who cheated at cards, and a mountain full of ghosts learning to be people again. Turns out that wasn't nothing. Turns out it was the first thing I'd ever chosen *for* myself instead of against somebody else."

Isandro was watching her hands as she talked—he always watched hands—but at the end he looked up at her face, and his eyes were bright, and he signed slowly, deliberately, so she'd catch every word:

*I'm glad you chose nothing.*

Vess laughed—out loud, full-throated, startling a heron off the reeds—and pulled him against her side and scrubbed her knuckles through his hair until he squirmed and fought and laughed too, both of them making noise on the quiet dock, and neither of them stopping.

* * *

Winter came down off the peaks early that year, soft at first, then serious. The school emptied of day-students from the valley but filled instead with residents—Maren and Dain and the twins wintering over, Corren refusing on principle to abandon his hives, three more arrivals who came in on the last passable roads before the snows closed them. Seventeen voices now, counting the household. Seventeen, when two decades ago there had been one, hiding in a tower, letting the covenant starve.

On the longest night of the year, they did a thing none of them had planned.

It started small. Supper was done, the fire was high, and Corren—because it was always Corren—began telling a story about his years among the bees, and halfway through he slipped into the First Tongue without noticing, the way he did, and instead of correcting him, the twins picked up the thread and harmonized, and then Maren added a line, low and resonant, and then Dain—shy Dain, flinching Dain—spoke a single phrase that made the firelight bend toward him like a listener.

And then Isandro stood up.

He didn't perform. That wasn't his way. He simply lifted his voice into the old language—freely now, easily, the words coming home to him—and what he said was simple, a child's grace, thanks for bread and fire and company. But the covenant heard it. Everyone felt it: a faint pressure, a warmth, the sense of some vast attention turning toward them through the bones of the mountain, gentle as a hand laid on a sleeping animal. The wraith-tide far below at the drowned gate stilled a little further. The realms breathed.

And Orsen—Vess watched her across the room—Orsen closed her eyes and let out a breath she'd maybe been holding for twenty years.

Afterward, when the young ones were abed and the fire had burned to embers, Vess found her out on the porch, standing in the blue snowlight, looking south toward the mountains' roots where the drowned gate lay quiet.

"Well?" Vess said, handing her a mug of mulled wine. "Seventeen voices. The gate must be getting fat and happy."

"The gate is fed," Orsen said quietly. "For the first time in generations, it's actually *fed*. And it barely takes anything—a word here, a song there, freely given. It was never meant to devour anyone." Her jaw tightened around the old grief: the harvesters, her mentor, the belief that renewal required butchery. "We were lied to for centuries. All of us. It only ever wanted company."

"Mm. Sounds like someone else I know. Twenty years alone in a tower, pretending she wanted to be there."

"Careful, ferrywoman."

"Or what? You'll challenge me to dawn sparring? Because Maren's beaten you twice this week and I have witnesses."

Orsen turned, and in the snowlight her face did the thing it still did only rarely and only ever for Vess and the boy: it went completely undefended. No swordspeaker, no last oath-bearer, no woman made of sealed grief. Just Orsen, tired and warm and standing in the doorway of a home she'd built without meaning to.

"I don't know who I am anymore," she said—which sounded like a lament until you heard the wonder underneath it. "Every day I find out a little more. It's like waking up in stages."

"Aye, well." Vess clinked her mug against Orsen's. "Some of us could've told you that losing yourself is easy. It's the finding that's worth the fare."

Below them in the valley, the river ran dark and steady under the ice-fringed banks, carrying its old song down toward the sea. Upstairs, a floorboard creaked—Isandro, sleepless, probably reading by candle stub again. On the schoolhouse wall, the twin scripts caught the moonlight, and if you looked long enough at the First Tongue lettering, you could almost believe the words were moving, alive, waiting for morning and all the voices it would bring.

Vess drained her wine and leaned her shoulder against Orsen's, and together they watched the snow begin again, soft and unhurried, covering the roads that had once been escape routes and were now, simply, the way home.

From the upstairs window, a small voice drifted down—half-asleep, half-singing, practicing his letters in the old tongue—and both women looked up at once, and neither of them moved to go inside, because there was nowhere else in any of the realms either of them would rather be.

But far to the south, where the river met the drowned gate, the water had begun, very softly, to glow.
