## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The rain came to Vessengard the way all weather came to Vessengard: honestly, stupidly, without a single drop of magic in it.

Jonah Sandoval stood beneath the striped awning of her charm-stall and watched it fall, counting the seconds between flash and thunder out of habit she couldn't kill. Four. Five. Six. Slow storm, far off, nothing in it but water. She exhaled through her nose and went back to polishing a luck-knot carved from river birch, turning it under her cloth until the grain shone like something worth three coppers instead of one.

"Morning, Mistress Sandoval." Old Tam the fishmonger passed with his cart, nodding at her wares without slowing. "Any of those wards actually work?"

"Every one of them," Jonah said, "if you believe hard enough."

He laughed. Everyone laughed. That was the trick of it — sell people charms that did nothing, price them cheap, and laugh along with them, because in a city where magic had been outlawed for twelve years, a charm-seller who took herself seriously would be reported before her second week. Harmless trinkets for a harmless woman. The Chancellery's inspectors had walked past her stall eleven times now, and each time Jonah had smiled her plain, forgettable smile and shown them knots and beads and painted river-stones, and each time they'd moved on to the spice-row, where someone always had something worth finding.

She was nobody. She had made herself nobody with the patience of a jeweler setting stones, one small dull fact at a time, until the pile was high enough to hide behind.

Only her left hand disagreed.

Jonah flexed it inside its glove — good leather, well-oiled, the kind a woman wore against autumn chill and nothing more — and felt the old ache stir beneath the scar on her palm. The scar was a sigil burned into her skin by her own circle on the last night they'd met, a severance-mark, an exile-brand: *you are cut off, you are no one's, your power will sleep in you like a stone at the bottom of a well.* Twelve years, and it still woke on damp mornings with a low, itching hum, like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

She pressed it flat against the stall's counter and let the wood take some of the ache.

The market filled around her by degrees. Bakers' boys hauling trays of seed-rolls; a dyer arguing color into existence; two Chancellery clerks in dove-gray walking their slow circuit of the square, clipboards tucked under their arms, looking for the shape of trouble rather than trouble itself. In Vessengard, trouble had a shape these days. It knelt too long in prayer. It kept candles lit in windows on the old holy-days. It whispered names — the Drowned Lady, the Ember-Father, the Nine-and-Two — that decent citizens had agreed to forget.

*Prayer-crime*, the edicts called it. First offense, fine. Second, public penance. Third, the gray wagon.

Jonah sold a warding-knot to a nervous bride, a fertility charm to a woman who winked at her own joke about it, and a painted stone inscribed SAFE PASSAGE to a carter who gripped it like a talisman and paid double because she told him the river-gods liked the blue ones. Lies, all of it. Pretty lies. The only honest thing on her table was a jar of honey-candies she made herself, and those she gave away to children who looked cold.

It was a good life, if you didn't look at it too closely. A dry room above a cooper's shop. Enough coin. Rain that was only rain. She had spent twelve years learning to love the smallness of it, the way a prisoner learns to love the exact dimensions of her cell.

Then the widow came.

*

Jonah noticed her first as a problem of inventory: a woman standing at the edge of the stall too long, hands wringing a shawl gone thin at the elbows, not shopping so much as hovering. Middle-aged, gray coming in at the temples, cheeks raw from wind or weeping. The kind of customer Jonah had learned to gently move along, because grief bought nothing and lingered past closing.

"Something for you, mistress?" Jonah asked anyway, because the woman's eyes had a drowned quality she recognized from mirrors.

"My husband," the widow said. Her voice came out sanded down to almost nothing. "Fisher. Off the Saltreach, eight days back. They found his boat." She stopped. Started again. "There's no body. And there's—" She glanced at the dove-gray clerks across the square, lowered her voice until Jonah had to lean in to catch it. "They say I can't pray for him. Officially. But the temple-priests are all gone, and I don't know how to do it myself, and everyone says the gods are dead anyway, so what's the harm, but I keep thinking—"

"Mistress." Jonah kept her voice level, warm, bored. "I sell knots."

"I know." The widow's eyes were wet and terribly steady. "But you're the only person in this whole city who looks like she might know what a prayer is."

The scar on Jonah's palm flared — sudden, hot, unmistakable — and Jonah nearly dropped the knot she was holding.

That wasn't how it worked. That was never how it worked. The brand slept; it itched on bad nights; once a year, maybe, it dreamed at the edge of her sleep with voices she refused to name. It did not *flare*. It did not answer.

"River-stone," Jonah heard herself say. "Blue ones are for safe passage. Three coppers."

The widow stared at the stones. Then at Jonah. Something moved behind her face — not hope, exactly. Hope's exhausted cousin. She put three coppers on the counter, took the bluest stone, and held it in both hands the way you'd hold a bird you weren't sure was alive.

"How do I use it?"

"You don't." Jonah heard the words come out of her mouth and could not stop them. "You just talk. Like he can hear you. That's all a prayer ever was — talking to someone who might be listening. The stone's just so your hands have something to do."

It was the most true thing she'd said aloud in twelve years, and she said it in a market square patrolled by clerks with clipboards, and the widow's face crumpled with gratitude like Jonah had handed her bread in a famine.

"Oh," the widow breathed. "Oh, thank you. His name is Aldric. Aldric Fenner. If you—if anyone's listening—"

"*Mistress.*" Sharp now, sharper than Jonah intended. The clerks were thirty feet away and drifting closer, drawn by some clerkly instinct toward the sound of devotion. "Take your stone. Go home. Talk quietly."

The widow went. Jonah watched her cross the square, shoulders hunched, lips already moving around the name *Aldric, Aldric, Aldric*, and the scar on Jonah's palm sang like a wire in wind, low and rising, and every hair on her arms stood up beneath her sleeves.

No, she thought. No, no. It's memory. It's the rain. It's—

Across the square, above the widow's head, the air *bent*.

There was no better word for it. The rain slanted wrong for the space of half a breath, curving around the woman like water around a stone in a stream, and in that curve Jonah saw — felt — something vast and patient and *old* turn its attention, the way a whale turns beneath a swimmer, unseen but displacing everything. Pressure rolled through the square. The luck-knots on her stall rattled softly against each other. One of the clerks paused mid-stride, frowned at his clipboard as though the numbers had shifted, and moved on.

The widow walked home through rain that would not touch her, praying, and something in the drowned places beyond the world heard her, and *answered*, and Jonah Sandoval stood behind her little table of harmless lies with her heart slamming against her ribs and her sleeping power stretching, for the first time in twelve years, like a body waking in the dark.

The pressure eased. The rain fell straight again. Somewhere out over the Saltreach, a fisher named Aldric Fenner — drowned or not, she'd know soon enough — drifted on a sea where an ancient thing had just been reminded that mortals still spoke its name.

Jonah sat down slowly on her stool.

Her hands were shaking. Inside the glove, the scar pulsed in time with her heartbeat, and beneath the pulse, faint as a knock through a wall, she could feel it: attention. Not hers. Not human. Directed, curious, *awake* — turned toward the little stall in the corner of Copperlane Market where a woman sold knots that did nothing, because one of them had accidentally been true.

"Well," Jonah said to no one, very quietly. "That's going to be a problem."

She closed the stall early.

*

Her room above the cooper's shop had a window facing west, over the rooftops toward the river, and Jonah sat on its sill with the shutter cracked and her glove off, studying her palm in the last gray light.

The scar looked the same as it always had: a closed circle bisected by a single stroke, fine as a hairline crack in glaze. The covenant-mark. Every member of her circle had worn one, cut into the palm at their binding, when thirteen young storm-callers and tide-speakers and ember-tenders had sworn themselves to the old compact in defiance of Chancellor Vael's edicts. *We hold the door open*, they'd sworn. *Neither realm locked away from the other.* Nine years ago, on a night of fire she still could not think about with her eyes fully open, that circle had burned. Two dead. The rest scattered. Herself branded, exiled, hunted for a season before the hunt lost interest and forgot her.

The Chancellery called it the Cinderhill Incident. Arson by unlicensed practitioners, tragically contained. The official history fit in a paragraph.

Jonah's version didn't fit anywhere, which was why she'd spent twelve years making sure she stayed too small to need one.

Now she turned her palm to the fading light, and the scar was doing something it had never done. Faintly, in lines of pale gold visible only when the light hit sideways, the circle was filling in. Stroke by stroke. As though something on the other side were tracing it from underneath her skin, patient as frost growing on glass.

She snatched her hand shut. Opened it again. The gold lines remained, delicate, incomplete, waiting.

"This isn't happening," she informed her palm.

Her palm continued happening.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Over the western rooftops the sky had cleared to a hard violet, and there — low, fat, one day shy of full — hung the moon. Ordinary. Silver. Except that Jonah, who had once been trained by people who knew the heavens, found herself counting without meaning to.

Twelve turns. The moon had made twelve full cycles since winter's start, and the almanacs — the ones printed before the ban, the ones Pell Wicke down at the observatory used to mutter about over cheap tea — said the year held twelve and no more. But the moon in the violet sky looked wrong to her tonight, subtly wrong, the way a face looks wrong when someone you know has died and you haven't heard yet. Too present. Too attentive. Hanging in the sky like a lamp hung in a window to tell someone *come home.*

Beneath her, in the street, a drunk was singing an old hymn to the Drowned Lady, badly, and no one was stopping him. Last month they'd have stopped him. Jonah listened to the ragged tune trail off into laughter and silence, and felt the city shifting beneath her feet like a floor tilting by fractions too small to see.

Prayers being answered. Scars filling in. Moons keeping count of something. After twelve years of nothing, the world had decided, all at once, to begin again — and it had begun with a widow and a blue river-stone and *her*, of all the tired hiding people in Vessengard.

Jonah pulled the glove back on, finger by finger, and pressed her marked palm flat against her knee.

One answer, she told herself. One bent breath of rain. It doesn't mean anything. You close the stall tomorrow, you sell your knots, you stay small, and whatever is waking up out there wakes up without you.

The scar hummed against her knee, warm as a coal wrapped in wool.

And then, through the shutter-crack, across the whole width of the darkening city, Jonah heard it — not with her ears, not exactly, but in the same place the thunder-counting lived, the place she'd boarded shut and named *nothing*: a voice like deep water moving under ice, vast and slow and unmistakably pleased.

*"Thank you for the door, little keeper."*

The moon rose another finger-width. In the morning, an astronomer with ink-stained fingers would come pounding on her stall-shutter with news that would end her quiet life forever.

But that night, Jonah Sandoval sat alone in her narrow room above the cooper's shop, glove clenched over a burning palm, and understood with perfect clarity that her hidden life had just cracked straight down the middle —

and that something ancient, patient, and grateful was already reaching through.
