## 2. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The knock came at the worst hour of night, which in Jonah's experience was the only hour astronomers knocked.

She had been counting her coin on the counter of the closed shop, a candle burning low beside her, when the tapping started — three quick raps, a pause, three more, like a bird pecking at glass. She blew out the candle first. Old habit. Then she crossed the dark shop floor and put her eye to the shutters.

A thin man stood in the street, wrapped in a coat too large for him, holding a lantern he hadn't lit. His hair stood up in gray tufts as if he'd been pulling at it for hours, and his eyes were enormous behind smudged spectacles. Jonah knew his face from the market square — Pell Wicke, the astronomer who set the city's almanacs, the man who could tell farmers when to plant by the angle of a shadow. Harmless. Officially harmless, anyway, which in Vessengard was the only kind of harmless there was.

"Master Wicke," she said through the shutter gap. "My shop is closed."

"I know." His voice was tight, breathless. "I know it's closed. I need it to be closed. I need everything about this to be closed, Mistress Sandoval, because if I'm wrong I'd very much like to be wrong in private."

Jonah's hand went still against the wood. Twelve years of hiding had taught her the sound a voice makes when it carries something heavy. She had heard it from her own mouth once, the night she ran out of the covenant-circle's ashes with her palm smoking.

"You've mistaken me for someone who cares about the sky," she said.

"No," Pell said simply. "I haven't mistaken you for anything. That's rather the problem."

* * *

She let him in because of that sentence, and hated herself a little for it. He sat on the stool across from her counter, coat dripping meltwater onto her clean floor, and produced from inside it a leather tube sealed with plain wax. From the tube he drew a long strip of paper covered in inked circles — moon-charts, she realized. Dozens of them, months of them, drawn with obsessive care.

"Do you know how many turns the moon should make in a year?" he asked.

"Twelve," Jonah said. "Everyone knows twelve."

"Twelve," Pell agreed. "Twelve full turns, one per month, since before the Chancellery was founded, since before the ban, since before anyone alive was born. The moon is the one thing in Vessengard that never lies. We outlawed magic, we outlawed prayer, we outlawed half the alphabet in old inscriptions, but the moon kept its schedule, and so we let it stay." He unrolled the chart across her counter, weighting the corners with her charm-tins. "Three nights ago, it finished its twelfth turn. It should have gone dark for the intercalary rest — the empty days, the thirteenth month that exists only on paper. Instead—"

He turned the chart around so she could see. The final circle wasn't dark. It was waxing. A thin silver crescent, inked fresh, still glistening wet at the edge.

"It's turning again," Pell said. "A thirteenth turn. In a single year. It has never happened. Not once in four hundred years of records."

"Then your instruments are wrong."

"My instruments are perfect. My eyes are better than my instruments. Go look out your own window, Mistress Sandoval. Look up. Tell me what you see."

She didn't want to. She went anyway, and lifted the shutter, and looked up through the gap between the rooftops, and there it was: a crescent moon where there should have been nothing at all, hanging in the cold black like a fingernail-paring of light, thin and new and utterly impossible. Something in her chest went tight and cold, some animal instinct far older than her exile, older than the ban, whispering that the sky had just made an error and errors in the sky were never small.

"When did it last happen?" she asked quietly.

Pell began rolling up his charts with hands that weren't quite steady. "That's the interesting part. Or the horrifying part. There are references. Old ones, pre-Chancellery, in temple annals my predecessors were ordered to burn. A chronicle fragment survived in a private collection — I paid a disgraceful amount of money for a look at it. It describes a thirteenth turn occurring twice in recorded history." He swallowed. "Both times in the years immediately preceding a covenant war. Both times, the chronicler wrote the same phrase."

Jonah turned from the window. "What phrase?"

"'The gods are counting again.'"

The candle smoke hung between them. Somewhere down the street a dog barked once and thought better of it.

"That's nonsense," Jonah said. "There are no gods. There's no covenant anymore. The Chancellery spent twelve years making sure of that. The temples are museums. The prayers go nowhere. I would know."

"Would you?" Pell asked, and it wasn't cruel, only tired, and terribly observant. "You sell charms, Mistress Sandoval. Little brass suns, knotted cords, river-stones painted with protection signs. Trinkets. I've watched you sell them. You always tell people they're nothing. You always tell them exactly what they are. And yet last week, when the widow Corrin bought a cord for her sick daughter, you charged her nothing, and you held it a moment longer than you needed to, and your lips moved."

"I was blowing dust off it."

"Were you."

Jonah looked at him for a long moment. The scar on her palm — the storm-scar, the exile-brand, hidden under a glove every waking hour for twelve years — pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat remembering itself. She pressed it flat against her apron.

"What do you want from me, astronomer?"

"Nothing," Pell said. "I want to give you something. A warning, freely given, from one person who pays attention to things everyone else has agreed not to see. The Chancellery received my report yesterday. Standard procedure — all celestial anomalies go to the Office of Records. I wrote it plainly, with all the numbers, expecting it to be filed and forgotten like every anomaly of the last decade." He laughed, a small broken sound. "It wasn't filed. Within hours, Chancellery officers were at the annals archive. By morning they'd requisitioned the private collection I told them about. And tonight—" he nodded toward the window, toward the dark streets beyond, "—tonight they're posting new edicts. Have you seen them?"

"I don't read edicts."

"You should read this one. They're calling it the Edict of Quiet Skies. Any citizen found praying aloud in public, keeping unauthorized devotional objects, or gathering for purposes of worship is to be arrested as a—" he paused, tasting the word like something rotten, "—a prayer-criminal. The penalties are indefinite detention. The Chancellor himself signed it. Isandro Vael. The beloved one. The merciful one." Pell's mouth twisted. "He gave a speech at dusk. I heard it from the observatory steps. He said the thirteenth moon is a hoax, a superstition, a trick of agitators — and then he doubled the watch on every temple ruin in the city. Hoaxes don't get guards, Mistress Sandoval. Only truths do."

* * *

They walked out together near dawn because Pell refused to stay under her roof a moment longer than necessary — a courtesy she understood; whatever came for him shouldn't find her name in the same room. The streets of Vessengard were gray and damp, lamplighters snuffing their wicks, fish-carts rumbling toward the river market. Ordinary. Deliberately ordinary. But Jonah saw it now, the way you see a crack in a wall once someone has pointed at it: the way people walked faster past the old temple row. The way two Chancellery officers stood at the corner of Candlemaker's Lane with a stack of fresh-posted bills and a length of rope coiled on the cobbles beside them, and the rope was not for tying packages.

One of the bills fluttered loose and skidded to her feet. She picked it up before she could stop herself.

BY ORDER OF CHANCELLOR ISANDRO VAEL:

In this season of celestial confusion, the Chancellery reminds all loyal citizens that the gods are silent, the covenant is ended, and prayer is a door best left unopened. Persons found knocking upon that door — aloud, in company, or in writing — shall be detained until the sky resumes its proper order.

Until the sky resumes its proper order. As if the sky were a servant who had wandered off and could be scolded back to its post.

"They're afraid," Pell murmured beside her. "That's the thing nobody understands about the Chancellery. All this power, all these laws, and underneath it they're terrified. Because if the gods wake up, then everything the Chancellor built stands on a foundation that starts moving. And men like Isandro Vael cannot bear moving foundations."

"How do you know what men like Isandro Vael can bear?"

Pell adjusted his spectacles. "I don't. I know numbers and I know light. But I've been reading the old annals all week, and there's a pattern in them, and patterns don't lie even when people do. Before both covenant wars, the same sequence: the moon turns wrong, then the prayers start being answered — badly, wildly, by powers half-asleep and starving — and then the rulers begin arresting the faithful. Not because the faithful are dangerous. Because answered prayers are evidence. And evidence, in a state built on denial, is the most dangerous contraband there is." He looked at her sideways. "The arrests will escalate. The answered prayers will escalate. And somewhere in the middle of it, people like you—"

"People like me are charm-sellers," Jonah said flatly. "Trinkets. Nothing. You said it yourself."

"I said your lips moved."

Down the lane, the officers had begun hammering a bill to the temple-row gate — the ruined gate of the old Covenant House, whose carved thirteen figures had been chiseled faceless a decade ago. The hammering echoed off the stone. Each strike landed like a knock on a door, and Jonah felt, absurdly, horribly, that somewhere on the other side of that door, something was listening. Something that had waited twelve years in the dark, counting.

Her scar pulsed again. Stronger this time. Warm, almost. Under her glove, the skin prickled with the memory of static before lightning, the taste of copper at the back of her throat, the feeling she had sworn off forever and dreamed about anyway, most nights, without meaning to.

"Mistress Sandoval." Pell had gone pale. He was looking at the sky over the eastern rooftops, where the false dawn was rising — and above it, faint as breath on glass, the impossible crescent still hung, refusing to set, refusing to be reasonable. "The intercalary days. The empty days of the thirteenth month. In the old reckoning they belonged to no god and every god — a hinge between years, a time outside time. If the moon is turning through them now..." He trailed off, and didn't finish, and didn't need to.

If the moon is turning now, then the hinge is swinging open.

Jonah folded the edict into quarters and put it in her pocket, though every instinct screamed at her to drop it, to walk away, to keep her head down and her glove on and her lips still. Twelve years of careful nothing. A shop, a routine, a name that fooled no one who mattered. She had built a life the size of a coin-purse and learned to live inside it.

But the moon kept turning, silver and stubborn, over a city that had outlawed wonder. And the officers' hammer kept knocking, knock-knock-knock, on a door that had already opened somewhere below the world.

"Pell," she heard herself say, and her voice sounded strange to her, lower, steadier, like a voice she'd buried. "This collection you paid to see. The chronicle fragment. Did it say where the covenant sigil was broken?"

Pell blinked. "Why would you—"

"Did it say where?"

"...It mentioned the fen," he said slowly. "The Godsfen. Why?"

Jonah looked at her gloved right hand, at the pulse beneath the leather, patient as a tide.

"Because I think," she said, "someone left something there for me to find. And I think the moon isn't the only thing that's counting."
