## 3. What the River Kept

The aqueduct had been dying for a century before anyone thought to live in its corpse.

Jonah followed the old pilgrim road out of Vessengard's Saltgate ward at first light, when the fog off the river was still thick enough to hide her face from the lamplighters and the early fish-carts. The road ran along the Vessen's north bank, past tanneries whose stink even the fog couldn't smother, past the drowned orchards where the river had claimed the low fields two generations ago and never given them back. And there, rising out of the mist like the spine of some enormous buried animal, the aqueduct.

It had carried water from the hills to the temple district once, back when the city still had temples worth watering. Now it carried nothing but pigeons and moss and, if Brannoc's directions could be trusted, one disgraced keeper of a god who no longer answered.

She climbed the weed-choked stair where the third arch had collapsed, boots slipping on stone that wept green. Below her, through gaps in the arches, she could see the shanty village that had grown up in the aqueduct's shadow — lean-tos of driftwood and salvaged tile, cook-smoke threading upward, a dog barking at nothing. The river people. The ones Vessengard had decided not to see, same as it had decided not to see her.

Brannoc lived at the far end, under the widest arch, where the stone had cracked and let the sky in.

Jonah stood at the top of the stair longer than she meant to. Her hand — her right hand, the one with the scar — ached the way it always did when she came this close to the river. Twelve years ago they had walked her down to the water's edge, six elders of the Chancellery in their grey robes, and the storm-scar on her palm had been pressed against the wet stones while the words of severance were read aloud. *You are unmade from the covenant. You are unwritten.* She remembered the cold. She remembered thinking, absurdly, about the charm stall she'd left unattended.

She hadn't come back to the river since. Not once. She had crossed bridges, taken the long way around, paid ferrymen extra to use the east landings. A stupid superstition for a woman who sold lucky knots and claimed to believe in nothing.

*The gods are waking,* Pell Wicke had said last night, his maps spread across her counter, his hands shaking too badly to hold them flat. *And whatever woke them knows your name.*

So here she was. Climbing down into the place where she'd been erased.

The arch at the end of the line was hung with wind-chimes made of broken glass and fishbone, dozens of them, and they sang softly as she ducked beneath them — a sound like rain starting. Beyond them the space opened up: an old shrine, half-swallowed by the aqueduct's bones. Someone had kept it clean. That was the first thing she noticed, before the man, before anything else. The flagstones were swept. The little basin carved into the rock still held fresh water, and someone had floated a single white flower on its surface, though no flowers grew within a mile of this place except the stubborn marsh-lilies downstream.

Brannoc sat by a small fire, mending a net.

He was bigger than she remembered. Or perhaps he had simply been a distant figure then, one of the temple-keepers who stood at the edges of ceremonies, robed and silent, and now he was a large old man in a patched coat with hands like knotted rope and a beard gone fully grey. He didn't look up. He said, "You walk like someone trying not to be heard. Bad news, coming down my stair."

"You heard me coming?"

"I hear everything." He set down the net. "That's what twelve years alone does. Sit down, Jonah Sandoval, before your knees give out. You've been standing in my doorway working up courage for the better part of a minute."

She sat on the bench across the fire. It creaked under her. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and river-mud and something older underneath, something mineral, like the inside of a mountain. Like temples. Her chest tightened unexpectedly.

"You knew I'd come," she said.

"I hoped." He fed a stick to the fire. "Hoping is different. Hoping is all I've done for twelve years, so I've gotten good at it." His eyes were pale, almost colourless, and they held hers without flinching. "Pell Wicke sent you."

"He sent a note. Under my door. Told me you were the only one who'd talk to me."

"Talk to you?" Something moved across the old man's face — anger, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Child, half the exiles in the Godsfen would walk barefoot to Vessengard for the chance. They think you're dead. Some of them pray for you. Did you know that? There are people who light candles for *you*, Jonah Sandoval, the storm-caller who vanished in the night the circle burned."

"I'm not a storm-caller anymore. I sell charms."

"Charms." He laughed, short and without any humour in it. "Lucky knots. Protection braids. Little glass eyes to hang over doors. And every one of them works, doesn't it? Just a little. Just enough that your customers keep coming back and you keep telling yourself it's coincidence."

Jonah said nothing. The fire popped.

"That's what I thought," Brannoc said gently. "Severance doesn't take it out of you, girl. It just takes away your permission. Power without permission — that's a dangerous thing to carry. Like a river behind a dam with cracks in it." He leaned forward. "Tell me why you're really here. Not Pell's reason. Yours."

She looked at her scarred palm. The mark was fainter than it used to be, a pale spiral like a fingerprint gone wrong, but it had been burning since the night of the widow's prayer — since the thing in the smoke above Candlemaker's Row had turned its attention toward the sound of a woman begging the dark for mercy, and *answered*. Since Jonah had felt something turn over in her chest, slow and vast, like a leviathan waking under ice.

"A widow prayed," she said. "Her husband died in the mill collapse. No body to bury. She bought a mourning-knot from me and asked me — not seriously, she didn't know what I was — she asked me if prayers did anything, in a city with no gods. And that night something came to her house. Left bread on her table. Left—" Jonah stopped. Her throat had closed. She started again. "Left her husband's wedding ring, polished clean, on the windowsill. Found it in the ash bucket where it had been lost since the collapse. Nobody could have—"

"The river keeps things," Brannoc said quietly. "That's the oldest teaching. Before the covenant, before the thrones, before names. The water remembers what we lose. And lately—" he glanced up, through the crack in the arch, at the thin morning sky — "lately it's been giving things *back*."

"Pell says the moon has begun a thirteenth turn."

"Pell Wicke sees far and understands half of it." Brannoc rose, joints cracking, and shuffled to the shrine basin. He touched the water with two fingers and drew something on the flagstone — a shape Jonah knew better than her own heartbeat. The covenant sigil. Thirteen points around a hollow centre, the geometry that bound mortal prayer to divine answer, drawn in water that refused to evaporate.

"When the covenant broke," he said, "the sigil shattered. Not metaphorically — physically. Thirteen shards, scattered across the realm, sunk into earth and water and bone. The gods didn't die, Jonah. Gods don't die. But they were locked outside their own house, hearing every knock and unable to open the door. Twelve years of listening to every prayer in the world go unanswered." His voice roughened. "Do you know what that does to a being that exists *to* answer? It drives them strange. It drives them hungry. And now something has cracked the seal, and they are pushing against the door, and they do not care whether the door is open gently or torn off its hinges."

"The Chancellery is arresting prayer-criminals," Jonah said. "They took a candle-seller off Lantern Street yesterday. For selling *candles*."

"Because Chancellor Vael knows exactly what's happening, and he is terrified of it." Brannoc looked at her across the fire, and there was something in his expression she couldn't name yet — pity folded around something harder. "Which brings us to the part you came for, though you don't know you came for it. The part about your exile."

Cold slid down her spine. "What about it?"

"Sit still," he said, and it wasn't unkind, but it wasn't negotiable either. "Twelve years ago, your circle — nine of you, storm-callers and tide-readers and one flame-dancer, meeting in the cellar of the old meetinghouse on Bellwether Lane — applied for covenant recognition. A renewal rite. Do you remember?"

"I remember everything." The words came out flat. "We filed the petition. We waited. We practiced the rites in secret because public practice was already restricted. And then one night the Chancellery came, and the cellar burned, and four of us ran, and two of us didn't make it out." She swallowed. "I never learned who informed. The others scattered. I was the only one caught, because I went back for Maren's daughter's christening-braid. Stupid. Sentimental."

"Not stupid. Loyal." Brannoc's voice dropped. "Here is what the river kept, Jonah, and what I have carried alone for twelve years because I had no one to give it to. The petition never reached the Chancellery's review board. It went straight to the Chancellor's private desk. Isandro Vael read it himself. And he wrote on it, in his own hand — the clerk who copied the file told me this on his deathbed, and swore me to silence until the seal weakened — he wrote: *"Let them gather. Let them commit themselves fully. Then we will have all of them at once."*

The fire seemed very loud.

"They weren't arresting a conspiracy," Jonah said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "There was no conspiracy. We were nine people who wanted to pray legally—"

"They were building a case. A spectacular one. Nine unlicensed practitioners gathered in defiance of the ban — enough to justify the total prohibition. Enough to scare the city for a generation. The ban on magic, the destruction of the temples, the severance rites — none of it was fear, child. Fear is what he *sold*. It was ambition. Isandro Vael wanted a godless city because a godless city has a vacancy at the top, and he intended—" Brannoc stopped himself, jaw tight. "Some things I will not say until I am certain. But your exile was engineered. Your circle was bait. And the fire that killed Tam and Ellery was lit by men carrying Chancellery oil, on the order of the man the whole city loves."

Jonah discovered she was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. The scar on her palm had gone from ache to burn, and somewhere far above, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky.

"Careful," Brannoc said quietly. "Permission-less power. Breathe."

She breathed. In through the nose, out through the mouth, the way Maren had taught her when she was eleven and lightning had jumped to her fingers during a tantrum. In. Out. The thunder faded, grumbling, cheated.

"Why tell me this now?" she asked, when she could trust her voice. "Why not twelve years ago? Why not warn us?"

"Who would have believed me? A disgraced keeper, living under an aqueduct, accusing the most beloved man in Vessengard?" He shook his head. "And I did try, after. I sent word to the surviving members of your circle. Two letters came back unopened. One came back burned. I think—" his gaze flicked to her scarred palm — "I think whoever received the third letter forwarded it to the Chancellery. Which is how I ended up here instead of in the temple district."

One of the survivors had sold him out. One of the people who had run from the fire beside her. Jonah felt the old wound tear open along seams she hadn't known were there. Four survivors. Herself, Maren, Dossa, and Kel. She had spent twelve years telling herself that whoever informed had been coerced, terrified, forgiven-by-necessity. She had built her whole quiet life on that foundation: *we were victims together; whoever broke, broke because they had to.*

"And now," Brannoc said, "the moon is turning wrong, the gods are pressing at the door, and Isandro Vael stands at the top of a city with no divine authority above him. Ask yourself, Jonah Sandoval, what a man like that does when the gods come back."

"He fights them."

"He *becomes* them." The old man said it simply, terribly. "But that is a worry for another day."

He turned to the shrine. From beneath the basin's lip he drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapped it with the reverence of a man handling relics — which, Jonah realized, he was — and held out what lay inside.

A shard of stone the size of her thumb. Pale, almost translucent, veined with lines that might have been writing in a language older than language. It hummed. Not audibly — she felt it in her teeth, in the scar tissue of her palm, in the place behind her sternum where the storm lived.

"One of the thirteen," Brannoc said. "Torn from the sigil when it broke. The river gave it up to me three winters ago, wedged in the roots of a drowned alder. I've waited ever since for the bearer it would choose."

"I'm not a bearer. I'm not anything. I'm severed—"

"You're the last storm-caller of the old circle," Brannoc said. "And the shard has been warm since you walked under my chimes. Look at your hand."

She looked. The spiral scar on her palm had changed. The faint lines had darkened, sharpened, and at their centre a new mark was surfacing like a bruise coming up through skin — a tiny perfect echo of the sigil's geometry, thirteen points around a hollow heart.

"It recognizes you," Brannoc said. "Take it. Though understand what you're taking. Each fragment carries its burden, child. This one carries *memory*. It will show you things the river kept. Some of them you'll wish it hadn't."

Jonah reached out. Her fingers trembled. The moment her skin touched the shard, the world tilted — just for a heartbeat — and she was standing in a cellar on Bellwether Lane, lamplit, nine voices raised in a renewal hymn, and someone at the top of the stairs was sliding the bolt home from the *outside*, and the smell of lamp-oil came down the steps ahead of the flames—

She gasped and pulled her hand back. The vision snapped shut. Brannoc steadied her elbow without comment.

"Now you know how it greets you," he said. "Wrap it well. Keep it against your skin. And Jonah — go home, bar your door, and decide quickly what kind of person you intend to be. Because Isandro Vael is going to summon you soon. Men like him always want the loose ends presented to them politely before they cut them."

"Summon *me*? I'm nobody. I'm a charm-seller—"

"You are the last piece of evidence of his crime, walking around with a pulse," Brannoc said. "He pardoned worse to bury quieter secrets. Be ready."

She wrapped the shard in the oilcloth, tucked it inside her coat against her ribs, where it beat against her heart like a second, stranger pulse. At the foot of the collapsed stair she paused and looked back. Brannoc had taken up his net again, mending, as if nothing had happened, as if the world were not tilting on its axis. But he spoke without looking up, his voice carrying easily across the shrine.

"The flower in the basin," he said. "Marsh-lily. It washed up against my step the morning after your widow found her ring. The river sends them when it wants a debt witnessed." He finally raised his pale eyes to hers. "Someone upstream is watching over you, Jonah Sandoval. Pray it stays friendly."

* * *

She didn't go straight home.

Her feet took her the long way, along the towpath, past the drowned orchards, and she let them, because the alternative was her shop and her shutters and the small safe life that Brannoc had just pried open like a crate. The shard pulsed against her ribs with every step. Fragments of vision leaked through its wrapping whether she willed it or not: rain on a temple roof. Maren laughing, younger, both hands full of lightning. A grey-robed clerk's deathbed whisper. The bolt sliding home.

Nine of them. She counted them again, the way she had every year on the anniversary, the way she counted the dead of other disasters. Tam. Ellery. Maren. Dossa. Kel. Herself. And the three youngest — Ovi, Saret, and—

The name wouldn't come.

She stopped walking. That was wrong. She knew the name; she had always known the name; she had dreamed it often enough in the early years, waking with tears on her face and no memory of why. She cast backward through her memory of the circle, the nine faces around the lamplit table, and found eight, and a ninth chair, and a smooth blank space where a face should be.

The shard warmed against her ribs. *Memory*, Brannoc had said. *It carries memory.*

"What did you take?" she whispered. "What did they take?"

The river slid past, brown and patient, keeping its secrets. Somewhere behind her a bell began to toll from the direction of the Chancellery tower — not the hour bell, not the alarm. The summons bell, the one they rang when the Chancellor wished to address the city. Jonah stood on the towpath with her hand pressed to her coat and listened to it roll across the water, nine strokes, ten, eleven.

Twelve.

Then a pause, long enough that she thought it finished — and a thirteenth stroke, deep and wrong, a note that no bell in Vessengard should have been able to make, a note that she felt in the shard and in her scar and in her teeth.

Across the river, the whole city fell silent.

Then, faint and terrible, from a thousand windows and doorways, people began to pray.
