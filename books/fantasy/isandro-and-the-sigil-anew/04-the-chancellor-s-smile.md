## 4. The Chancellor's Smile

The pardon came on a Tuesday, nailed to every door in the Copperlace like a proclamation and a promise at once.

Jonah read it three times before her hands stopped shaking enough for her to fold it.

*By order of Chancellor Isandro Vael, in his mercy and his wisdom: let it be known that Jonah Sandoval, twelve years exiled under the Edict of Ash, is hereby restored to the protection of Vessengard. Let no hand be raised against her. The Chancellor extends his personal invitation to dine at the Chancellery on the evening of the ninth.*

No reason given. No crime named — because there had never been a trial, only a sentence, only fire. And now, no explanation for the reversal. Just his name at the bottom, inked in that elegant, narrow script she had seen once before, by torchlight, through smoke.

She burned her copy in the shop's cold stove without lighting it first — just crumpled it into ash with her bare fingers, which was stupid, which left grey smears on the scarred palm she spent her life hiding — and then stood in the middle of her little shop surrounded by harmless tin charms and dried lavender and felt the walls come closer.

"He knows," she said to the empty room. "He has always known."

The bells of the Chancellery rang the hour, and somewhere above the rooftops, faint as a hairline crack in glazework, the moon hung wrong in the afternoon sky.

* * *

Sefa found her that night, because Sefa found everything, including things Jonah hadn't lost yet.

"You're packing," the thief said from the doorway, where she materialized the way she did, all elbows and shadow, a satchel already over one shoulder as if she'd been invited. She hadn't been. She never was.

"I'm not packing."

"You're holding a sock full of silver." Jonah looked down. She was. "And you've got that face. The one like a woman doing sums she doesn't like the answer to."

Jonah set the sock down. "The Chancellor pardoned me."

"I heard. Half the city heard. They're calling it *the Mercy*, capital M, like it's a festival." Sefa dropped onto the counter among the charm displays, scattering a wind-chime of tin fish, unrepentant. "Twelve years of the Edict of Ash, people hanged for whispering to their dead mothers, and he pardons a storm-caller the same week prayer-crimes start getting arrests? That's not mercy, Jonah. That's theater."

"Then why am I in the play?"

"Because you're the best actor they could cast." Sefa's flippancy thinned; beneath it, something older and warier surfaced, the look of someone who had grown up reading rooms for exits. "He wants you where he can see you. Or he wants the city to see him forgive you. Either way, if you don't show up to his dinner, you're an insult. If you do—" she shrugged, "—you're a guest. In a house where guests don't leave."

"You've been to the Chancellery?"

"Once." A flicker crossed her face, quick as a pickpocket's fingers. "Didn't care for the architecture."

Jonah almost laughed. Almost. It came out as breath, nothing more. She turned to the window, where Vessengard lay in its usual lamplit orderliness, the most obedient city in the realm, and thought about Pell Wicke's maps and his terrible arithmetic: thirteen turns, twelve months, a sky learning to count past its own nature. Thought about Brannoc under the aqueduct, pressing the sigil shard into her hand like a man handing off a live coal. *He'll come for you gently,* the old keeper had said. *That's how he takes things. Gently. You'll thank him while your pockets empty.*

"I have to go," Jonah said.

"I know." Sefa swung her legs off the counter. "That's why I'm coming. Someone has to count the knives."

* * *

The Chancellery had been a temple once. Everyone knew this the way everyone knew the river ran backward through the fen — as a fact too large and old to be useful. Before the covenant wars, before the Edict, the building had been the Hall of Thirteen Voices, its rotunda open to the sky so the gods could look down on the mortal assembly. Isandro Vael had roofed it. Glass now, beautiful glass, leaded and pale, so that the sky was still visible but only as something framed, contained, politely kept outside.

Jonah walked up the marble steps between rows of lanterns on the ninth evening and understood, with the clarity that terror sometimes grants, that the glass roof was a message. To her specifically. *I have closed heaven's eye over my own head. What do you imagine I can do to yours?*

An usher in dove-grey led her through corridors that smelled of beeswax and cold stone. Portraits lined the walls — Chancellors, all of them, going back generations, each rendered with the same serene authority. The newest portrait, near the great doors of the dining hall, was Isandro himself: younger than she expected, or perhaps exactly as young as she should have expected and refused to. Dark-haired, clean-featured, with the kind of face that made strangers confess things. The painter had given him a slight smile. It did not reach the painted eyes.

"Madam Sandoval." The voice came from behind her, warm and unhurried, and Jonah's body knew it before her mind did.

Her spine locked. Twelve years collapsed. She was twenty-three again, standing in a burning meetinghouse circle, watching her five friends' wards gutter, listening through the roar of the flames to a voice speaking calm instructions to men with irons — *not her, leave the storm-caller, she's the proof we need* — a voice pitched low and reasonable, the voice of a man ordering wine.

She turned.

Isandro Vael was not what the broadsheets promised. They drew him tall and stern, an oak of a man. In person he was slighter, softer around the eyes, dressed not in state black but in ordinary charcoal wool, as though he'd been interrupted at his own fireside. He looked tired in a genuine way, and kind in a way that was either genuine or the finest performance she had ever witnessed, and he smiled at her the way the portrait smiled.

"You came," he said. "I wasn't certain you would. I'm glad. I've wanted to meet you for a very long time."

"Chancellor." Her voice came out steady. She was proud of that, later, that it came out steady.

"Isandro, please. We're not formal here tonight. Dinner is just us — well, us and the staff, who are deaf by professional necessity." He gestured her through the doors.

The dining hall was small. That was the trap of it — she had braced herself for grandeur, for a table long enough to seat a council, and instead there was a round table set for two beneath the glass roof, two candles, a view of the moon sitting fat and wrong-eyed above the city. No guards in sight. No witnesses. Nothing about it said *power*. Everything about it said *you are already mine*.

"Wine?" He poured before she answered. "You'll forgive the presumption. I find most people drink when they're nervous, and I suspect you're the most nervous person in Vessengard tonight, though you hide it beautifully."

"Do I?"

"Mm. Your left hand keeps closing." His eyes flicked to it, courteous, precise. "The scar. May I ask — does it hurt?"

"It aches," Jonah said. "When storms come. When prayers get answered wrongly. It's been aching for a month."

Something moved behind his expression — satisfaction, maybe, or appetite — and was gone before she could name it. "Yes. The world is becoming noisy again. I hoped you might help me understand why."

He talked through the first course. That was his method, she realized quickly: he gave, and gave, and gave, information like gifts, each one slightly more intimate than the last, until the recipient forgot they were being mapped. He spoke of the prayer-criminal arrests with regret — "necessary, temporarily, the city panics without structure" — of the moon with scholarly concern — "Pell Wicke's observations are sound, whatever else may be said of the man's judgment; he fled, you know, the day after showing you those maps, which tells you something about what he thinks is coming" — of her exile itself, delicately, as though handling someone else's grief:

"The Edict was cruel. I won't defend it. Twelve years ago this city watched a covenant-circle burn and decided the safest response was to pretend power didn't exist. Fear makes blunt instruments of us all." He refilled her glass. "But I've read the record, Jonah. There was no trial. No evidence presented. Six mages, dead or scattered, and one girl exiled with a brand on her hand and her name struck from the rolls. Someone arranged that. Someone benefited."

"And who benefits now?" she asked quietly. "From pardoning me?"

His smile deepened by a degree. "Ah. Good. I was told you were clever. Brannoc still says so, when he's drunk enough to say anything."

The floor tilted. She kept her hands flat on the tablecloth and her face still, and hated him a little for making her do it, and hated herself more for the relief underneath the hatred — *Brannoc is alive, they haven't taken him yet* — and understood in the same instant that he had given her that relief deliberately, another gift, another hook.

"You know where he lives," she said.

"I know where everyone lives. That's not sinister, it's administration." He leaned back, candlelight moving on his face. "Jonah. May I tell you what I actually want? Not the rumor. Not the broadsheet version. The truth."

"I'd be astonished if you did."

That startled a laugh out of him — real laughter, brief and human, and the awful thing was how much she liked it, how some starved part of her responded to warmth after twelve years of keeping her head down. He saw that too. Of course he saw that.

"I want the same thing you want," he said. "For the gods to go back where they belong, and stay there. Quietly. Lawfully." He spread his hands. "They're waking, and they are *mad*, Jonah. Twelve years of silence and they wake up ravenous, answering prayers with plagues, granting wishes like a drunk with a sword. The mob in Saltrow prayed for rain last month and drowned forty sheep. A widow in the Copperlace asked for her husband back and got—" he paused, watching her, "—well. You know what she got. You were there."

The room went very quiet. The candles held their flames perfectly still, as if even fire was listening to him.

"You know about Marta," Jonah said.

"I know everything that happens in my city. That's not a boast. It's a burden." He rose, crossed to the tall window at the hall's end, and stood looking up through the glass at the swollen moon. "The Chancellery has records older than anyone remembers. From the first covenant war. Do you know what started it? Not gods run mad. *People.* People who found the gods waking and tried to seize them — to hold divine power alone, hoarded, unshared. Every account ends the same way. The holder burns. The world burns worse."

"That sounds like a warning," Jonah said.

"It is. It's the truest thing I know." He turned back to her, and for one unguarded moment — she would spend years deciding whether it was real — he looked exhausted beyond measure, a man carrying a city on his back and feeling it crush him slowly. "I have spent twelve years building a wall between Vessengard and that fire. Now the wall is cracking and I need allies who understand what's on the other side. You called a storm last week to save a tenement. I've seen the reports. Controlled. Precise. *Gentle.* Do you know how rare gentleness is, in someone with your gift?"

"And if I refuse?"

"Refuse what? I haven't asked for anything yet." He smiled again. "Dinner first. Alliances later. Go home, Jonah Sandoval. Walk freely. Buy lamp oil without flinching. Sleep in a bed instead of behind a barricade. Consider whether you'd rather fight the coming storm beside me — or simply be weather in it."

A servant appeared at the door, silent as furniture. The audience, such as it was, was over.

At the threshold Jonah stopped. She couldn't help it. Twelve years she had carried that voice through her nightmares, and she needed to hear it one more time, stripped, to be sure.

"Chancellor," she said. "One question. The night the Hall of Embermeet burned — six mages died there. My circle. I escaped with my life and nothing else. Whoever betrayed them knew our wards, our signals, our names." She turned. "Where were you that night?"

The pause lasted exactly as long as a heartbeat.

"In bed," Isandro Vael said pleasantly. "Like any sensible citizen."

And he wished her goodnight, and the usher walked her out through the portraits, and it was only on the steps, under the lanterns, breathing the cold honest air, that Jonah finally placed the detail she had been reaching for all evening — the thing her skin had known since the first word.

It wasn't his face. It wasn't the name. It was that he paused, always, half a beat before saying a person's name — *Jonah* — as though tasting it. As though names were things he collected.

The voice in the burning circle had done that too. *Leave the storm-caller. She's the proof we need.*

She stood on the Chancellery steps until the cold worked through her coat, and then she walked, fast, toward the aqueduct, because Brannoc was alive but alive wasn't safe, and because there was a shard of broken covenant in her pocket that had begun, faintly, steadily, to hum against her hip like a second heart.

Behind her, the glass roof of the Chancellery caught the moonlight, and the moon above it completed another sliver of its impossible thirteenth turn.
