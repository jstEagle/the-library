## 5. A Thief with Clean Hands

Sefa had stolen from priests, princes, and one extremely drunk admiral, and she had a rule for all of them: never take anything that hums.

She'd learned that rule the hard way, at nine years old, in the ash-market of Corvane, when she'd lifted a votive coin off a dying man's chest and felt every prayer he'd ever whispered pour through her like cold water through a sieve. She'd cried for an hour afterward and hadn't been able to say why. The coin went back. The rule stayed.

So it was a bad sign — a very bad sign, professionally speaking — that the little shard of metal in the charm-seller's shop hummed like a struck bell only she could hear.

* * *

Sefa had been watching Jonah Sandoval for six days, which was five days longer than any mark deserved.

The shop was called Underhill's Odds & Ends, wedged between a dyer's and a shuttered bakery on Latchet Row, and on the surface it was exactly what it pretended to be: a clutter of tin amulets against headaches, knotted cords for safe travel, painted river-stones promising luck in love. Harmless junk. Vessengard was a magic-free city by law and by temperament; its citizens bought charms the way they bought umbrellas, as superstition with a receipt.

But Sefa's hands were not ordinary hands. That was the whole trouble of her life.

When she touched an object, she could feel what had been poured into it — not magic, exactly, not anymore, not since the gods had gone quiet twelve years ago. Residue. Echo. The grease left behind by old belief. A wedding ring carried forty years of devotion so thick it made her fingers ache. A murderer's knife had once burned her so badly she'd dropped it down a well. Most things were clean. Most people in Vessengard believed nothing hard enough to leave a stain.

Jonah Sandoval's entire shop was clean. Scrubbed clean. Every trinket on those shelves was exactly the worthless tin it appeared to be, and that, more than anything, told Sefa the woman was hiding something. Honest frauds didn't scrub their merchandise. Only people terrified of being caught selling something real took that kind of care.

And then there was the box under the counter.

Sefa had brushed past it on day three, letting her knuckles trail across the wood the way she did, casual as breathing. What came back through her skin had nearly buckled her knees at the counter.

It sang. Not loudly. Not the shriek of a relic or the burn of a cursed thing. It hummed like a tuning fork pressed to bone, like the moment before thunder when the air itself holds its breath, and underneath the hum — this was the part that kept her up nights — underneath it she'd felt something she hadn't felt since she was a child kneeling in a ruined temple while her mother pressed a broken icon into her palms: *presence*. Faint. Half-asleep. But looking back at her through the wood like an eye through a keyhole.

Divine residue, live and warm, in a city where divinity was contraband.

Sefa sat on the roof across from the shop that sixth evening, eating a stolen apple and watching lamplight move behind the shutters, and gave herself the speech she always gave.

You don't know what it is. You don't know who it belongs to. Things that hum are things that bite. Walk away.

She finished the apple, spat the seeds into the gutter, and started picking the lock on the shop's back door.

* * *

The lock was insultingly easy. That should have been her second warning.

Inside smelled of lamp oil, dried lavender, and rain — rain, though it hadn't rained in nine days, rain soaked so deep into the plaster it seemed the walls themselves remembered storms. Sefa moved the way she'd been taught in the ash-markets, heel-to-toe, weight on the outside edge of the foot, counting exits out of habit. Front door. Back door. Window over the workbench. One sleeping loft above, curtain drawn.

She found the box under the counter in four seconds flat.

It was ironwood, banded in copper gone green with age, locked with a mechanism older and better than anything else in the shop — and here was the strange thing, the thing she would turn over for years afterward: the lock wasn't locked. The tumblers hung open like a hand unclenched. As if the charm-seller had stopped believing anyone would come for it. Or as if some part of her wanted it taken.

*Sefa Vey does not steal gifts,* she told herself sternly, and picked it up anyway.

Through the copper banding, the humming sharpened into something almost articulate. Her gift had limits — she got flashes, impressions, the emotional sediment of objects, never words — but the shard inside that box pushed against those limits like a swimmer pushing toward light. She caught a fragment of sensation: a circle of joined hands. Fire. A voice saying a name — *her* name? someone's name — and then screaming, and then twelve years of silence like a held breath.

Sefa stood in the dark shop holding a piece of something enormous and felt, for one vertiginous moment, that she wasn't stealing it at all. She was retrieving it. That somewhere, a temple with no roof had a space shaped exactly like her hands.

That was how they got you, her mother used to say. Not with chains. With belonging.

The floorboard creaked behind her.

"I wouldn't," said Jonah Sandoval, "if I were you."

* * *

Later — much later, after the knife, after the shouting, after the entirely undignified business of being pinned to the floorboards by a charm-seller who turned out to have the reflexes of a cat and the grip of a dock-loader — Sefa would reconstruct the conversation in her head and be embarrassed all over again.

Because her opening line, pinned, winded, one arm twisted behind her, was: "This is entrapment."

"It really isn't," said Jonah.

"You left the box unlocked. That's bait. That's textbook bait."

"I left it unlocked because I'm tired and careless, and because nobody in this city has tried to rob me in eleven years." The grip tightened. "What did you touch?"

"The box. Just the box. Your tin garbage is safe, if that's what—"

"What did you *feel?*"

And there it was. Sefa knew that question. Knew it the way a stray knows the whistle meant for another dog. In Corvane, before the ban, before everything, the priests had asked it gently and the magistrates had asked it with pliers, and either way the answer had ended with her family scattered across three provinces like spilled seed.

"Nothing," she said. "I feel nothing. I'm just a thief. Ordinary hands."

Jonah was quiet for a long moment. Then she released Sefa's arm, stepped back, and turned her around — not roughly, but with the impersonal efficiency of someone checking goods — and looked at her palms.

Sefa's hands were, and had always been, unnervingly clean. No calluses, despite the trade. No scars, despite the life. Her mother had called them choir-girl hands and crossed herself laughing. Thieves' guilds in two cities had refused to believe she was one of theirs until they watched her work. Dirt didn't seem to know what to do with her; even now, held up to the lamplight, her palms showed faint lines and nothing else, smooth as a saint's statue.

"Oh," said Jonah softly. "Oh, you poor thing. You're a vessel."

"I'm a *what?*"

"Nothing. Forget I said it." But she hadn't let go of Sefa's wrist, and her thumb had moved — absently, unconsciously — across Sefa's pulse point, the way you check whether something is still alive. "How long have you been able to do it? Feel things?"

"Feel things," Sefa repeated. "I don't feel things. I'm a pickpocket. I feel purses."

"You flinched at my counter on Tuesday. You keep your sleeves pulled down in summer. You cross the street to avoid the shrine on Candlemaker's Lane even though it's been empty for a decade, and you flinch again when you pass the Chancellery because the stones there are *screaming* and only you can hear it." Jonah let go of her wrist. "I sell charms for a living, girl. I notice what people carry."

The silence stretched. Somewhere out on Latchet Row, a cart rattled past, and the lamplight swayed, and Sefa became aware that she was still holding the box, and that Jonah was watching her hold it, and that neither of them had reached for a weapon in almost a full minute.

"If I give this back," Sefa said slowly, "and walk out that door, are you going to report me to the wardens? Because prayer-crime's a hanging offense now, and I've seen the new notices. They're arresting people for *humming hymns.*"

"No."

"No? Just like that?"

"Just like that." Jonah held out her hand. "Give me the box, and get out, and don't come back. That's the deal. It's a good deal. Take it."

Here is the thing about Sefa Vey, and she would deny it under torture: she was lonely. Profoundly, structurally lonely, the loneliness of a person whose defining trait was untouchable — literally untouchable, because handshakes were agony, because lovers always eventually noticed that her attention drifted whenever she touched their rings or their lockets, reading their histories like letters not addressed to her, until intimacy became eavesdropping and they left. She'd built a whole self out of cleverness and distance. Steal and go. Never stay anywhere long enough for anyone to learn what her hands did.

So when Jonah Sandoval stood there offering her the exact exit she'd been given a hundred times before, Sefa heard herself say:

"What's in the box?"

"That's not the deal."

"What's *in the box,* charm-seller?" And before Jonah could stop her — before either of them quite understood what was happening — Sefa cracked open the ironwood lid with her thumb and put her bare palm flat against the shard inside.

* * *

The world went white and then went deep.

She was standing in a circle of seven people under a sky full of too many stars, and their hands were joined, and the power moving through the ring was like a river deciding to be music. She couldn't see faces. She could feel them: seven distinct presences, seven flavors of trust. And threaded through all of it, a covenant — not words, she couldn't hold words, but the *shape* of words, the architecture of a promise being made between mortal hands and something vast and patient on the other side.

Then fire. Then betrayal — she felt it arrive from inside the circle, which was the horror of it, which was the wound of it: the breaking coming not from without but from within, a seventh hand opening, a name spoken aloud to men with torches. Then falling. Then twelve years of silence pressing down like snowfall on a grave.

Then — faintly, at the very bottom of the vision, like a voice through a wall — the hum again. Patient. Waiting. *Not dead,* the shard said, in the wordless way shards speak. *Only buried.*

Sefa came back to herself sitting on the floor of the shop. She didn't remember sitting down. Jonah was crouched in front of her, gripping her shoulders, saying her name — except she didn't know Sefa's name, so she must have been saying *girl*, *hey*, *breathe* — and the shard was back in its box, and the box was closed, and Sefa's nose was bleeding freely onto her best shirt.

"You saw it," Jonah said. Her voice had changed. All the wariness had burned off it, and underneath was something rawer. "You saw the circle."

"I saw—" Sefa wiped her nose, stared at the blood on her fingers with detached professional interest. "I saw seven people make a promise. And I saw one of them break it. And I saw your face, charm-seller. Younger. Screaming."

Jonah went very still.

"They'll hang you for that," she said quietly. "Do you understand? If the Chancellery knew what you are — what you can touch, what you can see — they wouldn't bother with trial. Wicke the astronomer disappeared last month for less. You need to take the deal. Get out. Don't come back."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true!"

"And you keep standing there hoping I won't." Sefa got her feet under her, wobbled, used the counter to steady herself. Her heart was still slamming from the vision, and beneath the fear was the other thing, the worse thing, the thing she'd spent twelve years burying under technique and irony: *someone else knows what my hands are.* Someone else had looked at her palms without crossing themselves or reaching for irons. "You want to know why I really came back tonight? Sixth night watching your shop? It's not the shard. Well. It's partly the shard. It's that I passed the Chancellery on Monday and I heard the stones screaming, same as always, and then I heard something *answer.* From underneath. From far away. Like something turning over in its sleep."

Jonah's face did something complicated. "That's not possible. Nothing's answered in twelve years."

"Well, something's answering now. And whatever it is, it's waking up hungry, and it's waking up *loud,* and I am the only person in this godless city who can hear it coming — except apparently you, and apparently whatever's in that box." Sefa jabbed a finger at the ironwood. "So here's my counter-offer, charm-seller. I don't take the deal. I don't take the shard. I stick to you like a burr until you tell me what's happening, because whatever's coming for this city, I'd rather be next to the one person who already knows its name than alone in a garret pretending I'm just a thief."

"That is a terrible offer."

"I know."

"You'd be safer—"

"Everyone says that. Everyone's *always* said that." It came out sharper than she intended, edged with twelve years of it — safe, Sefa, run along, stay small, don't touch, don't listen, don't be what you are. "I was safe in Corvane until the ban. I was safe in the guild until the purge. Safe is a place other people put you right before they decide you're evidence." She wiped her nose again, chin up, bleeding and furious and aware she must look half-mad. "So. Are we partners, or are you going to throw me out?"

Jonah studied her for a long, long moment. Lamplight moved across her face, and Sefa watched her weigh it — watch her arrive, visibly, reluctantly, at the same conclusion Sefa had already reached: that two hidden things were worth less apart than together.

"One condition," Jonah said finally. "You never touch that box again without me in the room. Whatever you saw in there — it saw you back. That's how these things work. And if it starts calling you—"

"It already has," Sefa said, before she could stop herself.

The silence that followed had teeth.

"...What do you mean, already?"

"First time I brushed past the counter. Three days ago." Sefa flexed her clean, choir-girl fingers, and told the truth because lying suddenly seemed beside the point. "It hummed. Objects don't hum, charm-seller. Coins don't sing. But that shard did — like it recognized me. Like it's been waiting for somebody whose hands work this way. My mother used to say I had temple-hands. Said the gods made a few people like me, back when there were gods, to be their..." She trailed off. "Vessel. You said that word. Before. When you grabbed my wrists. What did you mean?"

Jonah didn't answer at once. She walked to the window instead, checked the street through the slats — old habits, Sefa noted, the habits of a hunted woman — and drew the shutter closed. When she turned back, she'd made some decision, and it settled over her features like weather.

"Sit down," Jonah said. "There's tea in the back, if the mice haven't had it. And I'm going to tell you a story about a circle of seven, and a chancellor, and a night twelve years ago when the gods stopped answering. And then you're going to tell me everything you heard at the Chancellery. Every word. Because if the old powers are stirring under Vessengard, and Chancellor Isandro Vael is arresting people for praying—"

She paused. Something crossed her face that Sefa's gift, for once, didn't need to read, because it was written plain enough for anyone: grief and fury, fused into one hard bright thing.

"—then the thief with clean hands and the exiled storm-caller are going to need each other. More than either of us knows."

Outside, far off toward the river, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky. Neither of them mentioned it. Both of them heard it.

Sefa found the tea. It was terrible. She drank it anyway, and stayed.
