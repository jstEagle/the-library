## 6. The God Who Answers Wrong

The prayer came through the floorboards.

Jonah felt it before she heard it — a tremor in the soles of her feet, like the first distant grumble of thunder rolling in off the Saltreach coast. She was crouched behind her stall at the end of another long day, counting coins that weren't worth counting, when the sensation crawled up through the cobblestones of Copperlane and into her bones: *someone is asking.*

She dropped a copper. It rang against the stone and rolled away into the gutter, and she let it go, because for twelve years nothing had come through the floorboards of Vessengard. For twelve years the city had been sealed tighter than a tomb against anything divine — no whispers, no stirrings, no warmth behind the old shrine-walls. The Chancellery had made sure of it. The Chancellery had made sure of *her*, too.

And now something was knocking.

"Jonah." The widow Marta stood at the edge of the stall, wringing her shawl in both hands. She was one of Jonah's regulars — bought a luck-charm every month whether she needed one or not, paid in small coin and smaller smiles. Tonight there was no smile. "You'll think I'm mad."

"Most of my customers are mad. It's why they buy charms."

"The well," Marta said. "Behind the tanneries. People have been going to it. Praying at it. And last night—" Her voice dropped to almost nothing, as if the word itself might be overheard and punished. "Last night my sister asked it for rain. For the crops. We've had no rain since midsummer, you know how it's been, the barley's dying in the fields. And this morning—"

"This morning?"

"It rained, Jonah. Only over our farm. A perfect circle of rain, half a mile wide, while the rest of the valley sat in sunlight. My sister swears she could see the edges of it. Like a bowl turned upside down over our land."

Jonah's hands went very still on the counter. Behind her, the storm-scar on her palm — hidden as always beneath a leather glove, the way she'd worn it every day for twelve years — began to itch.

"That's not possible," she said, and hated how thin her voice came out.

"I know." Marta clutched her arm suddenly, and her grip was stronger than a woman her age had any right to. "I know it isn't. But Jonah, everyone's going. Half of Copperlane is at that well right now, asking for things. And the things are *happening*. Old Tam asked for his lungs back and coughed up twenty years of black and walked out breathing like a bellows. The fisherwomen asked for full nets and the harbor's so thick with fish the gulls can't find the water. Everyone's asking. Everyone's getting."

"And what," Jonah said carefully, "did they ask for last? Just now, I mean. What's happening *right now*?"

Marta's face did something terrible. It went blank with fear, the fear of someone who has only just understood the size of what she's stepped in.

"The millers' guild," she whispered. "Their grain stores are rotting. Mice, damp, everything. So they all went down together, forty of them, and prayed to the well for the mice to be gone."

* * *

Copperlane smelled wrong before Jonah even reached it.

Not blood — not yet — but the sweet-rot stench of things dying in enormous numbers, layered under the ordinary smoke and tallow of the district. She pushed through streets that were emptying rather than filling, doors slamming, shutters banging closed, mothers hauling children inside with the particular urgency of people who have decided not to explain why.

The well stood in a little cobbled square behind the tanneries, ringed by a low wall that generations of children had used as a bench. Someone had draped it in white cloth — a bridal veil, maybe, or a burial shroud; with the old faiths outlawed, nobody remembered which was proper anymore. Candles guttered around its lip in defiance of a dozen Chancellery ordinances. The mob had mostly fled, but a few remained: a woman on her knees, rocking; two men arguing in fierce whispers; a boy of perhaps ten who simply stared at the ground and would not lift his eyes.

Because the ground was moving.

Mice poured out of the walls of Copperlane in a river of grey-brown fur. They came from under doorstones and up through cellar grates and out of the gaps between cobbles, thousands upon thousands of them, and they were not fleeing the district. They were *converging* — streaming toward the well like iron filings toward a lodestone, and where they met the low wall they climbed it, and at the top of the wall they stopped, and fell inward, into the dark.

"Gone," the kneeling woman was saying, over and over. "He asked for them to be gone. He didn't say *where*."

Jonah stood at the edge of the square with her heart hammering against her ribs and understood three things at once, each arriving like a separate blow.

First: the god in the well was real.

Second: the god in the well was starving, and mad, and answering prayers the way a drowning man answers a shout — grasping, literal, without an ounce of the wisdom that once lived in the greater powers. Twelve years of silence had not killed the small gods. It had only starved them, and starvation does strange things to the mind.

Third: if forty millers could pull a miracle out of a well by wishing hard enough, then the Chancellery would hear about it by morning, and Isandro Vael's gray-coats would come to Copperlane with fire and chains, and everyone who had ever knelt here — including a ten-year-old boy who hadn't asked for anything except, perhaps, to be somewhere else — would be arrested as a prayer-criminal.

Unless the well stopped being miraculous. Unless the god stopped answering. Unless someone made it stop.

*Or,* said a voice in her head that sounded horribly like her own, younger and brighter and wearing the face she'd had before the burning, *unless someone teaches it to answer better.*

"No," Jonah said aloud.

A man near her flinched. She realized she'd spoken without meaning to. She pulled her hood lower and circled the square's edge, watching, cataloguing, doing the thing she'd trained herself never to do again: reading the shape of power in the world.

It was easy. That was the horror of it. It was as easy as reading weather. The well's divinity pooled around it like heat-haze, thin and ragged and flickering, a candle-flame of a god guttering in a draft. She could see where the prayers tore holes in it — too many askers, too much hunger, a being the size of a chapel saint trying to hold up a district's worth of desperate wanting. Every granted prayer cost it something. It was spending itself into madness, coin by coin, and the mob kept coming to spend it.

The mice were the least of it. If she left now, tomorrow the prayers would get bigger. Someone would ask for a dead child back and get something wearing their child's face. Someone would ask for justice and the whole street would burn clean. Starved gods didn't weigh their gifts. They just gave, and gave, and gave, until the giving broke everything.

Jonah looked down at her gloved hand.

Twelve years ago she had sworn — screamed it, actually, while the covenant-circle burned around her and her friends' names turned to ash — that she would never call a storm again. The oath hadn't been to the gods. The gods hadn't been listening. It had been to the smoke, to the memory of Sefa-and-others-not-yet-met, to the girl she'd been, whose calling had painted a target on every person who ever stood beside her. Exile was supposed to be the price of survival. Quiet was supposed to be the price of exile.

Her palm itched like a healing wound.

"Oh, hells," she said quietly, and took off her glove.

* * *

The scar ran from the base of her middle finger across the whole of her palm, a pale lightning-fork burned into her skin the night her circle died. In the years since, it had been exactly what it appeared to be: a scar. Dead tissue. A souvenir.

Now, as she peeled the glove away, light moved under it. Faint. Blue-white. Like summer lightning seen through cloud, far off, deciding whether to come closer.

The boy staring at the ground noticed first. Children always noticed first. His eyes went from the mice pouring into the well to Jonah's bare hand and back, and his mouth formed a small, silent *oh*.

"Don't look at me," Jonah murmured. "Look at the water. Can you tell me your name?"

"Tobin."

"Tobin. Do you live near here?"

He nodded. "Above the chandler's. But we're leaving. Mum says the mice are a curse."

"The mice are a mistake," Jonah said. "There's a difference. A curse wants to hurt you. A mistake is just power that hasn't been taught any better." She crouched beside him, keeping her scarred hand low, out of the sightline of the remaining adults. "Tobin, I need you to do something for me. Go home. Tell everyone on your street to stay indoors until morning — not because of the mice. Because of what comes after the mice. Can you remember that?"

"What comes after the mice?"

"That's the problem," Jonah said. "Nobody knows yet."

He went. And Jonah straightened, rolled her shoulders the way she had a lifetime ago in a training yard above the sea, and walked to the well.

The kneeling woman grabbed at her skirts as she passed. "Don't go near it! It takes! Whatever you ask, it takes the price out of—"

"I'm not asking," Jonah said. "That's the trick."

She set her bare hand flat on the well's stone lip, and called.

* * *

There are no honest words for what storm-calling feels like, which is why Jonah had spent twelve years not trying to describe it. Poets wrote about fury, about wrath, about the sky splitting open. All wrong. Calling a storm felt like *remembering*. Like some vast thing that had always known your name finally saying it again after a long absence, and every hair on your body standing up to listen.

The power rose through her scar and into her arms and it was *joy*, that was the unbearable part, that was the thing she'd spent twelve years running from. Not danger. Joy. Her whole body lit up with the sense of having been built for exactly this, of being, for one bright moment, precisely and completely herself.

Lightning answered. Not from the sky — the sky was clear and indifferent, stars wheeling overhead as if nothing were happening. The lightning answered from *her*: a web of blue-white fire crawling across the cobbles, up the low wall, threading through the air above the well in slow branching patterns that hurt to look at directly.

The mice stopped.

Every one of them, mid-stream, mid-climb, froze — and then turned as one, a tide of small bodies reversing itself, and flowed away from the well and out of Copperlane in orderly rivers, toward the fields, toward the granaries beyond the walls, anywhere but here. Gone, as requested. Just — elsewhere. Alive. Because Jonah held the image of it in her mind as she called: *gone means gone from here, not gone from the world.* A translation. An amendment to a badly drafted wish.

The god in the well rose to meet her.

She felt it as pressure, as cold, as a voice that wasn't a voice pressing against the inside of her skull: *WHO. WHO HOLDS THE OLD SHAPE. NO ONE HOLDS THE OLD SHAPE. THE OLD SHAPE WAS BURNED.*

"Not burned," Jonah said through her teeth. Sweat ran down her temples. Holding the call steady while talking to a starving god was like balancing a full cup on a rope bridge. "Hidden. Buried. Same as you. Listen to me — you're killing yourself answering them like this. You give them exactly what they say, and it breaks them, and it breaks you. You have to give them what they *mean*."

*I KNOW ONLY THE WORDS,* the god said, and its non-voice cracked, and Jonah felt something in her chest crack with it, because she recognized the sound. It was the sound of her own first year in Vessengard. The sound of a creature alone in a city that had outlawed what it was, performing sanity badly, forgetting pieces of itself one by one. *THE WORDS ARE ALL THAT IS LEFT. THE MEANING WENT WITH THE OTHERS.*

The other gods. The ones who'd gone silent or scattered when the covenant broke. This little well-saint had been part of something once — a household shrine-god, maybe, a protector of thresholds and water — and it had been left holding the whole weight of a district's faith with nothing above it and nothing beside it.

"You're not the only one left," Jonah said. "I've seen signs. Others are waking. You just have to hold on until—"

*UNTIL WHAT?*

Until we reforge the covenant, she almost said, and then thought of Pell Wicke's impossible thirteenth moon-turn, and Brannoc's warning that the fragments carried burdens, and Chancellor Isandro Vael's beautiful smiling face on every proclamation wall in the city, and she swallowed the promise unspoken.

"Until help comes," she said. "So stop answering everything. Rest. ration yourself. When they pray, give them *small*. Give them true-but-small. A fish where they asked for a feast. Can you do that?"

Silence. The lightning-web flickered. Somewhere in the dark below, something ancient and diminished considered her offer with the full attention of a being that had once watched over ten thousand kitchen fires and now couldn't remember most of their names.

*A STORM-CALLER,* the god said at last, wonderingly. *IN THE GODLESS CITY. THEY TOLD US YOU WERE ALL DEAD.*

"They told me you were all asleep," Jonah said. "Seems we were both lied to."

She let the call fade slowly, easing it down like lowering a sleeping child into bed, and the lightning dimmed, and the pressure lifted, and the night went ordinary again — except for the river of mice receding into the dark fields, and the smell of rain that wasn't falling, and the kneeling woman staring up at her with her mouth open.

"You," the woman breathed. "What *are* you?"

Jonah was already pulling her glove back on.

"Charm-seller," she said. "Stall at the end of Tanner's Row. Luck-charms, two coppers. Come by sometime."

She walked away fast, before the questions could catch her, and she was halfway down the street before she let herself feel it — really feel it — the joy still singing in her nerves, the terror rising right behind it like a second storm front. Because she had done it. After twelve years she had opened her hand and called, and the world had answered, and it had been the single finest hour of her life.

And Tobin's mother had seen the lightning from her window. And the kneeling woman knew her face and her stall. And somewhere in the Chancellery, in a tower office full of ledgers and quiet gray men, there were instruments designed for exactly one purpose: detecting unauthorized acts of the old power within the city limits.

By morning, Jonah thought, walking faster, her heart drumming, half-laughing and half-sick with it, there would be a name on a warrant. *Hers.* For the first time in twelve years, hers.

She rounded the corner onto Tanner's Row and stopped.

Sitting cross-legged on the counter of her shuttered stall, turning her luck-charms over with quick, clever, entirely too familiar fingers, was the pickpocket from the market — the one with the ink-stained cuffs and the eyes that saw too much.

"Took you long enough," Sefa said. "We need to talk about what you just did. Also, you should know you're being followed."
