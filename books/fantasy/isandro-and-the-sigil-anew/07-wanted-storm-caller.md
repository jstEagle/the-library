## 7. Wanted: Storm-Caller

The broadsheets found them before the sun did.

Jonah pulled one from under the door of a rooming house on Candlewick Lane — she'd paid for the room in a false name, but Vessengard's walls had eyes now, and paper had a way of finding her. The woodcut was crude, but the artist had gotten the scar right: a jagged line across the palm, rendered like a lightning bolt frozen mid-strike. Beneath it, in the Chancellery's tidy official type:

**WANTED: STORM-CALLER. LAST SEEN TANNER'S ROW. DANGEROUS. REWARD: 200 CROWNS.**

"Two hundred crowns," Sefa said over her shoulder, mouth full of stolen bread. "I'm insulted on your behalf. I once saw a man offered three hundred for stealing a bishop's wig."

"It's not the number that worries me." Jonah set the sheet down and studied the face they'd drawn of her. Too young. Too soft. Whoever had sketched it had worked from memory or from an old Chancellery file, and either way it meant Isandro Vael had kept records. Twelve years of records, of a girl he'd burned out of the world and then carefully remembered.

"You saved forty people from a god's tantrum," Brannoc said from the corner, where he sat wrapped in his threadbare temple-keeper's robe like a man wearing his own shroud early. "In the old days they'd have carved your name into the temple wall for that."

"And in the old days there were temple walls." She folded the broadsheet into quarters, then eighths, until it was small enough to disappear. "Now there's a price on my head and a mob that watched lightning come out of my hands. We can't stay in the city another night."

"We were leaving tonight anyway," Sefa said. "You said fen. I say fen. Fen it is. Though I'll register my objection to fens as a category — mud, midges, things that bite your ankles—"

"Sefa."

"I'm packed. I've been packed since you started folding that paper like it owed you money."

* * *

They went out through the tannery district at moonset, when the reek of lime and hide covered their scent and the watchmen's lanterns bobbed far away along the canal. Brannoc led them. He knew a way, he said, a smuggler's path along the drainage culverts where the city's refuse bled out into the Godsfen, and no one who wasn't already damned would think to follow.

The culvert was exactly as pleasant as it sounded. Jonah walked bent double in the dark with cold water to her knees, her sigil shard pressed against her sternum inside her shirt, humming faintly like a wasp trapped in glass. It had hummed ever since the tenement. Ever since the storm.

She tried not to think about how good it had felt. The power rising through her arm like a tide answering the moon. Twelve years of holding it down, and then one moment of letting go, and the sky had *known* her, had come to her hand like a dog hearing its name after a long absence. She could still feel the ache where she'd reined it back. It felt less like relief than like grief.

Behind her, Sefa slipped twice and swore twice, creatively both times.

"How much farther?" the thief asked.

"Until the culvert ends," Brannoc said.

"That's the kind of answer that makes me understand why your temples all burned down."

"Sefa," Jonah said again, but without heat. In the dark, in the stink, the bickering was almost a comfort. It was the sound of people still together.

* * *

The Godsfen began where the city's maps gave up and turned to blank parchment ruled with a single warning line: *Unsurveyed — Miasma — No Passage.*

It was not miasma. It was memory. The wetlands spread south of Vessengard for thirty miles, a drowned country of black channels and reed-islands and trees standing knee-deep in water like congregation waiting for a sermon that had been postponed twelve years. Once, Jonah knew, this had been holy ground — the fen held the drowned cathedral of a sea-god whose name the Chancellery had struck from every ledger. Now the water simply lay there under the grey morning sky, patient as something buried but not gone.

They poled a flat-bottomed boat Brannoc had arranged for — "the faithful remember me," was all he'd say about it — through channels that branched and doubled and lied. Sefa sat in the bow with a stolen spyglass, watching for pursuers. Jonah watched the banks.

And slowly, as the city sank behind them into haze, she noticed what she was looking at.

Reeds woven into shapes. A charm knotted from river-grass hanging on a dead branch, crude but real — real the way her shop trinkets had never been. A shelf of offerings on a stump: a clay cup, a bird's skull, a child's shoe filled with berries. Someone was praying out here. Many someones. The prayers hung in the air of the fen like mist off the water, and Jonah's scarred palm prickled at all of them, the dormant storm-caller in her skin recognizing its kin.

"There are people here," she said quietly.

"There have always been people here." Brannoc didn't turn around. "Where do you think the exiles went? Not everyone waited for the Chancellery to knock. Some of us walked into the dark on our own feet." A pause. "Some of us were carried."

The channel opened suddenly onto a wide mere, and Jonah understood what she was seeing only when it was too late to pretend otherwise: lights among the trees. Lanterns. Platforms built into the crowns of drowned oaks, walkways strung between them on rope, huts of woven reed and salvaged tile. A whole village lifted above the water on stilts and branches, hidden so well that from fifty feet away it was nothing but shadow and leaf.

Sanctuary. The exiled faithful hadn't scattered. They'd grown roots.

A sentry's voice called across the water — an old woman's voice, hard as a mooring post. "Names and business."

"Brannoc Hale," the old keeper called back, "late of the Temple of the Ninefold Tide, and two travelers under my protection."

Silence. Then, closer, from a platform near the bank, a man rose out of the shadows with a pole-ax across his shoulders, and by his lantern-light Jonah saw his face, and her stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat.

"Aldric Vey," she said.

He stared at her. He was older — they all were — grey shot through his beard, a burn scar she didn't remember pulling down the left side of his neck. But she knew him. She had known him when he was nineteen and laughing, when he'd stood in her circle with his hands full of candle-smoke and sung the dawn-opening beside her. Aldric Vey, circle-mate, friend, one of the seven voices of the covenant-circle of Vessengard.

One of the four who survived the fire.

"You," he said. His voice came out flat and strange, like a door opening on a room long locked. "Jonah Sandoval. They said you were dead. They said—" He stopped. Looked at Brannoc. Looked back at her, and his eyes went to her left hand, to the scar she'd stopped bothering to hide out here beyond the reach of the law. "Storm-caller," he said, and it wasn't her name anymore; it was the word from the broadsheet, and it landed like a stone. "You're the one who called lightning in Tanner's Row. You're why the watch is burning the reed-camps on the north mere."

"I saved forty people—"

"You painted a mark on all of us!" It came out of him like floodwater through a broken weir, twelve years deep. "Do you know what happens when the Chancellery hears the word *storm*? Do you know what they did to the Saltmarsh camp last spring because one fool lit a prayer-candle? They came at dawn with fire-oil, Aldous — they took the children to the workhouses — and now you sail into our mere with your face on two hundred crowns of paper, and you want a welcome?"

"Aldric." Brannoc's voice, low. "Twelve years, and you're still—"

"Don't." Aldric's pole-ax shifted. Around him, other figures had risen on the platforms, silent watchers, and Jonah felt their eyes like weather. "You weren't in the circle, Brannoc. You don't get to tell me what I'm still."

Sefa, very quietly, from the bow: "Jonah. How many ways out of this mere?"

"At least six," Jonah murmured back. "But we're not running. Not yet."

She stepped forward in the boat, unsteady, and made herself meet Aldric's eyes.

"You're right," she said.

He blinked. Whatever speech he'd braced for, it wasn't that.

"You're right that my name on a broadsheet is a brand, and brands burn whoever stands close. You're right about the Saltmarsh camp. I read about it in the city and I told myself it wasn't mine to answer, because answering meant the thing I swore off, and I was tired, Aldric. Twelve years of being tired." Her hand closed around the sigil shard through her shirt. "But I didn't come here to hide. I came because the gods are waking, and the covenant is broken, and I hold a piece of it. And because whatever else happened that night — whatever you believe about the fire — I have spent twelve years paying for it in a city where I wasn't allowed to say my own true name aloud. So if the price of your sanctuary is that I keep walking, tell me now, and I'll take the sixth channel and go."

The mere was absolutely still. Even the midges seemed to wait.

Then a new voice, cracked and dry and amused, came from high up in the largest oak:

"Oh, sit down, Aldric, you great dramatic post. If the Chancellery wanted to find us they wouldn't send a boat with a pickpocket in it — I can smell the lockpicks from here, girl, lovely work, the third one's upside down." Laughter moved along the platforms like wind through reeds. An ancient woman descended the rope-ladder with agonizing slowness, one rung at a time, leaning on a staff crowned with a knot of white fen-iron. Her eyes were milk-pale and missed nothing. "Jonah Sandoval. I presided over your circle's first covenant-oath. I was going to preside over your last one, too, before certain events." She reached the bottom rung and looked up at Jonah with those blind-bright eyes. "You've got the shard. I can hear it. It sounds like a bell underwater."

"You know what it is," Jonah breathed.

"Child, I helped break it." The old woman smiled, and it was terrible and tender at once. "Come up out of that boat. You look half-drowned and entirely stubborn. We'll feed you, and Aldric will glower, and then you'll tell us why the sky has been wrong for a season — because it has, hasn't it. Don't answer. I felt it in my knees."

Aldric stood a moment longer, jaw working. Then he stepped aside from the landing, and it was not forgiveness — Jonah knew the difference, wore it like a second scar — but it was passage.

"Welcome to the Godsfen," he said, toneless. "Mind the third stair. It's rotten."

* * *

They fed them smoked eel and fen-rice and a bitter tea that Sefa drank with the expression of someone being poisoned for a good cause. The sanctuary had perhaps sixty souls — exiled priests, hedge-witches, a family of charm-makers who'd fled the same laws that had shaped Jonah's disguise, three children born out here who had never seen a city. Above the common platform, where a temple altar should have been, they'd built a shelf of driftwood bearing small offerings, and Jonah caught herself staring at it until her tea went cold.

"They pray anyway," Brannoc said softly beside her. "Twelve years, no answers, and they pray anyway. That's the part the Chancellery never understood. You can outlaw the practice. You can't outlaw the reaching."

"The gods aren't answering out here?" she asked. "In the city, things are... waking. Badly. Half-mad."

"No. Out here it's quiet. That's what frightens us." The old priestess — Mother Ysolde, she was called — lowered herself onto the bench across from them with a sigh that used her whole body. "Prayer is a door, child. It opens from both sides. In the city the doors are bursting open with things stumbling through. Out here..." She tapped her milk-white eye. "Out here the doors are opening, and nothing comes through. Something is gathering. Something is choosing. And everything I know about gods says that when they start choosing, they've stopped asking."

Jonah's hand went, without her willing it, to her sternum. To the shard.

It was warm. Warmer than her skin, warmer than the tea, and the hum had changed pitch — no longer a wasp in glass but a note held by a throat, rising toward something.

Across the platform, Sefa was showing the children how the third lockpick worked, upside-down, and Aldric was watching them do it with an expression he probably thought was unreadable. Pell Wicke was thirty miles north with his star-charts, hiding in a cellar. Somewhere south, in the deepest drowned heart of the fen, the sea-god's remnant waited with the first fragment of the broken covenant — Brannoc had said as much on the boat, and Mother Ysolde had confirmed it with a nod: *the Drowned One keeps what fell nearest.*

And the shard against her breastbone sang a little louder, like a compass needle trembling toward true.

"Mother," Jonah said. "How soon can we reach the drowned cathedral?"

Ysolde smiled her terrible, tender smile.

"When the water lets you," she said. "And child — the water has been asking about you since the moment your boat touched my mere."
