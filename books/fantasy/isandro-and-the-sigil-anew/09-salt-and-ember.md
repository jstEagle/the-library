## 9. Salt and Ember

The Godsfen did not want them there. Jonah felt it in the way the water parted around their boat and then closed again behind them, seamless, as if the fen were erasing their passage from its own memory. Reeds taller than Brannoc leaned overhead like a congregation bowing through a funeral rite. The air smelled of iron and rot and something older underneath both — the smell of a thing that had been drowning for twelve years and had not yet decided to stop.

"Keep your hands in the boat," Brannoc said quietly. He sat at the stern with the pole, his ruined temple robes tied back at the waist, his bare forearms scarred with old ritual cuts that had never healed quite straight. "The remnant knows we're coming. It's been waiting since the moon turned wrong."

"It could have sent an invitation," Sefa muttered. She crouched in the bow like a cat pretending not to be afraid of water, one hand trailing just above the surface without touching it. "A card. A little note. *You are cordially summoned to be judged by a dead god.*"

"It isn't dead," Brannoc said. "Drowned. There's a difference, and you'd do well to learn it before we arrive."

Jonah said nothing. She sat in the middle of the boat with the sigil shard wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, and she watched the water, and the water watched her back.

Since the sanctuary had voted — narrowly, bitterly, some of them refusing to look at her while they raised their hands — she had slept perhaps four hours total. She kept seeing the faces of the elders who had spoken against her: *Your circle fell, and the ban came down on all of us like a door.* She kept hearing Pell's voice from two nights ago, dry and precise over the maps: *The first fragment lies in the Drowned Chapel of Vess, in the deep fen. The guardian will test whoever comes for it. The texts don't say how. They only say that most who go, stay.*

*Stay*, in the language of the old covenant, meant more than one thing.

The reeds opened all at once, like a curtain drawn by a hand too large to imagine, and the Drowned Chapel of Vess rose out of the fen.

It had been a temple once — you could see it in the bones of it, the buttresses like folded wings, the bell tower leaning at a pilgrim's angle of grief. But the fen had taken it a century ago, maybe two, and now it stood half-sunk in black water, its stones furred with weed, its windows glowing faintly from beneath the surface with a light that had no business being light. Salt crusted the upper walls in white veins, though the sea was eighty miles east. The whole structure breathed. Jonah could see it breathing, slow as a sleeping animal, the waterline rising and falling against the stone.

"That's not possible," Sefa whispered. "Fen water doesn't rise and fall. There's no tide here."

"There is now," Brannoc said. "He's dreaming hard enough to make one."

"He?"

"The god this chapel belonged to." Brannoc poled them forward into the shadow of the leaning tower. "Vess, the Tidefather. Or what's left of him. When the covenant broke, the gods didn't die — they couldn't die — but they lost their anchors. Most went quiet. Some went mad. And some," he brought the pole down and the boat glided between two sunken columns, "fell into the places that loved them best, and stayed."

The water inside the chapel was calm as glass and dark as poured ink. Below it, far below, Jonah could see lights moving. Not fish. Not lanterns. Shapes, vast and slow, circling the drowned nave like thoughts circling a mind that could no longer hold them.

Brannoc shipped the pole. "This is where I leave you. The remnant called me once, twelve years ago, and I answered wrong, and it has not forgiven me. If I come further, it may take my presence as an insult to you."

"Why would it care about insulting me?" Jonah asked.

"Because it's going to offer you something," Brannoc said, "and it needs you to come to the choice clean." He looked at her with those tired, kind, unbearably steady eyes. "Whatever you see down there, Jonah — whatever it shows you — remember that a test is not a trap. A trap wants you to fail. A test only wants to know what you are."

"And if what I am isn't good enough?"

"Then you'll drown," Sefa said flatly, "and I'll take your shard and your boots, and I'll feel bad about it for at least a week."

Jonah laughed despite herself — a short, cracked sound. She gripped the gunwale. "Wait for me until dawn. If I'm not back by dawn—"

"We know," Brannoc said. "Pell wrote it out three times so none of us could pretend we misunderstood."

She swung her legs over the side. The water took her boots, her knees, her waist, cold enough to stop breath but not moving to drag her down — holding her, almost gently, the way a hand holds a bird it means to examine. She looked back once. Brannoc raised two fingers off his chest in the old blessing of the tide-rites, the one the temples hadn't been allowed to give in twelve years. Sefa just nodded, jaw tight, and Jonah understood that the nod cost her more than the blessing would have.

Then she let go, and the fen swallowed her whole.

* * *

Down was longer than down should have been.

The light changed as she sank — fen-dark to green-dark to a blue so deep it was nearly a sound, a hum she felt in her teeth, in the scar on her palm, in the shard against her ribs which had begun, very softly, to sing. She should have been drowning. Her lungs should have been screaming. Instead each breath came easy, water passing through her like she was already half made of it, and she understood that the remnant was deciding, moment by moment, whether to keep her alive, and that this courtesy was not kindness. It was procedure. An altar does not burn the offering before it has read it.

She settled onto the flooded floor of the nave without a splash. Above her the chapel stretched upward into darkness; below her, impossibly, it stretched downward too, corridors and crypts descending into a depth no building had ever had, and everywhere the blue hum, and everywhere the lights — the great slow shapes — drawing closer.

They resolved as they came. Not leviathans. A face. One face, miles wide if it was anything, made of current and silt and the pale suggestion of features, a beard of drifting weed, eyes like the holes in the world where the sea had once poured through. The Tidefather's remnant regarded her from every direction at once, because it was not in the water. It was the water. She had been inside it since the surface.

**JONAH SANDOVAL.**

The voice did not come through her ears. It came up through the stone, through her bones, through the storm-scar, which flared hot as a brand.

"You know me," she said. Her voice worked underwater the way her breathing did — borrowed, permitted.

**I KNOW THE SHAPE OF YOU. TWELVE YEARS AGO YOUR KIND CAME TO MY CHAPELS AND POURED OIL ON THE WATER AND CALLED ME FATHER. THEN YOUR KIND FORGOT. FORGETTING IS A KIND OF DROWNING, LITTLE STORM-CALLER. I HAVE HAD TWELVE YEARS TO LEARN ALL ITS DEPTHS.**

"I'm sorry." The words came out small. She made herself not look away from those vast, hollow eyes. "I didn't forget. I was made to pretend I had. There's a difference, and I've had twelve years to learn it too."

Something moved in the currents — amusement, or contempt, or both. **THE SHARD AGAINST YOUR HEART. SHOW IT TO ME.**

She drew out the oilcloth. Unwrapped it. The sigil shard — a curved sliver of white-gold metal, etched with lines that hurt to follow, lines that seemed to continue past the edges of the fragment into somewhere else — caught the drowned light and threw it back wrong, throwing it backward, throwing it into the past.

The past came.

* * *

The meetinghouse above the river, twelve years ago. Lamplight. Rain on the roof like applause. Five of them sitting in a circle on the floor with chalk marks still dusting their palms, young and invincible and illegal in exactly the way that made them laugh about it afterward.

Maren, laughing hardest, always — Maren with the crooked teeth and the farmer's shoulders and the gift for earth-songs that could coax a dying orchard back into bloom. Pell, younger, already insufferable, sketching the circle's resonance in a notebook nobody had asked for. Brannoc, older than all of them, keeping watch at the window with the patience of a man who had already survived one purge. And across the circle, dark-haired, beautiful, with a voice like a struck bell —

Isandro.

Not Chancellor Vael. Not yet. Just Isandro, twenty-two years old, brilliant, hungry, the best of them, the one whose covenant-theory everyone else was only borrowing. He was saying something that made Maren snort wine through her nose. He caught Jonah looking at him and smiled, and the smile said: *we are going to change the world, you and I, and it will be easy, because we are the cleverest people alive.*

Jonah knelt in the cold water of the drowned nave and watched her own memory with a stranger's detachment, and hated how warm it was.

The vision turned, as she knew it would. It always turned.

The night of the fire. Boots on the stairs — not the Chancellery's grey-coats, not at first, but one set of footsteps she knew, running *up* while everyone else ran down. Maren. Maren slipping out the kitchen door into the rain with a paper in her fist, the paper with the circle's names, the safehouse locations, the rite-schedule, everything Isandro's agents would need. Jonah had found out three days later, from a dying priest in a cellar, and had spent twelve years not believing it, and believing it, in alternating weeks.

But the vision did not stop where her memory stopped. The remnant pushed deeper, crueler or kinder, impossible to tell. It showed her the kitchen door opening. It showed her the rain. It showed her Maren standing in the alley behind the meetinghouse with the paper in her fist, and it showed her Maren's face —

— and Maren's face was wrecked. Ruined. Wet-eyed, shaking, whispering to herself, *they have Da, they have Da, they said they'd let him go, gods forgive me, gods forgive me,* and when the grey-coats took the paper from her hand they gave her nothing, they took her father anyway, and Maren stood alone in the rain long after they were gone, and then she walked to the river, and the vision followed her under the water, and showed Jonah the place at the bottom of the river where Maren had stayed.

Jonah screamed. The scream came out as bubbles, and the bubbles came out as words, and the words filled the drowned nave:

**SHE BETRAYED YOU, AND SHE WAS BETRAYED FIRST, AND SHE COULD NOT LIVE UNDER THE WEIGHT. NOW. LITTLE STORM-CALLER. THE QUESTION.**

The vast face loomed close, close enough that she could see stars reflected in it, though there were no stars this far underground.

**YOUR FRIEND SOLD YOU TO THE MAN WHO BROKE THE WORLD. THE MAN WHO BROKE THE WORLD WEARS A CROWN OF OFFICE AND SMILES FROM EVERY WALL IN HIS CITY. YOU CARRY A FRAGMENT OF THE COVENANT THAT WOULD UNMAKE HIM. SO ANSWER ME, AND ANSWER TRUE, BECAUSE THE WATER REMEMBERS EVERY LIE EVER TOLD INTO IT:**

**CAN YOU FORGIVE THEM BOTH?**

The silence after was absolute. Even the hum stopped. Even her own heartbeat seemed to pause, awaiting her answer like a servant at a door.

Jonah hung in the black water and understood, finally, what Brannoc had meant. This was not a trap. A trap would have shown her only Isandro's smile. The remnant had shown her the whole of it — the chain of betrayals running backward like a lit fuse, Maren's terror, Maren's father, Maren's body drifting in the current — because the drowned god had spent twelve years learning the depths of forgetting, and it knew that forgiveness built on a partial truth was just forgetting with better posture.

Could she forgive them both?

Isandro's smile surfaced in her mind, the smile from the Chancellery steps, the voice she knew from the burning night, and everything in her said *no* — said it with heat, with a fury that made the water around her tremble and spark, tiny static ghosts crawling over her skin. No. Not him. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Forgiveness was not a debt she owed a man who was, right now, building a cathedral to himself out of stolen divinity.

But Maren —

Maren, who had been nineteen. Maren, who had traded five friends for one father and been cheated even of that. Maren, who had chosen the river because she could not choose anything else.

"Yes," Jonah said. Her voice shook. She let it shake. "For Maren — yes. Not today. Maybe not for a long time. But yes, I'll carry it. I'll try. Trying is what I have."

**AND THE OTHER ONE?**

"Not yet," she said. "Ask me again when he's not wearing a crown."

The silence held for three heartbeats. Four.

Then the drowned god laughed, and the laughter was the fen draining, the tide turning, a hundred shipwrecks settling comfortably into their graves, and it was terrible and it was, somehow, warm.

**NOT YET,** it echoed. **HONEST TO THE LAST. THE OTHERS WHO CAME HERE SAID *YES* TOO QUICKLY, OR *NEVER* TOO LOUDLY. YOU ARE THE FIRST TO SAY *TRYING*.** The great face began to dissolve, currents unwinding, the vast attention withdrawing like a hand opening. **TAKE IT, THEN. KNOW WHAT IT COSTS. THE COVENANT WAS NEVER FREE, LITTLE STORM-CALLER. IT WAS ONLY EVER SHARED.**

The floor of the nave split.

Light came up through the crack — white-gold, the same light as the shard, older than the city, older than the ban. And rising in the light, borne on a column of clear water that had been waiting in the dark for twelve years, was the first fragment.

It was larger than Jonah's shard — a wedge of the original sigil the size of a forearm, its etched lines blazing, its edges humming against the water like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. It rose until it hung before her at the height of her heart.

She reached for it.

She was not foolish. She knew what Pell's maps had said — *a bearer willing to carry each fragment's burden, a burden that reshapes the bearer* — and she reached anyway, because Brannoc was waiting at the surface, and Sefa was pretending not to count the minutes, and somewhere south a man was building a throne out of the sky, and someone had to try.

Her fingers closed on the fragment.

It burned.

Not like fire — like truth. Like every word she had ever swallowed coming up her arm at once. The sigil's lines crawled off the metal and into her skin, fine white-gold filaments winding from her palm to her elbow, sinking in, setting, cooling into something permanent. She felt it take the shape of her — or give her its shape, the border between the two gone meaningless — and in the last instant before the pain crested she heard it, faint and clear, a single line of the covenant's original words, spoken in a voice like Maren's and a voice like the Tidefather's and a voice like her own:

*What is shared cannot be hoarded. What is shared cannot be broken.*

Then the light went out, and the fragment lay quiet in her grip, and her arm bore a brand from wrist to elbow: the sigil's first curve, written in silver-white scar, faintly luminous, faintly singing.

The water lifted her. Gently now — genuinely gently, the courtesy of procedure become something else, something that might, in time, become respect. She rose through the blue dark, through the green dark, through the fen-dark, and broke the surface gasping under a sky just beginning to bruise toward dawn.

* * *

They were both in the boat, both awake, both on their feet before she'd finished coughing. Brannoc hauled her over the gunwale with strength that belied his age; Sefa threw a blanket over her shoulders and then, embarrassed by her own tenderness, immediately started complaining about the smell of fen-water.

"You're glowing," Sefa said. "Your arm. It's — Jonah, your *arm*—"

"I know." She pushed back the blanket and showed them. In the pre-dawn grey the brand shone softly, the first curve of the covenant sigil written in her flesh, and against her ribs the smaller shard had begun to hum in harmony with it, two notes reaching for a chord.

Brannoc took her wrist with a healer's gentleness and turned the arm in the light. His eyes were wet. "Twelve years I kept that chapel's rites in my head," he said, "in case anyone ever earned the right to hear them again. I thought I'd die with them unspoken." He pressed his forehead briefly to her branded knuckles — the old gesture, the forbidden one, the tide-blessing given openly for the first time since the ban. "Bearer of the first fragment. What did it ask you?"

"What it asks everyone, I think." Jonah looked out over the breathing water, at the drowned chapel settling back into its long dream. "Whether I could forgive. I told it I'd try." Her free hand curled slowly into a fist. "There's a second fragment in the mountains, according to Pell's maps. And a third in the sea. And between us and them—" she heard again the voice from the Chancellery steps, silk over steel, the voice from the burning night, "—stands a man who wants the whole sigil for himself."

Sefa snorted. "Then we'd better move faster than him. Also, question, small question: does the glowing arm do anything, or is it purely decorative, because if I'm smuggling a lighthouse across the country I want hazard pay—"

The brand flared.

Not painfully — urgently. The silver lines brightened, and the light bent, all of it, bending southward like iron filings around a magnet, streaming off Jonah's arm in a thin luminous thread that pointed away across the fen, toward the distant smudge of Vessengard on the horizon. Toward the city. And within the light, so faint she might have imagined it, Jonah heard a sound she had heard once before, on the worst night of her life, and had spent twelve years trying to forget:

A voice like a struck bell, raised in invocation, calling power that did not belong to it.

Isandro had begun to gather the second fragment's echo — or something worse. And her brand, her burden, her borrowed piece of the covenant knew it, and was pointing the way home.

"Change of plans," Jonah said, standing, the light streaming south from her arm like a compass needle made of wound and starlight. "We're not going to the mountains. Not yet. First — we're going back to Vessengard."

Sefa stared at her. "Back to the city that wants you arrested?"

"No," Jonah said, and felt the storm wake in her palm, patient, ready, hers. "Back to the city that's about to need me."
