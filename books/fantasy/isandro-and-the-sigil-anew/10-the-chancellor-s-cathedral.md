## 10. The Chancellor's Cathedral

Isandro Vael had always believed that the difference between a tyrant and a saint was a matter of architecture.

A tyrant built walls. A saint built something people wanted to enter. And so, on the morning of the cathedral's unveiling, he stood at the top of the new marble stair and watched the city of Vessengard climb toward him like water finding its level — ten thousand of them, maybe more, flowing up the Avenue of Bells in their good coats and their borrowed hope, craning their necks at the spires he had raised in eleven months on ground where the old temple ruins had sat as rubble for a generation.

They had wept when the scaffolding came down. He had seen it from his study window, ordinary men and women standing in the cold with their hats off, staring up at white stone that caught the light like a held breath. Some of them had knelt right there in the street, to no one, to nothing, because kneeling was an old habit the body remembered even after the law had spent twelve years teaching them to forget.

*Good*, Isandro thought. *Kneel now. Understand later.*

"Chancellor." Sereth Vane materialized at his elbow the way she always did, soundless despite her boots, her gray uniform buttoned to the throat. She did not look at the crowd. She never looked at crowds; crowds were weather to her, and weather was beneath a soldier's attention. "The siphon lines are holding. Third conduit is running at capacity since dawn."

"And the reservoir?"

"Fuller than it has ever been. Whatever woke in the fen last month, it's *pouring*." A pause, thin as a knife laid flat. "You should feel it yourself before the ceremony. The resonance has changed character. It's not ambient anymore. It's *directed*."

He knew what she meant. He felt it even now, a pressure behind his sternum like a second heartbeat, the stolen divinity of a dozen gutter-gods circulating through the cathedral's copper veins and pooling beneath the altar dais where he would stand. For twelve years the power had come to him in trickles — the residue of prayers nobody answered, the leakage from shrines the Chancellery demolished, the slow drip of faith with nowhere else to go. He had learned to live on trickles the way a desert plant learns to live on fog.

But since the moon had begun its impossible thirteenth turn, the trickle had become a river. Somewhere out there, the old gods were waking, stretching, remembering they were owed worship — and every prayer they stirred up in the hearts of the faithful passed first through *his* cathedral, through *his* conduits, into *his* keeping. They were pouring wine for a feast and he was drinking from the cask.

Let them wake. Let them call their faithful. Every candle lit to a dying god was a coin dropped into his treasury.

"You're smiling," Sereth said. Not an accusation. An observation, filed away.

"I'm inaugurating a cathedral," Isandro said. "Smiling is in the job description."

Below, the bells began.

---

He gave them a good speech. He always gave them a good speech; it was the one honest cruelty he permitted himself, that he never lied to the crowd so much as he curated the truth until it sang the note he needed.

"People of Vessengard," he said, from the dais, his voice carried by speaking-trumpets of his own design to the farthest corners of the square. "Twelve years ago you were told the gods had abandoned you. I am here today to tell you the truth your grandparents knew: the gods never left. They are *here*. They are in this stone, in these bells, in the order we have built together out of chaos and fear."

He spread his hands, and the cathedral responded — he let it respond, let a thread of the reservoir's power rise through the floor and bloom along the vaulting in slow rivers of soft gold light, and the crowd made a sound like a wave breaking, ten thousand voices exhaling awe at once. He timed the light to his cadence. He had rehearsed.

"The godless age told you that wonder was contraband," he continued, over the murmur. "That mercy was weakness. That order was a cage. Look around you. Is this a cage? This house belongs to no distant thunderer, no jealous sky-father who demands your groveling and gives you silence. This house belongs to the god of *order* — and the god of order asks only one thing of you."

He paused. Ten thousand people leaned in.

"*Live well,*" he said. "*Tend your gardens. Raise your children. Keep your streets clean and your neighbors fed. Order is not imposed from above, my friends. It grows from below, like light.*"

And the gold light bloomed again, higher, brighter, and somewhere in the crowd a woman began to weep openly, and Isandro Vael stood in the warm flood of ten thousand kinds of faith and felt himself grow by inches.

This was the secret he had never told anyone, not even Sereth, not even in the years when they had burned the circle together and sworn themselves to silence: that divinity was not a thing you seized. It was a thing that *poured*. Faith moved downhill like water, toward whatever vessel stood lowest and openest, and for twelve years he had made himself the lowest, openest vessel in the world. The gods had thrones and demanded sacrifice. He had a smile and asked for nothing but obedience freely given.

Which vessel would you pour yourself into?

Afterward, working the receiving line, shaking hands, letting the old woman clutch his sleeve and tell him about her grandson's fever, letting the mason boast of the stones he'd cut — after all of it, he retreated to the reliquary gallery above the nave, where the windows showed the cathedral's patron in colored glass: a robed figure with a compass in one hand and a plumb line in the other, face serene, features deliberately generic. The artisans had asked whose likeness to carve over the great door. He had told them: *no one's. Everyone's. The god of order has the face of whoever keeps the peace.*

It had his jawline anyway. Artisans were observant creatures.

Sereth found him there. She was carrying a folder, which meant trouble, and trouble lately wore one particular face.

"She's been asking questions," Sereth said. "The charm-seller. Sandoval. She went to Pell Wicke the night of the observatory break-in, and Wicke's star-charts are missing. And two days ago a temple-keeper named Brannoc was seen talking to her under the aqueduct — a keeper we had flagged years ago and chose not to arrest, if you recall your own instruction."

"I recall it." Isandro turned a page of the folder without reading it. He didn't need to read it. He had written most of this story already; he was simply waiting for the other characters to catch up to their lines. "And the shard?"

"No confirmation. But Brannoc kept relics. If anyone in this city still held a fragment of the covenant sigil, it would be him."

Isandro closed the folder. Below, through the colored glass, the crowd was still milling in the square, reluctant to leave the warmth of what they'd witnessed. Little rivers of gold light still crawled the vaulting. His power. His house. His city.

"Do you know what I remember about Jonah Sandoval?" he said.

Sereth waited. She was very good at waiting.

"She was nineteen, and she could hold lightning in her cupped hands like water, and she never once used it to make herself taller. Do you understand how rare that is? I have met a hundred magic-born in my life and ninety-nine of them wanted the world to kneel. She wanted the world to be *sheltered*. When our circle called the storm over the harvest fields, she stood at the center and took the worst of the backlash onto herself so the rest of us wouldn't feel it. Nineteen years old." He touched the glass, just below the glass god's generic, patient face. "I burned her name off the rolls myself. I made sure the exile order described her as dangerous. Unstable. A threat to public order."

"Why?" Sereth asked. She asked it the way she asked everything — flat, precise, wanting the mechanism, not the motive.

Because she had seen what I saw, Isandro thought. Because she stood at the center of the circle that night and felt what I felt when the covenant's true text unrolled itself in my mind — thirteen seats, thirteen shares, power divided until it was powerless — and she flinched from it. Because if she had stayed, she would have argued. She would have gathered the others. She would have been reasonable, and beloved, and immovable, and the covenant would have been reforged exactly as it was before: a cage with thirteen locks, mortals and gods forever rationed, forever small.

Because I could not become what I must become while she was looking at me.

"Because she was the best of us," he said aloud, "and the best of us make the worst witnesses. Send the invitation. Official seal. Full pardon, restoration of name and rights, a formal apology from the Chancellery for the excesses of the early purges — I'll sign it personally. Invite her to attend me here, in the cathedral, privately."

Sereth's eyebrow moved a fraction. In Sereth, that was astonishment. "You're pardoning her."

"I'm *restoring* her." Isandro smiled, and watched his reflection smile back from the dark glass, superimposed for a moment on the painted god. "Think, Sereth. The moon completes its turn in nine days. The old powers are stirring, and stirring *badly* — half-mad, starving, answering prayers with fires and floods. The city can feel it coming. They're frightened. And very soon, a storm-caller is going to do something public and heroic and reckless, because that is what Jonah Sandoval does, and the whole city will see it."

"And then?"

"And then the Chancellor offers the frightened city its choice. On one side: mad gods, mob panics, a fugitive criminal calling lightning over rooftops. On the other: order. Light. Me." He turned from the window. "But better — far better — if the storm-caller herself stands at my right hand when I say it. The exiled hero, pardoned, restored, *grateful*, lending her storm to the god of order. Her power legitimizes mine. Mine shields hers. And the covenant's fragments, wherever Brannoc hid them, come to me inside the one person I can trust to carry them — because she trusts *me*."

"You think she'll say yes."

"I think," Isandro said gently, "that Jonah Sandoval has spent twelve years being nobody, selling tin charms to people who need real ones, watching prayers go unanswered. I think she is tired down to the marrow. And I am offering her everything she lost — her name, her city, her *home* — in exchange for the one thing she was always too generous to hoard." He walked to the door, and the reservoir's power hummed up through the floor to meet his footsteps like a dog rising to greet its master. "Her strength. Freely given. That's all I've ever asked of anyone."

---

She came three days later, and he knew it before the herald announced her, because the whole cathedral *leaned*.

He felt it through the soles of his shoes — the siphon-lines singing, the reservoir surging, every stolen god in his treasury turning its attention toward the doors like iron filings snapping toward a magnet. Storm-blood walking into a house built of hoarded divinity. Of course the power noticed her. Of course it strained toward her. Power always recognized its opposite: his was gathered, hers was *called*; his was a dam, hers was the rain.

She looked wrong in the great nave and he catalogued why in a single glance: she looked like weather indoors. Her coat was travel-stained, her braid coming loose, a fresh burn-mark wrapped around her left forearm that the healers' salve hadn't fully hidden — the fen, then, or worse. She walked like someone who had recently run. Behind her, at a distance the guards pretended not to notice, slouched a sharp-eyed young pickpocket with ink-stained fingers, and hovering at the edge of the transept stood a tall, disheveled man with an astronomer's stoop who was trying very hard to look like furniture.

So. The little family had assembled itself. How quickly the exiles found each other, like sparks rolling to the same corner of the hearth.

"Chancellor." She stopped at the appointed distance and inclined her head — not a bow, not quite. Her voice was steady. Her pulse, he noted with the fine perception the reservoir granted him, was hammering. "You sent for me."

"I sent for *Jonah Sandoval*," Isandro said, "and instead they've given me a fugitive. Sit, please. Wine? You look as though you've been sleeping in ditches."

"I've been sleeping in the Godsfen."

"The fen." He poured the wine himself, unhurried, letting her watch his hands — ordinary hands, chancellor's hands, no scars, no brands. "Then you know what's waking out there. Then you've felt what I've felt." He handed her the cup and watched her decide, visibly, not to drink it. Wise. He'd have respected her less if she drank. "Tell me what you know, Jonah. Truly. Not the version you'd give an interrogator. The version you'd give—" he let the smallest pause fall, "—an old friend."

Something flickered across her face. There it was. He had known there would be; he had counted on it. Twelve years was long enough to bury a voice, but not deep enough. She had heard him that night through the smoke — he was almost certain of it, had been certain for years — the young chancellor's aide reading the exile decree in a steady voice while the circle-house burned, and some animal part of her had filed that voice away the way survivors file the sound of a specific engine, a specific footstep.

"You knew my circle," she said carefully.

"I audited it. Formally, I mean — the Chancellery required documentation of all registered workings, and yours was the largest in the eastern districts." He settled into the chair across from her, open posture, hands visible. Everything about the room was arranged for openness: low table, equal chairs, no desk between them, the great windows flooding them both with the same impartial light. "I read every page of your circle's filings, Jonah. Your harmonic workings were elegant. Economical. You wasted nothing. In another life I'd have hired you to redesign the city's aqueduct flows." He smiled. "In another life, perhaps I will."

"Why am I here?"

Straight to it. Good. He had prepared a long approach, a winding garden path of a conversation, but she had never had patience for winding paths and he found, to his mild surprise, that he didn't want to use the prepared speech. He wanted — and he examined this want with the detached curiosity he brought to all his own appetites — he wanted her to *understand*. Not to be managed. Understood. It was the one luxury corruption had left him, this need to be seen clearly by the only person alive who had seen him before.

"Because I'm going to tell you the truth," he said, "and then I'm going to offer you everything."

He rose and walked to the window, and as he walked he let the mask slip — just a fraction, just enough. The reservoir rose in him and he allowed her to feel it, the way one allows a guest to feel the heat of a hearth. The gold light in the vaulting brightened. The air thickened. Somewhere below, the bells shivered though no hand touched them.

"Twelve years ago I discovered the covenant's original text. Not the priests' version — the *first* version, the one carved before the first war. Do you know what it says, Jonah? It says there were meant to be thirteen. Thirteen gods, and thirteen mortal voices, and power shared thirty-six ways until nothing and no one was strong enough to break the world again. That was the covenant. Not reverence. *Rationing.* The gods agreed to be small, and they made us agree to be small with them." He turned. "And I looked at that arithmetic and I thought: this is why the world starves. This is why prayers go unanswered and children die of fevers and cities burn — because thirteen cowards signed a treaty making help illegal."

"You burned our circle," Jonah said quietly. "You burned *Maren*."

"Maren chose her side the moment she sold your meeting-place to my agents for a purse of silver and a promise of pardon." He said it flatly, without relish, and watched the blow land — watched her take it, stagger, refuse to fall. Yes. She knew. Some part of her had always known; grief is rarely surprised. "I don't expect forgiveness for the fire. I expect you to understand it. The covenant had to die, Jonah, and it could not die while people like you kept feeding it. So I ended the age of gods. I built the godless city. And it *worked* — no plagues of answered prayers, no mobs kneeling in the mud, no child sacrificed on an altar to make the rain come. Twelve years of the quietest, safest, most orderly realm in living memory."

"At the price of every unanswered prayer in it."

"Yes." He did not flinch. "Every price is paid by someone. I have simply always insisted on choosing who."

He crossed back to her then, and knelt — actually knelt, one knee to the marble, so that he looked up at her, and heard the gasp ripple outward through the guards at the doors, through Sereth motionless in the shadow of the pillar. Chancellors did not kneel. The god of order did not kneel.

"So here is the offer, and I will make it only once, and I will make it honestly. Full pardon. Restoration of your name, your rights, your citizenship — sealed today, effective the moment you accept. A seat at my council. Protection for your fen-born friends, amnesty for the astronomer, the thief, the keeper — all of it, everything, gone." He held out his hand, palm up, the gesture of a man offering a dance. "In exchange, I ask for your storm. Not your slavery — your *partnership*. The old powers are waking mad, Jonah, and they will tear this city apart answering prayers with fire unless someone stands between heaven and the streets. I can hold the door. But I cannot call the rain. Only you can do that. Be my storm-engine. Be the shield this city has prayed for. Come home."

The silence stretched. He watched her face with the total attention he gave to nothing else in the world — the tremor in her jaw, the shine she refused to let reach her eyes, the way her bandaged arm curled protectively against her ribs. She was wavering. He had known she would waver. Twelve years of exile, of tin charms and locked doors and sleeping with one ear open — he was offering her the end of all of it, and he was the only person in the world who could offer it credibly, because he was the one who had taken it from her.

*Say yes*, he thought, and was startled by the nakedness of his own wanting. *Say yes, and I will never have to be your enemy. Say yes, and you can stand beside me when I take the throne, and I will build you a garden in the new heaven, and it will be like the circle again, like the harvest storms, like before—*

"Your cathedral," Jonah said slowly, "is drinking."

He blinked. "What?"

"This light. This warmth. I can feel it from the doorway — it's not blessing, it's *siphonage*. You've got copper in the walls drinking from every shrine you haven't demolished yet. You're not the god of order, Chancellor." She rose, and she did not take his hand, and the air around her began, very faintly, to smell of rain. "You're a leech with excellent architecture."

The temperature of the room changed. Around them the gold light guttered, steadied, brightened — the reservoir responding to its master's spike of fury, and Isandro mastered it, mastered himself, and let the smile return to his face like a visor coming down.

"I had hoped," he said, standing, brushing the dust from his knee, "that you would be sensible. I had hoped it for your sake more than mine."

"You should have hoped it twelve years ago, before you taught me what your sensibility costs." She was walking backward toward the doors, toward her strange little family frozen in the transept shadows, and her voice carried down the whole golden nave. "Keep your pardon. I'll earn my name back myself."

At the threshold she stopped, one hand on the great door, and looked back at him — and for one unguarded instant neither of them wore a mask, and what passed between them across four hundred feet of marble was so old and so wounded that the reservoir itself went quiet, every stolen god holding its breath.

"I'm sorry, Jonah," Isandro said, and meant it, and hated that he meant it. "Truly. I am sorry for the fire. I am not sorry for the rest."

"Nine days," she said. "I know what the moon is counting down to. So do you."

The doors boomed shut behind her.

Isandro Vael stood alone in the nave of his cathedral, in the light of his stolen heaven, and after a long moment he laughed — softly, helplessly, the laugh of a man watching the tide come in over work he had built on the sand.

"Sereth," he said. "Ready the conduits. All of them."

Above him, through the colored glass, the generic god with his jawline gazed out at a sky where the moon was swelling toward its impossible thirteenth fullness — and far to the east, over the drowned fens, the first thunderheads of the season were gathering like an army that had finally, after twelve long years, remembered the way home.
