## 11. What Sefa Saw in the Dark

The cathedral smelled like a lie.

Sefa had spent her whole life learning what lies smelled like — cheap incense over unwashed bodies, gilt paint over rotting wood, a smile over a knife. The Cathedral of the God of Order, three years in the building and opened only last month, smelled of all of these at once: fresh-cut marble and old blood, frankincense and something underneath it, something sweetish and wrong, like fruit gone over in a closed room.

She slipped through the workmen's gate at the cathedral's flank just after the last bell, wearing the grey livery of a lamplighter's apprentice she'd lifted off a drying line two streets back. The Chancellery guards at the main doors checked papers. The workmen's gate had a lock older than the Chancellor's reign, and Sefa had picked locks older than that before she could write her own name.

Inside, the nave rose up into darkness like the inside of a bell. Even empty, even at night, the place hummed. Not with prayer — Vessengard hadn't prayed openly in twelve years, and this cathedral was barely consecrated — but with something else. A vibration in her teeth. In the fillings of the world.

Sefa paused between two pillars and let herself feel it properly, the way she let herself feel everything, because feeling was the one thing no one could take from her no matter how many times they took everything else.

Her gift — she'd never had a better word for it, though *curse* came close some nights — was touch. Skin against wood, skin against stone, and the residue of what had happened there rose up into her like water into chalk. Most objects held nothing much: a coin remembered the sweat of a hundred hands; a door remembered being slammed. But holy things, old things, things that had been loved or feared enough — those held *more*. She'd once touched a temple cornerstone in the Godsfen and heard four hundred years of hymns in the space of a heartbeat, and come away weeping without knowing why, and sworn off touching anything with a history ever again.

Then she'd met Jonah Sandoval, tried to rob her, and gotten a palmful of storm-scar and a life she hadn't asked for instead.

*Just get the proof,* she told herself, moving along the pillar's shadow. *In and out. Pell needs to see what's under the cathedral. You don't need to understand it. Understanding is for people who sleep well.*

The floor guided her. That was the strange part. Every few yards, set into the marble like coins pressed into bread, were sigils — small ones, worn, each one different. And when her bare fingers brushed them as she crawled, they *sang*. Faintly. Like tuning forks struck and muffled in cloth.

She knew them. Gods help her, she knew them, because Jonah carried a shard of the same design wrapped in oilcloth next to her heart, and Brannoc had spent three nights in the fen drawing it in the dirt until Pell could copy every line of it.

The covenant sigil. Broken into pieces. And here, in the Chancellor's own cathedral, its fragments were set into the floor like foundation stones.

*He didn't destroy it,* Sefa thought, cold crawling up her spine. *He built his house on top of it.*

* * *

She found the first body of evidence in the reliquary.

It was less a reliquary than a treasury — gold plate, jeweled processional staffs, the accumulated donations of a city terrified into piety. But behind the display cases, in a locked cabinet whose lock was, frankly, an insult, Sefa found ledgers. Dozens of them. She couldn't read most of the figures, but she could read the column headings, because Pell had drilled her on them until she dreamed in accounting: *inflow, outflow, yield.*

Yield. From what? Cathedrals didn't produce anything.

Except this one did. The entries went back six years — before the cathedral had even broken ground. *Site survey, Vael estate. Resonance confirmed. Extraction begins.* And then page after page of numbers climbing steadily upward, year over year, with notations in a tight, elegant hand:

*The Fen remnant resists. Increase siphon.*
*The mountain god dreams too loudly. Muffle the conduit.*
*Twelve remain. One seat vacant. Patience.*

Sefa sat back on her heels in the dark, ledger open across her knees, and felt the whole shape of it assemble itself in her head the way a pickpocket feels a purse's shape through fabric — by weight, by contour, by the places where it bulged wrong.

Isandro Vael wasn't building a cathedral to a god of order.

He was building a machine to *become* one.

* * *

It was the choir gallery that nearly killed her.

She'd meant to go up, see the organ, find whatever mechanism fed the siphons — Pell would want specifics, and Pell frightened her more than guards did, in the way that only a person who could do arithmetic on your soul can frighten you. So up she went, up the spiral stair behind the organ pipes, quiet as breath, and that was where she heard the voices below and froze with her hand on the rail.

Two men in Chancellery grey stood at the altar rail, talking low. Between them, on a wheeled frame like something from a butcher's shop, lay a covered shape the size of a large dog.

"—third one this month," one was saying. "The fen shrine went silent Tuesday. Completely. Whatever was left of the drowned god down there, it's gone."

"And the Chancellor?"

"Feeding." The man's voice did something complicated on the word. "You know how he gets after. Best we clear the hall before he—"

Below them, without any door opening, Isandro Vael stepped out of the shadow behind the altar.

Sefa had seen him before, of course — everyone in Vessengard had seen him, on the minted coins and the proclamation posters and the balcony of the Chancellery, silver-haired and smiling, the most beloved man in a city that had outlawed the divine. She'd expected the smile. What she hadn't expected was that he looked *hungry*. Not thin — never thin — but lit from within by something that consumed more light than it gave, the way a lamp consumes oil. His eyes had a shine to them that didn't blink often enough.

"Leave it," he said pleasantly, gesturing at the covered shape. "And the records from the fen extraction. I'll review them myself."

The guards fled. Smart men.

For a long moment the Chancellor stood alone in his cathedral, and Sefa, flattened against the gallery rail above him, watched him do something she would spend the rest of her life trying to unsee.

He knelt beside the covered shape and drew back the cloth.

What lay beneath wasn't a body. It was a *shrinkage* — a shape like a god might make if you wrung a god out like laundry and kept the shape it left behind. Dim, guttering, barely there. And Isandro cupped his hands around it the way a man cups a match against wind, and *drew*, and the little dying light streamed up into his palms like smoke obeying a draft, and he sighed — actually sighed, with pleasure, a soft domestic sound, the sound of a man settling into a warm bath —

—and spoke.

"Twelve," he said to the empty nave, in a voice rich with satisfaction and something underneath the satisfaction, something cracked and grieving that made Sefa's skin crawl worse than the feeding had. "Twelve little lamps, and one throne waiting. Do you remember, Jonah? You always said the circle should share everything equally. Well. I am sharing. I'm taking *all* of it, so no one else has to carry anything. Isn't that mercy? Isn't that exactly what you taught me?"

Sefa stopped breathing.

*Jonah.*

He knew Jonah. Knew her name, said it like a man says the name of a woman he'd loved and hated in equal measure, said it into the dark of his stolen cathedral at midnight like a prayer to the one god he couldn't consume.

And then, because the world was generous to thieves who lingered, he said more.

"You burned so beautifully," Isandro murmured, rising, smoothing his robes, the grief folding itself away beneath the civility like a letter into a drawer. "All of you. Twelve years I've carried what you cost me. Twelve years since I signed your exile order with my own hand and told the council the storm-caller was dead. And now the moon turns a thirteenth time, and you come crawling back to my city with a shard in your pocket, thinking you can rebuild what I have already *become*."

He laughed. It was a beautiful laugh. It was the worst sound Sefa had ever heard.

"The betrayer keeps the spoils, old teacher. That was always the rule."

* * *

Sefa didn't remember climbing down. Her body did it without her, muscle memory and terror working in concert while her mind replayed the words on a loop: *I signed your exile order. My own hand. Old teacher.*

Jonah's circle. The one that burned twelve years ago. The friend who betrayed them to Isandro — Brannoc had said it, in the fen, in his careful ruined voice, and Jonah had flinched like a struck animal and never once said the name.

There was no name to say. There was only the Chancellor of Vessengard, beloved father of the city, kneeling in the dark eating gods, and he had been *one of them*. He had sat in their circle. He had probably passed Jonah the wine. He had learned the covenant's words from her mouth and used them to build himself a throne.

And tomorrow morning, Sefa was going to have to tell her.

She reached the ground floor, pressed herself into the shadow of the baptistry, and made the mistake of putting her hand flat against the wall to steady herself.

The wall *screamed*.

Not aloud — inside her, through her palm, up her arm: the residue soaked into the cathedral's bones, layer upon layer of it, and now that she knew what she was feeling she couldn't stop knowing. Consecration performed over a siphon. Prayers harvested like crops. And beneath everything, threaded through the marble like veins through a leaf, the covenant fragments in the floor, singing their broken song — and answering, from somewhere far above, a single voice that sang the same song *whole*, hoarding every harmony, a choir of one.

The thirteenth turn of the moon was coming. Pell had shown them the charts. And Isandro Vael intended to be sitting on a divine throne when it arrived.

Sefa pulled her hand back. Her fingers were shaking. She'd been tortured once, briefly, by a fence in Saltrow who thought she'd skimmed his take, and she had cried and begged and survived it fine. This was worse. This was *knowing* worse.

Because she knew what telling Jonah would do.

She'd watched Jonah take the second fragment's price in the mountains — watched her give away her mother's face and come down the mountain smaller and harder and still standing, and Sefa had thought: *there is nothing this woman can't survive.* But this was different. This wasn't a memory surrendered to a god. This was the memory of her whole life rearranging itself around a single fact: the person who destroyed everything she loved had smiled at her across a Chancellery table and offered her a job, and she hadn't known, and now she would know forever, and there was no fragment, no god, no storm that could take the knowledge back out of her head.

*Loyalty means telling her,* said one voice in Sefa, the one that sounded like Brannoc.

*Loyalty means not breaking her before the fight,* said the other voice, which sounded uncomfortably like Sefa herself at her worst, the Sefa who'd spent twelve years surviving by deciding what people could bear to hear.

She was still arguing with herself when the light changed.

A glow spread across the nave floor — the sigil fragments in the marble waking, brightening, and above them the air itself began to fold, and Isandro Vael turned his face upward toward something only he could see, and said, softly, delightedly:

"There you are. Yes. Keep turning."

The moon. Through the oculus, Sefa caught a sliver of it — swollen, wrong-colored, hurrying through its impossible thirteenth arc. And the fragments in the floor pulsed in time with it, and Isandro's shadow on the marble had too many edges, and Sefa decided, with the clarity of the truly terrified, that she had gathered quite enough proof.

* * *

Getting out was harder than getting in. The guards had doubled at the workmen's gate — the Chancellor's moods, apparently, made everyone jumpy — so Sefa went out the way water goes out of a ship with a hole in it: downward, through the crypt drainage channel, a brick throat of a tunnel that stank of candle-grease and old incense and, near the end, faintly, of the fen. She crawled the last twenty yards on her elbows with the ledgers strapped to her back under her shirt, wrapped in oilcloth, and emerged through a grate into the night behind a tannery, where she lay in the reeking dark and laughed until she cried, quietly, the way you do when your body needs to discharge something your dignity won't permit.

Then she ran.

Through the tannery district, across the canal by the stepping stones only kids and thieves knew, up through the Weavers' Quarter where the curfew bells had long since gone hoarse, out the pilgrim postern whose watchman owed her eleven favors and was about to owe her forgiveness instead. The sanctuary in the Godsfen was a day's hard walk. She did it in a night and half a morning, running on fear and stolen apples, and came stumbling up the duckboard path as the fen mist was burning off, and Brannoc saw her first and shouted for the others, and then Jonah was there, wading toward her through the reeds with her storm-scarred hand already raised against threats that weren't there.

"Sefa." Jonah caught her by the arms. Up close she looked like she hadn't slept, which was fair; none of them had, not since Moonturn, not since Brannoc — no. Not yet. That was later. Sefa shoved the future away and concentrated on the present, on Jonah's grip, on the mud, on the words she had carried thirty miles strapped to her spine.

"I got it," she gasped. "Proof. Ledgers, dates, extraction records — six years of them. He's siphoning the gods, Jonah, he's been *draining* them, the fen god, the mountain god, all of them, one by one, and the cathedral is built right on top of the covenant fragments, he's using them as a — as an engine —"

"Slow down." But Jonah's eyes had already gone sharp, reading Sefa's face the way Sefa read rooms. "You're hurt."

"I'm not hurt."

"You're white as a drowned thing and you won't look at me."

"I'm looking at you right now."

"No," Jonah said gently, terribly. "You're looking at a spot about two inches to the left of my ear. Which is what you do when you're deciding whether to say something. You've done it since the day I met you, when you had my shard in your pocket and were lying to me about it." Her grip tightened. Not cruel. Just certain. "So say it. Whatever it is. Say it to me and not to the reeds."

Pell had come up behind her, notebook already out. Brannoc stood at the path's edge, very still, an old man braced for weather.

And Sefa stood in the fen mud with the truth in her mouth like a stone she'd swallowed miles ago and could finally neither spit out nor keep down, hating Isandro Vael with a purity she'd never managed toward anyone, because he had done this — he had put this weight on her tongue, made a thief carry a thing too heavy to steal and too heavy to drop.

"He was in your circle," she said. "Isandro. He was one of you. He called you *old teacher*, Jonah. He said he signed your exile order himself. He said—" her voice cracked and she pushed through it, because Jonah deserved the whole ugly weight of it, not the softened version, "he said you burned beautifully. All of you. And then he knelt down in his cathedral and ate what was left of the drowned god, and he talked to the dark like you were listening, and he said the betrayer keeps the spoils, and that was *always the rule*."

The fen went quiet. Even the frogs, Sefa thought wildly. Even the frogs are waiting.

Jonah didn't move. For three full seconds she was absolutely still, the way water is still right before it decides to be ice, and Sefa watched her — watched the knowledge land, watched it break something and, horribly, watched something else stand up in the wreckage, something with a spine of lightning and a face like winter.

When Jonah finally spoke, her voice was almost calm, which was the most frightening thing Sefa had heard all night, including the feeding.

"Say the last part again."

"*The betrayer keeps the spoils. That was always the rule.*"

Jonah nodded slowly. Her scarred hand opened and closed at her side, and high overhead, though the sky was cloudless, thunder muttered — one roll, distant, like something clearing its throat.

"Then he's kept them long enough," she said.

She turned and walked up the duckboard path toward the sanctuary, and the reeds bowed out of her way, and Sefa stood holding the ledgers against her chest and understood, with a thief's precision, that she had just handed Jonah Sandoval the last piece of armor she needed — and the first weapon.

Behind them, far to the south, over the towers of Vessengard, the moon hung fat and pale in the morning sky, still turning, still counting down.
