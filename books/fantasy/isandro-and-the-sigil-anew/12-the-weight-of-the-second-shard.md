## 12. The Weight of the Second Shard

Brannoc had carried gods for thirty years before the temples fell, and he had learned this much: divinity always announced itself by weight. Not thunder, not light — weight. The air thickened. The knees remembered kneeling. So when the mountain path bent upward through the pines and the air grew heavy as wet wool, he stopped walking and said, quietly, "We're close."

Jonah didn't hear him at first. She was three steps ahead, the way she had been for two days, the sigil shard from the fen wrapped in oilcloth against her sternum like a second heart. Sefa noticed, though — Sefa noticed everything — and fell back to walk beside him.

"Close to what?" she asked. "You've got that face. The one you make before you tell us something terrible."

"I have several faces," Brannoc said.

"Yeah, but that's the worst one."

He almost smiled. The girl had a way of needling grief into something bearable, like a bone set wrong but set. He nodded up the trail, where the pines were thinning and the stone beneath their boots had begun to show carvings worn nearly smooth — spirals, mostly, and small figures with raised arms. "The old high road to Vael's Rest. Pilgrims used to climb this in the winter, barefoot, to prove their prayers could survive the cold." He touched one of the carved figures with his boot toe. "This was the shrine road to the Keeper of What's Kept."

"And what's that?" Pell asked from behind, breathing hard, his maps clutched against his chest against the wind.

"Memories," Brannoc said. "The god who holds what people give away."

The wind came down then, sharp off the snowline, and it smelled — all of them felt it — like a room long shut. Like cedar and ash and old paper. Jonah finally turned around, and Brannoc saw her look up at the white shoulder of the mountain above them, and saw her jaw tighten in a way he recognized from twelve years ago, from council fires and circle meetings, from the young woman who had once argued theology with him until dawn because she couldn't sleep otherwise.

"Then we should be careful what we say on the way up," she said. "It's already listening."

* * *

The shrine sat in a bowl below the summit, half-swallowed by scree: a ring of standing stones no taller than a man, each one furred with frost, and in the center a pool so still it looked cut from black glass. No temple roof. No altar. The old faiths hadn't needed buildings at the high places; they'd needed only a place where the sky could see you plainly.

The weight pressed down as they entered the ring. Sefa stumbled and caught herself on a stone, swearing under her breath. Pell went gray and sat down hard. But Brannoc watched Jonah walk to the center of the ring as if the pressure meant nothing to her, and understood — not for the first time, and with a sorrow that had become familiar as an old coat — that the gods had never stopped recognizing her, even in exile. Even now. Especially now.

The pool spoke without ripples.

**TWELVE YEARS YOU WALKED PAST MY DOOR.**

The voice was neither male nor female, neither old nor young. It sounded like remembering. It came from everywhere the silence wasn't.

"You know why we've come," Jonah said. Her breath smoked in the cold. "The covenant is broken. We carry the first shard. We've come for the second."

**I KNOW WHY MORTALS COME TO THE HIGH PLACES,** the god said. **THEY COME TO TAKE.**

"We come to ask," Brannoc said, stepping forward, lowering himself to one knee the way he had knelt ten thousand times in a life that no longer existed. "Keeper of What's Kept. I kept your rites when keeping them was a crime. I buried your hymns in my memory when the Chancellery burned every book. Ask what you ask of us. We will weigh it honestly."

A long stillness. Somewhere above them, wind moved through stone with a sound almost like breathing.

**THE FIRST SHARD WAS PAID FOR IN FORGIVENESS ATTEMPTED,** the god said, and Brannoc saw Jonah flinch at the word *attempted*, saw her hand drift toward the branded skin of her forearm. **THE SECOND IS PAID FOR IN MEMORY. ONE MEMORY. GIVEN, NOT TAKEN. THE SHARD CANNOT BE HELD BY HANDS THAT REFUSE TO KNOW WHAT LOSING IS.**

"Any memory?" Pell asked, hoarse. His scholar's mind was already racing ahead; Brannoc could see the arithmetic of it in his face, the terrible efficiency. "Or does the — the quality of the memory matter?"

**IT MUST BE LOVED,** the god said simply. **A MEMORY YOU WOULD GRIEVE. OTHERWISE IT IS NOT A PAYMENT. IT IS ONLY LITTER.**

Sefa laughed, one short, sharp note with no humor in it. "So it's a robbery where we rob ourselves."

"It has always been so," Brannoc said quietly. "The old covenants were never free. That's what Isandro never understood — or understood too well. He wanted the power without the price. Look what it made him."

He said the name deliberately. He had been watching Jonah for days, watching how she carried the chancellor's name like a splinter too deep to dig out, and he thought — hoped — that saying it aloud here, in a god's presence, might draw a little of the poison. But she only looked at the black pool, and her face was the face of someone doing sums of her own.

And Brannoc knew, with the sick certainty of a man who has buried too many friends, what memory she was already choosing.

"No," he said.

She looked at him.

"Jonah. There are other memories. There are a hundred—"

"There aren't," she said. "You know there aren't. Everything else I'd give up, I'd give up and still be me. This one—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "My mother died when I was nine. Everything else about her — her voice, I've lost pieces of it. Her hands. The way she hummed when she cooked. It's all going, year by year, like water through my fingers. The only thing left whole, the only thing I can still see clear as glass, is her face. And if I give that up—" Her breath shook. She mastered it. "Then I'll have lost her twice. But everyone else keeps everything, and I lose nothing, and we fail, and Isandro crowns himself under a torn sky, and every prayer in the world goes unanswered forever. That's the trade. I've done the sums, Brannoc. I did them before you even knelt down."

The god said nothing. It didn't need to. It was listening the way mountains listen.

Sefa had gone very still by the ring of stones. Pell had taken off his spectacles and was polishing them with hands that weren't quite steady. And Brannoc found, to his own surprise, that his eyes were burning — because this was the thing he had failed to do twelve years ago, the thing that haunted him more than the fire itself. When the circle burned and Jonah fled into exile, he had grieved for the dead, for the temples, for the world. He had never once stood in front of her and simply *witnessed* what she had lost. He had let her carry it alone because carrying alone was what exiles did.

Not this time.

"Then we watch," he said.

"What?" Pell looked up.

"If she pays, we witness it. All of us. That's what witnessing *means*, in the old rites — you don't look away from another person's sacrifice. You hold it. You remember it for them, if they can't." He turned to the pool, and his voice cracked and he let it crack. "Keeper. We are four. If she gives, we will each carry the shape of what she lost, so that it is not erased from the world — only from her. Witnessed. Held. That is our part of the payment."

The pool was silent a long moment.

**THAT IS NOT REQUIRED.**

"No," Brannoc agreed. "Neither was hers."

* * *

They made camp inside the ring, because the god would not open its hand across a distance, and none of them suggested going back down. Pell built a fire with the mechanical care of a man who needed his hands busy. Sefa sat close to Jonah without sitting next to her, which was its own kind of tenderness, and passed her dried plums without a word. The stars over the bowl were brutal and clear.

"Tell me about her," Sefa said at last. "Your mother. While you still — while it's yours to tell."

Jonah was quiet so long Brannoc thought she wouldn't answer.

"She was a net-mender in Saltmere, on the coast. Hands like rope, always smelling of tar and salt. She wasn't magic — nobody in our family was, not for three generations — but she believed in the old courtesies. Left cream out at midwinter. Spoke to the sea politely. When I was small I asked her why, if the gods weren't listening anyway, and she said—" Jonah's voice caught, steadied. "She said, *they might start again any day, and I want them to find me being decent.*" She laughed, wet-eyed. "That was her whole theology. Decency, in case anyone was watching."

"She sounds like someone worth being decent for," Sefa said.

"She'd have liked you. She'd have caught you picking her pocket inside of a minute, and then she'd have fed you dinner."

"That's exactly what happened with Jonah, more or less," Sefa said, and even Jonah smiled.

Pell cleared his throat. "For the record — the maps, the fragments, the burden-bearers — I calculated perhaps a dozen viable memories among the four of us. I never once imagined you'd choose this one. I want you to know that I understand what it costs, even if I can't—" He put his spectacles back on. "Even if I can't imagine it. My mother is alive and writes to me weekly, tedious letters about her garden. I am aware of my luck."

"Tell her about the garden anyway," Jonah said. "When this is done. Tell her everything."

Brannoc said nothing. He simply moved, stiff-kneed, around the fire, and sat down beside Jonah, and put his old hand over her young one, scarred palm to scarred palm. She didn't pull away. Above them the thirteenth moon hung fat and wrong, a coin someone had minted from a lie, and he thought: *we are running out of sky.* He thought: *let her be quick.*

But when morning came, she was slow. She rose before any of them, washed her face in the black pool without asking permission — the god allowed it, the water taking her reflection gently — and stood in the center of the ring with the frost steaming off the stones around her, waiting for them to wake, because she would not do this unwatched. Because she had accepted his term.

"Keeper of What's Kept," she said, when all three of them stood at the ring's edge, breath smoking, hearts loud. "I bring a payment. A memory of my mother's face. Given freely. Witnessed by these three."

**SPEAK IT ONCE, SO IT LEAVES YOUR MOUTH BEFORE IT LEAVES YOUR MIND.**

And Jonah described her mother's face. Slowly. Thoroughly. The strong jaw. The laugh-lines first, always the laugh-lines, deeper than the worry-lines because she'd laughed more than she'd worried, and how she'd been proud of that. The gray coming in at the temples like frost on a window. One ear pierced, the other not, because she'd lost the earring the day Jonah was born and called it a fair trade. Eyes the brown of tea with milk in it. A small scar over the left eyebrow from a fish-hook, which she told people was a duel wound.

By the end, Sefa was crying silently, furiously, wiping her face with her sleeve as if annoyed at it. Pell had removed his spectacles again and made no move to clean them. And Brannoc stood with his hand pressed flat over his own heart, holding the image the way he had once held relics, the way he had held dying men, with both arms and his whole attention.

**IT IS LOVED,** the god said, and its voice had changed — gentler, or sadder, or both. **IT IS WORTHY. ARE YOU CERTAIN?**

"Yes," Jonah said.

**THEN LET GO.**

Brannoc saw it happen and would spend the rest of his life failing to describe it. Nothing dramatic. No light, no sound. She simply closed her eyes, and her face — her face emptied. Not like forgetting. Like a tide going out. For one horrible moment she wore the expression of a woman staring at a page she had read a thousand times and finding it blank, and her hand rose halfway toward her own cheek, reaching for something that was no longer there to touch—

—and then she lowered it, and opened her eyes, and Jonah Sandoval looked out at them from behind a loss so total it had gone quiet.

"Gone," she said. Her voice was steady. That was the worst part. "I know I had it. I know what it was. I know I loved it." She looked at each of them in turn. "Tell me I'm not making that up."

"You're not making it up," Brannoc said.

"The scar was over the left eyebrow," Sefa said, sniffling. "Duel wound. Fish-hook, actually. And the gray came in like frost on a window, and she laughed more than she worried and was proud of it, and one ear was pierced and the other wasn't, fair trade, and her eyes were tea with milk in them, and—" Her voice broke. "I've got it. All of it. I don't know why I've got it, I only heard it once, but I've got it, Jonah, I swear on every stolen thing I own."

"Because you witnessed it," Brannoc said. His own voice was rough as gravel. "That's what witnessing is. You hold it now. All of us do."

In the center of the ring, the black pool began to rise.

Not flood — rise. A single column of dark water lifting out of the basin like a slow exhalation, and within it, turning, a fragment of the covenant sigil: a wedge of ancient metal the size of a knife-blade, etched with lines that hurt to follow, glowing faintly with a light like moonlight underwater. The column bent, and laid the shard on the frozen ground at Jonah's feet, and withdrew, and stilled.

**TAKE IT,** the god said. **AND KNOW THIS, STORM-CALLER. THE THIRD FRAGMENT DOES NOT SLEEP IN EARTH OR STONE. IT DROWNED WITH ITS KEEPERS IN THE SEA-GOD'S HALL, AND WHAT GUARDS IT NOW IS NOT A GOD BUT A CHOIR — THE DEAD FAITHFUL WHO SANG THE COVENANT INTO BEING, STILL SINGING, UNDER THE WATER, WHERE NO LIVING VOICE HAS REACHED THEM IN TWELVE YEARS.** A pause, like a held breath. **YOUR THIEF'S EARS MAY HEAR WHAT OTHERS CANNOT. REMEMBER THAT, WHEN THE SEA CALLS.**

Then the weight lifted, all at once, like a hand withdrawing, and the ordinary mountain rushed back in — wind, cold, birdsong, the small indifferent sounds of a world that did not know what had just been paid for in its presence.

Jonah picked up the shard. Wrapped it beside the first. When she turned to them, her face was dry, composed, almost calm — and Brannoc saw that the calm was not peace. It was the calm of a woman standing in a room where a piece of the floor had fallen away, deciding not to look down.

"Two," she said. "One to go."

Sefa crossed the ring and hugged her, hard, abrupt, wordless. Pell bowed, actually bowed, the awkward formal bow of a scholar to a text he'd never fully translate. And Jonah endured it all with her chin up and her empty place hidden, and Brannoc thought: *witnessing isn't watching her be strong. It's knowing exactly how much the strength costs, and staying anyway.*

They broke camp as the sun cleared the summit. They were halfway down the pilgrim road, frost cracking under their boots, when Pell suddenly stopped and pulled his charts from his coat, hands shaking.

"Brannoc." His voice was thin. "The moon. I've been tracking its turn against the fixed stars every night — I checked it this morning before dawn, I thought the numbers were wrong, I checked them three times—"

He turned the chart around. The inked arc of the moon's path had bent, overnight, visibly, toward completion.

"At this rate," Pell said, "it finishes the thirteenth turn in eleven days. Maybe fewer. Isandro won't wait for coronation season. He'll move the moment the sky is right — and Jonah, the third fragment is at the bottom of the sea."

Above them, the thirteenth moon hung pale against the morning blue like a scar that hadn't finished healing.

Jonah looked at it for a long moment. Then she tucked the shards against her chest, and started down the mountain at a pace none of them could quite match.

"Then we'd better learn to swim fast," she said.
