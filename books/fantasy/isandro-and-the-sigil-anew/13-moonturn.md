## 13. Moonturn

Jonah felt the moon turn before she saw it.

She was on the sanctuary's eastern walkway, where the Godsfen gave way to black water and reeds that whispered even when there was no wind, and her storm-scarred palm ached in a rhythm too steady to be pain. She had learned, over twelve years of hiding it, to read the scar like an instrument. Tonight it was keeping time.

She lifted her hand. Beneath the skin, the old lightning-bough of her exile-brand glowed faint blue, pulsing once every few heartbeats — and each pulse matched, exactly, the slow swing of the moon overhead.

The twelfth moon of the year hung low and fat over the fen, and it was wrong. Everyone could see it now; no one needed Pell Wicke's instruments or his frantic marginalia. It had gone from silver to a bruised white, the color of bone held up to lamplight, and its face had developed what the fen-folk were calling *the crack* — a hairline seam across its southern hemisphere, visible to the naked eye, as though the moon were an egg something inside had begun to press against.

Behind Jonah, the sanctuary was lighting itself for the vigil. Lanterns in the reed-woven windows. The smell of barley soup and tallow smoke drifting out over the water. Somewhere inside, Sefa was teaching the children a cheating song about the Chancellery, and their laughter carried thin and bright across the marsh, and Jonah stood with her aching hand raised toward a cracking moon and thought: *we have so little time.*

Pell found her there, as he always did — materializing at her shoulder with his coat pockets full of brass and his eyes full of arithmetic he couldn't stop doing.

"Twelve," he said quietly.

"I know."

"Twelve turns, Jonah. In one year. And tonight—" he pulled a battered astrolabe from somewhere in the ruin of his coat, consulted it without really looking at it, "—tonight it begins the thirteenth."

"How long?"

"That depends entirely on how long Isandro wants it to take." He snapped the instrument closed. "The old records say the last covenant war lasted one final turn. Twenty-eight days. If he's following the liturgy — and he is, Jonah, he follows everything, that's what makes him dangerous — then in twenty-eight days he sits on the thirteenth throne under a full moon that shouldn't exist."

Jonah lowered her hand. The scar kept pulsing anyway, patient as a clock.

"Then we don't wait for the third fragment to come to us," she said. "We go to it. Tomorrow. First light."

"The sea-cathedral? Through drowned waters guarded by a choir of the dead?" Pell's laugh came out cracked down the middle. "Yes. Obviously. Yes. I'll start on the tide-charts." He turned to go, stopped, turned back. "Jonah. Whatever happens tomorrow. Thank you for not letting me do this alone."

"You're not doing anything," Jonah said. "You're reading maps. I'm doing this."

"That's not how it feels from where I stand." And he hurried off into the lantern-light before she could decide whether to be comforted by that.

* * *

They left at grey dawn, six of them, poling flat-bottomed skiffs through the fen channels toward the coast road and the drowned cathedral beyond it. Jonah in the lead boat with Sefa, Brannoc, and two of the sanctuary's fen-sworn — Maretta, broad and silent, and young Corvin, who had never seen the open sea and would not stop talking about it.

Brannoc sat at the stern of Jonah's skiff with the second sigil shard wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, because the fragments had decided among themselves who should carry what and none of them had asked Jonah's opinion. Hers rode against her ribs. His — the memory-shard, the one bought with her mother's face — lay over his heart like a coal banked in ash.

He had been quiet all morning. Not his usual quiet, which was the quiet of a man listening to water. This was the quiet of a man rehearsing something.

"Say it," Jonah said finally, not turning around.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing since the second channel. You hum when you rehearse. You've been humming since the heron rookery."

A long pause. The poles creaked. Somewhere off to the east, thunder muttered — not hers, just weather, but her scar answered it anyway, a sympathetic string.

"I was keeper of the Rivergate temple for nineteen years," Brannoc said at last. "I buried three hundred faithful in that time. I remember every name, because that was the work. Names and water. And in nineteen years I learned one thing about death worth saying aloud." He shifted, and the oilcloth crackled. "It doesn't come when you're ready. It comes when you're needed. And the only dignity left to us, at that point, is to be needed well."

Jonah turned. "That's a grim sermon for breakfast."

"It's not a sermon. It's instructions." He looked at her with those river-grey eyes that had watched her circle burn and had come, years later, to find her selling charms in a godless city, and she saw that he meant every word as a gift, the way you leave instructions for someone who will need them later and won't thank you now. "If it comes for me, Jonah — don't stop. Don't carry me. Carry the shards. Promise me the shards."

"Nothing's coming for you."

"Everything is coming for everyone. That's what a moonturn *is*." He smiled, small and crooked. "Promise."

"I promise," she said, because refusing him would cost more than the word, and hated how easily promises like that slide out, how cheap they feel in the mouth and how heavy they become in the keeping.

Sefa, curled in the bow pretending to sleep with one eye open, said nothing at all. But her hand found the hilt of her knife and stayed there.

* * *

They made the coast by midday and the drowned cathedral by late afternoon — a black spine of masonry rising from the shallows, gulls wheeling its broken towers — and that was where the sky broke first.

Not with thunder. With *color*.

The sun was still up, low and gold, when the light went wrong. It happened all at once, the way a candle gutters: the gold drained out of the afternoon and left everything washed in a pale violet that had no business existing between sunset and dark. Shadows fell the wrong direction. Jonah's own shadow stretched out ahead of her, pointing at the cathedral, though the sun stood behind her shoulder.

Above the horizon, the moon rose early. Fat, cracked, bone-white, hanging in a violet sky while the sun still burned, and the two lights stared at each other across the world like rival kings.

Corvin stopped talking. Maretta dropped her pole. Even the gulls went silent, settling on the towers in rows, watching.

"The thirteenth turn," Pell breathed. He had come up beside her in the second skiff, tide-charts forgotten in his hands. "It's started. Gods forgive us, it's *started*, and we haven't got the third shard yet—"

"Then we get it now." Jonah was already moving, wading toward the cathedral's flooded nave, and behind her she heard the others follow — Sefa swearing cheerfully, Brannoc's steady tread, the splash and stumble of the fen-sworn. Fear makes people either freeze or march, and she'd learned long ago which kind of family she'd gathered.

Inside, the drowned cathedral did its dead singing, and Sefa heard the words no one else could, and the choir opened the way, and the third fragment sang into Jonah's hands — but none of that mattered afterward, none of it survived in her memory intact, because they were still in the nave when Maretta came running back down the flooded aisle shouting one word:

*Sails.*

* * *

They came out of the western channel at dusk, and there were too many of them.

Three Chancellery gunboats, black-hulled, riding low with armed men, and above them — this was the part that stopped Jonah's breath — three figures standing on the air itself, robed in grey, faces smooth as eggs. Isandro's new order. The men called them the Tidy Ones, because that was what they did: they tidied. Prayer-criminals, heretics, unlicensed wonders. They went into a village and it came out clean.

"They followed the singing," Sefa said, white-faced. "The whole coast heard it, Jonah. The whole *coast*."

"We can't outrun gunboats in skiffs," Pell said. His voice had gone very high and very fast, his thinking-voice pushed past its limit. "And we can't fight three ascendants, we don't have — Jonah, tell me we have something."

Jonah looked at the fragments. Three of five, warm against her skin, humming together like struck bells. She looked at the gunboats closing on the channel mouth. She looked at the sanctuary, far south across the fen — at the lanterns, at the children learning cheating songs, at forty exiled faithful who had taken her in when half of them blamed her for everything.

"We run for the fen," she said. "The gunboats can't follow us into the shallow channels. The Tidy Ones can — so I'll give them something else to look at." She rolled up her sleeve. The scar was blazing now, answering the wrongness of the sky, drinking the violet light. "Get everyone home."

"You haven't called a real storm since—" Sefa started.

"I know when I called it." Since the tenement. Since the mob and the mad god. Once, in twelve years, and it had nearly eaten her alive. "Go."

They went — except Brannoc.

* * *

The channels of the Godsfen at night are a maze built by water for water, and Jonah called the storm to blind the hunters and buy the maze time to do its work.

She stood in the prow of the last skiff and reached up into the wounded sky with her ruined hand and *pulled*. It came the way it always had — joy first, terrible and total, the sky opening to her like a door — and then the danger, the sense of holding something that could hold her back. Lightning walked the clouds in violet ranks. Rain came down sideways. Thunder rolled across the fen in sheets, and behind her, in the reed-labyrinth, the gunboats' lantern-glow swung confused and angry, hunting a storm-caller who was no longer where the lightning said she was.

It was working. For four minutes, it worked.

Then the Tidy Ones simply rose above the weather. All three, ascending through the rain on threads of stolen divinity, unhurried, faces smooth, and began walking across the sky toward the sanctuary — not toward Jonah at all. Toward the lanterns. Toward the children.

"No," Jonah said, and hauled the storm around, and that was her mistake — the split attention, the pull in two directions, the sky feeling her hesitate. The lightning slipped her grip like a wet rope. She staggered in the prow, tasting copper, and the nearest Tidy One turned its smooth face down toward her with the mild interest of a housekeeper finding a stain—

—and Brannoc hit it out of the air.

She never knew where he found the strength. A sixty-year-old disgraced temple-keeper with a boat-hook and a shard of god-stuff bound to his chest, leaping from the skiff onto the reed-island at the channel mouth where the thing was descending, swinging the boat-hook two-handed into the grey robe — and the shard flaring through the oilcloth like sunrise, because that was what the memory-shard did, it *remembered*, and what it remembered was power given freely, shared, thirteen-fold, and there was nothing in all Isandro's stolen hoard that could stand against that.

The Tidy One came apart like smoke in wind.

The other two stopped. Considered. And converged on the little island, on the old man standing alone at the fen gate with a boat-hook and a burning chest, and Jonah understood — with a clarity that felt like the storm itself speaking — what he had been rehearsing all morning.

"*NO!*" She threw herself toward the shore. The storm came with her, all of it, a wall of rain and screaming wind, but the channels were shallow and the skiff was slow and Brannoc had promised himself to this the way he had promised everything: names, and water, and being needed well.

She saw the second Tidy One reach him. Saw the grey hands close on the boat-hook. Saw Brannoc smile — actually smile, crooked and small, the same smile from the skiff, *instructions, Jonah, this is the instruction* — and drive the shard's light forward into the thing's smooth face.

The second one died. The third did not wait to die politely.

What it did to him took three seconds. What it left took the rest of her life.

By the time Jonah reached the island, waist-deep, storm howling, the Tidy One was withdrawing into the rain — called off, perhaps, by some calculus of its own — and Brannoc lay in the broken reeds with the oilcloth scorched away and the shard glowing against his still chest, and the fen gate behind him, the narrow channel to the sanctuary, was unbuilt-upon, un-entered, safe. Every gunboat lantern was retreating. Every child's lantern, far to the south, still burned.

His eyes were open. They found her and they were still river-grey and still watching water, some last current of it going out.

"Names," he said. It was barely breath. "Remember the... names..."

"I will," Jonah said. "I swear it. I swear it on the storm, Brannoc, I swear it on all thirteen—"

But the rest went into the rain, and the rain took it, and the storm she had called stood over them both like a mourner who did not know how to stop.

* * *

They buried him the next morning where he had fallen, at the fen gate, because the fen-folk said the gate-keeper should stay at his gate. Maretta dug. Corvin wept without shame. Sefa stood apart with her arms crossed hard, the way she stood whenever feeling got too big for her pockets, and Jonah caught her pressing something small into the grave-mound after the others turned away — a stolen thing, of course, a copper charm from some market years ago, clean-handed theft, a gift for a man who kept things for people.

Pell read the names. All three hundred, from the Rivergate rolls he had copied years ago and carried ever since without explaining why, and now Jonah understood why, and understood that Pell had known, in the way cartographers know where rivers will flood, and had said nothing because there had been nothing to say.

The shard they lifted from Brannoc's chest was cool now, and lighter, as if some of what it remembered had gone with him. Jonah wrapped it in fresh oilcloth and carried it against her ribs with the others, and the weight of four fragments was not four times the weight of one. It was something else. It was a debt.

That night the moon completed its first day of the thirteenth turn, the crack across its face visibly wider, and Jonah stood at the fen gate alone with the rain coming soft and ordinary at last.

Twenty-seven days. Two fragments missing. One grave behind her, and a sky tearing slowly open above, and somewhere in Vessengard a man in a chancellor's grey smile preparing a throne.

Her scar pulsed. The storm inside her answered.

*Come and take the fifth,* she told the distant city, told the smiling man, told the wrong moon. *We're done running.*
