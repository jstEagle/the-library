## 14. Ashes, Argued Over

They buried what was left of Brannoc in a shallow grave at the edge of the Godsfen, because the ground there was too soft for anything deeper and because none of them could bear to dig longer than they had to.

Jonah did the digging. She insisted. Her hands blistered and bled around the spade's handle, and she let them, because pain was simple and grief was not, and because Brannoc had spent twelve years under an aqueduct keeping faith alive with candle stubs and stolen hymnals, and he deserved better than to be lowered into earth by someone whose hands were clean.

Sefa stood at the graveside with her arms wrapped around herself, which was how Jonah had learned to recognize that Sefa was frightened — not when she talked, but when she stopped. Pell had not come out of his tent since the sanctuary burned. He sat inside it now, surrounded by his salvaged maps, and every few minutes Jonah heard the scratch of his pen, writing something no one would ever read.

The fen had gone quiet after the attack. Not peaceful quiet — the quiet of a room where someone has just died. Even the drowned god's remnant, deep beneath the black water, had withdrawn its presence, as if it too were observing some mourning custom Jonah didn't know.

She laid him down. She said the words from the old rite, the ones Brannoc himself had taught her over a guttering lamp in the aqueduct dark: *What the river keeps, the sky remembers.* Her voice cracked on the second line and she finished the rest in silence, packing the mud down with her bleeding palms.

It was Pell who broke first.

He came out of the tent at dusk, spectacles fogged, ink-stained fingers trembling, and he looked at Jonah across the fresh grave with an expression she had never seen on him before — not curiosity, not wonder, none of the bright scholarly hunger that had made her trust him. It was contempt, worn thin over fear.

"You should have run," he said.

* * *

The argument started the way all their arguments started, with Sefa stepping between people like a wall built out of spite.

"She couldn't have run," Sefa said. "Isandro's riders were already in the fen. You saw the fires."

"I saw the fires," Pell snapped, "because half the exiles in this marsh knew her name before she ever arrived. *Storm-caller.* They whispered it like scripture. Do you think Isandro's spies needed more than a week to find us? Every prayer-criminal between here and Vessengard was talking about Jonah Sandoval come back from the dead, and you—" he jabbed a finger at her, and his hand shook so badly the finger wavered like a reed— "you let them."

"Pell." Jonah kept her voice low. The storm-scar on her palm ached; it had been aching since the gate fell, a dull throb like a tooth gone bad. "Brannoc stayed behind because he chose to. I begged him—"

"You begged him while standing in the middle of everything! You stand in the middle of everything, that's the whole problem. You walk into a city square and use your power in front of a thousand witnesses because a tenement is burning, and it's noble, it's *right*, and then we all burn with you." His voice climbed. Somewhere out in the reeds, a heron lifted off, startled. "I have spent my whole life being careful. I measured the moon's turns in secret for six years. I copied forbidden star-charts by candlelight with my door barred. And in one month of knowing you I am a fugitive with a price on my head, sleeping in a swamp, and the only man here who understood what any of it *meant* is in the ground."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. Jonah felt each ripple.

Because he wasn't wrong. That was the worst of it. He wasn't wrong.

She thought about the tenement fire, chapter and verse of it: the mob praying to a half-mad god, the god answering with catastrophic literalism, the floors giving way, the screaming. She had felt her power wake like a muscle remembering itself, and it had felt *good* — that was the part she never said aloud. Calling the storm had felt like coming home to a house she'd been locked out of for twelve years, and she had stood in the rain with her arms open and let every eye in Vessengard see her do it.

She'd told herself it was necessary. Standing here in the fen-mud with Brannoc's grave between her feet, she could no longer fully believe it.

"Say what you actually mean," Sefa said quietly. Her voice had gone flat and dangerous, the pickpocket's voice, the one she used right before she took something. "You're not angry about the tenement. You're angry because you're scared. So say that."

"I am saying that her fame is a beacon and we are all moths around it!"

"And I'm saying you're a coward who does sums instead of feeling things—"

"*Enough.*"

Jonah hadn't meant to say it loudly. The word came out with weight behind it, a pressure she hadn't intended, and the air above the grave shivered. A breath of wind moved through the reeds though there was no wind anywhere else. Both of them went silent and stared at her, and she saw in their faces — Pell's fear, Sefa's sudden wariness — exactly what she had become to them: not a person. A weather system.

That was the moment she turned and walked away.

* * *

She walked until the fen gave way to hard ground, until the black water became brown fields, until the fields became the broken outskirts of a town she didn't bother to learn the name of. She walked through the night without calling any light, because using power felt obscene tonight, and by gray dawn she found herself standing somewhere she had sworn never to return to.

The old circle's meetinghouse.

It sat on a hill outside Vessengard's walls, or what remained of it did. Twelve years ago it had been a low stone hall with a turf roof, where seven young covenant-bearers met in secret to practice the old rites — back when practicing them was merely illegal rather than capital. Isandro's men had burned it the night everything ended. Jonah had always assumed time would finish the job. But the walls still stood, charred black, holding up nothing, like ribs around a heart that had been cut out.

She stepped through the doorway that was no longer a doorway.

Inside, the floor was ash and moss and small white flowers that grew in ruins because flowers don't hold grudges. The great hearthstone where they'd lit the covenant flame was cracked down the middle. Someone had scratched words into it once — she found them with her toe, knelt, brushed away the moss. Seven names, in a circle, carved by a seventeen-year-old girl with a chisel and more hope than sense.

Her own name was third. Isandro Vael's was fifth.

She sat down on the hearthstone, in the cold, and took out the thing she had been carrying against her chest for three days without letting anyone see it.

Isandro's letter.

It had arrived two days before the attack, delivered by a rider who wore no livery and asked no questions — folded paper, sealed with plain wax, no crest. *A gesture of civility,* the cover note said. Inside, in a hand so beautiful it looked printed:

*Jonah. Twelve years ago I offered you exile as mercy, and you have made of it something remarkable. I have watched you become precisely what this age requires. The offer stands, and improves with time: full restoration of your name, your rights, your family's plot in the Lanternfield cemetery. In exchange, your storm, given freely, in service of order. Come to the Chancellery. Bring nothing. Fear nothing. You will be treated as what you are — the finest talent this kingdom has produced in a century.*

*You were wasted on the gods. They take and give nothing back. I give back.*

*I.,*

She had shown no one. Not Sefa, not Pell. She had told herself she kept it as evidence, and that had even been mostly true.

Now she read it again by gray morning light, sitting on the stone where her old life had burned, and the terrible thing — the thing she would carry to her grave — was how reasonable it sounded. How *kind*. Full restoration. Her mother's grave tended again. A bed that wasn't mud. An end to running. All she had to do was stop fighting, kneel, and become a weapon in a beautiful man's elegant hands.

And Brannoc would still be dead either way. That was the arithmetic that gnawed at her. Whether she fought or fled or knelt, the gate had fallen, the old man had burned, and Pell was right that her name had drawn the arrow. What difference did it make what she chose next? The universe had demonstrated, quite thoroughly, that her choices cost other people.

The sigil shard in her pocket — the fragment Brannoc had pressed into her hand under the aqueduct, the first piece of the broken covenant — hummed faintly against her thigh. It had been humming more lately. Waking, like everything else divine in the world. Sometimes she thought it sounded like a question.

She almost threw it into the weeds.

Her hand was actually raised to do it — arm cocked, shard gripped, the whole motion begun — when she heard footsteps on the ruined threshold and knew before turning that it was Sefa, because Sefa walked like a cat that owned the place regardless of whose place it was.

"Don't," Sefa said.

"Don't what?"

"Throw it. You've got the throw-face. I've seen it on marks deciding whether to call the watch." She picked her way across the rubble and sat down on the hearthstone uninvited, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. For a while she said nothing, which for Sefa was practically a eulogy.

Then: "Pell's a coward, but he's not wrong about everything. Your name does draw trouble. People look at you and see the storm, and storms don't bury their friends." She turned, and her dark eyes were fierce and wet at the same time. "But here's what he doesn't get, because he's never been nobody. Before you, I was a thief with a gift I couldn't explain and no one alive who knew what I was. You know what that's like. We both know what that's like. And then you caught me stealing from you and instead of turning me in, you fed me dinner and asked me questions about myself like the answers mattered." Her voice roughened. "Brannoc chose to hold that gate. I chose to follow you into this swamp. Pell chose to bring his maps. Nobody chose *for* us, Jonah. Stop letting him put his fear in your hands like it's yours to carry."

"He buried a friend today because of me."

"We buried a friend today because Isandro Vael wants to eat a god and wear its skin," Sefa said flatly. "Blame the right monster."

Jonah looked down at the letter in her lap. And then, because hiding things from Sefa had become impossible somewhere along the way, she held it out.

Sefa read it twice. Jonah watched her face go through fury, then something colder and more thoughtful, then a strange kind of pity.

"He writes beautifully," Sefa said at last. "That's the most frightening thing about him. My mother used to say the devil doesn't tempt with ugliness." She folded the letter with precise, respectful creases — the hands of a thief handling something valuable — and held it out to Jonah. Then, when Jonah didn't take it: "You're going to burn it, aren't you?"

"I should have burned it days ago."

"You should have shown us days ago." Sefa pressed it back into her palm and closed Jonah's fingers over it. "Keep it. Not because he means a word of it. Keep it so you can remember what quitting looks like when it's wearing its best clothes."

* * *

Pell came at noon.

Jonah heard him before she saw him — the uneven crunch of his boots on ash, the wheeze of a man who had walked miles of fen-country in shoes meant for observatory floors. He appeared in the broken doorway with his spectacles askew and his satchel clutched to his chest like a shield, and he stood there breathing hard, clearly having rehearsed a speech on the way and lost all of it.

"I owe you an apology," he managed.

"No, you don't."

"Yes, I do, and I'm going to give it badly, so let me." He pushed his spectacles up. "I blamed you because blaming you was easier than blaming myself. I decoded the thirteenth turn. I *told* you what it meant. I knew Isandro's clock was running and I still spent weeks perfecting footnotes while Brannoc organized our defenses alone. When the riders came, I hid in my tent with my maps." His voice failed and restarted. "He died holding a gate I could have warded months ago if I hadn't been so busy being careful. Careful men make excellent cowards, Jonah. I've had a lifetime of practice."

He crossed the ruin and set his satchel down on the hearthstone beside the seven carved names, and began unpacking it: charts, fragments, the rubbing he'd taken of the second shard's burden-script, all of it ash-dusted and water-stained and saved.

"So," he said, when the last map lay flat. "Either we quit, or we finish this. Those are the options. I've done the sums on quitting." He looked up, and behind the fear in his eyes there was something harder, something that had survived a fire. "Quitting loses."

Jonah sat with the letter in one hand and the sigil shard in the other, on the stone where her life had ended twelve years ago, and finally let herself feel the whole weight of it — Brannoc's laugh, the gate burning, Pell's contempt, Sefa's fierce loyalty, the sweet poisoned reasonableness of Isandro's handwriting. The dark night of it. How easy stopping would be. How many good reasons there were.

Then she looked at the seventh name carved in the circle. Her own. Carved by a girl who believed the covenant was worth her whole life.

She struck a spark off her knife — no magic, just steel and flint, just an ordinary woman doing an ordinary thing — and touched it to Isandro's beautiful letter, and held it until the words curled and blackened and the ashes joined the ashes of the meetinghouse floor, where they belonged.

"We finish it," she said.

Pell nodded once, sharply, and pulled the central map toward them — the master chart, every fragment's fall marked in red. "There's a complication," he said, tapping the third mark, far to the south, out past the coast. "The sea-god's drowned cathedral. The choir guards it. Living things don't come back from the choir, Jonah. Not unchanged."

Sefa leaned over the map, and Jonah saw the exact moment the thief's grin came back — crooked, hungry, alive.

"Good thing you've got a thief," Sefa said. "Dead things keep valuables. Somebody has to steal them back."

Far to the south, beyond the hills, the sea waited. And in Jonah's pocket, the sigil shard hummed louder than it ever had — not a question anymore.

A summons.
