## 15. The Third Fragment Sings

The sea had swallowed the cathedral a thousand years ago and never quite finished the meal.

Sefa felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the water's taste, salt laced with something older, like tears that had been wept so long they'd forgotten what they were for. She stood at the edge of the drowned terraces with her boots sunk to the ankle in black silt, and she listened, because listening was the only thing she'd ever been good at that nobody could take from her.

Beneath the waves, faint as a coin dropped down a well, someone was singing.

"You hear it too," Jonah said. Not a question. Jonah had stopped asking questions about Sefa's gift somewhere around the second fragment, when Sefa had put her bare palm against a mountain stone and flinched back from the memory sleeping inside it.

"I hear it." Sefa wiped her hands on her trousers — an old habit, though there was nothing on them. Thieves' hands were always moving, always pretending to be busy so no one looked too closely at them. "It's not like the others. The fen god hummed. The mountain god *brooded*, if gods can brood, which apparently they can, because I've met two now and neither of them has any manners whatsoever. This is different."

"How?"

Sefa searched for words and hated how long it took her to find them. Words came easy when she was lying and hard when she was telling the truth; maybe that was true of everyone, but she suspected it was especially true of her.

"It's a choir," she said finally. "A lot of voices. And they're not singing *at* us. They're singing *around* something. Like — you know when you watch a pickpocket work a crowd, and everyone's looking at the juggler, and the juggler isn't the point, the juggler is just where all the eyes are kept busy?" She pointed at the water. "We're the crowd. The song is the juggler. Something under it doesn't want to be looked at."

Pell Wicke, up to his knees in the shallows with his maps wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to his chest like a breastplate, made a sound that was half scholarly delight and half terror. He'd been making that sound for three days.

"The drowned cathedral of Ulmoen," he breathed. "Every cartographer since the Covenant War marked it as lost. Swallowed in the Sundering, they said. Gone past finding." His spectacles fogged and he wiped them with the frantic tenderness of a man handling scripture. "And yet here it is. Here it *is*."

"Here it is," Sefa muttered, "at the bottom of freezing water, guarded by whatever's doing the singing, and the moon turns its thirteenth turn in six days." She looked at Jonah. "So. Same plan as the fen? You walk in, something shows you horrible visions of your past, you come out branded and crying, and Pell writes it all down very excitedly?"

Jonah almost smiled. It was a thin thing, that smile — twelve years of exile and two fragments' worth of burden had worn grooves into her face that hadn't been there when Sefa first tried to rob her — but it was real, and Sefa had learned to treasure real things the way other people treasured gold. Real things were rarer.

"Something like that," Jonah said. "Unless you have a better one."

"As it happens." Sefa flexed her fingers. "I do."

* * *

They waited for the tide to fall, because Pell insisted the old causeway revealed itself at low water, and Pell was right, which was annoying, because Pell was usually right, which Sefa privately considered the most insufferable quality a person could have.

The causeway emerged from the retreating sea like the spine of some enormous drowned animal: basalt slabs, each carved with a word in the old covenant-script, worn nearly smooth. Sefa walked it barefoot. She didn't know why until she was three slabs in and realized she could feel the words through her soles — faint vibrations, like footsteps in a room above hers.

"What are they saying?" Jonah asked. She walked beside her, boots in hand, having followed Sefa's example without being asked. That was another thing Sefa treasured: Jonah listened to her. Nobody had ever listened to Sefa before. In Vessengard's underworld, listening to a thief was how you got robbed.

"They're not saying anything yet." Sefa crouched, pressed her palm flat to the stone. The divine residue rose up through her skin like heat from a hearthstone — that was her gift, the ugly little miracle she'd never asked for, the ability to touch what the gods left behind. Most people got fire or flight or prophecy. Sefa got fingerprints. "They're... waiting. Holding their breath. Everything down here is holding its breath."

"For what?"

"For somebody to sing back, I think." She stood, suddenly cold in a way that had nothing to do with the wind coming off the water. "Jonah. When Brannoc told us about the fragments — he said each one tests its bearer. The fen showed you your circle. The mountain took your mother's face." She said it carefully. They all said it carefully now; the absence of that memory sat between them like a fourth traveler. "What if this one doesn't test you? What if it's not waiting for you at all?"

Jonah studied her. Storm-grey eyes, patient, terrible in their patience. "Then who's it waiting for?"

Sefa looked down at her own hands. Clean hands. Thief's hands. Hands that had touched a dead man's prayer-bead in a Vessengard pawnshop and heard, for the first time in her life, the echo of a god still living inside an object — the moment her whole miserable life had cracked open like an egg.

"Me," she said. "And I'd really rather it wasn't."

* * *

The cathedral rose out of the shallows at the causeway's end, or rather, the top third of it did — spires like broken fingers, a bell tower leaning at an angle that hurt to look at, its bronze bell furred with barnacles. The rest descended into green-black water through archways that had once, Pell whispered reverently, been open to the sky.

Inside, the flooding had been kind, or cruel in an interesting way. The nave stood half-drowned, waist-deep in clear cold water so still it mirrored the vaulting above. Pillars marched off into darkness. And everywhere — everywhere — were the dead.

Not bones. Sefa had expected bones. Bones she could handle; she'd picked pockets off corpses during the plague summer and slept fine after. These were shapes. Figures of pale light standing in the water and along the galleries, rank upon rank upon rank, hundreds of them, thousands, robed in the vestments of every faith that had ever sworn to the covenant. They did not move. Their faces were smooth as river stones, featureless, and from their mouths — or the places mouths should have been — came the song.

Up close, Sefa finally understood what she'd been hearing from shore, and it undid her a little.

It was the covenant. The whole of it. Not the corrupted liturgy the temples had recited in her childhood, not the Chancellery's sanitized version about order and obedience — the *original*. She could hear it the way she heard residue in objects, layer under layer: thirteen voices braided together, mortal and divine in equal measure, a promise sung as a contract and a contract sung as a prayer. *Share the burden. Share the power. Neither alone, neither above.*

Twelve years of silence had frayed it. Some of the voices were missing entirely, leaving holes in the harmony like pulled teeth. But the song went on. The dead faithful had kept singing through the whole godless decade, holding the melody the way a mother holds a note over a cradle.

"Oh," Pell said softly, from behind her. He had his notebook out and then slowly put it away, as if paper would somehow profane this. "Oh, my dear girl. Do you understand what you're hearing? This is the *primary text*. Every copy we had was transcription. This is the source."

"I know what I'm hearing." Sefa's voice came out strange. Thick. "That's the problem."

"Why?" Jonah asked.

"Because it's asking me something." Sefa hugged her arms around herself, water soaking her sleeves, and hated that her hands were shaking. "All my life, my gift has been taking. I touch things and I take what's in them — a name, a face, a secret. Information is the only currency I've ever had. And this—" she gestured at the choir, at the vast patient dark, "—this doesn't want to be taken. Taking it would be like... like robbing a funeral. Like lifting the purse off a corpse while the family's still standing there." Her throat closed. "It wants to be *answered*. It's been singing for twelve years and nobody answered, and it wants — it needs — someone to sing back."

The choir faltered.

Just for a heartbeat — a stumble in the harmony, a collective intake of breath across a thousand stone-smooth faces. Every luminous head turned. Not toward Jonah, bearer of two fragments, storm-caller, the one the whole plan had been built around.

Toward Sefa.

"Well," she said weakly. "That's settled, then."

* * *

She waded out into the nave alone, because the shapes parted around Jonah and Pell like water around stones, closing ranks behind them, and because — if she was honest, and she was trying, lately, to be honest, it was Jonah's fault — some part of her had known since the fen that this chapter of the story belonged to her.

The water deepened. At the crossing of the nave, where the altar must once have stood, the floor opened into a flooded crypt, and down in that green gloom something glowed: a sliver of light the size of a finger-bone, wedged in a setting of coral-grown stone. The third fragment. It pulsed in time with the song.

Between her and it, the choir had gathered. Closest of all stood a figure slightly larger than the rest, wearing the tattered suggestion of a bishop's mitre, and when it inclined its smooth head toward her, Sefa felt addressed.

"I can't give you what you want," she said. Her voice echoed strangely; the whole cathedral leaned in to listen. "I'm not faithful. I've never been faithful. Faith is for people who believe someone's watching. I grew up assuming nobody was, and honestly, the evidence was on my side for most of it." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I steal things. That's the whole of me. I take what other people leave lying around and I survive on it, and I'm good at it, and I don't apologize."

The choir sang on. Patient. Twelve years patient.

"But I can hear you," Sefa said, quieter. "Nobody else can. Not really — Jonah feels you, Pell reads you, but I *hear* you, every note, every hole in the harmony where a voice used to be. And I know what it's like to sing into a city that's outlawed the sound." She thought of Vessengard's rooftops, of the prayers people whispered anyway, illegal and hopeless, into pillows and gutters and cupped hands. She'd stolen from half of them. She'd never once known what they were praying for. "You've been singing for twelve years. So. Fine. I'll answer. I don't know the words — well, I do, actually, I have all of them, I just don't know if I'm allowed—"

The nearest figures raised their heads. All of them. A thousand faces, smooth and waiting.

"—oh, you *do* want that. Right. Yes. Okay." She wiped her face with a wet sleeve. "One condition. When this is done, when the covenant's reforged — somebody sings for the ones who died singing. Somebody remembers the melody you kept. Deal?"

The great figure in the mitre extended one pale hand. In its palm lay nothing at all, an offering of empty air, and Sefa understood: the gift was the answering itself. The dead wanted only what they'd always wanted. To be heard.

Sefa opened her mouth, and the covenant poured out of her.

She didn't decide to sing. That was the terrifying, holy truth of it — the words she'd absorbed through her gift, thirteen voices' worth of promise, rose up her throat like water finding its level, and her rough street-thief voice, a voice that had talked its way past guards and out of handcuffs and through locked doors its whole life, became for a few measures something else entirely. She sang the mortal lines, because the mortal lines were hers by right: *neither alone, neither above, share the burden, share the power.* And where the divine voices should have answered, the choir answered — a thousand dead faithful filling the holes in the harmony, and beneath them, deeper than the crypt, vaster than the drowned dark, something enormous turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to hum.

The water glowed. The fragment blazed. The pillars shed a thousand years of silt in a single shivering sigh, and for one impossible moment the drowned cathedral stood restored in light — whole, roofed, sunlit, full of the living — a memory of itself offered up like a flower.

Then it faded, and the fragment floated free of its setting, drifting up through the clear water into Sefa's cupped hands.

It was warm. It weighed nothing. And it knew her — she felt it settle against her palms the way a cat settles against a person who has, without meaning to, become its person.

"There," she managed, when she could speak. "Was that so hard? Twelve years. One song. Honestly, gods are just lonely people with better real estate."

The choir bowed — all of them, a field of pale wheat bending — and came apart into motes of light that drifted upward through the water and out through the broken roof, toward a sky none of them had seen in a millennium. The great figure in the mitre lingered longest. As it dissolved, Sefa heard, distinct from everything else, a single voice — an old man's voice, warm and tired:

*The key was never hidden, child. It was only ever waiting for someone willing to knock.*

* * *

Jonah and Pell found her sitting on the altar steps, fragment in hand, crying with the ungraceful full-body ugliness she usually saved for private.

Neither of them said anything stupid. That was the thing about her found family, the thing she still didn't fully trust: they let her cry without making it mean more than it meant. Pell sat down on her left and wrote nothing, which for Pell was a heroic act of restraint. Jonah sat on her right and put an arm around her shoulders, and the two fragments — the fen-shard on Jonah's arm, the mountain-shard at her throat, and now this one in Sefa's grip — hummed together, three notes of a chord that had been broken for twelve years and was, chord by chord, remembering itself.

"You sang the original words," Pell said at last, wonder-struck. "Do you realize what that means? You've heard the complete covenant. All thirteen parts. Which means we no longer need to guess at the terms of the reforging — we have the primary text, verbatim, in the head of a pickpocket."

"A *very talented* pickpocket."

"And I intend to spend every waking hour between here and Vessengard extracting it." He was already pulling out his notebook. "Because Sefa — because if the thirteenth part says what I think it says, if the empty throne was always meant to be *shared* — then Isandro hasn't just betrayed the covenant. He's misread it. Fundamentally. Catastrophically. And men who build cathedrals to their own misreadings do not take correction gracefully."

Sefa let him talk. She watched the light-motes of the dead rising through the water, free at last, and turned the warm fragment over in her fingers.

Three shards. Six days. One coronation clock ticking down under a wrong-colored moon.

"Jonah," she said quietly, when Pell paused for breath. "When we get to the city. When we go into that cathedral after him." She looked up. "If it comes down to choosing between stopping Isandro and saving each other — I want to hear you say, right now, out loud, which one you'll pick. Because I've been the disposable one before. Never again. Not with this family."

Jonah was silent a long moment. Out beyond the broken roof, the sea muttered against the stones, and far to the north, faint even at this distance, a smudge of unnatural light stained the clouds over Vessengard — the cathedral of the god of order, burning bright against the coming thirteenth moon.

"I pick both," Jonah said. "I refuse to pick. That's the whole point of everything we're doing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have."

Sefa sighed, pocketed the fragment, and stood, wringing out her sleeves. "Wonderful. We're going to war behind a woman whose entire strategy is *no*." She started up the aisle toward the light. "Come on, then. Both of you. We've got a god-king to disappoint and I want dinner first. Stealing a covenant fragment earns a person dinner."
