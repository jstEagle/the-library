## 17. The Offer Refused

The dream came again, but this time it did not pretend to be a dream.

Jonah stood on the shore of the Godsfen at moonrise, though she had gone to sleep in the sanctuary's driest loft, wrapped in two blankets against the fen's damp. The water before her was black glass, and the drowned cathedral of the sea-god rose from it whole and unbroken, its spires silvered, its doors open. Light poured out of those doors like something spilled.

*Come in,* said a voice that was many voices braided into one — the tide's pull and the thunderhead's growl and the small green insistence of things growing through stone. *We have waited twelve years to say this properly.*

She did not walk so much as arrive. The cathedral's nave stretched ahead of her, and along both walls sat the gods — or what remained of them. Some were vast and dim, shapes like weather given faces. Some were small as hearth-flames, guttering. All of them turned toward her with an attention that made her skin feel transparent.

In the center of the floor, where an altar should have been, stood a figure wearing her own face.

Not a reflection. The figure moved when she did not, tilted its head, smiled with her mouth in a way she had never smiled. It wore her storm-scarred palm open toward the ceiling, and light fell into it like rain into a cup.

*This is what we offer,* the voices said. *A vessel. One body, one will, one clean channel through which all thirteen can pour. You have felt how the power wants you. You have called storms through a broken covenant and survived. No other mortal alive could hold this.*

The figure raised Jonah's hand, and lightning ran up its arm without burning, and it laughed with her laugh, and somewhere far away — in the waking world, in the sanctuary loft — Jonah heard herself make a sound of protest.

*I could end Isandro tomorrow,* the vessel-Jonah said gently, kindly, in the voice Jonah used for frightened customers in her shop. *One storm. One word. You would be home. Vessengard would remember your name the way it remembers founders.*

"That's the problem," Jonah said aloud, and her voice cracked the cathedral's silence like ice on a pond. "You're showing me the version of me that wins. You didn't show me the cost."

*There is no cost. There is only capacity—*

"There's always a cost." She thought of Brannoc holding the fen gate, of the second shard's guardian taking her mother's face out of her memory, of Pell's maps covered in annotations about burden and reshaping. "I've been paying costs since I was nineteen years old. I know what they look like. And I know what a single vessel looks like."

She walked toward the figure wearing her face. Up close she could see the seams in it, the places where thirteen different lights strained against one skin, and beneath the borrowed smile its eyes were already going white the way Isandro's went white when he consumed a god.

"I know a man who was offered exactly this," she said. "Twelve years ago. He sat in a circle with me and five others, and he listened to the covenant's terms — shared power, distributed, no one above anyone — and he decided he'd rather be the only one. He burned us for it." Her scarred palm ached; she realized she had clenched it. "You're offering me his seat. His logic. A thirteenth throne with better manners."

*We are not Isandro—*

"No. But give one mortal everything, and you'll build him anyway. Maybe slower. Maybe with kinder intentions. It ends the same place: one vessel, one will, everyone else kneeling." She looked around the nave, at the vast dim shapes and the guttering flames, and made her voice steady. "I didn't survive exile and bury my friends so I could become a prettier version of the thing I'm fighting."

The silence that followed had weight. Water pressure. Somewhere in the cathedral's heights, something enormous shifted.

Then the rage came.

It rolled off the walls in waves — the storm-god first, whose voice was a rolling front of thunder: *THEN YOU ARE NOTHING. TWELVE YEARS WE HAVE STARVED AND YOU REFUSE THE ONE HAND OFFERED.* The hearth-gods flickered and spat. Something in the rafters shrieked in a language older than the covenant, and the black glass of the fen-water outside began to tremble.

Jonah planted her feet. She had stood in worse weather.

"You starved because the covenant broke," she said over the rising din. "And it broke because someone tried to hold all of you in one grip. I'm refusing to do the same thing from the mortal side. That's not ingratitude. That's the lesson learned."

*THE VESSEL IS MERCY,* thundered the storm-god. *A SINGLE CHANNEL BURNS LESS THAN THIRTEEN.*

"A single channel *breaks*," Jonah shot back. "Ask Isandro's cathedral. Ask the tenement mob's god who answered wrong because half of him had rotted away alone in the dark. Power hoarded goes mad. Every time. You've watched it happen for twelve years and you're so hungry you've forgotten."

The thunder gathered itself for another roar—

—and stopped.

Because the water at the cathedral's threshold had begun to rise. Not violently. Slowly, the way tide comes, patient as grief. And out of it walked the drowned remnant — the fragment-guardian of the fen, the god who had tested Jonah with visions of her old circle and branded her arm with salt-light. It was smaller than it had been in the visions, worn down to something like a woman-shaped absence filled with seawater and old hymns, but when it moved, the raging gods quieted, because it had been drowning longest and it knew the sound of desperation when it heard it.

*She is right,* it said. Its voice was quiet and wet and went straight through Jonah's chest. *I held one fragment alone for twelve years under the fen, and it ate me hollow. Hoarded divinity does not make gods stronger. It makes them smaller and crueler until only the cruelty is left.* It turned the ruin of its face toward Jonah. *You took my shard knowing it would brand you. You carried the burden instead of passing it off. Why?*

"Because it was mine to carry," Jonah said. "That's the whole point. Burdens shared are still burdens. They don't vanish. Someone has to actually hold the weight, or sharing is just a nicer word for dumping it on whoever's weakest."

The remnant was silent a long moment. Around the nave, the smaller hearth-flames had crept closer, guttering attentively.

*Then hear our counter-offer,* it said. *Not a vessel. A covenant renewed as it was written — thirteen seats, and among them mortal voices who bear fragments willingly, each carrying their share, none carrying all. You would not be our god. You would be our partner. Our hands. Our memory of what it costs.*

"And if we fail?"

*Then we fail together, which is more than any of us has been offered in twelve years.*

Jonah looked at the figure wearing her face. It had gone still, and now, as she watched, it came apart — not violently, but the way fog lifts, the thirteen lights within it drifting apart and settling back along the walls to their places. The nave breathed out.

"Then here are my terms," she said, and her voice did not shake this time. "All of you, not just the ones who agree easily. The storm-god rages, fine — he rages *with* us, at Isandro, not at the people sheltering in my sanctuary. The hearth-gods get their altars back in Vessengard, legally, with the Chancellery's seal on it. And nobody — no god, no mortal — takes a throne alone. Ever. If Isandro falls, the thirteenth seat stays empty until we find someone who *doesn't want it*. That's the only kind of person who should ever sit in one."

*A hard set of terms,* the remnant said.

"I'm told I'm a hard woman to bargain with. Ask my landlord."

Something that might have been laughter moved through the hearth-flames, a warm flickering ripple.

*Then it is witnessed,* said the remnant, and the salt-brand on Jonah's arm flared — not with pain this time, but with recognition, the way a lock knows its key. *The drowned witness, the fen's remnant, agrees. Who stands with me?*

For a moment, nothing. The great dim shapes at the far end of the nave remained dark, and Jonah's heart sank — until the smallest hearth-flame darted forward and settled on her shoulder like a bird, weightless and warm. Then a second. Then the green growing-thing that lived in stone unfurled from a crack in the floor and wound gently around her wrist beside the brand.

*Three,* said the remnant. *The rest will come when they see Isandro fall. Hunger makes cowards of us all. But three is enough to begin.*

"It's more than I had a week ago," Jonah said. "I had a shop and a fake name."

*Then go, storm-caller. The moon turns in four nights. Your thief has found the way into the drowned cathedral, and your astronomer has calculated the hour the sky tears. Be there when it happens — and be there as yourself. That was always the term that mattered.*

The cathedral began to dissolve around her, spires going soft, light folding into water —

—and Jonah woke in the loft with the taste of brine in her mouth and the hearth-flame still perched on her shoulder, real, warm, casting a small gold circle in the pre-dawn dark.

Below her, she heard Sefa's voice arguing with Pell about tide tables, and Brannoc's empty chair pushed back from the table where no one had moved it yet, and she lay still for a moment and let herself feel all of it — the grief and the warmth and the terrifying, ordinary love of the people downstairs.

Then she got up, flexed her branded arm, and went down to tell her family that the gods had said yes.

* * *

They did not take the news the way she expected.

Pell took it best, in his fashion — immediately pulling out his charts and demanding exact wording. "'Witnessed' — did it say witnessed? By whom? Was there a formal invocation?" He wrote as she answered, quill scratching fast, muttering about precedent and the original covenant's ratification clauses. "If three gods witnessed your refusal of the vessel-offer and counter-proposed partnership, that establishes standing. Under the covenant's own terms, Isandro's claim to the thirteenth seat requires either unanimous divine consent or conquest. You've just made consent impossible."

"I made three gods possible," Jonah corrected. "The rest are still sulking."

"Sulking deities are a rounding error. Precedent isn't." Pell tapped the chart of the moon's impossible thirteenth turn. "Four nights. He coronates at full turn. If we can present a living counter-covenant before the sky tears—"

"If," Sefa said from the doorway, arms crossed, one eyebrow up. She had salt dust on her sleeves from the night's reconnaissance of the fen approaches. "Meanwhile, our brave storm-caller is planning to walk into the sea-god's drowned cathedral, past a choir of dead faithful, to dig the third fragment out of a god's ribcage. In four days. With three gods, two shards, and whatever's left of my nerves."

"You've got a better plan?"

"No," Sefa admitted. "I've got a *route*. Which is better than a plan, because plans assume things go well." She dropped into Brannoc's chair before she could stop herself, then froze, then deliberately stayed sitting, chin up, daring anyone to comment. Nobody did. Jonah's chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with brands or bargains.

"The choir won't hurt Sefa," Pell said absently, still writing. "I've been cross-referencing the liturgical records. The dead faithful of the sea-god's cathedral weren't guardians by choice — they were congregants caught in the flooding when the covenant broke. Their song is the liturgy. They don't stop intruders. They stop *silence*."

Sefa went very still. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning," Jonah said slowly, watching her friend's face, "that the way in isn't force. It's the words themselves. The covenant's original words." She remembered what the remnant had shown her in the salt-visions — fragments of hymn, half-heard. "Someone has to sing them back."

Everyone looked at Sefa.

"I'm a pickpocket," Sefa said flatly.

"You're a girl who can touch the divine residue on objects and hear what's soaked into them," Pell said. "You once described the consecration rite of a temple you'd never entered, from touching a stolen candlestick. You already know more of the liturgy than any living priest. You just learned it sideways, off stolen silver."

Sefa stared at him. Then at Jonah. Then at her own clean, clever hands.

"I don't sing," she said.

"You will," Jonah said, and reached across the table and squeezed her wrist, right over the faint shimmer of divine residue that never quite washed off. "That's what family's for. We hold the weight while you find your voice."

Outside, over the black line of the fen, the wrong moon climbed toward its final turn, and in the sanctuary's small kitchen, with three gods flickering unseen in the air like patient candles, the exiles began to rehearse a resurrection.

Far to the east, beyond the fen and the mountains, the lights of Vessengard's new cathedral blazed all night — and in its highest tower, a man who meant to be a god dreamed of a circle of six friends, and woke, and ordered the coronation moved forward one day.

They would have three nights, not four.

The tide, as always, was already turning.
