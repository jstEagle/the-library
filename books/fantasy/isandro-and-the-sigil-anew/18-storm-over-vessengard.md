## 18. Storm Over Vessengard

Sefa had stolen a great many things in her life — purses, rings, a bishop's seal, once an entire afternoon from a man who deserved to lose it — but she had never before stolen a city's attention.

She did it at the Saltgate, just past dawn, with a bundle of papers and a voice that shook only on the first word.

"Vessengard!" she called, standing on the back of Brannoc's old cart while Jonah and Pell held the wheels steady against the press of the crowd. "You've been praying in your cellars for twelve years, whispering into your pillows so the Chancellery wouldn't hear. I know. I heard you anyway."

The crowd was thin at first — fishwives, dockhands, a boy selling hot chestnuts who'd stopped mid-pour to stare. Sefa had planned for thin. Thin crowds grew.

"You want to know why the moon is wrong?" She held up the first page of her stolen proof, the one she'd risked her neck in Isandro's cathedral to take. The paper trembled in the wind off the harbor. "I'll tell you why. Your Chancellor has been drinking your prayers like wine."

* * *

Three days earlier, they had come down out of the Godsfen with Brannoc's ashes in a clay jar between them, and Jonah had not spoken for the whole of the journey except once, at night, when she'd said, "He held the gate." As if that were a prayer, or an accounting, or both.

Sefa had watched her carry the jar into the city herself, wrapped in sacking like a thing too precious for porters. She had watched Jonah set it down in the safehouse under the tannery row and stand over it a long time, storm-scarred palm pressed flat against the lid as though she could pass warmth through the clay to a man who no longer needed any.

"He'd hate this," Sefa had said finally. "The moping. He'd call it *liturgical self-indulgence* and then make us all tea about it."

Jonah had laughed — a cracked, wet sound, but a laugh. "He would. He'd say grief is fine but it shouldn't be allowed to go stale."

"So we don't let it." Sefa had hopped up onto the table, because sitting still had never once been possible for her. "We spend it. Tonight. Tomorrow. We spend every coin of it on Isandro Vael's ugly face."

And now here she was, spending it.

* * *

The proof itself was simple, which was what made it deadly. Sefa had learned early that the best lies were complicated and the best truths were not. Six pages, pulled from the cathedral's archive vault during those eleven minutes when the whole building had held its breath around her:

A ledger of siphoned devotion, dated back twelve years, in the Chancellor's own hand. Entries like a vintner's inventory: *Fen remnant, drained. Sea-choir, harvested. Minor god of hearth-smoke, consumed entire.* And beside each entry, a column of numbers that Pell had spent two sleepless nights decoding — numbers that matched, exactly, the counts of missing persons from the prayer-criminal arrests. The faithful hadn't been imprisoned. They'd been *ingredients*.

The last page was worse. It was a coronation rite, half-drafted, titled in Isandro's elegant script: *On the Assumption of the Thirteenth Seat.* Underneath, a single line: *Let the moon be my witness, and my clock.*

Pell had read it aloud once, in the safehouse, and then gone to the window and been quietly sick into the alley.

Now Sefa read it aloud again, at the Saltgate, to four hundred people and growing.

"—and let none contest the seat," she finished, "for the seat shall have eaten its contenders."

Silence. The bad kind, the kind where a crowd decides whether to believe you or hand you over. Sefa felt it tilting, felt the moment balance on its edge like a purse on a ledge, and she did the thing she was actually good at: she made it personal.

"Maren Ostwick," she said. "Taken by the Chancellery eight months ago for lighting a candle at her window. Her daughter thinks she's in a work camp. She isn't. She's ash in a Chancellor's chalice." She held up the ledger. "There are nine hundred names in here. Nine hundred of your neighbors. I stole this from the man who wrote it, and I'm telling you — he is going to finish the job tonight, under that moon, unless you stop being afraid long enough to be angry instead."

A woman near the front began to cry. Then a man behind her said, in a voice like a door breaking open, "My brother was taken in the spring," and after that the silence didn't stand a chance.

* * *

By noon, the city had turned over like a bedsheet.

It wasn't a riot — Sefa had seen riots, had picked pockets in three of them, and this wasn't that. It was stranger and more frightening: people simply stopped pretending. Candles appeared in windows in broad daylight. Old women who hadn't dared speak a god's name in twelve years stood on their stoops saying them out loud, testing the words like ice on a pond. Someone painted the broken covenant sigil — Jonah's sigil, the shard of which now rode against Jonah's chest beneath her coat — across the face of the customs house in whitewash, crude and enormous, and nobody painted it back.

The Chancellery guards came out in force and found nothing to arrest that arresting wouldn't make worse. Sefa watched a young guard — barely older than the chestnut boy — stand in front of a crowd of two hundred singing something old and slow, and watched him lower his truncheon, and then turn around, and then walk away. That, more than anything, told her the day could be won.

But the moon told her otherwise.

She caught sight of it in the afternoon sky, pale as a fingernail paring, already fat and wrong. The thirteenth turn. Tonight it would be full — full thirteen times in a single year, a number that had never happened outside of catastrophe. Pell had explained it with maps and mathematics; Sefa understood it simpler. The sky was a lock, and Isandro had picked it, and tonight he meant to walk through the door.

"We need the crowd at the cathedral by moonrise," Jonah said. They were in the safehouse, and she was doing a thing Sefa had never seen her do before: arming herself openly. No more hidden scar, no more charm-seller's apron. She wore her storm-caller's coat, the old one, sun-faded at the shoulders, and the sigil shard hung outside her shirt where anyone could see it. "Not to fight. To witness. Whatever happens in there, it can't happen in the dark."

"And if he kills you in front of everyone?" Sefa asked. She hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, the way true things did around Jonah lately.

"Then everyone will have seen it," Jonah said, "and dead martyrs are terrible for tyrants." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Brannoc taught me that. One of the last things he taught me."

"Don't quote the dead man at me like that's comforting."

"It isn't," Jonah agreed. "It's a plan."

Pell looked up from the maps spread across the floor, ink-stained and hollow-eyed and steadier than Sefa had ever seen him. "The aqueduct route gets us to the cathedral close without crossing the processional square. If we move within the hour—"

"*We*," Sefa said, and stopped, and looked at the two of them — the astronomer with his stolen charts and the storm-caller with her dead friend's ashes — and felt something in her chest do a thing she didn't have a name for. She'd been alone since she was seven years old. Alone was a craft she'd mastered, a coat that fit. These last months had ruined the fit entirely. "Right. *We.* Fine. But if either of you dies tonight I'm keeping your boots, and I want that on the record."

"On the record," Jonah said solemnly, and Pell, who almost never laughed, laughed — short and startled, like a man surprised by his own heartbeat.

They went up through the city together, the three of them, and the city came with them.

* * *

Sefa had imagined the march a hundred ways and gotten all of them wrong. She'd imagined fear, and there was some. She'd imagined fury, and there was plenty. What she hadn't imagined was the singing.

It started at Tanner's Row — someone began the old covenant hymn, the one about the thirteen lamps, the one that hadn't been sung publicly since before Sefa could steal — and it moved through the streets like water finding its level. By the Processional Bridge there were thousands. Fishwives and clerks and the chestnut boy, who had abandoned his brazier and marched carrying the sign from his stall like a banner. Guards along the route who looked at the crowd, looked at their orders, and stepped aside — one of them saluting, actually saluting, an old woman with a candle.

Jonah walked at the front, and the crowd parted around her the way water parts around a prow, and Sefa walked beside her, and Pell behind with his maps bundled under his arm like scripture, and above them all the moon rose fat and white and wrong, and the sky around it had begun — Sefa saw it, everyone saw it — to shimmer faintly, like heat-haze in winter, like something tearing at the seams.

"They're scared of it," Pell murmured, watching faces. "But they're walking toward it anyway."

"That's the whole trick, isn't it," Sefa said. "Being scared and walking anyway." She thought of Brannoc holding the fen gate. She thought of eleven minutes in a dark vault with her heart hammering and her hands steady. "Somebody should write that down."

"I intend to," Pell said, "if anyone survives to publish."

* * *

The cathedral rose ahead of them like a wound in the skyline — Isandro's grand white edifice, built in eighteen months on money that had come from somewhere and stone that had come from somewhere else, its spire aimed at the thirteenth moon like a needle. Light poured from its doors. Not lamplight. Something older, and hungrier, and Sefa's gift woke in her unbidden, the way it always did near divine residue — she could *feel* the building from fifty yards, feel it the way a thief feels a loaded strongbox, dense and humming and stuffed past capacity with stolen power.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, that's *wrong*. That's a god's worth of wrong in there. More than one."

"Can you tell whose?" Jonah asked quietly.

Sefa closed her eyes and listened the way she listened to locked rooms. Residue had flavors. The fen remnant tasted of rain and rot. The sea-choir of salt and song. This was — she went cold. "Hearth-smoke," she said. "And orchard-blessing. And — Jonah, these are the little ones. The small gods. He started with the small ones because nobody missed them." Her eyes opened. "He's been eating the gods nobody prayed to anymore. Because nobody would notice."

"Tonight everybody notices," Jonah said.

They reached the cathedral close as the moon cleared the rooftops, full and enormous, and the shimmering tear in the sky above the spire widened by a finger's width, and inside the cathedral a bell began to toll — deep, wrong, a bell struck from the inside.

The crowd stopped at the steps. Thousands of them, filling the square and spilling down every street, candles lit, hymn faltering into silence. And the great doors of the cathedral opened, and light came out like a tide, and standing in it, robed in white and gold, smiling the smile that had charmed a nation into giving up its gods —

Isandro Vael.

Sefa had seen him once before, from a distance, at a pardon ceremony. Up close he was worse. Up close she could see what her gift showed her plain as a brand: the man was *full*, brimming, leaking power at the seams like an overfilled wineskin, and his eyes had gone the color of the tear in the sky.

"My people," Isandro called, and his voice carried impossibly, reaching the back of the square without effort. "How good of you to come. I had hoped for witnesses."

"You don't get witnesses," Jonah said, stepping forward, and her voice didn't carry by magic — it carried because ten thousand people had gone silent to hear it. "You get testimony. Everything you did is in their hands now." She gestured at the crowd, at Sefa, at the pages that had crossed the city faster than any guard could chase them. "Nine hundred names, Chancellor. Read them yourself, if you still remember how."

Something flickered across Isandro's perfect face — annoyance, quickly mastered, and beneath it, horribly, something that looked almost like relief. Like a man glad, at the very end, to be recognized.

"The storm-caller," he said warmly. "Twelve years, and you come home for my coronation. Sentimental."

"Not for yours." Jonah reached into her collar and drew out the sigil shard, and it caught the moonlight and threw it back wrong, throwing it back in thirteen colors. "For theirs."

The tear in the sky widened. The bell tolled again. And Sefa, watching Jonah square her shoulders beneath the wrong moon with the whole stolen city at her back, understood suddenly and completely that everything — the march, the proof, the singing, Brannoc's ashes, all of it — had been prologue.

Inside those doors, the thirteenth throne was waiting.

And Jonah Sandoval meant to take it away from him.
