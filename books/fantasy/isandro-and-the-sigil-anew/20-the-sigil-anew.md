## 20. The Sigil Anew

The sky over Vessengard was tearing like old linen.

Jonah stood in the shattered nave of Isandro's cathedral, rain falling upward around her, and held the three fragments of the covenant sigil in her scarred palm. They were warm. They had always been warm, she realized now — warm as a hand held too long, warm as the last touch of someone leaving. The fen-shard pulsed with drowned light. The mountain-shard hummed with the weight of everything it had ever been given. And the third, pulled from the choir's drowned cathedral, sang so softly she could feel it in her teeth.

Behind her, Sefa was bleeding from a cut above her ear and grinning anyway. Pell clutched his maps to his chest like a shield, ink-stained fingers trembling. Around them, in the ruined air, the allied gods gathered — not as figures, not as voices, but as *pressures*, presences that bent the rain and made the broken stained glass sing.

And on the dais ahead, upon the thirteenth throne, Isandro Vael was burning.

Not with fire. With *light* — the stolen radiance of every god he had consumed pouring out of him faster than his body could hold it. His skin glowed like paper held before a lamp. His eyes had gone white as the moon at its thirteenth turn. He gripped the arms of the throne, and the throne gripped him back, and Jonah understood with terrible clarity that he had won his coronation and lost himself in the same breath.

"Jonah." His voice came out layered now, a dozen voices braided into one, some of them screaming. "You came. I knew you would. You always did run toward the fire."

"You ran toward it first," she said. "Twelve years ago. Our circle. Our friends."

Something flickered across the burning face — grief, maybe, or its fossil. "They were going to give it away," he said. "All that power, all those gods, and they wanted to *share* it. Dilute it. Scatter it among farmers and fishermen and thieves—" his white eyes found Sefa, "—and pickpockets. Someone had to keep it whole. Someone had to be strong enough to hold what others couldn't."

"That's not strength," Jonah said quietly. "That's just hunger with better posture."

The cathedral groaned. Above them, through the torn roof, the moon hung swollen and wrong, completing its impossible thirteenth turn, and the tear in the sky widened like a wound remembering how to bleed. Through it Jonah could see — not stars, but the other realm, the divine country, its borders fraying where Isandro's stolen divinity burned holes in the covenant's old seals.

She looked down at the fragments in her hand.

Three pieces of a promise older than the city. Three burdens. And four bearers standing in the wreckage, if you counted the ones no one else could see.

"It has to be reforged here," Pell said, coming up beside her, voice cracking with the urgency of a man who had spent his life being told his numbers were madness. "The sigil was broken at the circle where your covenant-circle died — no. No, wait." He flipped a page, hands shaking. "It was broken *in* him. When he took the first god, the sigil broke inside him, and everything since has been the pieces trying to find their way home." He looked up at her, glasses fogged with rain that fell upward. "Jonah. It doesn't need a forge. It needs a *circuit*. Something to carry the power through and around and back. A storm is a circuit. Lightning goes looking for the ground."

"And I'm the ground," Jonah said.

"You're the *bridge*," Pell corrected. "If you do it alone, you're Isandro. If we do it together—"

"—then it's shared," she finished. "Then it's a covenant."

Sefa stepped up on her other side, wiping blood from her eye with the back of her wrist. "Just so we're clear," the thief said, "I'm about to help save the world, and afterward I'm stealing something expensive. That's non-negotiable."

"Sefa—"

"*Non-negotiable*, Jonah."

Despite everything — the tear in the sky, the burning man on the throne, Brannoc dead in the fen mud a hundred miles behind them — Jonah laughed. It came out cracked and wet and real. "Fine. Steal something expensive."

"I already have," Sefa said, and touched the shard in Jonah's palm, and for one moment her gift flared and she *heard* it — heard the covenant's original words the way she'd heard them in the drowned cathedral, the way only she could hear them. Her grin faded into something older and more frightened. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, it's a *vow*. It's not a spell at all. It's a vow, and vows need witnesses."

"We're the witnesses," Jonah said.

"No." Sefa shook her head slowly. "*Everyone* is. Look."

And Jonah looked — really looked — past the cathedral walls, past the city, and saw what the storm had been showing her all along. In the streets below, the people of Vessengard had come out into the rain. The faithful and the fearful alike, the ones she'd rallied, the ones Sefa's stolen proof had turned, the ones who had watched a storm-caller walk openly through their magic-free city and had not looked away. Thousands of faces turned up toward the torn sky. And every prayer they had ever prayed, every desperate word whispered to a godless heaven for twelve years, was rising around the cathedral like heat off summer stone.

"They never stopped praying," Jonah breathed.

"The gods never stopped listening," said the fen's drowned remnant, and its voice was water finding its level. "They could not answer. The covenant was broken. But they listened. Twelve years of listening, Jonah Sandoval. That is what you carry. That is what we all carry now."

Isandro rose from the throne.

It cost him visibly — the light poured off him in sheets, and where it struck the floor, stone turned to glass. "No," he said, and the dozen voices in him disagreed with each other, some pleading, some commanding, one — the oldest one, the one she recognized from the night her circle burned — simply *tired*. "No. I am the thirteenth. I sat the throne. The power is *mine*—"

"It was never yours," Jonah said. "It was never anyone's. That was the whole point, Isandro. That was the vow." She lifted the fragments above her head. "You broke a promise because sharing felt like losing. So we're going to share it, and lose nothing, and you're going to watch."

She turned to her family.

"Pell. You mapped every fragment's fall. You carried the burden of knowing when knowing nearly killed you. Take the mountain-shard. Bear what it bears."

Pell took it with both hands, the way you take a sleeping child, and gasped as the memory-weight flooded into him — and held on.

"Sefa. You heard the words. You're the key and the witness. Take the singing shard."

Sefa took it, and the song moved through her, and she began — half-laughing, half-crying — to sing the covenant's original words back into the air, her thief's voice rough and true.

"And me?" Jonah raised the fen-shard, the drowned light washing her storm-scarred palm. "I'll take the burden of the betrayed. Of everyone who listened and wasn't answered. Of Brannoc, who held a gate so we could stand here. Of every exile in the Godsfen." She closed her fist. "Together. Not hoarded. *Shared.*"

The gods pressed closer. The drowned remnant, the mountain's patient weight, the choir's lingering harmony — they did not pour themselves into a vessel. They *joined*. They became part of the circuit, part of the round, and Jonah felt the difference immediately: this was not power filling her, this was power *moving through* her, out and around and back, a wheel with thirteen spokes and no throne at its center.

"Now, Jonah," Pell shouted over the rising wind. "Call it! Not as a weapon!"

"Not as a weapon!" she agreed, and raised both arms to the torn sky.

She called the storm.

But not the way she had called it in her old life — not the fist of it, not the hammer. She called it the way you call a friend across a crowded room. She called it *open-handed*. And the storm came down through the tear in the sky like a river returning to its bed, lightning arcing from the divine country into the fragments, through Pell's steady hands, through Sefa's song, through Jonah's scarred palm, around and around, a wheel of light spinning in the ruined nave — and then out again, up, back through the tear, carrying with it everything Isandro had stolen.

He screamed as it left him.

It was not a villain's scream. That was the worst part. It was the scream of a man being set down after carrying too much for too long — relief and terror tangled together, because who are you when you are no longer the thing you stole? The divinity poured out of him in rivers of light, and the wheel caught it, and shared it, and sent it home — back to the drowned remnant in the fen, back to the mountain's guardian, back to the choir, back to every god he had consumed, and the tear in the sky began, stitch by stitch, to close.

The moon shuddered. Began, slowly, to turn true.

Isandro collapsed against the throne, small now, human now, glowing faintly at the edges like a banked fire. Jonah walked to him through the dying storm. Rain fell downward again, ordinary rain, cold and clean.

"Jonah," he whispered. Her name, only her name, one voice, just a man's voice. "I remember the circle. I remember — before. Was I ever—"

"You were my friend," she said. "That's the truth. Both things are true. You were my friend, and you chose this." She knelt so their eyes were level. "The Chancellery will want a trial. The city will want justice. But I want you to know — the covenant didn't fail because people are weak. It failed because you couldn't bear to be equal. That's the only thing you were ever guilty of that matters."

His white eyes cleared, briefly, to the brown she remembered from twelve years ago, from a circle of thirteen laughing around a fire. "Equal," he repeated, like a foreign word.

Then the guards came, and the light went out of him entirely, and he wept without sound as they led him away down the nave.

* * *

Afterward, the four of them stood on the cathedral steps while the city came back to life around them.

The tear was sealed. The moon was only the moon, silver and honest, beginning its slow return to the arithmetic of ordinary years. In the streets, people were touching their own palms, looking at the sky, testing the air the way you test a doorframe after an earthquake — is it safe, is it real, can I believe it. Somewhere a temple bell that hadn't rung in twelve years began, hesitantly, to ring.

Pell sat down right where he was, on the wet stone steps, and started laughing — the unhinged, delighted laughter of an astronomer whose impossible numbers had all come true. "Thirteen turns," he managed. "I *wrote a paper*. They laughed me out of the observatory. I'm going to have it framed."

Sefa dropped onto the step beside him, and Jonah sat down on her other side, and for a long moment none of them spoke. The rain had stopped. Steam rose off the stones.

"So," Sefa said finally. "We saved the world."

"It appears so," Pell said.

"And nobody's exiled anymore."

"Not even me," Jonah said, and heard the wonder in her own voice.

Sefa nudged her shoulder. "Then what are you going to do tomorrow?"

Jonah looked out at the city — at the crooked rooftops and the smoking chimneys and the bell tower ringing its rusty, beautiful peal. At the shop she'd left that morning with the sign still hanging, *Charms & Small Blessings*, its paint faded, its door unlocked because she'd never expected to come back.

"Open early," she said.

And in her pocket, the reforged sigil — whole now, a perfect circle of thirteen marks, warm as a held hand — hummed once, contentedly, like something finally home.

Above them, the last cloud broke apart, and the first true star in twelve years came out over Vessengard, and somewhere in the fen, Jonah liked to think, Brannoc's ashes felt the rain wash clean over the reeds, and rested.
