## 1. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The salt came down the mountain in the dark, the way all good things did in Thornfell.

Isandro Kavanagh—though no one on this road knew that name, and no one ever would—moved along the goat track above the ravine with a hundredweight of white gold strapped to their back, breath steaming in the thin cold. The moon was wrong tonight. They had felt it since dusk, a pressure behind the eyes like the moment before a storm breaks, and when they glanced up through the black lattice of pines they saw why: it hung swollen and silver over the eastern peaks, one turn fatter than any moon had a right to be.

The thirteenth moon. The old women in the valley villages muttered about it when they thought no one listened—the year the calendar broke its fast, the year the sky owed a debt. Isandro did not believe in debts owed by skies. They believed in salt, in dry boots, in keeping their head down and their name buried so deep even the worms couldn't find it.

Three years of that belief. Three years since they had walked out of Maren Kavanagh's crooked little house with nothing but a stolen knife and a name scraped out of their own throat like a splinter. Three years of being *Bram*, the quiet smuggler with the scarred hands who carried salt down from the high pans and asked no questions and answered none.

They were halfway across the scree shelf below Raven's Tooth when the voice began.

It started as wind. That was what Isandro told themselves at first—the wind funneling up the ravine, worrying at the rocks, making sounds that wanted to be words. But the wind did not pray. The wind did not say, in a low cracked whisper that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, from inside Isandro's own skull:

*O Great Ones Below the Root, O Mothers of the Salt and Stone—we are lost in the pass, our mule is dead, our daughter's fever burns—show us the road, show us mercy—*

And then the wind answered.

Not with thunder. Not with any grandeur worthy of gods. It answered the way a neighbor answers over a fence, casual and terrible: *Turn east at the split birch. There is a shepherd's hut with a warm stove. Your daughter will live.*

Isandro stopped walking.

The voice that had spoken the answer was theirs.

Not similar. Not an echo or an imitation. It was *theirs*—the voice they had heard every morning for eighteen years saying goodnight to Maren, the voice they had used to swear oaths and sing off-key and argue about the price of tallow. A voice that had not existed in the world for three years, because Isandro had fed it to a fire along with everything else. Names could be burned; they'd learned that much from the witch before they learned to hate her. A hedge-witch's craft, the oldest trick in her grimoire: give your true name away, bury it somewhere it can't be found, and become a person the world's ledger has no line for. Unwritten. Unsummoned. Free.

So how—how—was the old god answering prayers in a voice that lived only in Isandro's memory?

Their hands were shaking. They could feel it in the straps of the salt pack, the buckles rattling faintly against the tin. Around them the night had gone very still, the kind of stillness that meant something enormous was listening.

*Please,* said another voice, farther down the slope, human and ragged. *Please, is someone there? I saw a light—I saw—*

Isandro should have kept walking. That was the rule, the whole architecture of their life: see nothing, know nothing, be no one. Pilgrims on the mountain at night during the thirteenth moon were trouble wearing a cloak, and trouble had a way of remembering faces.

But the voice was failing as it spoke, fraying like rope under load, and Isandro's feet were already moving down the scree before their mind finished objecting.

* * *

They found him in the hollow beneath the split birch—a man past middle age, pilgrim's gray gone to rags, one leg bent where a leg should not bend. His mule lay nearby in the manner of things that have stopped being mules. He had a wax-sealed token clutched in both fists the way drowning men clutch driftwood, and his eyes, when they found Isandro in the dark, went wide with something worse than pain.

"You," he breathed. "You're the one it said would come."

"I'm nobody." Isandro knelt despite themselves, pulling their flask free. "Drink. Slowly."

"The voice." The pilgrim drank, coughed, gripped Isandro's wrist with fingers cold as river stones. "It spoke to us in a young voice. It said—" He stopped. Looked closer. Whatever he saw in Isandro's face made him go pale beyond his injury. "It's yours. The voice is yours. Saints keep me, it *knows* you."

"Nobody knows me." The words came out flat and hard, a door slamming. "What token is that? What seal?"

He looked down at his own fists as if he'd forgotten them. The wax was black, stamped with a sigil Isandro had never seen and yet recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face in a nightmare—a crown woven of interlocking rings, each ring biting into the next, a design that hurt slightly to look at, that seemed to shift when unobserved.

"I walked from the capital," the pilgrim whispered. "Fourteen days. I was a clerk, once, in the Chancery under King Aldric. Before the king began to change." His breath was going shallow now, each word costing more than the last. "There's a thing in the crown. In the sigil worked into the crown. It eats him, do you understand? Little by little. Some days he's lucid, some days he screams orders in a language that makes the guards bleed from the ears. And it's *looking* for something. Sending riders out along every road. Looking for a name."

The cold that slid down Isandro's spine had nothing to do with the mountain.

"They say the old gods woke this spring," the pilgrim went on, faster now, urgent, blood foaming pink at the corner of his mouth. "Woke hungry, because something was stolen from them long ago—a key, a binding-key, sold by a witch to save a child. And the gods have been *answering*, all over the country, and every answer carries the same voice. The voice of the key. The voice of the one the crown wants." His grip tightened, impossibly strong for a dying man. "I stole this from the Chancery vaults. It's proof. It's the king's own seal, pressed in the hour he was lucid, begging whoever finds it to unbind him before the moon finishes its thirteenth turn. There are maybe nine nights left. Ten."

"Give it to a priest," Isandro said. Their voice sounded far away to their own ears. "Give it to an army. Give it to anyone whose name exists."

"There is no one else." The pilgrim smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gentle and knowing. "The gods chose you. I heard them choose you, an hour ago, on this very mountain. They answered a prayer in your voice and then they told me to wait here, that the salt-carrier would come down the scree." He pressed the token into Isandro's hands, folding their fingers closed over the wax with the last of his strength. "Maren Kavanagh's apprentice. That's what the voice called you. It said—"

He never finished. His chest hitched once, twice, and then he was simply elsewhere, his eyes fixed on the thirteenth moon with an expression of profound relief, as if whatever waited behind it had turned out to be kinder than expected.

Isandro knelt in the frozen hollow for a long time, the token burning against their palms though the wax was cold.

*Maren Kavanagh's apprentice.* The old woman had sold them. Had sold their name—to whom? To what?—and the purchase price had been Isandro's survival, and the interest had come due tonight, three years late, on a mountainside under a broken moon.

Far below, in the valley, a dog began to howl. Then another. Then a dozen, all along the Thornfell roads, a rising chorus of animal terror aimed at the sky.

Isandro stood, shouldered the salt they no longer cared about, and looked east toward the lowlands—toward the capital, the crown, the thing wearing a king like a coat. Every instinct they owned screamed at them to run north, to run higher, to find some deeper hole in the world and pull it in after themselves.

But the gods had used their voice. Which meant the voice was loose in the world again. Which meant the invisible life they had built was already burning, and running would only decide what it burned down first.

"Maren," they said aloud, to the empty mountain, using the voice that wasn't supposed to exist anymore. "What did you *do*?"

The wind carried the question down the slopes, toward the crooked little house on the far side of the pass, where a witch sat waiting—as if she had known, all along, exactly which night her debt would come home.
