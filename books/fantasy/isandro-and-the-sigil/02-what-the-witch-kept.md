## 2. What the Witch Kept

The pilgrim's token grew warm against Isandro's chest as they descended, and by the time the smuggler's road spat them out at the treeline above Hollowmere, it had begun to feel less like wax and more like a second heartbeat.

Three years. Three years of salt routes and false names, of sleeping in shepherds' huts and answering to *Bram*, of burning every letter and burying every keepsake until there was nothing left of the person they had been but a scar on the palm where Maren Kavanagh had cut the name out. Three years of being no one, and in one night on the mountain, the old gods had reached through the dark and called them by the name they'd paid so dearly to lose.

*Isandro.* The word still rang in their skull like a struck bell that would not finish fading.

Hollowmere hadn't changed. It never changed. The village sat in its bowl of peat and pine like something the world had forgotten to swallow—twelve crooked houses, a shrine to a god with no name carved over its door, and at the far end, past the rotting fence, the cottage with the herb-drying racks and the chimney that smoked green in summer and grey in winter. Smoke rose from it now, thin and ordinary, and Isandro hated how much their chest ached at the sight of it.

They told themselves they were walking toward the cottage because the token demanded answers. They told themselves it was strategy: find out what the dying pilgrim's seal meant, find out why the gods were speaking in a dead child's voice, and then vanish again before anyone could follow the thread back to them. They told themselves a great many things, and none of them were true, and they knew it, and their feet carried them down the mud lane anyway.

The door opened before they knocked.

Maren Kavanagh stood in the frame exactly as memory had built her and nothing like it at all. Shorter than Isandro remembered—or perhaps Isandro had grown. Her hair had gone from iron-grey to white, braided tight against her skull, and her hands, those quick clever hands that could stitch a wound or hex a well, were spotted brown as old eggs. But her eyes were the same: pale, sharp, and unbearably knowing.

"You look half-starved," she said. "Come in before the mountain sees you."

"I'm not staying."

"You're already inside." She stepped back, and Isandro realized with a lurch that they had crossed the threshold without deciding to. The cottage smelled of yarrow and tallow and woodsmoke, of three years ago, of a childhood spent grinding herbs while Maren muttered over her ledger of debts. "Sit. I'll put water on."

"I don't want tea, Maren. I want to know what this is." Isandro pulled the token from their shirt and set it on the table between them. The wax seal caught the firelight—a sigil pressed into crimson, a crown wrapped in thorns, and beneath it, small enough to miss if you weren't looking for it, a single letter scratched into the wax with a pin. *I.*

Maren looked at it for a long moment. When she raised her eyes again, all the sharpness had gone out of them, and what remained was worse. What remained was guilt.

"Where did you get this?"

"A pilgrim. Dying on the Thornfell pass. He said—" Isandro's voice cracked, and they hated that too. "He said there was a thing wearing the king's crown. He said the old gods woke up, and when they answered prayers, they answered in *my voice*. He knew my name, Maren. A stranger on a mountain knew my name, and I have been no one for three years."

Maren turned away and busied herself with the kettle, though Isandro saw her hands shaking too badly to lift it. "Sit down," she said again, quieter now. "Please. This is not a story I can tell standing up."

Isandro sat. The chair was the same one they'd occupied at fifteen, arguing about Latin declensions and lunar phases. That felt like another life, because it was.

"The year you ran," Maren began, her back still turned, "the king's men came through the vale. Not soldiers—crown sorcerers. They were hunting for a key."

"What kind of key?"

"The kind that binds." She finally faced them, and her face was terrible in its honesty. "There is an old thing under the capital, Isandro. Older than the kingdom. The first kings found it and thought they'd captured it, but you don't capture a god—you bargain with one, or you feed it. Aldric's crown is not a crown. It's a lock. And a lock wants a key, and the key it wanted was a name. A true name, freely given, freely taken, bound in blood and wax." She sat down across from Isandro, heavily, like an old woman, which she suddenly was. "The sorcerers came to me because I kept the old ledgers. Because I knew whose names were still unclaimed in the valley. And they offered me a choice."

The kettle began to scream. Neither of them moved to take it off the fire.

"They said the binding needed a young name. A strong one. One given in love, so it would hold." Maren's voice did not waver, and Isandro understood that she had rehearsed this confession for three years, alone, in this kitchen, to no one. "They said if I refused, they would take it anyway—and take my hands while they were at it, and burn the cottage, and then go looking through the valley for whatever else might fit. I told myself I was saving you. I told myself a stolen name was a small price for your life."

"You sold my name." The words came out flat, strange, as though someone else were speaking them. "You didn't even sell it to save yourself. You sold it to save *me*, which means I can't even hate you properly, because you'll have made yourself the martyr of it."

"I don't want to be a martyr. I want to be forgiven, and I know I haven't earned it." Maren finally rose and took the kettle off the flame; the silence it left behind was enormous. "I cut the name out of you afterward because once it's bound to the sigil, it will call to you. Every year, a little louder. I hoped distance would keep you deaf to it. I hoped I'd die before it got loud enough to matter."

"It woke the old gods." Isandro leaned forward, elbows on the table, and stared at the token. "That's what the pilgrim meant. The gods sleep under the mountains, under everything, and they answer prayers—and now the sigil is using my name as its mouthpiece. Every prayer in the kingdom gets answered in *my voice*. Do you understand what that means? Half the realm is going to start dreaming of me. The king's hunters are going to be looking for a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice who doesn't exist, except now everyone's heard them speak."

"Yes."

"And the moon, Maren. The pilgrim spoke of the moon's thirteenth turn. What happens then?"

Maren closed her eyes. "The binding completes. The sigil finishes eating the king, and the thing underneath wears him openly. And the key—the name—is consumed with him. Burned up entirely." She opened her eyes, and they were wet. "You wouldn't just be hunted, love. You'd be *unmade*. There'd be nothing left anywhere for the gods to remember."

The fire popped. Outside, somewhere in the dark, a nightjar called its two-note question into the empty valley.

"So that's it," Isandro said. "Three years of erasing myself, and I was erased anyway. Just slower. Just on someone else's schedule." They laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "What do I do? Tell me there's something I can do, or I swear I'll walk back up that mountain and let it happen, because I am *tired*, Maren. I am tired of running from a name I didn't even get to keep."

"There is a way." Maren reached across the table and took Isandro's hand—the scarred one, the one with the wound where the name had been. Her thumb traced the ridge of old tissue, gentle as a benediction. "A binding made freely can be broken freely. If you go to the sigil—if you stand in front of it and speak the name back, refuse it, renounce it—it must release the king. That's the law underneath all laws, older than the crown. But it will fight you. It has the whole court to wear as masks, and it knows every face you've ever wronged, every fear you've ever fed. And you'd have to walk into the capital. Into the open. Where every eye in the kingdom will be looking for the voice in their dreams."

"How long?"

"The moon turns thirteen times in nineteen days."

Isandro pulled their hand free and stood, and paced the length of the little kitchen, past the drying racks, past the shelf where the old ledgers sat with their spines cracked like knuckles. Nineteen days. Nineteen days to trade invisibility for a war.

"Why?" they asked finally, turning. "Why help me undo it? You bought my survival with that name. Unbinding the sigil burns the name anyway—so either way I lose it. Why does it matter to you which way it goes?"

Maren rose slowly. Up close, in the firelight, she looked ancient and small and utterly unrepentant of her love, if not her crime.

"Because survival isn't the same as living," she said. "And because I'd rather you hate me for the truth than love me for the lie. Go to the capital, Isandro. Take back what's yours—even if all you can do with it is give it away." She paused, and something flickered behind her eyes, some last card she hadn't meant to show. "And take this with you."

She went to the ledger shelf and drew out a slim book wrapped in oilcloth, bound shut with black cord. She held it out, and did not let go when Isandro took hold of it.

"The sorcerers' copy of the binding rite," she said. "Stolen, the night after they took your name. I've had three years to study it, and there's a page missing—one page, torn out, and I think I know who has it." Her grip tightened. "The king's own lucid hours are getting shorter. If you mean to reach him before the moon does, you'll need to move tomorrow. Before dawn. Before the road fills with pilgrims who've started hearing your voice in their sleep."

Outside, far to the north, beyond the mountains, beyond the capital's distant glow, the moon was rising—thin and white and patient, one turn closer to its thirteenth.

* * *

Isandro did not sleep. They sat by the banked fire with the oilcloth bundle in their lap and watched Maren's shadow move behind the curtain of her sleeping alcove, and they tried to remember a single moment of their childhood that hadn't been, in retrospect, a debt accruing interest.

Near midnight, the token in their pocket grew cold for the first time since the mountain—and then, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, from the hearth-ash and the hollow dark between the walls, a voice that was their own voice said:

*Isandro. We are waiting.*

They did not tell Maren. Some debts, they decided, a person ought to carry alone—at least until they knew whether they intended to forgive her, or simply use her.

Dawn came grey and early. On the table, beside the bundle, Maren had left bread, cheese, and a sprig of rowan tied with red thread—for protection, or for apology, or for both. Isandro took all three, and walked out into the cold, and pointed their boots south, toward the capital, toward the crown of hungry light, toward the name that waited to be spoken and surrendered both.

Behind them, at the edge of the trees, Maren stood watching until the mist took them both.
