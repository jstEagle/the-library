## 3. A Crown of Hungry Light

The capital smelled of rot beneath its perfume. Isandro had forgotten that—three years of pine and cold stone and honest smoke, and now here was Vaelmoor again, all gilt and garbage, the river running brown with tannery filth while the nobles' quarter dripped rosewater from a hundred fountains. They came through the Salt Gate at dusk with a carter's ledger in one hand and a false beard itching under their chin, and no one looked twice. That was the whole art of vanishing: be dull, be small, be nothing worth remembering.

But the city remembered them anyway.

It started small. A beggar by the gate turned his head as they passed, and his cracked lips moved—not begging, *praying*—and the words that fell out were in Isandro's own voice, the voice they'd buried with their name three winters ago. *"Bram. Bram. Come home."* Isandro walked faster. At the fountain square, a fishwife argued with her daughter in a voice pitched exactly like Maren Kavanagh's, down to the wet rasp at the back of the throat. By the time Isandro reached the shadow of the palace wall, every window above them glowed with candlelight shaped like faces.

The old gods weren't whispering anymore. They were *calling*, and they were calling through everything.

Isandro pressed into an alcove beneath the aqueduct and pulled out the pilgrim's token. The wax seal had gone soft as skin in their pocket. Beneath it, the sigil-work was crude but unmistakable—a binding circle keyed to a name, and the name had been scratched out so violently the lead had torn. Someone had tried to erase it too. It hadn't worked. Erasure never worked; Maren had taught them that much truth among all her lies. You could only ever *give* a name away or *take* one back.

The moon hung low over the palace spires, swollen and wrong-colored, one night short of its thirteenth turn. Thirteen turns since the king had last been seen in daylight. Thirteen turns since the prayers of Vaelmoor had started coming back answered, wearing Isandro's stolen voice like a mask.

*"I'm coming,"* Isandro whispered to the token, to whatever listened. *"Stop shouting."*

* * *

King Aldric woke inside his own skull the way a drowning man surfaces—violently, briefly, and knowing the air would not last.

He was standing at his bedchamber window. He did not remember walking there. His hands rested on the sill, and they were not entirely his hands; the fingers had grown long and pale as candlewax, and the light that lived beneath his crown was pulsing up through the veins of his wrists like something breathing.

*Minutes,* he told himself. *You have minutes. Use them.*

He thought of the ledgers first—the small, stubborn habit of a man who had ruled for thirty years by counting things. Count what you still hold. He held: his name, mostly. His memory of his daughter's face, though it flickered like a guttering lamp. His horror, which was his own, unsullied, and therefore precious.

What he did not hold: the throne room below him, where the sigil sat the court like a puppeteer sits strings. He could feel them even now, the lords and ladies moving through the halls with that smooth, synchronized grace, their eyes catching the light at angles eyes should not catch it. When the sigil spoke through them—and it did, more each night—it used voices he knew. His dead chancellor. His daughter, who had fled to the coast two turns ago and whom the sigil imitated so lovingly, so *patiently*, waiting for him to break and answer.

"Who bound you?" Aldric said aloud, to the light behind his eyes. "Whose name is on you?"

The light laughed. It had been laughing for thirteen turns, and lately the laugh sounded less like a crown and more like a boy—young, frightened, furious. Somewhere under the gold, someone's stolen name was screaming.

Aldric gripped the sill until the wax-pale knuckles whitened further. *Hold on,* he told whoever that was, whoever that boy had been before the witch sold him. *Hold on. I am still the king, and I do not yield.*

The light surged. The minutes ended. And King Aldric drowned again.

* * *

Isandro went over the wall at midnight, because gates had eyes and walls only had stones.

Three years of climbing Thornfell's cliffs in sleet had made the palace masonry feel almost courteous. They went up the north face where the ivy had died and left its grip behind, past a window where a lady stood motionless, smiling at nothing, her reflection in the glass turning its head to watch Isandro climb while the lady herself never moved. Isandro did not look back. Looking back was how the mountain took climbers; apparently it was also how palaces took thieves.

They came over the roof into the heart of the building, and the heart was *wrong*. Every corridor below blazed with candles—hundreds of them, thousands, burning without wax, flames the color of the hungry moon. And everywhere, softly, the sound of praying. Courtiers kneeling in alcoves, guards murmuring at their posts, all of them praying in a chorus of borrowed voices, and threaded through every voice like wire through wool: *Bram. Bram. Bram.*

Isandro's hands shook. They pressed them flat against the cold stone and breathed the way Maren had taught them—four counts in, seven held, eight out—until the shaking became something they could carry instead of something that carried them.

*Terror is just resolve that hasn't chosen yet.* Maren's voice, in their head, in their own voice, in every voice. Fine. Then let it choose.

The throne room doors were oak banded in gold, taller than three men, and they opened at Isandro's touch without a sound, which was worse than any alarm.

Inside, the court was waiting.

Not seated—*arranged*. Hundreds of figures stood in perfect concentric rings around the dais, facing inward, and when Isandro stepped onto the marble their heads turned together, one motion, one creak of necks, and every face resolved into a face Isandro knew. The carter whose salt run they'd robbed their first winter free. The border guard they'd hexed to sleep and left to freeze. Tam, the other apprentice, whom Isandro had abandoned when they ran, who had taken the whipping meant for both of them. Maren Kavanagh herself stood nearest the dais, rendered in candlelight and malice, her smile kind and terrible.

"You erased yourself," the court said, in a hundred mouths, in one voice. "So we kept you. Every prayer unanswered in this kingdom for thirteen turns, we answered *in your voice*. Every debt unpaid, we signed *in your hand*. You are written all over us, little thief. Come and be collected."

On the throne, the king sat slumped, crowned in light that burned without heat, and his chest rose once—twice—and stopped, and the sigil wore him like a glove.

The moon reached the apex of the thirteenth turn. Through the great window, its light fell across the floor in a white road straight to the dais, and Isandro understood, with the calm that lives at the center of terror, that they had always been walking this road. Every step since the pilgrim died in the snow. Every step since birth, maybe.

They walked it.

The court did not stop them. The court *leaned in*, hundreds of stolen faces drinking in the sight, and the sigil's light flared so bright the shadows of the pillars lay pointing at Isandro like accusations.

At the foot of the dais, Isandro drew the token, tore the wax, and spoke the first line of the unbinding—the one Maren had taught them at nine years old, the one she'd sworn she'd never used, the one that required, at its final word, a name freely given.

The light screamed. The court screamed with it, a hundred beloved and betrayed faces contorting, and the candles guttered, and somewhere far above the sigil's true shape uncoiled off the king's brow like a serpent of script, letters peeling off the air themselves to strike at Isandro—

And Isandro opened their mouth to give it the only thing they had left.

Their name.
