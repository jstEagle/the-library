## 4. The Name I Keep

The sigil did not want to die quietly.

It screamed through the throne room in a hundred borrowed voices—through the mouths of courtiers frozen mid-curtsey, through the herald by the doors, through the king himself, whose body arched against the throne like a man drowning upward. The faces it wore were Isandro's faces: the miller's son they'd cheated at dice in Thornfell, the carter they'd abandoned on the flooded road, Maren, Maren, always Maren at the edges of the crowd, her mouth shaping words Isandro couldn't hear over the roaring.

The unbinding circle burned silver around the dais. Isandro knelt at its center with the pilgrim's wax token cracked open in their palm, and inside it, glowing like a coal wrapped in skin, sat the stolen name.

*Isandro Kavanagh.*

Not theirs. Never theirs. A key cut by an old god's smith and sold for three years of silence, three years of salt-smuggling and sleeping in hollows, three years of being no one so thoroughly that even the gods had to *borrow* the name to find them.

"Give it freely," the circle whispered, in the voice of every prayer ever answered wrong. "A binding held by theft is broken by gift."

Isandro looked up at King Aldric. Lucid now—the devouring light receded from his eyes like a tide going out—and what remained there was not gratitude or terror but something worse: recognition. He knew what was about to be asked of them. He had worn a name that wasn't his own for thirteen turns of the moon; he knew exactly what it cost.

"Do it," he rasped. "Whatever it takes from you. Do it, and I will owe you what no crown can pay."

"The crown can't pay," Isandro said. Their voice sounded strange to them—unrehearsed, unhidden. "That's rather the point."

They opened their hand.

The name rose off their palm like smoke given weight, and the sigil lunged for it—one last desperate grab, wearing Maren's face, wearing the miller's boy, wearing *their own face*, young and soft and still believing in hedge-magic and kindness—and Isandro looked at all of it, all of it, and said:

"That isn't mine. It never was. Take it back."

And they gave it. Freely. To the fire.

The sound the sigil made as it came apart was not a scream but a *sigh*—thirteen months of hunger exhaling all at once, the hungry light guttering down the king's brow like a candle pinched between wet fingers. The courtiers collapsed into themselves, weeping, laughing, clutching strangers who had been monsters a heartbeat before. The old gods' presence withdrew from the room the way cold leaves a stone when the sun finally finds it—not gone, perhaps, never gone, but *quiet*.

King Aldric slid from the throne onto his knees. A man again. Old, gray, hollowed out, but a man.

In the gods' ledger, somewhere beyond the veil of the world, a line went blank.

Isandro felt it go. It was the strangest sensation of their life—stranger than hearing prayers answered in their own voice, stranger than the sigil's screaming. They felt the space where their name had been, and it was not pain. It was *room*. An empty page. A door standing open in a house they'd only ever rented.

"Well," said a voice behind them, dry as autumn leaves. "You've made a proper mess of the ledger, child."

Maren stood in the ruined doorway of the throne room, staff in hand, road-dust to her knees, looking exactly as she had the day Isandro ran—except older, except tired, except with something in her eyes that might have been pride if pride weren't too small a word for it.

"You followed me," Isandro said.

"I waited on the mountain road for two days, then decided waiting was for people with patience." She limped forward, past kneeling courtiers, past the king still on his knees, and stopped at the edge of the scorched unbinding circle. "So. Unnamed. In the eyes of heaven and the eyes of the law both." Her mouth twitched. "I've known you unnamed most of your life. You wear it well."

"It doesn't feel like nothing," Isandro said. They got to their feet. Their legs shook. "It feels like—" They searched for it. "*Space.*"

"Aye." Maren's voice gentled. "That's what I hoped you'd find, when I sold the name. Not safety. Room." She looked away, at the broken sigil-light still fading from the throne. "I told myself it was survival I bought. It was. But I think some part of me knew you'd need the emptiness more than the name."

"You could have *told* me."

"And you'd have stayed angry instead of becoming yourself? Perhaps." She shrugged, old shoulders rising and falling. "Perhaps I'm a coward. I've been called worse by better."

King Aldric rose last of all, one hand braced on the arm of his throne, and crossed the dais slowly, like a man learning his own legs again. Up close he smelled of ozone and incense and thirteen months of someone else's will.

"They tell me," he said, "that a debt outside the law is the only kind a king can honor fully. No treasury. No pardon. No title." His voice was rough, but steady. "So ask. Anything the law cannot give you, I will give you anyway."

Isandro thought about it. Truly thought about it, standing nameless in a throne room full of witnesses, while the thirteenth moon set through the high windows and the first honest dawn in a year came up gold behind the mountains.

"I don't want anything from the crown," they said at last. "But there are roads out of Thornfell where smugglers hang pilgrims for carrying the wrong token. There are villages that pray to the old gods and get silence, or worse. If the crown owes me—owe *them*. Open the roads. Let the old names be spoken without a price."

Aldric studied them for a long moment. Then he laughed—a real laugh, rusty, startled out of him—and it echoed strangely in the throne room, as if the walls themselves were surprised to hear it.

"A hedge-witch's runaway asks a king for a country," he said. "Very well. Witnessed by this court and whatever gods are listening: the roads open. The old names speak free. On your word, nameless one."

"On my word," Isandro said. And found, to their surprise, that the word held weight even without a name under it. Perhaps especially then.

* * *

They left the capital at midmorning, before the celebrations could congeal into ceremonies, before anyone thought to ask the nameless savior to stay and be sculpted into marble. Maren walked beside them on the north road, complaining about her knees, the state of royal paving stones, and the ingratitude of apprentices generally.

At the fork where the smuggler's path climbed toward Thornfell, she stopped.

"There's a thing," she said, not looking at them, fiddling with her staff. "Witches keep books. Names, births, bindings. Mine has a blank page I've been saving." She thrust a battered leather journal at Isandro, abrupt, almost rude. "Write yourself in it. Whatever name you choose. First entry in your own hand."

Isandro took the book. The page was thick and cream-colored and utterly empty, and it did not feel like erasure. It felt like the first morning of spring after a long winter, when the ground is bare but everything underneath it is awake.

They thought of names for a long time. The ones they'd been called. The ones they'd called themselves in the dark. The ones that would have fit the person they'd been running from, and the ones that might fit whoever they were about to become.

Then they uncapped the ink, and wrote:

*Bram.*

Just Bram. Chosen. Kept. Theirs.

Maren read it upside down and snorted. "Hedge-name. Salt-runner's name. Good enough for the ledger of heaven, I suppose."

"Good enough," Isandro agreed, and closed the book, and shouldered their pack, and started up the mountain road toward home—with Maren limping after them, arguing the whole way about which of them owed the other an apology first, and neither of them willing to lose.
