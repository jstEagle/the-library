## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley of Vell forgot things the way old men forget names—slowly, politely, and with the firm conviction that whatever was lost was never really important in the first place.

It had forgotten the taste of magic three hundred years ago, when the last of the ward-stones was set into the mountainside and the whole valley settled into a silence so complete that even the wind seemed embarrassed to whistle. No witch had been born in Vell since anyone's great-great-grandmother's time. No enchanted sword hummed in any attic. No fairy ring had been reported since old Marta Brenner swore she'd seen one in 1811, and everyone agreed Marta had also once claimed her cow could predict rain, which, granted, it could, but only because it got grumpy before storms like every other cow in creation.

Dominic Marchetti had lived his entire thirty-one years in that forgetting, and he liked it fine.

He liked the way the mornings came up gray and unhurried over the eastern ridge. He liked the smell of his own bread, the creak of the third stair from the bottom, the particular grumble his stove made when it was sulking about the wood he'd fed it. He liked that nothing in Vell glowed, whispered, or levitated, and that the most alarming thing that had happened all winter was Mrs. Okafor's goat getting into his turnip patch, an incident that had ended in negotiations, a small payment of apples, and a lasting diplomatic relationship.

Because here was the thing about Dominic Marchetti, the thing he kept folded up small and quiet in the back of his chest where nobody could find it: he talked to animals. And they talked back.

This should not have been possible. In the valley of Vell, magic was not merely absent; it was suppressed, smothered under the old ward like a fire under a wool blanket. A gift like his should have been stillborn there, a candle in a flooded room. And yet.

It worked. Faintly. The way you can hear a song through a wall—not the words, exactly, but the shape of them.

He'd discovered it as a boy, crouched in the henhouse, when the old rooster Bertram fixed him with one baleful amber eye and said—not in words, not in anything so crude as words, but in a meaning that arrived fully formed in Dominic's head—*you again. The one who listens.*

Dominic had fallen backward into the feed and sat there, heart hammering, waiting for the sky to open or the wardens to come. Nothing happened. Bertram went back to bullying the hens. And Dominic, seven years old and already careful in the way of children who have something to hide, had gotten up, brushed the chaff off his trousers, and told absolutely no one.

Thirty-one years of telling no one. He'd gotten very good at it. He had a whole quiet system, the way other men had systems for cards or drink. He never spoke aloud to the beasts where anyone might see. He listened more than he talked. And he never, ever let on how much he understood, because a man in Vell who could hold conversations with livestock was a man who would very soon be having conversations with a priest, and then possibly with a stake.

So he kept it small. A murmured good-morning to the goats, disguised as farmer's nonsense. A low hum when the dog wanted to tell him about the fox by the mill. A lifetime of listening, folded up small, kept safe.

It was, he told himself, enough. It was more than enough. It was, on most days, the entire secret warmth of his life, like a banked coal he cupped in both hands and never let anyone see.

* * *

On the morning the storm came, Dominic was arguing with a hen.

"Madam," he said, in the tone he reserved for the truly unreasonable, "I have fed you. I have watered you. I have, at considerable personal cost, defended your honor before a weasel. The least you can do is lay the egg in the *nest* and not on the—yes. Thank you. Thank you so much."

The hen, whose name was Duchess and who had the soul of a minor aristocrat, regarded him with the flat satisfaction of a creature who has won. From her meaning came something smug and layered, like a curtsy performed insultingly well. *The cold floor makes the shell thin. Everyone knows this. You know this. You simply enjoy scolding.*

"I do enjoy scolding," Dominic admitted, gathering her up and relocating her, with ceremony, to the nesting box. "It's the only conversation most of you can sustain."

*The dog talks too much,* Duchess observed. *All feeling. No structure.*

"He's a dog. Structure is for buildings."

The barn cat, who had been pretending to sleep on the feed sacks in the way of cats who are actually listening to everything, opened one eye and contributed, with the air of a scholar correcting a footnote: *The dog talks much. Says little. You, small man, talk little. Say much. This is why the goats respect you and the geese do not.*

"Nobody respects the geese. Not even the geese."

He was still smiling at that—his private, crooked smile, the one that came out only in the barn, where the audience couldn't testify—when the wind changed.

Dominic felt it before he heard it: a pressure behind his ears, a hush in the rafters. Every animal in the barn went still at once, Duchess mid-cluck, the cat's tail frozen mid-flick. It was the silence of a held breath, and Dominic had lived among beasts long enough to know what it meant.

Something was coming that they didn't have a name for.

Then the wind arrived properly, slamming against the barn doors like a creditor, and the first snow came with it—fat, fast, wrong. Snow in Vell came down gentle and sideways, polite valley snow. This snow came down like it had a grudge.

"Right," Dominic told the room at large, and the room, being full of animals, understood him perfectly. "Everyone inside, everyone calm, and Duchess, if you lay on the floor one more time I will personally reconsider your title."

*You would not,* said Duchess.

"I might."

*You would not,* she repeated, but she was already on the nest, so the point was moot.

He got them all settled—the goats muttering their grievances in overlapping chorus, the dog, Barley, pressed warm against his leg and narrating his entire emotional life (*cold, wind, you, good, cold, wind, you, good*)—and then he trudged back through the whitening yard to the house, shoulders hunched, already looking forward to the stove, the bread, the long soft evening of a storm shut out.

That was the whole of his ambition, most days. A storm shut out. A fire lit. A quiet that belonged to him.

He got exactly one of those things.

* * *

The knock came at dusk, when the snow had gone from a grudge to a siege and the world beyond his windows had been erased entirely. Dominic was on his second cup of tea and halfway through mending a fence-glove when the sound came—three small knocks, spaced oddly, like someone who had to think about each one.

He sat very still.

Nobody traveled in this. The road over the pass had been closed for a week. The nearest neighbor, Mrs. Okafor, was a twenty-minute walk in good weather and a corpse-run in this. And nobody knocked like that—small, deliberate, *counting* the knocks, the way you'd knock if you'd been taught the shape of knocking but not the habit of it.

Barley was already at the door, and he wasn't barking. That was the second strange thing. Barley barked at wind, at shadows, at his own reflection with great conviction. Now he stood with his ears up and his tail low and utterly silent, and the meaning that came off him was not fear, exactly. It was older than fear. It was the meaning a dog has for thunder, for fire, for the vet.

*Important,* Barley said. *Small and important. Like a bone, but—* the meaning wobbled, failed, tried again— *but alive.*

"Well, that's clear as mud," Dominic muttered, and picked up the poker, because a man could be careful and curious at the same time, and opened the door.

The night fell into his kitchen. Snow swirled across the threshold and died in the lamplight, and standing in the middle of it, up to his ankles in white, was a child.

A boy, maybe seven, maybe eight—thin as a winter branch, wrapped in a cloak that had been fine once and had since traveled a very long way. His hair was dark with melt. His lips were blue-tinged. He did not shiver, which was the first wrong thing, because everything shivers, even the goats, even Dominic. He stood in the killing cold with the stillness of something that had been cold for so long it had made its peace with it.

And his eyes—

His eyes were the color of the ward-stone. Dominic had found a shard of one once, as a boy, half-buried by the mountain trail: a gray so deep it was almost silver, with a light in it that moved when you weren't looking directly at it. He'd thrown the shard in the river and hadn't slept for two nights. This child had that light in his eyes, twice over, and it was *moving* now, in the lamplight, like something swimming under ice.

"Are you—" Dominic started, and the boy's head tilted, birdlike, and he raised one small hand.

Not for help. For *silence.* A gesture so precise, so old in its shape, that Dominic's mouth simply closed.

The boy looked past him, into the warm yellow room, at the stove, the bread, the mended glove on the table. Then he looked at Barley. And Barley—the great coward, the barker-at-shadows, the dog who once fled from a butterfly—walked forward, past Dominic, past the threshold's safety, and lay down in the snow at the boy's feet. Not cowering. *Bow.* The way a dog lies down before something it has decided, in its oldest and deepest heart, is above it.

*Oh,* said Barley, and the meaning that came with it was one Dominic had never heard from him, or from anything: a word-shaped awe, vast and simple. *Oh. Oh, it's you. I didn't know I was waiting. I was waiting.*

"Barley," Dominic said hoarsely. "Inside. Now."

The boy's gaze came back to Dominic. Up close, he could see the boy was shivering after all—not the body, but something behind the eyes, a trembling that had nothing to do with cold. The boy was afraid. He was more than afraid. He was afraid the way a person is afraid when they have run so long that fear has become the ground they walk on.

And then the boy opened his mouth and spoke.

One word. One syllable, really, if you were being strict about it, and Dominic's mind, in the moment before his blood turned to ice water, was strict about nothing at all.

The word was not in any tongue Dominic knew, and he knew, in the way of a man who talks to animals, a fair number of tongues that were not tongues—bird-count and wolf-road and the slow grammar of cattle. This was none of them. It was older than all of them. It was a word that seemed to arrive in the room *before* the boy's voice made it, the way lightning arrives before the thunder that made it, and the stove-flame stood up straight, and the lamplight bent, and somewhere in the walls of the old house something that had slept since before Dominic's grandfather's grandfather turned over in its sleep and *listened.*

The word was a name. Dominic understood that much, the way you understand a blow. It was a name for the boy himself, or for what the boy carried, or for the thing the boy had crossed a mountain range and a killing storm to find—and it was not a name any human mouth should have been able to make, because it had edges, and the edges were *true*, and truth like that did not belong in a kitchen in the valley of Vell.

The boy said it, and the storm outside went quiet for exactly one heartbeat, the whole valley holding its breath, and then the snow came down again as if nothing had happened, and the boy swayed on his feet, and Dominic caught him on pure reflex, an armful of frozen cloak and hollow bones and that impossible light.

*Small and important,* Barley had said. *Like a bone, but alive.*

Dominic stood in his doorway holding a child who had just spoken a word older than every kingdom he'd ever heard of, and the cozy little evening he'd built—stove, bread, storm shut out, quiet that belonged to him—lay in pieces around his feet like a dropped cup.

He should shut the door. He should carry the boy back out into the snow, because whatever this was, it was not for the valley of Vell, which had forgotten magic so thoroughly and so well, and Dominic Marchetti had spent thirty-one years being small and quiet and *safe*—

The boy's hand curled into his shirt. Weak as a bird's foot. Holding on.

"Right," Dominic said to the storm, to the dog, to the listening dark behind the walls of his house, in the voice of a man who has just watched his quiet life walk off a cliff and is deciding, rather against his better judgment, to follow it down. "Inside, then. Before we all freeze."

He carried the boy in, and shut the door, and did not see—because it was behind him, and because he was not looking—the snow on his doorstep, where the boy had stood, melting away in a perfect circle, as if the cold itself had been afraid to touch him twice.
