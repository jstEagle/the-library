## 2. A Word Older Than Kings

Dominic woke to the smell of porridge and the sound of his dog not barking.

That second part took him a moment to place, the way a wrong note takes a moment in an otherwise familiar song. Bramble was a good dog and a talkative one — or she would have been, if talking were a thing dogs did out loud. In the valley of Vell, where magic had been wrung out like water from cloth generations before anyone now living was born, Dominic's gift for hearing beasts survived only as a kind of faintness: a whisper under a door, a sense of meaning without words. He had learned, over forty years, to live with the whisper. He had never once told another living soul about it, because in Vell a man who claimed to speak with animals was either a liar or a madman, and both sorts tended to attract attention.

Attention was the one crop the valley grew in abundance.

So when he came down the stairs and found Bramble sitting perfectly still at the foot of them, ears up, gaze fixed on the hearth with something that could only be called *devotion*, Dominic's stomach performed a slow, cold roll.

The boy was awake.

He sat on the floor by the fire with the blanket around his shoulders like a small grey king in exile, watching the flames with an expression far too old for a face that couldn't have seen more than eight winters. His boots — someone's castoffs, three sizes wrong — were steaming gently. On the table beside him lay the stub of charcoal Dominic kept for marking feed sacks, and beneath it a piece of slate he did not remember leaving there.

Bramble looked from the boy to Dominic and back again, and what came through the whisper was so clear it needed no words at all: *be careful with this one*.

"Good morning," Dominic said, because a man has to start somewhere.

The boy didn't answer. Of course he didn't. He'd made no sound since arriving half-dead on the doorstep — no cry, no whimper, nothing even when Dominic had peeled the frozen clothes off him and found the bruises underneath, old ones, yellowed and green, the kind a child gets from hands that are supposed to be kind. He'd only watched Dominic work with those grave dark eyes, as if filing everything away against some future need.

But now the boy picked up the charcoal, and held up the slate, and turned it around.

He had drawn a bowl. Beside the bowl, a spoon. Beside the spoon, a stick figure with a worried mouth, which Dominic chose to interpret as himself rather than take personally.

"Porridge," Dominic said. "Yes. I'm making it. You'll notice I've already started, given the smell." He gestured at the pot with the ladle, feeling absurd. "You can put the slate down. We'll manage."

The boy set the slate down with great care, exactly parallel to the table's edge, and then folded his hands in his lap and waited, patient as a stone, while Dominic stirred and salted and muttered. It occurred to Dominic, somewhere between the salt and the muttering, that he had lived alone for eleven years and had forgotten how loud silence could be when someone else was keeping it with you.

He gave the boy the first bowl. The boy ate the way he did everything else — carefully, completely, wasting nothing — and when he finished he washed the bowl himself in the basin without being asked, drying it with the hem of the blanket, and then looked up at Dominic with an expression of such solemn apology that Dominic laughed before he could stop himself.

"It's all right," he said. "You can stay till the storm breaks. Then we'll see about getting you home."

At the word *home*, something crossed the boy's face. Not fear exactly. More like the look of a man shown a map of a country he'd been told didn't exist anymore.

He reached for the slate.

* * *

By midmorning the snow had stopped and the light outside had gone hard and blue, and Dominic had learned three things about his guest.

First: the boy could not or would not speak. Not a sound. When Dominic tried to teach him simple words — *bread*, *door*, *Dominic* — the boy's lips moved, shaped the syllables faithfully, and produced nothing but breath, as though speech were a room he'd been locked out of.

Second: the boy could draw like nobody's business. Not childish scribbles, either. Within an hour the slate held a recognizable map of the valley — the river's bend, the mill, the crooked pine on Torver's Hill — rendered in quick confident strokes, as if he'd been studying the landscape out the window all morning and memorizing it.

Third, and most troubling: the beasts loved him.

It began with the hens. Dominic went out to the coop with feed and found all six of them pressed against the wire nearest the house, silent, wings slightly lifted, in the posture hens assume when they are witnessing something holy. The boy stood among them — Dominic hadn't even heard the door — and they did not scatter. They turned their foolish orange eyes up at him and waited.

Through the whisper, faint as ever, came something Dominic had never felt from a hen in his life. Reverence. There was no other word for it. It rolled off them like heat off a stove.

"They're just chickens," he said aloud, and heard how unsteady his own voice was.

The boy looked at him. Held out one hand, palm up, unhurried. And the largest hen — Agatha, a tyrant among hens, a bird who had once driven off a fox through sheer force of personality — stepped onto the boy's palm and bowed her head.

Dominic stood very still in the snow with the feed bucket hanging forgotten from his fist.

In Vell, magic did not work. That was the whole point of the valley; everyone said so, proudly, the way you might boast of a wall that kept the sea out. Whatever gift Dominic had been born with had spent its whole life here muffled, starved, reduced to whispers. But the boy standing in his chicken run with a bowing hen on his hand was not muffled. The air around him felt *thick*, the way air feels before lightning, and every animal within a hundred yards knew it.

The boy set Agatha down gently and looked back at Dominic, and his expression was the expression of a child waiting to be punished.

"You're not going to peck me, are you?" Dominic asked the hen. It came out as a joke. It wasn't one.

Agatha said nothing, because hens say nothing, but the whisper off her was warm and certain and utterly alien: *the little lord is come home.*

Dominic went inside and put the kettle on and did not look at the valley for a full minute, because looking at the valley meant looking at the road, and looking at the road meant admitting what he'd been refusing to admit since dawn: that a child who made hens bow was a child other people might come looking for.

The boy drew while Dominic drank tea he didn't taste. Slate after slate. A tower, tall and thin, drawn with loving precision. Hands — two hands, cupped, holding a spark of light. Fire, once, drawn fast and hard enough to snap the charcoal, and then scrubbed away immediately with the boy's sleeve, scrubbed and scrubbed until the slate was clean and the boy's face was blank and his breathing was careful.

And once — only once — a word.

Dominic had asked him his name, that morning, and gotten nothing. Now, unprompted, the boy wrote four letters on the slate in a hand too fine for a child: *ISAN*. He stared at it a long moment, as if the word tasted strange, then added nothing more and set the slate face-down.

"Isan," Dominic tried.

The boy flinched. Not much. Just a tightening around the eyes, like a man hearing a half-remembered tune.

"Not Isan, then." Dominic turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Part of it. The front end of it."

The boy watched him. Waiting, Dominic realized, to see what he'd do with the piece he'd been given. Most people, he suspected, got no pieces at all.

"All right," Dominic said. "Isan. For now. I'm Dominic. Dom, if 'Dominic' wears your jaw out."

And the boy — Isan — did something extraordinary. He smiled. Small, crooked, rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in years. And through the open window behind him, from the coop, from the barn, from the winter-still woods beyond the fence, the whisper rose all at once, dozens of small voices braided into one glad murmur, and Dominic's skin prickled from wrist to shoulder.

They weren't murmuring at the boy.

They were murmuring at *him*. At Dominic. As if something had begun, and they had all been told, and he alone was the last to know.

* * *

The patrol came at dusk.

Dominic heard the horses first — six sets of hooves on the frozen road, moving in that disciplined cadence no farmer's horse ever manages — and was at the window before his mind finished the thought. They came down from the north pass in a line: riders in grey-and-black, faces masked to the eyes, and on each breastplate a device that made Dominic's mouth go dry. An open eye above a broken chain. The mark of the Inquisition of Kaelen Voss, which had no business being three hundred miles south of anything worth inquiring into.

"Inside," he said quietly, and Isan — who had been drawing at the table — was already moving, slate clutched to his chest, folding himself into the space behind the woodbox with the ease of a child who had hidden from men before. Bramble lay down across the gap in front of him, casual as a rug.

The knock, when it came, was polite. That was somehow worse.

Dominic opened the door on six masked faces and a wall of cold. The leader was a tall woman with frost in her eyebrows and a voice like a ledger being balanced.

"Evening. Inquisitorial survey. We're asking after a vessel — a child, likely traveling alone. Have you seen such?"

"A vessel?" Dominic let his brow fold, let his shoulders round, became in three seconds the dull, helpful, half-deaf farmer he had played his whole life. "Like a jug? My sister broke my good jug last spring, if that's—"

"A child," the woman repeated, without inflection. "Alone. Possibly mute. Possibly... unusual."

Every hair on Dominic's arms stood up beneath his shirt.

"Can't say I have." He scratched his jaw, bought a beat, sold the confusion honestly. "Lots of children in Vell, mind. Halvard's girls, the miller's boys. None mute that I know of. Unusual—" he chuckled, hollow and easy, "—it's Vell, mistress. Unusual left us years ago and didn't write."

The woman studied him. Behind the mask her eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of someone who had looked at ten thousand liars and knew precisely how many of them were good at it. Snow ticked softly against the eaves. From the woodbox, silence — total, practiced, professional silence, and Dominic marveled distantly that a child could hide so well, and hated the world that had taught him how.

"The ward-line here is old," the woman said at last. "Old wards fail strangely. If anything passes through it — anything that shouldn't be able to — you'll send word to the garrison at Cold Ford."

"What sort of thing passes through a ward that shouldn't?"

"That," said the woman, turning her horse, "is rather the question, isn't it."

They rode on down the valley, six points of black against the blue snow-light, and did not stop at any other house, which meant they already knew which house mattered, which meant someone had told them, which meant—

"Dominic."

His name. Spoken aloud, in a voice like a dry leaf, from behind the woodbox.

He turned. Isan stood in the firelight, slate raised, and on it, in that fine sure hand, were letters in no alphabet Dominic had ever seen — angular, ancient, wrong in the way a word is wrong when it belongs to a language older than the mountains — and beneath them, in plain honest Common, the boy had written:

*I know that word. The one they want. It is me.*

The fire popped. Outside, far down the valley, the hoofbeats faded toward Cold Ford, and every beast on the farm — hens, Bramble, the old mare stamping in her stall — fell silent at once, listening to a sound none of them could hear yet.

Something was coming. Something had already begun.

And the boy on his hearthstone, holding a slate like a shield, had just told him the war had found its battlefield: a farmhouse in a valley that forgot, guarded by a man whose only weapon was a whisper.
