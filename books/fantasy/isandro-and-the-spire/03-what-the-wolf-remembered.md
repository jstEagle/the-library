## 3. What the Wolf Remembered

The morning after the inquisitors left the valley, Dominic woke before dawn out of habit and lay listening to the house breathe.

It was a good house for breathing. Forty years of his family had lived in it, and the beams had settled into the particular creak-and-sigh of old wood that has made its peace with its burdens. The fire had burned down to a red memory in the grate. Somewhere above him, Isandro slept in the spare room — Dominic could hear the small, even rhythm of the boy's breathing through the floorboards, which was strange, because he shouldn't have been able to hear it at all.

That was the thing about Vell. The valley was supposed to be silent. Not quiet — silent, in the way that mattered. The ward that lay over it like a wool blanket smothered everything uncanny. Magic didn't work here. Gifts didn't reach here. For thirty-eight years, Dominic Marchetti's one secret — the thing he'd never said aloud to another living soul — was that his gift *did* reach here, faintly, like sunlight through a curtain. He could hear animals. Not words, not really. Impressions. A hen's dull contentment. A goat's opinionated irritation. The dog he'd had as a boy, whose name had been Bristle, and who had loved him with a simplicity that still made his chest ache to think about.

But last night, standing in his kitchen while Isandro drew spirals in spilled flour, Dominic had heard the valley's beasts *notice* the boy. A chorus of small attentions, like birds going quiet before a storm. And this morning he could hear a child breathing through floorboards, which was either his imagination or something worse.

He got up, stoked the fire, and put water on to boil, because dread is easier to carry with tea in your hands.

* * *

Isandro came down when the light did, padding into the kitchen in the sweater Dominic's mother had knitted for a nephew who'd grown out of it two decades ago. The sleeves went past the boy's fingertips. He held them up like a supplicant asking for breakfast, which was, Dominic was learning, exactly what they were.

"Porridge," Dominic said. "Again. I know. A man of my talents ought to manage eggs at least once."

The boy tilted his head — his way of asking *why not*, since his mouth wouldn't do the asking for him.

"Because the hens are sulking." This was true. He'd heard it on his way to the coop: a low, feathered grievance that someone had been late with the scratch grain two mornings running. "And a man doesn't argue with hens before sunrise. It sets a bad precedent."

Isandro's mouth twitched. In three days the boy hadn't spoken a single word aloud, but he'd drawn a fox, a wheel, a tower with too many windows, and — yesterday, unprompted, in careful flour-spirals on the table — a word that had made Dominic's skin prickle for hours afterward. He ate his porridge now with the solemn concentration of someone who had perhaps not always been certain there would be porridge.

Dominic was washing the bowls when he heard it.

Not the hens. Not the goats. Something at the treeline, at the far edge of the east field where the pines began and his land ended and the wild country started. A presence, large and low and *watching*, pressed against the boundary of his awareness like a palm against glass.

He set down the bowl very carefully.

In thirty-eight years, nothing wild had ever reached him from beyond the near pasture. The ward dulled everything; distance killed the rest. Whatever stood among those pines was reaching *through* all of it, deliberately, the way a voice carries across a lake when the caller cups their hands around their mouth.

And then, for the first time in his life, Dominic Marchetti heard an animal speak in words.

*Man of the valley.* The voice was female, dry as autumn grass, and arrived inside his skull with the unmistakable weight of being addressed. *You hear me. Good. That saves an hour of howling.*

Dominic gripped the edge of the basin. The bowl in it rattled.

"You—" he said aloud, and then felt foolish, because the kitchen was empty except for a child watching him with wide dark eyes. He lowered his voice. "You're speaking. Wolves don't speak. Nothing speaks, not properly, not *here*—"

*I am not properly here,* said the wolf, with what he could have sworn was amusement. *Come to the treeline, man of the valley. Alone, if you can manage it. Bring no iron. And bring food if you have any decency, because I have run four nights and eaten mice, and mice are an insult.*

"Dominic?" Isandro had slid off his chair. He couldn't have heard any of it — the boy's ears were ordinary ears — but he'd seen his guardian go white and start talking to the washbasin, and children learn quickly which adults are worth following into trouble. He tugged Dominic's sleeve and pointed, unerringly, toward the eastern window. Toward the pines.

Of course, Dominic thought numbly. Of course the boy would know.

* * *

He brought bread and the heel of last night's mutton, because whatever else she was, she'd asked politely, insofar as a voice in your skull can ask politely. Isandro walked beside him the whole way despite explicit instructions to the contrary, and Dominic found he lacked the will to enforce them. There was something in the boy's stride — purposeful, almost eager — that suggested he'd been waiting for this wolf the way you wait for a letter you already know is coming.

Frost silvered the stubble of the east field. At the treeline, where the pines threw their long blue shadow across the snow, she stood waiting.

She was larger than any wolf Dominic had seen, and he'd seen wolves, at a distance, in the years before the fear of them had faded from the valley. Her coat was grey shot through with silver at the throat and forelegs, and her left flank carried a wound — a long, ugly tear, crudely closed, that she was holding stiff, the way soldiers hold hurts they've decided not to mention. Her eyes were amber and ancient and fixed on him with an attention that made him feel like a specimen pinned to a board.

She did not growl. She sat, which somehow frightened him more.

"Hello," Dominic said, because thirty-eight years of talking to animals had apparently prepared him for nothing better than pleasantries.

*You're taller than I expected,* she said. *And softer. They told me to look for a keeper's line — strong shoulders, patient hands, a face that listens. You have the face, at least.*

"They? Who told you?"

*All in order.* She rose, limped two steps closer, and stopped at the exact edge of his field, one paw on frost, one on shadow, as though the boundary meant something to her. Which, he realized, it might. *First: the boy. Is he well? Fed? Unharmed?*

"He's—" Dominic stepped instinctively half in front of Isandro, who peered around his legs with frank curiosity. "He's fine. He's fed. Who are you, and how do you know about him?"

Something moved behind the wolf's eyes — relief, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. *My name is Serra. I was sent by the wardens of the Spire, what remains of them, which is three. I was sent to find the key before the usurper's hounds do. And I have found him standing in a frozen turnip field wearing a sweater with a hole in the elbow, so my report will be brief and humiliating.*

"The key." Dominic's mouth had gone dry. "The inquisitors used that word. *Vessel.* They were asking after—"

*Voss's people are not subtle,* Serra said. *They broke a courier hawk over the mountains to find me. They'll break worse things to find him. Which is why I ran ahead of them, and why I'm bleeding, and why you're going to invite me somewhere warm whether you like it or not.*

"I haven't agreed to anything."

*No,* she agreed. *You've agreed to feed me, though. I can smell the mutton.*

Wordlessly — what else could he do — Dominic crouched and laid the bread and meat on the snow between them. She took the mutton with a delicacy that contrasted absurdly with her size, swallowed it in two motions, and then, after visible internal debate, deigned to eat the bread as well.

*Mice,* she muttered, mostly to herself. *Four nights of mice.*

"Dominic," Isandro said.

Dominic nearly fell over. In three days the boy had made sounds — breathy half-words, a hummed note when the porridge was good — but never his name, never anything aimed with such precision. He looked down. Isandro wasn't looking at him. He was looking at the wolf, and his whole small body was leaning toward her like a compass needle finding north.

Serra's ears came forward. For a moment the dry irony drained out of her and something older showed underneath — reverence, worn soft with use, the way a prayer book's pages wear thin.

*Hello, little key,* she said gently. *You grew. The last time I saw you, you were a bundle in a woman's arms, and the sky over the Spire was burning.*

Isandro made a sound. Small, broken, urgent — and then he dropped to his knees in the snow and began to draw, fast, with one bare finger, sweeping the frost away in strokes: a tower. A spiral. Two hands, cupped, holding something bright.

*I know,* Serra said softly. *I know. I remember her too.*

"What," Dominic said slowly, "is happening?"

*What's happening,* Serra said, turning her amber eyes back to him, *is that the covenant that keeps magic leashed to law instead of ruin is dying, because its keeper died eight years ago and nobody renewed it. What's happening is that this boy is the only living thing that can renew it, and Kaelen Voss means to see it end instead, because a world without magic is a world where men like Voss win by default. What's happening is that I have crossed the mountains with a torn flank to fetch him, and every night I delay, Voss's hounds get closer. That's what's happening, man of the valley. I regret to inform you that it is also happening to you.*

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere behind them, in the barn, the goats registered opinions.

"I'm a farmer," Dominic said. "I keep bees. My most significant achievement this year was a fence the deer respected."

*The valley chose you,* Serra said. *Or the keeper did, before she died. Someone sent the boy to the one place magic couldn't follow, and put him on the doorstep of the one man in a hundred miles who could still hear a whisper through the ward. Coincidence is a story people tell when the truth is too large. You were chosen. Get used to it; everyone in this tale was.*

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "I have lived my entire life being *no one*. Quietly. On purpose. That was the whole arrangement. Nobody notices the beekeeper. That's the *point* of the beekeeper."

*Yes,* said Serra, and for the first time her voice lost its edge and became simply tired, and old, and kind. *And I have lived my entire life being a weapon, and I can tell you which of us sleeps better. But sleep is about to become scarce for both of us, so I suggest we spend what's left of it deciding what to do.*

Dominic looked at the wolf. Then at the boy, kneeling in the snow, drawing spirals with a finger gone pink with cold — the same spirals he'd drawn in the flour, the ones that had made the hens fall silent and the dog next door sit down in the middle of the road and refuse to move until the drawing was done.

Everything in him wanted to say no. To carry the boy back inside, bar the door, let the world stay outside where it belonged. Thirty-eight years of hiding sang in his blood like a familiar hymn.

But he had heard the beasts notice Isandro. He had heard the *reverence*. And he had spent his whole life listening to creatures that no one else could hear, and if there was one thing he knew, it was what it cost to be unheard.

"Come inside," he said finally. "There's a shed off the barn — straw, a stove, and a door that latches, which matters, because if my neighbors see a wolf in my kitchen we'll both be explaining ourselves to someone worse than inquisitors. I'll look at that flank. And then you're going to tell me everything, from the beginning, including what a *warden* is and why three of them are sending wounded wolves to fetch children."

Serra inclined her head, a motion of surprising grace. *Acceptable terms.*

"And," Dominic added, gathering his courage, "you're going to explain how you know his mother."

The wolf went very still.

*Ah,* she said at last. *Yes. That.* She turned toward the house, limping, and spoke over her shoulder as she went. *Then I'll tell you the other thing first, man of the valley, because you should hear it indoors, sitting down. Last night, on the ridge above the south pass, I smelled smoke on the wind. And under the smoke — ash, and burnt feathers, and old wards failing.*

She looked back at him, and her amber eyes were grim.

*Your neighboring village is burning. And the wind is blowing this way.*
