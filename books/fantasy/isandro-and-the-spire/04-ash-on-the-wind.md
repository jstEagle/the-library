## 4. Ash on the Wind

The wind brought the smell before it brought the sound.

Serra lifted her head from where she lay curled at the foot of Dominic Marchetti's bed—she had told herself she was guarding the child, though a smaller, more honest part of her admitted she simply liked the warmth of the hearth and the way the man snored like a contented bear—and tasted the night air coming under the door.

Woodsmoke. But not the good kind, not the sweet resin-smoke of a neighbor's hearth on a cold night. This was smoke with fat in it. Smoke with thatch and wool and something else underneath, something that made the old instincts rise along her spine like hackles.

Burning village.

She was moving before she had finished deciding to move, flowing over the sleeping forms—Dominic on his back, one arm flung out; Isandro curled small as a comma against his side—and nosing open the door with her shoulder. The boy's drawings were scattered across the table inside. She did not look at them. She was already looking east, toward Thornmere.

Thornmere sat two valleys over, close enough that on clear nights you could see its lamps strung along the river like dropped beads. Tonight there were no lamps. There was an orange glow sitting on the horizon like a wound that refused to clot, and above it, sparks rising and dying, rising and dying, patient as snowfall in reverse.

*They found something,* Serra thought, and the thought tasted of ash. *Or they decided to find something. With those men, it has never much mattered which.*

She stood in the snow at the edge of Dominic's dooryard for a long time, letting the wind carry what it carried. Screaming, very faint, or perhaps only wind through the pass—it was impossible to say at this distance, and that impossibility was its own particular cruelty. Horses. The creak of many wagons. And beneath all of it, so faint that a lesser nose would have missed it entirely, the smell she dreaded most in all the world: cold iron and consecrated oil. Inquisitors.

Behind her, the door opened. She didn't turn. She heard the shuffle of bare feet, the sharp intake of breath, and then Isandro pressed himself against her flank, small and warm, staring east at the glow with his enormous dark eyes.

"Hey." Dominic's voice, rough with sleep and already sharpening into fear. "Hey, what—"

He saw it too. Serra heard him stop breathing for a moment, the way humans did when the world rearranged itself without asking permission.

"That's Thornmere," he said quietly. "That's *Thornmere.*"

*Yes,* Serra thought. *And we both know who did it, and we both know why, and neither of us is going to say it out loud yet because saying things out loud makes them true.*

Isandro made a sound. It was barely a sound—a thin, high note in the back of his throat, the first noise Serra had ever heard him make—and his small hand closed in her fur hard enough to hurt.

She leaned down and pressed her forehead against his temple, the way she had once done with pups, long ago, in another life she did not let herself visit often.

*Not tonight,* she told him, knowing he couldn't hear her, or hoping he couldn't. *Tonight you are just a boy watching a fire far away. Let it stay far away.*

It did not stay far away.

* * *

By morning the refugees began to arrive.

They came up the valley road in ones and twos and then in a ragged procession—a carter with a burned hand leading a horse that had singed whiskers; an old woman carrying nothing but a cat in a basket, the cat glaring at the world with the affronted dignity of its entire species; three children walking in a row holding hands, soot-streaked, silent, their eyes doing the thousand-yard stare that Serra had seen on soldiers and beasts alike after the worst days.

Dominic met them at his gate with bread and blankets and the particular helpless industry of a good man confronted with a bad thing too large for his hands. Serra watched him from the fence line, ears pricked, cataloguing.

And cataloguing something else besides. Because threaded through the refugees' chatter—the names of the dead, the recitation of what was lost, the granary, the mill, poor Aldous Weaver who'd gone back for his loom—ran a darker thread, repeated over and over like a burr caught in wool:

*Inquisitors. They came asking about magic. They said someone had Seen traces. They burned the tanner's house first, and when he denied it they burned the rest.*

"They said," the carter told Dominic, his bandaged hand shaking as he accepted bread, "they said the traces led toward *this* valley. Toward Vell. They're coming here next, friend. Mark me. They'll be here within the week."

Serra saw it land on Dominic like a stone dropped into still water—the ripple going out and out, disturbing everything. He kept his face steady, because he was good at that, had been practicing steady his whole life. But his heart, which Serra could hear even without trying, stumbled like a man on stairs in the dark.

*They're coming here.*

* * *

That evening, with the refugees sheltered in the meetinghouse and Isandro asleep—truly asleep, finally, after a day of watching adults weep and pretending not to understand, which of course he understood better than any of them—Serra did the thing she had been circling for three days like a hawk deciding whether the field was worth the dive.

She spoke to Dominic.

Not in the half-voice, the impression-and-gesture way she had used since arriving, testing him, feeding him meaning in scraps. She sat before the hearth while he poked at a fire that needed no poking, waited until his eyes came to her, and let the words come out whole, in the voice that no human in the valley of Vell had ever heard.

"Dominic Marchetti. We need to talk."

To his credit, the man did not scream. He did drop the poker, which clanged against the hearthstone with a sound like a small bell announcing the end of the world, and he did sit down rather abruptly in the chair behind him, missing it by half a length and catching himself on the armrest with the grace of a startled deer.

"You," he said. His voice came out an octave higher than usual. "You *talk.*"

"I talk," Serra agreed. "I've been talking since before your grandfather's grandfather was whelped, which by your people's counting makes me either extremely old or extremely rude for not introducing myself sooner. My name is Serra. I am sorry for the deception. The deception was necessary, and also I was enjoying watching you narrate my thoughts incorrectly. You have a real gift for assuming I want mutton when I want rabbit."

Dominic stared at her. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves. In the loft above, Isandro slept on, breath slow and even.

"You've understood me," Dominic said slowly. "This whole time. Everything I've said to you."

"Every word. Including the embarrassing ones. Especially the embarrassing ones. The one about the widow Hale's jam, for instance—"

"We don't need to revisit the jam."

"Wise." She let a moment pass, let the fire crackle, let him begin—just begin—to settle. Then she took the warmth out of her own voice, because what came next deserved no warmth. "There is no gentle way to tell you what I have to tell you, so I will tell it plainly, the way wolves do. The village of Thornmere burned last night because men wearing white hoods found a trace of magic there. A trace. A child's charm, maybe, or an herb-wife's blessing, something so small it would not have lit a candle. They burned eleven homes and killed four people over a trace. And they believe the trail leads here."

"I know," Dominic whispered. "I heard—"

"You heard what the refugees said. You don't know what I know." She rose and paced to the window, looking out at the dark valley, at the mountains beyond it, at the invisible line in the passes where the world changed flavor. "So listen. The silence you've lived in your whole life—the deadness, the way magic slides off this valley like rain off oiled cloth—that isn't natural. It was made. It's called the ward of Vell, and it is the last knot of a covenant older than any kingdom on the map. Long ago, the mortal realm and the Spire beyond the mountains were bound together, mortal voice on one end, Spire's power on the other. The ward was its shadow, its anchor, the place where the covenant touched earth."

Dominic had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man standing on ice and hearing it begin to speak.

"The covenant had a keeper," Serra went on. "A woman named Maerlin. She lived in this valley, in a house that isn't there anymore, up past the old mill. She tended the knot. And nine years ago, she died."

"Nine years ago," Dominic repeated. Something moved behind his eyes—arithmetic, memory. "I remember... there was a woman. When I was young. The other children said she was mad. She talked to herself in the fields. Or—" he stopped.

"Or she talked to us," Serra said gently. "Yes. She was mad the way I am mad, the way you are mad, the way every soul who hears the world speak is mad according to people who have never listened. She was the keeper of Vell. And when she died, nobody replaced her, because there was nobody left to replace her, and the ward began—slowly, slowly—to fail. Like a roof with one missing shingle. For years it held. But knots unravel, Dominic. The silence is thinning. Magic is starting to leak back in around the edges. Small things. A beast here and there hearing a little more than it should. A gift that shouldn't work in a dead valley... working. Faintly. At the margins."

Dominic's hand had drifted, without his noticing, to his own throat.

"You," Serra said. "You are one of the leaks. The most remarkable leak I have ever encountered. A beast-speaker, alive and functioning inside a ward specifically built to smother such things. Do you understand how impossible you are? I crossed three hundred miles to reach this valley and I spent the first week convinced my senses had broken."

"Why?" His voice cracked on it. "Why would you cross three hundred miles?"

"Because of the boy."

The word landed between them and neither moved.

"His name is Isandro," Serra said. "Though that is the least true thing about him. He is not an orphan in the ordinary sense, Dominic. He was *made*. Grown in the last of the Spire's magic, shaped to be a living key—a vessel of the covenant itself, able to renew it or unmake it. When Maerlin died, the wardens at the Spire knew the covenant would fail without a keeper, and they knew Kaelen Voss's inquisitors would hunt any keeper they found. So they did the only clever thing left. They sent the key to the one place in all the world where magic could not follow. Where the hunters' own arts would go blind. They sent him here. To the dead valley. To the last place the covenant's enemies would never think to look, because looking requires magic, and here magic dies."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a fox screamed its thin winter scream, and Serra automatically translated it—*cold, hungry, territory*—and set it aside.

"They sent him to me," Dominic said at last. It wasn't a question. He was looking at the loft now, at the floorboards above which a mute child slept, a child who drew spirals in a language older than kings, a child who had arrived on his doorstep in a snowstorm with one impossible syllable in his mouth.

"A dying warden carried him through the passes," Serra said. "I was sent ahead, to scout, to guard. I failed at the second part; the warden died at the treeline and the boy walked the last mile alone, which should tell you something about what he is. I have watched him for nine days. I have watched him look at you." Her throat tightened, which surprised her; she had thought that capacity worn away centuries ago. "He is afraid of what he was made to be. And for the first time since he was made, he is not afraid of where he is. That is you, Dominic. That is what you've given him without knowing anything at all."

Dominic put his face in his hands. For a long moment the only sound was the fire and the wind and, faint and far to the east, a smell none of them could escape—the ghost of Thornmere riding the wind.

"So the ward is failing," he said finally, through his fingers. "Because she died. Because nobody—because I didn't—" He stopped. Shook his head. "I didn't even know there was anything to keep."

"No more than a man knows he should be keeping the sky up," Serra said. "You weren't failing, you were ignorant, and ignorance is at least forgivable. But here is the arithmetic, plain and ugly. The ward is thinning. The inquisitors burned a village over a *trace*, and their Seers will feel this valley's edges fraying the way you'd feel a draft under a door. Within a week, perhaps two, they come to Vell. And when they come, they will not find a trace. They will find a leaking ward, a beast-speaker, and the living key to the covenant sleeping in your loft. Everything Kaelen Voss fears most in this world, gathered in one farmhouse, waiting politely for him."

Dominic looked up. His eyes were wet, but his jaw had set in a way Serra recognized from the wolves she had known best—the ones who, cornered, stopped being prey.

"What do I do?" he asked. It was, she thought, the bravest question a frightened man can ask. Not *why me*, not *how can this be*. Just: *what do I do.*

"You leave," Serra said. "You take the boy, and you cross the ward-line, and you go to the Spire, where the covenant can be renewed and the key can be protected by something stronger than a farmhouse door. It will be dangerous. It will be terrible. Voss's hounds will hunt you the whole way, and the world beyond this valley is..." She paused, searching for a description fair enough to be honest. "Loud. Very loud, and full of people who burn villages over traces."

"And if I stay?"

"Then you die," Serra said simply. "And worse, the boy dies, and worse than that, the covenant dies with him, and every wild thing in the world stays chained and muzzled and enslaved to men like Voss forever. I have seen what his handlers do to captured beasts, Dominic. I carry scars from it. If you stay, you are choosing that—for him, and for all of us."

Silence again. Dominic rose, walked to the window, and stood looking east, where the horizon still held a faint bruise of light where Thornmere had been. Serra watched the play of emotions across his back the way she'd read weather in cloud-shapes: grief, guilt, terror, and underneath them all, stubborn as a root under frost, love.

"He's just a boy," Dominic said quietly. "Whatever they made him for. He likes honey on his bread. He laughs at the goat."

"Yes," Serra said. "That is rather the point of everything."

Above them, a floorboard creaked.

Both of them went still. Serra's ears swiveled. Dominic turned toward the ladder, and there, halfway down it, in his nightshirt with his feet bare on the cold wood, stood Isandro.

How long he had been listening, there was no way to know. His face was pale and unreadable—but his eyes went from Dominic to Serra and back again, and then he did something that undid them both. He held up one small hand, and in the other he clutched the stub of charcoal he apparently kept beneath his pillow the way other children kept knives, and he pointed east, toward the burning ghost of Thornmere, and then he pointed at himself.

Then, slowly, deliberately, he pointed at the door.

*Take me away from here,* the gesture said. *Before they come. Before anyone else burns because of me.*

"Oh," Dominic breathed. "Oh, no. No, sweetheart, this isn't—"

Isandro shook his head, fierce, and jabbed the finger at the door again, and this time his chin trembled and his eyes filled, and Serra felt something in her chest crack along an old seam.

*He knows,* she thought, grief arriving like the ash on the wind, inevitable and smelling of smoke. *He has known since the day he arrived. He has been waiting for us to catch up.*

Dominic crossed the room and knelt and pulled the boy against his chest, and Isandro clung to him, charcoal and all, and over the top of the boy's head Dominic looked at Serra with an expression she would carry with her across every mile that followed.

"When do we leave?" he asked.

Serra looked out the window one last time—at the quiet valley, the sleeping fields, the thinning veil of silence that had kept them all safe and asleep for a generation. Somewhere beyond the mountains, the light of Thornmere's pyre was still fading. Somewhere closer, riders were already turning toward Vell.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Before dawn. Pack light, bring the boy, and pray to whatever gods listen in a dead valley that the weather holds."

Outside, as if in answer, the wind shifted—and brought with it, faint but unmistakable, the iron-and-oil scent of inquisitors on the road.

Not a week away, after all.

Perhaps not even a day.
