## 5. Leaving Vell

Dominic had always imagined that if he ever fled his home, he would do it dramatically—out a window, perhaps, in the dead of night, with hounds baying behind him and snow whipping at his heels. Instead he spent the morning deciding which kettle to bring.

"The blue one," he told Isandro, holding both up. "The copper one whistles. Whistling is a luxury for people whose lives are not currently in danger."

Isandro considered this with the grave solemnity he brought to everything, then pointed at the copper one anyway.

"Of course you would." Dominic set it back on the shelf, then picked it up again, then set it down with the firmness of a man closing a coffin lid. He was not going to weep over a kettle. He had already wept over a kettle that morning, briefly, while the boy wasn't looking.

The satchel sat open on the table like a small hungry mouth. Into it went things that could not be left: his father's carving knife, a coil of good rope, the wool socks Marta Hale had knitted him three winters ago and pretended she hadn't, half a wheel of hard cheese wrapped in cloth, flint and steel, a shirt that was only slightly mended, and the small tin box of dried herbs he kept for fevers—which, he suspected with a sinking feeling, were about to become very relevant to his life.

Everything else stayed. The bed he'd built at nineteen and slept badly in for twenty years. His mother's loom, silent since her hands had stopped working it. The row of cups on the shelf, chipped in patterns he knew like handwriting. A lifetime, it turned out, weighed almost nothing when you tried to carry it. That was the cruelest part. You spent forty years filling a house, and when the moment came, all of it fit into one satchel with room left over for cheese.

Serra watched from the doorway, her grey flank blocking most of the greyer light. She had been watching him pack for an hour with the patience of someone who had crossed mountains and did not understand kettles.

"You're judging me," Dominic said.

*I am waiting,* Serra said. Her voice in his head was still new enough to make his spine prickle, like hearing his own thoughts answer back in someone else's accent. *Though if I judged, it would be about the second knife. You own two knives. One of them is decorative.*

"It's not decorative, it's for—" He paused. "All right. It's decorative. My father bought it off a peddler who swore it had killed a bandit lord, and I've never once believed it."

*Bring it,* Serra said. *Stories are heavier than steel but they keep better.*

Isandro sat by the fire with the slate Dominic had given him, drawing. The boy drew the way other children breathed—constantly, without thinking, lines flowing out of him in that old angular script that made Dominic's skin hum just to look at. Today's drawing was of the cottage itself: the crooked chimney, the door, and beside the door a wolf larger than the door, rendered with more affection than accuracy.

"We're coming back," Dominic said, and heard how it sounded even as he said it. "I mean—we're going somewhere safe first. And then. Eventually."

Isandro looked up. He didn't write anything. He just looked at Dominic with those dark, unchildlike eyes that somehow still managed to be entirely a child's eyes, and then he went back to his drawing and added smoke curling from the chimney. A promise, maybe. Or a memory of what home was supposed to look like.

Dominic turned away and packed the decorative knife.

* * *

The lie was the worst part. Not because lying came hard to Dominic—it didn't; he'd been lying by omission his entire life, smiling through market days with a whole second self folded up inside him—but because the people of Vell deserved better than what he gave them, and because some of them, he suspected, saw straight through it anyway.

He went to Bram Toller first, because Bram was nearest and because Bram owed him for two autumns of mending fence, and debts made lies easier to swallow.

"Taking the lad to kin," Dominic said, standing in Bram's yard with the satchel on his shoulder and Isandro's cold hand tucked in his. "Over the mountains. There's people on his mother's side. Distant, mind, but decent folk, apparently."

Bram looked at the satchel. Then at Isandro. Then at the treeline, where a large grey wolf stood in plain view, which no wolf in living memory had done within a mile of a human settlement.

"Distant kin who keep wolves," Bram said.

"She followed us from the high pass. Injured thing. Seems to have taken a liking to the boy." The words came out smooth and stupid and Bram nodded slowly, the way men nod when they've decided not to argue with a story they don't believe.

"Well," Bram said. "Mind the passes. And Dominic—" He scratched under his hat, a man wrestling visibly with several sentences and losing to all of them. "If it's trouble, you know. The kind with uniforms in it. My barn's got a loft nobody checks."

Something in Dominic's chest cracked cleanly, like river ice in spring.

"There's no trouble," he said, and put out his hand, and Bram shook it, and both of them let the lie stand between them like a fence post they'd agreed to build around.

Marta Hale was harder. Marta had known his mother. Marta had brought bread the day Isandro arrived, unasked, and said nothing about why a mute child might appear at a lonely man's door in the dead of winter, which in Vell was its own kind of eloquence.

"Kin," Marta repeated, pressing a parcel of food into his hands before he'd finished the sentence. "Mountains. Mm." She looked at Isandro, and Isandro, who feared nothing except loud noises and being touched without warning, reached up and patted her arm twice—the gesture he used for *thank you*, learned from watching Dominic do it. Marta's face did something complicated. "You take care of that boy, Dominic Marchetti."

"I will."

"And yourself." She lowered her voice to almost nothing. "There were riders at the mill yesterday. Asking after strangers. I told them I hadn't seen a soul but my own reflection, and God forgive me if my reflection starts asking questions."

Dominic's mouth went dry. "Riders. Grey cloaks?"

"Grey everything. Faces like unpaid bills." She shooed them off down the lane. "Go on, then. Go on. Don't stand there letting the whole valley memorize your face."

They walked. The village fell away behind them lane by lane, and Dominic catalogued it the way you catalogue a dream you know you won't keep: the smithy's smoke, the crooked well, Old Fen's dog raising its head as they passed and—this was new, this was terrifying and wonderful—Old Fen's dog saying, in a voice like gravel poured slow, *safe road, speaker,* and putting its nose briefly against Isandro's knee before returning to its porch.

The animals of Vell had always felt him faintly, the way you feel warmth from a banked fire. Now, with Serra walking openly at their side, they seemed to know something was ending or beginning, and they lined the edges of his path like a silent honor guard: the miller's horses, the geese—who said nothing, geese never say anything worth hearing, they simply screamed in their wordless way, though even that sounded today less like fury and more like farewell—a fox at the wood's edge, two ravens overhead who called down, *east, east, east,* until Serra snapped at them to be useful elsewhere.

*They like you,* Isandro wrote on his slate, holding it up without breaking stride.

"They're being dramatic," Dominic said. His voice caught in the middle of the word. He cleared his throat. "Everyone's being dramatic today. It's very trying."

* * *

The ward-line ran along the ridge above the north pasture, though no one in Vell had marked it, because no one in Vell knew it was there. You could see it only if you were wrong in exactly the way Dominic was wrong: a faint shimmer, like heat-haze in midwinter, like the air remembering something. All his life he had walked beneath it on his way to the high meadows and felt it pass through him like water through a net—taking something small away each time, a whisper here, a flicker there, leaving behind just enough of his gift to keep him company and just little enough to keep him safe.

Beyond the line, the world waited. Beyond the line, every gift he had spent forty years folding smaller would unfold all at once.

He stopped ten feet short of it and found he couldn't move.

It wasn't fear of what waited outside—though there was that too, riders and armies and a war with his name pinned to it now like a moth to a board. It was simpler than that. It was the terror of a man who has lived quietly in a dim room for so long that the prospect of daylight feels like violence. He knew what was on the other side of that shimmer. He had never once, in forty years, felt the full weight of his own gift. What if it drowned him? What if the man who walked through that line wasn't quite Dominic anymore?

A small hand tugged his sleeve.

Isandro held up the slate. On it, beneath a drawing of a door opening onto light, was written: *I'll hold your hand.*

And that—that undid him more thoroughly than any army could have. Dominic laughed, a wet, broken-edged laugh, and crouched, and pulled the boy into a hug that Isandro tolerated for a full three seconds, which by his standards was a state visit.

"All right," Dominic said. "All right. Together, then."

*Serra goes first,* Serra said. *Someone sensible should see what's on the other side.* She stepped through the shimmer and vanished—not vanished, merely crossed, but to Dominic's straining eyes it looked like the world swallowed her—and then her voice came back, strange and doubled, arriving both through his ears and through the new-old channel in his mind, louder than he had ever heard it, rich and layered and threaded with harmonics that made his teeth ache:

*Come and hear.*

He took Isandro's hand. The boy's fingers were ice and iron. They stepped forward together, and the ward-line broke over them like a wave.

Dominic had prepared himself for noise. He had not prepared himself for this.

Magic didn't flood back into him. It flooded back *through* him, and everything it touched woke up. The mountain grass spoke first—millions of green voices murmuring of frost and thirst, a sound like rain heard from underground. Then the stones, slow and deep, humming their patient stone-thoughts of pressure and time. The wind carried actual messages instead of mere weather: news from three valleys over, the scent-signature of deer, and underneath it, far off, a wrongness—a smell of ash and iron that made every hair on his body stand up. A hawk screamed overhead and the scream *meant* something, meant *hare below, mine, mine*. The trees groaned in a language older than any of it. Somewhere in his chest, a door he had nailed shut at age six blew open, and everything he had ever suppressed came pouring through the gap at once, and Dominic Marchetti dropped to his knees in the snow and laughed and sobbed in the same breath because the world was *alive*, it had always been alive, and he had spent his whole existence deaf in a cathedral.

*Speaker,* said the grass. *Speaker,* said the stones. *Speaker,* sang the wind, and a hundred small lives turned toward him across the mountainside like flowers finding the sun, and it was glorious, and it was unbearable, and it was the single worst strategic position he had ever occupied, kneeling in open ground radiating welcome like a lighthouse.

"Serra—" he gasped. "How do you *stand* it? How do you bear hearing everything?"

She stood over him, and for the first time since he'd known her, her voice was gentle. *You don't bear it all at once. You learn which voices matter. It took me fifty winters.* A pause. *You will learn faster. You're loud.*

Isandro knelt beside him in the snow, slate forgotten, watching Dominic's face with an expression of pure wonder—as if seeing, for the first time, the true shape of the man who had taken him in. Then the boy reached out and pressed two fingers lightly against Dominic's temple, and the roaring world softened, settled, arranged itself into something like music rather than avalanche.

*Better?* the boy mouthed.

"How—" Dominic wiped his face with his sleeve. "How did you do that?"

Isandro shrugged, the universal gesture of children everywhere: *magic, obviously.*

Dominic got up. His legs shook. The world sang. He shouldered the satchel with the decorative knife in it, took Isandro's hand again, and looked east along the valley of the high pass, where the mountains marched toward the Spire like a congregation facing an altar.

Then the singing stopped.

Not faded—stopped, all at once, the way a room goes quiet when a stranger walks in. Every voice on the mountainside dropped to a whisper, and the whisper was one word, passing downhill from peak to peak faster than any wind could carry it:

*Riders.*

Serra's hackles rose. Far below, where the road bent around the frozen lake, four figures in grey moved at a trot, and they were not, Dominic understood with terrible clarity, riding toward anywhere.

They were riding toward the place where the ward-line ended—and something at the ward-line's edge had just begun, very softly, to glow.

*Run,* said the whole mountain at once, in the voice of everything alive upon it, and they ran.
