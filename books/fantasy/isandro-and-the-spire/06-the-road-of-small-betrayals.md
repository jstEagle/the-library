## 6. The Road of Small Betrayals

*The boy remembers in pieces.*

---

A tower. Always the tower first.

It stands in the dark behind his eyes the way a tooth stands in a gum—aching when pressed, quiet otherwise. White stone that was never white, not really, but the color of held breath. Windows like closed eyelids. And at the top, a room where a woman hummed songs with no words, or words so old they had worn smooth as river stones.

He does not remember her face. He remembers her *hands*. Long fingers, ink-stained at the second knuckle. She wrote on everything—on paper, on the floor, once, memorably, on his own arm, and the letters had crawled under his skin like warm ants and made him laugh until she laughed too.

*You are a letter yourself,* she told him. *Did you know? The longest letter ever written. And someday someone will read you aloud.*

He did not know what she meant. He still does not. But sometimes, walking behind the man called Dominic, he presses his hand flat against his own chest and waits for the reading to begin.

Nothing begins. Only his heart, going on stubbornly, like a small animal scratching at a door.

---

The road is long. That is one thing about roads: everyone says it and no one means how long.

Isandro rides wrapped in a blanket that smells of woodsmoke and sheep, because the man said the wind would bite and the wind has kept its promise all morning. Serra lopes ahead, gray ghost between the trees, turning back every hundred paces to check on them. When she looks at him he feels *seen*, the way he imagines a candle feels seen by fire.

The man—Dominic—talks. To him, mostly, though the boy cannot answer, and this should make the talking pointless, but somehow it doesn't. It makes it safe.

"—so the trick to porridge," Dominic is saying, stirring the pot with a stick he stole from a squirrel's construction project and does not feel guilty about, "is salt. Everyone forgets salt. My mother said a cook who forgets salt is a liar serving sweet lies, which I thought was very dramatic until I tried unsalted porridge and agreed completely."

He hands over a bowl. Steam rises. Isandro eats, watching the man over the rim, and thinks: *he talks to fill the silence, but not because the silence frightens him. Because he thinks it might frighten me.*

It is a small kindness disguised as chatter. Isandro collects these now. A pebble placed in his boot so his cold foot doesn't notice itself. An extra strip of dried apple left casually at the edge of his share. The way Dominic angles his body uphill of him on windy stretches without appearing to decide to.

Small betrayals of his own gruffness. Isandro knows what they are. He has been studied before, by people far less kind, and he knows the difference between being watched and being *kept*.

---

But sleep brings the other memories, and those are not kind.

*Fire.*

Not near him—never allowed near him, that was the whole point of him—but through a window, far below, across a frozen field. A village burning in the night, and the flames moving strangely, deliberately, like dancers who had rehearsed. Magic-fire. Even from the tower he could feel it *humming* against the glass, hungry and pleased with itself.

And the woman's hands pressing him down beneath the window sill, ink-stained fingers trembling for the first time ever.

*Don't look. Whatever you do—*

He looked. Of course he looked. Children look. That is most of what children are for.

And the fire looked back.

He remembers that: the sensation of a flame turning its attention to the tower window, finding him there, and pausing. Considering. Deciding—not yet. Not this one. This one is spoken for.

He woke then, on the road, with snow in his lashes and Serra's muzzle resting heavy on his knee, her amber eye level with his, and he understood she had been standing guard over his nightmare the whole time.

*I'm here,* she said, in the way wolves say things, which is not words exactly but something older and more honest. *I'm here, small key. Sleep again. Nothing reads you while I watch.*

He almost believed her. Almost.

---

They walk through a forest that remembers magic even if it cannot practice it anymore.

That is the strangest thing about the valley and its edges—the ward that hangs over Vell like a lid on a pot. Everything there is *muffled*. Trees grow but dreamily, as if asked politely rather than commanded. Rivers run without ambition. In Vell, Isandro had felt like a shout swallowed by wool.

Out here, past the ward-line, the world has teeth again, and claws, and opinions.

The trees lean toward him as he passes. Not visibly—he checks—but he can *feel* it, a slow green curiosity, hundreds of years of patience bending slightly toward the small strange boy who walks with a wolf and a soft-spoken man.

*What are you?* the trees ask, in their creaking way.

And Isandro, mute since the tower, silent since the fire, answers the only way he can: he reaches out and touches bark, and sends them what he has. Cold. Snow. Porridge with salt in it. The man's laugh, low and surprised at itself.

The trees find this acceptable. Trees are easy to please. They have waited so long for anything at all that porridge counts as poetry.

Dominic watches him touch the tree and says nothing, but his eyes go soft and worried in equal measure, and later Isandro catches him muttering to himself: "Talking to trees now. Wonderful. Next he'll be negotiating with rivers." A pause. "Actually, I'd pay to see that."

---

Here is the thing Isandro cannot say, has never been able to say, the thing that sits in him like a swallowed stone:

He knows what he is *for*.

Not fully. Not in words. But in the body's way of knowing, the way a hammer knows nails, the way a key knows it is shaped like an absence somewhere. He was *made*. Grown, maybe, or written—the woman's hands writing letters up his arm suggests written—and he was made to fit into something enormous, and the fitting will change him, and possibly unmake him, and everyone who smiles at him on this road may be smiling at a tool without knowing it.

Even Dominic. Especially Dominic, maybe, because Dominic is good, and good men carry their duties gently right up until the moment they use them.

Isandro fears this. He fears it more than the hounds, more than the fire, more than the tall men in gray who once measured his skull with silk cords and murmured numbers.

But here is the other thing, the smaller and stranger thing:

When Dominic sleeps, Isandro watches him breathe and thinks, *if I am a door, at least this man knocks first.*

No one else ever knocked.

---

*Fragment:* a staircase, spiraling down, and his own small feet slapping cold stone. Someone behind him saying *faster, little lord, faster* — but not unkindly, the way you urge a lamb. And below, at the bottom of the stairs, a door banded in iron with a lock shaped like an open mouth—

He wakes with snow on his face again and decides some fragments are better left unfollowed.

---

On the third day they cross a river on stones, and the river talks to him.

Rivers are worse than trees. Rivers have *no manners whatsoever*.

*COLD COLD COLD COLD,* it announces, delighted. *COLD BOY ON MY STONES. COLD WOLF. COLD MAN WITH THE FUNNY HAT. EVERYTHING IS SO COLD AND I AM THE COLDEST AND ALSO I AM GOING TO BE THE SEA SOMEDAY.*

*Someday?* Isandro asks, balancing, gripping Dominic's offered hand.

*EVERYTHING GOES TO THE SEA EVENTUALLY,* the river says cheerfully. *THE RAIN. THE TEARS. THE LITTLE FISH. EVEN YOU, PROBABLY. NO OFFENSE.*

*Some taken,* Isandro tells it, and the river laughs in spray and foam, and for a moment—for one whole moment—he has spoken, truly spoken, voice or no voice, and the loneliness cracks open just enough to let a little light in.

Dominic glances down at him. "What's funny?"

Isandro points at the river, then mimes waves, then shivers theatrically.

"The river's cold?" Dominic guesses.

Isandro shakes his head, mimes bigger waves, points at the sea-shape on the horizon side.

"The river wants to be the sea."

Isandro nods solemnly.

"Huh." Dominic considers this as seriously as he considers everything, which is one of the boy's favorite facts about him. "Good for it. Ambition's rare in water." Then, quieter, almost to himself: "Rare everywhere, really. Most things just want to be left alone."

Isandro looks up at him—at the lines around his eyes, at the way his gaze keeps drifting south, back toward Vell, toward the cottage, toward the life folded up small in one satchel—and recognizes the shape of the sentence even if he doesn't know all its words yet.

This man wanted to be left alone. And here he is, carrying a mute boy and following a wolf toward mountains full of war, because leaving alone was the one thing the world wouldn't let him do.

Isandro takes his hand again. Not for balance this time.

Dominic pretends not to notice. His grip tightens anyway. Another small betrayal.

---

*Night fragment, the worst one:*

The woman's hands, cupping his face. Not writing this time. Just holding, the way you hold something already breaking.

*Listen to me,* she says, and her voice is the hum from the tower, the wordless song, except now it has words and he wishes it didn't. *If they come—if the gray men come—you must forget how to speak. All of it. Every tongue. Even the ones inside you. Can you do that?*

He shakes his head. He doesn't want to. Words are the only toys he has.

*Can you do it?* Her thumbs press his cheeks, gentle and terrible. *For me. For the long sleep. Forget, and keep forgetting, until someone comes who is worth remembering for.*

And little Isandro, who trusts her absolutely, who would have walked into the fire for her, nods.

And she weeps. He remembers that most of all—that she wept while doing it, that her tears fell hot on his face as the forgetting came down like snowfall, soft and total, burying the tower, the stairs, the mouth-shaped lock, her name, her name, her *name*—

He wakes gasping.

Snow. Blanket. Firelight. Serra's warmth along his back like a drawn bowstring. And twenty feet away, sitting up against a log, Dominic—awake, watching him, holding out a strip of dried apple without a single question in his face.

Just the apple. Just the waiting. Just the man who knocks.

Isandro crawls over and sits against his side, and Dominic arranges the blanket around them both and resumes talking into the dark, softly, nonsense, the porridge-salt philosophy, a story about a goat named Petunia who committed crimes, and the words wash over the boy like warm water, and somewhere in the middle of the goat saga Isandro falls asleep again, and this time there is no tower.

There is a cottage instead. Small. Smoke from the chimney. Two bowls on the table, both salted properly. A wolf asleep by the hearth.

A home that hasn't happened yet, dreamed by a boy who isn't sure he'll live to see it, and loves anyway.

---

On the fifth day the trees thin, and the land opens, and far off across the plain they see it: walls, rooftops, a hundred chimneys smoking, and above it all a great aqueduct marching toward the city on stone legs.

Harrowgate, Serra says. Free city. Full of people, which means full of trouble, which means stay close and speak to no one and let the man do the talking.

Dominic settles the satchel, straightens Isandro's blanket-cap, and studies the distant walls with an expression Isandro has learned to read: hope and dread sharing one face, each convinced the other is lying.

"City means scholars," Dominic murmurs. "Someone who can read your drawings, lad. Someone who can tell us what you—" He stops. Swallows the end of the sentence like a bone.

Isandro finishes it silently, because he has always known: *what I'm for.*

He takes Dominic's hand. Together they walk down toward Harrowgate, toward the noise and the smoke and whatever waits inside the walls—and neither of them sees, nailed to the mile-marker at the crossroads behind them, the fresh bill flapping in the wind, ink still wet, describing a man, a wolf, and a child.
