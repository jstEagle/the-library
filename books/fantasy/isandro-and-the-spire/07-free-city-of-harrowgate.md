## 7. Free City of Harrowgate

The road to Harrowgate taught Dominic one thing above all others: he had been raised in a valley the size of a soup pot.

For twenty-nine years Vell had been his whole world—two hundred souls, a mill, a chapel with a crooked bell, and woods that went quiet in winter. He had thought himself reasonably well-traveled because he had once walked as far as the stone bridge at Marrow Fen. Then the ward-line had crossed behind him like a door closing, magic had poured back into his blood, and every mile since had been a lesson in how much world there was.

Harrowgate was the lesson's final exam.

They crested the last hill at dusk, and Isandro made a sound—a small, punched-out *hah*—the first noise he'd made all day that wasn't a word drawn in frost or dirt. Dominic understood completely. The city sat in the crook of two rivers like a coin dropped into a beggar's palm: walls the color of old honey, ten thousand rooftops stacked and jumbled, lantern-light already kindling street by street until the whole bowl of it glowed against the darkening fields. Bells rang somewhere inside it, three different towers arguing over the hour.

"Big," Serra said beside him. She wore her wolf shape openly now; outside the ward there was no hiding what she was, and after the pass she'd decided pretending otherwise was more trouble than it saved. Travelers on the road gave her a wide berth, which, she noted with satisfaction, also kept pickpockets honest. "Bigger than anything I remember from before the Spire. And I remember things older than your grandmother's grandmother."

"Helpful," Dominic said. "Any memory specifically about not getting arrested?"

"Walk confidently. Smell unbothered."

"I'll do my best."

Isandro took Dominic's hand without being asked. That was new—the boy had spent most of the journey orbiting him like a shy moon, close but never touching—and Dominic's chest did something complicated about it that he chose not to examine while walking downhill.

The gate guards barely glanced at them. Harrowgate, they'd learn, looked at almost nothing twice; the city was too busy, too full, too determined to mind its own considerable business. Inside the walls the noise hit like weather—hawkers crying the price of eels, a man selling what he swore were genuine dragon-scale umbrellas (Serra sniffed one and pronounced it "cured pig"), cart wheels grinding, children chasing each other through the legs of a crowd that flowed around them like water around stones.

And everywhere, everywhere, refugees.

Dominic noticed them before he could name what he was noticing: families camped under awnings with everything they owned in blankets; a woman mending the same shirt over and over for want of anything else to do; an old man sitting very straight on a curb, holding a carved wooden bird, looking at nothing. They had a particular stillness, these people. A patience worn down to the wood. When a herald climbed the fountain steps near the Lambgate and began reading proclamations, half the square drifted toward him the way cold air drifts toward warmth.

"—by order of the Council of Nine, grain levies are suspended for displaced persons bearing borderland marks—" the herald read, bored, beautiful. "—the Council further reminds citizens that rumors of sorcery in the eastern passes remain unsubstantiated and that panic serves the usurper better than any army—"

A laugh went up from somewhere in the crowd, bitter and short.

"Unsubstantiated," muttered a broad-shouldered man next to Dominic, who had a farmer's forearms and a burn scar crawling up his neck like ivy. He caught Dominic looking and didn't apologize. "Tell that to Thornmere. Tell it to Ashvale." He said the second name quietly, and Dominic's stomach dropped, because he knew that name. Everyone from the valley knew Ashvale. It was the village on the far side of the western hills, close enough that its smoke would have been visible from the high pasture on a clear day—if anyone had been standing there to see it.

"What happened to Ashvale?" Dominic asked, though some part of him had known since Serra told him, since the smell of smoke had ridden the wind for two days before they left.

The man looked at him properly then—at his valley accent, at the mute child gripping his hand, at the enormous scarred wolf who was watching the crowd with yellow eyes—and something in his face closed like a shutter. "You're from east of the hills," he said. Not a question. "Then you'll find out soon enough." He shouldered his bundle and walked away, and did not look back.

* * *

They found lodging above a chandler's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, which Serra selected by the scientific method of declaring it acceptable and lying down in the doorway until the landlady agreed. The room was small and smelled of tallow, and the bed cost more per night than a week of Dominic's old life, but it had a door that locked and a window facing an alley rather than the street, and after the road it felt like a palace.

That first night, Isandro drew for him.

The boy sat cross-legged on the floorboards with a stub of charcoal—he'd charred a stick himself, with great ceremony, when Dominic couldn't afford proper drawing tools—and worked by candlelight, tongue between his teeth. What emerged was Harrowgate as Isandro saw it: rooftops rendered as overlapping scales, bells drawn with little motion-lines so you could almost hear them, and crowds of people drawn as simple upright strokes, hundreds of them, filling the streets like grass.

At the bottom corner of the page, apart from the rest, he drew three figures. A tall stroke with a satchel. A smaller stroke holding its hand. A four-legged shape with a ragged tail. Then, around the three of them, he drew a circle, and pressed his finger to it, and looked up at Dominic with an expression that needed no translation.

*Us,* the circle said. *Only us.*

"Yes," Dominic said softly. "All right. Us."

He slept badly anyway. In the morning the charcoal drawing was folded into his satchel, where it lived thereafter among the bread and the flint, and if he took it out sometimes when he thought no one was watching—that was nobody's business but his own.

* * *

Finding a scholar of old tongues in Harrowgate proved harder than finding lodging, food, or, according to a cheerful fishwife, a husband, an offer Dominic declined with what he hoped was diplomatic grace.

The problem was that everyone knew someone. The cobbler's cousin studied stars. The chandler's brother-in-law copied legal documents and owned *a book, a real one*. The archivist at the Hall of Records would see petitioners on alternate Thursdays. Alternate Thursdays. Dominic stood in the records hall queue on a Wednesday and nearly wept.

It was Serra, inevitably, who solved it—not through scholarship but through gossip, which she gathered the way other creatures gathered burrs, simply by existing among people long enough. Wolves, she explained, were ignored exactly to the degree that servants and stable-boys were ignored, and servants and stable-boys knew everything.

"There's a woman," she reported on their third evening, dropping onto the floor of the rented room with the boneless exhaustion of a creature who had spent the day being patted by strangers. "Yeva Corvane. She used to teach at the University until the University decided her subject didn't exist. Old tongues. Older scripts. She keeps rooms under a dyer's shop in the Weft district. The dyer's apprentices say she pays for candles and silence, and that once a year a man comes with books wrapped in oilcloth and leaves with nothing."

"Sounds promising and slightly illegal."

"The best people usually are both."

But it was the fourth evening that changed everything, and it happened in a market square, of all places, between a fruit stall and a puppet show.

Dominic had sent Serra ahead to scout the Weft district—wolves drew less attention than a tall man asking pointed questions—and was buying apples with Isandro perched on his hip, an arrangement the boy had initiated and Dominic had surrendered to within seconds, because there were some battles a man was always going to lose. Isandro was reaching for a pear. The fruit-seller was telling a long story about her sister's wedding. The puppet show was doing something violent to a dragon.

And then Dominic saw the notice board.

It stood at the square's edge, a slab of grey wood layered with papers the way old trees grow rings—proclamations, lost dogs, rewards for runaway apprentices, a scurrilous cartoon of the Council of Nine that someone had tastefully added a mustache to. And nailed across the center, fresh paper, crisp ink, posted high where it couldn't be torn down easily:

*BY ORDER OF THE HAND OF KAEL VOSS, LORD PROTECTOR OF THE REALM*

*A reward of five hundred crowns of gold for information leading to the capture of the following persons:*

The first description was of a child. Male, six or seven years of age, mute, of indeterminate origin. *Distinguishing marks: none required.* Five hundred crowns. For a child. Dominic's hand tightened on Isandro's hip before he could stop it, and the boy, sensing the shift in his breathing, went very still.

The second description was of a wolf. Grey, heavily scarred across the left flank. *Do not approach alone.*

The third—

The third was him.

*Also sought: a man of the eastern valleys, tall, brown-haired, aged approximately thirty winters. This man is believed to be traveling with the child and is considered extremely dangerous. He is known to converse with beasts, and may use this art to turn animals against honest men. Approach with armed company only.*

Five hundred crowns. It was, Dominic thought distantly, more money than his father had earned in fifteen years of farming. Enough to buy a farm. Enough to buy *several* farms. Enough that every hungry soul between here and the mountains had reason to look twice at a tall man with a mute child and think about winter coming on.

"They got my age wrong," he heard himself say. "I'm twenty-nine."

The fruit-seller followed his gaze, read the notice, and looked at him. Looked at Isandro. Looked at the notice again. Her face went through a small, complete war—Dominic watched it happen, watched the arithmetic of five hundred crowns fight whatever else was in there—and then, deliberately, theatrically, she turned her back to the board and began loudly describing her sister's wedding to a customer who hadn't asked.

"Terrible business," she said, at volume. "Weddings. My sister married a man with *no trade whatsoever*. You couldn't pay me five hundred crowns to relive it." She bagged the pears without being asked and shoved them into Isandro's arms. "On the house. Go on, then."

Dominic paid her anyway, pressing coins into her hand fast, and walked—did not run, *walked*, though every nerve screamed run—out of the square.

* * *

He told Serra that night in the low voice they used for important things, the voice below the hearing of neighbors. She listened with her ears flat, and when he finished she was silent for a long moment.

"Ashvale," she said finally. "Thornmere. The burned villages. He isn't hunting you specifically, Dominic. You're a loose thread in a coat he's trying to burn. But threads get pulled." She paced the small room, tail low. "Five hundred crowns is clever. Cruel-clever. He doesn't need to catch you himself. He just needs every town between here and the Spire to be full of eyes."

"Then we move at night. We go around the towns. There are shepherds' tracks through the hills—"

"And snow in the passes by month's end, and a boy who needs feeding, and a bounty that follows you like your own shadow." Serra stopped pacing. "No. You came to this city to find a scholar. So we find the scholar, tomorrow, at dawn, before the city finishes waking up. Yeva Corvane. Under the dyer's shop. And then we decide—with her, with someone who actually knows what Isandro is—what we do next. Because right now we're running blind, and blind things get caught."

Isandro, who had been listening the way he always listened—absolutely still, eyes moving from speaker to speaker—tugged Dominic's sleeve. He held out his hands, palms together, then opened them slowly like a book.

*A story?*

"You want a story?" Dominic managed a smile that cost him more than the pears had. "All right. Once there was a man who talked to animals, and everyone said he was dangerous, and do you know what? They were right. He was terribly dangerous. He could make a mule carry firewood without complaining."

Isandro's mouth twitched. He tapped his own chest, then pointed at Dominic, then made a solemn face and folded his arms exactly the way Serra did when she was judging someone.

"Yes, worse than Serra. Much worse. Now lie down before the dangerous beast-speaker makes you sleep."

Later, with the candle out and Isandro's breath gone slow and even against his shoulder, Dominic lay staring at the ceiling beams and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding all day.

There had been perhaps two hundred people in the square. Maybe forty had read the notice. If one in forty of those decided five hundred crowns was worth the risk of mentioning a tall stranger to the wrong person—and the wrong people were clearly in every town now, patient as traps—then Harrowgate had maybe one safe night left in it. Maybe two. The war Kaelen Voss was waging wasn't only fought with armies; it was fought with notices and hunger and ordinary people doing arithmetic in market squares. And it was swallowing the map one borderland at a time, and the valley he'd come from was just another thread waiting for the flame.

He had never asked to be part of any of it. He had wanted a quiet life, a garden, the small warm company of creatures who never lied. Instead he was lying in a chandler's garret with a bounty on his head, a wolf for a bodyguard, and a mute child asleep against his ribs who was, apparently, the hinge on which the whole fate of two worlds swung.

*Tomorrow,* he thought. *Corvane. Answers. Then we'll see.*

He was almost asleep when Serra's head came up off the floor, ears forward, and a heartbeat later he heard it too: boots in the alley below. Not passing. Stopping.

Three pairs. Four. Someone whispered—an instruction, low and clipped—and then came the soft, unmistakable sound of a lantern being shaded.

Serra rose without a single creak of floorboard, hackles lifted, and put herself between the window and the sleeping boy.

"Dominic," she breathed. "Whatever happens—don't let him wake up scared."

The knock, when it came, was polite. That was somehow the worst part.
