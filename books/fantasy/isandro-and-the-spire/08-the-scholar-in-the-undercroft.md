## 8. The Scholar in the Undercroft

The scholar's address was written on a scrap of paper Dominic had been given by a woman selling roasted chestnuts, which was not how he had expected the pursuit of ancient knowledge to begin.

"Third door past the tannery," she'd said, pressing the paper into his hand along with the chestnuts he hadn't asked for. "Knock twice, then once more. If someone asks if you've come about the drains, say yes."

"The *drains*?"

"You'll understand when you get there." She'd smiled at Isandro, who was regarding her with the solemn gravity of a small judge, and pressed another chestnut into his hand too. "Poor lamb. He looks like he carries the world on those shoulders."

*You have no idea,* Dominic thought, and had thought it roughly forty times a day since Harrowgate.

Now they stood before the third door past the tannery, which was exactly as unprepossessing as advertised: a slab of grey wood set into a wall of grey stone, in a street that smelled, unsurprisingly, of tanneries. Serra had refused to come inside. "Your scholars keep cats," she had said, with the particular disgust she reserved for cats, whom she considered liars. "I will wait on the roof. Roofs are honest." And she had flowed up the side of the building like smoke poured backward, leaving Dominic to knock twice, then once more.

The door opened a hand's width. One eye appeared in the gap, sharp and brown and ringed with ink stains, as though its owner had recently rubbed her face after a long night of writing and forgotten to stop.

"Have you come about the drains?" the eye asked.

"Yes," said Dominic.

"You're lying," said the eye, "which is correct. Come in, mind the books, don't touch anything with a clasp, and for the love of all surviving knowledge, don't let the child near the shelf on the left. The left shelf bites."

Isandro looked up at Dominic with an expression that said, plainly, *I like her already.*

* * *

The undercroft turned out to be less a room than a civilization. Bookshelves rose from floor to vaulted ceiling in crooked ranks, some leaning against each other like tired old friends at a wedding. Candles burned in clusters on every surface, including — alarmingly — atop several stacks of parchment. A kettle hung over a brazier in one corner, and the whole space smelled of beeswax, dust, lamp oil, and underneath it all something older and stranger, like the air in a cave where something holy had once happened.

The scholar was a small woman, perhaps sixty, with iron-grey hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils and a bone stylus. She wore three layers of robes of decreasing cleanliness and moved through her kingdom of books with the surefootedness of a goat on a cliffside.

"Yeva Corvane," she said, not turning around. "You're the ones from Vell. The chestnut woman sent word ahead — she has a nephew who watches the gates, and I have a standing arrangement involving chestnuts and gossip. Sit. Not there. There."

Dominic sat. Isandro sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was how the boy did everything now — always within reach, always checking, a habit that broke Dominic's heart a little more each time he noticed it.

Yeva finally turned, wiping her inky hands on a rag that made them no cleaner, and looked at Isandro for a long moment. Her expression did something complicated — softened, sharpened, softened again — like a fire finding its draft.

"Well," she said quietly. "So it's true."

"You know what he is?" Dominic leaned forward. "The chestnut woman didn't say — she just gave me your name. But you know."

"I know a great many things, most of them useless, several of them dangerous, and a handful that have kept me alive in this city longer than wisdom should allow." She dragged a stool over and sat down across from them, knees nearly touching Isandro's. "But let's do this properly. Show me what he draws."

Isandro glanced at Dominic. It was their ritual now, this silent question: *is it safe?* Dominic nodded, and the boy dug into the satchel he never let leave his body and produced the leather folder of drawings — dozens of them now, pages soft at the edges from handling, arranged in an order only Isandro understood.

He laid them out on the cleared corner of Yeva's table, one by one, with the care of a priest laying out relics.

Dominic had studied these drawings for weeks. He knew them the way you know a song stuck in your head — intimately, helplessly, without comprehension. There was the tower, always the tower, drawn in long vertical strokes that somehow conveyed both height and grief. There were hands — a woman's hands, cupped, offering, sometimes empty, sometimes holding a light that Isandro rendered as a scribble so dense it seemed to hum off the page. There was fire, drawn in quick jagged lines. There was a chain of small linked circles running through nearly every image, connecting things that shouldn't connect: the tower to the valley, the valley to the sky, the sky to a small figure that appeared again and again at the center of everything, arms raised.

The small figure had Isandro's hair.

"He drew this one the first night," Dominic said, tapping the oldest page, its edges darkened with age. "Before I knew anything. Before the patrol came through asking about 'a vessel.' Before any of it."

Yeva bent over the pages. She did not touch them. Her lips moved as she read — and Dominic realized with a jolt that she wasn't reading pictures. She was reading *writing*. The looping lines he'd taken for decoration, the spirals and knots that framed every image, the little marks that trailed off the edges like breath — they weren't embellishment.

They were words.

"Oh," Yeva breathed. "Oh, you clever, terrible, brave child." She looked up, and her eyes were wet. "Do you know what language this is? No, of course you don't, why would you, nobody has read it in four hundred years. This is High Covenant script. Spire-tongue. The language they spoke when the world was still *wired together*, Master Marchetti. Every drawing is a sentence. Some of them are paragraphs." She laughed, a cracked, wondering sound. "This one here—" she hovered a finger above the first page, "—this says, roughly translated, *'I was made in the dark below the light, and I am sorry.'*"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above them, muffled through stone, Harrowgate went about its loud, indifferent business.

"Made," Dominic repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "What do you mean, *made*?"

Yeva took off her spectacles, polished them on her least filthy robe, put them back on, and folded her hands — the gestures of a woman buying herself time, or courage, or both.

"Sit properly," she said. "This will take a while, and you'll want to be sitting for it."

* * *

"Four hundred years ago," Yeva began, "the covenant between the mortal realm and the Spire was whole. Magic moved through the world like water through a living stream — wild, but channeled, held, *bound* by oaths spoken at the Spire's heart. The wardens kept the oaths. The kings kept the peace. And the beasts—" she glanced upward, toward the roof where a wolf waited, "—the beasts walked beside men as neighbors, not weapons. It was not a golden age. Ages never are. But it was a *balanced* one.

"Then came the Sundering. Nobody agrees on what caused it — a warden's pride, a king's greed, a bargain gone wrong; the histories contradict each other because the survivors lied. What we know is this: the covenant shattered. Magic stopped flowing and started *flooding*. Kingdoms drowned in it or burned in it or both. And the last act of the dying warden order was to seal what remained of the bond — to bind the covenant shut, like closing a fist around a wound, and hide the key to it somewhere magic could never reach."

She looked at Isandro.

"They hid it in the valley of Vell."

Dominic felt cold move up his spine. "The ward. The silence. Everyone in Vell knows magic doesn't work there — we grow up thinking it's natural, like weather—"

"It isn't weather. It's a lock. The strongest ever made. Your entire valley sits inside a seal designed to smother magic absolutely — no spell can form there, no enchanted thing can enter, no creature of power can cross the line." Yeva's voice gentled. "And no *key* can wake up. Whatever was hidden in Vell would sleep, dormant, harmless, until the end of the world. That was the plan. That was the mercy of it, even. Whatever they hid there would live out its existence in peace, never knowing what it was."

"But the keeper died," Dominic said slowly. He remembered Serra telling him, ash still in the wind behind them. "Years ago. The one maintaining the seal."

"And seals are not stones, Master Marchetti. They are promises. Promises need someone to keep saying them. When the keeper died, the promise began to fray — slowly, invisibly, the way a rope frays. Which meant the lock began to weaken. Which meant that whatever slept in Vell..." She spread her hands. "...began to dream."

Every eye in the room — all three of them — turned to Isandro.

The boy sat perfectly still, hands folded in his lap, watching Yeva with an intensity that would have frightened anyone who didn't know him. Dominic knew him. He knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a child listening so hard it hurt.

"What is he?" Dominic asked. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Not *who*. You said *made*. Yeva — what is he?"

Yeva was quiet for a moment. Then she reached out, slowly, telegraphing every inch of the movement, and touched the drawing of the tower.

"When the wardens sealed the covenant, they had a problem. A lock needs a key, but a key can be stolen, copied, forged, destroyed. So they did something no one had done before or since. They grew one." Her finger traced the small figure at the center of the drawings, arms raised, holding up the sky. "From the last free magic of the Spire — its final breath, if you like — they shaped a living vessel. Not a person enchanted. Not a child possessed. A being *grown from the covenant itself*, flesh woven around oath, blood carrying the binding-words the way yours carry the memory of your mother's face. The old texts call such a thing a *living covenant*. There has only ever been one."

Isandro's hand crept across the table and found Dominic's sleeve. Dominic covered it with his own.

"And the key opens the covenant," Dominic said. Each word cost effort. "It can renew it. Reforge the bond. Bring magic back under law instead of chaos."

"Yes."

"And it can also—"

"End it," Yeva said simply. "Sever the bond forever. Unmake the connection between realms entirely. Either way, the choice requires the key to be *used* — spoken through, awakened, brought to the Spire's heart. Until then, it is only a child. Afterward, it is either salvation or a tombstone for every wonder left in the world."

The candle flames seemed to lean in. Dominic became aware of his own heartbeat, absurdly loud.

* * *

He stood up. He walked to the far wall, which took four steps, and stood there looking at nothing, because the alternative was looking at Isandro, and he couldn't yet.

*A vessel.* The inquisitor's word, from that day in the valley, came back to him with new teeth. He had assumed they hunted a relic, a boy carrying something, a child with a talisman sewn into his coat. Something you could take away from him.

There was nothing to take away. The thing they wanted *was* Isandro.

"Kaelen Voss," he said finally, still facing the wall. "Tell me about Kaelen Voss and my boy."

"Ah." Yeva's chair creaked. "Yes. That part."

He turned back. She had pulled a book toward her — an actual book, bound in scarred leather, its spine broken in the way of something consulted often — but she didn't open it. She just rested her hand on it.

"Understand something about Voss. Everyone paints him as a madman who hates magic, and that's comfortable, because madmen are weather — you endure them and rebuild after. But Voss is not mad. Voss is a man who watched the Sundering's leftovers tear the borderlands apart for thirty years. Wild magic eating villages. Rogue enchanters crowning themselves kings of burning hills. Beasts twisted into horrors roaming free. He decided, quite rationally, that magic is a fire that got loose, and that thrones survive by keeping fires *out*." She tapped the book. "His inquisitors don't destroy every trace of magic they find. They destroy every trace of *organized* magic. Of *renewal*. Because a renewed covenant means magic returns to the world — lawful, yes, bounded, yes, but *present*. Alive. And a world with living magic is a world where Voss's whole philosophy dies, and probably Voss with it.

"So when rumors reached him that the key to the covenant existed — that it had been found, that it was moving west with a beast-speaker from Vell — he didn't send soldiers to capture it." Her eyes flicked to Isandro, and her voice dropped. "He sent them to erase it. A key that is never used can neither renew nor end the covenant. Dead, the boy is simply... a tragedy. And the world stays exactly as broken as Voss needs it to be."

Silence. The kettle ticked softly as it cooled.

Dominic sat back down next to Isandro. The boy was looking at the drawing of himself — the small figure, arms raised — and his face was doing something Dominic had never seen it do. Not fear. Not sadness. Something older and wearier, a look that belonged on no child's face, the look of someone hearing their own biography read aloud and finding it worse than they'd guessed.

*Grown in the dark below the light, and I am sorry.*

He had known, on some level, since the doorstep. Since the snowstorm and the impossible word. But knowing a thing and having a scholar in an ink-stained undercroft explain it in complete sentences were different animals entirely.

"So he's not cargo," Dominic said quietly. "He's not a thing being carried to a place. He's—" He struggled. "He's a *decision*. A decision that hasn't been made yet."

"A decision with a heartbeat," Yeva agreed. "Which is precisely why everyone wants to make it for him. Voss wants the decision ended with a knife. The remnant factions in the east want the covenant renewed so they can claim magic's return as their mandate. Half the free cities would pay a fortune to control the outcome, and the other half would pay double to bury it. And the poor creature at the center of it all cannot even speak to say what *he* wants." She shook her head. "Though I suspect," she added, softer, watching Isandro, "that he speaks rather more clearly than anyone gives him credit for. Just not in words."

Isandro looked up at her. Then, deliberately, he picked up a charcoal stub from the edge of her desk, pulled a fresh sheet toward him, and drew.

He worked fast, the way he did when something mattered — strokes sure and unhesitating. In under a minute he pushed the page across the table.

A tower. The tower, always the tower. But this time the small figure was not beneath it holding up the sky. The small figure stood at the tower's base, and beside it stood a second figure — tall, broad-shouldered, unmistakably Dominic, down to the satchel strap — and the two figures were holding hands. Above them, where the sky usually strained and groaned in his drawings, Isandro had drawn something new: a circle of small linked rings, open, unbroken, resting gently over everything like a crown that weighed nothing.

Below it, in High Covenant script, in his careful looping letters:

*"Not alone. My choice. My hand."*

Yeva read it and had to take her spectacles off again.

Dominic found he couldn't speak at all. He put his arm around the boy and the boy leaned into him, and for a while nobody in the undercroft said anything, and the candles burned, and somewhere above them a wolf paced an honest roof.

*My choice. My hand.*

*He knows,* Dominic thought. *Somehow, some way, he has always known what he is. And he chose this road anyway. Chose me.*

* * *

It was Yeva who broke the quiet, and when she spoke again, all the wonder had drained out of her voice, leaving something flat and urgent.

"There's a practical matter we must discuss, and I'm afraid it cannot wait for sentiment. You came to Harrowgate seeking a scholar. Others come to Harrowgate seeking *you*." She rose, crossed to a cabinet, and pulled out a sheet of paper — a printed notice, cheap ink, the kind nailed to gateposts in every city Dominic had passed through lately, though none of them had borne *his* face. "This went up yesterday. The reward is obscene. Obscene enough that the chestnut woman's honesty is the only reason you're sitting here drinking my tea instead of lying in a cellar waiting for the inquisitors."

Dominic took the notice. His own likeness stared back at him, better-rendered than any portraitist had managed, *beast-speaker of Vell, traveling with a mute child of approximately eight years.* Below it, the reward figure. Below that, smaller: *Deliver both alive. The child must be unharmed.*

Unharmed. Because a damaged key might not work — or might not fail cleanly. Even Voss's cruelty had fine print.

"They know you're in the city," Yeva said. "Not where — the notice went up before confirmation, standard practice, flood the streets and let greed do the searching. But they will confirm. Inquisitors are thorough, and Harrowgate is full of desperate people, and you have been walking a wolf through the market district, which I hear has caused *comment*." She began gathering candles, snuffing some, lighting others, moving through her shelves with sudden purpose. "You cannot stay here tonight. Possibly not the hour. I have ways out of this city that don't involve gates — old ways, aqueduct ways — but I need time to arrange—"

The knock came from above. Three slow blows on the trapdoor in the ceiling, spaced evenly.

Serra's signal. *Come now. Trouble.*

Yeva froze, candle in hand. Isandro was already on his feet, satchel clutched to his chest, eyes wide and fixed on the trapdoor.

"That," said Yeva Corvane, setting the candle down with a steady hand that did not match her face, "was faster than I expected. Master Marchetti — how attached are you to staying dry?"
