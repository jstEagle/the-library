## 9. Hounds at the Gate

Serra had smelled trouble on the wind since dawn, but the wind in Harrowgate smelled of nothing but fish, tar, and too many humans, so she had stopped trusting it days ago.

She lay on the flat roof of the undercroft building, tucked into the shadow of a crooked chimney, and watched the morning market assemble itself below. Humans were strange creatures—noisy as magpies and twice as territorial—and she had spent three days learning their rhythms: the baker who shouted before he was awake enough to mean it, the fishwives whose arguments were a kind of love song, the beggar by the aqueduct arch who was actually a very competent spy for whoever paid him that week. She liked Harrowgate more than she expected to. It was a city with its hackles permanently raised, and she understood cities like that.

The boy was inside with Yeva Corvane, drawing his drawings. The man—Dominic, her soft-spoken, hopeless, infuriating charge—was fetching bread.

That was when Serra saw the beggar stop begging.

He stood up straight, all pretense gone from his spine, and turned his face east toward the harbor road. And though no human ear could have caught it over the market din, Serra's caught what followed: boots. Too many boots, moving in step. Not soldiers' swagger-march but something worse—the measured tread of men who had practiced being frightening until they'd gotten good at it.

Inquisitors. The word rose in her mind with the taste of old ash behind it. She had crossed paths with Voss's hounds once before, years ago, and still sometimes dreamed of the cages they carried.

She flowed off the roof and down through the alleyways, taking the shortcuts only something shaped like smoke could take, and found Dominic coming out of the bakery with a paper bag of rolls and an expression of mild contentment that made her want to shake him.

*Man,* she sent, pushing the thought hard against the thin channel between them. Their bond was still young and leaky; words came through as impressions, mostly, but *danger* needed no translation. *Boots. Many. Coming up Cooper's Row.*

Dominic went still in the way he did—not frozen like prey, but quieted, like a man listening to something far away. Then he nodded once, tucked the bread under his arm, and walked—did not run, because running drew eyes—toward the undercroft door.

She loved that about him, she realized later, and hated that she noticed such things at all. He was afraid. She could smell it rolling off him like rain off wool. And he kept walking anyway, at a baker's pace, carrying breakfast.

* * *

Yeva met them at the top of the cellar stairs with ink on her fingers and fire already in her eyes, which for Yeva was simply her face.

"They're here?" she said.

"Cooper's Row," Dominic said. "Minutes."

"Then we're done anyway." She swept Isandro's drawings into a leather folio and thrust it at Dominic. "Everything you need is in there. The chamber beneath the Spire, the sequence of the binding, the—" She stopped, looked at the boy, and softened by perhaps one degree, which for Yeva Corvane was practically weeping. "You remember what I told you, little scholar?"

Isandro nodded solemnly. Then he tugged Yeva's sleeve and pointed at her, then at himself, then made a shape with his hands—a question. *Are you coming?*

"I'm coming," Yeva said briskly. "Behind you. At a scholarly distance. Go down through the cistern—the aqueduct grate sticks, lift and push, don't pull. Serra, you know what I'm asking."

Serra did. She bared her teeth slightly to show how much she appreciated it.

They went down. The undercroft was Yeva's world: shelves of stolen knowledge, maps of cities that no longer existed, jars of things Serra preferred not to identify by smell. A low arched passage led from the back of the cellar to the old cistern, where Harrowgate's founders had once moved water through the city's bones and modern plumbers had forgotten half of it. It was dark, wet, and perfect for escape, which meant it was also known to every smuggler, thief, and—inquisitors were thorough—inquisitor.

Which was why the stair mattered.

It was a narrow spiral, stone worn hollow by centuries of feet, connecting the cellar to the street door above. One defender at the bottom of that stair could hold ten attackers, if the defender was fast and vicious and did not much care about surviving politely.

Serra sat down on the third step and waited.

Dominic crouched beside her. Up close she could see the fear on him plainly now, and underneath it, stubborn as a burr, something else. "There has to be another way," he said quietly.

*There isn't.* She pushed the certainty at him. *Stair. Me. You take the boy through the water. That is the whole plan.*

"Serra—"

*Man.* She touched her nose briefly to his hand, which was as close to an embrace as her shape allowed. *I am faster than they are. I am angrier than they are. Go.*

He held her gaze a moment longer—that was another thing about him, he actually looked at animals like there might be someone home, which there was, and which almost no human ever checked for—and then he took Isandro's hand and disappeared into the dark of the cistern passage.

From above, muffled by the cellar door, came the sound Serra had been waiting for: wood splintering.

* * *

They came down the stair four abreast, which told her they were confident, which told her they were fools. Gray cloaks, iron masks shaped like blank faces, and the smell of cold iron and sanctimony. In front walked a tall one with silver stitching on his collar—a handler, and slung at his wrist, a leash, and at the end of the leash—

Serra's blood went hot and then cold.

A hound. Not a dog—a hound, one of the mountain-bred shadow-runners, a cousin-kin, its muzzle strapped shut with a barbed band, its eyes dull with the particular misery of a creature made to do the one thing it was bred never to do: obey humans.

The handler saw her and smiled behind his mask. "Wolf," he said. "Collar it."

The hound on the leash whined—a small, terrible sound—and Serra felt something in her chest crack open sideways.

*I'm sorry, kin,* she thought, and then she stopped thinking and started fighting.

The stair was narrow and she was fast. She took the first two men at the knees, not to kill but to unmake their ability to stand, and the stair did the rest—they fell backward into the ones behind them, a clattering avalanche of iron and cursing. The handler yanked the leash and the poor hound surged forward, compelled by whatever cruelty lived in that barbed band, and Serra met it mid-leap, twisted, and let its own momentum carry them both past each other rather than into each other. Through the muzzle strap her kin's eye found hers, and what was in that eye was not hatred. It was *please.*

*I know,* she sent, as gently as she had ever sent anything. *I know. Hold on. This ends soon—I swear it on my mother's grave, this all ends soon.*

More boots above. They'd learned. They started dropping lanterns instead of coming down—oil and flame splashing across the steps—and the undercroft began to burn around her while she fought in the smoke. Fine. Smoke was hers too. She'd been born in forest fire season.

Somewhere below, faintly, she heard water moving, and a small splash, and she allowed herself one breath of relief. The man and the boy were in the aqueduct. Moving.

A blade found her shoulder—she'd misjudged a gap in the smoke—and pain lit her up white. She killed that one. Not maimed. Killed. The first human she had ever killed, and she noted it distantly, the way one notes weather, and kept fighting, because the stair was still holding and the fire was climbing and Yeva Corvane had not come down.

* * *

Yeva had said *behind you, at a scholarly distance.* What she had apparently meant was: *at the top of the stairs, barricading the cellar door with a bookshelf and shouting instructions.*

"The folio has the chamber diagrams!" she called down through the smoke, in the tone of a woman giving directions to a market stall. "Third drawing, the spiral! Do NOT let anyone convince you the binding requires a sacrifice, that's Voss propaganda, I've written a pamphlet—"

"Yeva!" Serra snarled upward. "Come DOWN!"

"And leave forty years of work to these book-burning philistines?" A crash above; glass breaking; then Yeva again, breathless and, horribly, laughing. "Oh, they've knocked over the jar shelf. That'll be memorable. Wolf, listen—the boy's drawings are the key, the man is the voice, don't let either of them doubt it. Promise me you'll get them up the mountain."

Serra put everything she had into the sending, raw and huge: *COME WITH US.*

A pause. Then, gentler than Serra had ever heard her: "Someone has to teach them what's in the books, dear. Better me than the fire."

The ceiling groaned. The stair was lost—flames eating the steps, iron-masked men retreating upward coughing—and Serra finally did what she should have done minutes ago: turned and ran, down into the dark, toward the sound of water, with Yeva's voice following her like a lamp:

"Third drawing, wolf! The spiral! And tell the man—" a cough, a crash, and then, fading, warm as embers, "—tell him he was kinder to a stranger than any scholar deserves."

Then the cellar came down behind her, and Serra ran, and did not look back, because wolves know better than anyone that some doors only open one way.

* * *

She found them a quarter mile downstream where the aqueduct surfaced into an old drainage ravine. Dominic was soaked to the waist, Isandro riding his shoulders with the folio clutched tight, both of them blinking in the gray morning light like creatures newly born.

"You're hurt," Dominic said first, seeing the blood, because of course that was the first thing he said. He set the boy down and reached for her shoulder with those careful hands, and Serra let him, because the touch helped in a way she would deny to her dying day.

*Yeva,* she managed, and couldn't finish the thought. Grief doesn't translate well.

Dominic went still. He looked back at the city—at the thread of black smoke rising from the quarter where the undercroft had been—and Serra watched him understand, watch the grief land on him and be carried, because that was what he did with things. Carried them.

"She stayed," he said softly.

Isandro slid off his shoulders, opened the folio with wet fingers, and found the third drawing. He looked at it a long time. Then he looked up at Serra, and his small hand pressed flat against her bloody shoulder—deliberate, gentle, a child's benediction—and she felt the touch go through her like sunlight through water.

*We go up the mountain,* she sent to them both, turning east where the peaks waited, white and pitiless and, for the first time in her long life, like home. *All three of us. For her. For all of them.*

And behind them Harrowgate burned and shouted and went on being Harrowgate, and ahead the road climbed, and somewhere on it, Serra knew without knowing how, the worst of it was waiting—but that, she decided, striding out in front with blood in her fur and the two of them at her back, was a problem for the mountain.

The mountain, after all, was where she'd been born. And mountains, unlike scholars, knew how to survive.
