## 10. The Beast-Speaker Revealed

The mountain road out of Harrowgate climbed in long switchbacks through pine and shadow, the kind of road that made a man feel like the world had folded itself up around him and forgotten he was inside.

Dominic didn't mind. He had spent most of his life being forgotten by the world, and had come to regard it as a professional courtesy.

"Left foot first," he told the mule they'd bought at the city's edge, a gray, ill-tempered creature named—by its previous owner—Nothing, because, the man said, it was good for nothing. "You're favoring the right. There's a stone in the shoe. Stop sulking and let me fix it."

The mule stopped sulking. The mule let him fix the stone. Serra, trotting ahead with her ears swiveling, gave him one of her looks—the wolf equivalent of an eyebrow raised over a teacup.

"You could at least pretend that's strange," Dominic said to her.

*It stopped being strange around the time I watched you argue a chicken off a windowsill,* Serra said. *In Vell. Where magic doesn't work.*

"It was a very stubborn chicken."

*It was a chicken, Dominic.*

Isandro walked between them, small hands buried in the pockets of the coat Dominic had bought too large on purpose, so the boy could grow into it and into the idea of being looked after. He'd been quiet since Harrowgate—quieter than quiet—and Dominic kept finding himself glancing sideways to make sure the boy was still there, still breathing, still his.

Three days out from the free city, the pines thickened and the road narrowed, and Serra went stiff all at once, like a drawn bow.

*Hunters,* she said. *Downwind. Two-legged. And they've brought leashes.*

* * *

They came off the rocks above the trail an hour past midday: six men in ash-gray cloaks, and behind them, hobbled together with chain and spiked collar, four beasts.

Dominic's stomach turned before his mind caught up. A bear, muzzled so tight its jaw had worn raw. A pair of hunting cats, lean and hollow-flanked, their eyes filmed with a dull obedience that was worse than pain. And worst—a stag. A great twelve-point stag, antlers wrapped in iron bands, standing with the patience of something that had forgotten it had ever been free.

He knew that patience. He had worn it himself for forty years, under a different name.

"Well," said the leader of the hunters, a broad man with a burn-scarred jaw and the cheerful voice of someone who enjoyed his work. "The scholar's description was accurate. One soft-looking fellow, one silent brat, and—" his eyes found Serra and brightened, "—oh, that's generous. That's very generous. The Spire's own courier wolf, if my eyes are any use."

*Your eyes are garbage,* Serra said, hackles rising.

"He's talking about you," Dominic translated quietly.

"I can see it negotiating," the hunter said, and laughed. "Kaelen Voss pays well for the key. Pays double for anything else we bring back breathing. Now here's how this goes—"

"No," Dominic said.

The word came out flat and calm, and it surprised him more than it surprised the hunter. He heard himself continue, in the same voice he used for stubborn mules and frightened sheep:

"I'm going to ask your animals a question instead. And they're going to answer honestly, because unlike you, they don't know how to lie."

The hunter's grin faltered. "What's he doing? What's he—"

Dominic knelt, slow, and opened himself the way he had only ever dared open himself a crack, all his life, in a valley where the gift should have been dead. Here, outside the ward, outside the silence, it came back to him whole—flooded through him like water through cracked ground.

He spoke. Not in words. In the old grammar underneath words.

*Little brother.* To the bear. *Who holds your hurt?*

The bear's head came up. Something moved behind the film of its eyes—not obedience. Recognition.

*The gray men,* the bear said, and its voice in Dominic's skull was a groan of old timber. *The gray men hold it. They have held it since the den burned.*

*And if the chains were gone?*

A long silence. Then, from the stag, a sound like a bell struck underwater: *Gone is a word we do not know. Show us gone.*

"Oh no," said one of the hunters, younger, backing up a step. "Cap. Cap, the cats are looking at him. Why are the cats looking at *him*—"

"Because," Dominic said, rising, and his voice shook now, not with fear but with the sheer unfamiliar pressure of speaking truth aloud, "they were never yours. You borrowed them. And the loan is called in."

He spread his hands—not a threat, an opening—and said, in the tongue that needed no throat:

*Go home.*

* * *

Afterward, Dominic would remember it in pieces, the way one remembers a storm: the bear rising onto its hind legs with the muzzle splitting down the middle as though the leather had simply given up; the cats flowing off the rocks like poured shadow; the hunters' shouts turning from command to pleading in the space of three heartbeats; Serra a blur of silver taking the legs out from under the scarred man before he could reach the crossbow.

But what he remembered most clearly, what he suspected he would carry to his grave, was the moment the stag bent its iron-banded head to Isandro, and Isandro—small, silent, hollow-eyed Isandro—reached up and pulled the band free.

Not with strength. With a wordless pulse of that ancient language, the one older than kings, and the iron simply forgot it had ever been iron.

The hunters fled. All but the scarred leader, who lay winded in the dirt with Serra's teeth resting—gently, almost politely—against his throat.

"The beasts," the man whispered, staring up at Dominic with the expression of a man whose entire cosmology has just caught fire. "You *talked* to them. You're the beast-speaker. Voss has files on you. There's a *file*—"

"There usually is," Dominic said wearily.

"What are you going to do with me?"

Dominic looked at him for a long moment—at the burn scars, at the whip handles hanging from his belt, at the raw places where the bear's muzzle had chafed—and felt something cold and unfamiliar settle into his chest. Not rage exactly. Arithmetic.

"Serra," he said. "What do we do with men like this?"

*In the old packs,* Serra said, without moving her teeth from the man's throat, *we took his weapons and sent him down-mountain barefoot. The mountain decides the rest.*

"That seems fair," Dominic said, and meant it, and hated how easily he meant it.

They took the man's weapons, his boots, his map case—which held, among other things, a fresh copy of Dominic's own likeness, rendered with flattering accuracy—and sent him stumbling down toward the valley. He ran rather than stumbled, in fact, with remarkable speed for a barefoot man.

Then the road was quiet again, except for breathing.

* * *

The freed beasts did not leave immediately.

The bear sat down heavily at the roadside, working its healed jaw, and watched them with the patient attention of a creature deciding something important. The cats draped themselves over a sun-warm boulder like spilled ink. The stag stood close to Isandro, so close its antlers brushed the boy's hair when it lowered its head.

*Where will you go?* Dominic asked them.

*North,* said the bear. *There are dens north that remember dens.*

*East,* said the cats, together, which was unsettling. *There is a forest east that owes us a debt.*

*With him,* said the stag, and touched its nose to Isandro's shoulder.

Dominic crouched to the boy's level. "You've made a friend."

Isandro looked up at him. And then—

Dominic would spend years trying to describe what happened next, and never quite manage it. It was not a large thing. It was barely a thing at all. The corners of the boy's mouth moved upward—hesitantly, experimentally, like ice remembering it could be water—and then settled into place, and Isandro smiled.

It was the first time. Three weeks of drawings and single impossible syllables and reverent beasts and burning villages, and Dominic had never once seen the boy smile, and now here it was, crooked and small and aimed directly at him, and Dominic Marchetti, forty-one years old, veteran of zero battles, felt his eyes sting and had to pretend to inspect the horizon.

"There he is," he managed. "There you are."

*Well,* said Serra softly beside him. *That's that, then.*

*That's what?*

*You were waiting to see if saving him was worth the cost.* She nudged his hip with her nose. *Now you know.*

The bear rumbled something that in bear might have been laughter, heaved itself up, and lumbered north. The cats unspooled eastward. The stag remained, lowering its head again, and this time Isandro pressed his small forehead against its brow, and something passed between them that Dominic chose not to eavesdrop on—even though, for the first time in his life, he could have.

* * *

They made camp early, in the lee of a granite shelf, because none of them—including, apparently, the stag, who stood sentinel at the edge of the firelight like a piece of the mountain that had decided to be polite—much wanted to move.

Dominic cooked barley and dried plums, and Isandro ate with the steady seriousness he brought to all meals, and Serra gnawed a haunch of something she refused to identify, and for a while it was almost cozy, in the way that camps after battles are cozy: the coziness of people who have discovered, abruptly, that they are still alive.

"You saw," Dominic said finally, to the fire more than to anyone. "What I did today."

Isandro looked up. Nodded once.

"It wasn't—I've never done that. Spoken freely. Turned creatures against men." He turned a stick in the coals. "In Vell, it was a whisper. A trick. Talking to a chicken." A pause. "Today it was a weapon. I took those men's own beasts and I—" he stopped, because the honest word was *unmade* them, unmade their authority, their safety, their whole understanding of the world, and some part of them would be running barefoot through their nightmares for years.

Isandro set down his bowl. He picked up a stick of charcoal from the fire kit—he carried charcoal everywhere now, hoarded it like other children hoarded sweets—and drew in the dirt between them.

He drew a hand. Open. Palm up.

Then, above it, a small flame. Then, above the flame, a bird, flying away.

Dominic studied it. "I... gave them back?"

Isandro nodded. Tapped the open hand. Then tapped Dominic's chest, firmly, twice, and nodded again, harder, with the particular ferocity of a child correcting a foolish adult.

*I am the open hand,* Dominic translated slowly. *Not the fist. The beasts weren't weapons. They were prisoners, and I unlocked the door.*

Serra, watching from across the fire, made a low sound that was almost approval. *The cub speaks more sense than the wardens, and he hasn't spoken a word.*

"He's right," Dominic admitted. He looked at his hands—farmer's hands, scribe's hands, hands that had spent a lifetime pretending they were ordinary. "I keep thinking I've become something terrible. But maybe I'm just... loud, for the first time. Maybe everything I did quietly my whole life just got louder."

The stag shifted at the firelight's edge and said, in its bell-underwater voice: *Loud is how mountains wake.*

"Thank you," Dominic told it. "That's either very comforting or very ominous, and I genuinely can't tell."

*Both,* said the stag. *That is how mountains speak.*

Isandro made a sound. Dominic turned—it was half a laugh, a breath of one, and the boy quickly hid it behind his sleeve, but not quickly enough, and the second smile of the day was smaller and better than the first.

* * *

Later, when Isandro slept curled against the stag's warm flank—apparently this was happening now, apparently the boy had a stag—Dominic sat up with Serra and watched the stars come out hard and cold over the pass.

"Voss has a file on me," he said. "Which means the next ones won't be hunters. They'll be soldiers. Maybe worse."

*Yes.*

"And the pass ahead—the Pass of Teeth, Yeva's maps called it—that's where his vanguard is dug in. We're walking straight into it."

*Yes.*

"With a mute child, a stag, and a wolf who gives tactical advice in one-word sentences."

*Two-word,* said Serra. *Sometimes.*

Dominic laughed—quietly, so as not to wake the boy—and it surprised him how easy it was. Something had come loose in him on that road today. Something that had been clenched for forty years.

He thought about the hunter's face when the bear rose. He thought about the muzzle, split like fruit skin. He thought about the file with his own face in it, and about Kaelen Voss somewhere south, reading it, learning that the beast-speaker of Vell was real.

Let him read it, Dominic thought, and was startled by his own boldness. Let him learn what else is real.

*Serra,* he said. *When we get to the Spire—if we get to the Spire—there'll be no going back to the way I was. You know that. Whatever the covenant needs, whatever the wardens ask—*

*You already know what you'll say,* Serra said. *You said it today, on the road, to six armed men and four chained beasts. You just haven't noticed yet.*

He looked at her. She looked at the stars, inscrutable, silver-edged, smiling in the way wolves smile, which is to say with her whole self while appearing to smile not at all.

Below the camp, far down the darkening valley, a signal fire bloomed—one, then two, then a chain of them, marching along the ridgeline toward the high pass. The vanguard, marking the road. Closing the door ahead of them.

Isandro stirred in his sleep and murmured—not words, not yet, but close—and the stag lowered its great antlered head over him like a roof beam.

Dominic fed the fire another branch and watched the enemy lights multiply in the dark.

"Well," he said to no one in particular. "No point knocking. We'll just have to go through the wall."

Serra's tail thumped once against the stone, which from Serra was a battle plan.
