## 11. What Loyalty Costs

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Serra decided she could not carry it another mile.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite, three days out from Harrowgate and two days short of the pass the maps called *the Teeth*. Isandro slept against Dominic's side, his small hands curled around a stub of charcoal even in sleep, as though he might need to draw his way out of a dream. The boy had not drawn anything since the aqueducts. Not a tower, not a face, not a single looping syllable of the old tongue. He ate when food was put in front of him. He walked when they walked. And he watched everything with eyes that had gone quiet in a way that frightened Serra more than any inquisitor's horn.

Dominic sat across the fire mending a strap on the satchel with clumsy, careful fingers. He was humming. He did not seem to know he was humming — some half-remembered valley tune about a miller's daughter, worn soft at the edges like everything else he owned.

*Tell him,* said the voice that had been following her for three days, the one that sounded suspiciously like her dead mother. *Tell him before the mountains do it for you.*

Serra shifted her weight on the stone. Her shoulder wound from the undercroft had closed into an angry pink seam, and it ached in time with her heartbeat, which felt appropriate.

"Dominic."

"Hm?" He bit the thread off with his teeth and looked up. Firelight caught the gray in his beard. He had the particular softness of a man watching a sleeping child, and she hated that she was about to ruin it.

"There is something you should know. Something I should have told you on the mountain road, before the hunters. Before any of it."

His hands went still. He set down the strap. "All right," he said, in that mild voice that always made her want to shake him. Even now. Even knowing what she knew.

She had rehearsed this a hundred times on the road. Standing orders. Chain of command. The clean military language of it. But sitting here, with the boy's breath whistling faintly through his nose and the miller's tune still hanging in the air, every formal word turned to ash in her mouth.

"When the wardens sent me south," she said, "they gave me two orders. The first was to find the key and bring it to the Spire alive." She paused. The fire popped. Somewhere far up the slope, snow slid from a branch with a soft sigh. "The second order was for if I could not do the first."

Dominic frowned slightly, waiting. He still didn't understand. She could see him assembling innocent possibilities — *if the key were lost, if the trail went cold, return home.* As if the wardens dealt in anything so gentle.

"If Voss's people were going to take him," Serra said, "I was to kill Isandro myself. So the key could never be used. By anyone."

The silence afterward was enormous. It had texture. It pressed against her ears like deep water.

Dominic looked at her for a long moment, and she watched the understanding arrive in stages — first confusion, then the horrible arithmetic, then the thing underneath, which was not confusion at all anymore.

"You were going to—" His voice came out strange, flattened. "He was six years old when they gave you that order. He'd been *alive* maybe a year when—"

"I know how old he was."

"Do you?" And there it was. The valley man's temper, which she had suspected lived somewhere under all that gentleness the way a river lives under ice. It came up slow and cold, not hot. Somehow that was worse. "Because you slept beside him, Serra. You carried him across the ford at Harrowgate when his boots filled with water. You let him braid that ridiculous knot in your tail fur that took me two hours to pick out. And the whole time, you were carrying an order to open his throat."

"Yes."

"*Yes?*" He stood. The satchel fell. Isandro stirred, and both of them froze — two predators going motionless over a sleeping fawn — until the boy sighed and burrowed deeper into the blanket. When Dominic spoke again it was barely above the fire's whisper. "Get up. We're walking."

"It's night, Dominic. There's a blizzard coming down off the Teeth, and—"

"Get. Up."

So she got up, because there are moments when the shape of your guilt leaves no room for argument, and they walked out into the cold together while the boy slept on alone by the fire, trusting them both absolutely, which was the worst part. That was the part that followed her through the dark like a second shadow.

* * *

The snow had begun — fat, windless flakes drifting down through the pines — when he finally stopped at the edge of a frozen stream and turned to face her. His breath came in white plumes. His eyes were wet, which he would have been mortified to know.

"Say it plainly," he said. "No soldier language. No 'standing orders.' Say what you were going to do to that child."

Serra looked at the stream. The ice had gone gray-green, thick enough to hold a wolf, thin enough at the edges to show the black water moving underneath. It seemed suddenly very relevant to everything.

"I was going to wait until he slept," she said. "Somewhere quiet. I was going to make it fast. And then I was going to walk back to the Spire and tell the wardens their covenant died with dignity." Her voice did not shake, which surprised her; she had expected it to. "That was the mission. That was the whole shape of my life for four years, Dominic. Find the key or end it. Those were the only two doors out of the room."

"And now?"

"Now the door's gone."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have!" The words cracked out of her louder than she meant, startling a roosting bird somewhere overhead into a flurry of wings. She lowered her head. Snow gathered along her spine and she let it. "You want me to tell you I never would have done it. You want me to swear the moment I saw him drawing pictures in your kitchen, some great love bloomed in my chest and dissolved the order like sugar in tea. And maybe that's the story you need. But it isn't true, and you deserve better than a pretty lie, because you've had enough of those — from the wardens, from Yeva, from everyone who ever called you *safe* while they moved you around a board like a piece."

"Then what is true?"

Serra lifted her head and met his eyes, because this, at least, she owed him standing up.

"What's true is that I spent four years keeping the order alive in me like an ember in a fist. What's true is that the night we left Harrowgate through the aqueducts, with Yeva holding that stair behind us, I checked — I *checked*, Dominic — whether the boy was close enough to reach. Because that's what I am. That's what they made me before the wardens ever found me, and what they remade after, and neither version of me has ever once been given a job that wasn't ending something." She swallowed. The seam on her shoulder throbbed. "And what's also true is that somewhere on this road — I couldn't tell you the day, I couldn't tell you the tree we slept under — I realized I was never going to do it. Not because the order changed. Because *I* changed. And I have been waiting ever since for you to find out and look at me the way you're looking at me now."

The snow fell between them. Down the slope, the fire was a small orange eye in the dark, and beside it a small blanket-wrapped shape, breathing.

"You should have told me," Dominic said finally. Quieter now. The cold anger draining out and leaving only the hurt, which was worse to hear. "Before the mountain. Before I fought beside you. Before I—" He stopped. Started again. "I trusted you with him, Serra. That's not a small thing for a man like me. I hid my whole life. I don't hand out trust like bread."

"I know."

"And you let me keep trusting you while you carried a knife in your pocket the entire time."

"I know."

He turned away from her then and looked up the valley toward the pass, where the peaks had begun to show themselves through the snow-gloom — jagged white teeth against a bruised sky. For a long time he said nothing, and Serra stood in the falling snow and understood that whatever happened next was being decided in the silence, and that she had no leverage at all except the truth, and she had already spent all of it.

"Why didn't you run?" he asked at last.

"What?"

"The night you realized you wouldn't do it. Why not just leave? Slip away in the dark, go back to your wardens, tell them the key was lost. Clean hands. No confession required." He glanced back at her. "You're a wolf who crossed half a continent without being seen. You could have vanished any night you liked."

It was, she thought, a very good question. She had been avoiding it for weeks with the skill of a creature built for avoidance.

"Because running away is just another way of lying," she said. "I'd have been lying to you every day after. And I—" Her throat tightened around the next part, and she had to push it through like a splinter. "I have loved very few things in a very long life, Dominic Marchetti. Most of them are dead. One of them is asleep by that fire down there. And one of them is standing in front of me calling me a liar, which I deserve, but which I would rather endure than escape. Loyalty that runs when it costs something isn't loyalty. It's a habit."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, broken, astonished sound, half sob, aimed mostly at the sky.

"A habit," he repeated. "Four years of murder-orders and you call it a *habit*." He rubbed his face with both hands. "You are the most infuriating creature I have ever spoken with, and I once spent an afternoon arguing with a mule about a bridge."

"The mule sounds reasonable."

"He was not." But some of the ice had gone out of his voice, and he came a step closer, boots crunching on the fresh snow. "Here is where I land, Serra. I'm not going to pretend I heard what I heard and felt nothing. If you had done it — if I'd learned it after — there is nothing in this world that would have mended it. Nothing you could ever have said." He held up a hand before she could speak. "But you didn't do it. And you told me. Badly, late, and after nearly getting us all killed on a mountain, but you told me, and I think — I think that cost you more than the killing would have."

It had. She said none of that aloud. Wolves do not confirm their wounds.

"So we start again," Dominic said. "Not clean. Nothing between people is ever clean. But honest. From tonight, no orders you're still carrying, no truths you're still holding. Whatever the wardens asked of you, whatever you're afraid of — it comes to me too. Both of us or neither. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Serra said, and found that the word loosened something in her chest that had been clenched since Harrowgate, since before Harrowgate, possibly since birth.

"One more condition." His mouth twitched, the ghost of the valley humor surfacing through the wreckage. "The knot. In your tail. You let him do it and you never once told me it hurt."

"It didn't hurt," Serra said with dignity. "It was undignified. There's a difference."

"Mm." He turned back toward the camp, then paused. "For what it's worth — and I'll deny saying this in daylight — I'm glad it was you they sent. Even with the knife in your pocket. Especially with the knife in your pocket. A guardian who's never been tempted doesn't know what she's guarding."

She had no answer for that, so she did what wolves do instead: she walked beside him back down through the snow, close enough that their shoulders touched, and let that be the whole speech.

* * *

Isandro was awake when they returned. Sitting up, blanket pooled around his waist, charcoal stub in hand — and on the flat stone beside him, a drawing.

Serra smelled it before she saw it: the faint copper-and-storm scent that rose off the boy whenever the old magic moved in him, the scent that had been absent for days. Her hackles rose before her hope did.

The drawing was simple, the way his drawings always were. Two figures by a fire — one large and round-shouldered, one four-legged with a crooked line for her scarred shoulder. Between them, in the air, the boy had drawn a knot. The same ridiculous tangle he'd once braided into her fur. And above it, in letters that shimmered faintly when the firelight crossed them, characters Serra could not read but recognized in her bones: the old tongue, the Spire's tongue, the tongue of covenants.

One word. Maybe two.

Dominic crouched slowly beside the boy. "What does it say, little one?"

Isandro looked at the drawing. Looked at Serra. And then he did something he had not done since the pass began to loom over their road: he reached out and touched the knot in the picture, deliberately, the way you touch a wound to prove it's healing.

Serra didn't need the translation. Some things cross between tongues without help.

*Forgiven,* the drawing said. Or perhaps: *Forgiving.* With Isandro it was often impossible to tell the difference, and perhaps that was the point.

Dominic made a sound low in his chest that he would also have denied in daylight, and pulled the boy against his side, and Serra lay down across the mouth of the hollow with her scarred shoulder to the wind, guarding the fire, guarding the two of them, guarding at last something she had chosen rather than been ordered to guard.

She was almost asleep when she heard it — far up the valley, past the treeline, past the teeth of the pass itself: a horn. Long, low, and wrong. Not an inquisitor's signal-horn. Something older, blown by lungs much larger than a man's.

The hair rose along her whole body. Isandro's hand found her fur in the dark and gripped it.

Beyond the pass, answered by another horn, and another — a conversation in the dark, moving closer, and whatever spoke in that voice was not Voss's soldiers at all.

It was waking up.
