## 12. The Pass of Teeth

The mountain had teeth.

That was what the shepherds in the last village had called it, an old woman with a face like a walnut shell and a voice like gravel poured slowly. "The Pass of Teeth," she'd said, and laughed at whatever she saw in Dominic's expression. "Jagged stone on both sides, and the wind comes through screaming like something being murdered. Nobody crosses it in winter."

"We have to," Dominic had said.

"Then you're fools." She'd sold them bread anyway, and a pot of honey, and pressed a charm of knotted red thread into Isandro's hand with a look that was either pity or blessing. Possibly both.

Now, three days later, Dominic understood her laughter. The pass lived up to its name and then some. The walls of it rose sheer and gray-black on either side, streaked with ice that caught the light like old knives, and between them the wind howled without pause or mercy, a sound so constant that after a while you stopped hearing it as sound and started feeling it as a weight pressing on your skull from the inside.

Serra trotted ahead through the knee-deep snow, a gray shadow against gray stone, and every hundred paces she paused, lifted her muzzle, tasted the air, and looked back at him. Her ears were flat. They had been flat for two days.

*Something's wrong,* she said, for perhaps the dozenth time since dawn. *The wind is wrong. It carries smoke and iron and too many men.*

"You said that yesterday," Dominic called over the gale, "and yesterday it was just a charcoal-burner's camp."

*Yesterday I was wrong about the direction. Not the smell.* She turned back into the wind, and he saw her shoulders set in that way she had, low and deliberate, like a drawn bow. *There are soldiers in this pass, Dominic. Many soldiers. And they are not here for the view.*

Isandro walked between them, wrapped in every layer Dominic owned plus two blankets and a sheepskin, his small face just a pale oval above the wool. He hadn't spoken — he never spoke — but he hadn't drawn anything in two days either, which frightened Dominic more than the soldiers did. The boy's slate hung unused at his side. When Dominic asked if he was cold, Isandro would nod or shake his head. When Dominic asked anything else, the boy simply looked at him with those dark, ancient eyes and said nothing at all, in any language, in any way.

Since the night at the fire, three weeks ago now, when Isandro had woken screaming from a dream and the flames had leapt six feet high and burned blue, the boy had been... quieter. Quieter than mute. As though he were holding himself in with both hands.

Dominic thought about that fire sometimes, usually at night, usually when he couldn't sleep. He thought about the way the blue light had reflected in Isandro's eyes, and the way the boy had looked at his own hands afterward as if they belonged to someone else.

He pushed the thought away and climbed.

* * *

They made camp at midday in the lee of an overturned boulder the size of a barn, and Dominic built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, because Serra insisted, and Serra's insistence had kept them alive this long. They ate bread and honey and the last of the dried plums. Isandro ate mechanically, watching the pass behind them, then the pass ahead, then the sky.

*They've stopped following us,* Serra said suddenly. She stood at the mouth of their little hollow, snow beaded on her whiskers. *That's what worries me. For three days there have been scouts behind us, always half a mile back, never closing. Now nothing.*

"Maybe we lost them."

*Wolves don't lose hunters. Hunters lose wolves.* She bared her teeth at the wind, which was not really an argument but felt like one. *If they've stopped following, it's because they no longer need to follow. Because they know where we're going, and they're already there.*

Dominic looked down the pass, northward, toward the Spire. Somewhere beyond these mountains, past the frozen highlands and the shattered bridge at Vael's Crossing, the tower waited — half ruin, half living thing, according to Yeva Corvane's frantic notes. The place Isandro had been made for. The place they had been crawling toward for two months, one frozen mile at a time.

"They'll be waiting in force," he said quietly.

*Yes.*

"And we can't go around. Yeva's map says there's no around. There's this pass or a month's detour through the ice fields, and we don't have a month's food, and Isandro—"

He stopped. The boy was looking at him. Not at the fire, not at the bread, but at him, with an expression Dominic had learned to read the hard way: the particular stillness of a child who has understood every word and wishes he hadn't.

"It'll be all right," Dominic said, which was a lie, and Isandro's eyes said *I know it's a lie*, and Dominic's own eyes said *I'm sorry*, and that whole conversation took about four seconds and was the most honest talk they'd had in days.

Serra lay down across the mouth of the hollow, her back to them, facing out. *Sleep,* she said. *I'll watch. And Dominic — if I wake you running, don't ask questions. Just run.*

He slept badly, in the way of a man who knows exactly what's coming and can do precisely nothing about it.

* * *

It came before dawn.

Not the soldiers first — the storm. The wind changed its mind all at once, the way weather does in high places, and the gentle steady scream of the pass became something else entirely: a white roaring chaos, snow moving sideways, the world reduced to arm's length. Dominic woke with ice on his eyelashes and Serra's muzzle shoved hard against his chest.

*Up. Now. They're coming.*

"How many?"

*I count thirty. Maybe more, hidden by the storm. Vanguard troops — black cloaks, white hoods. Inquisitors' guard.* A pause, and then, with grim satisfaction: *And four handlers with chained beasts. Two ice-wyrms and something big I don't want to name.*

"The enslaved kind?"

*What other kind does Voss use?*

Dominic got Isandro up, got the satchel on, got the boy's mittens on his hands — the boy let him, passive as a doll, which was worse than protest — and then stood in the white roaring dark and faced the arithmetic of it. Thirty soldiers ahead. Storm everywhere. One wolf, one man with a gift that worked best on animals, and one child who was either the salvation of two worlds or the most dangerous thing in them.

"There's no way past them," he said. "Is there."

*No,* Serra said. *But there might be a way through. The handlers chain their beasts with pain-wards — iron collars, binding runes. If you could reach the beasts—*

"I'd have to get close enough to touch the collars. Through thirty soldiers. In a blizzard."

*You asked if there was a way. I didn't say it was a good one.*

Despite everything — the cold, the fear, the boy's hollow silence — Dominic felt a laugh start somewhere in his chest, thin and slightly mad. "You know, when I met you, you were a wounded wolf who mostly wanted breakfast."

*People change.* She turned into the storm. *Stay behind me. Follow my voice. And whatever happens — keep Isandro moving north.*

Then the storm lit up white, and the shouting began.

* * *

What followed, Dominic would remember afterward in fragments, the way you remember a rockslide — not as events but as impacts.

A lantern-flare cutting the whiteness, and shapes resolving out of it: soldiers in black, shields up, a line of them strung across the pass like beads on a wire. A sergeant's voice booming commands that the wind shredded. The handlers behind them, gaunt men in gray, and at their feet the beasts —

Dominic had seen the war-beasts once before, from a distance, at Harrowgate. Up close was different. Up close you could see the scars where the binding-runes had been cut into the skin, could see the way the great creatures moved, not like animals at all but like machines wearing animal shapes, every natural grace amputated. The ice-wyrms were the worst: serpentine things the size of oxen, scales frosted white, their jaws muzzled with iron bands studded with runes that glowed a sickly green. Their eyes were the eyes of creatures dreaming under anesthesia, aware and unable to act.

And the fourth beast — Serra hadn't named it, and Dominic understood why when he saw it. It was a bear, or had been. Now it was a mountain of scarred muscle with a collar thick as a tree trunk, and it moved when the handler pulled, and stopped when the handler pulled, and its breathing was the saddest sound Dominic had ever heard.

*The bear first,* Serra snarled, already flowing forward along the rocks, a shadow among shadows. *Its handler is closest. Go left, along the wall — the snow drifts will cover you—*

The first crossbow bolt came out of the storm and buried itself in the drift a hand's width from Dominic's cheek.

After that, everything was noise.

He ran. That was most of it, honestly — the grand strategy reduced to running, bent double, through snow that grabbed at his knees, while bolts hissed past and men shouted and somewhere Serra's voice rose above it all, not words anymore but pure wolf-song, a challenge thrown at thirty throats. He heard soldiers scream — heard one scream in a way that meant Serra had reached him — and hated it and blessed it in the same breath.

The drifts. Left, along the wall. He found the channel of deep powder where the wind had piled snow against the eastern cliff, and he plunged into it, and the world went white and quiet except for his own heart hammering, and for one absurd moment he thought he might actually make it.

Then a soldier stepped out of the storm directly in front of him.

They stared at each other, equally astonished, close enough to touch. The soldier was young. He had a pimple on his chin and a crossbow half-raised and an expression of a man who had signed up for regular pay and found himself hunting children in a blizzard.

"Don't," Dominic said.

The young soldier's eyes flicked past Dominic's shoulder — to Isandro, small and bundled and staring — and something in his face wavered. His crossbow dipped an inch.

An inch was enough. Dominic slammed past him, shoulder-first, sending them both sprawling, and kept running, and did not look back to see whether the boy soldier followed. He heard boots behind him for ten strides, twenty, and then the boots stopped, and he chose — he would always choose — to believe the boy had simply decided not to.

The handlers were ahead. He could see them now through the churned snow: three men in gray with iron rods, and the bear, and behind it the writhing forms of the ice-wyrms, and the soldiers forming a ragged ring because their sergeant was screaming that the wolf was a diversion, find the child, FIND THE CHILD—

Dominic came out of the drift at a dead run and hit the nearest handler like a sack of turnips.

They went down together. The iron rod skittered away. The handler was stronger than he looked; he got a forearm across Dominic's throat and his knee in Dominic's stomach, and his face, inches away, was calm in a way that was far worse than rage. "The vessel," the man said. "Where is the vessel?"

And Dominic, choking, doing the only thing he knew how to do, reached out with the other part of himself — the part that had talked to cats and cart-horses his whole quiet life — and touched the bear's mind.

What he found there nearly broke him.

Pain. Layers and layers of pain, years of it, packed down like sediment. And beneath the pain, buried so deep it was almost archaeological: a memory of salmon rivers. Of standing hip-deep in cold bright water with fish flashing silver around his legs. Of a mate whose fur was the color of autumn. Of cubs.

The bear was not a machine. The bear was a prisoner who had been shown a river once, long ago, and remembered it every single day.

*Oh,* Dominic said, inside that vast bruised mind. *Oh, friend. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.*

The bear's head turned. Slowly, grindingly, against the pull of the collar and the rod and the handler's will, it turned, and one dark eye found the man lying in the snow beneath its master.

*The collar,* Dominic whispered into that pain. *Can you — if someone breaks the collar—*

The handler raised the iron rod to strike Dominic's face.

The bear's paw came down on the handler's chest with a sound like a door slamming in a cathedral.

Everything after that happened very fast, and none of it went the way anyone planned. The second handler screamed and drove his rod into the bear's flank, and the bear roared — a real roar, the first free sound it had made in years, and the ice-wyrms thrashed against their bonds as if the sound itself had loosened something. Soldiers wheeled toward the chaos. Serra erupted from the storm into the middle of it, a gray whirlwind of teeth, buying seconds. The sergeant was shouting orders that contradicted each other. A wyrm's tail swept two men off their feet. Snow, blood, torchlight, noise—

And through all of it, Isandro stood alone in the open, twenty yards back, exactly where Dominic had told him to stay, exactly where he should have been safe.

He wasn't safe. A soldier had found him. Dominic saw it happen as if underwater: a black-cloaked man breaking from the ring, sword out, lunging for the small bundled figure in the snow — and Isandro looking up at him, and the boy's face doing something it had not done in weeks.

It filled with terror. Pure, childish, unguarded terror — the terror of a small creature about to be hurt, with no plan and no protector in reach.

And the world answered him.

Later, Serra would try to describe it and fail. Later, Dominic would lie awake trying to build it again in memory and only ever hold pieces. But in the moment, there was no describing, only experiencing: a pulse of light that was not light, a sound that was not sound, a pressure like the whole mountain inhaling. Every flake of snow in the pass hung motionless for one impossible heartbeat. The runes on the wyrms' muzzles flared green and then shattered. The binding-collars split like eggshells. The soldier reaching for Isandro was lifted off his feet and set down — gently, gently, like a toy a giant tired of — fifty feet away in a drift, unconscious but breathing.

And every chained beast in the Pass of Teeth woke up.

The wyrms came out of their bindings shrieking — joyfully, horribly, the sound of creatures remembering they had voices. The bear reared to its full height, taller than two men, snow sliding off its scarred back, and bellowed at the storm. Soldiers fled. Handlers fled faster. The sergeant stood his ground longest, shouting about duty and the usurper's wrath, until a freed wyrm lowered its head to within a foot of his face and exhaled a breath that froze his beard solid, and then he discovered a previously unknown talent for running.

Then the light faded. The snow fell again. The wind resumed its screaming.

And Isandro collapsed into the snow like a puppet with cut strings.

* * *

Dominic reached him in seconds that felt like years. The boy was breathing — thank every god, breathing — but his eyes were open and fixed on nothing, and when Dominic gathered him up the boy weighed almost nothing, as if the pulse had taken something out of him that flesh couldn't spare.

"Isandro. Isandro, look at me. Look at me."

The dark eyes moved. Found Dominic's face. And looked through it.

"No. No, no — hey. Hey." He fumbled the slate from the boy's side, pressed the chalk into the small mittened hand, guided it. "Draw me something. Anything. Draw me the bread, draw me the stupid plum stones, draw me anything, please—"

The chalk moved. Weakly. It made a mark, a wavering line, and then another, and then the hand stopped, and the chalk rolled away into the snow, and Isandro's eyes drifted shut — not sleep, something deeper and more frightening, a shutting-down, a banked fire — and he was silent.

Silent even in drawings. Silent everywhere.

Serra appeared out of the storm, bloodied at the shoulder, snow crusted in her ruff, and stood over them both. *The soldiers are gone,* she said, and her voice was strange, shaken. *All of them. Even the ones who ran well. The beasts are... * She trailed off. Down the pass, the freed wyrms were coiling away into the high country, and the bear stood motionless in the falling snow, looking back at Dominic with an expression he had no words for. Gratitude, maybe. Or a question.

*What did the boy do?* Serra asked softly. *Dominic. What did he just do?*

"I don't know." His voice cracked on it. He pulled Isandro against his chest, wrapped every layer around the small body, felt the faint rabbit-quick heartbeat. "Yeva said he was grown from the Spire's last magic. She said he was a covenant with legs. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought that meant he could open doors. I didn't think it meant he could break collars with a scream. I didn't think it meant—" His breath fogged and tore away. "He emptied himself out doing it, Serra. Look at him. He's not sleeping. He's *drained*. Like a cup poured dry."

*Then we fill him back up,* Serra said, with the fierce practicality that had carried them through everything. *Food. Rest. Warmth. We get him to the Spire, where whatever he is, there are people who know what he is.*

"And if the Spire's people look at him the way I'm looking at him right now?" Dominic stood, the boy a fragile weight in his arms, and looked north up the pass, where the storm was beginning to spend itself against the highest peaks. Beyond them, invisible, the Spire waited. "Serra, he's eight years old. He drew me a picture of a house with a garden, back on the road, and he pointed at it and then at me, and I understood — he wants a home. A garden. A *life*. And instead he's breaking armies open with his bare soul, and every time it takes a piece of him I don't know how to give back."

Serra leaned her shoulder against his leg — the old gesture, the first one she'd ever offered him, back when she was a wounded stranger and he was a fool with bread. *Then we carry the pieces for him until he can carry them himself,* she said. *That's what family does. You taught me that. Don't stop teaching it now.*

For a long moment Dominic stood in the dying storm, the freed bear watching from the distance, the boy silent in his arms.

Then he walked north.

Behind them, the Pass of Teeth fell quiet. Ahead, past the last frozen miles, the peaks opened onto a high valley — and there, catching the first pale light of morning like a blade held up to the sun, stood a tower.

Half ruin. Half alive. And waiting.
