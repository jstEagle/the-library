## 13. The Spire Ascendant

The mountain stopped being a mountain on the third day.

Isandro felt it first through his feet, through the snow, through the old tongue that lived in his chest like a second heartbeat. The stone beneath them had a pulse. Not the slow sleepy pulse of ordinary rock—*that* kind of rock said nothing at all, or only grumbled about frost—but this stone was saying something urgent and repeated, over and over, in the way a heart says *now, now, now.*

He tugged Serra's fur to make her stop.

"What is it, little one?" she asked. She had taken to calling him that somewhere past Harrowgate, after he had slept three nights against her flank without once waking screaming. He liked it better than "the key," which was what everyone else called him when they thought he couldn't hear.

He pointed down.

Serra lowered her nose to the drifts. Dominic crouched beside her, breath steaming, eyebrows doing the thing they did when the world refused to behave—the left one up, the right one skeptical. Isandro had begun keeping a private catalogue of Dominic's eyebrows. They were more expressive than most people's whole faces.

"That's not an echo," Dominic said slowly. He pressed his bare palm flat against the snow. Even here, even now, with magic roaring back into the world around them like a river un-dammed, his gift worked strangely, quietly, sideways. The beasts heard him because he listened first. "That's... a heartbeat."

Isandro nodded. *Yes,* he thought at them, as hard as thinking could go. *Finally. You're catching up.*

Because he had known since the pass. Since the terrible bright moment in the blizzard when the thing inside him had torn loose and saved them and emptied him out all at once. Since he had woken in Serra's arms unable even to draw, his charcoal sticks feeling like foreign objects, his hands remembering shapes but not meanings. In those hollow days he had still been able to feel the Spire. It had been calling him the way a mother calls across a market square—not words, just the certainty of being looked for.

*Now, now, now.*

They climbed.

* * *

The Spire did not appear so much as admit them.

One moment there was only the pass's grey shoulder of rock and the wind trying to pry them off the world; the next, the clouds parted like a curtain drawn by a considerate hand, and there it stood.

Isandro had seen it before. He knew that the way you know your own hands—except his memories were broken plates, and someone else had glued them back together wrong. A tower. Fire below it. A woman's hands, ink-stained, pressing something small and warm against his chest. *Run,* she had mouthed, though he hadn't known yet what mouths did.

But no memory had prepared him for how *alive* it looked.

Half the tower was ruin—that much was true. Its crown had broken off centuries ago and hung above it in defiance of every law Dominic would have muttered about, a shattered ring of stone turning slowly in the sky like a crown tossed up by a child and never caught. Snow fell upward near its base. Vines the color of green glass climbed its flanks, and where they touched the ruined stone, the stone *grew*: new walls budding like scar tissue, windows opening like eyes blinking awake.

"It's healing itself," Dominic whispered. His voice had gone strange and soft, the voice he used for frightened animals and for Isandro after nightmares. "Or... trying to."

*Trying,* Isandro agreed. That was the word the tower used too. All that urgent pulsing in the rock was not triumph. It was effort. The Spire had been holding its breath for years, the way he held his own breath when the bad memories came, and it was very tired.

Serra's ears flattened. "I don't like it," she said—which, from Serra, was practically a love letter. She disliked everything she didn't want to protect. "It's watching us."

"Everything here is watching us," Dominic said. "Look at the goats."

There were, in fact, goats. White mountain goats stood along the ledges in perfect silent rows, all facing inward, all facing the path, like an honor guard that had been waiting a very long time and had opinions about their uniforms. As they passed, each goat bowed its head slightly.

Dominic stopped dead.

"They're—" He turned to Isandro, and his face did something complicated: wonder and fear fighting for the same real estate. "They're greeting *you*. Not me. Me they're just tolerating." A pause. "Story of my life."

Isandro almost smiled. Almost. His face still wasn't sure how.

He reached out and touched the nearest goat's nose, because that was polite, and because the goat's mind was a soft warm place full of lichen and dignity. *Thank you,* he told it.

The goat's eyes rolled joyfully. *THE LITTLE KING SPEAKS,* it announced to every goat on the mountainside, and forty goats began bleating at once in what was unmistakably celebration.

"Oh no," Dominic said faintly. "It's contagious."

* * *

The door was thirty feet of black wood set into living stone, carved with the same spiral script that lived under Isandro's skin.

He had never touched it before. But his feet knew the way, and his hand rose without asking him, and when his palm met the wood the spirals lit gold from within, spreading outward like dawn poured into water.

The door opened onto warmth.

Inside, the Spire was hollow—no, not hollow. *Open.* A vast shaft rose through the tower's heart, and light fell from the broken crown high above, and gardens grew in rings along the inner walls at every level, some lush, some greyed and sleeping, some nothing but bones of trellis. Water ran somewhere, singing. Air moved with intention. And everywhere, everywhere, the old tongue was written—in stone, in moss, in the arrangement of falling leaves—and everywhere it said the same thing:

*Hold. Hold. Hold.*

A covenant's whole grammar, Isandro thought, and grief moved through him like weather. So few words left. So much work still asked of them.

"They kept it alive," Dominic breathed. He turned in a slow circle, satchel still on his shoulder, looking like a man who had packed for an inn and arrived at a cathedral. "All these years, after the keeper died, after the ward-lines failed—one by one—they *kept it alive.*"

"Who?" Serra asked. Her hackles were up, her nose working. "Someone's here. Three somethings. Old somethings. They smell like—" She frowned in the way wolves frown, which is mostly teeth. "Like winter. Like libraries. Like dust that's *decided* things."

The lamps came first.

They floated down through the shaft in a slow spiral, seven lights of white flame that cast no heat, and behind them came the footsteps—slow, deliberate, the rhythm of people who had learned that haste broke things.

Three figures emerged from a garden terrace above: robed, silver-haired, ancient in the way of trees rather than men. Two walked. One glided, her feet an inch above the ground, whether from habit or power Isandro couldn't tell. Their faces were lined like maps of places Isandro had never been, and their eyes—

Their eyes found him, and everything in them broke open.

The gliding woman descended first. She drifted down the last ten feet as though stairs were a memory she'd outgrown, and knelt on the stone floor before Isandro with a crack like a branch giving way, knees against rock, head bowed, both hands pressed flat to the ground in the oldest gesture Isandro's borrowed memories knew: *earth before sky.*

The other two followed. The sound of three ancient wardens kneeling echoed up the whole hollow height of the Spire, and the gardens rustled, and somewhere far above, the broken crown of the tower rang once, softly, like a struck bell made of morning.

"We had given up hope of you," the gliding woman said, and her voice trembled. "Forgive us. We had *given up hope*, little vessel, and hope is the one thing we swore never to surrender."

Isandro stared.

No one had ever knelt to him. People had bowed, in the fragments of memory—servants, soldiers, the woman with ink-stained hands bending over his cradle—but kneeling was different. Kneeling was a weight offered freely. He didn't know where to put it.

Then Dominic stepped forward, and put himself between Isandro and the wardens, and Isandro could have laughed, except laughing required breath he didn't have.

"Up," Dominic said to three immortal guardians of the seat of all magic, in exactly the tone he used on stubborn mules. "Please. You'll frighten him. He's six."

"He is the covenant," said the tallest warden, rising with the groan of a man whose joints remembered five hundred winters. "He is the bond between realms grown into a child's shape, and we—"

"He is *Isandro,*" Dominic said. "And he likes honey cakes, and he's afraid of deep water, and last week he cried for an hour because a lamb died, and if any of you call him 'vessel' again I will—" He stopped, visibly renegotiating with himself about what, precisely, a man with no army and one wolf threatened the keepers of the Spire with. "I will be extremely rude," he finished, with tremendous dignity.

Serra made a sound that was suspiciously close to a snicker.

The gliding woman looked at Dominic for a long moment. Then she smiled, slow as thaw, and Isandro saw that her eyes were wet.

"You must be the speaker," she said.

"I'm nobody," Dominic said. "I'm a farmer who talks to animals."

"No," the woman said gently. "You are the man who stood between the covenant and strangers who frightened it. That is what a speaker is." She extended her hand toward the great stair winding up into the tower's heart. "Come. There are truths waiting for you, and I am too old to tell them standing."

* * *

The chamber at the tower's heart was round and warm, lit by the floating lamps, its walls a spiral of the old script rising toward the broken sky-crown visible through a wound in the ceiling. Isandro sat on a bench that was somehow also growing, small pale flowers opening along its edge, and Serra lay across his feet like a sentry who happened to be a blanket. Dominic stood. Dominic always stood when he was scared; Isandro had noticed that too. Standing made him feel useful.

"The truth," the gliding woman—who introduced herself as Warden Maerlin, while her brothers were Ossen and the nearly-silent Corvath—"the truth is that the covenant can be renewed. We know the rites. We have kept them alive in our memories for thirty years, word by word, the way you keep a fire alive by breathing on it."

Relief crossed Dominic's face like sun through cloud.

"But," Maerlin said, and the word dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

"There's always a *but,*" Dominic said wearily. "There's been a but since the doorstep."

"The covenant binds two worlds with one voice," Maerlin said. "When it was first forged, the speaker was the Keeper herself—a woman born half in the mortal tongue and half in the old. Every renewal since has required the same: one throat that speaks both languages truly. Not translated. *Truly.* The binding cannot pass through interpretation. It must pass through understanding." Her old eyes went to Isandro, soft and sorrowing. "The child holds the old tongue entire. It is woven into him. But the covenant must be spoken *through* a mortal voice—a voice of the valley-world, freely given, joined willingly to the Spire. The vessel alone cannot renew it. The vessel is the lock. Someone must be the key-turning."

Silence.

Isandro watched it land on Dominic the way he had watched the blizzard-magic land on the vanguard: total, and slow, and inescapable. He watched Dominic's hands go still. Watched him look at the spiral walls, at the gardens, at Isandro himself—and then away, quickly, the way you look away from a thing too bright.

"No," Dominic said.

"Dominic—" Maerlin began.

"No. No, I—you've got the wrong man. I'm not—I talk to *goats.* Goats and chickens and one deeply judgmental mule. I'm not a—I don't speak the old tongue. I've never even *heard* the old tongue until this month. I'm a farmer from a valley that forgets, that's the whole point of the valley, it *forgets*, that's why I loved it—"

"You hear the old tongue every time you listen," Ossen rumbled. "Beasts speak the oldest dialect of it, mortal-tongued man. Why do you think your gift survived the ward? Why do you think it *flickered*, faintly, in a place built to smother magic?" The old warden leaned forward. "Your gift was never a stray spark, farmer. It is a thread of the covenant itself, knotted into your blood, waiting. The valley hid you. The valley was *made* to hide you. And now the hiding is done."

Corvath spoke then, the first time, and his voice was like wind through a canyon. "There is one candidate," he said. "There has only ever been one candidate. We have waited thirty years knowing it, praying we were wrong."

Dominic sat down. It wasn't a decision so much as gravity winning.

Isandro slid off the bench and went to him. He didn't have words—he barely had drawings yet, his hands still shy after the pass—but he had this: he took Dominic's big rough hand in both of his small ones, and he held it, and he let the old tongue hum low in his chest where Dominic could feel it through their palms. *Here,* it said. *With you. Now, now, now.*

Dominic looked down at him. His eyebrows were doing something Isandro had never catalogued before: both of them down, both of them trembling.

"I finally found a quiet life," Dominic said to no one, to everyone, to the tower that had been holding its breath for thirty years. "Do you know how long it took me to find a quiet life?"

Maerlin's smile was sad and kind. "I imagine," she said, "it took you exactly as long as it takes everyone. And I imagine you already know quiet lives aren't found, farmer. They're *kept*—by people willing to stand in front of the dark and say no." She gestured upward, through the wound in the ceiling, toward the turning broken crown and the storm-light gathering beyond the mountains, toward the south, where fires burned. "Kaelen Voss marches with an army whose discipline is anchored in stolen wards. When the covenant dies fully—and it dies within the season—nothing mortal-made will stand against him. Or—" her eyes returned to Dominic, and did not waver— "someone stands at this Spire's heart and speaks the binding, and gives up the quiet forever."

The lamps flickered. Far above, the crown rang again, softer, like a question.

Dominic closed his eyes.

Isandro squeezed his hand, and felt the tremor in it, and understood—for the first time in his short, plate-glued life—that being chosen feels exactly like being afraid, and that the two can live in the same chest, and that love is what decides which one gets to drive.

Outside, far to the south, thunder answered the bell.

Maerlin turned her head, listening, and her ancient face went grim. "Ah," she said quietly. "It seems, farmer, that the choice may be shorter than we hoped. There are riders on the southern road—and beasts with them that walk in chains."
