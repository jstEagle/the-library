## 14. The Warden's Bargain

The chamber at the top of the Spire had no roof.

Dominic noticed this before he noticed anything else—that the highest room in the tallest tower in the world simply *ended*, its walls rising into open sky, and yet the wind that howled through the Pass of Teeth did not enter here. Snow hung in the air above them like a held breath, unmoving, glittering faintly in the light of three braziers that burned without smoke. He had the absurd thought that if he reached up, he could pluck a snowflake from the ceiling of the world and it would not melt against his palm.

He did not reach up. His hands were busy holding Isandro's shoulder, and Isandro was busy staring at the three wardens who had knelt to the boy and now stood regarding Dominic with expressions he could only describe as *patiently terrible*—the way mountains are patient, and terrible, and entirely unbothered by what you think of them.

The eldest of the three stepped forward. She was small, which surprised him; he had expected the guardians of the Spire to be tall as their tower. Her hair was white as the snow above and her face was seamed like old leather, but her eyes were young, or perhaps ageless, the way a hawk's eyes are neither. She carried a staff taller than she was, and she leaned on it with the ease of someone who had been leaning on it for centuries.

"You are Dominic Marchetti," she said. Not a question. "Of the valley of Vell."

"I am," Dominic said. "And you have the advantage of me, madam."

"Vessan," she said. "First Warden of the Spire, keeper of what remains." She gestured with her chin at her two companions—a broad man whose skin was dark as walnut and whose arms were wrapped in faded bandages, and a woman so thin and still that Dominic had half-suspected, on the climb up, that she was a statue someone had left out in the weather. "Oren. Liss. There were once four hundred of us. Now there are three, and one of those is mostly stone." The thin woman—Liss—turned her head with a creaking sound and gave him a smile that suggested she found this funny. "You will forgive us our bluntness. We have not had guests in a long time, and we have forgotten how to be polite."

"We noticed," Serra said dryly, from where she sat near the door, licking a wound along her flank. "Most hosts don't send a staircase that tries to eat you."

"The staircase tests," Oren rumbled. "Yours passed. Eventually."

Dominic cleared his throat. "Madam Vessan. You knelt to Isandro. You called him—" He stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be taken away. "You know what he is."

"We know what he is," Vessan said gently. "Better than anyone living. Better than the ones who made him." She looked down at the boy, and something in her ancient face softened. "Hello, little key. Do you remember us?"

Isandro did not answer. He rarely answered questions directly; instead he tugged at Dominic's sleeve, walked to the center of the chamber, and began to draw in the frost-dust on the floor with one careful finger. Dominic watched over his shoulder: a tower. A circle around the tower. Lines running from the circle outward, dozens of them, fanning across an imagined world like roots from a tree—and then, one by one, the lines going dark. Isandro drew each one going out with a small, deliberate stroke, and when only a single line remained—the one that ran toward the valley of Vell—he looked up at Dominic with eyes too old for his face.

"He shows you true," Vessan said quietly. "Once, every line burned. The covenant bound the magic of the Spire to the mortal realm—not as a leash, child, but as a promise. Magic would flow to the world, and the world would give back wonder, and neither side would ever again try to swallow the other. It held for nine hundred years." Her staff tapped the floor, once, like a heartbeat. "Then the last Keeper died, alone, in a village that had forgotten her name. And the covenant began to starve."

"The ward," Dominic said. His mouth felt dry. "Around Vell. That's why nothing magical can live there. Why my gift barely—"

"Your gift does not barely work in Vell," Vessan said. "It works *because* of Vell. The ward was not built to suppress magic, Dominic Marchetti. It was built to hide the last thread of the covenant while it slept. Your valley is not a prison. It is a cradle." She smiled, thin and sad. "And you—you were never supposed to be able to speak with beasts at all. But the cradle leaks, a little. It leaked into you. A boy born under a dying covenant's shadow, gifted with the one tongue the Spire understands best."

Dominic sat down. He didn't decide to; his legs simply informed him they were finished standing. Isandro immediately came and leaned against his side, warm and solid, and Dominic put an arm around him because it was the only thing in this impossible room that made sense.

"All right," he said. "All right. So the covenant is dying. So the boy is—was grown—to renew it. Yeva told us that much before—" He stopped. Yeva's name still caught in his throat like a fishbone. "Before Harrowgate. So bring the boy to your altar, say your words, light your candles. Why am I here? Why do you keep looking at me like I owe you money?"

Liss laughed—a sound like wind through dry reeds. Oren's bandaged arms crossed. Vessan did not laugh at all.

"Because we cannot use the key," she said. "A key turns. It does not choose. Isandro carries the covenant's seed, but a seed needs soil, and water, and a voice to call it awake. The binding must be spoken aloud—in both tongues. The tongue of the Spire, which Isandro knows. And the tongue of the mortal world, spoken by one who has lived in it, loved in it, and who can hear the old speech besides." She paused. "There is exactly one person alive who meets all three conditions. He is sitting on my floor, holding a mute child, smelling faintly of goat."

"That's the goat milk," Dominic said weakly. "For the porridge."

"Dominic." Vessan came closer, and her voice lost its formality, became almost kind. "To reforge the covenant, you must stand at the heart of the Spire and speak the binding aloud. All of it. Not a whisper, not a fragment. Your gift will pour through you and into the covenant, and the covenant will pour back—and when it is done, you will be joined to the Spire permanently. Your voice will carry to every beast within a hundred miles, always. Every creature that dreams will dream within earshot of you. You will feel the covenant's health in your bones the way you feel weather in an old scar. You will never again be merely a man in a quiet valley."

The silence after that was very long. Somewhere below them, the Spire groaned and settled, a sound like a ship at anchor.

Dominic looked down at Isandro. The boy was watching him with that terrible patience of his, the patience of someone who had already guessed the shape of this conversation and had come anyway.

"And if I refuse?" Dominic asked.

"Then the covenant dies," Vessan said simply. "Within a year, perhaps two. The ward around Vell fails completely—no more hiding, no more quiet. Kaelen Voss's armies march where they please, because there will be no awakened magic to break against them. The beasts of the world stay dumb and enslaved, and men like Voss muzzle them and chain them and point them at villages. The world becomes exactly what men like Voss want it to be: silent, obedient, and gray." She spread her small hands. "That is the arithmetic. I won't dress it up. Duty on one side. Your life on the other. Choose."

* * *

They gave him a room to think in, which was either courtesy or a trap, and Dominic honestly couldn't tell. It was round, with a window that looked east over the cloud sea, and a bed that was softer than any bed he'd ever owned, and he lay on it fully clothed and stared at the ceiling and did arithmetic.

That was the thing nobody warned you about duty, he thought. Everyone painted it in fire and trumpets. Nobody mentioned that it mostly felt like a farmer counting seed grain in a bad spring, deciding which fields got planted and which went fallow. *One life, freely given, against a world kept green.* Even a fool could work that sum. He had never claimed to be wise, but he wasn't a fool.

Except.

Except the arithmetic kept refusing to balance, because on the other side of the scale wasn't just *his* life. It was the specific life. Mornings in Vell with frost on the window and bread in the oven. The particular silence of the valley, which he had spent forty years believing was emptiness and now understood was a lullaby sung over a sleeping child. The goats, who were idiots, and whom he loved. Marta Two-Rivers, who pretended not to notice when he left extra cheese on her step, and who had lied to an inquisitor's face for him with a steadiness he would never forget. The small, warm, utterly ordinary existence he had built like a drystone wall—one stone at a time, no mortar, held together by gravity and habit.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest: the fear. Because he had heard what his voice could do on the mountain road, turning the hunters' own beasts against them, and it had felt like liberation and it had felt like horror, and both feelings had been correct. If he joined himself to the Spire, he would never again be able to close that door. Every night, forever, the dreams of animals washing over him. Every dying fox in every ditch within a hundred miles crying out where only he could hear. He was a man who had treasured quiet his whole life, and they were offering him a marriage to noise.

A soft weight settled onto the bed beside him. Serra, in her wolf-shape, though he knew better now than to think of it as her *real* shape—she was whatever shape she chose, and lately she chose wolf because, she said, beds were wasted on people who couldn't properly appreciate napping.

"You're doing the arithmetic," she said. "I can smell it. It smells like burnt porridge."

"Very funny."

"I'm hilarious. It's one of my many qualities." She rested her chin on his knee. "Say the sum out loud. It helps. Everything sounds smaller out loud."

"One life against the world," he said. "Easy."

"Mm. And?"

"And—" He laughed, without much humor in it. "And I'm grieving for a life I haven't even lost yet. That's pathetic, isn't it? Standing in the middle of the miracle, mourning my *goats*."

"It isn't the goats," Serra said, and her voice had gone gentle in the way it only went gentle for him and the boy. "You're mourning the man who gets to stay hidden. He's been your whole self for forty years. Of course you grieve him. Grieve him properly, then. Don't let anyone—including three ancient wardens and a war—tell you the grief is cowardice. It isn't. It's the price tag. Look at it straight on, pay it with your eyes open, and then go do the thing."

"When did you get wise?"

"I've always been wise. You were too busy being comfortable to notice." She nudged his hand with her nose. "Also, for the record: whatever you choose, the boy chooses with you. Have you asked him? Or are you two making decisions about his destiny over his head, the way everyone else always has?"

Dominic opened his mouth. Closed it. Serra's amber eyes gleamed with insufferable satisfaction.

"That," he said, "is why nobody likes wolves."

"Everyone likes wolves. They just don't admit it in company."

* * *

He found Isandro in the chamber below, the one with the broken mosaic floor, drawing again. The boy had covered yards of stone with chalk-lines Vessan had given him: towers, beasts, a stick figure with unruly hair that Dominic recognized, with a jolt, as himself. He sat down carefully on the cold floor beside him, joints protesting, and waited. Waiting was a language Isandro understood fluently.

After a while, the boy set down the chalk and took Dominic's hand. He turned the palm up, traced a line down the middle of it, and then pressed Dominic's fingers closed over the line, hiding it.

"Hiding," Dominic said softly. "Me. In the valley."

Isandro nodded. Then he opened Dominic's hand again, and with one finger drew the same line—but this time he continued it, past the wrist, up the arm, across the chest, and finally touched it, feather-light, to Dominic's heart. He looked up.

"Not hidden anymore," Dominic translated. "Part of everything." He exhaled slowly. "That's supposed to comfort me, is it?"

Isandro considered this with great seriousness. Then he shrugged, in the universal gesture of children everywhere meaning *it should, but adults are strange*, and climbed into Dominic's lap as if he were five instead of however old he was, and put his head against Dominic's chest, directly over the line he had drawn.

And Dominic understood, suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the boy wasn't comforting him. The boy was listening. Listening to the heartbeat of the one person in the world who had never once treated him as a key, a vessel, a weapon, or a choice—only as a child who liked porridge and hated boots.

"Well," Dominic said, and his voice cracked in the middle, and he decided not to care. "Well, then."

* * *

Vessan was waiting for him at the top of the stairs, alone, leaning on her staff. The other two wardens had melted away somewhere, or perhaps had simply stopped pretending to be in rooms.

"You've decided," she said.

"I've decided to ask a question first," Dominic said. "If I refuse—if I take the boy and run, find some corner of the world Voss never reaches—what happens to him? Truly. No diplomacy."

Vessan studied him for a long moment. "The covenant dies in him slowly," she said at last. "It won't kill him. But it will hollow him. He was made for this, Dominic. A purpose denied doesn't vanish; it rots. You would raise a sweet, silent boy who grows into a hollow man, wondering all his life why he was made and never told no one would let him be it." She tilted her head. "Whereas if you accept—he becomes heir to both worlds. Not a tool. A gardener. The first of a new keeping."

"And me?"

"You become the voice of the covenant. Loud, permanent, and yours." Something flickered in the old woman's face—regret, maybe, or memory. "I will not pretend it is a small thing. I watched the last Keeper carry that weight for six hundred years, and she carried it beautifully, and it cost her everything she might otherwise have been. That is the bargain. There is no version where you keep the quiet life *and* save the world. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something."

Dominic looked past her, up through the roofless chamber, at the held breath of snow and the stars beyond it. He thought about a farmhouse in Vell with frost on the windows. He thought about a boy's finger tracing a line to his heart. He thought about the arithmetic, and how it had finally, grudgingly, balanced—not because the cost had shrunk, but because he had finally counted everything on both sides, including the part where a world gone gray had no quiet valleys left in it anyway.

"Then I'll speak it," he said. "The binding. All of it." He swallowed. "But I want it noted, for the historical record, that I'm doing this under protest, that I want it written down somewhere that I complained the entire time, and that if your covenant gives me headaches, I reserve the right to be insufferable about it for eternity."

Vessan smiled, and for a moment she looked young. "Noted," she said. "Witnessed by the First Warden. You'll make a fine Keeper. Complaining is half the job."

Below them, far down the mountain, a horn sounded—once, twice, a third time—and the snow hanging in the open air above the chamber trembled, and began, at last, to fall.

Serra came up the stairs at a dead run, blood on her muzzle and her eyes wide.

"They're here," she said. "Voss is here."
