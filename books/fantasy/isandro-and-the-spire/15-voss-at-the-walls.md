## 15. Voss at the Walls

Serra smelled the army three days before she saw it.

That was the first cruelty of it. She had spent her whole life proud of her nose—the finest in her pack, her mother used to say, good enough to find a hare under new snow, a traitor's trail through old lies. And now that nose told her what was coming, and there was nothing she could do but stand on the Spire's broken eastern wall with the wind in her fur and count the days until it arrived.

Iron. Oil. Burnt hair. The sour stink of fear that had been left to soak into leather for so long it had become part of the leather. And underneath all of it, threaded through it like wire through meat: *beasts*. Dozens of them. Hundreds, maybe. Carried in cages, marched in chains, their misery so thick on the wind that Serra had to shake her head like a dog shaking off water just to think past it.

"You smell it too."

Dominic had come up beside her without a sound, which was a habit of his she'd stopped resenting somewhere around Harrowgate. He stood with his hands folded behind his back the way he did when he was trying very hard to look calm, which fooled no one, least of all her. Below them, Isandro sat on a fallen block of the wall, drawing in the dust with a stick. The boy drew the same thing over and over lately: a circle of small figures around one tall one, holding up something bright.

"I've been smelling it since the pass," Serra said. "I was hoping I was wrong."

"Are you ever?"

"Once. In the spring of my second year. I thought a beehive was a rock." She let a little breath out through her nose—not quite a laugh, but near enough that Dominic smiled, briefly, before the weight came back down on him. "It's a big host, Dominic. Bigger than the vanguard in the pass. And they're not marching like men who mean to besiege us. They're marching like men who mean to end something."

Dominic looked out at the mountains, where the morning haze still hid the road. "The wardens say we have four days."

"The wardens are optimistic," Serra said. "We have two."

* * *

She went down to the beasts' quarters that evening, because she always did now, because someone had to.

The Spire's lower vaults had once housed the guardian order's own companions—ward-beasts, messenger hawks, the great patient oxen who had hauled stone when the Spire was young. Now the vaults held refugees instead: creatures who had fled Voss's advance ahead of the human refugees, or been smuggled out by sympathizers, or simply run until running wasn't possible anymore. A blind old boarhound named Corrick who remembered every king's reign back forty years and complained about all of them. A family of marsh-otters who had taken over a dry cistern and defended it with the ferocity of creatures ten times their size. A young kestrel with a burned wing who perched on Serra's shoulder whenever she visited and pretended it was an accident.

They gathered when she came. They always gathered. Word traveled fast among the caged and the frightened, and word had spread through the Spire's stones that there was a wolf below who could speak for them—who could carry their voices up to the wardens and the man with the strange soft gift, and sometimes even get things done.

"What news?" Corrick asked, as he always asked, as though the answer might have improved overnight.

"They're coming," Serra said. "Two days. Maybe less."

A low sound moved through the vault—not quite a growl, not quite a moan. The otters went quiet in their cistern. The kestrel's talons tightened on Serra's shoulder, just slightly, just enough to feel.

"How many of ours?" asked a voice from the shadows of the far archway, and Serra's heart did the thing it always did when she heard that voice—dropped, and caught, and dropped again.

Maren stepped into the torchlight. She was a grey wolf like Serra, older, with a torn ear and a limp from a snare she'd carried for a winter, and she was Serra's mother's sister's daughter, which among wolves made her close enough to be almost a sister herself. She had come up the mountain with the last wave of refugees, half-starved, carrying news of the burning lowlands. She had also, before that, spent eight months in one of Voss's camps.

She never talked about the camp. But sometimes at night Serra heard her whimpering in her sleep, and the whimpers had words in them, and the words were names.

"Maren," Serra said gently. "You don't have to—"

"How many." It wasn't a question. Maren's eyes were flat and steady in the torchlight, the way eyes go when looking away hurts more than looking. "In his train. How many of our kind does he bring to these walls?"

Serra closed her own eyes and let her nose do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for three days. Iron and oil and fear—but under the fear, distinct now, familiar: the musk of northern grey wolves, her mother's line, her father's line. The deep-chested scent of hill bears. Cats, mountain and otherwise. Elk. Even, gods help them, a bull aurochs, an animal so old-blooded that its scent alone made the hairs along her spine lie down flat in ancient deference.

"Hundreds," she said. "Maybe more. Wolves—I think two packs' worth, maybe three. Bears. Cats. Things I can't name." She opened her eyes. "And they're muzzled, Maren. All of them. The iron in the wind isn't just chain-iron. It's bit-iron. Gag-iron. The kind they fit so a beast can't make a sound."

"Because sound is how we fight," Maren said dully. "Everyone knows that. Take the voice, take the war-cry, take the howl that calls the pack together." Her lip lifted off her teeth, not quite a snarl, more like grief wearing a snarl's clothing. "He learned that from us, you know. From the ones who broke first. Myself included."

"You didn't break—"

"I told them where the ford was," Maren said, flat as slate. "Eight months, Serra. Everyone tells them something eventually. That's how it works. That's how it's *designed* to work." She turned toward the dark of the archway. "When they come—and they will come—I want to be on the wall. Not hiding in a vault. On the wall, where they can see me standing free. Let them remember there's another way to be."

She limped off into the shadows before Serra could answer, and Serra stood a long moment with the kestrel trembling on her shoulder, and understood that she had just been given both a request and a warning: *this is what losing looks like. Remember it.*

* * *

The army arrived at midday on the second day, and it arrived the way a glacier arrives—slowly, inevitably, filling the valley mouth below the Spire's crag from edge to edge.

Serra stood on the eastern wall between Dominic and the eldest warden, a creature called Halvard who might have been a man once and was now mostly weathered oak and patience. Isandro stood at Dominic's side, holding the hem of his coat in one fist, the way he'd done since the pass. Since the pulse. The boy hadn't drawn a single picture since then. He just held on, and watched, and Serra wished with everything in her that she could unmake whatever he'd seen inside himself that day.

Below, the host assembled with terrible discipline. Rank after rank of infantry in Kaelen Voss's grey-and-black. Siege engines on ox-drawn sledges—real oxen, unchained, which meant they were either collaborators or hostages; Serra couldn't tell and hated not knowing. And behind the ranks, in long fenced corridors of wagons and cage-carts stretching back down the valley road:

The beasts.

Even at this distance she could see the muzzles glinting. Great cats pacing their carts in tight, mad circles because a circle was the only shape a chained life allowed. Bears swaying side to side, the motion of animals whose minds had gone somewhere else and sent their bodies a note saying *carry on without us*. And the wolves—her wolves, her mother's people—lying flat and silent in their cages, not sleeping, just *flat*, the posture of creatures who had learned that being noticed was the most dangerous thing in the world.

One of them raised her head as the wind shifted. A young female, grey-flanked, white-throated. Serra knew that throat-mark. She had licked that throat-mark clean when its owner was three weeks old and blind as a mole.

"*Rill*," she breathed, and the name tore going out of her.

Beside her, Dominic's hand found the fur between her shoulders and rested there. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. That was the thing about the soft-spoken man from the silent valley: he had learned, somewhere in his quiet life, exactly when silence was the kindest language.

Then a horn sounded from the valley floor—one long note, clean and unhurried, the horn of parley rather than assault—and a single rider came forward under a banner of truce.

Voss himself rode out to offer terms. Serra hadn't expected that. Usurpers, in her experience, sent heralds. But there he was: a broad man in plain black armor with no ornament at all, which was somehow worse than ornament, riding a black horse whose eyes were wrong—dulled, drugged, or worse. He halted well outside bow-range, and his voice carried up the crag unnaturally clearly, thrown by some device or some stolen scrap of ward-work.

"People of the Spire!" he called. "Wardens! And whatever else is listening up there—I know you listen. I know better than anyone what listens."

Halvard made a sound like a tree settling.

"My quarrel is not with you," Voss went on. "My quarrel is with magic itself—with the old tyranny that kept mankind kneeling to towers and wonders while it starved. I have burned that tyranny out of every province from here to the sea. One thing remains. One *child* remains." He paused, letting the silence work. "Give me the vessel, and I will withdraw. Every soldier, every engine, every cage. And more: the valley of Vell, south of the mountains, will be spared. Its fields, its villages, its people. Written into the peace, sealed before witnesses. No soldier of mine will cross its borders so long as I live."

Serra felt Dominic go rigid beside her. Because it was a real offer—that was the horror of it. Voss was many things, but he had built an empire on keeping his word precisely enough to be trusted by the frightened. He would spare Vell. He would spare it forever. All it would cost was the boy at Dominic's side, and everything the boy was made to open, and every beast in every cage from here to the sea, and—

"He knows," Serra said quietly. "He knows where Dominic comes from. He's offering the valley because he knows it's the one coin that spends."

Down on the wall's lower step, Isandro had gone very still. Then, slowly, he tugged Dominic's coat hem. Once. Twice. Dominic knelt, and the boy leaned against him, and pressed his face into his shoulder, and shook his head. Over and over. A small, fierce, mute *no*, the first full-bodied motion he'd made since the pass.

Dominic wrapped both arms around him and looked up at Halvard. "The answer is no," he said. His voice didn't shake. Serra was proud of him for that, and terrified for him because of it. "Tell him no. Tell him—" and here he did something reckless and entirely himself, leaning out over the wall so his voice, unaided, mortal, carried down the crag— "*tell him the child is not a vessel. He's a person. And he's mine.*"

Far below, Kaelen Voss sat motionless on his dead-eyed horse for a long moment. Then he turned, unhurried, and rode back to his lines, and the truce-horn sounded retreat, and the host began—methodically, expertly—to prepare for the morning's work.

* * *

That night the campfires below burned like a fallen sky, and Serra walked the walls alone.

She checked the watch-posts. She spoke to the refugee beasts in the vaults, telling them what she could, lying about what she must. She found Maren already sitting at the eastern battlements, facing the fires, waiting for morning the way soldiers wait—still and hungry-eyed.

And near midnight, she found Rill.

Not Rill herself—Rill was still caged in the valley, Serra could smell her, a mile of cold air away. But Rill's scent had caught on the night wind and come up the mountain, and Serra stood at the wall's edge with her nose full of her sister's-pup's fear and her heart full of something larger and angrier than fear.

They were going to loose those beasts tomorrow. She knew it the way she knew weather. Voss had not dragged hundreds of muzzled creatures up a mountain road to admire them. Tomorrow, when the siege engines failed or before they began, the cages would open and the tortured things inside would be driven up this crag with fire and pain, and they would tear at these walls with the last strength in their bodies, and they would die here, thousands of miles from anywhere they'd ever run free, and their last sight would be the faces of their own kind looking down at them from the battlements.

Unless.

Serra looked down at the fires. At the cage-lines, dark and orderly. At the small figures of handlers walking the rows with their iron prods and their torches.

*I know your voices,* she thought at them, at all of them, at Rill and the bears and the swaying cats and the broken-eyed aurochs. *I know what they took from you. I know what they made you forget. But I remember it for you. I remember it all.*

Behind her, footsteps—soft boots on old stone. Dominic came to stand beside her, wrapping his cloak tighter against the wind, and for a while neither of them spoke. Below, a wolf in the cage-lines began, very softly, to howl—a thin, muzzled, strangled sound, barely a howl at all, more the ghost of one. Others took it up. The handlers moved quickly, prods flashing, and the sound died row by row.

But for a few heartbeats it had risen, and Serra had heard, woven through it, unmistakable, the cadence of her mother's line.

"Dominic," she said. "Tomorrow, when they open those cages—someone has to be there to meet them. Not swords. Not wards. Someone who can tell them, in the only language they have left, that they aren't weapons." She turned to look at him, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Someone has to give them back their names."

Dominic was quiet a moment. Down in the vaults below them, faintly, Corrick the boarhound was complaining about something to the otters, and the kestrel was making her small fierce dreaming sounds, and the whole wounded world of the Spire breathed in its sleep.

"There's a chamber at the heart of this place," Dominic said at last, slowly, as if the words cost him something to find. "The wardens keep telling me it's waiting for a voice." He looked out at the fires, at the cages, at the thin ghost-howls rising and dying in the dark. "I think I finally understand what it's for."

Far below, at the center of the enemy camp, a great pavilion went suddenly, completely dark—and every handler on the cage-lines turned, all at once, to look at it, and Serra felt the night change its mind about something.
