## 16. The Night of Two Choices

The Spire hummed at night.

Dominic had noticed it the first evening, lying awake in a cell that had been made gently into a guest room—wardens were not much for hospitality, but they tried—and heard it through the stone: a low, patient sound, like a hive thinking, like a hearth that had learned to breathe. In Vell there had been silence. Twenty years of silence, thick as wool packed in his ears. He had thought he would miss it here, and some small traitorous part of him did. But most of him had decided, somewhere on the mountain road with Isandro's hand pressed over his heart, that he could learn to live inside the hum.

Tonight the hum was different. Faster. The whole tower felt like a held breath.

*Tomorrow,* Dominic thought, and then refused to think it, which of course meant he thought of nothing else.

He climbed the spiral stair to the boy's room with a candle stub in one hand and a satchel in the other, and the satchel was the problem, wasn't it? It was the whole problem, sitting in his hand like a confession. He'd packed it an hour ago, down in his own room, moving quietly the way he'd moved all his life—quietly, competently, invisibly. Dried meat. A water skin. The good wool cloak, the one Yeva had bought him in Harrowgate back when Harrowgate still had shops and Yeva still had— no. He wasn't going to think about Yeva either.

Bread for three days. Cheese wrapped in waxed cloth. A flint.

And a map, drawn from memory by one of the wardens—a route south and west, around the Pass of Teeth entirely, down into country so broken and wild that even Voss's armies wouldn't bother. There was a valley past the Graywater fens. Warden Ondrel had described it: deep, green, forgotten. No roads. No wards worth mentioning. No covenant, no Spire, no war.

Just quiet. Just the two of them.

Dominic stood outside Isandro's door and hated himself with a thoroughness that would have impressed the inquisitors.

Because here was the arithmetic, the cold little sum he'd been carrying since the wardens told him what renewal required: if he ran, Voss took the Spire. If Voss took the Spire, the covenant died unwitnessed, and every beast within a hundred miles stayed muzzled and mad, and the free cities fell one by one like teeth, and eventually—maybe not this year, maybe not in ten—but eventually someone came looking for the last beast-speaker in the world, and they would find him in his green forgotten valley, because there was no such thing as forgotten anymore. Not for men like Kaelen Voss.

And if he stayed—

If he stayed, he spoke the binding, and he was never again merely a man. That was how Warden Ilseth had put it, her old voice dry as seed husks: *You would never again be merely a man, Dominic Marchetti.* As though being merely a man were a small thing. As though he hadn't spent forty years building that man plank by plank—the cottage, the garden, the goat who tolerated him, the long winters with a book and a fire and nobody asking anything of him at all.

He had loved that man. He was mourning him already, standing in this hallway, and the funeral hadn't even happened yet.

"He'd understand," Dominic said to the candle stub, very quietly. "That's the worst of it. He'd understand."

The candle didn't answer. Candles never did; that was rather the point of them.

He pushed open the door.

* * *

Isandro slept the way he always slept now—curled small, facing the wall, one hand open on the blanket as though offering something to whoever might come. Since the pass, since the blizzard and the terrible white pulse of light that had come out of the boy and saved them all, he had been quieter than silence itself. He hadn't drawn. That was the thing that frightened Dominic most, more than the hollow eyes, more than the way the boy flinched from the Spire's hum: Isandro, who had spoken in pictures since before Dominic ever knew him, had stopped speaking altogether.

Three days of no drawings. Three days of a child folded up like a letter no one dared open.

Dominic set the satchel down just inside the door—he told himself it was only so he wouldn't forget it, and knew he was lying—and crossed to the bed. He sat on its edge, careful of the boards, and looked at the small shape of him. Seven years old, maybe eight. Thin as a winter branch. This was the living key to the covenant between realms, grown from the Spire's last magic, hunted by an usurper and guarded by a goat-farmer from Vell, and right now he was just a boy with chapped lips and a cowlick that refused all management.

"I had a plan," Dominic whispered. "You should know that. In case you wake up and I'm a coward. I had a whole plan. There's a valley—Ondrel says it's green. You'd like it. We'd get goats."

Isandro breathed slowly. In, out. The hum of the Spire moved through the floor and up through the bed and into the small rising fall of his chest, as though the tower were breathing with him.

"We'd get goats," Dominic said again, and his voice cracked on the word *goats*, which was humiliating, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes and sat there in the dark doing the thing he had gotten very good at lately, which was falling apart silently.

Below, far below, he could hear the wardens moving. Preparations. Tomorrow—or today, now; the candle said well past midnight—Voss's host would come up the mountain road, and Serra was down there somewhere among the freed beasts, teaching them revolt, and Dominic had seen her face when she spoke of the muzzled ones, her kin, and he had understood that Serra had already chosen her death's neighborhood. She'd simply picked the street.

And him. Him, with his satchel and his cheese.

"Pathetic," he told himself. "Genuinely pathetic. O. M.—no. None of that." He'd taken to scolding himself under his breath these days; it kept the fear from pooling. "You talked your way past a patrol of inquisitors using nothing but a lie about a dead aunt and a suspicious amount of confidence. You turned three beast-hunters' own hounds against them in under a minute. You are, objectively, a dangerous man, Dominic Marchetti, and you are contemplating running away with a child and a wheel of cheese."

The cheese really was excellent, though. Harrowgate smoked it with something—juniper, maybe. He'd remember that much of the city fondly.

He reached out and tucked the blanket higher over Isandro's shoulder, and that was when he saw the paper.

It lay on the floor beside the bed, half under it, face-down. Charcoal, from the look of the edge peeking out—one of the soft sticks the wardens had given the boy, back when the boy still drew. Dominic's heart did something complicated. He should leave it. It was private; the boy's drawings had always been private, even the ones that had saved their lives, even the tower and the woman's hands and the fire. He had never once looked at one uninvited.

But the paper was angled toward the door. Toward the door, where anyone entering would see it first. And Isandro did nothing without meaning, not anymore, not ever—the mute child who communicated in charcoal had the most deliberate soul Dominic had ever met.

He picked it up.

* * *

At first he couldn't make sense of it, and then he could, and then he wished for a moment that he couldn't, because it went through him like cold water through cloth.

A figure stood at the center of the drawing. Tall—not tall the way a man is tall, but tall the way a tree is tall, the way a spire is tall, rooted and reaching at once. The figure stood in a chamber Dominic recognized with a lurch: the covenant chamber, the heart of the Spire, which the wardens had shown them only yesterday, all worn stone and sleeping light. Around the figure's feet, radiating outward like ripples, lines of power spread across the whole page—out the chamber doors, down through the mountain, across rivers and valleys rendered in quick sure strokes, into a sky full of stars that the figure seemed to be holding up. Both arms raised. Holding up the sky.

The face was left blank.

But the hands—Dominic knew those hands. He had wrung those hands over accounts and garden rows and a stubborn goat named Petunia for twenty years. The charcoal had caught them exactly: broad-palmed, ink-stained at the knuckles, one scarred thumb from the winter he'd been foolish with an axe.

In the corner, small, almost hidden, was a second figure. A child, standing behind the tall one's legs, one hand gripping the fabric of a coat. Watching. And above the child's head, in that ancient script older than any kingdom—the script Isandro had written on Dominic's doorstep the night the snowstorm brought him—two words.

Dominic couldn't read them. He had never been able to read them. But he had heard them, once, faintly, in the covenant chamber when the wardens sang, and he knew what they meant the way you know the meaning of a bell: *stay. stay.*

"Oh," Dominic said. His voice came out very small. "Oh, you wretched, wonderful boy."

"You're supposed to be asleep," said a voice from the bed.

Dominic nearly ascended to a plane of existence the wardens hadn't mentioned. He spun, and Isandro was sitting up, watching him with dark steady eyes—not hollow tonight. Awake in every sense. How long had he been sitting there? Long enough. Long enough to watch a grown man whisper to a candle about cheese.

"You were awake the entire time," Dominic accused.

Isandro's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. Since the pass, the smiles had gone somewhere, and everyone pretended not to grieve them. But the twitch was something. The twitch was a door left open a hand's width.

He held out his hand for the drawing, and Dominic gave it back, and instead of putting it away the boy smoothed it flat on the blanket and looked up at him with an expression of terrible, gentle patience—the expression of someone explaining rain to a fish.

"I know," Dominic said. "I know what it means. I'm not stupid, Isandro, whatever my recent behavior suggests." He sat down on the edge of the bed again, heavily, the way tired men sit. "I packed a bag. Did you see it? Of course you saw it. You see everything. There's a valley, and it's green, and there are no wars in it, and I thought—I thought we could just—" He stopped. Started again. "I spent my whole life being safe. Do you understand that? Safe is a skill. I'm very good at it. And everything in me is screaming that we should run, because running has worked before, running got us out of Vell, out of Harrowgate, over the pass—"

Isandro touched his arm.

Just that. Small fingers on his sleeve, the way he'd done on the mountain road, the gesture that meant, in the boy's private language, *listen*. And then he pointed—at the drawing, at the blank-faced figure holding up the sky—and then at Dominic's chest, and then he did something he had not done in three days.

He drew.

There was charcoal in the boy's pocket—of course there was; the wardens' gifts had survived even the silence—and he pulled it out and took a fresh sheet from beneath the mattress, where a small hoard of them lived, and he bent over it with the fierce concentration Dominic remembered from that first morning in Vell, snow melting in his hair, drawing words no human mouth should know.

He drew quickly, roughly, without art: a stick figure running, satchel and all, toward a little house. And around the little house, closing in like weather, he drew soldiers. Many soldiers. Their spears were scribbled so hard the charcoal tore the paper in two places.

Then a second picture, beneath it: the same stick figure, standing still. Standing in the tower. Arms raised. And around this figure the darkness was still there, the war was still there, but it stopped at a line—a bright line, a boundary—and beyond the line, small and simple and unmistakable, Isandro drew a cottage. A garden. A goat with horns like question marks.

Petunia. He'd drawn Petunia.

"The same either way," Dominic said slowly, understanding arriving like dawn, ugly and beautiful at once. "The war comes either way. Running doesn't save the valley. Running just—" His throat tightened. "Running just makes sure I'm not there when it arrives."

Isandro nodded once. Then he tapped the second figure's chest, and looked up, and his eyes were wet but level, and he pointed at the blank face of the tall one in the first drawing, and then—gently, deliberately—he touched Dominic's cheek. Filling in the face. Naming him.

"That's not fair," Dominic whispered. "I'm supposed to protect you. That was the arrangement. I found you on my doorstep; the arrangement was goats and porridge and learning your letters, not—" He laughed, or something near it, wrecked and warm. "Not holding up the sky."

Isandro tilted his head. Then, with the gravity of a magistrate, he picked up the charcoal one more time and wrote beneath the second picture, in the old tongue, the letters flowing off his small hand like water finding its course:

Two words. The same two words from the corner of the first drawing. *Stay. Stay.*

And then, after a pause, a third word. New. The charcoal hesitated over it, and the boy's hand shook slightly, and he wrote it anyway, and Dominic felt the Spire's hum deepen around them as though the tower itself leaned in to read.

Dominic couldn't translate it. But he didn't need to. Some words arrive in the body before the mind. This one landed in his chest like a coal from a good fire, and he knew—it was not a command, it was not a plea—it was a gift. It was the boy handing him the thing he'd been too afraid to name: not duty. Not destiny. Permission. *You may choose this. I will love you whichever way you choose, and I am asking you to choose this.*

"When did you get wise?" Dominic asked, and his voice failed entirely on the last word, and he gave up on words altogether and pulled the boy into his arms, and Isandro came, stiff at first and then not, small fists gripping his coat the way the drawn child gripped the drawn coat, and they stayed like that while the Spire hummed around them like a hive deciding, like a hearth breathing, like a held breath almost ready to let go.

After a long time, Isandro pulled back and pointed, firmly, at the satchel by the door.

"Yes, yes." Dominic wiped his face with his sleeve, sniffling with tremendous dignity. "I'll unpack it. Though I'll have you know the cheese stays. That cheese has earned its place in history."

Isandro's mouth twitched again—that door, opening wider—and then, wonder of wonders, the boy took the ruined first drawing, the running man, and with great ceremony folded it into quarters and tore it cleanly in half. He handed the pieces to Dominic solemnly, as if returning borrowed courage no longer needed.

Dominic took them. "We burn it in the morning," he said. "With ceremony. And then—" He looked at the second drawing, the standing man, the bright line, the cottage beyond, the future with a goat in it. "And then we hold up the sky. Apparently. Me. With these hands." He flexed his ink-stained fingers and laughed at them, at all of it, at the sheer absurd tenderness of the universe. "Warden Ilseth is going to be insufferable. She'll say she knew. Wardens always say they knew."

Isandro patted his arm twice, briskly, the universal gesture of *there, there*, and Dominic marveled—as he marveled every day, as he would marvel until he died—that this mute scrap of a child had walked out of a burning tower and across a kingdom and through a blizzard to teach a cowardly goat-farmer how to be brave.

Outside, far below on the mountain road, the wind was changing. Dominic felt it before he heard it—a pressure in the hum, a tightening, the tower's held breath going deeper. Somewhere down in the dark, torches were being lit. Somewhere down in the dark, Serra stood among her kin, and muzzles waited to be struck off, and Kaelen Voss's host was coming up the mountain like a tide with a grudge.

Dawn was perhaps four hours away.

"Well," Dominic said, standing, rolling up his sleeves out of pure nervous habit, "no time like the present to learn how a covenant works. Come along, then. If I'm to hold up the sky, I want to rehearse."

Isandro slid off the bed, took the second drawing with both hands, and followed him to the door—and paused there, looking back at the torn pieces of the first, and then up at Dominic with an expression that needed no translation at all.

Together, they went down into the humming heart of the Spire, where the wardens were waiting, and where the first grey light of the last morning was already gathering at the windows like an audience taking its seats.
