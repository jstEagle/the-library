## 17. The Breaking of the Host

The dawn came up the color of an old bruise, and Serra smelled the army before she saw it.

Iron. Oil. The sour reek of fear-sweat from a thousand men who did not want to be there. And beneath it all, threaded through everything like rot through a carcass—the stench of the muzzles. Charmed leather and cold iron, soaked in whatever foul tincture Voss's handlers used to keep a beast's voice drowned in its own throat.

She stood on the Spire's outer wall with her claws hooked into the ancient stone and let the wind carry the smell to her, and she counted.

Not the soldiers. She could not have counted the soldiers; they moved like a gray tide across the pass road, banner after banner, pikes glinting like frost that had decided to march. She counted the *beasts*.

Bears in yokes of iron, their heads bowed under chains thick as her foreleg. Great cats—she saw at least six, and her heart stumbled on the seventh shape behind them, lean and tawny and walking with its ears flat. Wyrm-hounds bred in the northern crags, forty of them at least, slavering against their muzzles. And at the rear, half-hidden by the supply wagons, a shape so large it took her a moment to understand what she was seeing: a hill-troll, or what had once been one, its eyes bandaged shut because a troll that could see was a troll that might remember it had a name.

"They are coming," said Warden Ondrel beside her, in the tone of a man reporting weather.

"Yes," Serra said.

"You can speak to them."

"I can try." She flexed her claws out of the stone, leaving five small holes. "Muzzles don't stop the voice, warden. They stop the answer. That's the cruelty of them. You can shout into a muzzle all day and hear nothing back, until you start to believe you were never shouting at anything at all." She looked east, toward the gray tide. "Some of them have been muzzled since they were cubs. Some of them may no longer remember how to listen."

"And if they do?"

Serra thought of her sister's face. She had seen it last night, in the moonlight, through the spyglass Yeva Corvane had left behind—a face thinner than memory, scarred along the jaw, walking in the third rank of the beast-line with her head down and her steps measured like a creature that had learned hope was a wound.

"Then," Serra said, "we remind them."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, fit inside a walnut shell.

Warden Ondrel held the outer gate with his two fellow wardens and whatever remained of Harrowgate's refugee militia—two hundred souls, most of them holding spears for the first time in their lives, several of them holding spears upside down until someone gently corrected them. The gate itself was old covenant-work, stone grown rather than cut, and while the covenant slept it would hold about as well as a promise made by a politician.

Which left the beasts.

"Remember," Serra told them, gathered in the courtyard below the wall: the wolves of her own scattered pack, the mountain cats who had come down when the covenant began to wake, three bears of dubious temperament, and a very old raven named Corvus who insisted he had been advising kings since before the Spire was built and whom nobody had the heart to argue with. "You do not fight their soldiers. Soldiers are meat and metal and they will kill you for the pleasure of their officers' approval. You fight the *handlers*. Break the yokes. Tear off the muzzles. Every beast you free is a blade turned around in the enemy's hand."

A young wolf named Ferric—her sister's son, though neither of them had known it until this winter—shifted his weight. "And if they won't be freed? If the muzzle has done its work?"

"Then get clear and let the wardens deal with them," Serra said. "We are not here to win a battle, Ferric. We are here to buy time. There is a man inside that tower with a voice older than kingdoms, and when he speaks, none of this—" she swept her gaze across the courtyard, the wall, the gray tide beyond it, "—will matter anymore. We only need to still be alive when it stops mattering."

Corvus croaked from the battlement above. "In my experience," he said, "that is the entire art of war. Everything else is decoration."

"Thank you, Corvus."

"You're welcome. I also have thoughts on supply lines."

"Not now, Corvus."

* * *

They came at midmorning, under a sky the color of dirty wool, and the first hour was nothing but noise.

Serra had fought before—border skirmishes, a bad winter with the lowland packs, one memorable evening involving a tax collector and a very territorial badger—but she had never stood in a *battle*, and she learned quickly that a battle is mostly sound. Drums. Horns. The shriek of the wyrm-hounds being whipped forward. The wet crunch of the first volley of ballista bolts hitting the outer wall, one of which passed close enough to part the fur along her spine.

Then the bear-yokes came up the road, and it stopped being noise and became arithmetic.

Six great bears, chained shoulder to shoulder, driven toward the gate with goads of white iron. Behind them the handlers in their gray hoods, and behind *them* the pikelines, and above it all the banners of Kaelen Voss snapping in the wind like the wings of something that fed on carrion.

Serra went over the wall without waiting for the order.

Falling is a strange thing when you are built for it. She hit the churned mud of the road at full run, and the world narrowed to scent and motion, and then she was among them—under the first bear's belly, past a handler whose goad whistled down and struck only mud—and she did the thing she had been born knowing how to do, the thing the valley of Vell had spent twenty years trying to convince her she could not do.

She spoke.

*"Brother!"* she cried, in the deep speech, the old speech, the speech that lives below language in the place where hearts beat. *"Brother, your name is Ulmar and you were born under the larches of the Cold Fangs and your mother sang to you and the muzzle LIES!"*

The bear stopped.

It was a small thing, really—one enormous shaggy body halting in the middle of a charge, chains snapping taut, handlers screaming. But a charge is a river, and a river that stops becomes a flood, and the pikeline behind it broke apart like a dropped bundle of kindling.

Ulmar turned his head. His eyes above the muzzle were wild and white-rimmed, and for one terrible heartbeat Serra thought the iron had won, that there was nothing left in there but pain and obedience.

Then the bear's nostrils flared. He breathed her in—wolf, free wolf, wolf smelling of pine smoke and the Spire's waking stone and, faintly, of a quiet valley far away where magic had never quite managed to die.

*"Lies,"* Ulmar rumbled, and his voice came out cracked and rusted, a hinge unused for years. *"It lies. It lies. It LIES—"*

He reared. The yoke screamed. The handler nearest him had time to look up, and Serra gave the man credit for one thing only: he ran fast enough that the falling bear missed him entirely, which was more mercy than he deserved.

After that, the road came apart.

* * *

Here is what Serra remembered afterward, in fragments, the way one remembers a storm:

Ferric flowing through the pikelines like water through a broken fence, going for the wyrm-hounds' lead-lines, cutting them loose one by one and shouting their crag-names until they shook themselves like dogs coming out of a river and turned, bewildered, on the men who had held the whips.

The tawny cat from the seventh position—she found it later, crouched over two dead handlers, its muzzle half torn away, and when she called the old name of its pride it looked at her with eyes like coins and said, in a whisper of a voice, *"Is it morning? Is it actually morning?"* and wept, because cats weep, whatever men believe.

Corvus, wheeling above the chaos, shrieking tactical advice to nobody in particular ("Left! Your LEFT, you magnificent idiots, the flank, the FLANK—") and occasionally dive-bombing an officer's hat with unerring malice.

And her sister.

Ryn. Third rank of the beast-line, head down, steps measured. When the line broke, Ryn did not break with it. She stood in the mud with her muzzle gleaming wet in the gray light, and the two handlers beside her raised their goads, and Serra crossed thirty yards of battlefield in four heartbeats and hit the first handler like a landslide.

What happened next she did not do neatly. She was not proud of it and she was not ashamed of it either; some things simply are. When it was done she stood panting over the bodies, and Ryn stood beside her, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, because there is no word in any speech for ten years of muzzle-darkness ending in blood and mud on a field of corpses.

Then Ryn said, *"Your left ear. Someone bit it."*

*"A long time ago,"* Serra said. *"I'll tell you the story when we're old."*

*"Are we going to be old?"*

Serra looked up at the wall—at the outer gate, where Warden Ondrel stood like a piece of the mountain itself, stone-light crawling along his arms as he turned aside another volley; at the courtyard behind, where the militia was holding, barely, badly, bravely; at the Spire rising above them all, its living half green-gold in the dead light, its ruined half open to the sky like a broken tooth.

*"Ask me again tonight,"* she said.

* * *

By noon they had done it. By noon it was, impossibly, working.

The beast-line had dissolved—not defeated but *converted*, rank after rank shaking off its chains as word ran down the road faster than any officer could countermand it, a contagion of names, each freed animal calling out to the next, *"your name, your NAME, I know your name"*—and Voss's infantry, trained to fight enemies in front of them, found themselves fighting their own siege-train from behind while wolves and bears and one extremely enthusiastic hill-troll (the bandages had come off; the troll's name, it turned out, was Grum, and Grum had opinions) tore through the baggage lines.

On the wall, the militia cheered. Somewhere a man was singing. Corvus landed on Serra's shoulder, bleeding slightly from one wing, insufferably pleased.

"I told you," he said. "Decoration."

"We haven't won, Corvus."

"No," he agreed cheerfully. "But we're winning *beautifully*, which is nearly as good and considerably rarer."

That was when Serra felt it.

At first she thought it was the ground trembling—Grum was throwing a supply wagon, and Grum threw things the way avalanches happen. But the tremble kept on, steady, rhythmic, wrong. And beneath the drumbeat of it she heard horns, but not the army's horns. Deeper. Lower. Coming from the south side of the pass, where nobody was supposed to be, because the south side of the pass was a cliff.

"The cliff," she said aloud, stupidly, to no one.

"The cliff," Corvus agreed, and his cheer went out like a snuffed candle.

They came over the cliff because of course they came over the cliff. Later she learned the details—that Voss had spent three months building switchback roads on the far side under cover of the blizzard, that the vanguard had climbed by night with ropes and silence, that the whole southern approach had been a lie told with earthworks and patience. What she knew in the moment was simpler: the gray tide had a second shore, and it was pouring down the south slope in column after disciplined column, fresh, unbloodied, and moving straight for the inner ring.

Straight for the gate that no longer had enough defenders to hold it.

* * *

She tried to hold it anyway.

What followed was not beautiful. Beautiful had been the morning. This was the afternoon, and the afternoon was arithmetic of a crueler kind: two hundred militia against four thousand, one warden against a hundred, and every yard of the inner ring bought with someone's blood. Serra fought at Ondrel's side on the south wall and watched the old warden take a ballista bolt through the shoulder and keep standing, watched him turn aside a ram-blow that should have folded the gate like paper, watched him smile—actually *smile*—as the stone-light guttered in his arms.

"Warden!"

"Ah, the wolf." He sounded almost fond. "You've made a marvelous mess of their morning, you know. Voss will write angry letters about you."

"The gate, warden. How long?"

Ondrel considered. Stone dust drifted off him like snow.

"Longer than we have," he said pleasantly. "Shorter than forever. It balances out."

The gate held for another hour. It held while Serra dragged wounded militiamen out of the killing ground, while Ferric took a spear meant for her and lived, while Corvus directed traffic overhead with the serenity of a general who had personally outlived every other general he'd ever advised. It held while the sun crawled westward and the courtyard filled with the wounded and the dead and the merely terrified.

And then, in the way of all gates everywhere, it did not hold.

The sound of it going was smaller than she expected—a groan, a crack, a sigh of old covenant-stone giving up an argument it had been having with itself for a thousand years. The inner ring opened. The gray tide poured through.

"To the tower!" Ondrel's voice, cracked now, human now. "Fall back to the tower! Buy the stairs! BUY THE STAIRS!"

They bought the stairs. Serra never fully remembered how. She remembered the great doors of the Spire's base, and the narrow stair winding up into the living dark, and the freed beasts forming a wall of fur and fury at the bottom of it—bears and cats and wyrm-hounds and Grum, who filled the entire doorway and required no further strategy whatsoever. She remembered Ryn at her shoulder. She remembered thinking, with a clarity that frightened her more than any army: *every minute costs us someone, and we are spending people like coins, and the purse is nearly empty.*

And she remembered looking up the stair, into the dark where the covenant chamber waited, where a soft-spoken man from a quiet valley was climbing with a silent child toward a choice that had already cost the world too much.

The stair was long. The army was patient. And somewhere above them, faint through the bones of the tower, Serra heard—or dreamed she heard—a voice beginning to gather itself, the way thunder gathers, the way a held breath gathers before it is finally, finally spoken.

"Hold," she told the beasts at the door, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Hold the door. Whatever comes up those stairs—hold."

Something vast stirred in the dark below the tower's roots, old and slow and listening.

And the first hammer blow fell against the Spire's great doors.
