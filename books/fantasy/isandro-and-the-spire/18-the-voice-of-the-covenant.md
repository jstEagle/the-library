## 18. The Voice of the Covenant

The chamber at the heart of the Spire had no door worth the name.

Dominic understood that now, standing in its center with Isandro's small hand wrapped around two of his fingers, and he wondered why it had never struck him before. Of course there was no true door. A covenant was not a vault to be locked. It was a promise, and promises were made in the open, spoken aloud, witnessed. The great archway leading into this room was tall enough for a giant and wide enough for an army, hung with doors of black iron that were already shuddering in their frames like a beast testing a fence.

Beyond them, muffled by iron and stone, came the sound of Kaelen Voss's war.

Boots. Hundreds of boots, the rhythm of them deliberate, unhurried—the tread of men who knew the gate had fallen and were savoring the walk. Beneath that, worse: the groan of harness leather, the clink of chain, and beneath *that*, something that made Dominic's chest ache even through the wardens' last defenses. The beasts. The captured ones, the muzzled ones, dragged up the mountain behind the army like siege engines with hearts. He could feel them through the floor, faint as voices heard through a wall—*cold, cold, we are so cold, why does our own blood not answer us*—

"Stop listening," Serra said. She stood at his shoulder, gray fur stiff with frost and blood that was mostly not her own. Her flank trembled; she had been fighting since the outer ring fell, running on nothing but will and old stubbornness. "You'll want to run to them. You can't. Not yet."

"I know."

"You don't. You're a valley man. You hear something hurting and you go to it with a bucket and your bare hands." Her amber eyes flicked to the shuddering doors, then back to him. "That's the man I chose to follow. But if you open that mouth for anything but the binding before Voss is dealt with, everything Yeva burned for, everything the wardens bled to hold—"

The iron boomed. Dust sifted from the lintel. Somewhere beyond, a voice—Voss's voice, smooth as oiled mail—rose above the din, pitched to carry.

*"Dominic Marchetti."*

Dominic's stomach turned over. He had heard his name spoken a hundred ways in these last weeks—inquisitors' snarls, scholars' whispers, Serra's low growl—but never like this. Never almost gently.

*"I know you can hear me. You are, I'm told, a man who listens."* A pause, and the smile in it was audible. *"So here is my last offer, and it is better than you deserve. Walk out. Give me the boy. Your valley keeps its snow and its silence. Your wolves keep their skins. You may even keep your little gift, buried as deep as you like—I have no quarrel with a man who knows how to stay quiet. Quiet men built every empire I've ever taken."*

Isandro's grip tightened on Dominic's fingers. The boy's face was pale, hollowed still from the pass, from the pulse of raw covenant-light that had poured out of him and left him mute even in drawings. He hadn't drawn anything since. He hadn't tried. He simply held on, and watched the doors, and breathed.

But now his other hand came up. Slowly. And pressed itself flat against Dominic's throat.

Dominic looked down at him. "What is it?"

Isandro didn't answer. He couldn't. His eyes were fixed on the doors, and they weren't afraid anymore—or rather, they were afraid the way Dominic had seen him afraid only once before, on the night of the blizzard, right before the light came out of him. Fear stretched thin over something vast.

The iron boomed again. One of the hinges shrieked.

"The binding words," said Warden Orenn from the chamber's far side. The eldest of the three wardens stood beside the covenant-stone—a pillar of veined white crystal rising from the floor to vanish into darkness overhead, humming with a light so old it had gone the color of dust. Blood seeped from a wound along his jaw; he did not seem to have noticed it. "We told you what renewal requires. A speaker of both tongues. There is no other way, Marchetti. There has never been another way."

"And if I can't do it?" Dominic asked. Not because he didn't know the answer. Because some part of him still hoped someone would hand him one.

"Then the covenant dies tonight," Orenn said simply. "And everything after tonight belongs to men like him."

The doors took another blow. This time a seam split along the middle, a finger-width of black gap, and through it came torchlight and the smell of hot metal and the thin, terrible sound of a muzzled drake whimpering.

Dominic closed his eyes.

He thought of Vell. That was the strange thing—he had braced himself to think of fear, or death, or the cold arithmetic of duty the wardens kept pressing into his hands, and instead what rose in him was morning. Frost on the fence rails. The hens complaining about the cold in their small dry voices. The smell of bread and pine smoke. Isandro asleep in the loft with straw in his hair, and Serra a gray shadow at the treeline, watching the house with the patience of something that had decided, long ago, to belong there.

He had spent his whole life being quiet. He had learned quiet the way other children learned to walk—first, and forever. Quiet was how you survived a gift that shouldn't exist. Quiet was how you kept the valley safe, kept the beasts safe, kept yourself safe inside a life so small and warm it fit in one satchel. Every kindness he had ever done, he had done softly, so softly the world wouldn't notice and come asking questions.

And here, at the end of all of it, the world was asking.

*Give me the boy,* Voss had said. *Or speak.*

There was no third door. There never had been. The covenant did not need a keeper chained to a stone; it needed a voice willing to be heard, and it had been waiting—one year, ten years, thirty years—for a man who would rather be silent than safe, and who had finally, finally run out of silence.

"Dominic." Serra's voice, very low. "Whatever you're about to do. Do it fast."

He opened his eyes. He knelt.

It surprised even him—that was the first thing, the sheer physical strangeness of it, going down on both knees on the cold crystal floor with Isandro standing before him, so that for once in all their months together the boy looked down at him. Dominic took the small hands in his own. They were freezing. They were always freezing.

"Listen to me," he said. "I don't know the words. I know that. I know oxen-talk and hawk-speech and the long whining grammar of dogs, and none of it was ever meant for this. But you do know the words. They've been living in you since before you could walk. So here's what we're going to do." He squeezed the cold fingers. "You put them in me. However you have to. Through your hands, through your eyes, I don't care. And I'll carry them out loud. We'll braid them—you and me. Like rope. Like the way you twist two strands until neither one alone would hold a lamb on a hillside."

Isandro stared at him. And then—the gods help him—the boy *nodded*, slow and grave, the way he nodded at things that mattered: at graves, at promises, at the first thaw.

The doors broke.

It was not a boom this time but a tearing, a scream of iron giving up, and the black halves swung inward and torchlight poured in, and with it the vanguard of Kaelen Voss—twenty men at least in the first press, halberds and white-and-gold surcoats—and behind them, filling the archway, the things the army had dragged up the mountain. A muzzled bear the size of a haybarn, chains sunk into the flesh of its shoulders, its small eyes dead with despair. Two drakes, wing-joints pinned, throats wrapped in iron. Handlers with hooked staves. And at the center of them, walking through his own soldiers' ranks as though the battle outside were merely weather, came Kaelen Voss.

He was not what the stories promised. He was ordinary—middle-aged, broad, graying at the temples, with the pleasant heavy face of a man who had once been good at harvest festivals. Only the eyes were wrong: patient, weighing, the eyes of a man appraising livestock.

"There he is," Voss said warmly. "The listener. And the little vessel." His gaze slid to Isandro, and something in it sharpened with genuine appetite. "Do you know what you are, boy? Do you know what they grew you *for*? No? Then let me simplify your choices. Come to me, and live as a curiosity in a gilded room. Stay with him, and die in a crater of old magic. Those are—"

"Orenn," Dominic said quietly, without turning his head. "How long, once I start?"

"As long as the words take."

"Then buy me the length of a prayer."

Serra laughed—a short, wild, exhausted bark—and threw herself forward.

What happened next, Dominic would remember all his life in fragments, because he was busy doing the only important thing anyone had ever asked of him. He heard steel ring on warden-steel. He heard Orenn shout words that bent the air. He heard the bear roar against its chains—not for its handlers, he realized with a jolt, but *at* them, a confused, half-drowned fury, as though something in the bear had heard, even through iron and misery, that a voice in this room spoke its language. He heard Voss shouting orders, and the orders growing less smooth.

And he knelt at the covenant-stone with a mute child's hands on his throat, and began to speak.

It started badly. Of course it did. The first syllable Isandro fed into him was nothing any human throat was built for—a sound like ice cracking on a deep lake, like wind through a canyon too old for names—and it tore at him going out, scraped his chest raw, left him gasping. But the boy's palms pressed harder, and the second syllable came easier, and the third easier still, and then something happened that Dominic had no word for except the one he'd used all his life in secret:

They began to *talk*.

Not Isandro speaking and Dominic repeating. Something stranger, something braided—the ancient tongue flowing up through the boy's hands and meeting, halfway down Dominic's throat, the speech he had carried hidden for forty years, the beast-speech, the low warm language of fields and kennels and winter barns. And where they met they did not clash. They *wove*. The old tongue gave the words their bones; the beast-speech gave them breath. It was, he realized dimly, tears freezing on his face without his permission, exactly what a covenant was. Two languages that should never have fit, fitted. Mortal and magical, bound at the throat of one unwilling, ordinary man.

The stone woke.

Light climbed the white crystal pillar like dawn climbing a mountain—not the dusty ember-glow of before but a rising, roaring gold, and the hum in the floor became a chord, and the chord became a *voice*, vast and layered, a thousand years of promise remembering itself. The chamber filled with it. The torches guttered. The soldiers faltered, shielding their eyes, and their faltering had nothing to do with fear of men.

Dominic kept speaking. It hurt now—it hurt like birth, like the first cry of something, and perhaps that was exactly what it was—and he found he did not care. This was the truth he had swallowed his whole life, finally given air: *I hear them. I have always heard them. The oxen and the crows and the scarred wolf at my door, and I am not ashamed, I was never ashamed, I was only afraid.* Every word of the binding went out of him carrying that confession, and the covenant took it, and forgave him for the hiding, and asked for more.

Through the broken doors, the world answered.

The bear's chains snapped—not broken, *unlocked*, every link falling slack at once, because the wards that held them had been stolen magic, and stolen magic had just been called home. The muzzle split and dropped. The bear stood swaying in the torchlight, blinking, its small eyes suddenly, terribly *present*—and then it turned, slow as a mountain deciding, and looked at the handlers with their hooked staves.

The handlers ran. Most of them.
