## 20. Aftermath of Light

The Spire sang for three days.

Not the way a bell sings, or a woman at her loom, but the way a mountain might sing if it had spent two hundred years holding its breath and had finally been given permission to exhale. The sound came up through the floors and out through the broken crown of the tower, and it was less a noise than a weather—a pressure in the chest, a sweetness behind the teeth. Birds that had never been seen this far north landed on the shattered balustrades and did not leave. In the valley below, Dominic was told, rivers that had run gray since before his grandfather's birth ran clear.

Dominic spent most of those three days sleeping, eating, and being fussed over by wardens who were older than kingdoms and fussed like grandmothers.

"You are favoring your left side," said Warden Ondrel, the eldest of the three, materializing beside him in the refectory as silently as dust settling. She set down a bowl of something steaming and golden. "Sit. Eat. Your ribs knit crooked otherwise."

"My ribs are fine."

"Your ribs," Ondrel said, "are lying to you, which is ironic, given your particular talent." She pushed the bowl an inch closer. It was not a suggestion. It had possibly never been a suggestion in four thousand years.

Dominic sat. He ate. The soup tasted of honey and rosemary and something else he couldn't name—something that made the ache behind his eyes loosen its grip. Isandro sat across from him, working through a bowl twice the size of Dominic's own, and when their eyes met the boy grinned around his spoon.

*He hasn't stopped talking,* Dominic marveled, not for the first time. Not aloud—not yet, not in full sentences—but constantly, ceaselessly, in that old tongue that now lived in Dominic's own mouth like a second heartbeat. *Chatter, chatter, chatter.* Two weeks mute even in drawings after the Pass of Teeth, and now it was as if someone had opened a sluice gate. He told the wardens things. He told the walls things. He told the soup things, apparently.

"He says the soup is good," Dominic translated, "but he thinks you put too much honey in it, Warden Sef, and he would like to know if there's more bread."

Sef—the youngest warden, though youngest here meant merely *young for a geological formation*—laughed from the hearth where she was coaxing the fire higher without touching it. "Tell him there is always more bread. Tell him he may have the whole loaf if he tells me what the mountains say these days. They've been gossiping terribly since the covenant woke."

"They've been gossiping for two hundred years," Ondrel said dryly. "No one could hear them. Now everyone can. It's insufferable." But she said it with the ghost of a smile, and her hand, resting on the table, had stopped trembling for the first time since Dominic had known her.

That was the thing about the Spire now. Everything had *stopped*. The trembling. The dimming. The slow suffocation that had been killing the place by inches while the world forgot it existed. Light came through windows that had been dark. Doors opened onto rooms that had been sealed. And everywhere, woven through it all, Dominic felt the covenant humming against his own skin—because it lived in him now, permanently, the way a scar lives in you. He had spoken the binding with Isandro's hands on his throat and the boy's words braided through his voice, and when it was done, the gift that had been his secret, his shame, his small warm sin in a magic-dead valley, had become something vast and public and *his*.

He still wasn't entirely sure how he felt about that.

* * *

On the fourth day, they came to make him an offer.

They did it properly, because wardens did everything properly. They dressed in their formal grays—Ondrel in silver-threaded robes that whispered when she moved, Sef with her hair bound in the guardian's knot, and Warden Maelis, who had barely spoken since the battle, standing at the back with her one good eye fixed on Dominic like a hawk deciding whether a field mouse was worth the dive.

They gathered in the chamber at the Spire's heart, where the covenant burned in a column of soft gold light that hadn't existed a week ago. Isandro was there too—he refused to be anywhere else when matters concerning him were discussed, and Dominic had decided he'd earned that. The boy sat cross-legged on the floor with Serra's head in his lap, scratching behind her ears, radiating the studied innocence of a child who intended to eavesdrop on everything.

"Dominic Marchetti of Vell," Ondrel began, and her voice took on the cadence of ritual. "Speaker of both tongues. Voice of the renewed covenant. The Spire offers you—"

"A tower," Dominic said.

Ondrel paused. "...A tower."

"You're going to offer me a tower. I've been here four days. I've seen the looks. There's a whole wing of empty towers on the east side and every time I walk past one, all three of you get very interested in the architecture."

Sef coughed, which might have been a laugh. Maelis's expression did not change, but something in her eye did.

"And a title," Ondrel continued, with the patience of someone who had negotiated with glaciers. "Keeper of the Covenant. First Voice. And a duty: to remain, to anchor the binding, to be the bridge between realms for as long as you live. The covenant flows through you now. It would be... simplest, if you stayed where it could tend you, and you it."

The word *simplest* hung in the air. Dominic looked at the column of light. It looked back, or seemed to; these days he could never quite tell what the covenant was aware of, and the not-knowing kept him humble.

"I want to be clear," he said slowly, "that I understand what you're offering. And I want to be clear about what I'm about to say, so nobody spends the next century hoping I'll come around."

He crouched down so he was level with Isandro, who had gone very still, his hand frozen in Serra's fur.

"When I found this boy on my doorstep," Dominic said, "he was mute and freezing and someone had taught him exactly one word, and it was a word no human mouth should know. And do you know what I thought? I thought, *this child is going to ruin my quiet life.* I hid my gift for thirty years. Thirty years of biting my tongue every time a horse complained about its bit, every time a dog asked me why the cat got the better spot by the fire. I built my whole life around staying small and safe and silent." He straightened. "And then the world came knocking, and I learned that small and safe and silent is just another way of saying *alone*, and I am done with alone. All three flavors of it."

He turned to face the wardens fully.

"If I stay here—if I take the tower and the title and become the Keeper chained to this light—then what happens to him? Does he grow up in a corridor? Does he learn to speak among ghosts and stone? You said the covenant needs a speaker of both tongues. Well. So does he. He needs valleys. Neighbors. A garden to ruin. Goats to argue with." A pause. "Goats argue. Nobody warns you about that until it's too late."

"The covenant—" Maelis began, her voice like dry leaves.

"The covenant doesn't need a keeper chained to it," Dominic said. "You told me yourself, the night after the binding. You said the old arrangement failed *because* it was a chain—that a covenant held by one person's imprisonment is only as strong as that person's endurance, and people break. What it needs is friends. People willing to visit. People who carry it lightly." He spread his hands. "I'll carry it. I carry it already—it hums in my chest when I wake up, and I suspect it always will. But I'll carry it from home. From Vell. Where the light reaches, now. Where it *can* reach, if you let it."

Silence. The column of gold light flickered—or perhaps breathed.

It was Isandro who broke it. He tugged Serra's ear, signed something quick and fierce, and then—deliberately, looking each warden in the eye—spoke aloud, in the old tongue, two words that Dominic felt translate themselves in his own throat:

*"He stays mine."*

Well. That was one way to put it.

Ondrel looked at the boy for a long moment. Then at Dominic. Then, unexpectedly, at Maelis—and something passed between the two ancient women, some argument concluded or surrendered.

"The east towers will remain empty," Ondrel said at last, and there was rue in it, and relief too, Dominic thought. "But the door will remain open. And Dominic Marchetti of Vell—you will find that 'friends who visit' is a duty we intend to hold you to. The covenant will want news of the lowlands. The beasts will want news of you. And I—" her composure cracked, just slightly, into something warmer and older and lonelier than any title, "—I should like to know how the boy's handwriting improves. He has been sending drawings to the eastern wall. The wall shows them to anyone who passes. We have all seen the one of you falling off the mule."

"That mule tripped," Dominic said with dignity.

Isandro made a sound that was unmistakably a snicker.

* * *

Serra told him at dusk, on the western balcony, where the Spire's living stone had grown them a bench wide enough for a wolf and a man and left gaps for the wind to sing through.

Below, the host of freed beasts was dispersing in ones and twos and dozens—wolves and bears and hawks and the great shaggy aurochs that Voss had yoked to his siege engines, all of them drifting back toward the wild places, pausing at the treeline to look up at the tower that had woken them. Some of them bowed. Some of them simply watched. One elderly badger, who had participated in the battle primarily by biting an inquisitor's ankle and refusing to let go, had taken up residence in the kitchen and showed no signs of leaving.

"I'm staying," Serra said.

Dominic didn't answer right away. Below them, Isandro was in the courtyard teaching a flock of crows the names of colors, which was either adorable or the beginning of something terrible, and honestly with that boy it was usually both.

"For how long?" he asked finally.

"For as long as it takes." She was watching the treeline, her scarred muzzle lifted. "There are others like the ones Voss kept. Enslaved, muzzled, bound into engines. His generals fled north with whatever beasts they could drag along. And the guardian order is three old women and a badger." She glanced at him, and her yellow eyes held the dry humor he'd learned to love on the road. "Four, counting the badger. Someone has to rebuild it. Someone has to teach the young ones what the covenant means—not the chains version. Yours."

"It'll be years."

"Yes."

"You'll be alone up here with three cryptic ancients and a badger with opinions."

"The badger and I have an understanding." She leaned her shoulder against his arm, briefly, the way she did instead of the things other creatures said with words. "And you'll visit. Both of you. Every season. That is not a request. I have met your sense of direction, Dominic of Vell. Left unsupervised, you will get lost within sight of your own house."

"That happened *once*."

"Twice."

"...The second time the fog was genuinely unprecedented."

She huffed—a wolf laugh—and for a while they simply sat, watching the light spread westward over the mountains, gilding the pass of teeth, reaching, reaching, toward a green valley that was even now learning to hum instead of sleep.

"I keep thinking," Dominic said quietly, "that I spent thirty years believing duty was a weight. Something you carried until it broke your back, and the best you could hope for was to set it down somewhere soft. My father thought that. His father. Everyone in Vell carries something that way—the farm, the family name, the silence." He shook his head. "But this—" he touched his own chest, where the covenant hummed, "—this doesn't feel like a weight. It feels like a lantern. Heavy, yes. But meant to be *carried*. Meant to move. What's the point of a lantern you nail to a wall?"

Serra considered this with the gravity she gave all his philosophizing, which was considerable and slightly mocking.

"You have become wise," she said. "It's very annoying. I liked you better when you were frightened and polite."

"I was never polite."

"No," she agreed. "That's why I stayed."

* * *

They left on a morning so clear it felt invented.

The wardens walked them to the outer gate—all three of them, plus the badger, who had appointed itself to the honor guard and waddled ahead with tremendous authority. Sef pressed a parcel of bread into Isandro's arms and a smaller parcel of sweets that she pretended was medicine. Maelis gripped Dominic's forearm, warrior-fashion, and said only, "Lightly, Voice. Carry it lightly." Ondrel kissed the top of Isandro's head, which visibly astonished the boy, and then fixed Dominic with a look that contained a lecture, a blessing, and a threat, in that order.

"Seasonally," she said. "Both of you. If I must send the birds to fetch you, Dominic Marchetti, know that the birds talk now, and they hold grudges, and I will tell them where you sleep."

"Noted," Dominic said solemnly. "Ma'am."

And Serra walked with them as far as the first bend in the mountain road, because rules were rules, and then she sat down in the middle of the path and refused to go further, because if she went further she would follow them all the way to Vell, and they both knew it, and neither of them said it.

Isandro threw his arms around her neck and buried his face in her ruff for a long time. When he pulled back, his eyes were wet and furious about it, and he signed something fast and private that he did not translate, and Serra closed her eyes as if the words weighed a great deal and were worth carrying anyway.

"Go home," she told them both, gruffly. "Grow the garden. Ruin the garden. Argue with goats. And when the first snow comes—" her voice caught, just once, the only crack in all her armor, "—when the first snow comes, leave the door unlatched. Old habit. Humor me."

"We will," Dominic said. "Every snow. Latch rusted open. I'll see to it personally."

She watched them go. Dominic looked back exactly once, at the bend where the road dropped toward the lowlands—once was all he allowed himself—and saw her sitting tall against the sky, the last guardian at the gate of a waking world, and beside her, absurd and magnificent, the badger.

Then the road bent, and the Spire was gone, and it was just the two of them and the long bright morning and the covenant humming gently in Dominic's chest like a cat that had eaten well.

Isandro walked beside him, chattering softly to a hawk riding the thermals above, translating nothing, needing to translate nothing. After a while he slipped his small hand into Dominic's larger one, and signed with his free hand, lazy and content:

*Home?*

"Yeah," Dominic said, and found the word fit better than any word had fit in thirty years. "Home."

Behind them, far up the mountain, faint as memory, the Spire sang.

And far ahead—though Dominic could not yet know it, though the road gave no hint—down in the green dark of the valley of Vell, something old had noticed the light coming home, and had begun, patiently, to wait.
