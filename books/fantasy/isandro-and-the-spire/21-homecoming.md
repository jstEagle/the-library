## 21. Homecoming

The road down into the valley of Vell looked exactly as Dominic remembered it, which was somehow the strangest thing of all.

He had expected it to seem smaller. Everyone said that, didn't they? You leave a place, you go out into the wide and terrible world, and when you come home the hills have shrunk and the fences lean and the whole valley fits in your palm like something a child might have made. But Vell had not shrunk. If anything it seemed to have grown while he was away—the mountains taller, the pines darker, the river louder than he'd ever known it to be. Because now he could hear it properly. Now he could hear everything.

"Isandro." He reined in—there was no horse, only his own two boots and the boy's smaller ones beside them, but he reined in anyway, out of habit—and pointed with his chin at the treeline. "Listen."

Isandro tilted his head. The boy's face did its careful work, the way it always did: gathering information first, deciding what to show second. Then his eyes went wide, and he tugged Dominic's sleeve and pointed at the same trees, and made a small sound—not a word, not yet, though there were more words every day now—but a sound of pure recognition.

The birds. All of them, singing at once, and not just singing *at* each other but singing *about* things. A wren was complaining about a cat. Two crows were arguing over the ownership of a dead fox, an argument that had apparently been going on for some time and showed no signs of resolution. And beneath it all, faint and steady as a heartbeat under snow, came the ward itself.

That was the difference. That was what he heard when he stood very still on the ridge with the wind pulling at his coat: the old ward of Vell, the one that had pressed down on his whole life like a hand flat against a drumhead, was no longer deafening silence. It hummed. Gently. The way a hearth hums, or a hive, or a mother humming over a cradle without quite knowing she's doing it. Magic moved through the valley now the way water moves through a meadow—not held out, not locked away, just... welcomed, and slowed, and given somewhere kind to go.

"It's learning," Dominic said aloud, and a jay overhead repeated him, badly, and Isandro laughed for the third time that day, which by Dominic's private count put him three ahead of the entire previous year.

They came down into the valley proper in the gold end of the afternoon, when the light goes long and honest and shows you every fence post you failed to mend before you left. Dominic's cottage sat where he'd left it, which surprised him more than it should have. He had half expected it to have burned, or been confiscated, or simply evaporated from embarrassment at what its owner had turned out to be. Instead there it was: stone walls, turf roof, woodpile half-rotted where he'd stacked it wrong, door standing slightly ajar because Marta Hale could never be trusted to shut anything.

Marta Hale. Who had told an inquisitor, to his face, that no such child had ever been seen in the valley, while standing in front of the very child's footprints in the snow. Dominic had learned about it later, from Serra, who had learned about it from a passing fox, who had found the whole affair extremely funny.

He hadn't let himself think about that too much on the road. Thinking about it meant hoping, and hoping was a currency he'd learned to spend carefully.

A figure was coming up the lane. Dominic knew the walk before he knew the face—Tomas Hale, Marta's husband, moving with the deliberate unhurried gait of a man who has decided in advance not to look nervous and is finding the decision harder than expected.

Dominic's stomach did something complicated. His hand found Isandro's shoulder without asking permission; the boy stepped closer, which was itself remarkable, because eight months ago Isandro would have hidden behind a tree rather than be touched by anyone.

"Marchetti." Tomas stopped a respectful ten feet off. His eyes went to Isandro, then back. "You're—people said. On the mountain roads. They said you were dead."

"People say a lot of things."

"They do." Tomas shifted. Looked at the sky, the way men do when the alternative is looking at another man. "We heard about the Spire. About the battle. Marta says the whole eastern sky lit up for a night and a day." He paused. "She also says she told the inquisitor you'd gone east toward Harrowgate, which was a lie, because you'd gone north, and she wanted them walking in the wrong direction long enough to freeze."

"I know," Dominic said. "I heard."

"You *heard*," Tomas repeated, and there it was—the flinch, the old reflex, the thing Dominic had spent thirty years cultivating in everyone around him without meaning to. People lowered their voices around animals when he was near. People apologized to their dogs. Nobody had ever once met his eyes while doing it.

But then Tomas did something new. He lifted his chin, and he looked at Dominic Marchetti straight on, the way you look at a neighbor, and said: "Good. I'm glad you heard. I'm glad you'll always hear it. Marta will want to say it again herself, so prepare yourself, she's been rehearsing since midwinter."

Behind Dominic, Isandro made a small huffing sound that was unmistakably, even across species, a laugh.

* * *

The valley took some getting used to, in its new skin.

The animals had changed—or rather, they had stopped pretending. That was the thing nobody warned you about, when a land stops suppressing magic: the beasts of Vell had been living behind the same deafness their whole lives, and now that the veil was thin, they were *chatty*. The goats had opinions about the fence line. The chickens had developed a theological dispute about the nature of the red-tailed hawk that Dominic was asked to mediate twice weekly. Old Bramble, the Hale farm dog, eleven years stiff-jointed and dignified, had taken to following Isandro everywhere and narrating her devotion in terms that would have embarrassed a much younger animal.

"He knows," Marta said one evening, weeks after their return, watching Isandro sit in the yard with a ring of farm cats arranged around him like a council. "Everyone knows. The boy talks to them and they talk back and half the valley's seen it, and do you know what the worst part is?"

"Tell me."

"The worst part is how *long* we all pretended otherwise. Thirty years, Dominic. You'd come to market and the horses would go quiet and polite whenever you passed, like children straightening up when the schoolmaster walks by, and every farmer in Vell thought to himself, *huh, funny, the horses like Marchetti*, and not one of us—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. When she went on, her voice was rougher. "Not one of us asked you a single question. Not one of us made it easy for you to answer."

"Marta."

"No, let me finish, I've rehearsed this too." She wiped her eyes briskly, furious at them. "You carried that alone in the quietest valley in the world, and we called it peace. Well. It won't be quiet anymore. The Spire sends letters. There are folk coming through asking questions—good questions, mostly, scholars and such, and one terrifying woman with grey eyes who left a book for the boy. And the valley's going to change, and I want you to know that when it does, you won't be carrying anything alone. That's all. That's the speech. Now take these eggs and don't argue."

Dominic took the eggs. He found, to his considerable surprise, that his throat had closed entirely, and that the only adequate response was to stand there holding six eggs like a man who had never seen eggs before.

From the yard, Isandro watched him over the heads of the cats, and smiled—that easy, unguarded smile that still stopped Dominic's heart every time, because he remembered the boy who had arrived mute in a snowstorm, drawing words older than kingdoms in the frost on a windowpane.

* * *

Spring came up the valley like a rumor turning true.

Dominic rebuilt the woodpile correctly. He mended the fence line, and the goats supervised, offering unsolicited structural criticism through the entire project. He planted the garden late and the garden forgave him. And every morning he walked the ridgeline with Isandro and Serra—when Serra was there, which was most days now, though she ranged far, carrying messages and warnings and, increasingly, students—for the simple pleasure of hearing the valley wake.

It was on one of those mornings, with the mist still sitting in the low fields like milk poured into a bowl, that Isandro stopped at the old boundary stone—the one that had marked the edge of the ward's deepest silence, the line Dominic had crossed in terror with a satchel and a child and a wolf, magic flooding back into him like drowning in reverse.

The boy crouched. Drew in the dirt with one finger: quick, sure strokes. A tower. A valley. A thread running between them, bright and unbroken.

Then he looked up at Dominic and spoke—clearly, in the old tongue first, and then, deliberately, word by word, in the language of Vell:

"The valley remembers. So do I."

Dominic crouched beside him in the wet grass. Below them the village was waking—smoke from chimneys, the Hale dog announcing the sunrise to an audience that had heard it all before, someone laughing at the mill. Above them the mountains stood guard, and beyond them, faint as a star at dawn, the Spire held its post.

There was a letter waiting on his table when they got home. It bore the seal of the guardian order, and inside, in Yeva Corvane's unmistakable hand—a hand he had mourned, and then rejoiced to learn had survived the undercroft—were four lines:

*Other keys may sleep. Other valleys keep their silence. The covenant is young again, and young things grow in directions no one plans. Come when you can. Bring the boy. Bring the wolf.*

Dominic read it twice. Then he set it on the mantel, between the clay whistle Isandro had made and the jar of Marta's honey, and he left it there, unopened further, unanswered—for now.

Outside, Isandro was teaching the goats to sing.

Some things, Dominic thought, could wait until after breakfast.
