## 22. The Boy Who Spoke First

The geese were arguing again.

Isandro crouched at the edge of the millpond, arms folded on his knees, and listened to them squabble the way he had once listened to rain—without understanding a word of it, and loving it anyway. Now he understood. That was the difference three seasons could make.

*The north bank is better grazing,* honked the old gander, who had appointed himself mayor of the pond sometime around midwinter and defended his office with the ferocity of a much larger bird.

*The north bank is where the fox lives, you feathered idiot,* said his mate, with the weary precision of someone who had made this argument every morning since the thaw.

*The fox moved.*

*The fox did not move. The fox is a fox.*

Isandro let them wear themselves out for a while before he answered, because part of the joy was in the listening. Then he spoke—not in the valley tongue, but in the old language, the one that lived somewhere behind his ribs where words used to be missing. The syllables came out round and warm, like river stones.

*Both banks are yours,* he told them. *But if you quarrel about it tomorrow, I'm telling Serra, and she'll eat one of you as an example.*

The gander considered this. Geese are not quick thinkers, but they are honest ones, and honesty has its own kind of speed. He dipped his head—a small, grudging bow—and led his family to the south bank, muttering something about tyrants and children that Isandro chose not to translate for anyone's benefit.

From up the lane came the sound of Dominic's boots, unhurried as ever, and the jingle of the pail he carried everywhere now that spring had come back to Vell. Isandro didn't turn around. He didn't need to. He knew the particular rhythm of those footsteps the way he knew his own heartbeat: steady, a little uneven on the left, the walk of a man who had spent thirty years pretending to be ordinary and had finally, blessedly, given it up.

"Let me guess," Dominic said, settling onto the grass beside him with a groan that was mostly performance and entirely sincere. "The gander filed another complaint."

"He wants the north bank."

"The north bank has a fox."

"That's what his wife said." Isandro grinned. Speaking still felt like a miracle some mornings—the simple mechanics of it, air becoming meaning. "He claims the fox moved. She claims the fox is a fox."

"And who won?"

"I threatened to tell Serra."

Dominic laughed, that low rolling laugh that had once been rare as snow in summer and now came easily, the way breathing did. "Cruel. Effective. You're learning politics."

"Serra says politics is just threatening to eat people politely."

"Serra," Dominic said, "has been spending too much time with the Harrowgate merchants who come through buying wool."

He handed Isandro the pail. Inside, two new lambs blinked their wet yellow blinks at the world, unimpressed by everything they saw. Isandro reached in and touched their heads, and they pressed into his palm with the absolute trust of creatures too young to know there was anything in the world worth distrusting.

*Hello,* he said to them, in the old tongue. *It's loud here, but it's good. You'll see.*

One of them sneezed. It was, Isandro decided, a fair review.

They sat together while the morning warmed up, man and boy, watching the pond settle and the valley do its slow green work. Three seasons ago, Isandro had stood on a doorstep in a snowstorm, mute, carrying a word no human mouth should have known, and the man who opened the door had looked at him not with fear but with a question. *Where did you learn that?* And when Isandro couldn't answer—couldn't answer anything—Dominic had stepped aside and let him in anyway.

That was the whole story, really. Everything else—the Spire, the war, the burning sky over the pass, the night the covenant sang through Dominic's throat and woke every chained creature within a hundred miles—all of it grew from that one small act. A door opening.

Isandro had tried to explain this to the geese once. They had found it unremarkable. Doors open; that is what doors are for. Sometimes he thought the beasts understood grace better than any warden ever had.

* * *

Midmorning brought work, because Vell was still Vell, magic or no magic, and the sheep would not shear themselves.

The ward that had once lain over the valley like a lid—dead, deaf, suffocating—hummed now instead, gentle as a hearthfire. Isandro could feel it when he walked the boundaries: a low sweet note running under the world, the sound of two things that had been enemies learning to be neighbors. Magic came and went through Vell freely these days, and the valley had discovered, to its own enormous surprise, that it liked it. The orchards fruited heavier. The wells ran sweeter. Old Marta down the lane had grown tomatoes the size of fists and refused, absolutely refused, to admit magic had anything to do with it.

"They're just good seeds," she'd told Dominic, red-faced, while her tomatoes sat there being impossible.

"Of course," Dominic had agreed solemnly. "Good seeds."

Neighbors met Isandro's eyes now. That had taken longer. For years they had lied for Dominic without knowing what they lied about, had turned away inquisitors and kept their silence, and shame had sat on some of them like a wet coat. But shame fades faster than fear, and fear had gone out of the valley entirely the day the usurper fell. Now children followed Isandro around the pastures asking him to *say something to the cows*, and the cows, who were flatterers, always said something nice back.

Today it was shearing, which meant Bram, the ram, which meant negotiation.

*I want to keep my coat,* Bram announced from atop his rock, where he had stationed himself with the dignity of a deposed king. *I grew it. It's mine. Finders keepers.*

*You can't even find your own way out of the barn,* said his daughter, who had inherited her mother's precision.

*It keeps me warm.*

*Bram,* Isandro said patiently, climbing the rock to sit beside him, *it is nearly summer. You were panting yesterday. I heard you complain about the heat to the fencepost.*

*The fencepost is a good listener.*

*We will wash the wool and spin it, and Marta will knit you a blanket, and you will be warmer in winter than you have ever been, and everyone will admire you.* He paused. *Also, if you don't come down, I'm telling Serra.*

Bram looked at him for a long moment. "Everyone threatens me with the wolf," he said, in the valley tongue, which he had been secretly learning and pretended not to know. "It's abuse, is what it is."

Isandro fell off the rock laughing, and Bram, mortified at having revealed himself, allowed himself to be shorn while radiating the dignity of a martyr, and got his blanket by first frost, and wore it, and was admired.

* * *

Serra came at noon, the way she always did on the days the Spire's letters were expected.

She came down out of the treeline like a rumor, gray and scarred and moving with that economy of motion that still made strangers' hands drift toward their knives until they learned better. She carried things in her mouth now sometimes, out of habit, though she could speak as well as anyone—better, when the truth needed saying plainly, which was most of the time.

"Letter?" Dominic asked, before she'd even stopped.

"You've gotten predictive," Serra said. "Yes. And before you ask—it's not bad news. It's never bad news anymore. I checked with the wind, and the wind agrees with me, and the wind is rarely wrong about anything except itself."

The letter was heavy paper, sealed with wax the color of dawn, and it bore the mark of the guardian order: three interlocked circles, drawn in a hand that trembled slightly, because Warden Ophel was very old and very stubborn about writing her own correspondence. Isandro took it and weighed it in his palm.

Letters from the Spire came four times a year. They were usually short. *The east tower is rebuilt. The library smells less of mold. Yeva Corvane's students have transcribed another shelf of the old records—she survived, somehow, and teaches now, and terrifies them all, and sends her regards and also complaints about your silence, Dominic, which we forward verbatim.* Little stitches in a seam that was holding. Proof that the world they had remade was staying remade.

This one was thicker than usual.

They ate lunch first, because Ophel's letters deserved attention and attention deserved bread and cheese, and then they sat in the shade of the big oak above the house and Isandro broke the seal.

Ophel's hand marched across the page like a soldier who had earned the right to march however she pleased:

*To Isandro, key of the covenant, voice of two tongues, and (though you will blush) heir of both worlds—*

*The eastern vaults are opened at last. It took us three seasons to clear the collapse, and what we found beneath the rubble changes the shape of what we thought we knew.*

*The covenant you renewed was not the only one.*

*There were others. Older ones. Sealed in the far places of the world—under the ice, beyond the southern deserts, in the drowned cities off the western coast. Each bound to its own keeper, its own key. And each, as far as our records show, still sleeping. Still intact. Waiting for voices that may or may not ever come.*

*Some of those keys may be children. Some may be nothing of the kind—we simply do not know, and I am old enough to say so plainly. What we know is this: the magic in them sleeps as yours slept, and the world above them does not yet know they exist. There are those who would hunt such keys, as Voss hunted you. There are those who would use them. And there are places where a sleeping covenant, disturbed carelessly, might wake angry.*

*We are not asking anything. Hear an old woman clearly: we are not asking. You are fourteen seasons past your saving of the world and owe it nothing further. But you are also the only living soul who knows what a sleeping key feels like from the inside, and someday—soon, or in ten years, or when your beard is gray, Dominic, stop laughing—that knowledge will matter to someone, somewhere, and they will need to find you.*

*Until then: live well. Tend your valley. Argue with geese. We hold the door open.*

*—Ophel, First Warden, who is too old for this and doing it anyway*

*P.S. Yeva says the drowned-city records mention a second language variant and she will come to Vell herself to argue about it if you continue to ignore her letters. She means it. Check the map. Vell is not that far from anywhere, despite your best efforts.*

Isandro read it twice. Then he read it aloud, slowly, in the old tongue, for the benefit of the oak tree, who liked stories and had opinions about endings.

When he finished, nobody spoke for a while. The wind moved through the leaves. Down in the pasture, Bram wore his blanket and pretended not to watch them.

"Other keys," Dominic said at last. His voice was careful, the way it got when something large was moving underneath it. "Other children. Possibly. Sleeping somewhere in the dark, waiting for—" He stopped. Started again. "Waiting for a door to open."

Isandro looked at him. He knew exactly what Dominic was seeing, because Dominic never quite managed to hide it: a snowy doorstep, a knock, a mute child with a word in his mouth. The memory lived in the man's face the way scars live in skin—part of him now, weathered and tender.

"We could go," Isandro said. Not a question. Not yet a promise either. Just a door, held ajar, testing whether the hinge still worked.

"We could," Dominic said. "Someday. When the lambs are grown, and Marta's tomatoes have confessed, and Bram has finished abusing his blanket." He smiled, but his eyes stayed serious. "Or we could stay forever, and that would be enough too. Both of those are true, Isandro. You don't have to choose today. You learned that from me, remember? The worst lesson I ever taught anyone, and the best."

*Someday,* said the oak tree, in the creaking voice oaks use for important agreements.

Isandro folded the letter along its old creases. He did not put it in the house. He did not put it in the box where he kept his drawings from before—the tower, the fire, the woman's hands, all of it safely past. Instead he tucked it inside his shirt, against his chest, where he kept the things that weren't finished yet.

Not today. Today there were lambs to check and a gander to appease and Yeva's complaints to answer (eventually, and probably with deliberate slowness, because some pleasures should be savored). Today the valley hummed its gentle note and the geese had reached, at last, a fragile peace treaty regarding the south bank.

But the letter lay warm against his heart, and it weighed exactly what a door weighs: nothing at all, until you push it.

* * *

Evening came gold across the valley, the way it did when the season was being generous. Isandro walked the boundary path alone, as he liked to at day's end, one hand trailing along the invisible line of the ward, feeling it sing.

At the treeline, Serra waited for him, sitting the way wolves sit when they are pretending they weren't waiting at all.

"Well?" she said. "The letter. Are we going?"

"Someday," Isandro said.

"Someday." She tasted the word, rolled it around. "I've chased that word across half the world. It's slippery. It bites."

"It's also true."

"Yes," Serra admitted. "That's the worst thing about it." She leaned her gray shoulder against his leg, briefly, the way she did perhaps twice a year and never explained. "You were a good key, little one. Whatever comes next—you'll be a better door."

Behind them, up the lane, Dominic's lamp bloomed in the kitchen window, and the smell of bread reached them even here, and somewhere down by the pond the gander was telling his family, with tremendous authority, about the time he had personally defeated a fox.

Isandro stood a moment longer, feeling the ward hum beneath his palm, feeling the letter rest against his heart, feeling the whole wide sleeping world stretch out beyond the mountains—full of locked rooms and quiet children and words waiting in the dark for someone to speak them first.

He smiled.

Then he turned toward the light, and the bread, and home—and began, for the hundredth time that day, to talk with the beasts.
