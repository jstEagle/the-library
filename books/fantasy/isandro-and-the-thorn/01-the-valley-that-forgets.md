## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley did not want Wendell Almeida's magic. It wanted nothing of them at all.

That was the whole point, of course. Hollowmere sat in a fold of the world where power went to die—where a storm-caller could stand on the highest hill in a howling gale and feel only wind, ordinary and dumb, pressing against their skin like any other mortal thing. The clouds here didn't answer. The lightning didn't listen. Even the rain fell straight, without opinion.

Wendell had lived with that silence for three years, and they still hadn't decided whether it was mercy or punishment. Most days it felt like both, braided together too tight to unpick.

They woke before dawn out of old habit—the hour when storms used to gather, when the sky would come looking for them—and lay in bed staring at the water stain on their ceiling that resembled, vaguely, a map of nowhere. Today was Thursday. Today they had a date.

*Date.* Wendell said the word silently, testing its weight, and it nearly buckled them. They were thirty-one years old and had not been on a date since before the exile. Before whatever it was they had done. The thought arrived on schedule, as it always did: the great locked door at the center of themselves, the one they had been carrying for three years without ever once finding the handle.

They knew the shape of what was missing. They knew they had been someone else, somewhere loud and electric—a storm-caller of enough renown to be *exiled*, which was different from merely banished. Exile to Hollowmere wasn't a sentence passed by any court. It was a choice, the innkeeper had told them once, over watery stew. Exiles came here because they *chose* to forget. The valley drank magic, and it drank memory with it—not all of it, never everything, just the parts you couldn't live with. You kept your name if you fought for it. You kept your face. You woke some mornings knowing you had done something terrible, without ever learning what, and you made tea anyway, and swept your floor, and let the valley hold the worst of you so your hands could stay empty enough to work.

Wendell had fought hard for their name.

Everything else, they'd let go under.

* * *

By midmorning Wendell had swept the floor twice, rearranged the jars of dried herbs on the sill three times, and burned the first attempt at breakfast into a blackened tribute to anxiety. Their cottage stood at the crook of the Larkwater stream, small and stone and stubborn, with a garden that grew vegetables out of spite rather than soil. On the table, where anyone entering might see it, lay the book.

Isandro's book.

He'd left it with them a week ago—"to mend," he'd said, though there was nothing wrong with it that Wendell could see. It was an antique, leather gone soft as old skin, its spine stitched with thread that caught light strangely, silverish one moment and green the next. Isandro Veyra was the bookbinder who worked out of the narrow shop between the chandler's and the shuttered apothecary, a quiet man with ink permanently ghosting his fingertips and a way of listening that made you feel like a rare text he intended to treat gently.

They had met over a damaged atlas. Wendell had brought in their grandmother's—no. Not their grandmother's. They *thought* it was their grandmother's; that was the trouble with Hollowmere, you reached for a memory and sometimes your hand closed on nothing. An atlas, anyway. Torn at the corner, and Isandro had looked up at them across his cluttered bench with those grave dark eyes and said, "I can save most of it," and something in Wendell's chest had come loose like a knot finally slipping.

One conversation became two. Two became an afternoon lost entirely, talking about paper and weather and whether rivers remembered their courses. And then, last market day, standing among the turnips with his hands in his pockets like a boy afraid of his own question, Isandro had said: "There's a place past the mill where the evening comes down gold through the alders. I thought—if you weren't doing anything. Thursday."

Thursday. Today.

Wendell picked up the book, meaning only to move it, and found themselves holding it instead, turning it over in their hands. It had no title on the cover. No title inside, either—or none they could find. Pages of dense, beautiful script in no alphabet Wendell recognized, looping and thorned, like ivy grown over a fence. Isandro said he'd acquired it from a traveling peddler years ago and had been restoring it slowly, binding it back into itself page by page.

"It hums," Isandro had admitted once, embarrassed. "Barely. Probably just the glue drying."

Probably. But Wendell had held it close, those first nights, and felt something faint beneath the leather—like a pulse heard through a wall. Like weather far away. In Hollowmere nothing should have hummed. Magic couldn't follow them here; that was the covenant of the valley, or the curse of it, depending whom you asked. Yet this book murmured against their palms like a sleeping animal dreaming of storms, and Wendell had told no one, and had not given it back as promptly as they might have.

They set it down now and dressed with the care of someone arming for battle. Good shirt—the blue one, the color of the sky right before rain. Hair wrestled into submission. Boots brushed. In the small mirror above the basin, a stranger regarded them: sharp-eyed, hollow-cheeked, a scar through one eyebrow they had no memory of earning. *You did something,* the stranger's face said. *You know you did.*

"Not today," Wendell told their reflection. "Today you're just a person going for a walk with a kind man."

Their reflection, notably, did not believe them.

* * *

Hollowmere in late afternoon was all long shadows and woodsmoke, the mountains ringing the valley like cupped hands keeping the world out. Wendell walked the main street past the baker's, where Mrs. Corvel pressed a heel of honey-bread on them unasked ("For your walk, dear, you're too thin"), past children skipping stones on the Larkwater with the flat, contented boredom of kids who had never once seen the river rise at a stranger's command.

That was the ache, Wendell thought, watching a stone skip twice and sink. Not the lost power itself—they'd made their peace with the quiet, mostly—but this: being *small* in a small place, and not knowing if smallness was what they deserved. Every exile in the valley wore the same invisible brand. Old Tam, the ferryman, flinched at the word "oath." The widow Sennet had burned every portrait of herself. And Wendell woke some nights with their hands raised, fingers spread, feeling phantom thunder roll through bones that no longer conducted it, hearing a voice scream words they couldn't keep—

"Careful," they muttered aloud, reining the thought in. Down that road lay the door again. The locked one. The valley had taken the handle, and maybe that was kindness.

The path past the mill climbed through alder stands, leaves turning early-gold, and there—as promised—was Isandro, waiting by the bend where the stream pooled deep and green. He'd brought a basket. Of course he'd brought a basket; Wendell could have laughed or wept. He wore a coat too warm for the season and stood like a man rehearsed and unready, and when he saw Wendell coming he smiled in a way that rearranged the entire landscape.

"You came," Isandro said.

"You doubted it?"

"I hoped loudly and doubted quietly." He handed over a piece of honey-bread as if it were a formal treaty. "Mrs. Corvel intercepted me too. We're clearly the thinnest people in this valley."

They spread a blanket on flat stone above the pool, and the evening did exactly as advertised: came down gold through the alders, laying long amber stripes across Isandro's hands as he unpacked bread, sharp cheese, apples, a bottle of cider that fizzed when opened like a small applause. He talked about his shop—a customer wanting a hymnal rebound in a week, impossible; the mice in the back room he'd named after philosophers. Wendell talked about the garden, the stream, the way the valley fog rolled in off the peaks at dusk like something breathing. Small talk. Safe talk. But underneath it ran a current neither of them mentioned: two exiles, or near enough—one who'd fled something, one who perhaps had too, sitting closer than strangers on a stone in the gold light.

Isandro laughed at something Wendell said about the philosopher-mice, and Wendell thought: *I could stay here. I could be this person forever—the one who makes him laugh. Whoever I was before, whoever I'll find out I was—I could build someone new.*

Then Isandro reached into the basket for the second bottle and went still.

"Wendell." His voice had changed—quiet, careful, the voice he used for fragile bindings. "Did you... is this yours?"

In his hands lay the book. The untitled one. Wendell's stomach dropped. "I was going to return it tonight. I should have asked—"

"No, look." He opened it across his knees, and even from a pace away Wendell could see it: fresh ink, black and gleaming wet on pages that had been blank an hour ago—blank, Wendell was certain, they had checked just that morning. New lines of that looping, thorned script, and beneath it, in smaller letters, almost shy:

*Two sit by the green pool at the fall of gold light. One calls storms. One binds books.*

And below that, in the same impossible fresh ink, two names.

**Isandro Veyra.**

**Wendell Almeida.**

The alders shivered though there was no wind. Somewhere far above the valley rim—impossibly far, impossibly high—Wendell heard thunder.

In Hollowmere, where magic could not follow.

Isandro's face had gone pale as vellum. "That wasn't there yesterday," he whispered. "None of this was ever there."

The book lay open between them, humming now, no longer faintly, and the ink began to move—rewriting itself, line by line, as though something on the other side of the page had just picked up its pen.

And the storm above the mountains, patient and hungry, began to climb over the ridge.
