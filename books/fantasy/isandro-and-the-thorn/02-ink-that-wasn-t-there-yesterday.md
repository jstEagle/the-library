## 2. Ink That Wasn't There Yesterday

The book arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in oilcloth that smelled of river mud and old smoke, and Isandro Veyra knew before he cut the twine that it was going to ruin his week.

He had learned to trust that feeling the way other men trusted weather in their knees. Twenty-two years at the bench had taught him that books were honest only about their damage. A volume would lie about its contents—prophecies lied constantly, histories worse—but it never lied about its wounds. The split spine, the foxed leaves, the water stain shaped like a weeping eye: these were confessions, and Isandro's whole trade was listening to them and answering with glue and thread and patience.

This one confessed a great deal. *The Covenant of Root and Rain*, the title page said, though half the letters had been scraped away by someone who'd wanted the book to forget its own name. The binding was oak boards under calfskin gone the color of weak tea. The sewing stations had been punched twice—the original holes, then a second, clumsier set a century later when someone had rebound it in a hurry, or in fear. And threaded through all of it, faint as veins under skin, ran a corruption Isandro had no name for: a darkness in the paper itself, as if the fibers had been dipped in something that drank.

He set it on his bench beneath the good lamp, rolled up his sleeves, and began.

His shop sat at the crooked end of Lantern Row, where the valley's one street gave up pretending to be straight and simply followed the stream. Outside, the evening did what evenings in Hollowmere always did: settled like silt. No wind moved the shutters. No weather came over the rim of the hills, because no weather could—the valley kept its sky the way a miser keeps coins, close and unchanging. That was the point of the place. Exiles came here to be forgotten, and the valley obliged so thoroughly that even the clouds declined to remember them.

Isandro had not been exiled from anything, as far as he knew. He had simply arrived, eleven years ago, with a satchel of tools and no memory of choosing to come, and the valley had folded him in like a leaf pressed between pages. He sometimes thought he should find that alarming. Mostly he found it restful.

Tonight, though, restlessness sat in his chest like a swallowed moth, and he knew exactly why, and knowing made it worse.

Wendell Almeida. The storm-caller who wasn't allowed to call storms. The tall, awkward exile from the flower stall who had looked at Isandro across three weeks of small purchases—a single stem here, a bundle of dried lavender there, purchases that made no horticultural sense—and finally said, with the desperate courage of someone stepping off a ledge, *Would you have dinner with me? Tomorrow? There's a table at the Gilded Perch and I've been told the trout is edible.*

*Tomorrow* was now today. Today was now nearly tonight. And Isandro had said yes with a speed that embarrassed him every time he replayed it.

He bent over the book and told himself to think about leather instead. Leather was reliable. Leather did not make his hands sweat.

The first signature came free easily enough—he lifted the old paste with a bone folder and warm breath, working along the spine until the quire sighed loose into his palms. He checked the collation by habit, counting leaves, checking for loss, and it was during this counting that he saw the ink.

It was on the verso of the third leaf, in the margin where a reader a hundred years dead might have left a note about weather or grain prices. But this was not a century-old note. This ink sat *on top of* the page's age like frost on old stone—bright, wet-looking, black as a pupil. And it read:

*The binder will find this line at dusk, with lamp-oil smell on his hands and doubt in his throat.*

Isandro put the book down. Then, because putting it down felt absurd—he was a grown man alone in his own shop, and books did not write back—he picked it up again.

He tested the ink with the tip of one finger. Dry. Perfectly dry, and yet it had the depth of wetness, the way a well looks deep even in daylight. He held the leaf to the lamp. The light passed through the paper and the words stayed dark, which was wrong; iron gall ink went brown and translucent with age, and this was neither brown nor translucent nor aged. It was new. It was new in a way that made his teeth ache.

*The binder,* he read again. Him. Obviously him. Which meant someone had written this recently, knowing he'd be the one to restore the book—which was possible; commissions traveled by word of mouth, and half the valley knew where Wendell Almeida ate lunch. Someone with a fine hand and a cruel sense of humor.

Except the hand. He turned the leaf toward the light and studied the script properly, and the humor drained out of the room.

It was a beautiful hand. It was a *familiar* hand. The downstrokes carried weight on the left and feathered off to the right; the ascenders leaned just slightly, like grass after wind; there was a particular way the writer crossed their t's—in short, quick strokes, slightly high—that Isandro had seen every day for eleven years, because it was the way *he* wrote. It was his own handwriting, aged backward. His hand as it might have been before eleven years of mending and tea and quiet had softened it.

He did not write prophecies. He had certainly never written this one. He had never, insofar as he knew, been anywhere near whatever distant place produced a book bound in oak and fear.

"Someone is playing a joke," he said aloud, because the shop was very quiet and his voice needed the company.

The book disagreed.

As he watched—as he stood there with the lamp burning and the moth still beating in his chest—the ink *moved*. Not smeared. Not bled. It gathered, the way iron filings gather to a magnet, and re-formed, letter by letter, into a new sentence below the first:

*At the seventh bell he will sit across from the storm-caller and pretend his hands are steady. They will not be.*

The shop seemed to tilt. Isandro sat down on his stool without deciding to. The seventh bell was the dinner bell at the Gilded Perch; everyone in Hollowmere knew the bells the way they knew their own pulse, six for supper's end and seven for the hour when the lamplighters made their round. Tonight. It was writing about *tonight*. About Wendell. About his unsteady hands, which were, at this exact moment, extremely unsteady.

He should stop reading. He understood that distantly, the way one understands one shouldn't look directly at an eclipse. He turned the leaf anyway.

More text had surfaced on the next page—not in the margins now but across the body of some older, scraped-away prophecy, filling the hollowed spaces like water filling footprints. The original text beneath was illegible, deliberately effaced, but the new ink didn't seem to care. It lay over the ruins and wrote its own story:

*They will meet beneath a roof of smoked glass and call it chance. It is not chance. Nothing in this valley is chance; the valley is a held breath, and breath must be released.*

*The thorn remembers what the valley forgets.*

*When the storm-caller laughs—truly laughs, the first true laugh in nine years—the page will open, and the page is a door, and the door has teeth.*

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. He read the lines four times. *The thorn remembers what the valley forgets.* He looked around his shop—at the shelves of mended books, the jars of paste and pigment, the little brass weather-glass on the windowsill that had pointed to *change* for eleven years without ever once being right—and felt the valley's famous peace suddenly reveal itself as something else entirely. Not peace. Suspension. A pond so still because nothing in it was allowed to move.

And Wendell—quiet Wendell, who bought single flowers and apologized to furniture he bumped into—was a storm-caller. Everyone knew. It was the kind of thing everyone knew and no one mentioned, the way no one mentioned why any of them lived here. Nine years since Wendell had come down out of the pass with burned sleeves and an empty look, and in nine years Isandro had never once heard him laugh. Smile, yes. The particular wry almost-smile he aimed at his own clumsiness. But laugh—

Isandro realized he wanted, very badly, to be the reason Wendell laughed. And the book had just told him that wanting it was a trap.

"No," he said to the book. "No, you don't get to—" He stopped, hearing himself argue with a object, and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

When he lowered them, the ink had written again.

*The scribe watches. The scribe has always watched. Ask the scribe what he paid.*

He went cold from the scalp down. *The scribe.* He looked at his own hands—ink-stained, capable, ordinary—and felt them become evidence. Because the handwriting was his. However impossible, however cruel a trick, the hand in the book was his hand, and the book knew it, and the book was taunting him with a debt he could not remember incurring.

There was a knock at the shop door.

He startled hard enough to knock the bone folder off the bench. Through the fogged glass stood a tall silhouette holding something long and thin—a flower, he realized as he crossed the room on legs that felt borrowed. Wendell stood on his doorstep at dusk with a white lily and an expression of terrified hope, and behind him the valley sky did its usual nothing, and somewhere in the world beyond the hills Isandro understood, with total certainty, that weather was gathering.

"Wendell," he said, opening the door. "You're early."

"I was—" Wendell looked at the lily, at his own boots, anywhere but Isandro's face. "I couldn't sit still. Is that strange? That's strange. I can put the flower back if—"

"It's lovely." And it was; and Wendell's face when he said so was lovelier; and Isandro felt the trap close around his heart with a gentleness that was somehow worse than teeth. "Give me two minutes to close up."

Behind him, on the bench, in the lamplight, the book waited. As he shrugged on his coat he saw, in his peripheral vision, fresh ink gleaming wet on the open page—one final line, added while his back was turned:

*He goes willingly. That is the tragedy. That was always the tragedy.*

Isandro closed the shop. He did not lock the book away. He did not burn it, though his hands shook with the wish to. Some part of him—the part with the scribe's hand, the part that had paid something once—knew that burning it would change nothing except to leave them unwarned.

The Gilded Perch smelled of woodsmoke and trout. They got the corner table under the smoked-glass lamp, and Wendell told a story about a customer who'd tried to buy flowers for a funeral using turnips, and Isandro laughed—then caught himself, appalled, because Wendell was laughing too, really laughing, head tipped back, nine years of gravity cracking open all at once—

—and in Isandro's satchel, where he had sworn he would not bring it and had brought it anyway, the book opened itself with a sound like a sigh, and the pages began, softly, hungrily, to turn.
