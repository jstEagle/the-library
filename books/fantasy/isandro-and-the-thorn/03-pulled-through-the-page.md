## 3. Pulled Through the Page

The valley had no weather worth the name.

That was the first thing Wendell had noticed when they arrived seven years ago, half-dead and wholly silent, carried down the pass by a caravan of exiles who asked no questions because they had none of their own answers either. The sky above Thornhollow was a ceiling of pale wool that never tore, never thundered, never offered so much as a gust of honest wind. Rain came when it rained—softly, apologetically, like a guest who knew they weren't wanted—and the air hung still as water in a sealed jar.

Wendell had learned to love it. Or to need it, which felt like the same thing from a certain distance.

Tonight, standing before the spotted mirror in their rented room above the tannery, they hated it with every nerve in their body.

"You look like a person," Wendell told their reflection. "A perfectly ordinary person going on a perfectly ordinary evening walk to eat bread and cheese with a bookbinder."

The reflection looked dubious. It had good reason to. Wendell Almeida owned three shirts, all of them the color of old porridge, and the one they'd chosen—the least porridge-colored—had a mended seam at the collar that Isandro would almost certainly notice, because Isandro Veyra noticed everything made by hands. He'd once paused mid-conversation at the market stall to turn over a stranger's satchel and murmur, *saddle stitch, but rushed*, in the reverent tone other people saved for church bells.

Seven years of nothing. Seven years of the valley's soft gray quiet pressing down on whatever it was Wendell used to be, and then a man with ink-stained fingers and a voice like a closed book being opened carefully had asked them to dinner, and Wendell's heart had done something it hadn't done since the world still had weather in it.

It had *crackled*.

They'd stood there in the market square holding two pounds of flour they didn't remember picking up, feeling that faint static lift the fine hairs on their arms, and thought: *oh no.* And also: *oh yes.* And mostly: *don't you dare.*

Magic did not work in Thornhollow. That was the whole point of the place. The valley was a cupped hand around a candleflame of ordinary life, and whatever Wendell had been before—whatever word the exiles' ledgers used, though Wendell had never dared look up their own entry—could not follow them past the ridge. They'd tested it exactly once, in their first terrified winter, standing in the frozen creek bed with their palms out and tears freezing on their lashes, calling the way they'd apparently always called, the way a lung calls for breath.

Nothing. Not a spark. Just silence, and the strange mercy of it, and the strange grief.

So the crackle tonight was impossible. Which meant, probably, that it wasn't magic at all. It was just what happened to a body when hope got into it after too long without. A chemical weather. Nothing more.

Wendell buttoned the porridge-colored shirt to the throat, took one last look at the stranger in the mirror—a thin face, dark eyes, hair that had given up on any style years ago—and went out into the windless evening to meet Isandro Veyra for bread and cheese, and possibly the beginning of a life.

* * *

Isandro had set the table in the back room of his bindery, among the presses and the paper stock and the sweet animal smell of leather and glue, because—as he'd said, blushing faintly—"the light's better here." It wasn't better. It was a single oil lamp and a candle stub. But it fell gold across the workbench he'd cleared and covered with a linen cloth, and across the bread, and the hard yellow cheese, and the two cups of cider, and most of all across Isandro himself, who sat with his hands folded like a man at prayer and watched Wendell come through the door as though Wendell were something rare that had wandered in out of a storm.

"You came," he said.

"You asked."

"People ask me things all the time. I mend their books, I bind their letters. Nobody asks *me* anywhere." He poured the cider. His hands were steady; his eyes were not. "I wasn't sure you would."

"Why not?"

"Because you have the look of someone keeping a great many doors closed." He said it gently, without accusation, the way he might note a cracked spine or foxed pages. "And I've learned not to take that personally. But I hoped."

Wendell sat. The bench creaked. Somewhere in the walls the building settled its old bones, and outside, the windless night pressed close against the shutters.

"I don't talk about before," Wendell said. "The time before here. You should know that now, at the start, so you can decide if this is—" They gestured vaguely at the bread, the candle, themselves. "—worth your trouble."

"I'm not interested in before." Isandro broke the loaf and gave them the larger half without appearing to think about it. "Before is dead paper. I'm interested in what gets written next."

Something in Wendell's chest pulled tight as a binding cord. They ate. The cheese was sharp, the cider was rough, the lamplight was kind, and slowly—degree by degree, like frost melting off a window—the knot of dread in their shoulders loosened. Isandro talked about his craft: the secret that old vellum remembered pressure, that a palimpsest could be coaxed into giving back what was scraped away, that every book was a held breath. Wendell talked about nothing—about the tannery, about the quality of the autumn apples, about a dog they liked—and found that nothing, said to Isandro, sounded more like something than anything they'd ever managed before.

The candle burned low. At some point their hands ended up near each other on the table, not touching, occupying adjacent territory like two nations negotiating an amicable border.

Then Isandro said, quietly, "There's something I should show you," and reached beneath the workbench.

The book he set between them was ancient and wrong. Wrong the way a smile is wrong on a corpse. Its cover was black calfskin gone greenish at the edges, and its title—if it had ever borne one—had been effaced so thoroughly the leather bore a smooth depression, like a scar where a name had been cut out. The pages, when Isandro opened it, were the color of ivory left too long in tea.

"It came in last month," he said. "No owner, no provenance. Found walled up in a cellar in the old town. There are texts like this sometimes—prophecy cycles, covenant records, things from before the Sundering. Mostly gibberish. This one..." His finger traced the margin of the open page. "This one's been changing."

"Books don't change."

"This one does. Three days ago it described a rain we haven't had. Yesterday it named a woman in Marrowgate who died that morning. And today—" He turned the page fully toward Wendell. "Today it says this."

The lamp flickered. Wendell leaned in.

The ink was fresh. That was the horror of it—not faded, not ancient, but wet-black and sharp-edged, as though written minutes ago in a steady, beautiful hand. And it described a small room above a bindery. A linen cloth. Bread and cheese. Two cups of cider. A mended seam at a collar. It described all of it, down to the exact words *before is dead paper*, and below that, in the same unhurried script:

*Two names enter. One name remembers. The thorn wakes where the covenant broke, and the Sundered door swings on the hinge of a storm.*

Beneath that, two names, side by side:

**Isandro Veyra. Wendell Almeida.**

The room went very quiet. Quiet the way the valley was quiet—that sealed-jar stillness—but closer, tighter, as if the walls had drawn a breath and meant never to let it out.

"No," Wendell said.

"Wendell—"

"That's not possible. Names aren't written down. Names aren't *known*. That's why people come here—" Their voice climbed. "Who told it? Who wrote this? Because someone wrote it, someone's hand—"

"Look at the hand," Isandro whispered. He was very pale. "Look closely. Tell me whose hand that is."

And Wendell looked—at the letterforms, the slant, the peculiar hooked ascenders—and understood that they recognized it the way you recognize your own reflection in an unexpected mirror. It was Isandro's hand. Of course it was. Every day of it, every curve.

But it was aged somehow. Weathered. As if the same hand, grown a hundred years older, had reached back through the paper.

The candle went out.

Not guttered—*went*, snuffed by a cold that dropped through the room like a stone through water, and in the dark Wendell heard a sound that did not belong to any world with bread and cider in it: a creaking, a groaning, the sound of a great woody thing flexing joints it had not moved in a century.

On the table, the prophecy book began to grow thorns.

They erupted from the spine—black bramble, glossy and wet-looking, barbed like fishhooks—and the pages whipped open all at once, fanning faster and faster until the flutter became a roar, and the smell hit Wendell like a fist: loam and lightning and something rotten-sweet underneath, the smell of a memory going bad.

"Isandro—"

He was reaching for the book—he was always reaching for books, it was in his bones—and a tendril snapped around his wrist like a manacle. He cried out. Wendell grabbed him, both hands, meaning only to pull him free, meaning only the simplest thing in the world—

—and the static came back.

It came back like a dam breaking. It rose up through the floor, through the soles of their feet, through the marrow of them, seven years of held breath releasing all at once, and Wendell's whole body lit with a power they had no memory of learning and could not have forgotten if they tried: the storm. *Their* storm. It sang in their teeth, arced blue-white between their fingers where they gripped Isandro's arm, and for one incandescent instant terror and joy were the same feeling, indistinguishable, enormous—

The brambles struck.

They came fast as falling timber, wrapped them both—wrist, waist, shoulder, the barbs biting through porridge-colored linen—and hauled. The workbench overturned. The cider cups shattered. The last thing Wendell saw of Thornhollow was the lamp, upright and burning serenely amid the wreckage, illuminating a room suddenly empty of everything except the smell of ozone and the torn-open dark where a door had been.

Then there was no room at all.

They fell through a darkness shot with veins of violet light, tumbling, brambles cinching tighter, Isandro's hand finding Wendell's and gripping hard enough to grind bone. Wind screamed past them—real wind, wild wind, wind with weather in it—and Wendell felt their power rise to meet it, answering like calls to like, and beneath the terror a treacherous, singing exhilaration uncoiled in their chest, vast as the sky they were plummeting through.

*Don't enjoy this,* Wendell told themselves. *You're being dragged into hell by a haunted weed. Do not enjoy this.*

Below them—below, now, the fall resolving into direction—the violet dark parted like curtains, revealing a horizon broken into floating shards of land, forests hanging upside down from the undersides of mountains, rivers running silver through empty air. A realm in pieces. A sky full of sundered country.

The thorn-cage hurtled toward the largest fragment, toward a coastline of gray ash where a forest of black bramble stood waiting, miles wide, thorns the size of ship's masts, and at its heart something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to pay attention.

Wendell called the lightning without deciding to. It came down their arms like coming home.
