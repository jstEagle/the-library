## 4. The Court of Broken Oaths

The road to the fae court was paved with things that had forgotten what they were.

Isandro noticed it first in the stones beneath his boots—river-smooth once, he guessed, but now they hummed faintly with a wrongness, as if each one was trying to remember being water and failing. Then the trees: birches that had shed their bark in long curls, pale and naked, their branches reaching not upward but *inward*, toward some memory at their own center. The air smelled of ozone and old paper, a combination that made his chest ache in a way he could not name.

Wendell walked half a step ahead, one hand raised loosely at their side, fingers twitching. Storm-caller's habit, Isandro thought. Even here, where the sky hung low and bruised but utterly silent, they were listening for thunder.

"You keep looking at your hands," Wendell said, without turning around.

"I'm checking I still have them." He flexed his fingers. They were his—ink-stained knuckles, the crooked index finger from a childhood break he'd never actually had, or couldn't remember having. That was the trouble. Since the thorn had pulled them through, every memory felt like a book rebound by someone else's hands. The boards were right. The text was right. But the stitching was wrong somehow, and he kept waiting for pages to fall out.

"The court's close," Wendell said. "Can't you feel it? Like standing before an open oven."

Isandro could feel it, though it wasn't heat he sensed but *attention*—the weight of something vast turning its regard toward them, the way a reader turns toward a page when the story finally reaches the good part. His skin prickled. Somewhere behind his ribs, under the bone, there was a pulling sensation, thin and taut as thread.

He did not tell Wendell about the handwriting.

He had told them everything else, or nearly: the book in his workshop, the prophecy that had rewritten itself overnight, fresh ink describing a dinner date at Marisol's—the candle stub, the rain that never came, the exact words Wendell would say when nervous, which were *I'm not nervous*. He had shown them how the ink moved while they watched, letters crawling like ants, the passage about tonight growing longer even as they read it. He had watched Wendell go pale, then furious, then—strangely, wonderfully—resolute, planting both feet on the floorboards of his shop and declaring that if some cursed book wanted to ruin their evening, it could at least have the decency to do it after dessert.

But he had not said: *This is my handwriting. I know the way my own pen drags on its downstrokes. I wrote this a hundred years ago, and I don't remember doing it.*

Some truths were too heavy to carry across a border. And they were approaching a border now—he could feel the seam of the world ahead, a place where the air changed texture like fabric worn through.

The trees ended all at once, as though cut by shears.

Beyond lay a valley of glass. Not a metaphor—a literal plain of black glass stretching to the horizon, mirror-flat, reflecting a sky that did not match the one above. Overhead, clouds rolled grey and sullen; below, in the glass, stars burned in constellations Isandro had never seen, wheeling slowly around a darkness shaped like a wound. At the plain's center rose a palace—or the idea of a palace, its spires built from what looked like fused antlers and frozen lightning, its walls shifting between transparency and opacity as though the building itself could not decide whether to exist.

Bridges of woven moonlight arced from the shore to its gates. On the nearest bridge stood a figure, tall and still, wearing robes the color of dusk falling on snow.

"That'll be our welcome," Wendell murmured. Their hand drifted toward Isandro's—not quite touching, hovering near his sleeve the way a compass needle hovers near north. "Stay behind me if anything happens."

"Anything happens," Isandro repeated. "You mean if the giant glass desert tries to eat us."

"It's not trying," Wendell said grimly. "That's the thing about this place. Nothing here *tries*. It just is, and you adapt or you don't." They started onto the bridge, and the moonlight took their weight without complaint, rippling outward from each footstep in slow silver rings.

The figure on the bridge resolved as they approached into something almost human, almost not. A woman, perhaps, or the memory of one—her face beautiful the way a sentence is beautiful, precise and deliberate and slightly inhuman in its perfection. Her eyes were the problem. They held no whites, only a deep liquid dark, and in that dark swam small drifting lights like plankton in midnight seas.

"Two mortals walk uninvited," she said. Her voice was layered, three voices speaking a half-beat apart. "One who calls storms and does not remember why she swore never to call them again. One who—" She stopped. Her gaze fixed on Isandro, and the lights in her eyes went suddenly still, all of them at once, like fish startled by a shadow.

Isandro's mouth went dry. *She knows.* The thought arrived cold and complete. *She can see it stitched into me.*

"One who carries a debt older than his bones," she finished smoothly, and Isandro could not tell whether the pause had been mercy or cruelty. "Come. The Court of Broken Oaths receives you, because it must. Prophecies are binding contracts here, and yours has already been signed."

They followed her across the bridge. Below, in the glass, the false stars wheeled, and Isandro caught glimpses of other reflections that should not have been there: a city burning, a field of white flowers, a hand holding a quill over parchment. He looked away. Looking too long at reflections in the Sundered Realms, he suspected, was a way of letting them in.

* * *

The throne room of the fae court was a cathedral of suspended moments.

Chandeliers of captured lightning hung motionless overhead, each bolt frozen mid-branch. Fountains ran with liquid silver that fell upward. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks—tall folk with moth-winged shoulders, small folk with eyes like coins, beings whose faces were smooth as eggs until they chose otherwise—and every one of them turned to watch the two mortals enter with an attention so total it felt physical, a pressure against the skin.

At the chamber's heart, upon a throne grown from a single enormous bramble—black, thorned, and yet somehow *dormant*, its barbs sheathed in crystal—sat the ruler of the court.

It had been a king once, perhaps. Now it was mostly absence: a silhouette of regal bearing with nothing inside it, a coat of shadows shaped like a man, wearing a crown of white iron. When it spoke, its voice came from everywhere, including, unpleasantly, from inside Isandro's own skull.

"Wendell Almeida." The shadow-king rose. "And the Scribe."

The word landed in the silence like a stone in a well. Isandro heard it fall all the way down.

"I'm sorry?" he said. "I think you've mistaken me for someone else."

"Mistaken." The crown tilted, considering him. "No. Though you may be forgiven for hoping so. You hoped so hard you buried the hope along with everything else. That is what payment costs, little Scribe. That is what it has always cost."

Wendell's head turned slowly toward him. In the frozen lightning-light, their face was carefully blank, the way faces go blank right before they stop being blank forever. "Isandro. What's it talking about?"

"Later," Isandro said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Please. Whatever this is—later. First I want to know what we're standing in."

The shadow-king laughed, and the sound had no warmth in it at all, only the echo of warmth, a recording of laughter played in an empty hall. "A mortal asks the right question at last. Very well. Look upon the covenant, then, and understand what broke."

It gestured, and the air above the throne split open like a curtain.

What hung within was a vast tapestry of light, or had been one. Isandro saw it whole for only an instant before understanding it was broken beyond any whole: two great rivers of radiance, one warm gold and one cool silver, braided together down the length of the vision—the human world and the fae, bound strand over strand. Along the braid ran words. He could not read them at distance, but he knew them anyway, knew them the way you know a song you learned before speech:

*By leaf and ledger, by oath and ink, let neither realm forget the other.*

"A century ago," the shadow-king said, "the covenant was shattered. Not frayed. Not weakened. Shattered—one clean stroke, from the mortal side, by one of your own kind. A storm-caller of terrible gift, who struck the binding at its heart." The silhouette's emptiness seemed to deepen. "The covenant did not simply fail. It tore, and in tearing, it *infected*. From the wound grew the Thorn—a hunger given root, that feeds on what binds worlds together. And what binds worlds together, mortals and fae alike, is memory."

"The Thorn eats memories," Wendell said quietly. It was not a question. Isandro glanced sideways and saw their hand pressed flat against their sternum, as though holding a door shut.

"The Thorn eats memories," the king confirmed. "It began at the tear and has spread ever since, a bramble of forgetting creeping through both realms. Villages wake unable to name their children. Rivers forget their courses and lie down in the dust. My court survives because we are creatures of oath rather than recollection—but oaths, even ours, grow brittle without remembering why they were sworn. A century more, perhaps less, and there will be nothing left anywhere except the Thorn, which remembers nothing, wants nothing, and therefore cannot be reasoned with, bargained with, or killed."

Silence, except for the upward-falling fountains.

"And us?" Wendell asked. "Why does your prophecy want *us*?"

"The prophecy does not want you. The prophecy *requires* you." The shadow-king drifted closer, and up close its absence was worse than any face would have been—an outline of power with a hole where a person should be. "The covenant can be reforged. There is a Forge, old as the first oath, at the world's broken heart. What feeds the Forge is sacrifice freely offered—memory, willingly surrendered, burned as fuel to bind the strands anew. This was known to those who foresaw the need. Those who set events in motion a hundred years past, so that when the hour came, instruments would exist to meet it."

Instruments. The word sat in Isandro's stomach like swallowed glass. Beside him, Wendell had gone very still, and he recognized the stillness—it was the same quality as the silence before lightning, the air gathering itself to strike.

"You're saying," Wendell said, each word placed like a foot on ice, "that a hundred years ago, someone arranged for a storm-caller to be born, exiled, and delivered here. Someone *made* us for this."

"I am saying the Thorn devours memory, Wendell Almeida, and the Forge demands it, and the prophecy names you both by name. Draw your conclusions. The court does not deal in comfort."

"And the storm-caller who shattered the covenant," Isandro said. The question left him before he could weigh it, pulled out by the same taut thread behind his ribs. "Who were they?"

Every light in the chamber dimmed. The courtiers did not move, but the quality of their stillness changed—from attentive to *afraid*.

The shadow-king regarded him for a long moment. When it answered, its voice was almost gentle, which was somehow the most frightening thing it had done all evening.

"Now *that* is an interesting question, Scribe. Because the records say the betrayer's name was struck from every page, every mind, every stone it was carved upon—the price of their exile, paid to bury the act. No one remembers who broke the world." The empty crown inclined. "Except, of course, whoever struck the names out. And whoever *wrote them down in the first place*."

The thread behind Isandro's ribs pulled tight enough to hurt.

Beside him, Wendell's hand found his sleeve at last—not hovering now, but gripping, knuckles white, anchoring themselves to him as though he were the last solid thing in a dissolving room. Their eyes, when they turned to him, held no accusation yet. Only the question, patient and terrible, that he had been dreading since the moment he'd seen fresh ink crawling across century-old vellum:

*Isandro. What aren't you telling me?*

Above the throne, the great torn covenant shuddered in its frame of air, and somewhere far away—too far to see, too close to ignore—something with ten thousand roots turned its hunger toward the court, and began, patiently, to grow.
