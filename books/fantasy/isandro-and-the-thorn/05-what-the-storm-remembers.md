## 5. What the Storm Remembers

The Ashmoor did not begin all at once. That was the cruelest part of it.

One moment they were walking through the last green fringe of the Sundered Realms—moss soft underfoot, air thick with the smell of wet stone and something almost like rain—and then, without ceremony, the world simply gave up. The moss thinned to grey. The grey became ash. And ahead of them stretched a plain of cinders and bone-white dust that ran to a horizon the color of an old bruise, featureless except for the crooked spines of petrified trees rising here and there like the ribs of drowned ships.

Wendell stood at its edge and felt their skin remember.

"This is where storms go to die," they said quietly.

Isandro, walking half a step behind as he always did—bookbinder's habit, keeping his hands free and his body between others and whatever was on the table—came up beside them. He had the prophecy book slung across his chest in a harness he'd improvised from his own belt, and his knuckles were white against the leather.

"You've been here before."

It wasn't a question, but Wendell answered it anyway, because not answering felt like lying. "I don't know. That's the whole problem, isn't it? My feet know this place. My lungs know it." They breathed in, and the ash tasted of ozone and old grief. "I just don't know *why*."

They walked.

The Ashmoor swallowed sound the way the valley swallowed magic. Their footsteps made no echo; Isandro's breathing seemed to happen somewhere far away. Overhead, the sky churned with a pressure that had nothing to do with weather—a low, bruised ceiling of cloud that never broke, never rained, only *waited*. Wendell could feel it pressing against the sealed doors inside them, against the place where the storm-calling lived, locked and rusted and aching like an old break in winter.

Three days into the crossing, the first memory came.

* * *

It happened because they needed water, and the water was poisoned, and the only way to clean it was the one thing Wendell had sworn off for a decade.

"Just a little," Isandro said, kneeling at the edge of a black pool ringed with salt crusts. He'd dipped a strip of linen in it and was watching it darken. "There's rot in it. We drink this, we'll be seeing things by morning."

"I know what's in it."

"Then you know what I'm asking."

Wendell looked at the sky—that waiting, bruised ceiling—and hated how familiar the gesture was, tipping their face up like a farmer reading a field. Ten years in the valley, ten years of dampening salves and iron charms and never once letting the lightning touch them. Ten years of being small on purpose.

But Isandro was looking at them with those steady dark eyes, and there was no pity in them, which was somehow worse, and Wendell thought: *I would rather be dangerous than useless in front of him.*

So they reached for the door.

It opened easier than it should have. That frightened them more than anything—the lock they'd imagined rusted shut swung wide at a thought, and the storm came down through them like light through glass. Not out of the sky; there was no rain here to call. Out of *them*. Their hands went silver-blue at the fingertips, and when they pressed both palms flat to the black pool, the charge sang through the water and boiled the rot away in a bloom of steam and the smell of lightning-struck earth.

And in the flash—

*a hall of white pillars, and someone screaming their name, and their own voice answering, cold as a struck bell:* ***Let him burn.***

Wendell staggered back from the pool. The steam was still rising. Isandro was on his feet, reaching for them, saying their name, and Wendell heard it from very far away, because underneath his voice another voice was still echoing, their own voice, saying words they could not have said, could not have—

"Wendell."

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding." He took their hand and turned it over. Their palms were scored with thin burns, Lichtenberg lines feathering up the wrists, beautiful and terrible, the mark of current passed through flesh. Wendell stared at them and felt nothing in the hand at all.

"It does that," they said. "It used to do that." A pause, and the lie came out smooth and awful: "I don't remember why I stopped."

* * *

The second memory came two nights later, and it did not wait for a storm.

They had made camp in the hollow of a petrified tree, a cavern of mineralized wood wide enough for two bedrolls and a small fire that burned strangely colorless in the Ashmoor air. Isandro slept badly these days—they both did—but tonight his breathing had finally gone deep and even, and Wendell lay watching the colorless flames and let their mind drift toward the horizon.

The dream took them like a hand closing around the throat.

*Rain on marble steps. A city of white towers under a sky full of thunderheads stacked like siege engines. And a figure running toward them through the downpour—young, laughing, calling* ***Wendell! Wendell, they'll listen, we can still make them listen*** *—and Wendell, in the dream, turning away. Turning away with a sigil already burning on their palm, the seal-sigil, the exile-mark, and speaking the words that would close the valley's borders forever behind them.*

*"Not everything can be fixed," dream-Wendell said. "Some things have to be buried."*

*"Then bury me too," the young man said, and stopped laughing.*

Wendell woke with ash in their mouth and their heart slamming and the fire dead, though the wood beneath it was untouched and cold. Across the camp, Isandro sat up instantly—he always woke like that, all at once, as if sleep were a room he merely visited—and said nothing, only watched them with an expression Wendell couldn't read in the dark.

"A bad dream," Wendell said.

"Yes," Isandro said softly. "I get those too."

There was something in the way he said it. Something weighted. But the Ashmoor night was vast and cold, and Wendell was too busy being afraid of themselves to pull at the thread.

Because here was what the dream had left behind, sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone: the young man in the rain had been *real*. Not a symbol, not a fear-shape. Real, with a chipped front tooth and a scar through one eyebrow, and Wendell had known his name once, had loved him or betrayed him or both, and had sealed a door on his face forever.

*I exiled myself,* Wendell thought, staring at the colorless coals. *No one banished me. I wrote the sentence and served it. What did I do that was so heavy I chose to forget carrying it?*

* * *

On the fifth day, the Thorn found them.

It came out of the ground without warning—a single black bramble, thick as a man's arm, erupting from the ash thirty paces ahead and rearing up like a serpent, thorns gleaming wetly in the dead light. It didn't move like a plant. It moved like hunger. Where its shadow fell, Wendell's thoughts went soft-edged and slippery, and they lost three seconds trying to remember why they were walking anywhere at all.

"Run?" Isandro suggested.

"Run," Wendell agreed, and they ran.

The bramble pursued at a lope, splitting the ash behind it, and more of it rose ahead of them, herding, herding, until the plain around them was a cage of thorn and the air itself had begun to taste of forgetting. Wendell could feel pieces of themselves flaking loose—*the name of a teacher, the smell of bread, the color of the valley in autumn*—and understood, with cold clarity, that if it touched them they would not die. They would simply stop having ever been.

Which meant there was exactly one option left, and it was the one they'd spent ten years refusing.

"Get behind me," Wendell said. "And whatever you see—whatever I look like while I do this—don't decide who I am yet. Promise me."

Isandro's jaw tightened. "Wendell—"

"*Promise.*"

"...I promise."

Wendell turned to face the Thorn and unlocked every door at once.

The storm that came out of them was not weather. It was a decade of dammed grief given voltage. Lightning sheeted across the Ashmoor in forks that struck upward from the ground, and the clouds overhead finally, finally broke—not with rain but with a roar of wind that flattened the thorn-bramble like grass before a scythe. Wendell walked into the middle of their own tempest, hair lifting, eyes blazing white-blue, and the Thorn recoiled, shrieking in a register below sound.

And the memories came with the storm. All of them. At once.

*A courtroom of white pillars. Their own hands signing a treaty with fae ink that drank the light. The young man with the chipped tooth standing beside them—no, opposing them—pleading—*

*Betrayal. The word arrived without details, a verdict without evidence, and it fit their shape perfectly, the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.*

*Their own voice, formal and broken:* ***I, Wendell Almeida, storm-caller of the Third Covenant, do abjure my name and my works—***

The Thorn screamed and drove forward through the gale, one great limb lancing toward Isandro, and Wendell threw the lightning sideways, caught the bramble mid-strike, and *held* it, muscles of light straining against muscle of shadow, the two of them locked in a stalemate of hunger and fury.

And in that locked moment, the last fragment surfaced—small, quiet, worse than all the rest:

*A table. An open book. Fresh ink drying. And their own hand guiding another hand—guiding Isandro's hand—as it wrote the opening line of a prophecy neither of them had finished.*

The vision shattered. The Thorn, seared to its root, collapsed into ash and was still.

The storm died. Wendell dropped to their knees in the cinders, shaking, palms scored with new silver scars, and Isandro was there, catching them, holding most of their weight without comment or complaint.

"The Covenant," Wendell rasped. "I helped write it. Or unwrite it. Isandro, I *broke* something—I broke it with—" They stopped. The fragment would not resolve further; it slid away like smoke through fingers. But one detail remained, sharp as a splinter: the feel of guiding someone else's pen. The warmth of a shoulder against theirs. The particular stillness of a person who listened.

They looked up at Isandro—at the soot on his cheek, the book harness across his chest, the eyes that held theirs with a steadiness no stranger's should.

"Why do I keep seeing your hands?" Wendell whispered.

Isandro went very still.

It lasted only a heartbeat—an ordinary traveler's pause, a man considering a question about distance or weather. But Wendell had spent ten years learning to read the silences of people hiding things, because they were one of them, and in that heartbeat they saw it: recognition. Not surprise. *Recognition*, old and guilty and grieving, passing behind Isandro's eyes like a cloud over the Ashmoor sun.

"Because," Isandro said carefully, "you've seen me write. In the shop. You watched me work."

"That's not it." Wendell rose slowly out of his grip, legs unsteady, heart pounding with something colder than fear. "That dream I told you about—the young man in the rain. When I tried to picture his face just now, it wouldn't come. But his *hands* came. His hands, holding a pen." They stepped closer, and the ash swirled between them. "Isandro. Whose handwriting is in that book?"

The wind moved. Somewhere behind them, far across the plain, another bramble breached the surface, and then another, a forest of black spires rising against the bruised horizon—too many now, and all of them turning, slow as compass needles, toward the two small figures standing in the ash.

Isandro's hand drifted, helpless and involuntary, to the book at his chest.

"We should run," he said.

"Whose handwriting, Isandro?"

He closed his eyes. "Mine," he said. "It's mine. It's been mine for a hundred years."

And the Thorn began to move.
