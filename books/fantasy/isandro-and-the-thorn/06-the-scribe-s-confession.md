## 6. The Scribe's Confession

The Thorn found them at dusk, in the ruins of a chapel that had forgotten it was ever consecrated.

Isandro knew the moment before it came — the way he always knew, in the small hours of restoration work, when a book was about to betray him. The air thickened. The light went the color of old varnish. And beneath his breastbone, where his soul had been stitched into paper a hundred years ago, something began to *pull*.

"Wendell." His voice came out smaller than he intended. "We need to move."

Wendell stood at the chapel's broken altar, hands raised, coaxing a storm out of a sky that had none to give. They had grown better at it across the Ashmoor — frighteningly better, as though every mile they traveled peeled back another layer of whatever the valley had buried in them. Lightning answered them now in threads and whispers, coiling down their arms like silver ivy.

"It's coming from the east," Wendell said, not turning. "I can feel it in my teeth."

"That's what worries me. It's not supposed to be able to feel you at all yet."

But the Thorn could. Isandro had known that for days, had watched the bramble-shadows bend toward Wendell like iron filings toward a lodestone, and had said nothing. He was growing very good at saying nothing. A century of practice.

The first tendril came through the floor.

It rose between the shattered flagstones like a black vine given thought — no, worse than thought: given *hunger*. It moved without haste, tasting the air, tasting the dust, tasting the place where memories had soaked into stone over centuries until even ghosts had nothing left. Where it touched, the chapel forgot itself. An archway sagged, suddenly unsure it had ever held a roof. A saint's statue went faceless, its features smoothed away like wet clay.

Wendell turned, and the storm came up with them — a wall of crackling blue-white that struck the tendril and split it into smoking ribbons. But more came. Through the walls. Up from the crypt below. From everywhere and nowhere, because the Thorn was not a creature; it was a *lack*, and lack has no edges to strike.

"Isandro!" Wendell's voice cracked like the lightning did. "What is this? Why does it keep finding *us*?"

He should have lied. He had been lying so long it should have come easily, smooth as paste and glue, the bookbinder's craft applied to his own history. Instead he heard himself say:

"It finds us because I wrote it here."

The words fell into the ruined chapel like stones into a well. Even the Thorn seemed to pause, tendrils quivering mid-reach, as if the truth itself were something it might feed on.

* * *

They ended up cornered in the bell tower — or what remained of one, a spiral of stairs ascending into a roofless dark, with the Thorn climbing after them slow as spilled ink. There was nowhere left to run, and Isandro discovered that when there is nowhere left to run, the body simply stops trying, and everything you have swallowed for a century rises all at once.

Wendell pressed their back to the tower wall, storm-light guttering around their knuckles, spent and flickering. Their eyes — eyes Isandro had memorized in a single afternoon over tea and burnt shortbread — were fixed on him with something worse than fear. Expectation.

"You wrote *it*," Wendell said. "Here. This tower. A hundred years ago."

"The prophecy. Yes." He sat down on a step because his legs would not hold him any longer, and the admission poured out of him the way ink pours when a bottle tips — unstoppable, spreading into everything. "I was a scribe in the Court of Broken Oaths before it was broken. Before the covenant tore. I was young, and I was proud, and I believed that words could hold the world together if only they were the right words, arranged in the right order."

Lightning lit the stairwell, once, twice — Wendell striking at the tendrils below, buying seconds neither of them believed in.

"I saw the Sundering coming," Isandro said. "I saw what would happen when the human world and the fae realms stopped honoring each other — how memory itself would curdle, how forgetting would spread like rot through a spine. So I wrote a warning. I wrote a *map*. A prophecy that named the storm-caller who would one day reforge the covenant, and the scribe who would carry the map to them."

"And the payment," Wendell said quietly. "There's always a payment."

Isandro laughed, and it was an awful sound, thin and rusted. "You learn fast. Yes. The magic that lets a prophecy see forward demands a soul to anchor it — a life bound to the book, so the words have something living to remember with. I signed my own name into the binding. My years stopped. My body stopped. Every century, the book wakes me, sets me somewhere new, gives me a trade my hands remember — binding, mending, restoring — and waits for me to find you."

The next bolt Wendell threw was wilder than the others, and it missed the Thorn entirely, scorching the stairwell wall. Their breathing had gone ragged. "So the date. Our first date. The tea shop. The rain that started exactly when the prophecy said—"

"I didn't arrange it," he said quickly. "*It* arranged me. I walked into that valley because the pull told me to. I asked you to dinner because the ink made my mouth form the question. But—" He caught himself. He heard, too late, the shape of the word *but*, and watched Wendell hear it too.

"*But,*" Wendell repeated. The storm-light died in their hands. Somehow that was more terrible than the Thorn climbing the stairs toward them, its tendrils now visible through the broken windows, black against a bruised sky. "Say the rest of it, Isandro. Say what you've been swallowing since the Ashmoor. Since the court."

He looked at his hands. Binder's hands. Scribe's hands. Hands that had written a person into existence like a character in a book, and then had the audacity to love her — to love *them* — as though that changed anything.

"When I restored the book," he whispered, "and saw our names side by side, fresh as morning — I felt wonder. For one whole day I felt chosen. Blessed. And then I read further, into the older pages, the pages in my own hand from a hundred years ago, and I understood what I'd done." His throat closed. He forced the words through anyway, because Wendell deserved every jagged piece. "I understood that I hadn't found you. I'd *made* you. Or made the conditions of you. The prophecy needed a storm-caller exiled from their own power, hollowed out, hungry for belonging — someone whose loneliness would make them say yes to a quiet bookbinder's nervous invitation. Someone whose erased past would be waiting at the Covenant Forge like coin in a purse. I wrote the specifications, and the world filled the order."

The silence afterward was absolute. Even the Thorn had stopped climbing.

"So my exile," Wendell said, and their voice was very level, which Isandro knew — from one single afternoon of learning them — was how they sounded when they were closest to breaking. "The thing I can't remember doing. The catastrophe I fled. You're telling me the prophecy *needed* it. That I lost whatever I lost so your book would have a protagonist."

"I don't know," Isandro said, and hated himself for the honesty. "I never knew what you'd done. The book doesn't show causes, only conditions. But I knew someone had to be broken in exactly that shape, and I let myself hope the world would choose gently." He looked up, finally, and met their eyes, and let them see all of it — the cowardice and the grief and the terrible, stubborn love. "And then I met you over burnt shortbread, and you laughed at my terrible joke about bookcloth, and I realized I hadn't written a hero. I'd written a trap, and baited it with my own heart, and you'd walked into both."

Wendell stepped back. One step, two, until their shoulders met the tower wall and there was nowhere behind them but sky.

"All this time," they said. "Every kindness. The way you looked at me when I called the lightning — I thought you were seeing *me*. And you were seeing a specification."

"No—" 

"Was the Ashmoor in the book?" Their voice rose. "Was the Court? Did you know about the Thorn eating memories while we slept in that waystation, and you said *nothing*? You let me walk toward the thing that devours what people are, knowing full well what it wanted with mine!"

"I was afraid—" 

"Of course you were afraid! I'm afraid! But I didn't write anyone into existence and hide the pen!"

Below them, as if the argument had been the signal it waited for, the Thorn surged up the stairwell — a flood of bramble-dark, tendrils splitting and multiplying, and where they touched the steps the stone went smooth and anonymous, unremembering. Isandro felt the pull at his soul sharpen to a scream. The book. It wasn't just hunting Wendell's recovered past.

It had come for its scribe.

"Wendell," he said, standing, backing toward the edge of the roofless tower. "Listen to me. Whatever you decide I deserve — and you'd be right — the Forge is three days east. The covenant needs a memory freely given, yours, the ones the storms are waking. Don't spend them on anger. Don't spend them on *me*. Save them for the reforging."

"Don't tell me what to do with my own past," Wendell snarled — and then the light came back into their hands, brighter than before, furious and grieving and magnificent, and they thrust it downward into the rising dark. Thunder detonated against the night. Tendrils charred and shrieked — the Thorn did shriek, a sound like a library burning — but more came, always more, and Isandro saw the mathematics of it and knew they could not win by force.

He also saw the tendril that had slipped past Wendell's storm, silent along the outer wall, reaching for his chest — for the stitched seam where his soul met his skin.

He did not step aside.

If the Thorn took him, it would take the scribe and spare the storm-caller. It was, he reflected with a strange calm, the last thing of value he had left to give: a hundred borrowed years, returned to close the account he'd opened.

"Isandro—" Wendell saw it, saw everything, and lunged —

—and the tendril struck home.

Cold flooded through him, cold and *emptying*, and the last thing Isandro Veyra felt before the dark took him was Wendell's hand closing around his wrist, holding on with a grip that had nothing of specifications in it, nothing of prophecy, only fury and refusal and something that might, despite everything, still be love.

Then the world forgot him, and pulled them both down into the thorns.
