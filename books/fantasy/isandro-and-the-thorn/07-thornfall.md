## 7. Thornfall

The bells of Vesperhold rang wrong.

Wendell had learned the city's voices in the weeks since they'd crossed out of the valley—learned which bell sang for dawn, which for market, which for the evening watch. This one was none of them. It was a cracked, arrhythmic tolling, the sound of something that had forgotten how to be a bell and was only remembering in fragments, note by dying note.

They stood on the balcony of the Wayfarer's Rest with Isandro beside them, both of them staring east, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise left too long unpressed. The Thorn came as a wall of black bramble taller than any cathedral, its thorns each the length of a spear, and where it touched the world, the world grew *vague*. Fields lost their edges. A windmill on the outskirts simply stopped being a windmill and became the idea of one, then not even that—a smudge where memory had been, a blankness shaped like absence.

"It's faster than they said," Isandro murmured. His hands were ink-stained as always; Wendell had noticed he never seemed to wash them fully, as if the stain were part of him. "The Court's scouts gave us three days. It will reach the walls by nightfall."

"Then we have until nightfall," Wendell said.

Isandro looked at them. In the grey light his face was pale, but steady—the steadiness of a man who had already lived through the worst thing that could happen to him and was waiting, patiently, for it to finish happening. "You could still go. The western gate is open. The Court has ships on the Mirrorsea. You've done enough. You found the covenant's heart. You carried the shard across the Ashmoor when it should have killed you."

"You'd come with me?"

Something flickered across Isandro's face—guilt, or grief, or the shape those two things make when they wear each other's clothes. "I can't leave the book."

"I know." Wendell turned back to the bruised horizon. "So I'm not leaving either."

* * *

They ran through streets that were forgetting themselves.

It was the sound that undid Wendell most—not screams, though there were screams, but the quieter horror underneath: a baker standing in his doorway holding bread he no longer recognized, turning it over and over, saying *what is this, what is this*; children who had stopped mid-game because they couldn't remember what game it was; an old woman singing a lullaby whose melody decayed with every line until she was humming static. The Thorn didn't kill. It didn't need to. It ate the thread that made a person a person, and left the body walking around inside the hole.

Wendell's storm stirred under their skin like a caged animal smelling blood.

They had been rationing it since the Ashmoor. Every time they called the lightning, more of the past came with it—flashes they hadn't asked for, didn't want. A tower in flames. A voice screaming their name in a language they no longer spoke. Hands—*their own hands*—doing something terrible beneath a sky the color of an open wound. The exile they'd believed imposed upon them had been chosen, they knew that much now. Chosen to bury something. And every bolt they pulled from the sky pried the lid up another inch.

At the eastern wall, the defenders were losing.

Not to assault—to erosion. The soldiers on the ramparts kept forgetting why they were there. Wendell watched a captain give an order and then stand blinking, sword half-raised, as if the word *order* had been scooped out of her. The bramble hadn't even reached the walls yet. Its mere proximity was enough, a tide of forgetting lapping at the shores of every mind in range.

"They'll break before it touches them," Isandro said quietly. He had the book out now, the prophecy, its pages fluttering though there was little wind. Fresh ink crawled across them in lines that made Wendell's skin crawl—lines describing this moment, this wall, this hour, in handwriting that Isandro always angled away from view.

"Then we hold them together," Wendell said. "You keep the book. I'll give them something to remember."

"And what's that?"

Wendell stepped up onto the parapet, boots on stone, the whole starving dark of the Thorn spread before them like a second night. Their heart hammered. Their mouth went dry. And underneath all of it, patient as bedrock, the storm waited.

*Me*, they thought. *They'll remember me.*

They raised their arms, and called the lightning down.

* * *

It came the way it always did now—too easily, too gladly, like a dog that had waited years at a door. Power poured through Wendell in a silver flood, and they shaped it into something the whole city could see: a crown of storm above the eastern wall, thunder rolling in deliberate waves, rain falling upward in glittering sheets. A spectacle. A landmark. Something the minds of Vesperhold could anchor to when everything else dissolved.

And the memories came.

They hit between heartbeats, each bolt a key turning in a lock Wendell had sealed themselves. A hall of white marble. Fae lords bowing—or was it mocking? A covenant ceremony, candles, oaths spoken in two tongues. And at the center of it, *themself*, younger, radiant with power, raising a hand—and the oath shattering like glass struck with a hammer. Not broken by the fae. Not broken by accident.

Broken by Wendell Almeida.

"No," they whispered, even as the lightning answered the word, arcing out to split a descending bramble-limb into smoking halves. "No, that's not—that wasn't how it—"

But it was. They felt the truth of it settle into their bones like cold water: a century ago, they had shattered the covenant. Whatever reason, whatever wound or rage or desperate necessity, the Thorn existed because of them. The exile had been mercy they'd granted themselves. The forgetting had been a kindness they no longer deserved.

Below, the defenders rallied. They saw the storm-crown. They remembered, suddenly and fiercely, that they were the last free city, and free cities do not kneel. Arrows flew. Ballistae groaned. For one bright hour, the wall held.

Then the Thorn *noticed* them.

It came like recognition—the whole black mass shifting, orienting, a hundred thousand thorns turning toward the figure wreathed in lightning on the parapet. And Wendell felt it feel them: felt the bramble taste their mind and find, buried under the fresh scars of recovered memory, the oldest scar of all. The original sin. The thing it had grown from.

*There you are,* the Thorn seemed to say, in a voice like pages burning. *There is where I began.*

"Wendell!" Isandro's hand closed around their wrist, grounding them, warm and human and terrified. "It's using you as a beacon—you're drawing it straight to the wall—we have to fall back to the Forge, now, while it's focused on you—"

"If I stop channeling, it eats the city."

"If you don't, it eats *you*—it's pulling at your memories right now, I can see it, Wendell, your eyes—" 

"My eyes what?" But they knew. They could feel it: the recovered past unraveling, the marble hall going soft at its edges, the burning tower fading. The Thorn was drinking them, sipping the century they'd just gotten back, and worse—it was reaching deeper, toward the years in the valley, toward Isandro's face in a bookshop doorway, toward the nervous hours spent choosing what to wear for a first date with a quiet bookbinder who might be the only good thing exile had ever given them.

Wendell thought about running. The western gate. The ships. Safety, and a small life, and the slow leak of everything they loved.

They had run once already. They had built a whole valley around running.

"No," Wendell said aloud, and the word came out as thunder.

They planted their feet on the parapet and opened every door inside themselves at once. Not rationing. Not rationed. The full storm, the true storm, the power that had once shattered a covenant between worlds now bent to a single purpose: holding. Lightning sheeted down in curtains, carving trenches of glass where the bramble advanced. Rain fell in spears. Wind screamed off the Mirrorsea and Wendell caught it, braided it, threw it back as a gale that peeled thorn-vines from the walls by the acre.

The city saw. The city *remembered seeing*, held fast by the spectacle of it, and fought on.

But the Thorn was patient in the way only hunger is patient. It withdrew, regrouped, and came again along three fronts at once, and Wendell felt their strength guttering like a candle in a draft. Blood ran from their nose. Their knees shook. The memories were going faster now, sluicing out of them with every bolt—the marble hall gone, the burning tower going, the faces of the fae court dissolving like sugar in rain.

Let them go, they thought wildly. Let it take the old ones. Take the guilt. Take the betrayal. Take everything except—

Except Isandro's hand on their wrist. Except the smell of glue and leather and old paper. Except the way he'd smiled, shy and astonished, when Wendell had asked him to dinner.

*I won't run again,* Wendell vowed, to the storm, to the Thorn, to whatever fragment of their old self still listened. *Whatever this costs. Whatever I have to feed it. I am done running.*

The lightning flared white. The wall held. And through the roar, dimly, Wendell heard Isandro shouting—not at them, but at the book in his other hand, at the crawling ink, at whatever the prophecy was writing now:

*"No—no, not this part, you can't—there has to be another way—"*

Wendell turned, lightning still dancing from their fingertips, and saw Isandro's face.

They had never seen anyone look like that. Not afraid of the Thorn. Afraid of a page.

"What does it say?" Wendell shouted.

Isandro's mouth worked. The ink-stained fingers trembled against the vellum. Below them the Thorn gathered itself for a final surge, and above them the storm-crown flickered, and between those two darknesses Isandro lifted his eyes and said, in a voice cracked clean through:

"The Forge. It says the price. Wendell—it says the price is *you*."
