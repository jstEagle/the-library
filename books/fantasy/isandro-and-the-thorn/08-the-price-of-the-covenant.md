## 8. The Price of the Covenant

The Covenant Forge did not look like a forge.

Isandro had imagined anvils, perhaps, or the great iron lungs of mortal smithies he had catalogued in his years among books—bellows and hammers, the orange reek of hot metal. Instead, the chamber beneath the Sundered Realms was a cathedral of standing glass, each pane a different color of dying light, and at its center a single anvil of black stone floating above a well of slow, silver fire. The fire did not flicker. It breathed.

The heat of it reached Isandro before anything else did, sliding under his skin like a needle finding an old seam. He recognized the sensation and hated that he recognized it. *I have been here before.* The thought arrived with the certainty of a scar: not memory, exactly, but the shape memory leaves behind when it is torn out.

Wendell stood at his shoulder, storm-light still crawling faintly along their knuckles, residue of the crossing over the Ashmoor. They had not spoken much since the confession. Since Isandro had said *I wrote it* and watched something close behind Wendell's eyes go dark as a shuttered window. They had walked on anyway. That was the thing about Wendell that Isandro kept failing to prepare for—they walked on anyway, furious or grieving or afraid, they walked on.

"Keeper," Wendell said to the figure waiting by the well. "We've come to reforge it."

The Keeper of the Forge had been a woman once, perhaps. Now she was more loom than person, threads of pale light running from her fingertips into the air itself, weaving and unweaving something Isandro's eyes kept sliding off. When she turned, her face was kind the way old gravestones are kind.

"One of you has come to pay," she said. It was not a question. "The Forge takes only what is given freely, and only what is living. A memory, held in the flesh, surrendered whole. Not a copy. The thing itself. The one who gives it will not remember having given, or what was given, or why. The covenant will drink it and be strong for a century. Perhaps two."

The words fell into the chamber and rang there.

Isandro felt Wendell go still beside him—not the stillness of fear, but of arithmetic. He knew that stillness. He had watched it a hundred times across a bookbinder's bench, Wendell turning over a broken spine, calculating what could be saved and what had to be cut away.

"And yours," Wendell said quietly, not looking at him. "Yours are already spent. Aren't they."

It was not a question either. The Thorn had shown them that, in the moment it had coiled around Isandro and found nothing to devour—no memories to strip, no past to unmake. It had shrieked, that time, in something like frustration. A hollow man. A ledger with the entries burned out.

"Yes," Isandro said. "A hundred years ago, when I wrote the prophecy, the binding took my memories as its ink. Every year of my life before that night. I kept my craft. I kept my hands. I kept—" he stopped, and made himself finish, "—I kept enough to love you. That was the bargain's one mercy, though I did not know it was a mercy then."

*Enough to love you.* He had said it aloud at last, in this glass cathedral at the end of the world, and the words did not echo because the Forge drank echoes too.

Wendell finally turned to him. In the silver light their face was stripped of everything—no anger left, no blame, only a terrible clarity. "So it's mine. It was always going to be mine. You wrote a prophecy that ends with me standing here, handing over the only self I've just gotten back."

"I didn't know—" Isandro began, and stopped, because it was a lie by half and they both knew it. He had not known it would be Wendell. He had known *someone* would stand here. He had written the price into the page with his own hand, a hundred years before he met them, and then he had gone to a valley where magic could not follow and opened a bookbinding shop and waited to fall in love with the person the ink chose. "I didn't know it would feel like this," he said instead, which was the true half.

The Keeper's threads whispered. "The Forge is patient," she said, "but the Thorn is not. It ate the memory of the eastern road this morning. By tomorrow it will have the name of this place. Decide."

* * *

They walked to the edge of the chamber, where the glass panes showed the Sundered Realms in fractured panorama—the fae courts dim as coals under ash, the mortal lands beyond the valley flickering like a lamp low on oil, and everywhere, everywhere, the Thorn's black bramble creeping through the seams of the world, drinking. Isandro could see a village in one pane, its windows going dark one by one as the Thorn passed over it, and he understood with a scholar's cold precision what that meant: not death. Forgetting. The village would remain, its people would remain, waking tomorrow with no names, no children, no griefs, no loves. A world of people with nothing behind their eyes.

"They'll forget the covenant ever existed," Wendell said, following his gaze. "Both sides. And then the realms won't be sundered. They'll just be... nothing, pressed together."

"The Thorn was born from the breaking," Isandro said. "It eats what the broken covenant used to hold. Memory is the mortar, Wendell. That's what I learned too late, writing the prophecy. The old covenant wasn't a treaty. It was a shared remembering—every fae oath and every human promise, held in common, renewed each century. When it shattered, all that remembering had to go somewhere." He looked down at his hands, the hands that still remembered their craft when nothing else remained. "It went into the Thorn."

Wendell was quiet for a long moment. The storm-light on their knuckles had gone still.

"Tell me what you'd choose," they said finally. "If it were your memories on the table. If the Forge could take them."

Isandro opened his mouth to say *I would give them, gladly, I have already given everything, what is the rest*—and found he could not. Because it was not true, and Wendell deserved better than the beautiful lie. "I don't know," he said, and the honesty of it hurt his throat. "I gave mine a hundred years ago and I have been a hollow house ever since. I walk through rooms of my own life and the rooms are empty. I know that I loved someone before the binding. I do not know who. I know that I betrayed something, or someone. I do not know what. If the Forge offered them back to me—" his voice cracked, and he let it, "—I would be lying if I said I wouldn't reach for them. Even now. Even knowing what they cost."

Wendell nodded slowly. "And mine," they said, "are the ones I *chose* to bury. The storm, the betrayal, the exile. The Keeper of the valley told me I sealed them myself. I spent three days in that valley hating whatever I'd done, and then the Ashmoor gave them back piece by piece, and now I know—" they stopped. Pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. "Now I know what I did, Isandro. And they're still mine. That's the worst of it. They're mine, and they're awful, and I would still rather be the person who did that thing than be no one."

"I know," Isandro said. "That's why I can't ask you."

"You're not asking."

"I wrote the book. I don't have to ask. The asking was done a century before you were born."

The silver fire breathed. Somewhere far above them, muffled by leagues of stone and Sundered sky, the Thorn's assault on the last free city had begun—he could feel it in the Forge's glass, a tremor like a scream held in a fist.

* * *

They sat together on the cold floor at the chamber's edge, backs against a pane of green glass that showed a mortal orchard in high summer, apples like small suns. Isandro did not remember orchards. He only knew the word, and that the image made something in his chest ache with a homesickness for a home he could not name. He wondered if that was what Wendell would become. A person made of words for things they could no longer feel.

"I keep thinking," Wendell said at last, "about the date. Our first one. The night the thorn came through the book." A ghost of a smile. "You'd spent two hours choosing the restaurant. You'd asked the owner, secretly, to put candles at our table. You were so nervous you read the entire menu twice and I pretended not to notice."

"You remember that?"

"Every hour of it." Wendell's voice went rough. "That's the cruelty of it, Isandro. The Forge wants a living memory, freely given. It doesn't say which one. It says *a* memory. Whole." They turned to look at him, and their eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "So it could be the storm. The betrayal. The century of exile. The worst things I ever did and everything I paid for them."

"But."

"But it doesn't have to be." Wendell took his hand. Their palm was warm, faintly crackling, the way it always was now, as if their skin had learned to hold weather. "It could be the orchard. It could be the date. It could be every morning in the valley, you in the shop with ink on your fingers, the way you hum when you paste the endpapers. It could be you. All of you, every piece of you I've gathered since I walked into that shop—and I could stand here and give it, and save both worlds, and walk out of this forge not knowing your name."

The words went through Isandro like the thorn had, once, through the book—clean, total, leaving nothing standing behind them.

"You'd choose that," he said. "You'd choose the worst memories over—"

"I'd choose *you*," Wendell said. "Over the past. Over the storm. Over the person I was before I was yours. If the price is a life, I'll pay it in the only currency I don't want to lose."

And Isandro, who had spent a hundred years as a hollow house, who had written a prophecy that used a stranger as its hinge, who had loved this person across a century of not remembering why—Isandro understood, in that moment, the full architecture of what he had built. The prophecy had never been a trap for Wendell. It had been a trap for *him*. The ink had known, even where he had not, that the only way to teach a hollow man the weight of a memory was to make him watch someone else give theirs away.

He could not allow it. And there was no other way. Both things were true, and the Forge waited between them like a held breath.

Wendell stood. Drew the storm up out of their skin, out of the recovered century, out of every thunderhead the Ashmoor had given back—and the whole glass cathedral lit blue-white, the panes shivering, the silver fire leaning toward them like a listener.

"Keeper," Wendell said. "Open the Forge."

Isandro rose with them, and took their hand, and did not let go. "Then we do it together," he said. "Whatever it takes. Whatever it costs. I held the book for a hundred years, Wendell—I will hold you through this. Anchor me to you and don't let the storm take the part of you that chooses."

Above them, the glass began to crack in a pattern like lightning finding a tree—and the Forge opened its silver eye, and asked, in a voice like the first rain after drought:

*What do you remember?*
