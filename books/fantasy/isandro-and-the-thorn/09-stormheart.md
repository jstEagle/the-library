## 9. Stormheart

The Covenant Forge stood at the bottom of the world.

Wendell had imagined furnaces—iron and coal and the stink of hot metal—but this was nothing so crude. The Forge was a wound in reality, a chasm where the fabric of both realms had been stitched together once, long ago, by hands that believed such stitching could hold forever. Light poured up from it in slow ribbons: gold from the fae side of the seam, silver from the human, braiding and unbraading as though searching for a pattern it had forgotten. The air tasted like ozone and old paper. Like every storm Wendell had ever called. Like every book Isandro had ever mended.

And above them, coiling down through the fractured sky like a root seeking water, came the Thorn.

It had followed them across the Ashmoor. It had torn through the gates of the last free city while its people screamed names they were already losing. Now it hung against the dark, vast and patient, a lattice of black bramble studded with thorns each longer than a spear—and along every thorn glimmered stolen memory, little lights of other people's lives: a wedding dance, a mother's voice, the color of a childhood sky. It wore them like dew.

"It's beautiful," Wendell heard themself say, and hated their own voice for it.

"That's how it hunts," Isandro said quietly. He stood at their shoulder at the Forge's edge, his hands ink-stained even now, even here, as if the centuries of binding pages into him had dyed the skin beneath. "It shows you what you'll miss before it takes it."

The Queen of Broken Oaths had given them the truth an hour ago, and the truth was still sitting in Wendell's chest like swallowed glass. *A living memory, freely given.* That was the price of the seam. Not blood, not magic, not sacrifice in the abstract—a memory, whole and chosen, poured into the Forge to bind what magic alone could not. Isandro's memories were already spent; he had paid them a century ago, page by page, until all that remained of the man who wrote the prophecy was the prophecy itself.

Which left Wendell. Which left everything Wendell had clawed back out of the storm—the exile, the betrayal, the catastrophic act buried at the bottom of their own mind like a stone dropped down a well. Every fragment the Ashmoor had shaken loose on the crossing. Every name and face and sin.

Every version of who they had been before the valley made them small.

"I can do this," Isandro said. "I can anchor you. I've read enough souls to know how to hold one open." His jaw was set, but his eyes—his eyes were terrified, and he wasn't hiding it anymore, not after the confession, not after Wendell's fury had burned through them both and left something rawer and truer standing in the ash. "You don't have to give all of them."

"The Forge doesn't take installments." Wendell looked down into the light. "I asked. The Queen told me. It takes what's freely given—all of it, or none."

* * *

They had argued. Of course they had argued. Isandro had tried to offer himself anyway—as if a soul with no memories left to spend could pay a debt priced in memory—and Wendell had gripped his face in both hands and said, with a steadiness that surprised them, "You already gave everything once. You gave it for a world that forgot to thank you. I'm not letting you do it twice just so I can keep my ghosts."

Now the Thorn began to fall.

It came without sound at first—the way avalanches come, the way storms come, the terrible hush before the weight of the world arrives. Then the sound caught up: a shriek of tearing reality, bramble screaming against the seams of the sky, and the first wave of thorned branches plunged toward the Forge like spears thrown by a god.

"NOW," Isandro roared, and his voice cracked like the spine of an old book, and Wendell opened themselves to every storm they remembered.

They remembered the monsoon over the Sundered coast, when they had been young and arrogant and had pulled the rain out of a clear blue sky just to feel it answer. They remembered the ice-storm on the pass, the one they'd called down in grief after the exile decree, lightning walking white ridges like a thing hunting. They remembered the storm inside the valley's border—the one moment magic had flickered through the dead air and shown them what they'd lost. They remembered the storm of the Ashmoor, the black thunderheads they'd ridden across a desert made of ground-down years.

And beneath all of those, rising last because it hurt most, they remembered the storm they did not want to remember: the one they had raised a century ago—or someone wearing their face had raised—over a field full of people whose names they still couldn't reach. The betrayal-storm. The reason for the valley. The reason for everything.

Wendell took all of it, and held it, and let it go.

Lightning went out of them like breath. It didn't arc downward—it poured, a river of silver-white fury plunging into the Forge's seam, and the gold-and-silver braid of light seized it hungrily, weaving the storm into the covenant's broken warp and weft. Wendell felt each memory leave as it burned: the monsoon going first, taking with it the taste of salt and young triumph; then the ice, taking the cold and the grief-shape it had worn; then the valley storm, taking the ache of the dead air. Each loss was a door closing softly behind them. Each loss made the Forge blaze brighter.

The Thorn struck the light and recoiled, shrieking. Memory-fed, it could devour anything—except the thing currently unmaking itself into raw remembering. You cannot eat a fire by swallowing the person burning.

But the Thorn was old and clever, and it did not retreat. It turned instead toward the two small figures at the Forge's rim—the anchor and the storm.

"Wendell!" Isandro's hand found theirs, and his grip was iron and paper and something older than either. "Stay with me. Whatever it shows you—stay with me."

* * *

The Thorn stopped attacking and started offering.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next: the black lattice bloomed, and every thorn became a window, and every window showed Wendell a life. Their mother's kitchen, steam on the windows. A lover whose name sat just out of reach, laughing in rain. The first time they'd felt magic answer—eight years old, furious about something small, and the whole sky had flickered green. The exile road. The valley gate closing. Isandro's shop, the smell of glue and leather, the first date neither of them knew was written down anywhere.

*All of this,* the Thorn seemed to say, without words, in the language of longing itself. *All of this is still yours. Stop. Close the Forge. Keep yourself.*

Wendell staggered. The storm stuttered. For one horrible second the seam dimmed, and they saw the Thorn reflected in it, vast and patient, and understood that this was how it had eaten a hundred years of worlds—not by force, but by asking nicely.

Then Isandro stepped into their line of sight.

He was bleeding somewhere along his hairline, and there was ink on his fingers, and he was smiling—that quiet, crooked smile from the bookshop, from the date, from every ordinary moment Wendell was about to lose.

"You're right," he said, over the roar. "It's beautiful. So are you. And it can't have either of us."

"I won't know you," Wendell gasped. The words tore coming out. "Isandro—I won't know your name. I'll look at you and see a stranger—"

"No." He pressed their joined hands flat against the Forge's edge, against the blazing seam, and the light climbed their arms like ivy. "You'll know me anyway. That's the whole point. That's what I finally understand, after a century of writing fate down—you don't need the memory to keep the meaning. The valley taught you that. It took your past and you're still *you*. Still stubborn. Still kind when you think no one's watching. Give it the memories, Wendell. Keep the self."

The Thorn descended.

Wendell closed their eyes and gave it everything else.

The mother's kitchen went, and the exile road, and the lover's laugh in the rain. The catastrophic storm rose up out of the deepest dark of them—the field, the faces, the unforgivable thing—and Wendell did not flinch away from it this time. They looked at it fully, for one final instant, and whispered, "I'm sorry. Whoever I was—I'm sorry," and let the Forge take the guilt and the act and the name that went with them, all of it pouring into the seam like molten silver, sealing century-old wounds shut with the only metal strong enough: a life, freely given.

The Thorn hit the light and came apart.

Not destroyed—unmade, unwoven, every stolen memory ripped loose from its brambles and drawn streaming into the reforging seam, thousands of lives' worth of remembering flooding home to the people who'd lost them. The shriek it made was almost human. Almost pitiful. Then it was only wind, and then not even that, and the sky over the Sundered Realms cleared for the first time in a hundred years.

The Forge went quiet. The gold and silver braid settled into a single steady line of light, humming, whole.

Wendell opened their eyes.

They were kneeling at the edge of a chasm filled with gentle radiance. Their body ached everywhere. Their mind was—

Their mind was a room with the furniture gone. Vast, and clean, and echoing, and empty. They knew language. They knew how to stand. They knew, with a certainty that lived deeper than memory, that they had just done something enormous and that it had cost exactly everything.

Someone was holding their hand.

Wendell looked up. A man knelt opposite them—dark-haired, ink-stained, bleeding slightly, looking at Wendell the way people look at things they have waited a very long time for. His expression was careful. Hopeful. Afraid.

"Hello," Wendell said. Their voice sounded strange to them. New. "Do I know you?"

The man's smile wavered—grief and joy fighting openly on his face—and then steadied.

"Not yet," Isandro said. "But we have time."

Far below, in the heart of the Forge, the new-cast covenant pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat learning its rhythm—and somewhere in the light, faint but unmistakable, a page turned.
