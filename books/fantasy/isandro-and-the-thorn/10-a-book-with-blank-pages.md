## 10. A Book With Blank Pages

**Wendell**

The valley smelled of rain that had not yet fallen.

Wendell stood at the window of the small stone cottage—*their* cottage, though they could not say how long it had been theirs—and watched the clouds gather over the eastern ridge. Something in their chest leaned toward those clouds the way a plant leans toward light. An old habit, maybe. A hunger with no name attached to it.

They pressed a hand flat against the glass. The glass was cool. The hand was theirs. That much, at least, was certain.

Most things were not.

The healers in the valley had been gentle about it, in the way of people who had seen this before and would see it again. *You came through the pass in autumn,* they said. *You were hurt. The hurt took some of you with it.* They did not say what kind of hurt. They did not say why Wendell's hands sometimes curled around empty air as if gripping a rope, or why thunder made them weep without knowing why, or why the sight of a certain bookbinder's shop on Lantern Row made their heart ache like a bruise they couldn't stop touching.

They remembered nothing before the pass. Not their name's true weight—not the way *Almeida* sat in the mouth like a word from another language, which perhaps it was. Not the storms. Not whatever they had done, or failed to do, that had carved them hollow and set them down here among the forgotten.

But there were other things. Small things. The way their fingers knew the shape of a kettle handle. The way they flinched from mirrors on gray days. The way, when Isandro Veyra smiled at them across a table, something deep and drowned in them turned over and said, softly, *oh — there you are.*

Isandro was due within the hour. He came every evening now, or nearly every one, carrying bread or ink or nothing at all, and they would sit by the fire while he mended books and Wendell watched his hands move and felt, for reasons they could not explain, impossibly safe.

There was a book on the table behind them. It had appeared on their doorstep three days ago, wrapped in brown paper, no note. Blank pages, every one of them, bound in leather gone soft with age. Wendell had carried it inside anyway. Some instinct older than memory told them it mattered.

Tonight, they thought, they would ask Isandro about it.

Outside, the first drops began to fall, and Wendell did not flinch from them. They opened the window instead, and let the storm breathe against their face, and for one strange, suspended moment they almost heard it speak.

* * *

**Isandro**

He had learned to live inside the lie gently.

That was the only way to do it—if you were going to carry a century of knowing, you carried it the way water carries a boat: all around the thing, never letting it touch. He knew everything. He knew the Sundered Realms stitched whole again beyond the pass. He knew the Thorn unmade, its hunger ended, the covenant reforged in stormlight at the Forge where he had once knelt as a young scribe with ink-stained fingers and too much faith. He knew what it had cost.

He knew that Wendell had paid it.

And Wendell knew none of it, and Isandro had decided—after long nights of argument with himself, after the fae queen's cold counsel and his own colder grief—that this was not a lie so much as a mercy with edges. You cannot give a man back a knife he used to cut himself open. You can only sit beside him while he learns his hands again.

So he came each evening with bread and quiet, and he let Wendell be new.

But tonight his hands were not steady. Tonight the blank book waited on the table between them like an open question, and Isandro had recognized the binding the moment he'd seen it through the cottage window two days past. He knew that leather. He had sewn those headbands himself, a hundred years ago, in a workroom that no longer existed except in him.

It was the prophecy's book. Emptied. Every word spent, every line fulfilled, the pages scoured white when the last clause closed—the covenant sealed, the price paid, the story finished. It should have crumbled. Instead it had crossed the pass and found its way home, the way old dogs do, the way debts do.

He knocked. Wendell opened the door with rain still beaded in their hair, and the look on their face undid him a little, the way it always did—hope trying to be casual about itself.

"You're wet," Isandro said.

"You're late," Wendell said, and pulled him inside by the sleeve, and laughed, and the sound filled the small stone room like light filling a jar.

* * *

**Wendell**

They ate. They always ate; Isandro cooked like someone making up for lost time, though lost time in what, Wendell couldn't have said. Then the plates were cleared and the fire settled and the silence arrived—that particular silence that meant one of them had something to say and both of them knew it.

Wendell put the blank book on the table between them.

"This came for me," they said. "Three days ago. No note. And I know it sounds mad, but I think—" Their throat tightened. "I think I already love this book. Isn't that absurd? It's empty. There's nothing in it. But I keep touching it like it might break."

Isandro looked at the book for a long time. In the firelight his eyes were the color of strong tea, and Wendell saw something move through them—grief, maybe, or its close cousin, relief.

"It's not mad," he said quietly. "Some books hold more than words."

"Whose is it?"

"I made it." His voice caught on the admission, and he steadied it. "A long time ago. For a very important purpose. And then the purpose was finished, and the book was empty, and I thought—I hoped—it would find someone who needed somewhere to begin."

Wendell turned the book over in their hands. The leather was warm, somehow, warmer than the room. Beneath their fingertips the pages whispered though nothing stirred them.

"And you?" they asked. "Do you need somewhere to begin?"

Isandro laughed—a strange, cracked sound, like a hinge opening after years shut. "More than you know."

"Then let's begin together." Wendell said it before courage could fail them, before the old hollow ache could rise up and remind them of everything they weren't. "I don't remember who I was. I've stopped pretending that doesn't frighten me. But I know who I am when you're here. I know what my hands want to build. So—" They pushed the book across the table. "First page. You write. I'll tell you what to write. We'll make ourselves up as we go. Is that allowed? Making yourself up?"

Isandro's eyes were shining. "I think," he said, "that might be the oldest magic there is."

* * *

**Isandro**

He ground the ink himself, because some rituals deserved the slow way. Walnut gall and rainwater and a drop of iron, the same recipe he had used a hundred years ago when he was a fool with a quill and a vision of saving two worlds. His hands remembered even if his heart wished they wouldn't.

Wendell sat across from him, chin propped on their fists, watching the nib move.

"Say something," Isandro said. "Anything. The first line of us."

Wendell was quiet a moment. Outside, the storm they'd welcomed earlier had gentled into a steady silver rain, drumming soft on the roof, and their face turned toward the sound with that expression Isandro had learned to read in another life and loved again in this one—the look of someone listening to a friend.

"Write this," Wendell said at last. "*Two people in a valley where nothing is remembered, deciding to remember forward instead.*"

Isandro wrote it. The ink sank into the blank page and stayed, and the page seemed—though he would never swear to this, not under torture, not before queens—to receive it gladly. Like soil taking seed. Like a held breath released.

"Your turn," Wendell said. "Add a line. Anything."

He thought of a hundred lines. *I knew you before you knew me. I wrote your name in prophecy before I ever heard your voice. I am sorry. I am sorry. Thank you.* All true. All too heavy for a first page.

Instead he wrote: "*The rain sounds like applause.*"

Wendell laughed, bright and startled, and reached across the table and laced their fingers through his ink-stained ones, and Isandro felt the last cold chamber of his long-bound heart unlock.

* * *

**Wendell**

Later—much later, the fire burned to embers, the book lying open between them with four lines in two hands—Wendell woke from a half-dream they wouldn't be able to keep.

In the dream they had stood in a place of ash and broken towers, lightning pouring off their arms like water, and someone had been holding onto them, anchoring them, shouting their name against the wind. In the dream the name had been *Wendell*, spoken with such ferocious tenderness that waking, they said it aloud just to feel it in their mouth.

Beside the hearth, Isandro stirred. "What is it?"

"I dreamed I was a storm," Wendell said. "And someone loved me anyway." They looked at him—at this quiet man with ink on his fingers and centuries, somehow, folded into the corners of his smile. "Was that you? In the dream?"

Isandro held their gaze. Whatever passed behind his eyes was vast and old and gentle, a library closing its doors at night.

"Every version of me," he said, "in every version of the dream."

Wendell believed him. They didn't know why—they didn't know anything, really, not their exile, not their crimes or glories, not the covenant humming now beneath the roots of the world like a second heartbeat. But belief, they were learning, didn't require evidence. Sometimes it required only a hand offered in the dark, and the wild, unreasonable decision to take it.

They took it.

"Tomorrow," Wendell murmured, drifting, "we'll write the next page."

"Tomorrow," Isandro promised. "And the next. As many as you want."

The rain went on falling, soft as forgiveness. On the table, the blank book lay open to its first page, waiting—patient as prophecy, hungry as hope—for everything they had not yet become.

And far beyond the pass, in realms newly stitched whole, the storms rolled on without anger for the first time in a hundred years, carrying, in their long gray voices, something that sounded almost like a blessing.
