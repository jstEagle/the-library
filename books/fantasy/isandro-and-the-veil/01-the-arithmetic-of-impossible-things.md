## 1. The Arithmetic of Impossible Things

There are three rules to keeping accounts for the Gilded Veil, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way I learn anything.

One: never write a number you can't defend. Two: never trust an inventory that balances too neatly — perfection in a ledger is like a smile on a moneylender, a sign that something underneath is rotten. Three: never, under any circumstances, count what's in the vaults after dark.

I broke rule three my first week, out of stubbornness. I'd spent the day cataloguing and I wanted to check my sums against the shelves themselves, and so at half past nine that night I took my lamp down into the vaults beneath the guildhouse, where the air tastes faintly of rain that fell somewhere else. The bottled memories were where I'd left them, ranked on cedar shelving like preserves in a pantry: *Grief of a Fisherman, 40 years aged; First Snowfall, Child's Own; The Taste of a Name You've Forgotten.* Each vial glowed its own color when the lamp passed over it. Some of them glowed back.

And in the far corner, on the shelf reserved for items awaiting appraisal, sat a jar of borrowed luck with a label written in my own handwriting — except I hadn't labeled it yet. I stood there doing arithmetic in my head: the jar had come in that morning, unlabeled, and here was a label dated three days from now.

I went upstairs, closed the ledger, and told myself the sensible explanation was that I was tired. It wasn't. But I have found that a lie told precisely is nearly as good as the truth, and considerably easier to carry.

That was two years ago. I still keep the ledgers. I still don't go down after dark, and I still don't entirely believe in this place, no matter how long I stay.

* * *

The Gilded Veil trades in impossible things, and I mean that as a bookkeeper's statement, not a poet's. Bottled memories. Borrowed luck, sold by the spoonful. Years of life, transferred from the dying to the desperate at rates I set quarterly based on demand. There is a shelf in the deep vault for *weather someone else is having*, and another for *the sound of a door you'll never open again*. We sell second chances at a markup. We buy regrets by weight.

Most people who find us are looking to sell something they can't afford to keep. A widow sells the memory of her husband's last hour because she cannot sleep inside it anymore. A soldier sells his fear before a battle he doesn't expect to survive, then fights better without it, which raises certain philosophical questions I've learned not to ask during business hours. A young man once sold his talent for music to pay his mother's debts, and came back every month afterward to stand outside our door, listening to a building he could no longer hear properly.

I understand him. I sold nothing when I came here, but I arrived carrying things I couldn't afford to keep either.

My name is Ada Crane. That sentence used to cost me something every time I said it. Now it mostly just costs me caution.

* * *

The morning I'm telling you about began, as most of my mornings do, with a discrepancy.

"Page forty," Isandro said, appearing at my elbow the way he does — silently, suddenly, smelling faintly of ink and cold stone. He's the guild's cartographer, though *cartographer* undersells him the way *housecat* undersells a panther. Other mapmakers draw places that exist. Isandro draws places that don't exist yet. His maps arrive ahead of the world, like letters posted before their writers finish them.

"Page forty has a discrepancy?" I said, not looking up. "Impossible. Page forty is mine."

"There's a memory unaccounted for. Vial sixty-one. *A Childhood Summer, Whole.* It came in Tuesday. It's not on your intake list."

"It's on page forty-one."

"You filed a whole summer on the overflow page?"

"I filed it where it fit. Summers are large." I turned the ledger toward him. "See? Vial sixty-one, *A Childhood Summer, Whole*, appraised at eleven marks, condition excellent, seller wept but did not haggle."

Isandro read it twice. He has the kind of face that makes you want to know what he's thinking, which is inconvenient, because he almost never tells you. Dark hair going gray early, hands stained permanently with map-ink, eyes the color of the sky on days when the weather can't decide. He looked at my entry, then at me, and something crossed his face too quickly for me to name it.

"Wept but didn't haggle," he repeated. "That's sad."

"That's Tuesday."

"No, I mean—" He stopped. Rubbed the bridge of his nose, leaving a gray smudge among the freckles there. "Never mind. It's fine. Your filing system is a crime against bookkeeping, but it's fine."

"My filing system has never lost a thing."

"Neither has mine." He said it lightly, but his hand had gone to his breast pocket, where he keeps his current map, and stayed there a moment longer than casual. Then he smiled at me, the crooked one, and asked if I'd eaten, and the moment passed the way moments do around Isandro — quietly, without leaving a mark.

Except I noticed. Noticing is my trade. Numbers don't move unless something pushes them, and neither do people.

* * *

We ate lunch on the loading dock behind the guildhouse, which is the only place in the building where Vess's rules don't follow you. Vess runs the Gilded Veil the way a captain runs a ship in a storm she's been expecting for years: competently, grimly, and with an eye always on the horizon. She calls me her *arithmetician*, which sounds grander than *bookkeeper* and costs her nothing. She calls Isandro her *cartographer* and pays him in paper, privacy, and the freedom to draw whatever he draws, which between those three of us, we all understood to be the real currency of this place.

Everyone at the Veil is paying off something. I've done the books long enough to know that's not cynicism. It's arithmetic.

"So," Isandro said, tearing bread. "The Ferren woman came back."

"The widow?"

"She wants to buy back her husband's last hour."

I stopped chewing. "She sold it four months ago. She signed the standard release. She wept on the standard form, which I noted in the margin, because I note everything."

"I know."

"And the buyback clause—"

"—doesn't apply. I know. I read your contracts, Ada. You write them tighter than anyone in the city." He looked out past the dock, toward the mountains east of town, where the peaks had gone strange and white in the noon light. "She just stood in the front room holding the empty spot on the shelf where it used to be. Like she could see the shape of it."

"That's not how the inventory works."

"I know that's not how the inventory works." He tore the bread again, though he hadn't eaten the first piece. "I'm telling you what I saw."

I took the bread from him and ate it myself, because someone had to, and because handing food back and forth is as close as either of us comes to saying the softer things. This is the shape of our friendship: he notices people, I notice numbers, and between us we almost make one complete observer.

"Why did you really come find me this morning?" I asked. "Page forty was an excuse. You're never wrong about a number, and you knew it was on forty-one."

He was quiet long enough that I counted his breaths. Four of them. In through the nose, out slow, the way Vess breathes before she signs something she hates.

"I couldn't sleep," he said finally. "So I worked. And around the third hour I realized I'd drawn the same stretch of road six times. Same road, same bend, same milestone marker — a marker that doesn't exist anywhere I've ever walked. And the seventh time I drew it, I wrote a label on it without thinking, and the label was a word I didn't know." He shrugged, and the shrug didn't sit right on him. "My hand knew it. I didn't."

"What did it say?"

"I washed it off." He met my eyes. "Before I could read it."

* * *

Here is what I should have done, looking back. I should have pressed him. I should have opened a fresh page right there on the loading dock and started a ledger entry: *Subject, Isandro, cartographer. Anomaly, recurring involuntary cartography, unknown destination, unknown label, subject distressed.* I should have treated him like any other item in my care and tracked the discrepancy to its source, because discrepancies don't resolve themselves. They compound.

Instead I said, "You've been working too many hours. Even impossible maps need sleep," and he laughed, and I let myself believe the laugh, because believing it was easier than the alternative, and because — this is the true thing, the thing I guard even now — I am afraid of how much I would do for him.

You don't build a life on ledgers because you love numbers. You build it because numbers hold still. Every friend I've ever had was a variable I couldn't control, and variables get people hurt. Maren Crane taught me that lesson so thoroughly it's grown into the bone. So I keep my accounts balanced and my affections audited, and I tell myself that watching Isandro walk away across the yard, ink-stained and humming, is a safe way to keep a person: at a distance, in good light, fully documented.

But here is what the ledgers have also taught me, the lesson I resist daily: some entries refuse to balance. Some columns won't close. Some things you count and count and count, and the total keeps coming out wrong, and eventually you have to admit the error isn't in your arithmetic. It's in your assumption. I had assumed I could belong to the Gilded Veil the way I belong to a ledger — held, contained, safe. Sitting on that dock with the crumbs of Isandro's bread on my sleeve, I felt the assumption shift under me like a floorboard.

I belonged here. That was the problem. Belonging is just debt wearing a kinder coat, and I know what happens when debts come due.

* * *

Vess summoned me at dusk. Her office sits above the front room, and the window looks east, toward the mountain, though she keeps the curtain drawn over it more often than not lately. She had the old ledgers out — the founding ones, the ones kept in a hand so cramped it takes me an hour to parse a single page — and she was sitting very still behind them, the way she does when she's already decided something and is waiting for the world to catch up.

"Ada." She didn't look up. "When you close the quarter's books, I want a full accounting of everything we owe. Not coin. Everything. Every promise, every deferred payment, every line item marked *pending*."

"The pending column is mostly administrative—"

"All of it. Pending, deferred, contingent, oral. If the Gilded Veil owes anything to anyone, I want it written down in your hand, on one page, by week's end." She finally raised her head, and I saw something in her face I had never seen in two years of quarterly closings. Vess does not frighten. Vess *appraises*. But there, behind her eyes, was the look of a woman reading a bill she'd hoped would never be presented.

"May I ask why?" I said. "For the record."

"For the record," she repeated softly, and almost smiled. "Because the record is the only thing that will save us, Ada. When the time comes. Keep your pages clean. Keep them exact." She drew the curtain aside, just an inch, and looked east at the mountain going dark against the darkening sky. "Keep them exact, and trust nothing that balances too easily."

I walked back down through the front room, where the lamps were being lit and the day's sellers were being gently shown the door, and I thought about rule two. Never trust an inventory that balances too neatly.

Then I thought about Isandro's face when he said *my hand knew it and I didn't*, and about the jar in the vault labeled in my own future handwriting, and about the mountain standing east of the city like a creditor at a funeral.

That night I opened a new ledger. Fresh page, clean ruling, my best pen. At the top I wrote:

*Anomalies, Outstanding.*

First entry: *Isandro. Draws roads that don't exist. Labels he can't read. Hands shake. Watch.*

Second entry: *Vess. Founding ledgers open. Asking for debts. Watch.*

Third entry, after a long pause, because I write only what I can defend: *This place. It has been too good to me for two years. Nothing balances that neatly. Watch.*

I blotted the page, closed the cover, and sat in the lamplight listening to the guildhouse settle around me — the creak of old wood, the tick of the case clock, the faint, patient silence of the vaults below, where the bottled memories glowed in the dark whether anyone watched them or not.

Somewhere under the mountain, I would think later, something had begun keeping its own books. And its arithmetic, whatever else you could say about it, was very, very old.

I just didn't yet know whose name was written on page one.
