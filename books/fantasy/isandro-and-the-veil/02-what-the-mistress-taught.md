## 2. What the Mistress Taught

The first thing Maren Crane ever taught me was how to weigh a toad.

Not a metaphor. An actual toad, fat and indignant, cupped in my nine-year-old palms while she held the brass scales and told me that everything in this world had a price, and the trick to a good life was knowing the price before you paid it. The toad was worth three coppers to a man in Harrow's Bend who made love philtres out of worse things. It was worth nothing at all to the toad, I said, and Maren laughed so hard she had to sit down on her chopping block, and for one shining moment I thought I'd pleased her.

Then she weighed the toad anyway, and we sold it, and I learned the second lesson: pleasing her and being useful to her were two different ledgers, and only one of them got balanced.

I want to be fair to Maren Crane. That's difficult for me, like being fair to a fever, but I'll try, because a good accountant records both sides of an entry. She took me in when my mother died — took me in is generous; she took the house, which came with me attached, like a cellar that cried at night. She fed me. She taught me letters, and numbers, and the grammar of bargains: what a thing costs, what a thing is worth, and the wide gray country between them where hedge-witches make their living. She taught me that magic wasn't candles and chanting. Magic was *terms*. Magic was knowing exactly what you were giving away and making the other poor fool say it out loud twice.

She was, I understand now, preparing me for sale.

But I'm getting ahead of the ledger. Entries go in order. Let me do this properly, because order is the only prayer I've ever trusted.

* * *

Maren lived at the crook of the Drown Road where the marsh started pretending to be farmland, in a house that leaned east like it was eavesdropping on the neighbors. She was not the hag of fireside stories. She had good teeth and a laugh like a cart going over a bridge, and she wore her gray hair in a braid thick as a ship's rope, and when she walked into the market at Harrow's Bend, farmers who wouldn't take coin from a stranger would hand her their whole purse and say, *you know what I need.*

What they needed, usually, was luck. Borrowed luck was our best-selling line — a thimble of it sewn into a coat hem before a horse race, a pinch of it stirred into soup before a courting visit. Luck, Maren taught me, was the most honest commodity in the trade, because everyone knew it ran out. Nobody felt cheated by borrowed luck. They'd had it on loan. They'd known the terms.

It was the other stock that taught me to count twice.

Bottled memories, pale as pond water, lined up on the shelf behind the good dishes. A jar of someone's wedding day, sold by a widower who couldn't bear to keep it and couldn't bear to lose it, so he'd split the difference and sold half. Years of life, decanted from the dying into the ambitious, thick as honey and twice as sweet to swallow. And names — though we never stocked those. Names, Maren said, were above our line. Too dear. Too dangerous. A name was the handle the world used to pick you up, and if you let someone else hold the handle, they could set you down anywhere they liked. Or pour you out.

I asked her once, aged eleven, elbows on her accounting table — my table, really, because I kept it — why anyone sold anything at all. Why not just keep what you had?

She looked at me over her spectacles with something I mistook then for tenderness.

"Because keeping hurts more than selling, little bird. Everything you own, owns you back. Debt runs both directions." She tapped the ledger under my hands. "That's why numbers are kinder than people. A number never pretends to love you."

I wrote that down. I still have it written down somewhere, in a child's careful hand, underlined twice. God help me, I believed it was wisdom. It took me years to hear what it actually was: a woman telling a child not to expect love, so the child wouldn't notice its absence.

* * *

Here is what I was to Maren Crane, itemized:

One pair of hands, small enough for fine work but strong enough for hauling. One head for figures — mine, genuinely rare; I could look at a column of numbers the way other people looked at faces and know instantly which one was lying. One apprentice's labor, unpaid, on the grounds that room and board were compensation, an argument I accepted until I was fourteen and worked out that room and board cost her perhaps four silver a year while my work brought in thirty, at which point I raised it with her and she said, *ah, but I'm teaching you the craft*, and I said, *then put it in the ledger*, and she laughed that cart-over-a-bridge laugh and did not put it in the ledger.

And one name. Ada Crane. Which she'd given me herself, or so I thought — taken me in, named me, made me hers the way you brand a calf. I wore that name like it fit. It never once occurred to me that a name could be inventory.

I found out the way I found out most things: through arithmetic that didn't balance.

* * *

I was sixteen. I was doing the quarterly reconciliation, which meant sitting up late with candle stubs and cold tea, matching what we'd earned against what we owed, and Maren's accounts had always been a little soft around the edges — debts to suppliers in the capital, a standing obligation to somebody called the Ferryman that she never explained and I never asked about, because asking questions about the Ferryman felt like knocking on the bottom of a boat.

That quarter, the soft edges weren't soft. They were bleeding.

We owed more than we had. Not by much — Maren was too good for ruin — but by enough, and the shortfall had a shape. It had been growing for years, steady as a taproot, and every quarter she'd covered it by selling a little deeper into stock we couldn't spare. Luck reserves: down. Memory stock: down. Her own years, I saw with a lurch — she'd been decanting her own life to cover interest, selling herself to herself, a circle with no bottom.

I sat there past midnight doing what I always did when the numbers frightened me, which was to make them balance on paper just to see what it would take. Sell the house. Sell the stock. Sell the scales. Even then, even stripping us to bare walls, the hole stayed open like a mouth.

There was one asset left unlisted. Not hidden — Maren didn't hide things, she just didn't write them down, which is different and worse. But I knew her habits, and I knew where she kept the papers she didn't file, and I knew that a debt to the Ferryman was settled in a particular currency, because I'd once seen the receipt.

I should have gone to bed. I want that recorded: I knew it was a door I shouldn't open, and I opened it because I was sixteen and the numbers were screaming and I thought that if I understood the shape of the disaster, I could fix it. That belief has cost me more than anything else I've ever owned. I still haven't entirely stopped believing it.

The papers were under the loose brick behind the stove, wrapped in oilcloth. Most of it was what I expected — notes of debt, receipts, a contract with the Ferryman's mark on it, which was not a signature but a long horizontal stroke, like water seen edge-on.

And one document, older than the rest, written in Maren's hand but in the formal script she used for high-value sales. A bill of sale.

For a name.

*Ada Crane*, it said. *One true name, freely surrendered by the guardian thereof, price and terms overleaf.* And beneath it, in the space where the seller signs, Maren's signature — dated, witnessed, complete except for one thing. The buyer's line was blank. She hadn't found a buyer yet. Or she hadn't settled on a price.

Freely surrendered. By the guardian thereof. As though the name were a heifer and I were merely the field it stood in.

I remember very clearly that I didn't cry. I remember thinking, with a clarity that has never left me: *she's been fattening me.* All those lessons. All that arithmetic. Every hour she'd spent making me precise, capable, trustworthy — I had read it as inheritance. It was husbandry. You don't teach a thing you intend to keep. You teach a thing to raise its value.

And underneath that thought, quieter, the one that actually broke something: *the toad.* Nine years old, cupping a toad, learning that everything has a price. She hadn't been teaching me about the world.

She'd been weighing me.

* * *

I did not run that night. That surprises people when I tell this story; they expect the dramatic flight, the window, the road. But I was my mother's daughter and Maren's apprentice both, and I did the thing that came most naturally.

I audited.

For six weeks I gathered evidence. I copied the bill of sale in my own hand and hid the copy inside the binding of a book on drainage charms, because nobody has ever opened a book on drainage charms. I tracked the Ferryman's debt back through five years of entries and mapped its growth like a fever chart. I learned things about Maren's finances that Maren herself likely didn't know, including the fact that she'd already taken inquiries — letters, unsigned, asking after the availability of *a young name, unencumbered, trained*. Three letters. Three buyers sniffing around. She'd answered none of them yet, which meant she was holding out for a better price, which meant I had time, which meant — and here is the accountant's curse — I could calculate exactly how many days I was worth.

Forty-one days, as it turned out. That was when the largest buyer's letter said the offer would lapse.

I planned my departure the way I planned everything: line by line. What to take — warm clothes, the drainage-charm book with its secret spine, the scales (sentimental, heavy; I took them anyway), and the ledger. Not our sales ledger. My own, the practice book where I'd copied every lesson, every formula, every price Maren had ever taught me. If she was going to sell my name out from under me, I was going to walk out of that marsh knowing precisely what everything cost, including her.

What I could not solve, in all my columns, was the question underneath: *why?* Not the money — I understood the money; the Ferryman's debt was real and hungry. But she'd had other ways. She had stock. She had her own remaining years, thirty of them at least, and a buyer in the capital who paid well for vintage. Selling my name was not necessity. It was preference. A name like mine — unencumbered, trained, young — fetched a premium, and Maren Crane had never in her life left a premium on the table.

Unless — and I only understood this years later, in the Gilded Veil's archive, staring at a contract with a familiar signature — unless she'd owed someone a name specifically. Unless certain debts are only payable in certain coin, and somewhere in her tangled books there was an entry that said *one name, owing*, and mine was simply the nearest one to hand.

I don't forgive her for that either. Understanding isn't forgiveness. I want that in the record too.

* * *

On the last night, she almost caught me. Of course she did; she was Maren Crane, and I was the student, and students are always one lesson behind.

I was at the stove, prying up the brick for the final time, when her voice came from the doorway, easy as morning: "Couldn't sleep either, little bird?"

I had the oilcloth bundle against my chest and nowhere to put it. So I did the only thing I could think of. I turned around and lied to her with a straight face and exact numbers — the first great bargain of my life, struck at seventeen, terms spoken aloud.

"The Ferryman's debt," I said. "I've been through it. Interest compounds at midsummer, not quarter-day. You've been paying early for six years. You've overpaid eleven crowns and four shillings."

Silence. Then the cart-over-the-bridge laugh, delighted, genuine. "Have I? Show me."

"Tomorrow," I said. "Tonight I want to sleep."

And she believed me — or she wanted the eleven crowns so badly she chose to believe me — and she went to bed, and I lay on my pallet listening to her snore and feeling the strangest grief of my life, because part of me had wanted her to catch me. Wanted her to say *don't go, you're not stock, you're mine, mine like family, not like inventory.* Wanted one entry in the ledger that wasn't a transaction.

She snored on. The marsh ticked and gurgled outside. I got up before dawn, left the eleven crowns' correction on the table where she'd find it — because I wanted the last thing between us to balance, even if nothing else ever had — and walked down the Drown Road with the light coming up gray behind me, and I did not look back, because looking back is an expense and I was done spending.

* * *

People assume I hate magic. I don't. Magic is just terms; I respect terms. What I hate is the moment of the scales — the instant when someone looks at you and sees not a person but a sum, and starts doing arithmetic with your insides. Maren did it without malice, which is the worst version. Malice at least admits you matter enough to hate.

So yes: ledgers. Numbers. Columns that add up or don't, and never pretend otherwise. When I fled to the city with a stolen practice book and eleven crowns of expertise, and Vess of the Gilded Veil looked over my figures and hired me on the spot — *you count like someone who's been burned*, she said, and I said, *counting is what keeps you from burning twice* — I thought I'd finally found work that couldn't lie to me.

A ledger doesn't love you. Fine. Neither does a knife, and knives are honest about cutting.

I built myself a life out of entries. Inventory in, inventory out. Bottled memories, borrowed luck, years of life — impossible things, all of them, but each one priced, each one logged, each one *known*. Nothing in the Gilded Veil would ever sneak up on me the way that bill of sale had. Nothing would ever be unwritten. That was the whole religion of it: if it's in the book, it can't be denied.

I believed that for six years. Right up until the night the stars went out, and Vess went white over an old ledger page, and I learned that some entries get written so deep in a book's history that nobody living remembers signing them.

Including, as it turned out, the person whose name was on page one.

But I'm ahead of the ledger again. Entries go in order. And the next entry begins with a sound like tearing silk.
