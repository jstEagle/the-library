## 3. A Night Without Stars

The trouble with impossible things, I've learned, is that they're never impossible in the way you'd hope. Bottled memories don't shimmer. Borrowed luck doesn't glow. A year of life, decanted into a stoppered vial, looks exactly like pond water with pretensions. The impossibility is all in the paperwork.

That's why the ledgers matter. Anyone can sell a miracle; it takes real discipline to account for one.

I was three columns deep in the autumn reconciliation — cross-referencing our stock of bottled Tuesdays against the delivery manifest from a client who insisted he'd paid us in advance and was, per my records, lying — when the first ward fell.

I didn't hear it so much as feel it, the way you feel a tooth loosen before it hurts. Somewhere north of the city, past the tanneries and the old toll road, something that had been holding for three hundred years stopped holding. The ink on my page shivered. My quill skipped a line.

"Damn," I said, to no one, because the counting room at midnight contains only me, the lamps, and forty-one years of guild accounts arranged by fiscal quarter. This is, I will note, my favorite company in the world.

The Gilded Veil keeps its books in a cellar beneath the counting house, because paper is the only truly valuable thing we own and everyone knows it but no one says it. Above us: the showroom, where clients browse shelves of small glass sorrows and larger glass joys. Above that: Vess's office, where the guild master conducts whatever it is guild masters conduct. And above everything, the roof, where Isandro sometimes goes to look at stars he swears aren't there yet.

He'd drawn one of them last week. A star over the eastern ridge, faint, penciled in with his peculiar careful hand, labeled *not yet*. When I'd asked him what that meant, he'd shrugged and said, "It means it isn't there. It means it's coming." As if that were an answer. As if anything about Isandro were ever an answer instead of a very polite deferral.

The second ward went while I was still frowning at my quill.

This one I heard. Not loudly — that was the horror of it. A sound like tearing silk, somewhere far above and far away, long and slow and unhurried, as though the night itself were being unwrapped by someone with excellent manners and all the time in the world. The lamps guttered. The ink shivered again, harder this time, and a drop fell from my nib and bloomed across the Tuesday column like a wound.

I sat very still and did arithmetic, which is what I do instead of panicking. Three wards on the northern border. Two had fallen within a quarter hour of each other. Wards do not fail in pairs. Wards are not dominoes. Wards are load-bearing, and when one goes, the others strain to carry its weight, and if a second has gone—

The third made a sound I felt in my back teeth.

Then the lamps went out.

Not guttered. *Out.* All of them, at once, the way a held breath is let go. And through the high slit windows of the counting room came light that wasn't lamplight — a gray, sourceless gloom, the color of a sky trying to remember what it was for.

I climbed the stairs two at a time, which I never do, because stairs and I have an understanding. The showroom was dark. The street beyond the windows was dark. And overhead, where the stars should have been —

Nothing.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only prayer I know. It wasn't cloud cover. Clouds have texture, movement, the suggestion of something behind them. This was absence. Every star in the sky, extinguished in the same instant, the way you snuff a row of candles with one sweep of your hand. The moon was up — I'd seen it on my walk to work — and now it hung in the black like a coin dropped down a well, giving off less light than a dying ember.

Somewhere below me, in the cellar full of honest numbers, my ledgers were fine. Everything balanced. That was somehow the worst part. The sky could end and my columns would still sum correctly, because sums don't care.

* * *

Vess was already awake when I reached her office. Of course she was. Vess Aldery sleeps the way cats sleep — lightly, and with one ear open for trouble — and I have privately suspected for years that she hears the wards the way sailors hear weather.

She stood at her desk with a ledger open before her, and I want to describe her face carefully, because I've spent three years learning to read that face the way I read figures, and what I saw frightened me more than the dark sky did.

Vess is not a woman who pales. She's negotiated with things that eat names. She once stared down a debt-collector from the Salt Court until it apologized. Her complexion is a fixed point in an unstable world. But standing over that open ledger, lamplight — candlelight, rather; someone had lit the office candles — catching the underside of her jaw, she looked like paper left too long in the sun. Faded. Old, suddenly, in a way that had nothing to do with years.

"Ada," she said, without looking up. "Close the door."

I closed the door.

"Do you know what tonight is?" she asked.

"It's the fourteenth," I said. "Fiscal third-quarter close is in seventeen days, and if you're about to tell me the sky going out affects the delivery schedule—"

"The sky going out *is* the delivery schedule." She turned the ledger toward me at last. "Look."

It was an old book. Older than any in the cellar — older than the guild itself, by the smell of it, leather gone soft and brown as autumn pears, corners rounded by centuries of hands. The page she'd opened to was written in a hand I didn't recognize, formal and cramped, the letters pressed hard enough to emboss the paper behind them.

And at the top of the page, in ink that had gone the color of dried blood:

*Payment deferred until the night the sky forgets us.*

Below it, terms. Below the terms, a signature. I read it twice, then a third time, and each time my eyes slid off the name the way they slide off a figure you already know is wrong — that little itch at the back of the skull, the accountant's instinct that says *fraud lives here.*

"I don't understand what I'm looking at," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because some part of me understood perfectly and wanted very much not to.

"No," Vess said quietly. "You wouldn't. You weren't meant to. None of us were. That was rather the point." She closed the ledger with both hands, gently, the way you'd close a coffin. "Three generations ago, the founders of this guild bought their fortune from someone beneath the mountain. The price was deferred. Written into the contract. *Until the night the sky forgets us.*" She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "We always assumed it was poetry, Ada. Founders love poetry. It lets them sign things they couldn't say aloud."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight the sky forgot us."

The candles flickered. Down in the street, I could hear the city waking badly — doors opening, dogs barking, someone shouting about fires that weren't there. Panic travels fast through a city at night. It moves like water finding cracks.

"What does the contract say happens now?" I asked. Because that's what I am, in the end. Ask me how the building burns and I'll ask you who holds the deed.

Vess was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in a voice stripped of everything except the truth: "Collection."

* * *

By the time the bells started — the ward-tower bells, ringing the pattern I'd only ever heard drilled, never rung, the pattern for *the border is open* — Vess had given three orders and I had carried out two of them.

Order one: seal the vaults. I did this myself, walking the cellar's perimeter with the sealing chalk, drawing the guild's marks across every door while my hands shook just enough to make the lines wobble. The vaults hold inventory worth more than the city. They also hold the contracts. If collection was coming, it would come looking for paper first. Paper is where debts live.

Order two: wake the inner circle. Bram first. I sent the apprentice Tammy running with a message, and she went pale when she saw the sky and paler when she saw my face, and I told her, "Don't look up, it doesn't help," which is the most honest thing I've said all year.

Order three, Vess gave herself, standing in the middle of her office with the old ledger under her arm like a shield: "Find Isandro."

"He'll be on the roof," I said. "He's always on the roof when—" I stopped. Because I'd been about to say *when the sky does something strange*, and the sky had never done anything strange before, and yet I'd said it like a known fact, like a quarterly date, like something entered in a ledger years ago and waiting only for confirmation.

Vess looked at me sharply. "When what?"

"Nothing," I said. "Roof. I'll go."

* * *

Isandro was on the roof, yes. But he wasn't looking at the empty sky.

He was drawing.

He sat cross-legged near the roof's edge with his portfolio across his knees, working by the dead moon's failing light, and even from the stairwell door I could see that his hand was moving fast — faster than I'd ever seen it move, and Isandro is a man whose fastest work looks like other people's slowest. He wasn't drawing the city. He wasn't drawing the ridge or the river or any of the places that don't exist yet, which is his usual trade and his usual excuse.

He was drawing a staircase. Going down.

"Isandro," I said.

He didn't startle. That frightened me. Isandro notices everything — a door opening, a change in breathing, a lie forming three sentences before it's spoken. But he didn't turn, didn't lift his pencil, just kept laying down lines, quick and sure and wrong, wrong, wrong, because I could see even from ten feet away that the staircase he was drawing had no bottom.

"Stars went out," I said, crossing to him. "All of them. At once. Vess wants the inner circle in her office, and she specifically said to find you, and I'm supposed to tell you that the wards are down and—"

"I know," he said.

"You *know*?"

His pencil paused. He looked up at me then, and his face in the moonless dark was calm in a way that made my chest hurt, because it wasn't peace, it was resignation, and those look identical from the outside and are nothing alike within.

"Ada," he said, "look at the map."

I looked. The staircase descended page after page — he'd clipped loose sheets together, a dozen of them, twenty, each one continuing the last, each one darker, tighter, the walls closing in as the lines went down. And at the bottom of the last sheet, drawn in a hand steadier than his own handwriting ever was, waited a door.

Beneath the door, in letters small enough to miss: *not yet*.

"Isandro," I said slowly, "when did you draw this?"

"I don't know." His voice was flat. Honest. Terrified in the specific way of a man reporting symptoms to a physician. "I found it in my portfolio an hour ago. All of it. I don't remember drawing any of it." He looked down at his hands, and I watched them shake, and watched him notice them shaking, and watch him try to make them stop. "My hands knew before I did."

Down in the city, the bells kept ringing. Up above us, the sky stayed empty. And between those two facts, on a rooftop belonging to a guild built on a debt none of us understood, my only friend sat holding a map of a place he'd never been, drawn by hands that remembered what his mind had been made to forget.

"Come inside," I said. "Vess needs to see this."

He rose. He folded the map along its creases, careful, precise, the way he folds everything, and tucked it against his chest like something he was afraid would be taken from him.

"Ada," he said, at the stairwell door. "Whatever's down there — whatever this is — don't let them put it in a ledger."

"Why not?"

"Because," Isandro said, "ledgers are how they find you."

And we went down together, out of the dark and the cold, toward the one lit window in the Gilded Veil, where Vess Aldery waited with a three-hundred-year-old contract and a face like fading paper, and where the first bell of the night finally stopped ringing — not because the danger passed, but because whoever rang it had realized, all at once, that the wards hadn't fallen.

They'd been opened. From underneath.
