## 4. The Founding Debt

I have kept the Gilded Veil solvent through two currency collapses, one plague of forgetting that took the eastern quarter's entire childhood, and a fire that ate our warehouse of bottled grief down to the shelves. I have haggled with things that wear their teeth on the outside and signed contracts with entities whose signatures were still wet from the last century. I am not easily rattled.

But when the stars went out — all of them, at once, like candles pinched by a single enormous hand — I did what any sensible guild master does.

I went to my office, poured myself three fingers of plum brandy, and unlocked the drawer I have not opened since my predecessor pressed the key into my palm along with her regrets.

The ledger inside was older than me. Older than the building. Its cover was the color of dried blood, which I had always assumed was dye, because assuming it was anything else made the book harder to shelve.

I sat with it for a long time before I opened it. That is the thing about dread: it arrives quietly, through bookkeeping rather than battle. No horns. No armies. Just a page you have known about your whole life, waiting for the night you finally have to turn it.

* * *

The inner circle assembled in the map room at half past midnight, because Bram insisted that if we were all going to be ruined or devoured, we should at least do it somewhere with good sightlines to the doors. He'd posted two of his people outside anyway. Old habits. The Veil has been robbed exactly once in ninety years, and Bram talks about it the way other men talk about a lost war.

Veyra came up from the vaults still wearing her gloves, the ones she uses for handling bottled memories, and she would not take them off. Sable arrived smelling of ink and panic, which for Sable is standard. And Ada came last, carrying the night's ledgers under her arm like a shield she didn't know she was holding.

I almost sent her away. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head: I looked at that sharp-eyed girl who balances our impossible books and I thought, *she doesn't need to carry this too.* She came here fleeing something. You can see it in how she counts everything twice, as though arithmetic might abandon her the way people did.

But she was already in the room, and there are some debts a guild cannot pay without its accountant.

"Close the door," I said.

Bram closed it. Then he stood in front of it, arms crossed, which was his way of saying he'd already decided this meeting was either an ambush or a confession, and he was prepared for both.

"I'm going to tell you something," I said, "and then none of you are going to speak for a while, because if you speak immediately you'll say something stupid, and I will remember it, and I hold grudges the way the Undercourt holds collateral."

Sable laughed. Nobody else did. Sable laughs at funerals; it's how he keeps his hands steady.

"The stars," Veyra said. Her voice was very quiet. "You called us here about the stars."

"I called you here about a contract." I set the old ledger on the table between us. It landed with a sound heavier than a book that size should make. "Three generations ago, the woman who founded this guild — Ondine Vess, my great-grandmother's employer, and yes, the name is not a coincidence, we've all been keeping her accounts ever since — needed capital. Real capital. Not coin. Coin can be stolen; she wanted something no thief could touch. So she went beneath the mountain, and she made a bargain."

"With the Undercourt," Veyra said. She said it the way you'd say *with the plague.*

"With the Undercourt," I confirmed. "And the terms were generous. Absurdly generous. Gold enough to found the guild. Wards strong enough to keep our inventory from eating each other. A charter of protection written into the mountain itself. In exchange, she gave them—"

"A soul," Ada said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't raised her voice. She was looking at the ledger the way she looks at a column of numbers that won't balance — not horrified, exactly. More like she'd caught the error early and was bracing herself for how far back it went.

"You know this?" I asked.

"I know arithmetic," she said. "Generous terms are never generous. If someone offers you more than the thing is worth, the difference isn't a gift. It's interest." She paused. "Whose soul?"

And there it was. The question I had spent thirty years making sure no one would ask me, sitting in the mouth of a girl who'd been with us barely a season, asked plainly, because she hadn't yet learned that some questions in this guild are load-bearing.

"That's the part," I said, "that I need you all to hear before anyone throws anything."

I opened the ledger to the first page. The contract was written there in Ondine Vess's own hand — or mostly in it. The final clause was in another script entirely, thin and silvered, the kind of writing that seems to move slightly when you're tired. I had read it once, at twenty-two, the night Marisol handed me the key, and I had not slept properly for a decade afterward.

"The debt was deferred," I said. "That was the genius of it, if you want to call fraud and damnation genius. Ondine didn't sell a soul outright. She sold one *payable on demand*, with the demand date specified in advance. The Undercourt named the condition. They always name the condition; it's how they make sure they win. The clause reads: payment due upon *the night the sky forgets us.*"

"The wards," Bram said flatly. "The border wards fell tonight. With the stars."

"The wards fell *because* of the stars," Veyra corrected him, and her gloved hands were shaking now, just slightly. "They were anchored to starlight. Everyone knows that. It's why the founding charter was so cheap to maintain — the Undercourt underwrote it. We thought—" She stopped. Recalculated. I watched her arrive at the same sum I had arrived at an hour ago, alone, with brandy. "We thought the protection was a gift."

"It was a leash," I said. "Every ward, every charter, every year of easy trade — it was all interest accruing against a principal we never looked at directly. Because looking at it meant reading the clause. And the clause meant knowing that someday the sky would go dark, and on that night, the guild would owe the court beneath the mountain a human soul."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind, mixed together like sediment in old wine.

It was Sable who broke it, because Sable always breaks silence; it's his function, like a fuse. "Whose?" he said. "Whose soul, Vess? Because if the answer is *whoever we choose*, then this is a horrible problem. And if the answer is *someone specific*, then this is a different kind of horrible, and I'd like to know which before I finish this drink."

I turned the ledger toward them.

"The contract names its collateral," I said. "Ondine didn't choose. She couldn't have; the Undercourt required the debtor to be bound to the debt itself, blood and bone, so it couldn't be transferred or dodged. The soul owed is the soul of the founder's line." I tapped the page. "Except it isn't. Read the signature."

Ada leaned forward first. Of course she did. She reads faster than any of us and trusts slower.

"That's not a Vess name," she said slowly.

"No."

"This handwriting—" Her finger hovered over the silvered script without touching it, the way you hover over a stove you already know is hot. "This hand is old. Older than the document claims to be. Look at the letterforms. This style of script hasn't been used in—" She stopped. I watched her do the thing she does, the terrible efficient thing, where she takes scattered facts and makes them add up whether you want them to or not. "Who is Isandro?"

Bram moved. Just slightly. A shift of weight, the way a man shifts when he realizes the room he's guarding has a second door.

"He's our cartographer," Veyra said. "He's been with the guild eleven years. He draws maps of places that don't exist yet. Why are you asking about—"

"Because the signature on the founding contract," Ada said, and her voice had gone very careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something fragile across a long distance, "is his name."

* * *

I let them argue for a quarter hour. It seemed kinder than talking over them, and I needed the time myself, if I'm honest. Even now, even having read the page a hundred times in my head since midnight, hearing it spoken aloud in the map room — in the room where Isandro's maps hang on every wall, the beautiful impossible ones, the cities that won't exist for fifty years rendered in his patient gray ink — even now it felt less real and more real at the same time, which I believe is the definition of a curse.

Bram wanted to deny it. "Names repeat," he said. "Half the families in this city share three surnames. It proves nothing."

"It proves the debt has a face," Veyra said, "and the face eats lunch in our kitchen."

Sable wanted to know if the contract could be renegotiated. "Everything can be renegotiated. I've talked a river spirit out of drowning a client. I've talked a widow out of her own ghost. There's always a counteroffer."

"There isn't," I said, "when the counterparty wrote the original terms specifically to prevent one. Read the clause again. *Payment due upon the night the sky forgets us.* Not 'payment may be tendered.' Not 'a soul of equivalent value.' The contract doesn't say we owe *a* soul. It says we owe *the* soul. The one named. There is no substitution clause. I've looked. For thirty years, I've looked."

"Why thirty years?" Ada asked.

Ah. There it was. The question underneath the question, the one she'd been circling since she walked in.

"Because Marisol told me the night she gave me the key," I said. "She sat where you're sitting, poured the same brandy, and told me: *one day the sky will go dark, and on that day you will open this drawer, and you will hate me for not burning this book.* And I asked her why she didn't burn it, and she said—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I took a breath and finished. "She said, *because the debt exists whether we look at it or not, and a guild that stops looking at its debts deserves what collects them.*"

"So she knew," Bram said. "Your predecessor knew. And hers. Three generations of guild masters, all sitting on this, all waiting for the sky to fall, and none of you—" He caught himself. Unclenched his jaw with visible effort. "None of you told the rest of us. We built lives here. I built a *company* here. On top of a debt we were all going to pay for?"

"We paid for it every day," I said. "In ignorance. Which is cheaper than souls, but not free."

"And the boy?" Veyra asked softly. "Does he know?"

"No," I said. "And before anyone volunteers to tell him — think carefully. The debt collects through knowledge as much as through time. The clause requires the debtor to be *called*. The calling starts when the debtor understands what he owes. Every hour he doesn't know is an hour he's still whole."

"How do you know that?" Ada asked.

"Because Marisol tested it," I said. "Fifty years ago, a guild master's apprentice read the first page by accident. Told Isandro's predecessor — the cartographer before him, a man named Ellery, who held the same position, drew the same maps, had the same name." I watched their faces do the arithmetic. All except Ada's. Hers had gone still and white and terribly clear. "Ellery began forgetting within the week. First small things. Then larger. Within a month he walked out of the guild and into the mountain, smiling, and we never saw him again. Marisol erased the apprentice's memory of the page herself. It cost her ten years of her own life to do it cleanly. She told me it was the best trade she ever made."

"The same name," Ada said. "The same position. Eleven years." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and precise, all at once, which I have since learned is simply her face. "He's not the first one. He's the third. Or the thirtieth. The guild has been feeding the mountain cartographers for generations and calling it employment."

I could have lied to her. I want that noted too: I considered it, briefly, the way you consider a door marked *exit* during a fire, and discarded it, because Ada Crane is the sort of person who audits exits.

"Yes," I said. "We call it a tradition. Maps of places that don't exist yet. We told ourselves it was a gift — that they saw farther than other men. We never asked why every one of them was drawn to the mountain."

* * *

The meeting broke apart near dawn, or what passes for dawn when the sky is a blank gray ceiling with nothing behind it. Bram went to double his watch. Veyra went down to the vaults to check the wards' anchoring, though we both knew what she'd find. Sable stayed longest, turning his empty glass in slow circles, and finally said, "There's always a counteroffer," again, quieter, like a prayer he didn't believe in, and left.

Ada stayed too. She stood at the head of the table with the ledger open in front of her, and she read the contract end to end, twice, her lips moving slightly, and I recognized the posture because I'd worn it myself at twenty-two: a person trying to find the flaw in a thing everyone says is flawless.

"You'll want to close the archive," she said at last, without looking up. "The sealed section. Tonight's events will be logged, and logs get read."

"They will."

"And Isandro—" She stopped. Started again, in that careful carrying-voice. "You said the calling begins with knowledge. But he's been drawing descent maps for weeks. Before the wards fell. Before any of this. His hands shake when he inks the lower passages. I've seen it."

I had known that too. I will admit it here, in the privacy of my own guilt: I had noticed the maps, and I had told myself they were artistic obsession, and I had slept at night, most nights.

"Then the calling may not wait for our permission," I said. "Which means whatever we decide to do, we decide quickly."

She closed the ledger with both hands, gently, the way you close a coffin.

"What would you do," she asked, "if it were your name in that book?"

And I, who have run this guild for thirty years on the principle that every question has a price and most prices are payable, found I had no answer ready. So I told her the truth instead, which is rarer and costs more:

"I'd read every line," I said. "Twice. And then I'd find the mistake. Because there's always a mistake, Ada. Contracts are written by hands, and hands lie, and hands tire, and hands make errors. The Undercourt writes perfect law — but they write it *through* people. And people are where the flaw hides."

She looked at me for a long moment. Something settled behind her eyes, some decision being entered into a ledger I couldn't see.

"Then I'll need access to the sealed archive," she said. "Tonight. Before anyone else touches it."

"Ada—"

"You said it yourself," she said, already moving toward the door, already halfway into whatever trouble she was about to make for both of us. "A guild that stops looking at its debts deserves what collects them. I intend to look very, very closely."

I should have stopped her. I have replayed that moment perhaps four hundred times since, and I always arrive at the same conclusion: I could no more have stopped Ada Crane than I could have stopped the sky from forgetting us.

I unlocked the archive myself. That much, at least, I could give her — a key instead of a cage, for whatever the difference is worth on a night with no stars.

She was gone down the stairs before the lock finished turning.
