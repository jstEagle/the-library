## 5. Isandro's Map Has No Bottom

Three days after the sky went dark, I stopped sleeping.

Not from fear, exactly. Fear is a thing with edges, and what kept me awake had none — just the low hum of the guild at night, the vaults sealed behind three feet of stone and iron, and somewhere beneath it all a silence like held breath. I'd taken to doing the accounts by candlelight because the numbers were the only thing in the world that still behaved. Debits on the left. Credits on the right. Everything accounted for.

Everything except one page.

The Gilded Veil trades in impossible things, but even impossible things have weight, and lately the inventory had been shifting. Bottled memories that came back lighter than they went in. A jar of borrowed luck that had gone cloudy, like milk left out. Vess had ordered the ledgers reconciled twice, and both times the totals refused to sit still, drifting by fractions so small you'd miss them if you weren't me — and I was always me, unfortunately, down to the last copper of my attention.

So on the third night I gave up on sleep entirely and went looking for Isandro, because if anyone could tell me why the guild's arithmetic had gone soft, it would be him.

He kept a workroom at the top of the map-tower, under the roof where the guild kept its charts of places that didn't exist yet. That sounds like madness if you haven't seen his work. Isandro draws maps of cities that won't be built for fifty years, roads that will be cut through hills not yet quarried, harbors where no river has decided to run. Merchants pay obscene sums for them. Vess says it's because he has the sight. Isandro shrugs and says he just draws what the paper wants. I've watched him do it, and I'll tell you honestly: the paper wants things. I've seen it tug against his hand like a dog on a lead.

I climbed the tower stairs with a candle and a ledger under my arm, rehearsing how I'd ask him about the drifting totals without sounding like a woman coming apart at the seams. The door was ajar. Candlelight spilled through it, thin and gold, and underneath it I heard the sound of a nib scratching paper — fast, urgent, the way nobody writes who isn't afraid of running out of time.

"Isandro? It's past midnight. Even the ghosts are asleep."

No answer. I pushed the door open.

He was bent over the drafting table, and he did not look up.

I want to be fair to that moment, because fairness is all I have some days. Isandro is the quietest man I know — quiet the way deep water is quiet, all the noise happening far below the surface. In two years of friendship he has never once failed to look up when I came into a room. It's a small courtesy, but courtesies are how I take a person's measure, and his had never wavered.

Now he sat hunched like a man sheltering from rain, drawing.

I set the candle down and looked over his shoulder, and the words I'd rehearsed died somewhere in my throat.

It was a map of the mountain.

Not the guild's mountain — well, yes, ours too, our own dear Gilded Veil perched on its shoulder like a barnacle — but the mountain proper, the one that loomed north of the city with the old wards carved into its roots, the one every child learns not to dig near. He'd drawn it before, of course. Every cartographer has. But this wasn't a surveyor's map. This was a descent. The lines went *down*, terrace after terrace, gallery after gallery, each one smaller than the last, spiraling inward like the rings of a tree cut crosswise — except trees grow outward from the heart, and this grew inward toward something, and the something at the center of the spiral was drawn as a blank space no bigger than my thumbnail.

Around that blank space he'd written nothing at all. Just left it empty. Which, for a man whose whole gift was filling empty spaces, was the most frightening thing on the page.

"How long have you been working on this?" I asked.

"Since the stars went out." His voice was flat. Distant. "Maybe since before."

"Isandro."

"I don't sleep," he said, in the tone of a man reporting weather. "I sit down to rest my eyes and my hand starts moving. I wake up and there's more of it. Look—" he turned the sheet over, and there were older versions beneath, five, six, seven of them, layered like skins, each one further along than the last. "I keep redrawing it. The same map. I finish it and it's wrong somehow, and I start again."

"Wrong how?"

He hesitated. That hesitation cost him something; I watched it go out of him like heat from a cup.

"The scale keeps changing," he said. "Every version, the bottom of the map is deeper. As if the place itself is... settling. Or as if I'm getting closer to understanding how far down it goes." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old vellum. "There's no bottom, Ada. I've drawn eleven versions and there's no bottom to it. The stair just keeps going."

* * *

Here is what I should have done: I should have gone straight to Vess. Woken her, put the map on her table next to the old ledger page she'd gone pale over, and let the two of them do whatever grim arithmetic guild masters do when the world ends quietly.

Instead I stayed. Because Isandro was my friend, and because there was a thing happening to his hands.

They were shaking. Not the fine tremor of exhaustion — I know that one intimately, we're old acquaintances — but a deeper shake, rhythmic, almost deliberate, like a pulse moving through him from somewhere else. When he tried to steady the nib against the paper, the line wavered. Isandro's lines never wavered. I once saw him draw a perfect circle freehand while drunk on festival wine, and he'd been insufferable about it for a month.

"Your hands," I said.

"They started yesterday." He flexed his fingers, watching them like they belonged to a stranger. "It stops when I draw. Did you notice? While the pen is moving, they're steady. It's only when I stop that they—"

He stopped talking. Stopped moving. And I swear on every ledger I've ever balanced, the pen lifted a fraction off the paper, all on its own, the way iron filings lift toward a lodestone.

He slammed it down flat and looked at me, and his eyes were wrong. Not mad-wrong. *Elsewhere*-wrong. The look of a man hearing his name called from another room and trying very hard not to answer.

"Ada," he said carefully, "why am I drawing this?"

"You tell me."

"I don't know." The words came out fast now, tumbling. "That's what I keep — I don't *know*. There's no commission. Nobody asked. I just — the paper wants it, I told you the paper wants things, but this isn't the paper, this is—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "When I try to think about anything else, my thoughts slide off. Like wet glass. But the mountain — the mountain I can see perfectly. Every terrace. Every gallery. Like I've *been* there."

"Have you?"

"No." And then, quieter, in a voice I'd never heard him use: "I don't think so. I don't — Ada, I can't remember whether I've been anywhere. I tried to remember my mother's face tonight, the way you do, and there's nothing there. Not forgotten — *gone*. There's a shape where the memory should be and the shape is smooth, like a river stone, like something that's been handled so many times the details wore off." He looked up at me. "Did I ever tell you about my mother?"

"You told me she died when you were young. You told me she used to sing while she worked."

"Did I?" He said it with genuine wonder, and my blood went cold in a way I couldn't explain yet, though I would, later, when it was far too late to matter. "That sounds right. That sounds like something true. I can't find it anymore, though. I can't find any of it."

* * *

I did what I do when the world misbehaves. I took notes.

I sat down across from him with my ledger and I made an inventory, because inventory is the closest thing I have to prayer. Item: one map, descending, no bottom. Item: two hands, shaking, steadied only by motion toward the mountain. Item: one friend, missing pieces, aware of the missingness, which is either the mercy or the cruelty depending on your theology. Item: one night, starless, three days old, during which the guild's accounts began to drift and a cartographer began to dig with ink.

And then I wrote the item I didn't want to write, in the little private shorthand I use for things too dangerous for plain script:

*Item: someone is calling him, and he does not know who.*

Because that's what it was. I've spent my life around bargains, first Maren's and then the Veil's, and I know the sound a debt makes when it wakes up. It doesn't roar. It doesn't threaten. It *calls*, politely, patiently, the way a creditor writes letters — each one courteous, each one a little harder to ignore than the last. Whatever owned the bottom of that map had begun sending letters, and Isandro's hand was the only address it had.

"Put the pen down," I said.

"If I put it down, they shake worse."

"Put it down anyway."

He did. They shook. Hard enough that the table buzzed faintly, hard enough that the candleflame seemed to shiver in sympathy, though flames don't sympathize, whatever poets say. And then — and I will carry this image to whatever grave I get — the pen rolled to the edge of the desk, stopped precisely at the edge, and pointed. Downward. Toward the floor. Toward the foundations, and whatever lay beneath them.

Toward the mountain's root.

Neither of us touched it.

"Ada," Isandro said, very calmly, the way people speak when calm is the last wall standing, "what's under the guild?"

"Cellars. Vaults. The sealed archive. Bedrock."

"And under that?"

"Nothing. Bedrock is where 'under' stops. That's what makes it bedrock."

He nodded slowly, and looked at the blank space at the center of his spiral, the thumbnail-sized emptiness he'd left unfilled, and I watched him understand something about it that I didn't yet — something that made him go gray around the mouth.

"It's not blank," he said. "I keep leaving it blank because I can't draw it. Not because it's empty. Because it's *mine*." His voice cracked on the word. "Ada, why would the middle of the mountain be mine?"

And because I am a coward in the ways that count, I said: "I don't know."

But I knew something. Three nights ago, when the wards tore and the stars went out, Vess had opened a ledger I'd never seen, gone white to the lips, and sealed the vaults. Guild masters don't seal vaults over weather. And debts, as I've said, wake politely — but they wake *first* in the collateral.

I looked at my friend, who was being eaten from the inside by a hunger with beautiful manners, and I made a decision that would have horrified Maren Crane, which is the only recommendation it will ever need: I was going to find out what Vess was hiding, even if I had to read every sealed page in this guild to do it.

"Come away from the table," I said. "Eat something. Sleep — or pretend to. I'll stay."

"You don't have to."

"I know." I took the pen off the desk and put it in my pocket, where it lay against my hip like a live coal. "That's rather the point of me."

He almost smiled. It was a ruin of a smile, but it was his, and I counted it as an asset against the dark.

* * *

He slept, finally, in the chair, his shaking hands folded in his lap like a good funeral. I sat across from him with the ledger open on my knees and did not write anything, because there was nothing left to catalog. Instead I watched him breathe and thought about the shape of a debt.

Maren Crane taught me everything I know about owing. She taught me that a debt never sleeps, that it grows in the dark, that it always — *always* — comes due at the moment least convenient to the debtor. She taught me that the cruelest contracts are the ones signed by someone else on your behalf, which is why I have guarded my own name the way other women guard their virtue, and why I will die before I sign anything I haven't read twice.

And she taught me, without meaning to, that the person who pays another's debt is rarely the person who incurred it.

Isandro stirred in his sleep and murmured something. I leaned close. It might have been a name. It might have been a word in a language I didn't know, spoken in a voice too deep to belong to him — a voice like the bottom of a stair that keeps going down.

Outside the tower window, the sky was a black lid, starless, seamless, patient.

Inside my pocket, the pen trembled once, gently, like a compass needle finding north — and pointed down.
