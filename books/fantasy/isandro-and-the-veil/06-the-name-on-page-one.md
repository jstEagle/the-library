## 6. The Name on Page One

There are three ways into the Gilded Veil's sealed archive, and I know about all of them because I keep the books that say where everything is kept.

The first is the official door: iron-banded oak at the end of the cellar corridor, locked with a key Vess wears around her neck and a ward that tastes your intentions like a suspicious cook tasting soup. The second is the coal chute, which hasn't been used since the guild stopped burning coal in favor of bottled fire — a trade good we sell at scandalous markup to people whose hearths have opinions. The third is a gap in the accounting itself.

That third one is mine. Every archive has one, if you look hard enough. Somewhere in the inventory there's always an entry that doesn't reconcile — a crate of borrowed courage listed twice, a shelf of bottled griefs that appears in the winter ledger but not the spring. Bookkeepers assume these are errors. Errors are just doors nobody's opened yet. I'd found this particular discrepancy three years ago, when I was still new enough to flag it honestly, and Vess had looked at my report for a long moment and then told me to correct the entry and never mention it again. I corrected the entry. I also measured the space behind shelf forty-one where the numbers said there ought to be nothing, and found a crawl of darkness exactly as deep as the missing inventory claimed.

I am not proud of how long I sat on that knowledge. I am also not ashamed. A person who runs from a hedge-witch learns to hold her cards close enough to warm her hands on them.

But tonight the stars were still gone — three nights now, a sky like a slate wiped clean — and Vess had ordered the vaults sealed, and Isandro had come to breakfast unable to remember whether he liked honey in his tea, and I had run out of patience the way you run out of candle: all at once, near the end, with the dark pressing in.

So at midnight I took the coal chute.

* * *

Let me tell you what breaking into a place feels like when you've spent three years being grateful to that place. It feels like picking your mother's lock. Your hands know every tumbler by heart and hate themselves for it.

I went down the chute feet-first, skirts tucked, counting the rungs out of habit — twenty-two, same as always, though the last four were slick with something cold that wasn't water. The cellar air hit me the way it always did: ink, dust, and underneath it the faint sweet rot of memories past their keeping. Bottled things breathe, even in the dark. Especially in the dark.

The ward on the official door hummed as I passed it, and I felt it taste me — a pressure behind the eyes like a thumb pressed to wet clay. I thought, very deliberately, about balancing columns. About sums that came out even. Whatever the ward made of that, it let me pass, which either means clean intentions read as boring or the wards are going soft along with the sky. I didn't stop to find out.

Shelf forty-one stood where it always stood, wedged between the luck reserves and a rack of years — actual years, decanted into smoked glass, each one labeled in Vess's cramped hand with the age they'd been taken from and the temperament of the seller. I put my palm flat against the shelf's back panel and pushed where the arithmetic told me to push.

The panel swung inward with a sound like a held breath released.

Beyond it: the true archive. Not the tidy front room where Vess kept the ledgers she showed to auditors and guild inspectors, but the deep stacks — older than the building above them, older maybe than the street, walls of stone that hadn't been quarried so much as persuaded. No lamps burned here, but the dark wasn't total. Along the ceiling ran a faint gray shimmer, like moonlight through dirty ice, and I understood with a lurch that it was starlight. Bottled starlight, banked into the mortar itself. The guild had been skimming the sky for generations and storing the skim in its foundations.

Three nights ago, every star in the sky had gone out at once. And here, under my feet, was where some of them had ended up.

I stood in that cold glow and did what I always do when the world stops making sense: I started counting. Shelves: eleven. Aisles: seven. Ledgers visible from where I stood: more than I could number without moving, which was the point. This was the room where the guild kept the books it didn't want balanced.

Somewhere in it was the founding contract. Vess had gone white over a page of it the night the stars died — I'd seen her through the office door, seen her hands flat on the desk like a woman holding down a map in wind. She'd ordered the vaults sealed within the hour. Which meant the contract mattered, which meant it was dangerous, which meant it was exactly where I needed to be.

I'm aware this logic would horrify any sensible person. Sensible people don't descend into cellars at midnight while the sky is broken. But sensible people also don't watch their only friend lose his own history one spoonful at a time and stand politely aside while the grown-ups decide his fate in council. Bram wanted to run. Vess wanted to pay. Nobody — nobody — had asked what the debt actually *was*. And I had learned from Maren Crane, may her garden rot, that the first rule of any bargain is to read the contract before anyone tells you what it says.

I started with the oldest shelf.

* * *

The deep stacks organize themselves by age, which sounds simple until you realize the guild trades in time and therefore has inventory older than the concept of shelving. The earliest ledgers weren't paper. They were vellum scraped thin as onion skin, and before those, bark, and before those, things I chose not to touch — tablets of some black material that drank the starlight off the walls and gave back a warmth like a sleeping animal. I gave those a wide berth. Some entries you don't need to read to know they're about you.

Two hours in, my fingers black with vellum dust, I found it.

It wasn't hidden. That was the thing that stopped me cold, crouched in the gray light with my heart going like a debt collector's knuckles. The founding contract lay in a plain cedar box on the second shelf of the seventh aisle, unlabeled, unwarded, resting as casually as a receipt in a drawer. As if whoever put it there had never once imagined someone would come looking. As if the truth of it had been safe precisely because no one would believe it.

Or as if it had been waiting.

I lifted it out. The box smelled of cedar and, beneath that, of mountain — cold stone, deep water, the mineral breath of places the sun has never audited. The contract inside was a single sheet, longer than my arm, written in two hands. The first was formal, looping, the kind of script that costs extra: the Undercourt's terms, laid out in clauses that made my professional soul flinch. Payment deferred. Consideration received. And the trigger condition, right there in the middle of the page, underlined twice in ink that still gleamed wet after three generations:

*Payable in full upon the night the sky forgets us.*

The night the sky forgets us. Not "the night the wards fail." Not "when the debt comes due." The sky forgot us three nights ago because the sky was *collected* — and I was crouching in the proof of it, in a cellar lit by the stolen change.

I made myself keep reading. Terms of deferral. Interest accrued — and oh, the interest, compounding across decades in figures so elegant they were almost beautiful, the work of a bookkeeper far better than me, or no bookkeeper at all but the debt itself, writing its own arithmetic. My eyes slid down the page toward the signatures, and I braced myself the way you brace for bad news in a letter: already composing your face, already deciding who you'll blame.

Vess's great-grandmother. It had to be. Odile Vess, guild-founder, the name on the plaque downstairs, the woman whose portrait hung in the council room with eyes that followed you to the wine. Three generations of guilt calcified into duty — Vess had said as much herself, that night, her voice flat as a closed ledger. The founder sold a soul to build the Veil. Everyone in the inner circle knew it. It was the worst-kept terrible secret in the guild.

I reached the signature block.

The seller's line was signed. One name, in a hand that was small and careful and utterly certain of itself, the handwriting of someone who had known exactly what they were doing and had done it anyway.

*Isandro.*

I laughed. Actually laughed, one sharp syllable that bounced off the stone and came back wrong. Because obviously it was a mistake, obviously the archivist had misfiled, obviously — except my eyes had already done what my eyes do, what three years of ledgers had trained them to do, which is *check*. And the check failed. There was no second signature. No Odile Vess anywhere on the page. The seller's line bore one name and one name only, and it was the name of the quiet man upstairs who drew maps of places that don't exist yet, who took his tea with honey except when he'd forgotten he liked it, who had taught me the city's rooftops by walking me through maps of them until I could navigate blindfolded.

And the hand — I knew Isandro's hand. I'd watched him letter map legends for hours, that patient draftsman's script with its little flourishes on the descenders. This wasn't it. This was smaller, tighter, older — the penmanship of someone who'd learned to write when ink was mixed by hand and every letter cost you something. A child's diligence in an old man's discipline. A hand too old for him by centuries, wearing his name like a coat cut to measure.

I sat down on the cold floor, because standing had become theoretical.

Here is what I want to be clear about, since I've had three sleepless nights since to turn it over: my first feeling was not horror. My first feeling was *relief*, ugly and immediate — because if Isandro had signed it, then Isandro had chosen it, and a chosen thing can be understood, and an understood thing can perhaps be survived. For one whole breath I stood in the comfortable country of betrayal: he knew, all along, the quiet cartographer with his shaking hands, and he let us plan councils around him —

Then the breath ended and the arithmetic caught up, and I saw what I should have seen first.

The date.

Contracts have dates; it's the first thing you check, before names, before terms, before anything. Maren taught me that, in her way — by trying to slip one past me when I was fourteen, a sale dated a season early so she could collect interest on goods she hadn't yet delivered. I'd caught it. It was the first time I ever caught Maren Crane in anything, and it was the last kindness she ever did me, though she'd have flayed me for calling it that.

The seal on this contract was pressed the winter three generations past. The signature beside it was dated the same day, same hour, in the same old-young hand.

Isandro is twenty-six years old. I know because I do the guild's payroll, and because he told me once, on the roof, the summer we mapped the comet, that he'd been born in a winter so cold the well froze from the bottom up. Twenty-six years. The signature predated him by better than sixty.

Which left exactly two possibilities, and I turned them over like coins, testing their weight.

One: someone else had signed his name. Someone who owned it — because a name, in the trade we practice, is property, and property can be spent by whoever holds the deed. Someone had held Isandro's name the way Vess holds the vault key, and had spent it, quietly, three generations before he was born, and he had walked into this guild as a young man and taken up his pens and never known that he was working inside the shape of his own sale.

Two: he had signed it himself, in some way I couldn't reckon — a life earlier, a self I didn't know, a hand that remembered being old. And if that was true, then the man upstairs wasn't losing his memories to the debt at all.

He was remembering his way back to them.

I don't know which possibility is worse. I've stacked them against each other every night since, and they balance perfectly, which is the most frightening reconciliation I've ever performed. In one, Isandro is a victim twice over — sold before he existed, collateral without his knowledge, a man whose whole life has been a clause in someone else's contract. In the other, he is the author of his own unmaking, and the trembling hands, the vanishing winters, the map he redraws compulsively downward into the mountain — none of it is the debt collecting him.

It's the debt *coming home*.

I copied the signature. I'm not proud of how long I crouched there copying it, stroke by stroke, into my pocket-book, as if a forgery of a signature could tell me more than the original — but hands betray their owners, that's the whole basis of my trade, and I wanted this hand somewhere I could study it in daylight, away from the drinking starlight and the mountain-smell and the box that had waited for me like it knew my name.

Because that was the other thing. The thing I haven't said yet, the thing I set down last in my notes and first in my fear.

When I lifted the contract from its box, the gray light in the walls dimmed — all of it, every vein of banked starlight, dipping like candles in a draft. And when I put it back, it brightened. And in the moment between, in that held-breath dark, I heard — felt — whatever keeps this archive breathing take notice of me. Not the ward at the door, which tastes intentions. Something older, deeper down, patient as accrued interest. It didn't speak. It didn't need to. I am a bookkeeper; I know the feeling of being entered into a ledger.

Somewhere below the cellar, below the stacks, below the mountain's polite surface, a line had been drawn under my name.

I climbed back up the coal chute with the copy of a dead hand folded against my heart, and I stood in the yard behind the guild and looked up at the empty sky, and I made myself say it plainly, the way you enter a loss in the books — no rounding, no fudging, the full figure:

Someone wearing Isandro's name sold a soul to the court beneath the mountain before he was ever born. The debt has come due. And the man it's collecting doesn't remember enough to know what's being taken — which means that if anyone is going to read this contract all the way to the bottom, it has to be me.

I went inside to find him. His lamp was still burning in the map room. It was nearly dawn, and he was drawing again — and when I looked over his shoulder at the fresh lines, my blood went cold all over again, because the stair he was drawing this time wasn't hypothetical anymore.

It had a door at the top. And the door was drawn open.
