## 7. He Doesn't Remember Winter

I woke before dawn the way I always do, with my hands already aching to hold a pen, and for three full seconds I was happy.

Then I reached for my mother's face and found nothing there.

Not a blur. Not a face half-gone at the edges, the way memories fray when you haven't looked at them in years. Nothing. A smooth white absence where her face should have been, like a page someone scraped clean with a knife. I lay in my narrow bed above the map room and did what any sensible man does when his own mind betrays him: I blamed the hour. I blamed the wine from Vess's table the night before. I blamed the strange pressure in the air since the stars went out, which pressed on the skull like deep water and made everyone in the guild walk around slightly deaf, slightly slow, as if we were all underwater together.

My mother's face. Everyone has it. It's the first thing you own.

I got up, lit the lamp, and sat down at my drafting table because sitting still is how the fear gets in. The map of the descent was spread across the boards — the one I've been redrawing every night without quite knowing why, the stair that spirals down through the mountain's roots, each version more detailed than the last, as though some patient hand were feeding me the lines one memory at a time. My hands were steady. That was the strange part. My hands are always steady; they're the steadiest thing about me. But this morning they shook so badly I blotted the ink twice, and when I looked down at the shaking I thought: *this is what it looks like from the outside.* This is what Ada must have seen yesterday, watching me draw the same corridor for the ninth time.

Ada. Good. Start with Ada. Her name is solid in my mouth, warm, like a stone kept in the sun. Ada Crane, who keeps our ledgers, who counts everything twice and trusts nothing once, who brought me tea last winter when I had the fever and scolded me for drinking it while it was too hot and then sat with me until it wasn't. I held onto that the way a drowning man holds a rope, and then, testing, I reached past her —

Winter.

The word arrived alone, unaccompanied. I know the word *winter*. I know snow, cold, the way breath fogs, the smell of woodsmoke off wet wool. But somewhere behind all of that there should have been a specific winter — a particular one, mine, with a particular house and a particular cold and a particular person shoveling the walk or cursing the ice or pressing warm bread into my mittened hands — and there was nothing. Just the concept, floating loose, like a button in a drawer whose coat has been burned.

I stood up too fast and knocked the ink bottle over. Black spread across the lower corner of the map, pooling in the little valley I'd drawn at the mountain's foot, filling it exactly, neatly, as though the ink had been waiting for permission.

*It's fine,* I told myself. *Memory is a leaky vessel. Everyone's is.*

But everyone's leaky vessel doesn't drain all at once, in order, from the bottom up.

* * *

I went looking for proof of myself the way other men go looking for a doctor.

The guild keeps records — of course it does; Ada would sooner let the building burn than let a record go unkept — and among them, in the common room's back shelves, there's an old census ledger, decades stale, listing everyone who ever lodged under the Gilded Veil's roof. I found my own entry. Isandro. Cartographer. Taken in, aged eleven, no family listed.

No family listed.

There should have been a mother. There was a mother — I knew there was a mother the way you know there was a floor beneath you even after the floor is gone, by the shape of where you're standing. I could feel the outline of her, the negative space she left: a woman-shaped hole in my history with warmth around its edges. I remembered — no. I remembered *remembering*. I could recall that once there had been a voice singing off-key in another room, hands that smelled of tallow and thyme, someone who called me by a diminutive I can no longer pronounce because —

Because I couldn't find it. The name she called me. Gone. And with it, apparently, the face, and the voice, and the winter, and whatever town had a walk that needed shoveling.

"Isandro?"

Bram stood in the doorway, arms crossed, filling most of it. The enforcer looked at me the way he looks at everything — as if calculating how much force it would take to remove it from his path.

"You're up early," he said.

"I couldn't sleep."

"You look like a man who saw something in the dark."

"I look like a man who didn't sleep," I said, and managed to make it sound wry, because that's the trick, isn't it? You put the fear in a nice coat and introduce it politely and nobody asks what's underneath.

He grunted and went to the window. Outside, the sky was the color of dirty pewter, starless still, going on four days now. The whole city moved beneath it like people pretending not to notice a corpse at dinner.

"They sealed the vaults," Bram said. "Vess won't say why. You're the one who draws maps of places that don't exist yet. Draw us a way out of this."

"I don't know what *this* is."

"No," he said, turning, "you don't. Funny thing, though." He came closer, and lowered his voice, though there was no one to hear. "Funny thing, the wards fell the same night your maps started drawing themselves downward. Vess thinks I don't notice things. I notice things."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" He studied me for a long moment, and there was something almost gentle buried in all that granite. "You've gone grey around the eyes, cartographer. Whatever's calling you —" he tapped his own temple — "it's collecting."

He left before I could answer, which was a mercy, because I had just realized something and needed very badly not to say it aloud.

*Collecting.* Yes. That was the word. Not losing — losing is random, chaotic, grief-shaped. This was orderly. First the smallest things, the farthest things, working inward with terrible patience, like a clerk settling accounts. Like a ledger being balanced line by line, oldest entries first.

Someone was doing arithmetic with me. And I recognized the handwriting, because it was the same hand that drew my maps.

* * *

I tried to fight it the only way I know how: I wrote everything down.

If my head was a leaking vessel, then paper was a second vessel, and I meant to fill it fast. I sat in the map room with fresh pages and began with what remained, cataloguing myself like inventory. Name: Isandro. Trade: cartographer. Age: twenty-six, or near enough. Likes: strong tea, rain on slate roofs, the sound of Ada arguing with arithmetic. Dislikes: —

I stopped. Dislikes. What did I dislike? I knew I disliked things; the disposition was there, the habit of dislike, but the objects of it had gone strangely thin, like paint worn away by handling. I wrote *crowds*, tentatively, and it felt true, and then I wondered if it felt true because it was true or because I wanted it to be, and the question frightened me more than the emptiness did.

So I moved to harder goods. My eleventh year — the year the census said I arrived here. There should have been ten years before it. Ten years of streets and meals and scraped knees and a language learned and a bed slept in. I reached for them and found the reach going on and on into dark, like lowering a bucket into a well that has no bottom, and the bucket coming up empty, and empty, and empty.

Eleven years old. No family listed.

Had I been sold? Given away? Had there been a debt? Children arrive at the Gilded Veil the way driftwood arrives at a shore, and nobody asks the sea its reasons, and I had never asked mine, because I had never needed to. I had a trade. I had steady hands. I had a place at a long table where Ada saved me the seat across from hers and Vess pretended not to notice that the two quietest people in her guild had become each other's whole conversation.

That was enough. That had always been enough.

Except now the sea was asking to be paid, and it was taking its reasons back one at a time.

By midday I had filled nine pages. By late afternoon I could no longer remember whether the tea I liked was taken with honey or without, and I stared at my own handwriting — *strong tea* — and felt the floor tilt, because a preference is such a small thing, such a cheap thing, and losing it shouldn't hurt, and it hurt like a tooth coming out.

Ada found me there at dusk, surrounded by pages, and read the situation off my face in about a second and a half, because she reads everything, it's her curse and her gift.

"What's wrong," she said. Not a question. An audit.

"Nothing that nine pages can't fix," I said, and pushed them toward her, and watched her read, and watched her face do the thing it does when the numbers stop adding up — a small tightening at the corners of her eyes, the accountant's alarm.

"This says you don't remember your mother."

"It says I don't remember her face. It's different."

"How is it different?"

"A face you can lose gradually," I said. "Like a coin wearing smooth. This isn't worn. This is *removed*. There's a difference between erosion and theft, Ada, and I'm an expert in lines drawn on surfaces, and these lines are clean."

She set the pages down with exaggerated care, the way she handles anything valuable, and said, "When did it start?"

"The night the stars went out."

Something crossed her face then — quick, suppressed, filed away for later. She's been carrying something since that night; I've seen it riding her shoulder like a crow. Everyone has. Vess aged five years in an evening. The vaults are sealed. And now my past is being repossessed, entry by entry, oldest first.

"There's a contract," Ada said slowly, as if testing whether the words would bear weight. "An old one. From before Vess. Before Vess's grandmother, maybe. I think — Isandro, I think it might have your name in it."

I laughed. I actually laughed, because the alternative was screaming, and laughter at least keeps its shoes on.

"My name? In a founding contract? Ada, I was driftwood. I was eleven. I'm the man who draws the maps; I've never been the man the maps are about."

"Page one," she said. "I need to see page one. Don't argue with me. Don't *draw* anything tonight. Promise me."

And because she asked, and because her hand was resting on my nine pages of myself as though guarding them, and because I would have promised her anything in that moment and we both knew it — I promised.

* * *

Night came, and with it the pressure again, that deep-water weight, stronger now, rhythmic, almost tidal. Pull and release. Pull and release. Like breathing. Like something enormous and patient inhaling through a straw stuck into the world.

I lay awake and took inventory one last time, the way a merchant checks his strongbox before a journey.

Isandro. Cartographer. Twenty-six. Strong tea. Rain on slate. Ada arguing with arithmetic. Steady hands. The seat across from hers. The stair that goes down. The stair that goes down. The stair that —

I sat bolt upright in the dark.

The stair. Every night for weeks I've drawn it, and every night I've told myself I don't know why, and tonight, lying in the dark with my own self draining out of me, I finally heard the reason arrive, and it arrived in my own voice, in my own memory, except I have no memory of ever saying it:

*I know the way down. I've always known the way down. They only borrowed the knowing; now they've come to collect the rest.*

Somewhere below the city, below the guild, below the deepest cellar Vess ever sealed, something turned a page.

And in the morning, when Ada broke into the archive and came to me white-faced with a single sheet of vellum trembling in her hands, I already knew what would be written on it. I knew before she spoke. I knew the way you know the last line of a song.

Because the signature on page one of the founding contract of the Gilded Veil — the debt that fell the stars, the debt that is eating me alive from the edges inward — was written in my own hand.

Older. Shaking. But mine.
