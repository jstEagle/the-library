## 8. The Guild Votes

### Ada

The Gilded Veil had a rule about council meetings: no one spoke until the tea was poured. It was meant to be a courtesy. In practice it gave everyone time to rehearse the lies they intended to tell, and I had learned to read a room by what people did with their cups. Bram held his in both fists like a weapon he hadn't decided to use. Vess turned hers three times clockwise, always three, and I'd once watched her do it over a ledger entry that turned out to be forged.

Tonight she turned it four times.

We sat in the map room because it was the only chamber with a door thick enough to muffle sound and a table long enough to hold the founding ledger without anyone having to touch anyone else. Isandro should have been there — he was inner circle, technically, the way a lighthouse is technically furniture — but when I'd knocked on his door an hour ago he hadn't answered, and when I'd pressed my ear to the wood I'd heard the small dry whisper of a quill moving very fast.

I told myself that was fine. I told myself a lot of things that night.

"Read it again," Bram said. "Out loud. Slowly."

Vess didn't sigh, which was how I knew she was frightened. She only sighed when she was annoyed, and annoyance was a luxury she'd apparently decided she could no longer afford. She opened the founding ledger to the first page — the page I'd seen her go pale over two nights ago, when the sky went out like a pinched candle — and read:

"'One soul, freely rendered, to be delivered upon the night the sky forgets us. Payment deferred in perpetuity, interest accruing, until such night as the stars withhold their witness.'" She closed the book. "That's the whole clause. There's no name attached."

"There's always a name attached," Bram said. "Debts don't exist in the abstract. That's not how any of this works, and you built a guild on knowing exactly how all of this works."

"The name is sealed." Vess touched the page where, beneath the terms, a strip of black wax had been laid over something. "Undercourt seal. You don't break one of those from this side. You don't break one of those from *any* side, that's rather the point of them."

"So we pay," Bram said. "Find a soul. Deliver it. Done."

I want to be fair to him here, because history — our history, the one we wrote afterward — has been unkind to Bram, and fairness is the one thing I can still do well. He wasn't a monster. He was a man who had spent thirty years keeping forty people alive in a business where the inventory could bite, and his arithmetic was simple and honest: one soul against forty. Any merchant would make that trade. The horror wasn't in the math. The horror was that the math worked.

"Pay with *what*?" I said.

"With a volunteer," Bram said. "Or with a criminal. Or—" and here he did the thing I'd known he would do, the thing he'd been circling since the wards fell, "—with whoever the contract actually names. If we can find out who that is."

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when everyone has thought the same thought and no one will say it.

Because there were only so many people in the Gilded Veil who mattered to the founding debt. Only so many whose lives braided back through three generations of guild business. And only one of them had spent the last two nights locked in his room drawing the same map over and over, of a staircase going down into a mountain, in a hand that shook.

"We don't know it's him," Vess said.

"We don't know it isn't," I said.

"That's not evidence, Ada."

"No," I agreed. "It's arithmetic. We have one cartographer who's being haunted by a map, one sealed clause, and one guild master who turned her cup four times tonight instead of three. I keep accounts for a living, Vess. I know what it looks like when someone's hiding a line item."

She flinched. Actually flinched, this woman who once negotiated with a river spirit for eleven hours without blinking. And I felt the guilt arrive on schedule, punctual as interest, because Vess had taken me in when I was nothing — a runaway clerk with stolen paper and a mistress's curse on my heels — and you don't speak to the person who saved you like a debtor in arrears.

Except that was exactly what she'd made me. What she'd made all of us. I just hadn't read the fine print until now.

* * *

### Vess

My grandmother built the Gilded Veil on a lie so old it had become load-bearing.

That is the truth of every institution, I suspect, though most never learn theirs. Mine I had carried for forty years, since the night my mother handed me the founding ledger and said, *you'll know when*, and I had waited every dark night since, checking the sky like a farmer checks for frost. When the stars went out, some part of me — the part I'm least proud of — felt relief. At last. No more waiting.

Now the council wanted the name under the seal, and I could not give it to them, and the reason I could not give it to them was that giving it to them would kill Isandro faster than the Undercourt would.

Not because the seal would break. Because they would *believe* it. Belief is a currency too; I ran a guild, I knew its exchange rates better than anyone alive. Tell forty frightened people that their quiet friend's soul is the collateral under their beds and their livelihoods, and watch what belief does. It doesn't stay contained. It leaks. It gets into the walls. And the Undercourt feeds on exactly that kind of leak.

So I lied, which was my grandmother's trade before it was mine.

"The contract specifies delivery at the mountain's root," I said. "Whoever the soul belongs to, the Undercourt will identify them itself. Our task is not to guess. Our task is to choose what we send."

"And if it's one of us?" Bram asked. Flat. He already suspected. Bram always suspected; it was why I kept him.

"Then we send a substitute and let the lawyers argue jurisdiction for eternity. The Undercourt respects process. It's the only thing it respects."

"You've dealt with them before," Ada said. Not a question. Her eyes were doing the thing they did when she was reconciling figures — going somewhere flat and bright and merciless. "You said 'the Undercourt respects process' the way someone says a sentence they've rehearsed. Where, Vess?"

"My grandmother bought the deferral," I said. "Three generations ago. She paid for time, and time is what we've had. I intend to buy more."

"With what? The vault's bottled luck won't cover a soul. A year of life won't cover a soul. Nothing in our inventory covers a soul except—"

"A substitute soul," I said. "Yes. Which is why I called this council."

Ada looked at me across the table, and I watched her do the sum I had been dreading for two days: the sealed clause, the cartographer's shaking hands, the map of the descent, and her own mistress's lessons in how names get sold. She was hedge-witch raised. She knew what a layered contract looked like. God forgive me, I had hired her *because* she knew, because a guild that trades in impossible things needs one person who reads the fine print, and I had hoped — I had genuinely, cowardly hoped — that she'd never need to read *this* page.

"I move we void it," she said.

And the room changed temperature.

* * *

### Bram

Here's what nobody understands about running: it's not cowardice. Cowardice is staying when staying costs more than you have. Running is just honesty about the exchange rate.

I'd run twice before I came to the Veil. Once from a war, once from a wife, and both times I'd survived, which is the entire review you get to write of your own decisions. So when the girl said *void it*, I laughed, because I've heard that word before. Void. As if contracts with the Undercourt are laundry tickets. As if anything written in blood-wax and sealed under a mountain comes unstitched because you found a loose thread.

"You can't void it," I told her. "You can't even open the seal. And even if you could — say you march down there, say you stand in front of whatever sits under that mountain with your little ledger and your clever sums — what's your argument? 'Sorry, the paperwork's bad'? They *wrote* the paperwork. They invented paperwork. We got the concept from them secondhand."

"Forgery isn't their invention," Ada said. "Forgery is ours. Mortals invented lying, Bram. They just perfected the collection."

I looked at Vess to shut this down, because that was the arrangement: I provided muscle and pessimism, she provided direction, and neither of us let the clerks set policy. But Vess was turning her cup again — five times now, six — and saying nothing, and I understood with a cold drop in my stomach that the girl had said something true enough that even Vess couldn't dismiss it.

Which meant Vess had suspicions of her own. Which meant the founding debt was worse than a debt.

"Even granting your theory," I said slowly, "even if the contract's rotten — you walk into that court and accuse them of fraud, what happens? Best case, they laugh. Worst case, they decide you're interesting. Nothing down there is more dangerous than being interesting."

"And if we run?" Ada said. "Where? The border wards are down. The stars are out. Every road out of this valley passes within ten miles of the mountain. We wouldn't be running from the debt, we'd be carrying it to new customers."

"Then we dig in. Seal the vaults, ward the doors, wait it out. Debts age. Even theirs."

"Do they?" she asked, softly, and I hated her a little for the softness, because it was the voice you use on a dying man. "Bram, the stars went out in one night. All of them. That's not a debt aging. That's a debt *collecting*. Whatever patience they had, they've spent it."

I drank my tea. It had gone cold, which felt appropriate. Everything had gone cold.

"I vote we run," I said finally. "Sealed vaults, scattered routes, regroup at the coast. Forty souls walk away from this tonight, or forty souls don't walk anywhere ever again. That's my vote. One life for forty — I know how that sounds. I also know I've buried friends over worse math."

Nobody spoke. Then Vess said, quietly, "It's not one life, Bram. We don't know that it's one life."

And the way she said it — the way she *wouldn't* say more — told me everything, and I wished to every dark star overhead that it hadn't.

* * *

### Ada

Vess abstained. Of course she abstained. Guild masters abstain; it's how they stay masters. But her abstention had a shape to it, and the shape was: *find out the name before you do anything irreversible.* Which meant, translated out of guild-speak: *go ahead, girl, break into my archive, I'll leave the lock easier than usual.*

Bram voted to run. His vote was honest and it was wrong and I loved him a little for making it, the way you love a dog that barks at thunder. Someone in this family had to prioritize survival. Someone had to say the ugly arithmetic out loud so the rest of us could feel superior refusing it.

And then it was my vote, and I found my hands were steady, which surprised me, because inside I was sixteen again and Maren Crane was holding a knife over a contract and saying, *names are just words, dear, and words can be sold.*

"It's not enough to delay," I said. "Delay is what Vess's grandmother bought, and look where it got us — three generations of profit built on top of a bomb. Delay means the next council meets in worse straits with less inventory and fewer options. There's only one move that ends this, and it's the one nobody wants: we go down, we put the original contract on the table, and we prove it's worthless."

"On what grounds?" Vess asked. Give her this — she asked like an auditor, not like a woman hoping to be reassured.

"Grounds I haven't found yet." I met her eyes. "But every contract has grounds. Every forgery especially. Forgeries are made by people in a hurry, and people in a hurry make mistakes, and mistakes leave marks in margins and ink and dates. I've spent six years reading this guild's books. I know what hurried dishonesty looks like. Whoever built the founding contract was hurrying."

"You're betting Isandro's life on marginalia," Bram said.

"I'm betting *everyone's* life on marginalia. Including mine. That's what makes it a real bet."

Silence, again, but a different silence — the silence of a thing tipping. Vess looked at Bram. Bram looked at his cold tea. And I realized, with the strange clarity that comes in the moment after a decision, that none of us had mentioned the obvious: that whatever we voted, the decision might not be ours to make. That the debt had already begun collecting. That upstairs, behind a locked door, a man who didn't know why was drawing a staircase into a mountain with hands that shook, and every line he drew was a signature.

"Motion carries," Vess said at last, so quietly it was almost not words. "Two votes, one abstention. Ada — you'll lead. Bram, you'll equip her. And nobody tells Isandro."

"Why not?" Bram asked.

"Because if the contract names him," Vess said, and now she finally lifted her eyes from the cup, and they were the oldest things in the room, "then every hour he doesn't know is an hour the fear doesn't feed it. Fear is a currency too. I won't let this guild pay in advance."

I stood to go, and my legs nearly failed me, and I covered it by gathering the founding ledger into my arms like a child.

"One more thing," Vess said as I reached the door. "The archive key. Third drawer of my desk. The lock will be... difficult."

"I hadn't planned to ask permission."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm telling you where the key is."

I climbed the stairs to the map room's landing with the ledger against my chest, past the corridor where Isandro's door still glowed faintly with lamplight, and the quill inside was still whispering, whispering, whispering — faster now, I thought, or maybe that was the wind through the dead wards, or maybe it was the mountain itself, counting along.

I stopped at his door. I stood there long enough to hear the quill falter — just once, a skipped heartbeat of sound — and then resume.

Tomorrow I would break into the archive. Tonight I pressed my palm flat against the wood, the way you press a hand to a fevered forehead, and whispered, to no one, to him, to whatever was listening down at the bottom of his map:

"Hold on. I'm coming to audit them."
