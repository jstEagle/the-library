## 9. Down the Stair That Isn't There

The door was in the cellar, which was the first lie of it, because the Gilded Veil's cellars had been counted twice by me and once by Bram's people and there had never been a door in the east wall. There were barrels of bottled fog. There were crates of borrowed luck, each one rattling faintly like dice in a cup. There was a shelf of years — actual years, decanted, amber-colored, labeled by decade — and I had personally audited that shelf. No door.

And yet at four o'clock in the morning, three days after Isandro's hands had started shaking so badly he couldn't hold a pen, I came down for a candle and found him standing in front of forty feet of blank stone with his map spread across two barrels, and the stone was opening.

Not swinging open. *Unhappening.* The wall simply stopped agreeing to be a wall, the way a word stops meaning anything if you say it long enough, and behind it was a stair going down into dark that my candle refused to reach the bottom of.

"Isandro." I said it quietly, because you don't shout at a man standing on the edge of himself. "Isandro, look at me."

He looked. His eyes were wrong — not wild, just *thin*, like paper held up to a lamp. Like something on the other side could see straight through him.

"It opened," he said. "Ada, it finally opened. It's been waiting for me to finish the map."

He said it like good news. That was the worst part.

* * *

Here is what I knew, because I'd spent the last three days doing what I do, which is reading things until they confess: the debt was collecting early. Vess wouldn't say it outright — Vess communicates exclusively through ledger entries and meaningful silences — but the archive copy of the founding contract had begun to change. Ink moving on its own page is bad bookkeeping anywhere in the world. The payment date, which had read *the night the sky forgets us*, now read *the next moonrise*, and then, this morning, *now*. Whoever wrote that contract was tired of waiting, and whoever it was written against was starting to pay in installments.

The installments were memories. Chapter seven of Isandro's life, being withdrawn without his signature.

So when he turned from the door with that thin-eyed look and said, "I have to go down," I did the only sensible thing an accountant can do when an asset she cannot afford to lose announces its intention to walk off the books.

"I'm coming with you," I said.

"You don't have to—"

"Isandro." I took his wrist, the one that shook. "You've forgotten your mother's face. You told me yesterday you weren't sure whether you had a brother or you'd invented one. You are not walking into a hole in the world alone. Besides—" and here I made myself smile, because he needed one more than I needed to mean it— "someone has to keep the accounts."

He laughed. It cracked in the middle, but it was a laugh. "What accounts?"

"Yours," I said. "Every one of them. Come on."

* * *

We told no one, which sounds reckless and was, but the guild had voted the night before and the vote had gone badly — Vess wanting to buy time with somebody else's soul, Bram wanting to run for the coast, me wanting to go under the mountain and burn the contract at its root. Three factions, one family, and no majority. If I'd announced we were descending through a wall that didn't exist, Vess would have sealed the vaults again and posted Bram at the cellar door, and Bram would have done it, because Bram loves us in the way of men who express affection through barricades.

So I left a note on my desk instead, weighted under the inkwell, where Vess would find it within the hour and understand within the day: *Gone to settle the founding debt. Do not pay anyone anything. — A.C.*

Then I packed like the pragmatist I am. Chalk. Two candles and a third for sentiment. Bread, cheese, a flask of the guild's decent brandy. And the thing I was proudest of: a small ledger, fresh, half-bound, with Isandro's name lettered on the first page in my own hand.

"What's that?" he asked as we stood before the opening wall.

"Insurance," I said. "Walk."

* * *

The stair went down farther than the mountain had any right to be deep, and it did something worse than descend. It *edited.*

I noticed it first as a smell. Wet slate, then pine resin, then — absurdly, horribly — bread baking, the specific yeasty warmth of a kitchen, and Isandro stopped dead on the step above me and said, "This is my mother's house," in a voice like a man reading his own eulogy.

There was no house. There was a stair cut from living rock, sweating cold. But the air around us had decided to be a kitchen, and when I raised the candle I saw the walls had grown faint suggestions of shelving, a ghost of a window with ghost-light in it, and on the step directly ahead, worn smooth in a way the other steps weren't, a hollow exactly the shape of a boy's feet, standing, waiting.

"It's building itself out of what you remember," I said. "Your map — the one you've drawn forty times? It wasn't a map of the way down. It was a map of *you.* The passage is following it."

Isandro put his hand flat against the wall, and where he touched it, the stone remembered being plaster. Warm yellow plaster. He snatched his hand back like he'd been burned.

"Then the parts I've already lost—"

"We won't come to those," I said, and heard how much I sounded like a ledger promising a balance that wasn't there. So I did the thing I'd brought the little book for. I opened it to the first page, held the candle close, and began to read.

"Isandro Vale. Born in the town of Harrow's Bend, in the house with the crooked chimney, third from the millrace. Your mother's name was Sefa. She sang off-key and didn't care who knew. You had a dog named Bristle who was afraid of sheep. You broke your left arm falling off the mill roof when you were nine, and you cried, and your mother told you that crying over a broken arm was honest and crying over a broken pride was optional, and you chose both."

He had stopped on the stair. His eyes had come back from thin to merely wet.

"I remember the arm," he whispered. "I remember Bristle. Ada, I don't remember her *face.*"

"Then we'll rebuild it," I said. "Step by step. Keep walking."

* * *

That was the shape of the descent, and I will set it down plainly because it deserves plainness: he walked, and the mountain ate him from the edges inward, and I walked beside him reading him back to himself like a merchant defending stock from thieves.

The passage took whatever he still held and made it solid around us. When he remembered the winter roads — he'd been apprenticed young to a mapmaker who worked the northern passes — the stair became switchbacks, and frost feathered the walls, and somewhere below us wind moved with the particular loneliness of a pass at dusk. When he remembered learning to draw coastlines, the air went salt, and the walls grew the fine crackle-lines of old charts, and I swear the stone under our boots felt briefly like the deck of a ship.

But between the remembered stretches came the gaps. And the gaps were the true horror, because the passage didn't go dark in them. It went *generic.* A stretch of stairs with no character at all, no texture, no smell, just steps — the architectural equivalent of a shrug. Places he'd already lost, rendered in the mountain's best guess at neutral.

The first gap frightened him. By the fourth, he'd learned to dread them silently, and I learned to talk faster.

"The mapmaker you apprenticed to was called Odrik," I said, filling one such gap, our footsteps echoing in the nothing-smelling dark. "He had a beard like a storm cloud and he cheated at cards and he taught you that a map is a promise: that the place will still be there when you arrive. You kept that. You make maps of places that don't exist yet because you're keeping his promise forward instead of backward."

"How do you know all this?" Isandro asked. "I've never told you half of it."

"You told me all of it," I said. "Over six years, in pieces, mostly when you thought I wasn't listening. I listen professionally. It's in the ledgers — everything anyone says near the counting room ends up written down somewhere."

"That's slightly sinister."

"I'm an accountant. Sinister is the polite word for thorough."

He laughed again, and the gap ended, and the stairs remembered themselves into a lamplit study with Odrik's storm-cloud beard scratched into the wall in charcoal, and we walked through a dead man's memory like weather.

* * *

We rested once, on a landing shaped like a tavern — his first night at the Gilded Veil, apparently, rendered in woodsmoke and spilled-beer ghosts, because that was the memory the mountain reached for when he thought of safety. We shared the bread and split the brandy, and he sat looking at his own hands like they belonged to a stranger who'd been kind to him once.

"Ask me something," he said suddenly. "Anything about me. I want to hear it from outside. Maybe it sticks better that way."

I considered, and chose carefully, the way you choose which coin to hand a desperate man.

"Why cartography?" I said. "You could have done anything. Why maps?"

He was quiet a while. The tavern-ghost flickered.

"Because everywhere I lived got taken away," he said at last, slowly, feeling for the words like a man walking on river ice. "Harrow's Bend. Odrik's passes. Even before — there was something before, wasn't there? Something I sold?" His face did something terrible and brief. "I can feel the hole where it was. It doesn't hurt. That's the awful part. It's just *smooth.*"

"Isandro—"

"No, let me finish, I'll lose it." He breathed. "Maps are how you love a place without owning it. You draw it, and the drawing stays true even after the place is gone. I wanted to be the kind of person who keeps things by describing them." He looked up at me, and the thinness was back in his eyes, but underneath it, stubborn as bedrock, was him. "Which is why you're reading that ledger at me all the way down, isn't it. You're mapping me."

"Someone has to," I said. My voice did not behave perfectly. I let it. "You're the most important asset this guild has ever carried, and I don't mean the contract. I mean *you.* And I have never once in my life lost track of an asset I was responsible for. So we're going to walk down this stair, and every time the mountain takes a piece of you, I'm going to write down what it took and read it back until you're sick of your own life. And when we get to the bottom, you're going to be whole enough to hate what's down there properly. Understood?"

"Understood," he said softly. Then, wry, because he was still Isandro: "You know you sound like Vess when you lecture."

"I sound like someone who balances books. Vess sounds like someone who's afraid of them. There's a difference."

* * *

Near the end — though I didn't know it was near the end; depth down there is a rumor — the passage changed tactics.

It stopped rendering his memories and started rendering his *losses.* We came around a bend and the wall showed a doorway, ordinary, wooden, and beyond it a room that was not a room: a smooth white absence, featureless, warm-lit, like the inside of an egg. And standing in the doorway, Isandro made a sound I hope never to hear again, because it was the sound of a man greeting a stranger wearing his own childhood.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's the hole. Whatever I gave away — it happened in there. I can almost—"

"Don't." I stepped between him and the doorway. "Whatever's in there, it's bait or it's a toll, and either way we don't pay it on the stairs. We pay nothing until we're standing in front of the thing that holds the contract. Those are the terms. *Our* terms."

He stared past my shoulder at the white room, and I watched him want it — watched the hunger to be whole war with everything I'd spent the descent rebuilding. His shaking hand found mine. Cold fingers. Steadier than they'd been in days, though. Reading works. I intend to note that in the guild's methods, if we survive to have methods.

"Keep talking," he said. "About me. While we pass it. Please."

So I did. I read him past his own missing piece — Harrow's Bend, Sefa singing off-key, Bristle trembling at sheep, the broken arm, Odrik cheating at cards, the first night at the Veil — voice steady, pages turning, while the white room breathed its egg-warm silence at my back and tried its best to be interesting. And Isandro walked with his eyes on my face instead of the doorway, and we came through, and the white room was behind us, and neither of us spoke for a hundred steps.

When he finally did, it was to say, quietly: "Whatever I traded, in there — I think it was to save her. My mother. I can't remember it, but I can remember the *shape* of why." His jaw tightened. "Ada. If I sold my name to save her, and someone used it to build this guild's debt—"

"Then we'll prove it," I said. "Fraud voids a claim. Bad ink breaks contracts. It's the one law older than magic, and I have spent my entire life getting good at finding bad ink."

"And if the ink isn't bad?"

I closed the little ledger and held it against my chest like a shield, which is what it was.

"Then I'll find the flaw anyway," I said. "I always find the flaw. Now walk. I can hear water."

Below us, far down, something vast shifted in the dark like a sleeper turning over — and the stair beneath our feet shivered, and every ghost of every memory the passage had built flickered at once, like candles in a draft.

And then, from the darkness ahead, unhurried and patient as interest accruing, came the sound of oars.
