## 10. The Tollgate of Small Mercies

The stair ended where the stair should not have ended: at water.

Black water, flat as poured ink, stretching in both directions until it dissolved into a dark that wasn't shadow so much as the *absence* of any opinion about light. There was no ceiling we could see, though we had been descending for — I checked my tally-marks, scratched into my forearm with Isandro's spare stylus because I refused to lose count of anything ever again — for what my marks insisted was eleven hours. My legs insisted otherwise. My legs were claiming something closer to three days and a personal grudge.

"A river," Isandro said, "underground."

"Underground rivers exist," I said. "I've read about them. They're very fashionable among geologists."

"This one is under a mountain that is also a court."

"Fashionable among no one, then."

He almost smiled. That was the arithmetic I was running these days — every hour he lost another memory, and every hour I spent a little more of myself narrating his life back to him to keep the ledger balanced. His mother's kitchen smelled of cedar smoke and burnt honey. He learned to swim in a millpond with a green bottom. He once stole an orange from a market stall in Vell and felt so guilty he returned it and then bought two more out of shame. Small things. The small things go first; I'd learned that watching Maren's debtors forget their own children's birthdays before they forgot their own names. Debt eats from the edges inward.

So far I'd kept him whole by sheer stubborn bookkeeping. But the river was new, and new meant unaccounted-for, and unaccounted-for meant danger.

The ferry was already waiting.

Of course it was. Nothing down here waited politely; things down here *presided*. It sat at the edge of the black water on a skiff that looked grown rather than built — pale wood like bone, ribs curving up at prow and stern into shapes that might have been hands or might have been petals, depending on how charitable you felt. And the creature poling it —

I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy turned out to matter enormously, but accuracy is difficult when your eyes keep trying to file the thing under several categories at once. It was tall. It wore a coat of grey feathers arranged with the precision of a clerk's ledger-lines. Its face was long and gentle and wrong, the way a face drawn by someone who has only had faces described to them is wrong — features present, proportions negotiated. Around its neck hung a scale. Not a metaphorical scale. An actual brass balance, small, hanging from a chain like a locket, and both pans were empty.

Its eyes found us the way an auditor's finger finds a discrepancy.

"Toll," it said. Its voice was dry paper and river-stone. "Crossing is not free. Crossing is never free. Payment in kind." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "Memories."

Isandro went rigid beside me. I felt it happen — his whole body pulling tight as a knotted cord, because of course this creature had asked for the one currency he was already bleeding.

"No," he said.

"Yes," the ferryman said, without malice, without anything. "One each. Fair toll. Small mercy: you choose which."

"You choose which memory," I repeated. "That's the arrangement?"

"The arrangement."

Here is what I knew about arrangements, having spent six years keeping accounts for people who traded in bottled luck and borrowed years: every arrangement has a seam. Every contract, every toll, every bargain struck beneath heaven or under mountains has a place where the wording bends, and if you find the bend, you can drive a cart through it. Vess taught me that without meaning to, back when she explained why the Gilded Veil never sold anything outright — always leased, always licensed, always with a clause. "Ownership invites audit, Crane," she said. "Ambiguity is armor."

I looked at the ferryman's empty scales. I looked at its patient, negotiated face. And I thought about what memories actually are, underneath the poetry.

They are records.

And I had been falsifying records since I was nine years old.

* * *

Let me be clear about what I mean, because there's a difference between lying and lying *precisely*, and the difference is the whole trade.

Maren Crane kept her debts the way spiders keep webs — everything stuck in it was technically hers. When collectors came, she'd hand them a jar with a memory in it, sealed in wax, and they'd drink it down and leave satisfied, and Maren would turn around and sell the same memory again to someone else, decanted thin. She called it "stretching the stock." I called it fraud, quietly, in the margins of her books, in a cipher of my own devising, because even at twelve I understood that the only safe place to tell the truth was somewhere no one could read it.

But here's the part that matters. To stretch a memory — to make one jar serve three buyers — you have to understand exactly what makes a memory feel real. It isn't the big shapes. Anyone can sketch "a kitchen" or "my mother." What convinces the drinker, what makes the memory take root and pass inspection, is the *grain*. The irrelevant specifics. The way the floorboard by the stove creaked on cold mornings specifically and not warm ones. The exact pitch of the kettle versus the wind. Memory isn't made of events; it's made of texture. And texture can be manufactured by anyone obsessive enough to do the work.

I had manufactured textures before. Under duress, for Maren, to keep her ledgers plausible. I had built false memories the way other apprentices built model ships — carefully, lovingly, hating every minute, learning every minute.

The ferryman wanted one memory each. It did not say *true* memories.

It said payment in kind. Memories. You choose which.

Precision, I thought, is power. Maren taught me that too, though she'd be furious to hear it credited.

* * *

"Before we pay," I said, stepping forward before Isandro could sacrifice something real, "I'd like to review the terms."

The ferryman tilted its head. Somewhere in the feather-coat, something rustled — amusement, possibly, or just feathers.

"Review," it said.

"I'm an accountant. Reviewing terms is what I'm for. You've stated: one memory per traveler, traveler's choice, paid upon boarding, crossing delivered upon payment. Have I missed anything?"

"One memory each," it agreed. "Choice freely made. Payment true."

My stomach dipped. "*Payment true.*"

"Payment must be a memory," the ferryman said, and its voice acquired the faintest edge, the sound of a clause sharpening itself. "It must be remembered. It must be given willingly. It must be yours."

There it was. The seam, and the teeth guarding the seam. Not *true events*. A memory. Remembered. Given willingly. Yours.

A memory is not the event. A memory is the record of the event. And a record — any record — can be authored.

"Yours," I said slowly. "Meaning originating from me. Meaning I hold it, I recall it, I surrender it. Correct?"

"Correct."

"It does not need to correspond to anything that happened."

The ferryman was silent for a long moment. The black water made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than lapping. Then: "Clever travelers sometimes ask this. Clever travelers are sometimes disappointed. The toll weighs what is given. False coin rings hollow. Hollow coin is refused, and refusers—" it gestured with one long grey wing-arm at the water, eloquently, "—stay on this bank. Indefinitely. With me."

"Understood," I said, and my heart was going like a hammer on a nail, and I kept my face the way I'd learned to keep it across Maren's counting table: pleasant, blank, slightly dim. "Then let's weigh honestly. Both of us."

I closed my eyes.

And I built.

This is the part I will never be able to explain properly to anyone who hasn't done it, so I'll simply report it. Building a memory from nothing is like setting a double-entry ledger in reverse. You start with the entry you want to exist and work backward through every column it touches until the whole thing balances. I needed a memory that would *weigh*. Not a big one — big ones invite scrutiny, scrutiny invites disaster. A small one. Dense. Textured. True-feeling in every particular, because every particular would be specified, cross-checked against itself, internally consistent down to the grain.

I chose winter. Everyone has winters; a winter asks no questions.

I built a morning in a house I invented, with frost ferns on a window I invented, and a chipped blue cup on a table worn pale at one corner where a thousand elbows had rested. I gave the tea a specific bitterness — oversteeped, second leaves, the good ones saved — and I gave the room a smell of wet wool drying by the stove, and outside, a cart passing with a loose iron rim that rang twice, *twice*, never three times, because the wheel hit the rut twice and then the driver steadied the horse. I put a person in it whose face I left deliberately soft, unfocused, the way real memories actually are — faces go first, texture stays — and I gave that person a habit: humming four notes of a song and never the fifth, because the fifth note belonged to someone gone, and everyone in that invented household knew better than to hum the fifth.

I built the cold spot on the floorboards. The scratch on the doorframe at the height of a child measuring herself against it, the notch marked *Ada, age seven*—

No. Wait. Careful. I caught myself at the last instant, pen hovering over the page of my own mind. Not Ada. Never Ada. If I signed my own name into a memory and then surrendered that memory, I would be surrendering a piece of my name, and my name was the one asset I had sworn on Maren's grave — well, on the patch of ground where Maren would have a grave if anyone had bothered — never to spend.

I changed the notch. *E., age seven.* A different child. A sister I had never had, in a life I had never lived, in a winter that had never fallen.

Then I did the thing that mattered most, the thing Maren never understood because Maren thought forgery was about copying. I *remembered* it. I took the constructed morning and I recalled it — walked through it, drank the bitter tea, flinched at the ringing wheel, stood on the cold board, heard the four notes and the silence where the fifth should be — until it settled into me with the weight of something lived. Until the memory was mine in every way a memory can be mine except the one way the ferryman hadn't asked about.

It weighed something. I could feel it weighing, like a stone in the chest.

"Ready?" I asked Isandro.

He was staring at me with an expression I couldn't parse — fear, maybe, or awe, or the particular horror of a man realizing his friend keeps tools he's never mentioned. "What are you going to give it?"

"A winter," I said. "Don't watch. It's easier if you don't watch."

* * *

His toll first, because I wanted the creature's attention on him when it happened, not on me.

Isandro chose — and I hated him for it, and loved him for it — a memory of his mother. Not the most precious one; he'd been hoarding those, rationing them like a starving man rations bread, and I'd helped him build the inventory list. He chose a middling one: her hands kneading dough, flour to the wrists, the window fogged with oven-heat. Real. True. Given willingly, remembered, his. The ferryman extended one grey hand and Isandro pressed his palm to it, and I watched the memory leave him — watched it physically leave, a faint silver shimmer rising off his skin like breath in cold air, coiling into the ferryman's empty scale-pan.

The brass balance swung. Settled. Level.

"Paid," the ferryman said, and Isandro swayed, and I gripped his elbow and felt him grip back, and neither of us said anything about the fact that his mother's hands now existed only in a creature's toll-pouch, and might, for all we knew, be resold downstream.

Then it turned to me. Those negotiated eyes. The empty pan waiting.

I reached for the winter. I gathered it up — the frost ferns, the bitter tea, the twice-ringing wheel, the four notes and the missing fifth, E.'s notch in the doorframe — and I held it out the way you hold out a purse you're pretending is heavier than it is.

"Payment," I said.

The moment it touched the ferryman's hand, I did the last trick, the one I'd been building toward, the one that was really just good accounting: I let it go *completely*. No half-measures, no mental footnote saying *this was fabricated, remember the fabrication*. Because here's the flaw in every forged coin: the forger remembers forging. The doubt clings to the coin like a fingerprint. So I burned my own bridge behind me — I surrendered not just the memory but my knowledge of its manufacture, filed it away in the deepest cipher I had, the one I'd invented at twelve and never used because using it felt like dying a little. The winter became, for all purposes I could access, a true thing that had happened to me and was now gone.

The scale received it.

The scale *considered* it.

For three full seconds — I counted; counting is my rosary — the brass balance trembled, pans whispering against their chains, and the ferryman's face did something complicated, features renegotiating, and I stood there in the dark with my heart performing percussion and my face performing serenity, and I thought: *balance, you bastard, balance.*

The pan dropped level.

"Paid," the ferryman said.

And the terrible, wonderful thing — the thing I would carry up that mountain and out the other side — was the grief that arrived a half-second later. Because the memory was gone, and I mourned it. I stood on that bone-white skiff mourning a winter that had never happened, a sister who had never existed, a fifth note I'd composed myself in the dark of my own skull — and the grief was *real*. The loss registered as true loss. Which meant the fabrication had been flawless, which meant I had won, and knowing I'd won didn't ease the mourning at all. That's the price of precision nobody puts in the brochure: if you lie perfectly enough, the lie's absence hurts exactly like the truth's.

The skiff slid onto the black water without a pole touching it. The ferryman stood at the stern, feathers stirring in a wind that came from nowhere, and after a while it spoke again, unprompted, which I suspected was rare.

"Clever travelers," it said, "sometimes ask what happens to what they pay."

"Do I want to know?"

"Nothing below is destroyed. Everything below is *kept*." The scale at its throat turned slowly, catching light that had no source. "Your winter is in the vaults now. With the stars. With the names. With everything the court has ever collected." A pause. "Some things down here are still waiting to be claimed back."

I turned that over as the black water slid past, silent as an unsigned page. Kept, not destroyed. Waiting to be claimed. I filed it — where else — in the ledger.

Isandro leaned close, and his voice was rough. "What did you give it? Truly. I want it on the list, so I can help you remember—"

"You can't," I said. "That's rather the point."

"Ada."

"Don't." I kept my eyes on the water. "Add it to the list as *one winter, paid, contents unknown*. That's accurate. That's all I've got."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then, because he was Isandro, and because he had spent years drawing maps of places that didn't exist yet and therefore understood authorship better than anyone I knew, he said softly: "You made it up. Didn't you. Whole cloth. And it *took*."

"The Undercourt can be lied to," I said, "if you lie precisely."

Something kindled in his face — hope, actual hope, the first I'd seen since the stars went out — and I understood that I'd just shown him a weapon, and that weapons change what people are willing to attempt. He looked toward the dark ahead, where the water bent around some unseen shoulder of stone, and where, faint as a rumor, a light had appeared. Not starlight. Warmer. Lamplit gold, the color of the Veil's best inventory, growing as we slid toward it.

"If memories can be forged," he murmured, "what else can?"

I didn't answer. Ahead, the lamplight resolved into gates — vast, bone-white, standing open like a held breath — and above them, carved deep into the mountain's living rock, letters older than any alphabet I kept in my head:

**ALL ACCOUNTS SETTLED WITHIN.**

Which, in my professional experience, is exactly what a place says right before it presents you with an invoice you cannot possibly pay.
