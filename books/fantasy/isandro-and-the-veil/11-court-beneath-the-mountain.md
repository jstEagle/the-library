## 11. Court Beneath the Mountain

There is a particular kind of silence that comes after a stairway ends and a door opens, and I have decided it is the silence of an audience deciding whether you're worth the trouble of looking at.

The door at the bottom of the Stair That Isn't There was bone-white stone veined with something dark — I told myself it was mineral, because telling myself it was anything else would have meant stopping on the threshold forever. It opened inward without being touched, which is the manner of every door I've ever distrusted, and beyond it the Undercourt spread out before us like a cathedral that had been designed by someone who had heard about beauty secondhand and improved upon it.

Isandro made a sound beside me. Not a word. A small exhalation, the kind a person makes when they see something they half-remember from a dream they'd rather not have had.

I understand why he did. I'll do my best to describe it, though I warn you now that my ledgers have failed me for the first time in my career, because there is no column for this.

The hall was vast enough to have its own weather. Bone-white pillars rose out of sight into a darkness overhead, and between them hung — I keep wanting to write *lamps*, but that is a coward's word — between them hung stars. Actual stars. The ones that had gone out over our heads three nights ago while Vess went pale over her ledger and ordered the vaults sealed. They were caged. That was the thing my eyes refused and then accepted: each star sat inside a lattice of thin black metal, like a bird in a too-small cage, or a coin under glass, or — and this is the image that stuck, because I am what I am — like an asset held in trust against a debt not yet called due. Their light fell through the lattices and broke into long pale stripes across the floor, so that walking through the Undercourt meant wading through the shadows of bars.

The missing sky, kept here. Hoarded. And somewhere above us, a whole country of people had looked up at nothing and felt smaller, and none of them knew their smallness had been itemized.

"Count them," Isandro murmured.

I looked at him. He was staring up, and his face had gone strange — not frightened, exactly. *Recognizing.* His hands were steady for the first time since the night the wards fell, which frightened me more than shaking would have.

"Why?"

"Because I think you can," he said. "And I think if anyone counts them right, it will matter."

That is the trouble with working alongside a cartographer. They develop a cartographer's faith: that everything can be mapped, and that mapping something gives you power over it. Normally I share that faith. Normally I *am* that faith, wearing better shoes. But counting those stars — hundreds of them, maybe thousands, hanging in their cages down the length of that impossible nave — felt less like inventory and more like reading someone else's private correspondence. Still, old habits. My eyes moved along the rows and numbers assembled themselves behind them the way they always had, unbidden, unwanted, useful. Four hundred and eleven visible from where we stood, arranged in nine tiers. Nine. The guild's founding ledger has nine seals. I didn't say that part out loud. Some coincidences you keep in your pocket like a knife.

* * *

The courtiers came to meet us, if you can call it meeting when the things approaching you are deciding, collectively, whether you exist yet.

They wore robes of grey and silver and one color I don't have a name for — the color of the moment before you remember something, if that makes sense, which it won't until later. They moved beautifully. That was the first wrongness: everything down here moved as though choreographed by someone who had studied grace but never once been hurried. And their faces —

Their faces were unfinished sketches.

Not blank. Blank would have been kinder and easier. Each face was *partial* — a jaw drawn in fine confident lines, an eye suggested by two strokes and a smudge, a mouth that existed only at its corners. Some of them were more finished than others; one courtier near the front had a complete left side, cheekbone to chin, rendered with loving precision, and then nothing on the right but the faint graphite ghost of where a face might go, waiting. Waiting, I understood, to be filled in. By what, I did not want to learn.

"They're looking at us," Isandro said quietly.

"I know."

"No. I mean — they're looking at us the way a man looks at a full purse." He paused. "Sorry. That was unkind. To purses."

I laughed. I want that recorded, because whatever else happens in these chapters of my life, I want it noted that I laughed in the Undercourt, in front of four hundred caged stars and forty unfinished faces, because my friend made a joke about purses. Fear does that to me. It always has. Maren used to say it was my worst quality — that I met terror with arithmetic and jokes, and neither would save me. She said a lot of things. Most of them were strategies for making me feel small enough to sell.

A courtier stepped forward from the others. Older-seeming, if such creatures seemed any age — his sketch was more complete than most, both eyes done, mouth nearly whole, though his ears remained suggestions. When he spoke, his voice sounded like pages turning in a room far away.

"You are expected," he said.

"Of course we are," I said. "We've been expected since page one."

He tilted his head, and the unfinished parts of his face shifted in a way that finished parts should not. "You make a joke."

"It's how I count."

"Ah," he said. "One of those." And I felt — genuinely felt — several of the courtiers behind him lean slightly closer, the way merchants lean toward a customer who has just revealed she knows the market price. Whatever "one of those" meant down here, it was either very good for us or very bad, and I suspected the difference would be a matter of a few decimal places.

* * *

They walked us through the court, and I will tell you what I saw, because someone has to put it in the record and apparently that someone is me.

The cathedral extended farther than architecture should allow. Pillars gave way to colonnades, colonnades to galleries carved into the living mountain, and everywhere the caged stars hung in their tiers, shedding their barred light. Between the light-stripes lay pools of ordinary dark, and in those pools stood furniture of black wood, and fountains that ran upward, and gardens of flowers made of some material like frosted glass that chimed faintly when the air moved. Everything beautiful. Everything *wrong* in the specific way of a thing built by someone who has never needed it to be useful.

And everywhere, the courtiers. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Standing in their pools of dark, watching us pass with their partial faces, and here is the detail I could not stop snagging on, the splinter in my mind:

Some of them were *more* finished near us.

It was subtle. A nose gaining definition as we drew level with its owner. A mouth acquiring a lower lip. As we passed, the sketches sharpened — and as we moved on, glancing back, I saw them soften again, blur, return to their waiting state. Like drawings responding to proximity. Like faces that took their detail from whatever they were nearest to.

"Isandro," I whispered. "Look at the tall one by the fountain."

He looked. We watched together, discreetly, the way you watch anything in a place where watching back might be fatal. The tall courtier stood beside a chime-flower garden, and his face was perhaps a quarter rendered — brow, one eye's outline. But as a third courtier passed close behind him, the tall one's jaw gained a line. Borrowed. Taken from proximity to something *finished*.

"They feed on definition," Isandro breathed. "On — specifics. On being *near* things that are fully themselves."

"And they live down here," I said, "surrounded by each other. Half-drawn things making other half-drawn things." I thought of the guild's inventory then, absurdly: bottled memories shelved in their cases, borrowed luck in its little leaded bottles, years of life decanted like wine. All of it specificity. All of it *detail*, harvested from the world above and stored in vaults. I thought of Vess saying, *we trade in impossible things,* with such pride, such comfort, as though impossibility were a business model and not an appetite.

"Ada." Isandro had stopped walking. He was staring up at the nearest caged star, and his voice had gone flat in the way it goes flat when he's trying not to feel something large. "The stars aren't decoration."

"No," I said. "I know."

"They're not just hoarded. Look at the light. Where it falls on the courtiers—" He did look. I watched him look, and I watched him arrive at it, and I hated every step of the journey for him. The stripes of starlight crossed the courtiers' bodies, and where the light touched, their sketches *held*. A courtier standing in a bar of starlight all day kept her finished eye sharp, her half-mouth crisp. The light preserved them. The light was the only thing down here that didn't fade.

"They're keeping the stars," I said slowly, working it through the way I work through a fraudulent ledger, line by line, "the way a merchant keeps capital. Not to spend. To *hold*. Because holding it keeps everything else solvent."

"The sky isn't dark because they stole the lights," Isandro said. "The sky is dark because the lights are collateral. Pledged. Held against—"

"Against the founding debt." My mouth had gone dry. "Three generations of interest, paid in starlight."

Somewhere ahead of us, doors taller than our guildhall opened onto a deeper dark, and the turning-pages voice of our escort said, "The King will receive you now," and I understood — finally, completely, in the marrow-understanding way that no bookkeeping ever reaches — what keeps the Undercourt running.

Us. The world above. Every memory bottled, every year of life sold, every scrap of luck traded away by people who didn't know what they were really selling. Specificity. Definition. The raw material of selves. Three generations of the Gilded Veil's honest-seeming commerce, draining upward-facing details downward, and the court below growing fat on them, and the stars caged to keep it all from collapsing like an ill-balanced account.

Maren taught me that every debt has a shape. She never taught me that some debts have *architecture*.

* * *

Before the great doors, our escort stopped and turned to us, and for the first time I saw him clearly in a stripe of starlight, and I wished I hadn't. Up close, in the barred light, his finished parts were *too* finished — the completed eye hyperreal, wet, detailed past the point of comfort, while the rest of him stayed smudged. A creature maintained at minimum viable existence, spending everything it had on one good eye.

"A warning," he said, "freely given, which down here makes it rare and therefore expensive. The King speaks in questions. Answer none of them directly unless you mean to be bound by the answer. He knows your names already, hedge-witch's girl. Both the one you carry and the ones you don't."

"We don't have names down here," I said. "We have references. Ask Vess."

Something happened around his mouth that might have been the sketch of a smile. "Yes," he said. "*One of those.*" And he turned to Isandro, and the single finished eye traveled over him with an attention that made my skin try to leave my body. "And you. The one who signed."

Isandro went very still. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," the courtier agreed softly. "You don't. That is rather the point of you." He gestured, and the great doors began to open without hands, grinding outward on hinges older than the idea of hinges. Cold air rolled out, smelling of ink and iron and, underneath, faintly, of winter — real winter, the kind Isandro couldn't remember anymore, the kind that used to have a name and a hometown attached to it.

Beyond the doors: a throne room, lit by a single caged star hung low and near, close enough that its barred light fell across a floor of polished bone-white stone like a spotlight on an empty stage. And on the stage, at the edge of the light, a figure seated on a throne that seemed less built than *accreted* — grown from layers of contracts, I realized with a lurch, thousands upon thousands of pages fused into a seat, the accumulated paperwork of every bargain ever struck beneath this mountain.

The figure rose. It was tall and spare and its face was the most finished thing I had seen in the entire court — complete, calm, almost kind, in the way a well-kept trap is kind to the animal it wants.

As the doors finished opening, the King inclined his head toward me with terrible courtesy, and asked, in a voice like a question mark learning to speak:

"And what shall we call the woman who counts — now that we both know what her mistress intended to call her?"

Behind me, Isandro's breath caught. Ahead of me, four hundred and eleven caged stars dimmed, all together, like held breath.

I had promised myself I wouldn't answer any questions directly.

But he had said her name. *Maren.* He had put her into the room with me, deliberately, the way you set a piece on a board, and every instinct I owned — the ledger instincts, the survival instincts, the Maren-crane-shaped wound instincts — all of them rose at once and demanded I say something.

So I did the only safe thing left.

I answered his question with a number.

"One," I said. "There is one thing you may call me. And you'll have to earn it, same as everyone else."

The King smiled. Somewhere in the galleries, dozens of unfinished faces turned toward the sound, hungry as moths.

"*Interesting,*" he said, and sat back down, and gestured me forward into the light.
