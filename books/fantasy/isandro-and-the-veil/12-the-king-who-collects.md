## 12. The King Who Collects

The throne room of the Undercourt was the largest room I had ever stood in, which was saying something, because I had once inventoried the Gilded Veil's Hall of Borrowed Weather and that room could hold a hurricane with room left over for the apology.

This one was bigger. Bone-white stone, vaulted so high the ceiling vanished into a darkness that wasn't empty — it was *full*. I looked up and understood, with the particular nausea of an accountant catching an error in her own books, where the stars had gone. They hung in cages of black iron, hundreds of them, thousands, each one dimmed and shuttered like a lamp in a window whose owner has died. Their light didn't illuminate so much as *seep*, pooling on the floor in silver stains that the courtiers waded through as though it were nothing. As though light were a thing you could get on your shoes.

The courtiers themselves were worse. They wore faces the way a tailor wears a half-finished coat — features suggested rather than committed to. A mouth here, an eye there, the rest of the face smooth as an unwritten page. One of them turned toward me as we passed, and I had the distinct impression of being *read*.

"Don't look at them," Isandro murmured. His voice had gone thin. Since the stair, since the passage that had reshaped itself around his evaporating memories, he had been speaking less and less, as if words were coins he was afraid of spending. "They take an interest, and interest compounds."

"Everything compounds," I said. "That's not a threat, that's arithmetic."

He almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile in three days, and I held onto it the way you hold onto a railing on a ship in bad weather.

We had been escorted — *herded* was the more honest verb — through corridors of white stone by a chamberlain whose face was the most finished in the court, which somehow made it more unsettling, the way a perfect forgery is more unsettling than an obvious one. He had a mouth that knew how to be polite and eyes that had never learned. He had not asked our names. I had noticed that. In a place where names were currency, asking for one was either a courtesy or a robbery, and the chamberlain had clearly decided we weren't worth the trouble of either.

At the foot of the dais, he stopped, and said, in a voice like a drawer closing: "The King will see the debt's keepers."

*Debt's keepers.* Not guests. Not petitioners. I filed the phrasing away. In my experience, what a creditor calls you tells you exactly how they intend to collect.

* * *

The Underking did not sit on a throne so much as the throne had grown around him, the way a tree grows around a fence post it has decided to keep. He was tall in the way that architecture is tall, robed in a darkness that had actual weight — I could see the hem of it pressing grooves into the stone. His face was the only complete face in the room. That should have been a relief. It was not. It was a handsome face, patient, faintly amused, the face of a man who has never once been surprised and has grown gently bored by the fact.

He looked at me. And then he said his first word, and I understood immediately why the whole court spoke in questions, because his voice was not a voice at all. It was an opening. A door held ajar. Everything he said was shaped like a question because everything he said *was* one — an invitation for you to walk into your own ruin and call it an answer.

"Ada Crane," he said. "Daughter of no one. Apprentice of Maren Crane, who taught you letters and ledgers and how to boil a memory down to its essence without scorching it. Keeper of the Gilded Veil's books for four years, two months, and eleven days. You balance to the copper, don't you? Not the coin. The copper."

I had never told anyone my full name in this place. I had never told anyone my full name *anywhere*, not since I ran from Maren, and even before that I had guarded it like a coin held between my teeth. My name was the one thing she had never gotten her hands on, the one asset she had tried and failed to liquidate, and I had carried it out of her house sewn into the lining of myself.

"You know my name," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the only trick I have ever been grateful to my mistress for teaching me.

"Is that what you keep in the lining?" he asked, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was warm. "Where do you suppose I keep mine?"

Isandro shifted beside me. I felt it more than heard it — his shoulder brushing mine, the small involuntary lean of a man whose body was trying to stand behind someone even though his mind had forgotten what standing behind someone meant. Three days ago he had known my name. Two days ago he had known my face. This morning, on the last stair, he had looked at me with the polite blankness of a stranger on a ferry and asked if we had met, and I had said *every day for four years*, and he had nodded as though I'd told him the weather.

I was keeping his life for him in a ledger I recited as we walked. I intended to keep reciting it until one of us ran out of pages.

"We've come about the contract," I said. "The founding debt. The guild wants to negotiate."

"Does it?" the Underking asked. "Or do you?"

"The guild sent me."

"Did it?" A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Or did you volunteer, because you are the only person in that gilded little house who reads a contract all the way to the end?"

I said nothing, which he seemed to find satisfying, the way a fisherman finds a tug on the line satisfying.

"Bring it," he said, not loudly, but the word went through the room like a bell through water.

* * *

They brought the contract on a tray of black iron, carried by two courtiers whose faces were only half-drawn, as if whoever was sketching them had been interrupted. The document itself was — I will be honest, because I am always honest about documents, it is the one vanity I allow myself — beautiful. Three generations old and the vellum hadn't yellowed. The ink hadn't faded. The seal at the bottom was pressed deep enough to hold a shadow.

I had seen this contract once before, or a copy of it, in Vess's private ledger, the night the stars went out. She had read the payment clause aloud in a voice like someone reading their own diagnosis. *Payment deferred until the night the sky forgets us.* One soul, owed to the Undercourt. The guild had been built on the deferred payment the way a house is built on a filled-in well — everyone living in it, drawing water from it, pretending the ground was ground.

"Three generations," the Underking said, "your guild has profited. Bottled memories. Borrowed luck. Years of life, bought cheap and sold dear. Do you know where those goods come from, Ada Crane? Have you ever asked where the Veil acquires its inventory?"

"I keep the books," I said. "I don't source the stock."

"No," he agreed. "You don't. That is the luxury of ledgers, isn't it? They tell you *what* and *how much*, and let you skip *whence*. But everything comes from somewhere. Every bottle of luck was poured out of a life. Every borrowed year was taken from someone who wasn't finished with it. And every soul—" he touched the contract with one long finger, not quite touching the ink, the way a man touches a wound he has decided not to reopen, "—every soul has a name."

He turned the contract toward us.

"Read it," he said. "You are the one who reads to the end. Read it *all* the way to the end."

I stepped forward. Isandro's hand caught my sleeve — a reflex, I think, older than his memory, some part of him that still knew to be afraid for me even if he no longer knew why — and I covered his hand with mine for a moment and then let go and walked to the tray.

The contract was written in a formal hand, the careful, self-important script of a guild founder who wanted posterity to take notes. I read the preamble. I read the consideration — a founding stake of impossible goods, the Undercourt's seed capital, if you wanted to be crude about it, and I usually did. I read the payment clause. One soul, freely given, due upon the night the sky forgets.

And then the signature.

I had expected Vess's ancestor. Some founder with a name like Aldous or Merrick, some dead man's ambition made flesh in the form of a debt. That was the story the guild told itself. That was the story Vess had told her inner circle with tears in her eyes, guilt calcified into duty.

The signature was *Isandro's*.

Not similar. Not an echo. *His* — the particular way he crossed his *d*, the small upward flick at the end of the final *o*, a hand I had watched a thousand times across four years of shared desks and shared tea and maps of places that didn't exist yet. I knew that handwriting the way you know a voice through a wall.

But the ink was wrong. The ink was *old*. Older than the paper it sat on should have allowed, older than the founder's own signature above it, as though the name had been written first and the contract grown around it afterward, the way the throne had grown around the King.

"Isandro," I said. My voice did something I didn't authorize. "Come here."

He came. He stood beside me, and he looked down at the page, and I watched his face do a thing I had never seen it do: nothing. Complete, smooth nothing. The face of a man looking at a stranger's handwriting.

"Is that mine?" he asked.

"Yes," I said.

"I don't remember writing it."

"I know," I said, and my chest did something painful and structural. "I know you don't."

"A child's hand," the Underking observed, from his throne of grown stone. "Isn't it? The letters are careful because the child was being careful. Children sign carefully when the thing they are signing frightens them. What does a child have to trade that a court like mine would want, Ada Crane? What is the one thing a child owns outright, that no one can take from him except by his own hand?"

His name. A child's name. Isandro had signed his name away — when, to whom, for what, I didn't know yet, and the not-knowing was a splinter working its way toward my heart.

But that wasn't the worst. The worst was below.

Because beneath Isandro's signature — beneath the old ink in the child's careful hand — there was a second signature. Smaller. Crowded into the margin like something added later, or like something that had always been there and had only now decided to be seen. A woman's hand. Sharp, slanted, economical, the hand of someone who never wrote a word more than the bargain required.

I knew that hand too.

I had seen it on the flyleaf of every book in Maren Crane's house. On the labels of every bottle in her stillroom. On the ledger she kept of her own debts, the one I had found as a girl, hidden under a floorboard, the one that had taught me that a ledger could be a confession. I had run from that hand. I had crossed a border and built a life specifically, deliberately, at the cost of everything else, so that I would never see that hand again.

*Maren Crane.*

The room got very quiet, or I got very quiet, which amounts to the same thing from the inside.

"She was here," I said. "She came here. She—"

"Did she?" the Underking asked, mild as milk.

"Her signature is on the contract."

"Is it?" He tilted his head, and the caged stars overhead seemed to dim in sympathy, or in warning. "A signature is an interesting thing, Ada Crane. Do you know what it actually is? Not the hand. Not the ink. It is a *claim*. It says: I was here, I agreed, I am bound. But a signature can be placed on a page in a great many ways. Signed in person. Signed by proxy. Signed under duress. Signed by someone who bought the right to sign. Signed—" and here his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which from him was like a knife wrapped in velvet, which is still a knife, "—signed by a woman who had recently acquired, through her own bargain with a frightened child, the full and legal ownership of that child's name. Tell me. In your books. When a debtor signs with an asset that is not hers — when she spends what she has merely *held* in trust, or held by trickery — what do your ledgers call that?"

"Fraud," I said.

The word came out of me flat and cold and certain, and the moment it left my mouth, every courtier in the cathedral turned. All those half-finished faces, all at once, like a field of flowers turning toward the sun. Somewhere in the iron cages overhead, a star shifted in its sleep.

"Fraud," the Underking repeated, tasting it. "And what does fraud do to a contract, in the arithmetic you trust more than magic?"

"Voids it," I said. "Voids it *entirely*. Root and branch. Every clause. Every signature. Every debt."

"Then you have your answer," he said, and spread his hands, and the gesture was so reasonable, so magnanimous, that every instinct I owned stood up and screamed. "The contract is before you. The signatures are before you. The child's name was not Maren Crane's to sell, and yet she sold it — layered it into your guild's founding like a poison layered into a wedding feast. Your guild has lived three generations in a house built on a forged foundation. You have only to prove it." He smiled again, that warm, patient, terrible smile. "You are the one who reads to the end. You are the one who balances to the copper. So tell me, Ada Crane — can you prove it? Here, where truth has teeth? Before a court that has been hungry for a very long time?"

I looked down at the contract. At the child's careful signature. At Maren's sharp, slanted hand in the margin, crowded in like a thief's mark on a door.

My past and my present had just been introduced, and they had shaken hands, and the handshake had gone on too long, and now they were both looking at me, waiting to see which one I would betray.

Isandro's hand found mine in the dark. He didn't know my name anymore. He didn't know his own. But his fingers knew mine, some ledger deeper than memory, and they closed around them the way a hand closes around a railing on a ship in bad weather.

"Yes," I said to the King. "I can prove it. Give me the contract, a lamp, and until moonrise."

"Until moonrise," the Underking repeated, and the question in his voice was the sharpest one yet, because it wasn't really a question at all. It was a countdown. "And what will you do, Ada Crane, if you find what you are looking for — and it is not enough?"
