## 13. Bargaining With a Hole Where a Heart Was

The Underking's throne room had a way of making silence feel like an invoice.

Ada stood at the base of the dais with her satchel open at her feet, and the courtiers drifted around her like sketches someone had abandoned halfway — faces that were almost noses and almost mouths, eyes suggested rather than committed to. They wanted to look at her. She could feel it, the pressure of unfinished attention. But they were waiting for the King to finish looking first.

He sat on a chair of black stone that might have grown out of the floor rather than been placed there, and he did not look like anything so simple as a man. He was tall in the way a doorway is tall — you passed through him or you didn't. His face was there when she looked directly at it and gone when she glanced away, which she suspected was deliberate, because everything about him was deliberate.

"Have you counted your goods?" he asked. His voice came from everywhere the light wasn't.

"I've counted them twice," Ada said. "I'm an accountant. Counting twice is a religious observance."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been the face rearranging itself. With the Underking, the distinction seemed academic.

"Then show me."

She knelt — not out of reverence; she'd learned long ago that kneeling was just a negotiating posture that happened to spare your dignity by spending it — and opened the satchel.

Vess had packed it herself, back in the guildhall, before the descent. Ada remembered standing in the vault while Vess lifted each item from its case with the tenderness of a woman handling her own organs. The Gilded Veil's reserve stock. Three generations of acquisition, and here it was in one bag: the guild's entire liquid wealth, carried down a stair that wasn't there by a bookkeeper who still couldn't quite believe any of this was legal tender anywhere.

"Three vials of bottled luck," Ada said aloud, laying them on the bone-white floor where their glow pooled like spilled honey. "Harvested from a gambler in Corvane who won forty hands straight and then, per our standard practice, lost everything else. One year of life, single vintage, unspent — taken consensually from a dying woman who preferred her granddaughter have it and was talked out of that preference by our best saleswoman." She paused. "Which I have always thought was the ugliest transaction we ever made, for what it's worth."

"It is worth something," said the Underking, "that you know it is ugly."

"Also: a jar of borrowed courage, half-full. A needle threaded with three days of borrowed patience. And—" she hesitated, then set down the last item, small and dull as a river pebble— "a stone of true quiet. We never found a buyer. Nobody wants silence badly enough to pay what it costs."

The courtiers stirred at that. A ripple went through the unfinished faces, mouths completing themselves briefly with hunger and then smoothing away again.

"You bring me scraps," said the Underking.

"I bring you inventory," Ada said. "And inventory has prices, and prices are negotiable, and that makes this a negotiation instead of an execution. You could take everything Isandro has left without my permission. You haven't. That tells me you want something from this room besides him." She kept her voice level. Her heart was doing arithmetic of its own — fast, ugly sums. "So let's find out what."

Behind her, Isandro shifted. She heard his breath catch — that wet, frightened sound he'd been making since the memories started leaving him, since his mother's face went and then his hometown and then, two hours ago on the stair, the name of the street he'd grown up on. He'd asked her, in a voice stripped down to the studs, whether he'd ever told her about the winter the well froze. She'd said yes, you told me, and narrated it back to him word for word until he could hold it again. That was becoming her whole job now: keeping his life on deposit for him, paying it out in installments.

"The contract says the soul comes due," the Underking said. "The night the sky forgets us. That night has come and passed. By rights I should collect."

"By rights." Ada tasted the words. "Your Majesty, I've read the founding contract. I know what rights are written there. But I also know contracts are instruments, and instruments can be played slowly or quickly, and you've chosen slowly for three generations. Why?"

Silence. The imprisoned stars overhead — the missing sky, caged in bone-white lattice above the cathedral vaults — flickered in their prisons, as if listening.

"Because collection is easy," the Underking said at last, "and I have had very little that was easy. Everything under this mountain must be bargained for. Even forgetting. Even mercy." He leaned forward, and the darkness around him deepened to make room. "You wish to buy time. Very well. Name your terms."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about bargaining with a king who collects debts by unmaking people: the trick is not to want anything too visibly.

Ada had watched Maren Crane negotiate a hundred times over the hedge-witch's crooked table — with farmers, with spirits, once with a tax collector and once with something wearing the tax collector's skin. Maren always wanted things loudly. That was how she lost them. She'd reach for a price with her whole face and the other party would see exactly how much she needed it, and need is leverage handed to your enemy gift-wrapped.

So Ada laid out the terms flatly, the way she read ledger lines.

"One moon's time. Until the next full moonrise. In exchange, all of it — every item in this satchel. Bottled luck, the year of life, the courage, the patience, the quiet. The guild's entire reserve, surrendered without recourse."

"And what does the guild receive?" the Underking asked.

"A deferral. Nothing more. Isandro remains... intact. Whole. Remembering what he remembers." She swallowed. "For one month."

The Underking regarded her. Or the place where regarding would happen if he had eyes in the conventional sense.

"You understand," he said, "that the debt does not shrink while it waits."

"Yes."

"You understand that interest accrues. That he will continue to forget, even deferred. Slowly. The way ice melts in a cellar. And that at the next moonrise, if the debt stands unresolved, he will be unmade entirely — not killed. Killed leaves a body. Unmaking leaves a shape where a person used to be, and then not even the shape." He said it the way she wrote figures in a margin: precisely, without cruelty, which somehow made it crueler.

"I understand," Ada said. Her voice held. She was proud of that later, and ashamed of being proud.

"Then why defer at all? You gain a month of watching him fade. That is not survival. That is grief with a schedule."

"Because a month is enough time to void the contract," Ada said. "If it can be voided. And if I'm wrong—" she looked at the satchel, emptied now onto the pale floor, everything the Gilded Veil had hoarded and hidden and traded souls' worth of years to accumulate— "then at least the guild dies solvent."

The Underking was quiet for a long moment. Above them, a star turned in its cage, restless, throwing thin light across the courtiers' half-made faces.

"You are unlike the others who have stood there," he said finally. "They begged. They offered me other souls, other names, other people's winters. You offer me objects and arithmetic." A pause. "Why do you not offer yourself? I have been waiting for it since you arrived. Everyone offers themselves eventually. It is the cheapest currency and the most common."

Ada felt the question go through her like a splinter finding old wood. *Everyone offers themselves.* Maren had tried to sell her name when she was sixteen years old. She had run from that table with nothing but a talent for numbers and a stolen ledger, and every bargain she'd struck since had been designed to keep her own self off the market. Ledgers were safe. Numbers didn't bleed.

But the Underking was watching, and the courtiers had gone very still, and somewhere behind her Isandro breathed.

"Maybe I'll get there," she said. "Maybe that's next chapter. But today I'm trading goods, not persons. Take it or leave it."

A sound moved through the court — not laughter, but the memory of laughter, the sketch of it.

"Take it," said the Underking.

* * *

The transaction itself took less time than the negotiation, which Ada found obscene. All those years of the guild's accumulation — Vess's careful acquisitions, the sealed cases, the triple locks — signed away in the space between two heartbeats. The Underking did not sign, exactly. He simply looked at the items, and they were his. The luck vials dimmed and sank into the floor like coins into deep water. The year of life went last, and as it went, Ada felt the whole room inhale.

"That one was expensive," the Underking said, almost gently.

"They're all expensive. That's the point of them."

"You mistake me. I mean to *you*. I watch what traders reach for last. You touched that vial twice before offering it." He tilted his head, the gesture slightly wrong, slightly too far. "A year of life. Whose year was it meant to be?"

Ada didn't answer. Because the truth was that Vess had bought that vintage eight years ago, and had kept it in her private case rather than the general reserve, and had told no one why, and Ada — who balanced the books, who knew every entry — had never asked. Some entries you don't audit. Maybe Vess had been saving it for someone. Maybe that someone had been Isandro, though Vess had never once treated him as more than the quiet cartographer. Maybe it had been meant for Vess herself, at the end, the way rich people save medicine for their own final hours.

It didn't matter now. It belonged to the dark under the mountain.

"The deferral is granted," the Underking said. "One month, measured from this hour. The debt stands. The interest runs." He rose, and the throne-room shadows rose with him, and for a moment the entire cathedral of bone-white stone seemed to lean in. "But hear the rest of the term, keeper of accounts, because I give it to you freely and freely given truths bind deeper than any signature."

"I'm listening."

"At the next moonrise, regardless of what you carry back up your impossible stair — regardless of forged papers, voided claims, overturned law — Isandro Vale will be unmade." 

The name hit her like cold water. *Isandro Vale.* She had never heard his surname. She hadn't known he had one. Neither, she realized with a lurch, had he — not anymore, not since the stair, not since whatever had eaten it out of him on the way down. The King said it the way you read a title deed: as fact, as ownership.

"That is not a threat," the Underking continued. "It is a property notice. The contract holds him. Your goods purchased delay, not exemption. If you wish him to survive the month's end, you will not merely postpone the debt. You will destroy its claim to exist." He smiled, or the space around his face suggested smiling. "I tell you this because I am curious what you will do with it. Curiosity is the only luxury I have left."

"Why?" Ada heard herself ask. "Why tell me the rules? Kings don't tip their hands."

"Kings who collect debts," said the Underking, "must occasionally be sure the debt is worth collecting. Prove it is. Or prove it isn't, and take him home." He settled back onto the black stone. "Either outcome interests me. Now go. The ferryman grows impatient, and patience is the one thing under this mountain that is never free."

* * *

They climbed back toward the tollgate in silence, because Isandro couldn't speak yet.

When they reached the antechamber where the ferryman waited — that grey, patient creature poling its boat through a river of dark — Ada stopped and turned to him, because some things shouldn't wait for better lighting.

"Isandro. Look at me."

He looked. His eyes were still his — that was the mercy of it, the terrible mercy. Whatever the debt took, it took from the edges inward, and his eyes hadn't been touched. Yet.

"What did he say? At the end?" Isandro's voice was rough. "I heard the word 'moonrise.' I heard the rest go strange."

"He said we have a month." Ada took both his hands. They were cold, cartographer's hands, ink-stained at the knuckles even now, even here. "And he said that at the end of it, unless we break the contract entirely — not delay it, not appeal it, *break* it — he takes you anyway."

Isandro absorbed that the way he absorbed everything: quietly, thoroughly, like paper absorbing ink. Then he laughed — one short, cracked note that startled them both.

"A month. I gave the guild eight years of maps and got a month." He shook his head. "Ada. What did it cost? What did you give him?"

"The reserve stock. Everything. Luck, the year of life, all of it."

"That's not everything. He doesn't grant audiences for stationery." His grip tightened. "What else?"

"Nothing else," Ada said. "That's the whole bill."

And it was true — technically. Technically, she had paid in goods. But standing there in the dark with his fading hands in hers, she understood what the Underking had actually seen in her, and why he'd accepted so easily, and what he was really collecting, month by month, transaction by transaction.

He wasn't buying bottled luck. He was buying the sight of what people spent to save each other. Every payment taught him the exchange rate of a human heart.

And she had just shown him hers, priced at one year of somebody's life, and he had filed it away with the rest.

"Come on," she said, steering him toward the boat, because the ferryman was watching and the ferryman's fare was already paid and she refused to think about what memories she'd have to feed it on the return trip. "We've got a forgery to find."

"Forgery?" Isandro asked, as the grey water took the hull.

"The founding contract," Ada said. "Nobody sells a soul clean, Isandro. Not even kings. There's a flaw in that paper — there's always a flaw — and I'm going to find it, and then we're going to make your king eat it."
