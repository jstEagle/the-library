## 14. What Isandro Sold

The first thing I remembered was the smell.

Not the memory itself — the smell came first, the way a key comes before the door it opens. Woodsmoke and fever-sweat and crushed yarrow, and under all of it a sweetness like apples rotting in a cellar, which I would learn much later was the smell magic makes when it's being spent badly.

I was sitting at my drafting table with a pen in my hand and no idea what I'd been drawing. The map beneath my fingers showed the mountain again — it always showed the mountain now — and my hand had traced the same descending stair eleven times, each line a little deeper than the last, as though I were trying to dig through the paper into whatever waited below it.

And then the smell arrived, and the door opened behind it, and I fell thirty years down.

* * *

Her name was Sefa. My mother. I hadn't known that an hour ago. I'd known I had hands that could draw and a face people called forgettable and nothing else — no mother, no hometown, no winter I could name. The debt had been eating me from the edges inward, quietly, like moths in a coat you only wear once a year.

Now I was seven years old, and my mother was dying, and I knew her the way you know your own heartbeat: completely, without needing to think about it.

She lay in the bed by the eastern window because she liked to watch the light come up over the terraces, except there was no light coming up anymore, just grey, and her breathing had the wet rattle in it that even a child knows is the sound of a body being argued out of its lease.

The village was called Thornlow. I could see it whole — the crooked bell tower, the mill pond with the duckweed, old Hetta's goats getting into the barley every single autumn while Hetta swore and wept and never once fixed the fence. I could see my own small room above the kitchen. I could see my mother teaching me letters by writing them in flour on the table, because paper cost money and flour, if you failed, you swept up and used anyway.

*Learn everything, little one,* she'd say. *Names especially. A name is the one thing they can't tax.*

I want to laugh at that, now. I don't have it in me. Grief takes up all the room where laughter used to live.

The hedge-witch came on the ninth day of the fever. I remember the ninth day specifically because my father — Tomas, a cooper, hands like split oak — had counted the days aloud each morning the way other men count money, as though arithmetic could hold death at bay. Nine days. On the tenth, he said, he would ride for the physician in Marrowgate, though we both knew there wasn't coin for a physician and the roads were bad and my mother's hands were already cold.

Maren Crane arrived instead. She came on foot with a staff too big for her and a satchel that clinked, and she looked at our house the way a merchant looks at stock.

I knew her name before she gave it. Everyone within three valleys knew Maren Crane, the witch who could set bones and curse wells and find lost things, and whose prices were never money. That was the part parents said in low voices, after the children were asleep: *never money.*

My father let her in because hope makes beggars of proud men. He sat across from her at our own table like a man across from a magistrate, and she didn't take off her cloak, and she smelled of woodsmoke and rotting apples.

"I can pull her back," Maren said. She said it to my father but she looked at me, sitting on the stool by the fire where I did the ledgers for Hetta's egg money, because even then I was the one who kept accounts. "Fever like this, nine days in — I can turn it. But the working needs a price."

"We have the good barrels," my father said. "The ones I haven't sold yet. And the cow—"

"Not money." She smiled. I have spent my life drawing maps of places that don't exist, so I know a false landmark when I see one, and that smile was drawn onto her face by someone who had never studied the original. "Something rarer. Something *true*."

My father's face did what faces do when they realize the trap has been around them the whole time.

"What then?"

"A name." Maren turned fully toward me then, and I felt it like weather changing. "A true name freely given. They're worth more than gold, more than years. There are buyers below—" and here she stopped herself, smooth as a ledger line ruled straight, "—there are buyers who pay well for such things. I can save your wife tonight."

"She's seven," my father said.

"Names ripen young. Better now than later, when they've gone and tangled themselves up in the world." She leaned forward. "Or you can ride for Marrowgate tomorrow. If she lives that long."

I have thought about that moment more times than any other moment of my life, and I still cannot make it kind. He was a cooper. He loved my mother with the dumb, stubborn love of a man who had made her a cradle before they had a child for it. He was being asked to choose which of us to keep whole, and the witch had made very sure there was no third option on the table.

But here is the thing I found in the bottom of the memory, the thing that burns worse than anything Maren Crane ever did:

*I spoke first.*

"Take mine."

Seven years old, and I had heard *a name is the one thing they can't tax*, and I understood — the way children understand things, sideways and absolutely — that names mattered, and therefore that mine must be worth something, and therefore that I could buy my mother's life with it. I thought I was being clever. I thought I'd found the loophole. Sell the one thing they can't tax, buy back the one thing we couldn't afford to lose.

Maren Crane looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been surprise, or appetite, or — and I will never be able to rule this out — the briefest flicker of shame, quickly drowned.

"Well," she said softly. "There's a bargainer already."

My father said my name — my real name, the one I am not going to write here, because some things should stay buried even inside your own skull — and I remember that he said it the way you say a word you're trying to keep hold of. And then he cried, which I had never seen him do, and he said, "Isandro. Not your name. Never your name. Promise me."

So they gave me another one to wear instead, a plain name, a coat-name, *Isandro*, which means nothing anywhere, which is exactly why it fit. And Maren took my true name out of me with two fingers and a whispered word, the way you draw a splinter, and I watched it go. It looked like — this is the strange part — it looked like a small bright thread, and she wound it around her finger and put it in a locket, and she smiled her false-map smile, and upstairs my mother's breathing changed.

By morning the fever had broken. By spring she was planting beans and scolding me for reading at supper, alive, whole, hers.

I have never once regretted the trade. That is the worst of it. Whatever rage I carry, I cannot aim it at the bargain itself, because the bargain bought me every morning since — her voice, her flour-letters, her hands in my hair the winter she finally got better. You cannot hate the price of the only thing you wanted.

You *can*, however, hate what happened next.

* * *

Because here is what I saw, in the memory, that the child could not understand:

After Maren left our house, she didn't go home. I know because I followed her. Seven-year-olds are terrible spies and I was no exception; I trailed her along the mill path crunching through frost, and when she stopped at the crossroads and took out the locket, I hid behind Hetta's fence post and watched.

She wasn't alone. There was a man waiting at the crossroads, and he was wrong in a way I didn't have words for then — dressed fine but standing like something that had learned to stand by watching other things stand. His face kept almost resolving and then not quite, like a sketch someone kept erasing and redrawing.

"You said one season," Maren said. "One season of holding, then you return it."

"Contracts amend," the man said. His voice sounded like questions feel. "New terms. Better terms. Your debts, cleared. All of them."

"I don't want them cleared. I want the boy's name *back*. It was collateral, not payment—"

"It was inventory." The man held out his hand, palm up, patient as stone. "And inventory appreciates, Mistress Crane. There is a guild forming above. Gilded. Ambitious. They need founding capital they don't know they're borrowing." The almost-face curved. "Layer it into their charter. Bury it deep. In three generations no one will look past page one."

"And the boy?"

"The boy will grow up forgetting he ever owned anything worth taking." A pause, and the voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was somehow the most horrifying note in the whole memory. "Forgetting is mercy, mistress. We deal in it. Ask anyone below."

I pressed my face against the fence post until the bark bit, and I understood none of it, and I understood all of it, the way children do.

Then the memory tore loose from me — the way memories do when they've been locked somewhere dark — and I was back at my drafting table, an adult, with ink drying on the eleventh stair, shaking so hard the pen chattered against the bottle.

* * *

Ada found me there. Of course Ada found me; she finds everything, it's her whole nature, she is a creature made entirely of finding things out. She stood in the doorway of the map room with her arms crossed and her ledger under one arm like a shield, and she looked at my face and stopped pretending to be casual.

"You remembered something," she said.

"My mother's name is Sefa." My voice came out flat and far away, like a report read by someone else. "Our village was Thornlow. My father made barrels. She wrote letters in flour because paper was dear." I laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Three hours ago I didn't know whether I'd ever had a mother at all. Now I can't stop seeing the mill pond."

Ada sat down across from me without asking. She does that. Most people ask; she calculates that the answer costs more than the silence, and sits.

"Tell me the rest," she said.

So I told her. The fever. The witch. The price. And when I got to the crossroads — the man with the unfinished face, the word *inventory*, the guild forming above — Ada went very still, in the specific way she goes still when numbers start agreeing with each other behind her eyes.

"Say the last part again," she said.

"'Bury it in their charter. Three generations, no one looks past page one.'"

"The founding contract," she whispered. "Page one. Vess keeps it sealed — she sealed the archive the night the stars went out. Isandro. Your name isn't just *on* that contract." Her knuckles whitened on her ledger. "Your name may be the *collateral*. You've been working inside the debt your whole life. Every wage the guild paid you came out of — out of interest on *you*."

I looked down at my hands. Mapmaker's hands, ink-stained, steady except when they weren't lately. Hands that had drawn coastlines and mountain passes and cities that didn't exist yet, for a guild built on top of a stolen boy.

I should have felt horror. I expect I'll get to the horror eventually. What I felt first, rising up through thirty years like water through a cracked floor, was rage — clean and total and strangely clarifying, the way a ledger balances, everything suddenly in its right column.

They let me draw their maps. They paid me in coin minted from my own soul. I ate at their table and called it family, and the whole time I was the cellar stock, the buried asset, the founding capital. Maren Crane took a child's ransom and resold it to furnish her ambitions, and the Undercourt tucked it into a charter like a forged signature in a margin, and three generations of impossible commerce grew up like ivy over a grave.

"They're meeting tonight," Ada said. "Vess called the council. Bram wants to run. Vess wants to pay." She hesitated, and I watched her decide to tell me the truth, because she always decides that eventually; it's her best quality and her most expensive one. "I told them I want to void the contract. Go down and break it at the source."

"With what?" I asked. "You've seen how they write. Nothing down there is written to be broken."

"No," Ada said. "It's written to be *audited*." And there was a look on her face I'd never seen before — the look of a woman who has found the error in the book, the one misplaced figure that unravels the entire column, and is deciding whether to say it aloud yet. "Stay close to me tonight. And Isandro — if the council votes to give them a substitute soul—"

"It won't be yours," I said.

"That's not your decision either," she said, wry and gentle at once, and left me with my maps and my rage and my mother's name, which I repeated to myself like a man counting coins he'd been told were long spent. *Sefa. Sefa. Thornlow.* Everything they buried is coming back up. I intend to be standing on the surface, holding a shovel, when it arrives.

Below the mountain, something heard me think it. I felt the stairs on my map shift — just slightly — like a throat clearing.

Twelve lines now. Deeper than ever. And for the first time, I knew exactly where they led.
