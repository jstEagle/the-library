## 15. The Forgery

I did not sleep.

This will surprise no one who has ever balanced a ledger the night before an audit: there is a particular insomnia that comes not from fear but from a number that refuses to sit still. Most of my sleepless nights have been that kind — a column three coppers off, a page that won't reconcile, some small stubborn wrongness scratching at the inside of my skull like a moth against glass. You don't rest until you find it. You *can't*. The world is out of true by exactly the width of one entry, and your mind walks the halls of itself turning over ledgers until it finds which door is open.

Tonight the wrongness was not three coppers. Tonight it was a soul, and a mountain, and a king beneath the world who collects debts by unmaking the people who owe them. But the itch was the same itch. That is the terrible comfort of arithmetic: it does not care how large the sum is. A discrepancy is a discrepancy, and it wants finding.

So I sat in my narrow room above the counting floor with the founding contract spread before me under three candles, and I read it again. And again. And then I stopped reading it like a confession and started reading it like what it actually was.

A ledger entry.

* * *

Let me tell you something about documents, since I have spent eleven years learning it and might as well spend the hours before dawn teaching. Every document is a story told twice: once in its words and once in its materials. The words are what the writer wanted said. The materials are what actually happened — the ink's age, the paper's grain, the pressure of the pen, the order in which things were done. Words can lie all day long. Materials are honest the way weather is honest. They simply report.

Hedge-witches know this. Maren Crane knew it better than anyone I ever met, though she'd have hexed me for saying so. "Paper remembers," she used to say, tapping a contract with one yellowed nail. "People forget, dear, but paper was there." She taught me to date every entry, to witness every signature, to seal everything twice — once for the parties and once for the truth of it. She taught me forgery too, though she called it "correction," and she taught it so I would recognize it. That was her one act of accidental kindness. She built the trap she'd later try to spring on me, and she showed me its teeth first.

I hated her for eleven years for what she tried to do. Sitting at my desk with the Undercourt's contract under my hands, I hated her a little less, because every cruel lesson she'd beaten into me had been leading here, to this table, this night, this page. She had made me a weapon without knowing what I'd be aimed at.

The contract was old. Old the way bedrock is old. The vellum had gone the color of weak tea, soft as cloth at the edges where generations of thumbs had held it. The seal — the Undercourt's seal, a mountain with a door in it, pressed in wax gone black as dried blood — sat fat and intact at the foot of the page. Above it, two signatures.

Isandro's name. Written in a hand too fine and too old for the boy I ate breakfast with. Beneath it, smaller, cramped, hurried:

*Maren Crane.*

My mistress. My captor. The woman whose debts I had fled across half a country to escape, sitting there on the most dangerous page in the guild's possession, in ink older than my grandmother.

When Vess had shown me this contract — when she'd confessed the founding debt to her inner circle with her voice flat as a closed account book — I had read those signatures the way everyone reads signatures. As names. As proof of who had done the selling and who had been sold. Isandro sold himself, or someone wearing his name had; Maren Crane had countersigned. A horror, but a legible one. A story with a beginning, middle, and end.

But signatures aren't just names. They're events. And events happen in an order, whether or not anyone bothers to write the order down.

* * *

Here is what kept me awake, in the end. Not the names. The margins.

The contract's margins were dense with notation — Undercourt script, the legal shorthand of a court that has had longer than recorded history to develop bureaucratic habits. I couldn't read most of it. But I didn't need to read it. I needed to measure it.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that had been scratching at me since Vess laid the page on the council table and Bram had sworn and Vess had gone gray as ash: the marginalia around Maren Crane's signature was *fresher than the marginalia around Isandro's.*

Not newer-looking. Fresher. The ink itself.

I want to be careful here, because this is the whole knife of the thing and I refuse to dull it. Old ink does not merely change color. It changes *behavior*. Ink that has lived on vellum for centuries stops sitting *on* the page and starts sitting *in* it. It sinks into the grain. It bonds. If you run a thumb across genuinely ancient writing, you feel nothing — the letters have become part of the landscape, hills worn down by rain. Fresh ink sits proud. It catches. It has edges.

Maren Crane's signature caught.

I tested it six ways, because I am who I am and because if I was going to bet a soul on a hypothesis I intended to show my work. I breathed on it — old ink stays matte under breath; hers gleamed faintly, the way recent iron-gall does. I tilted the page against candlelight — her letters threw shadows Isandro's did not. I took the finest needle from my sewing kit and touched it, barely, to the tail of the final letter of her surname, and a fleck came away on the point, black and wet-look, while the same test on his signature left the needle clean.

And then I did the thing Maren would have done first, and which I had been too frightened to do until now, because doing it meant admitting what I suspected: I turned the page over.

Signatures bleed. Especially old ones, especially iron-gall, especially through vellum thin enough to hold a seal this heavy. Isandro's name ghosted through to the reverse — pale, reversed, sunk deep into the fiber, unmistakably native to the page. It had been written when the vellum was fresh and thirsty, and it had drunk its way through.

Maren Crane's signature had no reverse. Nothing. Clean vellum, front and back, as blank behind her name as a lie.

You cannot write on the front of a page and leave nothing behind. Unless you wrote after the page stopped being able to drink. Unless the page was already old, already sized and hardened by centuries, when your pen touched it.

Unless the signature was added later.

* * *

I sat back. The candles guttered in the draft from the sealed windows — Vess had ordered the vaults sealed, and the whole Gilded Veil had gone quiet as a held breath, and my little room smelled of tallow and dust and my own unwashed panic. Somewhere below, Bram's boots crossed the counting floor on patrol, slow and regular, a man walking the perimeter of a life he expected to lose.

Later, I thought. Later I'll feel what this means. Right now there is work.

Because a late-added signature raises a question, and the question is not *who forged it* but *when*, and *when* has an answer, and the answer is written right there on the page in the only language I trust absolutely.

Dates.

The contract bore its own record of its making, as contracts must. The body of the text named the terms — one soul, held in surety against the Gilded Veil's founding capital, payment deferred unto "the night the sky forgets us" — and the text concluded with the formal attestation clause, the part every clerk in every court in the world copies without thinking: *sealed and witnessed upon* such-and-such a day, in such-and-such a year, by the reckoning of such-and-such authority. The Undercourt reckoned time differently than the surface did — their calendar ran backward, or sideways, or according to principles I'd need a month and a captive scholar to untangle — but the attestation gave a conversion, as careful documents do, a surface-date equivalent inscribed by whatever clerk had drawn up the instrument.

The contract was sealed, by its own attestation, one hundred and forty-two years ago. Three generations. Vess's grandmother's grandmother signing away a stranger's soul to buy a warehouse and a license to trade in bottled luck. That was the story. That was the debt. One hundred and forty-two years of interest accruing in the dark.

Now. Here is where being Maren Crane's apprentice stops being a wound and becomes an armory.

Maren dated everything. Obsessively. She said a document without dates was a door without hinges — technically functional, practically useless. And she taught me that dates don't just mark time; they *cross-reference*. No document exists alone. Every date on a page touches other pages, other records, other events, and if a forger is careless about the web — if they get one thread wrong — the whole net shows the hole.

So: what else happened one hundred and forty-two years ago? What records touch this contract?

The Gilded Veil keeps everything. Eleven years in these archives had taught me that Vess's predecessors were compulsive archivists, hoarding receipts the way dragons hoard coin. I went down to the sealed archive — yes, sealed, yes, against orders, yes, I picked the lock with a hairpin and a prayer to no one in particular, and if Vess wants to dock my wages she knows where my ledger is — and I pulled the founding records. The charter. The first inventory. The original lease on the building. The correspondence between the first guild master and the licensing office of the Crown Wardens.

And I found the thread. Of course I found the thread. There is always a thread; that is the faith I keep instead of religion.

The guild's charter — the document establishing the Gilded Veil as a licensed trade — bore a date. The charter was granted *after* the contract's seal date. Fine. Sensible. You secure your collateral, then you incorporate. The sequence was coherent.

Except.

The charter's preamble — the recital clause, the bit where a legal document tells the story of why it exists — said otherwise. Preambles are where forgers slip, because preambles are prose and prose is hard to fake consistently. This one recited the guild's founding thus: *the undersigned masters, having secured by prior instrument the surety requisite to underwrite the venture herein described...*

Prior instrument. The charter claimed the contract came first. The contract's attestation claimed the charter's date came second. Both could be true. Both were, on their faces, perfectly consistent.

But the licensing correspondence wasn't. Because the Crown Wardens' office, in its infinite bureaucratic wisdom, had sent a query letter — I found it, third folder, water-stained, filed under the wrong year by some clerk dead two centuries, bless him forever — asking the fledgling guild to produce *evidence of its surety* before the charter could be granted. And the guild's reply, preserved in the same folder, was dated four days *before* the contract's seal date, and in it the first guild master wrote, in ink I now examined with a needle and a breath and a tilted candle:

*Be advised the surety instrument is executed and in hand.*

Four days before the contract was supposedly signed.

The guild master had lied in an official letter — or the contract's date was wrong. And official letters of that era were archived by the Wardens' own clerks, cross-filed, duplicated, beyond the reach of any single forger's pen. You can forge a contract. You cannot forge, retroactively and invisibly, an entire independent archive's agreement with your lie.

The contract claimed to be one hundred and forty-two years old. The paper was. The seal was. Isandro's signature was. But Maren Crane's signature — and, I now understood, the deferred-payment clause bound to it, the clause that made the debt come due tonight, the clause that had emptied the sky — postdated the seal. By years, possibly. By decades. Long enough for the vellum to stop drinking ink. Long enough for someone to take an old, dormant, harmless instrument of surety and turn it into a soul-debt with a due date.

Someone had edited history. Someone had taken a binding-but-benign founding document and grafted onto it a harvest clause, and signed the graft with a hedge-witch's name, and waited three generations for the sky to go dark.

* * *

I want to describe what that clarity felt like, and I find I can only describe it as arithmetic.

There is a moment in every reconciliation when the columns finally agree — when the mystery difference resolves, when the stray copper turns up behind the drawer's runner, when the whole system snaps into balance with an almost audible click. People think accountants love money. We don't. We love that click. Money is just the medium. What we love is the moment the world admits it was knowable all along, that the wrongness had a shape and a size and a location, that confusion was never the truth of things but only the interval before understanding.

I sat in the dark archive with a stolen candle and a dead man's misfiled letter, and I heard the click.

The founding debt was a forgery. Not metaphorically, not morally — *technically*. The claim that owned Isandro, the claim that had switched off the stars, the claim Vess was prepared to pay with a substitute soul rather than contest — it was fraudulent on its face. Executed after attestation. Countersigned out of sequence. Corroborated by no independent record. Built, like every fraud I'd ever catalogued, on the confidence that no one would check the margins.

Three generations of the Gilded Veil had trembled before a piece of bad bookkeeping.

And underneath the triumph — and it was triumph, bright and vicious and entirely new to me — underneath it, a colder thought settled, the way sediment settles in still water. Forgers don't forge for free. A forgery is an investment; it expects a return. Which meant someone had wanted this debt to come due. Someone had engineered a night without stars and a guild in terror and a boy who drew maps he didn't understand toward a door he didn't remember. Maren Crane's name sat on that graft like a fly in amber — either she had done the forging, or someone had used her, or someone had used her *signature*, and I knew enough of the Undercourt's habits by now to suspect that a name, freely or foully obtained, is a tool that outlives its owner.

That was a question for later. Questions for later are a luxury I have learned to seize without apology.

What mattered now, what mattered at four in the morning with the sky still black and the moon still bleeding down to nothing, was this: we did not owe them. The debt was void. Fraud unravels everything it touches — Maren taught me that too, though she phrased it as "a rotten beam brings down the house" — and a contract procured by forgery is not a contract at all. It is a costume worn by extortion. And extortion, even by kings, even by kings beneath mountains, does not survive contact with evidence.

I gathered the contract, the charter, the query letter, the reply. Four documents. Four threads of one net. I wrapped them in oilcloth against damp and candle-spit, and I held them against my chest, and I noticed my hands were shaking, and I let them, because shaking hands can still carry a ledger straight.

Then I went to find Vess, and Bram, and Isandro — poor, fading Isandro, who was losing his mother's face even now while I held the proof that no one had ever rightfully owned it — and I told them the only sentence that has ever mattered to me in eleven years of impossible inventory:

"It's forged. I can prove it. And in the morning, we're taking the books to court."

Below us, beneath the mountain, something stirred — I felt it in the floorboards, faint as a heartbeat through a wall. As if the King Who Collects had felt the click too, and knew his ledger was about to be audited.
