## 16. Evidence Before a Hungry Court

I had presented ledgers to angry men before. The Gilded Veil's quarterly audits drew creditors like rot draws flies, and I'd stood in front of more than one of them with my figures held out like a shield, daring them to find an error. Angry men want you to flinch. Numbers don't flinch. That was always my advantage.

The Undercourt was not going to be an audit.

We were given no warning of when we'd be called. The court simply *happened* around us — one moment Isandro and I were standing in the antechamber of bone-white stone, watching a courtier with a half-finished face drift past like smoke deciding to be a person, and the next there were thrones where there hadn't been any, and galleries where there hadn't been any, and a floor that had rearranged itself into a long aisle pointing at us like an accusation.

"They do this," Isandro murmured. His voice was steadier than his hands. Since the memories had started flooding back — since Maren Crane's name had surfaced beneath his own on that cursed contract — he'd been cycling through grief and rage in waves, and right now he was riding the rage, which I preferred. Rage could carry a person. Grief made them sit down.

"Do what?"

"Make the room before they need it. The whole court is... anticipatory. It shapes itself toward whatever is about to matter." He glanced at me. "It's already pointed at you."

"Wonderful. I've never been pointed at by architecture before."

"You have. You just called it a deadline."

* * *

They brought us forward down the aisle, and I did what I always do in hostile territory: I counted.

Four hundred and eleven steps of gallery seating, roughly two-thirds occupied. One hundred and six courtiers visible, their faces varying in completion from nearly-finished — eyes, mouth, the suggestion of a history — to barely begun, smooth ovals with a single line drawn where a nose might go. The unfinished ones sat closer to the throne. I filed that away. Proximity to power and incompleteness of self seemed correlated here, which told me something about how this place worked, and none of it was comforting.

The Underking sat at the top of seven steps on a chair that was less a throne than a debt made furniture — black wood grown through with veins of pale stone, the way a tree grows through a fence. He wore no crown. He didn't need one. The room crowned him continuously, the way your tongue crowns a missing tooth.

He looked at me the way Vess looks at a ledger she suspects has been cooked.

"Ada Crane," he said, and my name in his mouth felt like a hand going through my pockets.

"That's me," I said. "Though I notice you didn't ask a question."

A ripple went through the galleries. Somewhere above, a courtier with only a mouth drawn so far laughed — a small, delighted sound, like ice cracking pleasantly.

"The room enforces truth," the Underking said. "You know this."

"I know it's been mentioned. Repeatedly. By everyone. Which makes me think someone's worried I might lie." I let that sit. "I'm not going to lie. Lying is for people whose case can't survive scrutiny. Mine can. I've checked it twice."

"And what is your case, keeper of accounts?"

I'd rehearsed this. In the antechamber, in the ferryman's boat while Ada-the-false-memory paid our toll, in every quiet moment since I'd found the flaw in the margins. But rehearsal is one thing and performance another, and the audience here ate hesitation, I could feel it — the whole room leaning in like diners smelling the first course.

So I didn't hesitate. I turned to Isandro. "May I borrow your hands? Mine will be busy."

He passed me the contract tube without a word. We'd agreed on this part too: I would handle the document. His fingers still shook when he touched it, and shaking hands undermine an argument about precision.

I unrolled the founding contract on the air itself — because that's how documents behaved down here; you held them up and the dark behind them became a page — and the court stirred. Even from the lowest gallery I could see faces sharpening, lines being added to chins and cheekbones as attention gave the courtiers more of themselves. Attention was food here. I resolved to be extremely interesting.

"This," I said, "is the founding contract of the Gilded Veil. Three generations old. It sells one soul to this court, payment deferred until — and I quote the clause exactly — 'the night the sky forgets us.' Two nights ago, the stars went out. The condition was met. Your claim came due. All of that, I concede. I'm not here to dispute that the contract says what it says."

"Then what are you here to dispute?" the Underking asked, and it wasn't quite a question. It was a door being opened so the guest could walk into the trap.

"I'm here to dispute whether the contract *is* what it says." I lifted the document higher. "Every contract has three layers. What it promises. Who signed it. And when. Merchants audit the first layer because it's where the money is. I audit all three, because I'm the kind of tedious person who reads margins."

"Margins," said a voice from the third gallery — a courtier with a beautifully finished face, a woman who looked like she'd been drawn by someone who loved her. "She's going to talk about margins."

"I am," I said, "and some of you are going to wish I hadn't."

* * *

Here is what I found, the night after the bargaining, sitting cross-legged on the floor of our borrowed cell with the contract spread across my knees and Isandro asleep — actually asleep, the first real sleep he'd managed since remembering — against the wall beside me.

The Undercourt seals its contracts with starlight. Of course it does. It *keeps* the stars; it would seal its papers with them the way a miser seals letters with wax from his own hoard. Every authentic document produced in this court carries a seal pressed in frozen light, and frozen light decays — not evenly, but measurably. A three-generation-old seal has a specific depth of dimming. I'd spent two days with the guild's inventory of bottled starlight — yes, we traded in it, yes, Vess had let me take samples, yes, that had required a conversation — and I now knew exactly how much a star dims per decade in captivity.

The seal on the founding contract was too bright.

Not by much. By perhaps forty years' worth of decay. If the contract had been sealed when it claimed — three generations ago, before the guild existed, before Isandro was even a possibility — the seal should have faded to a certain grey. Instead it glowed with the freshness of a document pressed within living memory.

And that mattered because of the second signature. Maren Crane's.

Maren Crane was my mistress. My teacher. The woman who raised me on nettle soup and arithmetic and the doctrine that everything has a price and names have the best ones. She was also, according to the recovered memory Isandro had finally clawed back, the woman who had bought a child's name for the price of a mother's life — and then resold it, layered into a forged founding contract, making an innocent boy into the hidden collateral of an entire guild.

Maren Crane died four years ago. I know because I buried her ledger under her floorboards myself, the night I ran.

A dead woman cannot sign a contract three generations old. Therefore either the dead can sign contracts in the Undercourt — in which case I had several other complaints to file — or the contract was written recently and backdated, and the seal, pressed fresh, betrayed the fraud.

That was the argument. Simple. Terrible. Exact.

* * *

Now I stood in front of a hungry court and laid it out.

"The seal on this document," I said, "was pressed no more than forty years ago. I can prove it. I have measured the decay rate of captive starlight across sixty-two samples spanning a century of the Gilded Veil's inventory. I have the figures. I will recite them if anyone doubts me, and I warn you—" I let myself smile, thin and unpleasant, "—I have a very good memory for numbers and very little else to offer this court, so I *will* recite them."

Silence. The good kind. The kind where the room stops eating and starts listening.

"The contract claims to predate the guild by two generations. Its seal says otherwise. And here is the consequence—" I turned the document so the signatures faced the galleries. "This name. Isandro's name, in a hand too old for the child who owned it. And beneath it, this one: Maren Crane. A woman born eighty-one years ago. A woman who could not have signed a document sealed a century before her birth. Unless—" I raised my voice slightly, letting it carry up into the galleries, "—unless this court routinely accepts signatures from people who do not yet exist. In which case I'd like to sell you some futures. I hear you pay well."

The laughter that went up wasn't entirely on my side, but some of it was, and laughter in a courtroom is a currency too, and I banked every coin of it.

A courtier in the second gallery rose — the beautiful finished woman again — and her voice rang off the bone-white walls. "The seal-decay method. Is it known to this court?"

"It's known to me," I said. "Invented last week, by candlelight, by a bookkeeper with nothing better to do than save her friend. But the mathematics underneath it are not new. Your own archivists keep starlight in bonds — I saw the vaults. Anyone here who works with captive light knows it dims. Check my figures yourselves. I *want* you to check them. A forgery claim that survives checking is worth ten that don't."

And they did check. That was the terrible, wonderful thing about this room: it enforced truth, but it also enforced *verification*. The Underking spoke a word that wasn't a word, and three courtiers detached from the galleries and drifted down to examine the contract, their unfinished faces tilting over the seal like herons over water. One of them ran a finger along the sigil and the finger came away faintly luminous, and whatever the light told her made her draw a single new line on her face — an eyebrow, arched.

"The seal is young," she announced. "Younger than the document claims. The discrepancy is consistent with the mortal's figures."

Another courtier, this one barely begun — a smooth oval with two dots for eyes — spoke in a voice like wind through a keyhole. "I was sold under a seal. I remember the pressing. It was warm." A pause. "Mine was warm too."

The galleries moved. Not much. A shifting, the way a crowd shifts when a thought passes through it that no one wants to say aloud. But I heard it repeated, passed hand to hand like contraband: *warm. mine was warm. mine was warm.*

How many souls in those galleries had been purchased with paperwork that wouldn't survive an audit? How many of those half-finished faces were unfinished not by nature but by *fraud* — sold under contracts that never truly bound them, their names caught in escrow between a liar's promise and a king's collection?

I hadn't planned on allies. I'd planned on surviving. But standing there, watching a court full of the possibly-swindled begin to do arithmetic of their own, I understood something Vess had tried to teach me years ago and I'd been too proud to learn: the most dangerous thing you can hand a crowd isn't a weapon. It's a number they can check themselves.

* * *

Isandro stepped forward then. We hadn't scripted this, but I'd hoped for it, and hope is just planning with worse odds.

"I am the soul named in that contract," he said. His voice carried better than I expected; rage does that. "I was a child. I traded my name to save my mother's life, which I do not regret and will not apologize for. What I regret—" his jaw tightened, "—is that the woman who took it didn't stop at taking. She resold it. She built a forgery around it. She made me the collateral of a guild I served faithfully for nine years, and neither I nor anyone in that guild knew. The debt you claim is real in its cruelty and false in its origin. The contract is forged. Void it."

The Underking had watched all of this in silence. Now he leaned forward on his throne-of-debt, and the room leaned with him, four hundred seats creaking like a ship.

"You present a flaw," he said. "A genuine one. The seal betrays the paper. The second signature betrays the first. The court's own examiners confirm it." He paused, and the pause had weight, the way a held breath has weight. "You have argued well, Ada Crane. Better than most who stand where you stand, and most who stand there are already mine."

My heart was doing something unprofessional. I kept my face still. Faces are just ledgers for other people to read; keep the entries clean.

"So I ask you," the Underking said, and his voice dropped into a register I felt in my teeth, "one final question. Answer it precisely. Precision is your religion, I'm told. So answer as a priest of it."

Above us, in the dark beyond the galleries, something vast shifted — and I realized with a lurch that the missing stars weren't merely stored here. They were *watching*. Hundreds of imprisoned lights turning in their cages like eyes.

"The contract is forged," the Underking said. "Conceded, before this court, before the sky itself. But tell me, keeper of accounts — the guild that was built upon this forgery. The luck it bottled. The years it sold. The fortunes it made on paper that was never true." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man opening a ledger to a page he's been waiting decades to reach. "Who, precisely, accepted those benefits?"

The room went very quiet.

And every instinct I had — the same instinct that once told me Maren's kindness had a price tag hidden somewhere — began, quietly, to scream.

"*Answer,*" said the King, "the question."
