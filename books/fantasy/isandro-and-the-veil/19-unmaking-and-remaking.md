## 19. Unmaking and Remaking

### Ada

There is a particular silence that falls over a room when a contract dies. I have watched ledgers close a hundred times — a debt settled, a line ruled through, a small ceremonial sigh from whoever held the pen. It never sounds like this. This is the silence of a held breath in a cathedral made of bone, and I am the one holding it.

"Say it again," the Underking says. He does not command. He never commands; he only asks, and the asking is worse. "All of it. Precisely."

He knows I will say it precisely. Precision is the only thing I have ever owned outright.

"My name is Ada Crane." The words come out of me like thread pulled from a hem, and I feel each one catch. "Given to me by my mother, who took it from no one, who owed it to no one, who was not bought or sold or traded when she bore me. I give it freely. No coin, no consideration, no condition. I give it to dissolve the contract of the Gilded Veil, founding debt and all counterclaims, in full."

The court stirs. The courtiers with their unfinished faces lean in like a field of grass bending toward weather, and I understand, suddenly and horribly, that they are hungry. Not for me. For the *shape* of what I'm doing. Most of them were sold here, one way or another, and none of them ever got to give anything freely. I am, as far as this room knows, the first.

The contract unrolls between us on the black stone — the founding debt, three generations of deferred payment, Isandro's name in a child's careful hand, Maren Crane's signature beneath it like a stain that never dried. The Underking's finger rests at the margin where I found the flaw, the seal date that came before the signature, the fraud sitting in plain sight for a hundred years while an entire guild built its vaults on top of it.

"You understand," he says, "what you are offering. A name is not a word. It is the sum of every account ever opened against you. Every debt. Every credit. Every hand that ever wrote it down." His voice is a question even when the words aren't, and I hate how much I want to answer it. "You will not lose a word, Ada Crane. You will lose the *ledger*."

"I know what a ledger is," I say. "I've kept one since I was nine years old."

Isandro makes a sound beside me. Not a word. He has been trying to speak since the forgery was proven, since his memories came back in a flood that dropped him to his knees on the bone-white floor, and what comes out of him is mostly my name, over and over, as if he's checking that it still works. *Ada. Ada.* Like a man tapping a wall to find where it's hollow.

"Don't," I tell him, without turning around. If I look at him now, I will stop. I know this about myself the way I know the guild's accounts — completely, and against my will. "Don't talk me out of it. You had your turn. You were nine years old and your mother was dying and you didn't even know what you were selling. I'm twenty-six, I know exactly what I'm selling, and I'm selling it *on purpose*. That's the whole difference. That's the entire argument."

"Ada—"

"You'll remember me," I say. "That's the trade. I get to stop being defined, and you get to remember being defined. It balances." My voice does something I don't authorize. It cracks, just slightly, down the middle, like a seal date that doesn't match a signature. "It balances, Isandro. Check my work."

The Underking lifts the contract. The parchment is older than the guild, older than the mountain's patience, and when he holds it up to the imprisoned starlight — the stolen stars, caged in their lanterns of bone, burning in the walls like flies in amber — the whole document begins to glow.

"Freely given," he says. "Freely accepted. The old law turns over in its sleep." And then, and this is the part I will try to describe later and fail, he sounds — almost — *relieved*. "Let it burn."

The contract catches fire from the inside out.

* * *

I expected pain. Everyone expects pain; it's the default currency of stories, and I have read enough of Isandro's maps to know how stories go. But it isn't pain. It's *peeling*.

It starts at my hands. I watch the name leave me the way you watch frost leave a window — not vanishing but *relocating*, moving somewhere I can't follow. My name was the first thing I ever owned. Maren Crane wanted it so badly she was willing to sell a child to get it, and I ran from her at fifteen with a stolen ledger and a talent for numbers, and I built a life in the space between my name and everyone who might want it. *Ada Crane* was a locked room I lived in for eleven years. Now I stand in the doorway and watch the room empty itself: the girl who counted fence posts to fall asleep, the apprentice who hid her true name in a false one, the woman who balanced impossible books for a guild of impossible things — all of it peeling away like skin from a sunburn, painless and intimate and absolutely irreversible.

I grab for it. Of course I grab for it. I am Ada Crane and I have never let go of anything in my life, not the ledger, not the grudge, not the locked room.

But here is what nobody tells you about a freely given thing: it doesn't fight you. It just *goes*, the way a bird goes when you finally open your hand, and the going feels less like loss than like setting down a weight you'd forgotten you were carrying. Eleven years of guarding. Eleven years of flinching every time someone said my name like they might be quoting a price. It peels away and underneath it there is —

Underneath it there is nothing. And the nothing is not empty. The nothing is *blank*, the way a fresh ledger page is blank, and I have spent my whole life loving blank pages.

The last thing to go is small and stupid and I would have chosen it last anyway: my mother's voice, saying it. Just the name, in her voice, one time. It goes up out of me like a spark off a fire, and I think — I genuinely think, in the last moment I am anyone — *she'd have understood. She gave it to me free. I'm only giving it on.*

The contract is ash. The ash is rising. And the stars —

The stars are screaming.

### Isandro

I remember everything.

That is the first thing. Before the light, before the burning, before Ada — I remember everything, all at once, and it is like being born drowning.

My mother's face. There it is, whole, the mole below her left eye, the way she bit thread instead of cutting it. The winter town, the smoke, the frozen well with its rope of iron. The fever. The stranger at the door with her satchel of hedge-cures and her eyes like an auditor's, and me, nine years old, standing in the doorway of my own life while she explained the trade in words a child could survive hearing: *just a name, love, names grow back.*

They don't grow back. That's the lie that killed me slowly for twenty years while I drew maps of places that didn't exist yet, because I could not draw the one place I came from. I built my whole self inside the debt that owned me. I inked coastlines for a guild that was collateral on my soul, and I never knew, and the not-knowing was the cruelest mercy anyone ever did me.

Now I know. Now I kneel on the floor of the Undercourt with twenty years of my own life pouring back into me like water into a cracked vessel, and it hurts, it hurts *wonderfully*, the way a set bone hurts, the way truth hurts when it arrives all at once instead of in the installments a person can afford.

And Ada is burning.

I see it happen and I cannot stop it and I knew I couldn't stop it, that was the bargain, that was her arithmetic, and knowing does nothing, knowing has never once done anything. Her name comes out of her the way mine came back into me, in reverse, a tide going out, and she stands very straight while it happens because of course she does, she would balance the books of her own unmaking and rule a neat line under the total.

"Ada," I say. It is all I have. It is the only word left in me that isn't memory. "Ada, Ada—"

She said *don't*. She said it the way she says everything, precisely, like a figure entered twice in two columns so it can't be lost. But she is peeling, she is coming apart into light, and the last thing — I see it, I am the only one who sees it — the last thing that leaves her is small and shining and it hangs in the air above her hands for one heartbeat like a key looking for its lock.

I take it.

I don't decide to. There is no decision. There is only the oldest law in any court, older than the Underking's questions, older than debt itself: *what is given in love may be carried in love.* She gave her name freely. Nobody said it had to fall on the floor.

I catch her name the way you catch a falling star, which is to say: it burns going down, and I let it, and I hold on.

And then the cages break.

* * *

### Ada

I am no one. It is the strangest thing I have ever been.

I am no one, and I am standing, and the room is full of light that has nowhere left to be imprisoned. The stars — the real ones, the stolen ones, three generations of night sky locked in bone lanterns while the world above went dark — are shattering their cages. I hear it: a sound like tearing silk, the same sound the border wards made when they fell, and I understand that everything in this mountain that was ever held by force is letting go at once. The Undercourt is not being destroyed. It is being *released*, and the courtiers with their unfinished faces are lifting their sketch-hands to the light, and some of them are weeping ink.

The Underking watches his stolen sky leave him. He does not reach for it. I look at him — at the hole where a heart was, at the question that wears a crown — and I think: *he couldn't give anything freely. That was all. That was the whole sickness. He could only ever take, because no one ever gave him anything without a price, and so he became a price.*

The stars go up. They go up through the mountain, through the stone, through everything, streaking for the surface and the night that has been empty without them, and the ceiling of the world opens to let them home. I feel them pass through me. They pass through me the way my name passed out of me — painless, total, relocating somewhere I can't follow — and for one moment I am a blank page with starlight written across it, and the writing isn't words. It's just light. It's just *yes*.

Then it's done, and the mountain is only a mountain, and the silence that falls is not the silence of a held breath.

It's the silence after an account closes. A ruled line. A small ceremonial sigh.

Isandro's hand closes around mine. His hand is shaking, and it's full of something I can't see, and he is saying a word to me over and over, softly, like a man counting the only coin he has left. I can't make out the word. It isn't in any language I know, and I know — I *knew* — eleven.

"Can you walk?" he asks.

"I don't know who you're asking," I say, and my voice is mine, and it isn't, and it's the only voice I'll ever have now, and I find — this is the part I will never tell anyone, not Vess, not Bram, not the new guild we'll build on honest paper — *I find I like it.* "But I can climb. Take me toward the dawn. I want to see what I bought."

He laughs. It comes out of him broken and whole at once, twenty years of grief and one night of getting all of it back, and he pulls me up, and we start up the stair that isn't there anymore — the stair that is there now, that will be there forever, because the mountain has nothing left to hide.

Overhead, through the opening stone, the first star finds its place in the sky and lights.

Then a second. Then a third.

Isandro watches them the way I once watched ledgers — like a man confirming that the numbers finally, finally balance — and his lips are moving. Counting. Naming them, one by one, in an order that means something only to him.

He is not counting stars.
