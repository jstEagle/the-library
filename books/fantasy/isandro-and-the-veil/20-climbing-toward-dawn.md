## 20. Climbing Toward Dawn

The first thing I understood about the stair out of the Undercourt was that it had opinions.

It had carried us down shaped by Isandro's memories — a passage that remembered his mother's kitchen when he could not — and now, with those memories returned to him whole and my name burned off the world like frost off a window, the stair seemed unsure whose story it was telling. The walls flickered. One moment they were bone-white court stone; the next, rough-hewn mountain rock sweating cold water; the next, something I had no word for, a surface like the inside of an eyelid, warm and dark and faintly pulsing.

"Keep walking," Isandro said behind me. His voice was rough. He'd been crying, or the equivalent of crying for someone who'd just gotten back three generations of stolen self, which I suspect involves more of the throat than the eyes. "Don't give it anything to work with."

"I'm not *giving* it anything," I said. "I don't have anything left to give. I checked." I patted my pockets as proof. Ledger pencil, half a biscuit wrapped in wax paper, a stub of chalk. The complete inventory of Ada Crane, minus one name. "I'm the emptiest woman in two kingdoms. It should love me."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh. That was the thing I kept noticing, over and over, the way you keep touching a bruise: he was still in there. Whatever the contract had taken from him over the years — his winter, his mother's face, the town with the crooked bell tower — it had all come flooding back when the parchment burned, and what remained underneath, at the bottom of the man, was still him. Dry humor. Steady hands. The particular way he said my—

Well. Not that. He couldn't say that anymore either, because there wasn't a that to say.

"You're doing the arithmetic face," he said.

"I'm always doing the arithmetic face."

"You're doing the *bad* arithmetic face. The one where the numbers won't balance and you're deciding whether to burn the ledger or yourself."

I stopped on the stair. Above us, through a crack in whatever the ceiling currently was, I could see darkness — real darkness, the kind the sky makes when it's empty. When we'd descended, the sky had been empty too. Every star in the world sat caged down here in the Undercourt, burning in their bone-white prisons while the land above forgot what light at night even looked like.

But that had been before the contract burned. Before the cages shattered. Before the stars went streaking upward through the mountain's roots like sparks off a grindstone.

"Isandro," I said. "Look up."

* * *

The crack widened as we climbed, and through it we watched them come home.

One star first — low on the horizon, so faint I thought at first it was a trick of my eyes, some afterimage of everything we'd lost. Then it steadied. Then it *burned*, white and hard and unmistakable, and somewhere below us I swear I heard the mountain sigh.

Then another. Then three at once, scattered across the black like coins dropped on a table.

"It's working," Isandro whispered. "Ada, it's actually—"

"Don't jinx it. I've read enough contracts to know better than to celebrate before the ink dries."

"The ink burned."

"All the more reason. Burned ink is exactly when things go wrong." But I was smiling. I could feel it happening to my face without my permission, and I let it, because there was no one down here to see me be soft except him, and he'd already seen everything else.

We climbed. The stars came back. That was the whole shape of the night, really, though neither of us knew yet how long the night would last — a stair going up, and light returning to the sky one small victory at a time, each one arriving with no fanfare, no trumpet, just a point of fire deciding to exist again. I counted them. Of course I counted them. It's what I do. By the time we reached the first landing I was at forty-one, and I had assigned each one a number in my head the way I used to assign entries in the guild ledger, and I thought: *this is the only inventory I have left.* Bottled memories, borrowed luck, years of life — I'd catalogued all of it, priced all of it, and none of it had saved anyone by itself. But forty-one stars, relit by a burned forgery and a spent name? That balanced. That balanced clean.

Except.

Except there was a hole in the middle of my counting, the way there's a hole in a mouth where a tooth was. My tongue kept finding it. Every time I reached for myself — for the thing under my skin that made me *me*, the word Maren Crane had tried to sell when I was nineteen and had guarded ever since like a coin sewn into a hem — every time I reached for it, I found smooth nothing. Not pain. Pain would have been fine. Pain has edges. This was the absence of edges, a question I couldn't formulate about an answer I couldn't remember.

I knew I had lost something. I knew it mattered. I knew it the way you know a room has been robbed: not because you can say what's missing, but because the whole space feels wrong, rearranged, *handled*.

"Tell me," I said, on the second landing. We'd stopped to drink from a seam of meltwater running down the wall, and the water tasted like cold iron and, faintly, like morning. "Whatever you're not saying. Tell me."

Isandro crouched by the water, cupping it in his hands, and didn't drink. In the starlight leaking through the crack above — sixty-three stars now, I'd counted — his face looked older and younger at once, the way faces do when they've been remade.

"You know what you gave," he said carefully.

"I know *that* I gave. I don't know *what*. There's a difference, and the difference is eating me alive, so please, before I start making jokes about it, which is never a good sign — tell me what my name was."

"I can't."

The words landed like a dropped weight. I actually swayed on the stair.

"You *can't*? You stood in front of a king made of questions and lied to his entire court with a straight face, and now you tell me you can't—"

"Because it isn't mine to give back." He stood, water dripping from his fingers, and took both my hands in his, and his hands were shaking, and I realized with a lurch that this cost him too — that carrying my name was already a labor, already a debt he'd chosen, the first debt either of us had ever chosen freely. "You gave it *freely*, Ada. That was the whole point. A coerced name can be stolen back, traded, forged, resold — yours was given, and a gift belongs to whoever holds it. Right now that's me. And I am not giving it away, and I am not spending it, and I am not telling it to you on a mountainside at four in the morning like a drunk man confessing, because if I speak it aloud and the wrong thing hears—"

"What wrong thing? The Underking conceded. The contract's ash. The court is—" I gestured vaguely downward, at several miles of rock. "—having a very bad century."

"Vess says the old laws survive their kings. Vess says a lot of things." His grip tightened. "When we're home. When the wards are rebuilt and the ledgers are closed and there are wards around the *wards*. Then I'll find a way to give it back to you properly, if you want it. Until then I carry it. That was the vow. You don't get to talk me out of a vow. You were there. You cried."

"I did not cry."

"You absolutely cried. There was a moment with the ferryman's toll—"

"That was *water*."

"It was not water, it was—"

"*Water,* Isandro."

He grinned, and it cracked something open in my chest, this grin I'd seen across ledger desks and map tables for three years, this grin that had nearly been unmade forever, and I laughed despite everything, and the laugh echoed up the stair and came back changed, softer, like the mountain was laughing too.

* * *

We walked. The stars multiplied.

Somewhere past the hundred mark I started naming them, because counting wasn't enough anymore — counting felt too much like bookkeeping, and bookkeeping had gotten us into this, or rather out of it, but either way I wanted to do something with the sky that wasn't accounting. So I named them badly. That one was *Bram's Star*, because it hung alone and stubborn near the horizon and probably owed money to the others. That cluster was *the Council*, bickering eternally. The bright one rising over the eastern ridge I named *Maren's Ruin*, and spat after it, and Isandro didn't ask why, because he'd recovered the memory in chapter fourteen of his own life, so to speak — the memory of being nine years old and trading his name to a hedge-witch to save his dying mother, and the hedge-witch folding that name into her schemes like a counterfeit coin passed hand to hand until it landed, three generations deep, at the bottom of my guild's founding contract.

"She'll still be out there," I said quietly. "Maren. The forgery voided the debt, but it doesn't void *her*. She sold you twice. Once when you were a child, once to the Veil."

"I know."

"And you're calm about this?"

"I'm furious about it." He said it mildly, which from Isandro was how you knew it was true. "But she's an old woman with bad debts and worse magic, and I've spent the last day watching a king unmake people for owing less than she does. Let her have her winter. I got mine back." He glanced at me sideways. "Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"There's a gap. Small. Around the edges of things." He frowned up at the climbing dark. "I remember my mother's face now. I remember the town, the bell tower, the snow. I remember selling my name — I remember the taste of the tea in Maren's kitchen, which apparently was chamomile cut with something bitter, and I remember thinking, *nine years old and this is the price of Mama breathing another spring.* What I don't remember—" he hesitated, and I watched him grope for it, watch a man who'd just been handed back his entire life find one shelf inexplicably empty, "—is what I called myself. After. Before the Veil. Between losing the name and finding the guild. There were years, Ada. I think there were years where I was nobody, walking. And those years are still gone."

The wind moved through the crack above us. Somewhere far off, another star lit — one hundred and twelve, not that I was counting, except that I was always counting.

"Maybe," I said slowly, "that's not damage. Maybe that's just... blank page. Maybe some parts of a person aren't owed back. Maybe they're supposed to be written new."

He looked at me for a long moment. "That," he said, "is either the wisest thing you've ever said or the saddest."

"With me it's usually both."

* * *

Near dawn — or what I judged to be dawn, since my internal clock was the only clock left in a world that had spent a night without stars — the stair ended.

Not dramatically. Stairs in stories end in doors and trumpets and thrones. Ours ended in a hillside. We simply ran out of underground, pushed through a curtain of hanging root and frost-killed bracken, and stepped out onto the southern slope of the mountain with the whole sky overhead doing its slow, staggering work of repair.

Two hundred and six stars. I'd stopped pretending I wasn't counting.

Below us, the valley. The borderlands, gray in the pre-dawn, the collapsed wards visible as a shimmer of dead air along the ridge lines, like heat-haze in winter. And beyond them, hours away, the rooftops of the guild quarter, and the Gilded Veil's crooked chimney smoking its crooked smoke, and — my chest did something complicated — lights. Candles in windows. Someone had lit candles in every window of the guildhouse, dozens of them, a constellation of our own making pressed against the dark, waiting for the sky to answer.

"They waited up," I said.

"Vess waited up. Bram waited up and will deny it. The apprentices waited up and will make it a festival within the week, mark me. There'll be a song. It'll be terrible. It'll rhyme 'ledger' with 'pledger.'"

"We should go down."

"In a moment." He turned to face me fully, and the first true light of dawn caught him — caught us both — and I saw that he'd been holding something back all night, some final thing, and now the sky was repaired enough and the distance short enough and the hour kind enough that he could finally spend it.

"Before we walk into whatever comes next," he said, "I want you to hear this while it's just us and the mountain. Because down there it'll be councils and votes and Vess re-founding the world, and there won't be a quiet minute for weeks. So." He breathed. "I remember everything now. All of it. The winter, my mother, the tea, the years of walking as nobody. And the last three years — the map table, your terrible handwriting in the margins, the time you caught me drawing a city that didn't exist yet and instead of laughing you asked me what its exports were. I remember *you*, Ada. Every day of you. And I know what you paid, even if you don't. I watched it happen. I watched you stand in front of that hungry court and peel yourself open like it was nothing, like you were just settling an account, and it was the bravest thing I have ever seen and I will see it every time I close my eyes for the rest of my life."

"Isandro—"

"So here is my vow, and I mean it the way the old laws mean things, the binding way, the way that survives kings." He took my hands again. His had stopped shaking. "Until you can hold your name again — and you will, I'll spend the rest of my life making sure of it — I will carry it for you. I will keep it safe the way you kept everyone else's accounts safe. I will not spend it, sell it, trade it, or whisper it to rivers. And every morning I will look at you and know exactly who you are, even on the days you don't. Especially then. You are not a hole, Ada. You're a page waiting for ink. And I—" his voice went, briefly, to pieces, and he rebuilt it, "—I intend to be here for every line."

Above us, the last stars of the night were fading into the blue — not dying, just yielding, the way stars do, handing the sky over to the sun. Two hundred and thirty-one, all told. I knew because I'd counted, and I would always count, because counting was the shape of my soul whether or not my soul had a name attached.

"I don't remember what I lost," I told him. "I need you to understand that. It's not hiding in me somewhere. It's gone gone. And maybe that should terrify me, and maybe it will later, when I'm not standing on a mountain having just survived a king. But right now all I can feel is that it *mattered*. Whatever it was. It mattered enough to save you with, and that means it was the best thing I ever owned, and I'd do it again."

"I know," he said. "That's the worst part. You'd do it again in a heartbeat, and I'd have to let you."

"Then let's make sure nobody ever has to." I nodded down the slope, toward the candlelit windows, toward the guild, toward the wreckage and the rebuilding and the ledgers that needed rewriting from page one. "Come on, cartographer. Draw us a road home."

He smiled — that grin, my favorite entry in any inventory — and pulled his chalk from his pocket, because of course he had chalk, because some things survive even the Undercourt. And on the flat stone beside the trail, in quick sure strokes, he began to draw: the slope, the valley, the river, the road, the crooked chimney with its crooked smoke. A map of the way home. And beside the little guildhouse, where a signature would go, he drew two small marks side by side.

"One for you," he said, "and one for me. We'll fill in the names later."

Far below, a door opened in the guildhouse, and a small figure came running up the road — Bram, unmistakable even at a mile, moving with the urgent lope of a man bringing news he'd rather have died than sit on. Behind him, faint on the wind, I could have sworn I heard Vess shouting something about *ward-stones* and *immediately*.

Isandro pocketed the chalk. "Ready?"

"No," I said honestly. "But I've balanced worse books."

And we started down the mountain together, into the dawn, toward whatever we would write next.
