## 21. New Ledgers, Old Hands

The Gilded Veil smelled of smoke and old paper for three days after we came back up the mountain.

Mostly smoke. The guild's cellars had caught fire when the wards collapsed — Bram's doing, though he'd never admit it was panic rather than strategy — and the smell had soaked into the stone like guilt soaks into a confession. I spent those three days doing what I always did when the world ended: I balanced the books.

It was easier than thinking. The ledgers didn't care that I no longer had a name. They cared only that the columns added up, and I could make them add up in my sleep, which was fortunate, because sleep was mostly what I was doing — standing at my desk with a pen in my hand, waking every few minutes to the memory of starlight shattering its cages, of my name peeling away like bark from a birch.

Here is what nobody tells you about losing your name: you don't feel the hole. You feel the edges of it. Like a tongue finding a missing tooth. I would reach for myself — *Ada Crane, hedge-witch's runaway, keeper of accounts* — and find nothing there but a smooth blank place where the words used to live. Isandro said he could see it when he looked at me. He said it looked like the space where a portrait had been hung for years and then taken down: the wall remembers the shape even after the picture is gone.

I told him to stop being poetic and hand me the inkwell.

He did. He always did. That was the whole miracle of him, really — not the memories flooding back whole, not the cartographer's hands steady again, but this: he handed me the inkwell, and sat down across from me, and did not once look at me like I was broken.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Vess called a meeting of everyone the guild still employed, which by then was eleven people and a cat who had wandered in during the fires and refused to leave. She stood at the head of the long table in the counting room with the founding ledger open in front of her — page one, the page I'd broken into an archive to read, the page with two signatures where there should have been one.

"I'm going to say this plainly," Vess said, "because I've spent sixty years saying things plainly and it has never once made anything worse."

Bram snorted. "You've made plenty worse."

"Thank you, Bram." She didn't look at him. "Three generations ago, someone built this guild on a lie. A forged contract. A soul sold to the Undercourt that was never truly sold — or rather, sold twice, by a woman named Maren Crane, who layered one theft into another until the whole edifice stood on fraud." Her voice didn't shake. I'd have respected it more if it had. "We profited from that fraud. Every bottle of luck, every borrowed year, every impossible thing in our vaults — bought cheap because the debt beneath us kept the Undercourt's prices down. We were not victims. We were beneficiaries. And last week we nearly paid for it with the life of the kindest man in this building."

Isandro, sitting beside me, studied his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. In a way they had, for a while.

"So," Vess went on, "here is what happens now. The Gilded Veil, as founded, is dissolved. Not reformed. Dissolved. Every contract in the vaults gets reviewed, and any of them built on coercion — and there are more than I'd like to count — get burned. The debt ledger goes into the fire too. All of it. We start over." She turned the founding ledger around so we could all see the page. "Which means we need new signatures. A new founding. And I am not signing alone."

She looked at me.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I don't need to hear the offer. The last time someone offered me a place in something, I was fourteen, and the offer came with fine print I spent twenty years reading in my nightmares." I closed my ledger. "Also, I can't sign anything. You may recall. No name."

Vess smiled, and it was the worst smile she owned — the patient one, the one she used on clients who thought they'd found a loophole. "Ada. You broke into a sealed archive, out-argued a court that eats liars, and voided a contract older than the guild using a margin note. If you think I'm letting you walk out of this building over a technicality, you have misjudged me for six years."

The room was quiet. Eleven people and a cat, all looking at me. Bram had his arms crossed, which was how he prayed. The cook, Marta, was crying a little, which was how she did everything.

"What exactly are you offering?" I asked, because I'd learned long ago that the only way through a bargain is straight down the middle of it.

"Partnership. Equal shares. Your name on the founding documents beside mine and Isandro's." Vess slid a fresh sheet of paper across the table. It was blank except for the heading, written in her careful clerk's hand: *The Honest Veil.* Someone had clearly been up all night choosing that name. "And before you object again — I've consulted. There are ways to sign without a name. A mark will hold. A seal will hold. But there is one way that holds better than all of them, and it's standing right next to you."

I turned to Isandro.

He was already holding the pen.

* * *

Here is what he proposed, and I want it recorded accurately, because someday — if there is a someday for people like us — someone will ask how it worked, and I'll be too busy adding columns to explain.

A name, freely given, can carry another. That was the law that saved us under the mountain; it was the law that damned Maren Crane, too, though she never understood it. The Underking had conceded our victory with that terrible question of his — *"Who accepted the benefits of a false thing?"* — and Ada Crane had answered with herself. But the reverse also held. What is given freely can shelter what was lost. Isandro couldn't give me back my name; names don't work that way, no matter how many bottles of them sit in vaults. But he could sign mine beside his. He could stand witness. He could say, in front of witnesses and wax and whatever gods bother to watch bookkeeping: *this person exists, and I will answer for her existence.*

"It's not a replacement," he told me quietly, while the others pretended very hard to study the ceiling. "It won't make you Ada Crane again. Nothing does that. But it means that when someone asks who you are, there's an answer. Two answers. Mine and yours together."

"And if you die?"

"Then you'll finally have to learn to write your own story instead of just balancing everyone else's." He smiled. His hands were steady. They had been steady since the stars came back, and I still hadn't gotten used to it. "Terrible fate. I weep for you already."

"You're insufferable when you're whole."

"I'm told it's an improvement over being unmade."

Vess cleared her throat. "If the two of you are finished flirting, some of us have a guild to found."

"We were not flirting," I said. "We were negotiating terms."

"Same thing," said Bram. "I've watched you two negotiate terms for six years. It's disgusting."

But when Isandro set the pen to the paper, nobody laughed. He wrote his name — his true name, the one Maren stole and resold and that he'd clawed back out of a king's vault, letter by letter, in the dark under the mountain. And then, beside it, without hesitating, he wrote:

*Ada Crane, called by her own choice, witnessed and answered for.*

Not my name. My name was gone, spent, dissolved into a contract that burned to ash in a cathedral of bone. But the shape of it. The word itself, chosen, claimed, held out like a coat offered to someone standing in the rain. He signed the letters he knew weren't legally mine anymore and made them mine anyway, the way you make anything true: by saying it in front of people who matter and refusing to take it back.

Marta cried harder. The cat jumped onto the table and put a paw directly in the wax, which is why the guild's founding seal has a cat print in it to this day, and I have forbidden anyone to fix it.

Then Vess signed. Then Bram, grumbling, muttering something about how he'd wanted to run south and open a tavern, and how he supposed somebody had to keep honest books now that dishonest ones were banned. Then the rest, one by one, eleven signatures and a paw print, on a single page that owed nothing to anyone.

I burned the old founding contract myself. Page one. The signature too old for the boy who wrote it. Maren Crane's name beneath it, postdated by nine days — the flaw that saved us, the margin note that unmade a kingdom's claim. I fed it to the cellar fire that Bram had started and never confessed to, and I watched it curl, and I felt — nothing. Or almost nothing. Just the smooth blank place, and beside it, warm, the knowledge that the blank place had company now.

"Maren Crane was my mother," I said to the fire, because Isandro was the only one there, and he already knew.

"I know."

"She sold you before she ever tried to sell me. Did you know that? You were a child buying your mother's life, and she—" My hands had gone still on the ledger I wasn't reading. "She priced you. She put a number on you and sold you twice."

"Yes." Isandro's voice was quiet. "And I spent thirty years building my life inside the debt that owned me, and it turns out the life was real anyway. The maps were real. The friend was real." He touched the edge of the burning page with a poker, pushing it deeper into the flames. "She doesn't get to be the ending of either of our stories, Ada. She was a bad beginning. That's all beginnings are good for — being survived."

I looked at him. Six years I'd known him, and I'd catalogued him like inventory: quiet, precise, draws maps of places that don't exist yet, do not appraise closely. And underneath all that, all along, a boy who had traded away everything to save his mother and been betrayed by the transaction itself. We were the same wound wearing different clothes.

"The mistress taught me that ledgers were safer than magic," I said. "Numbers don't lie about what they cost."

"She was wrong, though. Wasn't she?" He nodded at the fire, at the ash that had been a kingdom's claim on both of us. "The numbers lied constantly. They just lied neatly."

"They lied *in columns,*" I said. "There's a difference."

He laughed — really laughed, the sound of it strange and good in the smoky room — and for a moment neither of us said anything, and the fire ate the last of the old guild, and outside the narrow window the sky was doing its new trick, the one I still hadn't tired of: being full of stars. All of them back. Every cage shattered, every stolen light home again, arranged in patterns no astronomer had ever charted, because the sky had been rearranged by what happened under the mountain and hadn't bothered to apologize.

One of them, low near the horizon, burned a little warmer than the rest. Isandro swore it watched us climb. I chose to believe him. Believing things had gotten easier lately, which was either growth or brain damage, and I'd decided not to investigate which.

* * *

The Honest Veil opened for business on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays are for beginnings and everyone knows it deep down even if they won't admit it.

The rules were posted on the door in Vess's handwriting, and I checked them every morning for the first month, the way you check a lock on a door that used to be kicked in:

*Nothing sold here is taken without consent.*
*Nothing bought here is paid for with what the buyer cannot spare.*
*Every price is stated before the bargain, in plain language, twice.*
*Any contract found to rest on coercion is void, and we will help you prove it, free of charge.*

That last rule cost us money. Clients came from three kingdoms away to stand in our doorway and read it, and some of them wept, and some of them spat, and one old woman pressed a dried flower into Marta's hands and said, "Someone finally wrote it down." We helped forty-one people void coerced contracts in the first year. Bram kept a tally. He said it was the only ledger he'd ever enjoyed keeping.

And me? I kept the other kind. The honest kind. Columns that meant what they said, prices that matched their sums, a vault inventory of possible things only — bottled courage honestly distilled, luck fairly lent at fair interest, years of life sold only by those who had years to spare and signed papers proving it. It was less profitable. Vess said the word for that was *better*, and I'd learned, slowly, painfully, at the bottom of a mountain, to believe her.

My desk sits by the window now. Isandro's drafting table stands next to it, close enough that our elbows bump when he reaches for the ink, which he does more often than strictly necessary, and which I have never once mentioned, because some entries you leave off the books.

He's drawing a new map. Of course he is. This one isn't compulsive — his hands don't shake, he sleeps at night, he sometimes stops mid-line to eat or argue with Bram or fall asleep with his cheek on the vellum, which I have documented with sketches of my own, for leverage. This map is different. It shows roads that don't exist yet, towns not yet built, a river that will change course in forty years if the rains hold. Places waiting to be real.

Yesterday he tapped a spot near the map's eastern edge — empty vellum, nothing drawn there yet — and said, "What do you think goes here?"

"A town," I said. "With a decent archive. And a baker who doesn't water the ale."

"A town with a decent archive," he agreed, and drew it: little rooftops, a square, a building with a tall window, and in the window, small as a grain of rice, a figure bent over a desk with a lamp.

"That's not me," I said.

"It's whoever you decide to be," he said. "That's the whole point of the map. It's not where you've been. It's where you might go."

I looked at the little figure in the little window, at the blank space around it where a name would go if maps carried names, and I felt the smooth place inside me — the tooth-shaped absence, the wall remembering its portrait — and for the first time it didn't hurt. It felt like a margin. Like room left deliberately blank for something not yet written.

Tonight the ledger is balanced. Every column closes clean. Outside, the stars are out in their new arrangements, and one of them near the horizon is burning warm again, watching, maybe, or just shining — and tomorrow Isandro will put fresh vellum on the table, and I will sharpen my pen, and somewhere east of everywhere there is a town with a decent archive that doesn't exist yet, waiting for someone to write it real.

I have decided I'm going to be the one who writes it.

Whatever my name turns out to be.
