## 22. The Woman Who Is Her Own Blank Page

The first thing I did with my new life was buy a notebook.

Not a ledger. I want to be clear about that, because Isandro teased me about it for a week. A ledger has rules — columns, balances, the cold comfort of numbers that add up or don't. This was a plain stitched book of cheap paper from the stationer on Candlemaker's Row, the kind schoolchildren ruin with ink blots and bad poetry. It cost me four coppers, and it terrified me more than the Undercourt ever did.

Because a ledger tells you what *is*. A blank page asks you what you want to be.

I sat at my desk in the guild house — my desk, still mine, Vess had been very firm about that — and I opened to the first page and held my pen over it for the better part of an hour.

What do you write when you are no one?

I don't mean that in the dramatic way. Well. I mean it partly in the dramatic way; I've earned some drama. But I mean it literally too. Ada Crane is gone. Not dead — dissolved. Spent, down in the dark, poured out like the last of a bottle of borrowed luck to settle a debt that was never honestly incurred. The woman who fled Maren Crane's cottage with a stolen ledger and a talent for sums gave her name to a hungry king so that her friend could walk out into the morning whole.

And here is the strange arithmetic of it: I do not miss it. I can't. You cannot miss what you cannot hold in your mind, and my name slides through me now like water through a net. I know I had one. I know it mattered — the ache where it used to be tells me that much, the way an amputee's ghost limb itches. But when I reach for it, all I find is the shape of the reaching.

What I have instead is this: I know who I chose to be. That's not nothing. That might be everything.

* * *

Let me back up, because the last month deserves recording, even if only so future-me understands why present-me flinches at certain sounds.

The Gilded Veil re-founded itself the way a snake sheds skin: slowly, uncomfortably, and with everyone watching to see if the new skin would hold. Vess stood before the assembled guild — traders, enforcers, the quiet archivists who smell like dust and regret — and read aloud every contract in the vault. Every one. It took eleven days. She read the terms of bottled memories and years of life and borrowed luck, and then she read the fine print underneath, the parts we'd never shown clients because showing them would have meant admitting how much the Veil took and how little it returned.

Then she burned them. All of them, in the courtyard, in a brazier built for the purpose, while Bram stood at the door making sure no one left mid-speech to warn a client or spirit away a document. Some stormed out anyway. Three of our best memory-brokers resigned by throwing their keys at Vess's feet, which she caught, one by one, without flinching, and thanked them for their service in a voice like a closing door.

What remained — maybe sixty of us — signed something new. Shorter than my hand. No deferred payments, no collateral clauses, no souls. Just this: *We trade fairly. We say what things cost. We take no one's name.*

I watched Isandro sign it. Then I watched him turn the page toward me, and there was my line, waiting, and nothing for me to sign it with.

"No name," I said. "No signature."

"Then we'll make you one," he said, as if we were discussing ink supplies. He wrote *Ada* beside his own name — just Ada, no surname, no lineage, no history — and dated it, and initialed it twice, once for himself and once, he said, "on your behalf, until you're ready to countersign."

"I may never be ready."

"Then I'll keep initialing." He capped his pen. "It's not a debt, Ada. There's no term. No interest accrues. It's just — me, holding a place for you."

That's the difference, I suppose, between him and everyone else who ever held something of mine. Maren held my name the way a merchant holds stock. The Underking would have held it the way a collector holds a butterfly, pinned and labeled and beautiful forever. Isandro holds my place the way you hold a door open. Not gripping. Just — keeping it from swinging shut.

* * *

The stars came back over nineteen nights.

I kept count, because of course I kept count; some habits survive the loss of a soul's paperwork. Nineteen nights, nineteen stars, each one returning to its old post in the sky like a soldier drifting back from desertion. The astronomers' guild paid us a visit in week two, very stern, wanting to know what we knew about the celestial irregularities, and Vess told them the truth — the actual truth, which is the new policy — and they left looking like men who'd been handed a live eel when they'd asked for a fish.

The imprisoned starlight came back with them. Or rather, it went back. When the contract burned, the cages of bone-white stone shattered, and every stolen light in the Undercourt streaked upward through the mountain, through the rock, through the world, home. I felt them pass. Everyone did, I think — a shiver up the spine of the whole valley, a sound like a thousand small bells rung underwater.

Some of the courtiers came up after. Not many. A handful of unfinished faces who'd been sold under false pretenses themselves, generations back, and who emerged blinking into real starlight and asked, in voices like sketches learning shading, whether the world above was always this bright. Isandro spent three days drawing maps for them — real ones, places that exist, roads and wells and honest distances — before they scattered to whatever lives the unowned build for themselves.

One of them stayed. She works in the archive now. Her face is still faintly unfinished around the edges, like a portrait the artist abandoned, but she laughs, and the laugh fills in a little more of her every time. Her name — the one she chose, the one nobody gave her — is Wren. She picked it herself, off a bird she saw on the windowsill her first morning above ground. I asked her once if it frightened her, choosing. She said, "Less than being chosen for."

I've written that down twice.

* * *

Which brings me back to the notebook, and the problem of the first page.

Isandro found me at it near midnight, three days after I bought it, still staring at the same blank sheet. He didn't ask what I was doing. He pulled a chair around backward and sat on it the wrong way, arms folded on the backrest, and waited, because he has learned that I talk eventually and rushing me only makes me dig in like a tick.

"It should be easy," I said finally. "I've rewritten harder things. Contracts. Ledgers. Myself, arguably. And I can't put one word on this page."

"Because?"

"Because everything I write will be true, and I'll be stuck with it." I turned the pen over in my fingers. "A name is a commitment, Isandro. Ask anyone who's tried to get out of one."

He was quiet a moment. Then he said, "Show me your map of yourself. Right now, as you understand it. Not on paper — just tell me."

"That's absurd."

"You're absurd. Tell me."

So I did, because he asked, and because midnight makes cowards of us all. "I'm good with numbers. I don't trust gifts. I trust receipts. I ran from someone who wanted to sell me and I hid inside a business that sells people, because at least there the selling was itemized. I love—" and here my voice did something embarrassing, so I cleared my throat and continued, "—I love a man who draws maps of places that don't exist yet, and I gave up the only thing I ever protected so he could remember his mother's face. I'm angry about that sometimes. Not often. Mostly I'm glad. I like strong tea and I hate being pitied and I have never once in my life done anything brave until recently, and it turns out I'm apparently capable of it, which is deeply inconvenient information. That's the map. It's incomplete and badly surveyed and half of it is guesswork."

Isandro nodded slowly, the way he does when a coastline turns out to match his chart.

"And none of that," he said, "required your name. Not one word of it. You described yourself entirely without it. So the page isn't empty, Ada. You just haven't realized it's already full."

I stared at him. "You're saying I already exist."

"I'm saying you were never the name. The name was just the label on the jar. The jar's still here." He tapped the notebook. "Write the contents."

* * *

Here is what I wrote, that night, in the notebook, on the first page, in my own careful accounting hand:

*I am the woman who balanced the books of impossible things and found the error that broke a kingdom's claim. I am the one who lied precisely to a ferryman and exactly to a king. I count. I keep. I choose. I was given away once and I will never again be given, taken, traded, or owed. What I am next is mine to author, and I authorize myself.*

Underneath, smaller:

*Provisional. Subject to revision by the management.*

Isandro read it over my shoulder and said, "You've made yourself a contract."

"I've made myself a *partnership*," I said. "Between who I was and who I'm deciding to be. Both parties retain rights of revision."

He laughed — that low, surprised laugh he still does, like he keeps forgetting he's allowed to have things — and kissed the top of my head, and said, "Counterparty accepts."

* * *

Spring is coming to the city. I can tell because the gutter outside my window has started running at night, and because Wren has filled the archive with cuttings she swears are decorative and everyone else suspects are an infestation, and because Isandro has started a new map.

Not a descent this time. An ascent — or rather, an outward one. He spreads it across the big table in the map room some evenings and adds to it while I do the accounts, and it has grown from the city outward in slow rings: the river road, the hill towns, the passes east where the border wards are being rebuilt by people who finally understand what they're warding against. At the far edge, past everything surveyed, he's drawn the coastline of somewhere unnamed and marked it with a small question mark, and beneath the question mark, in his tidy cartographer's hand: *for her.*

"We could go," he said last night, not looking up from his inking. "When the guild's steady. See what's past the edge of the paper."

"You hate travel."

"I hate traveling alone." He set down the pen. "Also, I owe you a life, and I intend to pay it in installments of scenery."

"You don't owe me anything. That was the entire point."

"Then consider it interest-free," he said, "and freely given," and I let him have the last word, which is another thing I've learned to do lately, and which costs me nothing, because words aren't currency anymore. Not between us. Between us they're just — weather. Something that moves through and leaves the sky clearer.

* * *

I still balance the ledgers. Of course I do. The Gilded Veil trades honestly now, which turns out to be harder than trading dishonestly — margins are thinner when you tell people what things cost — but Vess says the guild sleeps better, and Bram says the guild sleeps worse but deserves to, and both of them are right, and I record both figures in the annual report because I believe in complete records.

But the notebook stays separate. The notebook isn't business.

Some entries from this month, transcribed for the permanent record:

*Day 12: Learned that I like the smell of rain on hot stone. Previously unknown. Filed under: self, discoveries, minor.*

*Day 15: Argued with Vess about interest rates and won. Felt no guilt whatsoever. Growth.*

*Day 17: Isandro drew my portrait without asking. It's accurate except he made the mouth kinder than it is. I have decided to grow into it.*

*Day 19: The nineteenth star came back tonight. I stood in the courtyard and watched it happen and thought: that one's mine. Not really. None of them are. But I paid for the sky, in a way, and so I get to feel proprietary about it, and no one can invoice me otherwise.*

*Day 23: Realized I no longer flinch when someone asks my name. I say "Ada" — just Ada — and it doesn't hurt. It's not a wound anymore. It's a pen name. Everything I am is written under it, and everything I am is mine.*

Maren Crane died in the winter, alone, in a cottage that smelled of herbs and unpaid debts. I sent nothing. I mourned nothing. But I recorded it, day 9, in the notebook, one line: *The account is closed.* Because I believe in complete records, even the ones that cost nothing and return less.

* * *

Last night I dreamed of the Undercourt.

Not often, but it happens — the bone-white cathedral, the sketch-faced courtiers, the throne where the king sat collecting. In the dream the hall was empty. The cages shattered. And on the floor where the throne had been, someone had left a single blank page, and I understood, in the way of dreams, that it was mine — that it had always been mine, that the king had never owned anything of mine at all except the fear of him, and the fear had burned with the contract, and what remained was paper, and paper waits.

I woke before dawn and lay listening to the gutters run, and Isandro breathed beside me in the slow rhythm of a man whose memories are all his own again, every one, even the terrible ones, especially the terrible ones, and I thought about the question the blank page asks.

What do you want to be?

Not who were you. Not what were you owed, or what were you sold for, or what did your mother's mistress write you down as in her private ledger of assets. Just: what next. What now. What will you author.

I got up in the dark and lit the lamp and opened the notebook to a fresh page, because the first page was finished — signed, sealed, subject to revision — and a finished page is only the beginning of the next one.

At the top, I wrote:

*Chapter One.*

And below it, after a moment's thought:

*The woman who is her own blank page begins here. All errors are intentional. All revisions are hers.*

Outside, the last of the night was thinning toward gray, and somewhere east Isandro's question mark waited at the edge of the map, patient as an unwritten sentence. I set the pen down. I put the kettle on.

And when he woke and found me there — ink on my fingers, tea going cold, morning coming up gold over a city that remembered its stars — he looked at the open notebook, and then at me, and smiled like a man reading the first line of a very long book.

"Where to?" he said.

I turned to a clean page.

"Anywhere," I said. "I'm the author. Let's find out what happens next."
