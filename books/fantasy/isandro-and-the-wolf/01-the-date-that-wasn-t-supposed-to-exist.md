## 1. The Date That Wasn't Supposed to Exist

The morning the stranger came to my stall, I was arguing with a dead woman about turnips.

Not my mother — she'd been quiet for years, and I'd learned not to miss her too loudly. This was old Marta Hessel, dead eleven winters, who had sold turnips from the very spot I now occupied and apparently still had opinions about commerce. *Underprice the early ones,* she kept saying, in that thin whistle of a voice she'd had at the end. *Get them hooked on you. Then you own them.*

"I'm not going to own anyone, Marta," I murmured, arranging the little carved bones on my cloth like a jeweler laying out scandal.

"Then you'll die poor," said Marta, "like your mother."

My mother had died anything but poor in spirit, but I let it go. Arguing with the dead is a bad habit; they always win, because they have nothing else to do.

I am Beatrix Winterbourne, bone-carver of the Saltmarket in Vellamark's capital city — though nobody calls it Vellamark, just as nobody calls me anything but Trixie, which is fine by me. Small names keep you safe. I carve charms: knucklebones polished smooth for luck, tiny fish for travelers crossing water, a sleeping bird for mothers with colicky infants. Nothing grand. Nothing that hums too loudly. In twenty years I have learned one lesson above all others, and it is this: *keep your magic small enough to fit in a pocket.* Anything bigger gets noticed, and things that get noticed get taken.

The Saltmarket was doing its usual morning business around me — fishwives screaming prices, a tinker beating dents out of a kettle, the smell of tar and frying onions and the river underneath everything. Ordinary. Deliberately, carefully ordinary. I have spent two decades building ordinary the way other people build houses, stone by stone, and I live inside it.

He came out of the crowd the way a cold draft comes under a door.

Tall, dark-haired, travel-worn, with a coat cut in no style I recognized and dust on his boots from no road I knew. He stopped three paces from my stall and looked at my wares with an expression I couldn't place — not browsing, not shopping. More like a man reading a letter he'd been told was addressed to someone else.

"You're the carver," he said. Not a question.

"I'm a carver," I said, which is different, and safer.

"The one who hears."

The market noise seemed to step back from us, the way water steps back from a dropped stone. I kept my hands busy with a half-finished charm, a hare, all ears and caution. "I don't know what you mean. I sell trinkets. Luck and nonsense."

"Nonsense," he repeated softly. "Yes." And then, because he was either very rude or very desperate: "I need something. Something that remembers."

Now, there are questions strangers ask and questions they shouldn't, and that was firmly in the second category. *Something that remembers* is not what you say when you want a luck-charm. I looked up at him properly then, and he had eyes the color of strong tea, tired and steady, and he was afraid. Not of me. Of something behind me, or above me, or coming after him down the long road he'd clearly walked to get here.

And then my mother spoke.

She has been dead twenty years, and she had been silent for most of them — I'd assumed she'd finally gone wherever the dead go when they're done with us. But her voice arrived whole and warm and urgent, right against my ear, the way it used to when I was small and she wanted me to hear something the grown-ups shouldn't:

*Trixie. Don't sell to that man.*

I didn't flinch. Twenty years of practice. But my hands went still over the hare, and the stranger noticed — he noticed everything — and something in his face shifted, like a man watching weather change.

"You heard something," he said.

"I heard a customer," I said. "They're rare enough to be remarkable."

*Don't sell to him, Trixie. Don't touch his coin. Some doors only open from the inside.*

My mother had never warned me off a customer before. She'd spent her life telling me the opposite — that the dead were company, that hearing them was a gift, that I should never be ashamed of it. To hear her now, after all these years, saying *no* —

That should have been the end of it. A strange man, a warning, a polite refusal. I could have sent him away and gone back to Marta Hessel and her turnips and another twenty years of careful silence.

Instead I heard myself say: "What kind of remembering?"

He exhaled, slow, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried a long way. From inside his coat he drew a purse, and from the purse a single coin — old, worn nearly blank, stamped with a design I almost recognized and couldn't name. It made my teeth ache to look at it, the way a tune does when you've forgotten the words.

"A charm," he said. "For luck. The ordinary kind. But make it from bone that's *old*. Bone that was here before — before whatever came before." He hesitated. "You'll know the piece when you hold it."

I should have refused. My mother was still whispering against my ear, *Trixie, Trixie, don't*, and Marta Hessel had gone quiet too, which frightened me more — the whole graveyard holding its breath.

But my hand was already moving across the cloth, past the hares and fishes and sleeping birds, to the back corner where I keep the pieces I don't display. Old stock. Found stock. A rib bone I'd dug from the riverbank years ago, dense and yellowed, older than any beast I could name, that I'd never been able to carve because every knife stroke felt like interrupting someone.

I set it on the cloth between us.

"That one," he said quietly. "Yes. That one."

"It's not finished. It's not anything. It's a piece of—"

"Of Vellamark," he said.

The word went through me like a needle finding a nerve. I have no idea how I know the word. If you asked me where I learned it I would tell you I've always known it, the way you know your own heartbeat, and if you asked me what it means I would open my mouth and find nothing there. A country? A name? A word from a song?

"Two coppers," I said, because my voice needed to do something ordinary or I was going to scream.

He paid without haggling — which any market wife will tell you marks a man as either rich or doomed — wrapped the rib in his scarf, and turned to go. Then he paused.

"What's your name?" he asked. "Your real one."

"Trixie," I said.

He smiled, faintly, sadly, like a man greeting an old friend who doesn't remember him. "Beatrix," he said, "Winterbourne. Yes. I thought so."

And he was gone into the crowd, and the market roared back in around me, and my mother's voice faded like smoke — but not before I caught the last of it, so soft I might have dreamed it:

*It's started again.*

* * *

I did not sleep that night.

I told myself it was the wind. The wind comes off the river in autumn and worries the shutters, and a body can lie awake listening to it and call it weather instead of dread. I lit my one candle, I mended a cloak seam, I drank tea I didn't want. All the small rituals of a small life.

But under the floorboard beneath my bed — the loose one, the one I lift maybe twice a year — I keep the book.

I found it when I was nineteen, newly orphaned, clearing out my mother's things. Wrapped in oilcloth, hidden in the chimney cavity where she hid everything that mattered. A heavy volume bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, no title on the spine, no maker's mark. Inside, written in a hand that changed from page to page as though many people had taken turns — or one person had lived a very long time — were prophecies. Dates. Events. Small ones, mostly. A flood. A fire at the tannery. The year the bridge bell cracked.

Every one of them had come true. Every one of them had already happened before I ever read them.

Except the last pages. Those were dated ahead — years ahead, decades ahead — and those I did not read. I looked once, the night I found it, saw tomorrow's date sitting there in ink like a trap, and closed the book and put it under the floor and told myself it was a curiosity, a fraud, a madwoman's ledger. I built my ordinary life on top of it, literally, and slept above it every night for twenty years, and never once lifted the board.

Tonight I lifted the board.

I don't know why. That's the honest truth, and I hate honest truths. Something about the way he said my full name. Something about the coin that ached. Something about my mother's voice coming back from wherever the dead go, just to say *don't* — because in my experience the dead only break their silence when the living are about to do something irreversible.

I set the book on my knees. The leather was cold. The candle guttered in the wind off the river, steadied, guttered again.

I turned to the last written page.

The date at the top was tomorrow's. Tomorrow's date, in ink dried a hundred years ago — I know the age of the thing the way I know the age of bone, in my hands, in my teeth. And beneath the date, in that shifting, patient hand:

*A bone-carver sells a rib of the old country to a traveler with no shadow on his left side. She charges him two coppers. She does not ask his name, though he asks hers.*

I sat very still. The candle flame leaned and recovered. Somewhere below, the river kept up its low idiot muttering against the pilings.

No shadow on his left side. I hadn't noticed. I hadn't looked. Who looks at a man's shadow in a crowded market on a bright morning?

I turned the page.

There was one more entry. One line, in fresh black ink — ink that glistened slightly, as though the words had been written while I sat there holding the book, as though the pen were still wet somewhere, still moving:

*Tomorrow, she asks his name.*

I closed the book. I put it back under the floor. I pressed the board down flat and lay in the dark with my heart going like a trapped bird, telling myself the only thing I have ever told myself: *keep it small, keep it quiet, keep it ordinary.*

But I knew, lying there in the dark above a century-old book, that the ordinary had cracked. I could feel the cold coming through it, like a draft under a door.

Tomorrow I would ask his name.

God help me, some part of me already wanted to.
