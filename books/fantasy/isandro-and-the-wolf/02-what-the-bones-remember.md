## 2. What the Bones Remember

He came back at dawn, when the market was still gray and the fishwives were arguing over eels, and he stood in front of my stall like a man who had every right to be there.

"You read it," Isandro said. Not a question.

I kept my hands busy with a half-carved hare femur. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"The book. You found your name in it." He set a copper coin on the counter between us, the same one he'd paid me yesterday, as if returning it would undo something. "You went pale. I watched you go pale through the window."

"I go pale all the time. Bad blood."

"Beatrix Winterbourne." He said my name slowly, tasting it, and I felt it land somewhere under my ribs like a swallowed stone. "The book says you'd say that."

My knife stopped moving. The market noise went on around us — gulls, haggling, a cart wheel squealing — but it sounded suddenly far away, like weather in another country.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Sent," he said simply. "By the book itself, if you believe what it says about me. Which I didn't, for a long time. But I've learned that disbelief is just a slower kind of obedience."

* * *

I should have turned him away. Twenty years of practice said turn him away, keep small, keep quiet, keep the magic no bigger than a thimble. But my mother's voice had been silent since yesterday — she who warned me about him — and her silence frightened me more than his presence. As if even the dead weren't sure whose side this was on.

So I took him up the narrow stair to my rooms above the stall, which was stupid, and I put the kettle on, which was stupider, and I laid the prophecy book on my table between two candle stubs like a patient I couldn't diagnose.

It looked worse in daylight. The cover was some kind of skin I refused to think too hard about, cracked and dark, and the pages had gone the color of old teeth. But the ink hadn't faded at all. That was the wrong part. Ink fades. Everything fades. This looked written an hour ago.

"Read it aloud," Isandro said. "Not to me. To the room."

"That's superstition."

"So is everything else you do for a living."

I opened it to the page with tomorrow's date and made myself read.

*"On the day after the luck-charm is sold, the bone-carver will open her door to the stranger twice. On the second opening, the Custodians will come asking after unregistered memory. She will deny them, and they will mark her door, and from that marking the road begins."*

I closed it.

"No," I said.

"Keep reading."

"There's nothing about Custodians here. There are no such people. I've lived in Vellamark—" I stopped, because the sentence wouldn't finish. It sat there in my mouth, incomplete, like a bone missing its joint.

"In Vellamark," Isandro said quietly, "what?"

"In Vellamark my whole life, and nobody comes asking after memory. Memory isn't registered. Memory isn't anything. It's just — yours."

"Is it?" He leaned forward. "Tell me about your grandmother. The one whose lullaby you hum when you carve."

"How do you—"

"The book told me. Tell me anyway. Prove me wrong."

And I tried. I opened my mouth to give him the lullaby, three lines of it, the one about the river going home, and found — nothing. Not nothing exactly. A shape where the song should be, the way you can feel a missing tooth with your tongue. I knew I had known it. I could not know it anymore.

The kettle began to scream and neither of us moved to take it off the fire.

* * *

After that I did the only thing I trusted: I went to the records.

The Hall of Registers stood on Cordwainer Street, all pale stone and official gloom, run by archivists who charged by the question and answered by the hour. I'd been there twice in my life, both times to prove I existed — birth record, tax record, the little papers that let a person buy flour without being a ghost.

"Vellamark," I said to the clerk, a dry man named Ostrey with ink-stained cuffs. "As a country. I want its borders, its founding, whatever you have."

He looked at me the way you look at someone who has asked for the weight of a color. "Vellamark?"

"The country. This country. The one we're standing in."

"We are in the Marches of Corvel," he said patiently. "We have always been in the Marches of Corvel. Before that, the Corveli Protectorate. Would you like the Protectorate rolls?"

"Yes," I said. "All of them."

He brought them. I read for four hours while Isandro waited outside because his face made the clerks nervous. I read the founding charters, the border treaties, the census rolls going back past my grandmother's grandmother. I read the names of kings and the dates of floods and the prices of wool across two hundred years of meticulous, dusty certainty.

The word *Vellamark* appeared nowhere. Not once. Not in a footnote, not in a misprint, not scratched into a margin by a bored apprentice. Two hundred years of records, and the name of the country I had lived in my entire life existed only in one book — a book written before any of these.

But here is the part I have not told anyone, the part I will carry until they carve it on whatever passes for my grave:

Every register I touched hummed.

You have to understand what that means, coming from me. Bone hums. Bone remembers; that's my trade and my curse. Wood doesn't hum. Paper doesn't hum. Stone doesn't hum. In twenty years of handling everything the living own, I have never once felt memory in anything that wasn't bone.

Except those registers hummed. Faintly, wrongly, like a voice heard through a wall. And when I pressed my palm flat against the oldest census roll and let myself listen — really listen, the way I listen to a dead man's knucklebone — what came through was grief. Old, vast, patient grief, the kind that has forgotten it is allowed to stop.

The paper was grieving. All of it. Every page in that hall was grieving for something none of it remembered.

I walked out into the noon light and Isandro fell into step beside me, and I said, "Tell me what you know. All of it. Don't arrange it first."

"It's not mine to arrange," he said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They came at dusk, and they knocked politely, which was somehow worse than if they'd broken the door down.

There were two of them. Gray coats, gray gloves, faces so unremarkable that looking away from them felt like relief, and I understood immediately why no one ever described a Custodian twice. They stood in my doorway filling it without seeming large, and the taller one held a ledger bound in the same cracked skin as my prophecy book, and my mother's voice rose in me at last, thin and urgent:

*Don't let them touch the bones, Bea. Don't let them touch anything.*

"Beatrix Winterbourne," the tall one said. "Bone-carver. Licensed charm-seller, third district market." He consulted the ledger without appearing to read it. "A complaint has been filed. Unregistered memory, detected in your goods. Charms sold yesterday carrying resonance beyond their declared function."

"My charms are luck-charms," I said. "Rabbit bones, river stones, the usual. Nothing resonant."

"May we inspect?"

Everything in me said no. Everything in me also said that saying no was how you got marked, and marked was how you disappeared, and I had spent twenty years learning exactly how quietly a person could vanish in Vellamark.

"They're on the stall below," I said. "Inspect there. My workroom is private."

The shorter one smiled, or arranged his face into something adjacent to smiling. "Of course. We find craftswomen are often protective of their spaces. Memory does accumulate in places where work is done." He paused. "Yours especially, we're told. You hear the dead, don't you? Unusual gift. Unusual *retention*."

The word landed like a hook. Retention. As if my dead were inventory. As if my mother's voice were a discrepancy in an account.

They went down to the stall. I watched from the stairhead as they lifted my charms one by one in their gray gloves, and I felt each touch like a finger dragged along the back of my neck. When the tall one picked up the luck-charm — Isandro's charm, the hare bone I'd carved the night before last — he went very still.

"This one," he said. "This one is singing."

"It's a rabbit," I said. "Rabbits don't sing."

"Not the bone." He turned it over, and I saw the moment the hare's skull caught the lamplight, and I saw something move behind his face that I had never seen behind any face: recognition. "The *name* on it. You signed your work, Mistress Winterbourne. In the old script."

"I sign in Common, same as everyone."

"Do you." He set the charm down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you handle a thing that might be evidence. "Then perhaps you could explain why your signature reads *Béatrix Wintarbúrn* — and why I haven't seen that script in a very long time, and why neither, I suspect, have you."

I hadn't. I looked at my own hands as if they belonged to a stranger, and somewhere in the walls of my skull my mother's voice was repeating one word, over and over, soft as a lullaby with the tune torn out of it:

*Run. Run. Run.*

The tall Custodian closed his ledger. "We'll need to return," he said pleasantly. "Soon. Do keep working, Mistress Winterbourne. Craft is memory, after all. And memory—" the shorter one finished, together with him, in perfect unison—

"—belongs to everyone."

Their footsteps faded down Cordwainer Street. Isandro stepped out of the shadow of the stairwell, and he wasn't smiling, and he wasn't afraid either, and that was the most frightening thing in the whole long day.

"Now do you want to hear what I know?" he asked.

I blew out the candles. Outside, the first Custodian lanterns were already moving through the market, gray and patient, counting doors.

"Pack a bag," I said. "And start talking."
