## 3. A Country With No Borders

The market at night was a different animal. By day it was commerce — haggling, salt fish, the clatter of scales. After dark it became a nervous system: lanterns strung like ganglia, runners carrying messages through the crowd, and everywhere the low hum of people pretending not to watch each other.

Isandro moved through it with the book pressed flat against his ribs, under his coat, and Beatrix's wrist locked in his grip.

She had not come willingly. She had come because the Custodians knocked on her door for the second time in one day, and this time they did not knock politely.

"Let go of me," she said, not loudly. A woman who kept her magic small for twenty years knew how to keep her voice small too.

"Not until we're past the tannery." He pulled her left, down an alley that smelled of lime and rot, then out again into the fishmongers' row where the lanterns burned thickest. Crowds were cover. Crowds were mercy. "They'll have someone watching your stall."

"They'll have someone watching *you*. You're the stranger here."

"I'm the only reason you're still standing in your own skin," he said, and felt her flinch, and hated himself a little for using it.

He had rehearsed this. On the road, in the weeks before Vellamark's capital — before whatever this city called itself now — he had rehearsed the telling of it a hundred times, and every version had gone better than this. In the rehearsals she believed him by the second street. In the rehearsals nobody was following them.

"Talk," she said. "You bought my luck-charm. You came back with a book older than my grandmother. Now men in grey coats are asking my neighbors whether I keep 'unregistered memory,' whatever that means, and you're dragging me through the night market like a smuggler. So talk, Isandro. And if I hear one word that sounds like madness, I'm going to scream, and this crowd will decide whose story is true."

"That's fair," he said.

"It isn't. Nothing about today is fair."

* * *

They stopped under the awning of a shuttered spice stall, where the dark pooled deepest between two lanterns. He turned to face her. Up close her eyes were the color of river ice, and they did not blink enough.

"What do you know about this country?" he asked.

"I know its name," she said. "Everyone does."

"Say it."

Something crossed her face — a hesitation so fast he almost missed it, like a fish turning under water.

"Vellamark," she said.

"And where are its borders?"

She opened her mouth. Closed it. He watched her reach for something obvious — north to the mountains, south to the sea — and watched the answer slide away from her like water off oiled cloth.

"The borders are—" She frowned. "East. The borders run east along the... the river is..." Her hand made a shape in the air, tracing a line that dissolved as she drew it. "I've lived here my whole life. I should know where my own country ends."

"You can't know," Isandro said gently. "No one can. Because Vellamark doesn't end anywhere. It doesn't begin anywhere. It hasn't existed for a hundred years."

The market noise went on around them — a cart, a laugh, a dog — and none of it touched the little circle of silence they stood in.

"That's insane," Beatrix said. But her voice had changed. It had gone thin and careful, the voice of a woman testing ice.

"Then explain the book." He drew it out from under his coat, and even in the dark it seemed to hold light wrong, the way deep water holds light. "A hundred years old. Written by an archivist named Ondrej Vasquez —"

Her breath caught.

"— who records, on its final page, a date. Not a date a century past. Tomorrow's date. And on the page before that, a list of names. The last citizens of Vellamark. People who were never supposed to exist, being born into a country that was never supposed to survive." He let that sit. "One of those names is mine."

"You could have forged it."

"With what? Paper that old? Ink that old? And why forge it at all? Why buy a luck-charm from a bone-carver first? Why knock on your door twice?" He shook his head. "Beatrix. Your surname is Vasquez. Ondrej Vasquez wrote this book. That's not chance. That's blood remembering itself."

"My grandfather came from over the mountains," she said automatically, recitationally, the way people say things they've been told and never examined. "From— from—" Her hand rose halfway to her temple and stopped there, hovering, as if she'd forgotten what it was reaching for.

"Do you remember his face?"

"Yes." A pause. "Yes, of course I—"

"Describe it."

Silence. Long silence. Somewhere across the market a bell rang the third hour, and she flinched at it like a struck animal.

"I don't—" she whispered. "His face is... there's a shape where his face should be. There's always been a shape. I thought everyone's grandfathers were like that."

"No," Isandro said. "That's the erasure. That's what it looks like from inside. They didn't kill Vellamark, Beatrix. Killing leaves bodies, graves, grievances. They did something cleaner. They folded it shut — the country, the language, the history — and they folded its people into the neighboring nations like a letter into an envelope. And then they took the memory of the folding away too. Everyone in this city is living inside a country that was deleted, walking streets that belong to no map, speaking a language that survives only in their sleep."

"In their sleep?" she said sharply. "What do you know about how people sleep here?"

Enough. More than enough. He'd spent three years among the border villages listening to farmers talk Vellamarkish in their dreams and wake up calling it nonsense syllables. But he said only: "The book told me. The book tells me everything. It's the only record of Vellamark that survived, because it was written outside the fold — carried out by a man who saw what was coming. It's not a prophecy, exactly. It's a map. A map of everything that was lost. And maps have destinations."

"Which is?"

"The Door of Unmaking."

She stared at him. "That sounds like a story you tell children to make them behave."

"It sounds like that because the Custodians want it to sound like that. They're the ones who maintain the erasure. Grey coats, soft voices, questions about 'unregistered memory.' They harvest what's left of Vellamark out of people's heads — scraps, songs, grandfathers' faces — and feed it back into the erasure to keep it sealed. Every memory they take makes the forgetting stronger. And every year there are fewer memories left to take." He stepped closer. "The Door is the one way through. Out of the fold. Into the real world, where Vellamark is a wound other countries still remember. Someone has to carry its memory out, or when the last of it expires, a hundred years of lives just... stop having happened."

"You're saying I'm not real."

"I'm saying you're more real than anyone here knows, and that's exactly why they're hunting you."

* * *

She laughed. It was a terrible sound, short and hard, with nothing in it but refusal.

"No," she said. "No. I'll tell you what this is. This is a con. A beautiful one — the book, the name, the grandfather I can't quite picture. You found a bone-carver with one foot in the grave-trade and you built me a haunting. Well, I hear the dead for a living, stranger. Do you know what the dead have never once said to me? *Vellamark.* Twenty years of touching bones, twenty years of griefs and grudges and dying whispers, and not one soul has ever mentioned a country called Vellamark. If your erased kingdom were real, the dead would remember it. The dead remember *everything*."

For a moment he had no answer, because she was right, and he had wondered the same thing himself on the long road. Then he remembered the ossuary outside Harrow's Ford, and the bones that hummed.

"Ask them," he said quietly.

"What?"

"You touch bones and they speak. So touch one. Right now. Any dead thing will do — you must have something in your kit, you always do, I saw the stall." His heart was going hard against the book. "Ask them what country they died in."

She reached into her coat with the automatic motion of long habit and brought out a finger-bone, yellowed, wrapped in linen. A working piece; he could tell by the wear on the wrapping. She held it in her cupped palm and closed her eyes, and her lips moved without sound, and Isandro stood very still and listened to the market breathe.

Ten seconds. Twenty.

When her eyes opened they were wet and wide and full of something worse than fear.

"It's humming," she said. "It's never done that. Bones *speak*, they don't—" Her fist closed around the finger-bone until the knuckles went white. "It's grieving. It's grieving something it can't name. It keeps reaching for a word and the word isn't there, there's just a hole shaped like a word, and the hole has a *name-shaped absence* and I can almost —"

"Lantern," Isandro said.

Because down the fishmongers' row, moving against the flow of the crowd, came two figures in grey coats, unhurried, and the crowd parted around them the way water parts around a keel — without being asked, without seeming to notice.

Beatrix saw them. Whatever else she believed or refused to believe, she had survived twenty years by knowing what danger looked like, and her body understood before her mind did. She went for the alley behind the spice stall. Isandro went with her, and they were three steps into the dark when the grey coats simply *were there*, the way they always were, as if distance were a courtesy they didn't observe.

The taller one smiled. It was a kind smile. That was the worst part, Isandro thought, watching it happen — it was genuinely kind, the way a surgeon is kind.

"Beatrix Winterbourne," the Custodian said. "Thank you for making this easy. We only need a small tithe. Unregistered memory. You won't feel it — well. You'll feel it briefly."

"Don't—" Isandro started forward, and the second Custodian didn't even look at him; something like pressure simply moved him back, gentle as a tide, and pinned him against the wall.

The tall one extended one white hand and laid two fingers, feather-light, against Beatrix's temple.

And Isandro watched a childhood leave her face.

It happened in the space of a breath. Her eyes lost focus, then refocused — but differently, the way a room refocuses after furniture has been removed. Her mouth opened around a word that never arrived. Something behind her eyes went out: some small summer thing, some afternoon, some voice — and he could see her *grabbing* for it, both hands, the way you grab at a dream on waking, and feel it come apart like wet paper.

"There," the Custodian said softly, withdrawing his hand. "A lullaby, I think. Half-eaten already, poor thing. Thank you for your contribution to the health of the nation."

Beatrix stood swaying. The finger-bone dropped from her hand and rolled in the gutter dust.

"What did you take," she said. Her voice was level. That was the frightening thing. It was perfectly, dangerously level. "What did you take from me. What was it. What was his name — my grandfather — what was his *name* —"

"We didn't take anything," the Custodian said kindly. "There was never anything to take. There's no such place as Vellamark."

And Beatrix Winterbourne looked at him — at the smiling grey coat, at the whole patient machinery of the world — and Isandro saw the denial die in her face and something else be born there, something with teeth.

"No," she said. "There is. And I'm going to make you sorry it ever was."

Behind her, in the alley dark, the dropped finger-bone was humming loud enough now that Isandro could hear it too.
