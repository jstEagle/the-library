## 4. The Custodians' Tithe

The archivist's name was Ondrel, and he was dying in the back room of a bookbindery that smelled of glue and rot.

Beatrix knew it the moment they crossed the threshold — not from any magic, but from the sound of his breathing, a wet rattle like water finding its way through gravel. Isandro had brought her here at dawn, moving through streets still gray with sleep, and she had followed because the alternative was staying in her stall and waiting for the Custodians to come back with better questions.

"You said he could help us," she whispered.

"He can. If we're quick." Isandro knocked a pattern on the doorframe — three, pause, two — and something inside shifted, a chair scraping, a body rising with effort.

Ondrel turned out to be a narrow man folded into a narrow chair, wrapped in a blanket that might once have been white. His eyes were the problem. They were wrong — not blind, but unfixed, as if they kept trying to look at something behind the walls, or beneath them. When they settled on Beatrix, they stayed.

"The bone-carver," he said. His voice was papery. "I know your mother's work. Before."

"Before what?" Beatrix asked.

"Before there wasn't a before." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, gentle and ruined at once. "Sit down, girl. You've been carrying something heavy for twenty years and you don't even know its weight. I can hear it from here."

* * *

He told them while the light came up through the bindery's one grimy window, and Beatrix listened the way you listen to a diagnosis — every word landing somewhere in the body, cold and specific.

The erasure was not a wall. It was not a spell laid over Vellamark like a cloth over a cage. It was an appetite. A hundred years ago, when the kingdom was unmade, the act required feeding — and it had never stopped requiring it. Memory is the only substance that can hold a country together, Ondrel explained, and memory is also the only substance that can be eaten. So the Custodians harvested. Generation after generation, they moved through the populations into which Vellamark's people had been folded, taking the memories that still carried the old country inside them: a grandmother's lullaby, a street name spoken in sleep, the shape of a festival no calendar recorded anymore.

"They don't take everything," Ondrel said. "That would kill the host, and a dead carrier's memories go... nowhere useful. They take the Vellamark out of a memory and leave the rest. Your grandmother becomes a woman who sang *something* to you, some tune, you couldn't say what. Your childhood street becomes just a street. The country drains out of you drop by drop, and each drop feeds the erasure another century of strength."

"How do you know all this?" Beatrix asked.

"Because I was the royal archivist's third assistant," Ondrel said, "and I have been alive far longer than a third assistant should be." He coughed, wet and long. "We hid the records. We hid them inside people, in the end — the only vaults the Custodians couldn't open without killing the treasury. And then we waited to die, because dying is the one delivery they can't intercept."

Isandro sat very still beside her. "You're one of the carriers."

"I'm the last of the first ones." Ondrel's unfixed eyes found him again. "And you, boy, are the key they were keeping me alive to describe. But we'll come to that. First — the carver should understand what's being taken from her. She deserves to see the shape of the theft."

"What theft?" Beatrix said. "I'm nobody. I've spent twenty years being nobody. I don't have anything worth—"

"Lullaby," Ondrel said simply. "Sing your grandmother's lullaby."

"I don't remember any lullaby."

"You do. Sing it."

And because he was dying and had been kind about it, she opened her mouth to refuse — and the refusal came out as sound instead.

It was a tune. It rose in her throat without her permission, six notes, seven, and then it broke. Not ended — *broke*, the way a sentence breaks when someone tears the page. There was a hole in the middle of it where a phrase should have been, and around the hole the remaining notes leaned inward like people crowding toward an accident. She tried again. Same shape, same gap. Her hands went cold on her knees.

"That's not how it goes," she heard herself say. "I've known that song my whole life. That's not—" She stopped. She had known it her whole life, and she could not have said when she'd last sung it, and now she understood why: because every time she reached for it, something had already been there ahead of her, eating.

"There's a verse missing," she said. "There's a whole verse missing and I never noticed."

"No one ever notices," Ondrel said softly. "That's the craft of it. A tithe, not a robbery. One memory in ten, thinned so thin the host feels nothing but a small unease they blame on weather. Multiply that by everyone who carries the blood, across a hundred years, and you have a country held in permanent erasure — paid for, forever, by the descendants of the people it belonged to." He drew a breath that seemed to cost him. "You, carver, are rich beyond most. Twenty years of hearing the dead has kept your memories dense, well-worn, hard to thin quietly. Which is why they've started coming to your door."

* * *

She made him show her. It was the worst thing she did that day, and she did it deliberately, because Marguerite-be-damned if she was going to spend her past without counting it.

Ondrel had her close her eyes and walk backward through her own history, naming things: her mother's voice, her first stall, the smell of the tannery row, the winter the river froze solid. Most of it came whole. But here and there a memory surfaced wrong — a name missing from a face, a place with no location, an event with no year. Her mother teaching her to carve: present, vivid, complete. Her mother's funeral: a room, mourners, and a blank where the reason for gathering should have been. She had always assumed grief had eaten that part naturally. Grief did eat things. That was allowed.

But grief left edges. This was smooth. Sanded. *Harvested.*

"They took my mother's death from me," she said, when she could speak again. "They took the day itself. I was there. I know I was there. And they took—"

"They took the Vellamark out of it," Ondrel said. "Your mother died on a day that mattered to the old calendar. The Feast of Returning Bones, it was called, in the old capital. Any memory anchored to the old country is priority harvest. They will have taken yours within the week of your mourning, because fresh memories feed best." His voice gentled. "I'm sorry. I truly am. But you needed to feel the shape of the wound, because in four days you're going to start making wounds of your own, on purpose, and you should know exactly what you're spending."

"Four days?"

"The Door," Isandro said quietly. He had barely spoken through all of it, and when Beatrix looked at him she saw something she hadn't seen before: recognition. He was hearing this story the way you hear a story you were told as a child. "The book says the Door of Unmaking stands where the oldest bones lie. I thought that meant a graveyard. A battlefield. But—"

"It means the catacombs under Aldenmere," Ondrel said. "The drowned capital. Three hundred years of Vellamark's dead lie stacked beneath the flood line, older than the erasure, older than the Custodians' charter. Bone remembers what flesh is made to forget — ask your carver, she knows. The Door was built into the deepest ossuary because bone is the one archive they cannot enter. Memory lives in the living; bone keeps only what cannot be eaten." He smiled again, that ruined gentleness. "Which is why they hate carvers. And why they'll burn this shop by nightfall, so let's be quick."

* * *

He gave them what he had while the light climbed and his breathing worsened: a route through the hill country, the name of a village where the old tongue survived in dreams, and a warning — the Door opens two ways, and both ways cost, and he was too near the end to explain more than that the book itself would explain when the time came.

Then he asked Beatrix for a favor, and it was the favor that undid her.

"Carve me," he said. "Not my face. My ledger. Everything I just told you, cut into bone, small enough to hide in a shoe. Because when I die tonight, the Custodians will take what's left of my memories within the hour — it's how they clean up after their carriers. Everything I am will go into the erasure. Unless it's already outside me, in something they can't eat." His unfixed eyes held hers. "You hear the dead, they say. Well. Make sure I'm one of the ones worth hearing."

She carved him through the afternoon while Isandro watched the street. She worked fast and small, the way she'd learned to work luck-charms, but this was no charm: the tithe system, the harvest schedule, the Feast of Returning Bones, the catacombs of Aldenmere, the Door and its double price. Her knife moved and her grief moved with it, and somewhere in the second hour she stopped carving facts and started carving the lullaby — the six broken notes and the hole where the seventh phrase had been, scored into the rib-bone as a gap, an absence given permanent shape. *This much was taken,* the gap said. *Count it.*

When she finished, Ondrel held the little bone ledger in both hands like a bird.

"A hundred years," he murmured, "and the first new record anyone's bothered to make." He looked up at her, and for a moment his eyes fixed — truly fixed — on her face. "One more thing, carver, and then you should run. The Custodians didn't come to your stall asking questions because you're interesting. They came because you're *scheduled*. Your tithe is due. There's a list, and your name moved to the top the moment the stranger touched your counter." He pressed the bone back into her hand. "They won't thin you this time. Rich carriers get emptied whole, at the end, when the erasure runs hungry. And child — it has been running hungry for a month."

Outside, the bells of the evening market began to ring, and beneath them, faint and wrong, Beatrix heard hooves.

Isandro was already at the door. "Back way?" he asked.

"Back way," she said, and closed her hand around the bones of a dead man's testimony, and ran.
