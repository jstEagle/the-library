## 5. The Ossuary Road

The road north smelled of rain and old stone.

Isandro felt it before he understood it — a pull in his chest like a fishhook set behind the ribs, drawing him toward the drowned capital. Beatrix walked beside him with her pack riding high on her shoulders and her jaw set in that way he was learning meant she'd made a decision and dared him to question it. Three days out of Vellamark's edge-lands, if you could call them that, if you could call any of this country real, and she hadn't turned back once.

"You keep touching your collarbone," she said.

He dropped his hand. "Habit."

"It's not a habit. You've done it at every crossroads since we left." She didn't look at him when she spoke; she watched the road, the hedgerows, the sky for riders. Twenty years of keeping her head down had built her into a creature of exits. "You're listening to something."

"The bones," he said, because it was true, and because the truth was easier than the whole truth. "The old ones under the fields. They pull north."

"Everything pulls north," Beatrix said grimly. "That's the problem."

They walked. The country here was wrong in ways only they seemed to notice — a bridge over a river that ran dry on one bank and full on the other, a churchyard where every grave bore the same date, a hillside of sheep that never once looked at them. The erasure was thinning. Isandro could feel it the way you feel weather coming in an old wound: the world around them fraying at its seams, memory bleeding through like water through plaster.

By dusk of the third day they came down into a valley where smoke rose from chimneys in straight, unhurried columns, and a village waited with its lamps already lit.

* * *

The place was called Hesselmere on the signposts, but the signpost leaned at an angle that suggested no one had read it in years. The innkeeper was a broad woman named Dagny who took their coin without counting it and fed them barley soup and dark bread without asking a single question, which Isandro found more unsettling than interrogation would have been.

It wasn't until well past midnight that he understood why the village had felt familiar from the first step.

He woke to voices.

Not arguing voices — singing, almost. Low and wandering, rising and falling in patterns that snagged on something deep in him. He dressed in the dark and followed the sound down the stairs and out into the inn's yard, where four of the village elders sat on a bench beneath the eaves in their nightclothes, eyes closed, speaking in their sleep.

Speaking Vellamarkish.

Isandro stopped breathing. The language poured out of them — halting, half-drowned, mixed through with words of the conqueror tongue, but unmistakable. An old man murmured something about the tide-gates at Sarnholt. A woman with hands knotted by age repeated, over and over, a child's name: *Liesl, Liesl, come in from the water-meadow.* And the fourth elder, the oldest, was singing — a boat-song, a lullaby about the moon pulling the harbor home, and Isandro knew it. He knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. His mother had sung it to him in a room with a blue door, in a city that no longer existed, a hundred years ago.

"Say something to them," Beatrix said quietly behind him.

He hadn't heard her come down. She stood in the doorway wrapped in her cloak, watchful, her breath fogging in the cold.

"They wouldn't understand me awake," he said. "They don't know what they're carrying."

"Try anyway."

So he stepped into the yard, into the cold, and he answered the old woman in her own sleeping tongue. *Liesl is safe,* he said softly, in Vellamarkish. *She came in from the water-meadow long ago. She grew old under a kind sky.*

The old woman's face changed. Not waking — settling. Her mouth curved, and tears slid from beneath her closed lids, and she murmured, *Thank you, thank you,* and then drifted into silence, her hands going still in her lap.

The other three quieted too, one by one, as though his voice were a hand laid gently over each of them. The yard fell silent except for the wind in the eaves.

When Isandro turned, Beatrix was looking at him with an expression he had dreaded since the day they met.

"You speak it fluently," she said. "Not learned-fluently. Bone-fluently. You corrected her grammar in your head before you spoke — I saw it. Nobody corrects a dead language in their head unless it was never dead to them."

"The book taught me—"

"The book doesn't teach accent." She came closer, and her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "You sang along with that lullaby. Under your breath. Before you decided to speak. Your body knows that song, Isandro. I carve bone for a living. I know what remembering looks like, even when the rememberer won't admit it."

The hook behind his ribs pulled north. He touched his collarbone.

"I'm tired," he said. "We should sleep. We leave at first light."

* * *

They left at first light, but the valley held them one more day, because the oldest elder — a tiny woman called Maren who claimed a hundred and two years and looked every season of it — asked Beatrix to look at her hip.

"The ache," Maren said, sitting in her kitchen while rain ticked against the shutters. "Sixty years the doctors have shrugged at it. But you're a bone-woman. I can tell. You have the look of someone who listens."

Beatrix glanced at Isandro. He gave the smallest nod. They had agreed — after the Custodians, after the tithe, after everything — that her gift would not go back into hiding. Small magic, but hers.

She took Maren's hand and pressed her palm flat against the old woman's hip through the wool skirt, and Isandro watched her face do the thing it did: the slight unfocusing, the listening. Then her eyes opened wide.

"There's a name in there," Beatrix said slowly. "Under the ache. The bone remembers a name it's been told to forget. *Mareike.* Your mother was Mareike, and she was born—" Beatrix stopped. Looked up. "Born in Vellamark."

Maren's teacup rattled against its saucer. "There's no such place," she said automatically, the way a person recites a lesson. "Never was. My mother was born in Hesselmere."

"Your mother was born in Sarnholt," Beatrix said gently, "on the coast, and she sang to you in a language you've been dreaming ever since, and someone made you forget, and your hip has been grieving for sixty years because grief has to go somewhere." She released the old woman's hand. "I can't fix it. But I can carve it. If I carve her name into bone and you keep it in your house, the ache may quiet. Memory likes having somewhere to live."

Maren wept without noise, the way very old people do, and nodded.

While Beatrix worked — a small piece of antler, her knife moving with the speed of long practice — Isandro sat by the fire and let himself feel the terrible warmth of the kitchen. This was what he had been kept outside of. Not the Door, not the prophecy, not the great machinery of erasure — this. Kitchens. Old women. Names carried in hips and dreams. A hundred years of scattered people carrying splinters of his country inside them without knowing, and every one of them a small miracle of survival, and every one of them a wound.

"You're doing it again," Beatrix said without looking up from her carving.

"Doing what?"

"Deciding things alone." She blew bone dust from the antler. "Whatever you're not telling me, you've started saying goodbye to me in your head. I've watched my mother do it. I've done it myself. It has a look."

"Maren should hang the carving above her bed," Isandro said. "Facing east."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have today."

She held his gaze for a long moment, and he saw her decide — visibly, deliberately — to let it go. For now. He loved her a little for that, and hated himself more for what the letting-go would cost her later.

* * *

They left Hesselmere on the fourth morning, and the Custodian riders came on the fifth night.

Isandro woke to orange light on the ceiling of the barn where they'd sheltered, three miles south of the village, and knew before he reached the door. Fire. Back the way they came. He stood in the cold dark watching the glow pulse above the hills and felt Beatrix come to stand beside him.

"The dream-records," she said. Her voice was hollow. "Maren's records. Every family keeps them — the sleep-talk, written down, generation after generation. They don't even know why they keep them. The Custodians will burn every page."

"Yes."

"And the elders?"

"Custodians don't kill," Isandro said. "Killing leaves bodies, and bodies leave questions. They'll take memories. Maren won't remember her mother's name by morning. The ache in her hip will stop, and she'll call it a blessing."

Beatrix's hands curled into fists at her sides. "And we led them there. We walked into that valley carrying the book, carrying *you*, and they followed us like wolves follow blood."

"We didn't lead them," Isandro said. "They were always coming. The erasure is starving — the Door is close, and it's hungry, and it's pulling its Custodians toward anyone who still carries Vellamark. That village has been dying by inches for a century. We just made it faster."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No. It's supposed to make you angry enough to keep walking."

She laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it — and then pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. When she lowered it, her face had gone hard and clean, the face of a woman converting grief into fuel.

"Then we walk," she said. "And Isandro — whatever you're carrying, whatever you keep touching your collarbone about, you'll tell me before the Door. Not after. Before. Because I've spent enough of my life being managed by people who thought they were protecting me, and I will not be managed by you."

Above the hills, the firelight began to fade as the riders moved on, patient as tide.

Isandro touched his collarbone, where the hook pulled north, harder now, almost cruel. Almost home.

"All right," he said. "Before the Door."

But in his pack, the prophecy book had grown warm against his spine, and when he slept that night he dreamed of a blue door, and his mother's voice singing, and a page turning to reveal writing that was not yet dry.
