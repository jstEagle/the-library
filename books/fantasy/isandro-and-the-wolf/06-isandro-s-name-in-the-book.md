## 6. Isandro's Name in the Book

The chapel had no roof left, only ribs of it — black beams gone soft as old horn, holding up nothing but weather. Beatrix stood in the doorway and felt the place before she saw it: a low hum in her back teeth, the way bones sang when they'd been waiting a long time to be asked something.

"Ossuary chapel," Isandro said behind her. "They built these over the bone-houses. The living prayed upstairs. The dead kept the foundation."

"You know a lot about a country that doesn't exist."

"I told you. I read."

She didn't look at him. Three days on the road since the village with the dreaming elders, three days of him speaking Vellamarkish in his sleep and denying it awake, and she had stopped arguing and started watching. A bone-carver learns patience the way other people learn prayer.

Inside, the nave was ankle-deep in leaf-rot and pigeon filth. Along both walls ran stone niches, hundreds of them, most collapsed, some still sealed. In the ones that had fallen open, skulls sat in rows like an audience that had outlasted the play. She had carved skulls for forty years. She knew what they looked like when they had something to say.

This one said it all at once.

The hum went through her like cold water. Every skull in the wall, every fragment in the rubble, all of them turning toward her — not literally, bones don't turn, but *toward*, the way a room turns when someone says your name. And under the hum, faint as a voice through a wall, a word repeated so many times it had worn smooth:

*Vellamark. Vellamark. Vellamark.*

"They remember," she said. Her voice came out strange. "All of them. They're still —"

"Don't touch them yet." Isandro moved past her into the aisle, and the skulls' attention shifted with him, a tide following the moon. "There's something here I have to find first. The book said —"

"The book." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "Of course. The book said."

He stopped. Turned. And there was something in his face she hadn't seen before, something that looked almost like apology, and her stomach dropped twenty years of careful silence in one long fall.

"What did the book say, Isandro?"

"It's in the crypt. Under the altar. But you should sit down."

"I've been walking for three days. I'll stand."

* * *

The altar was a slab of green-veined marble, cracked down the middle, and beneath it a stair went down into air that tasted of chalk and centuries. He lit a candle stub from his pack. The flame bent toward the dark as if being pulled.

The crypt was small. Bone niches again, but these were different — these had names. Not carved. *Inked*, in a hand she knew better than her own heartbeat, because she had spent two nights hating it: the same script that filled the prophecy book, page after page of dates and deaths and one line about a luck-charm sold at a market stall.

And in the center of the floor, on a stone bier, lay a single skeleton, whole and articulated, wired together with copper at the joints. Someone had loved this dead person enough to keep them standing, once.

Isandro set the candle down. "Open the book," he said. "To the last written page. Not tonight's date. The one before it."

Her hands were steady. Twenty years of practice; her hands were always steady. She took the book out of its oilcloth and turned past tomorrow's date, past the blankness beyond it, back one page.

The date at the top was a hundred years old. She knew the style of the numerals now; she'd learned to read this book's calendar the way you learn a neighbor's accent. Below the date, in that same patient ink, a single entry:

*The last child born under the Vellamarkish sky is carried out through the eastern pass. His name is Isandro. He will be kept. He will be the key.*

She read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change. Words never changed; that was the whole point of words, the thing she loved about them, the thing that made carving names into bone worth doing.

"His name is Isandro," she read aloud, because if she said it in her own voice it couldn't own her. "Dated one hundred years ago."

"Yes."

"You were born here."

"Yes."

"Before the erasure."

"Yes."

"And you let me believe —" Her voice cracked on it, and she hated the crack more than the betrayal. "You stood in my stall and bought a charm and lied to me with your whole face. You watched me find my country in that book like it was news to you. You watched a Custodian take a piece of my childhood out of my head and you *knew* —"

"I knew what the book needed you to feel." He said it flat, no armor on it, which was somehow worse. "Beatrix. If I had told you what I was, you would have run. Not from me. From the book. From the shape of your own life having been written by someone else. You'd have burned it and gone back to small charms and keeping your head down, and you'd have been right to, and Vellamark would have finished dying with no one outside to say it lived."

"That's not your choice to make for me."

"No." He looked at the wired skeleton on the bier. "It was hers."

* * *

"She was the archivist," he said. "Not the dying man in Corrow Lane — he was her apprentice, third generation removed. This is Maren Ostrevna, Keeper of the Record of Vellamark. When the Custodians came to unmake the country, she couldn't stop them. Erasure isn't fought with armies. So she did the only thing left. She wrote."

"The book."

"The book. Every date, every death, every small event — your charm sale, Beatrix, your mother's voice warning you about me — she wrote it a century ago, because she had seen the pattern of the erasure and she knew memory could be smuggled out inside a story. Stories are the one container the Custodians can't fully strip. They feed on memory held in minds. Ink held in paper, carving held in bone — those slip the net. That's why your craft matters. That's why *you* matter."

"Stop." She pressed her knuckles against her mouth. "Stop telling me why I matter. Tell me why you let me walk into it blind."

"Because the book required it." He crouched by the bier, gentle as a man approaching a sleeping animal. "Maren wrote me too. I was four years old. My parents put me in a cart with a family fleeing east, and Maren paid them to keep going and never tell me what I was. She wrote that I would be kept outside the erasure — that a country being unwritten needs one citizen left standing outside the sentence, or there's no one left to appeal it. I'm not a man, Beatrix. I'm a loophole. A hundred and four years old, kept alive by the same trick that keeps this book — a life written down can't be fully erased. I am the last line of Vellamark's census, walking around in boots."

The candle flame leaned hard. Somewhere above them, in the ruined nave, the skulls had gone silent — the specific silence of listeners.

"And our meeting?" she said. "The market. The charm. Was that in her book too?"

He met her eyes. "Page one."

She thought of it then — she had never actually read page one; she'd started where the dates began to matter. Her hands moved before her mind agreed, turning the brittle leaves back, back, to the beginning, and there it was in Maren Ostrevna's careful hand:

*A bone-carver who hears the dead will sell a luck-charm to a nervous stranger. Do not let her learn his name until the road has taught her to need him. She will hate this page. Let her.*

Beatrix closed the book very slowly, the way you close a door on a room where someone has just died.

"So it wasn't chance."

"No."

"My mother's voice — the warning —"

"Written. A hundred years before she was born."

"Then none of it was mine. The dread, the road, the —" She couldn't say *you*. She said instead: "I'm a character. I've been a character since before my grandmother's grandmother."

"No." For the first time his voice rose, and the skulls hummed with it. "You're the reader. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. Maren wrote the road, but she couldn't write what you'd do on it. She wrote that you'd open the Door or fail to. She wrote that you'd spend your memories or hoard them. She wrote possibilities, Beatrix, the way a carver lays out blanks of bone — the shape was hers, but the cutting is yours. That's why the last pages are blank past tomorrow. No one knows how it ends. Not even her."

"Except the Custodians are coming," she said, "and we're standing in a hole full of the dead, and I have just learned my entire life is a plot."

"Yes."

"And you're a hundred and four years old."

"Yes."

She looked at him — really looked, for the first time since the market — and saw it, the thing she'd been refusing to see since the village: the slight wrongness of him, the way firelight sat on him like it was remembering how. A man kept alive by ink. A boy of four, filed under *will be kept*.

She should have hated him. She reached for the hatred and found it tangled through with something else, something worse: recognition. She had spent twenty years making herself small so the world wouldn't notice her magic. He had spent a hundred years making himself nothing so a country could keep one witness. They were the same cowardice, worn for the same reason, and she could no more despise him for it than she could despise her own reflection.

"I'm furious with you," she said.

"I know."

"I'm going to stay furious. It's the only part of this that's mine."

Something moved in his face — relief, maybe, or grief wearing relief's clothes. "Keep it," he said. "Spend it however you like."

Above them, the silence of the skulls broke all at once, and the hum came back changed — no longer *Vellamark, Vellamark*, but a new word, urgent, passed skull to skull down the walls like a bucket-line against fire:

*Riders. Riders. Riders.*

Beatrix blew out the candle.
