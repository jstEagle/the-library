## 7. The Price of the Threshold

The capital had drowned a century before it was erased, and the water never left.

Isandro felt it through his boots first — that deep, patient cold rising from the flooded streets below the catacombs, the city's drowned heart still beating somewhere under black water. Above them, Vellamark's memory-sky was fraying at the edges. He'd been watching it for three days now: stars going out one by one, not like dying but like being *edited*, the constellations rearranged into shapes that belonged to other countries' myths.

Beatrix climbed down the last of the ossuary stairs behind him, her lamp held high, her face bone-pale in its light. She hadn't spoken since the chapel. Since his name. He could feel her silence against his back like a blade laid flat between his shoulder blades.

"You could have told me," she said finally, on the last step. Not angry anymore. Worse than angry — *tired*.

"Yes," he said. "I could have."

"That's not an apology."

"It wasn't meant to be." He turned to face her in the narrow stair, the walls around them packed with niches, each one holding a skull whose eye sockets were stuffed with centuries of dark. "It was meant to be true. I'm not sorry, Beatrix. If I'd told you what I was, you'd have run. And if you'd run, you'd be dead with everyone else when this place finishes expiring."

"And instead I get to watch it happen up close."

"Instead," he said, "you get a choice. That's more than anyone else in Vellamark ever got."

She looked past him then, and he turned too, and there it was.

The Door of Unmaking stood at the end of the great gallery, where the catacomb corridor opened into a vault carved from the living rock of the old capital's foundation. It was smaller than Isandro had imagined all his life. Smaller than the book promised. Just a door — oak gone grey as driftwood, iron hinges green with age, standing free in the middle of the floor with nothing behind it but stone. No frame in a wall. A door to nowhere, waiting.

Around it, the floor was inscribed with a ring of text so worn it was barely legible, and beyond the ring, the niches began again — thousands of them, tens of thousands, climbing the vault walls into the dark. Every skull in this chamber faced inward. Toward the Door. A hundred generations of the dead, arranged like an audience.

Or like witnesses.

"The oldest bones," Beatrix whispered. "The archivist was right. This is where they brought them. The founding dead."

"They built the country on top of its ancestors," Isandro said. "Literally. Every street above us, every market, every church — all of it resting on this." He stepped toward the ring of script. His hands were shaking. He noticed, distantly, the way you notice weather. "The book said the Door opens only here because here is the only place the erasure can't reach. You can't unmake what's already been given back."

He knelt at the ring's edge and pressed his palm flat against the inscription.

Nothing happened.

For a long moment he knelt there, waiting, and the silence of the vault filled up around him like water filling a lung. Then, faintly — so faintly he might have invented it — he heard the Door breathe. A slow exhalation through the gap beneath the oak, carrying a smell that stopped his heart: woodsmoke and river-mud and rye bread, his grandmother's kitchen, a morning a hundred years gone.

"Isandro?" Beatrix's voice came from very far away.

"There's a price," he heard himself say. His own voice sounded like someone else's. Like the book reading itself aloud through his mouth. "Of course there's a price. There was always going to be a price."

* * *

It took him an hour to work it out from the ring of script, kneeling in the dust with the lamp while Beatrix read over his shoulder, translating the older dialect faster than he could. She was better with dead languages than he was. She'd spent twenty years talking to the dead; he'd spent a hundred years avoiding them.

"The Door doesn't break the erasure," she said slowly, tracing a line of glyphs with her finger. "It *feeds* it. That's what this says. The erasure isn't a wound — it's a mouth. It ate Vellamark once, and it stays open on what it ate, and it hungers. To open the Door from inside—" she stopped. Started again. "You can either feed it something it wants more than what it already has, or you can give it the one thing that anchors it shut."

"Which is?"

"Me," Isandro said. "Or you."

Beatrix went very still. In the lamplight her face did the thing it did when she was carving — total attention, every stray thought burned away. "Say it plainly."

"The Door opens two ways." He kept his voice level. He'd rehearsed this in his head for a hundred years without knowing he was rehearsing. "First: someone who carries Vellamark inside them surrenders every memory of it. Willingly. All of it. The erasure drinks those memories and the Door swings wide, and the person walks through whole but hollowed — no grandmother, no mother, no lullabies, no twenty years of small charms. Nothing. They leave as a stranger to their own life."

"And the second way?"

"The last citizen gives himself back." He met her eyes. "Me, Beatrix. The anchor of the erasure is me. The one child carried out. The country was erased *around* my absence — I'm the hole it was cut from, and I'm the thread still stitched through it. If I walk back into it — willingly, fully — the memory-land gets its final inhabitant, the erasure completes itself, and the Door stands open behind me long enough for one person to walk out."

"One person," Beatrix repeated.

"One person."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere far above them, or perhaps not above at all, something groaned — the sound of a sky being taken apart.

"No," Beatrix said.

"It's arithmetic—"

"Don't you dare call it arithmetic." She grabbed his collar, pulled him close, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "I've done the arithmetic my whole life. Keep your magic small, keep your head down, spend nothing, lose nothing. Do you know what that adds up to? Twenty years alone in a stall selling luck to people who don't know why they're unlucky. That's the sum. That's the total. And you want to stand here and tell me the answer is *you walk back into oblivion and I walk out and live*?" Her grip tightened. "That's not arithmetic. That's just grief wearing a clever mask."

"Then what would you have me do?" He didn't pull away. "Feed it your memories? All of them? Your mother's voice — which you've told me you barely remember hearing anyway? Your grandmother? Everything you carved, everything you survived, everything that makes you *you*? Walk out of here alive and complete and empty?" He shook his head slowly. "I've been dead for a hundred years, Beatrix. I just didn't lie down. You'd be spending a life. I'd be spending an afterlife. It's not even a fair trade."

"That's not yours to decide."

"No," he agreed quietly. "But it is mine to offer."

* * *

They heard the Custodians before they saw them — not footsteps but a kind of *absence* of sound, a moving pocket of hush descending the ossuary stairs, eating the echo ahead of them. Isandro knew that silence. He'd grown up in the last year of Vellamark listening to the Custodians' predecessors harvest the dying country's records, its songs, its names, feeding the mouth that would eventually eat it all.

"Lamp," he breathed.

Beatrix killed the flame. Darkness dropped over the vault like a lid — total, absolute, broken only by the faint grey seep of the Door itself, which glowed now, faintly, like a thing remembering fire.

Six figures resolved at the top of the stairs. Tall, robed in grey that seemed woven from fog, faces smooth as eggs where faces should be. They did not descend quickly. They never needed to hurry. They moved down into the vault the way frost moves across a window.

"They'll strip you," Isandro murmured, his lips at her ear. "Whatever you've kept. Whatever's left. They'll take it and feed the erasure and the resealing will be permanent."

"Then we choose fast," she breathed back.

The lead Custodian reached the bottom of the stairs and paused. Turned its smooth face — slowly, deliberately — toward the Door. Toward Isandro.

And spoke. Its voice was the sound of a page being torn out.

***Last citizen.*** It extended one grey hand, palm up, an invitation a hundred years in the making. ***Come home.***

Everything in Isandro went quiet. Not calm — quiet. The difference mattered. Calm was peace. Quiet was the moment on a cliff edge when your body understands before you do.

Behind him he felt Beatrix move — heard the soft scrape of her knife coming out of her belt, the whisper of bone against leather. Her smallest knife. The one she used for fine carving. For fine work.

"I'm not letting them take you," she said, low and fierce, "and I'm not letting you take yourself. So we're going to do this my way, Isandro. We're going to do it together, or—"

The Custodians took a step forward. The Door exhaled woodsmoke and river-mud. And in the grey glow, Isandro saw the final page of the prophecy book written plain in the air between them, the ink still wet after a hundred years:

*A door, a debt, and a woman who carves.*

He took Beatrix's hand.

"Then listen fast," he said, "because I think I know how we cheat the arithmetic — and it starts with you giving me something instead of losing it."

Above them, the sky finished going out.
