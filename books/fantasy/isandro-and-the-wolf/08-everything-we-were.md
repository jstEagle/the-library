## 8. Everything We Were

The Custodians came down the catacomb stairs like water finding a crack — patient, inevitable, filling every gap.

Beatrix heard them before she saw them: the soft scrape of grey robes on old stone, and beneath it that sound she would never forget now, the sound of a memory being swallowed. A wet, satisfied quiet. Somewhere above them, in the dark of the ossuary's upper galleries, one of her pursuers was eating someone's childhood.

"They've found the other entrance," Isandro said. He stood with his palm flat against the Door, which was not a door at all when you looked at it straight — only a wall of fused bone, femurs and ribs and skulls pressed together so tightly they had become something else, something like amber, something like glass. "We have minutes."

"Then tell me again what it wants." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. That was the bone-carver's trick, the one thing twenty years of small magic had taught her: your hands can shake as long as your voice doesn't.

"Memory," he said. "Given freely. Not taken — taken is what *they* do, and it feeds the erasure instead of starving it. It has to be surrendered. Willingly. Completely." He turned to look at her, and his face in the bone-light was terrible with what he wasn't saying. "It doesn't have to be you, Bea. There's another way—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "I know what the other way is. I've known since the archivist died telling us. The last citizen gives himself back." She looked at him — at this man who had walked out of a book written before her grandmother was born, who spoke Vellamarkish in his sleep, whose name sat in that prophecy like a stone in a shoe. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. Because I have an idea, and it's either brilliant or it's the stupidest thing I've ever done, and I've done some very stupid things for money."

The Custodians were closer. She could hear them murmuring now, that liturgical drone, words like *unregistered* and *residual* and *tithe*, words that meant *yours, we want yours.*

"Tell me," Isandro said.

She unslung her kit from her shoulder and knelt on the cold floor, laying out her tools the way she'd laid them out ten thousand mornings at her market stall: the fine gravers, the burins, the little saw, the blocks of bone wrapped in wool. Cow bone mostly, in the old days. Sometimes antler. Once, memorably, a saint's finger, though that was a story she'd never told anyone.

"The Door eats memory," she said. "Fine. I'm a woman who makes a living turning nothing into something. So I'll feed it. But I'm not going to let it take anything whole." She picked up a block of bone and turned it in her hands. "Every memory I give it, I carve first. While I still have it. While it's still *mine*. The carving stays even after the remembering goes. Do you understand? They want to be the only keepers of what Vellamark was. I'm going to make sure they're not."

Isandro went very still. "You'd lose all of it. Your grandmother. Your mother. Twenty years of—"

"I'd lose the remembering," she said. "Not the record. There's a difference. My mother taught me there's a difference, actually, though by tomorrow morning I won't remember that she did." Her throat closed. She worked past it. "Carve fast, Bea. We haven't got all night."

* * *

She started with the lullaby.

It was the obvious place to start because it was already half-eaten — the Custodians had gotten their teeth into it weeks ago, back in the city, and what remained was wrong the way a mirror is wrong. She could hum the melody but the words came out scrambled, her grandmother's voice singing nonsense where the old country's name should have been.

So she closed her eyes and reached for the oldest version of it she had, the one stored deepest, the one from before she understood language at all — just warmth and vibration, a chest she'd been held against, a rhythm like a second heartbeat. And she began to carve.

She didn't plan the design. That wasn't how it worked; it had never been how it worked. The dead spoke through her hands, her mother used to say, and whether that was true or just kindness didn't matter — the truth was that when Beatrix stopped deciding and started listening, the bone told her what it wanted to be. This piece wanted to be a cradle. A cradle made of reeds, floating on a river, and inside it a shape too small and too new to be anything but a child, and around the cradle a border of notes — actual notes, the seven shapes of the Vellamarkish scale, which she had never consciously learned and which poured out of her graver like water.

She sang while she carved. She had to, to hold it. The half-eaten words and the deep-stored melody, forced together, made a whole song for about ninety seconds, and in those ninety seconds her grandmother was alive in her mouth.

Then she lifted the finished carving to the Door and said, "Take it. Take the memory. Leave the record."

The Door drank.

There was no better word. The bone-glass surface rippled, and something reached through it — not a hand, not a mouth, something older than either — and took the lullaby out of her. She felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave: pressure, then absence, then a strange lightness where weight used to live. She knew, intellectually, that she had once been sung to sleep. The knowledge sat in her head like a label on an empty jar.

But the carving stayed. Cradle, river, notes. Whole.

"It worked," Isandro breathed.

The Door had changed. Where the surface had been dead glass, a seam now showed — hairline, faintly warm, running floor to ceiling like a crack in ice. From somewhere beyond it came a smell: woodsmoke and river water and bread.

And the Custodians screamed.

Not with mouths. With that drone, suddenly cracked, suddenly furious. Beatrix understood: every freely given memory starved them. Every carving was a theft from the thieves.

"Again," she said, and reached for the next memory.

* * *

Her mother's voice.

That one cost more. She knew it would, and she chose it anyway, deliberately, because if she saved the dearest things for last she would never spend them at all — she knew herself too well for that. Better to burn the treasure first, while the fire still felt like courage instead of arithmetic.

She carved her mother at the workbench. Hands first — always hands first, that was the rule of portraiture in bone, hands before faces because hands told the truth. These hands held a graver worn smooth by forty years. Then the face, tilted, listening to some customer or some ghost. Then, around her, the stall itself: the awning, the hanging charms, the crooked sign that read WINTERBOURNE — BONES, CHARMS, HONEST WORK.

"You're crying," Isandro said quietly.

"Good," she said. "Salt gets in the grain. Makes the lines hold better." It wasn't true. It didn't need to be true. Carvers were allowed their superstitions; it was practically the job description.

When the figure was finished — and it was good, it was the best thing she'd ever cut, grief had sharpened her like a whetstone — she pressed it to the seam and gave up her mother's voice. The sound of it. The particular roughness on certain vowels, the way she'd said *Bea* like two syllables, the way she'd laughed at her own jokes a beat before anyone else.

Gone. She tested herself and found a silence where the sound should be. She remembered that her mother had spoken. She could no longer hear her.

The carving held everything. The tilt of the head. The listening.

The seam in the Door widened to the width of a hand. Beyond it, impossibly, she could see evening light on water, rooftops, a bell tower she had never seen and recognized anyway.

* * *

They came for her in the twelfth hour.

Not the Custodians — those were still fighting their way down through galleries full of bones that had begun, subtly, to resist them, because every carving Beatrix finished seemed to wake the ossuary a little more. No: what came for her was the cost itself. She was on her eleventh piece — twenty years of charms, each one a small story, the luck-knot for the fisherman's daughter, the mourning-bead for the drowned boy, the fertility figure that had failed and the one that hadn't — and her own history was coming apart in her hands faster than she could render it.

She couldn't remember her stall's location. She couldn't remember the color of her first knife. She was losing the connective tissue, the ordinary days between the extraordinary ones, and without them the extraordinary ones floated loose like beads off a snapped string.

"Stop," Isandro said. He crouched beside her, and his hands hovered over hers without touching. "Bea. You've opened it enough. Look at it."

She looked. The seam was wide enough for a person now. Through it, Vellamark burned gold in its last remembered sunset, and she could hear — faintly, like music through a wall — a market. Crowds. Life. A hundred years dead and still going, inside its own expiring memory.

"One more," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her. "One more, and it stays open. I can feel it. It's not sealed anymore, it's just... leaning. Like a door someone's holding shut from the other side."

"That's exactly what it is," Isandro said, very softly. "Someone is holding it shut from the other side. And I think we both know who has to walk over there and ask them to stop."

"No." She grabbed his wrist. Her fingers left bone-dust prints on his skin. "No, there has to be another—"

"There isn't, and you know the arithmetic better than I do." He smiled, and it broke her heart, because it was the smile of a man reading his own final page. "But here's what you don't know. Here's what I figured out while you were carving, while you were doing the bravest thing I've ever watched anyone do." He leaned close. "The erasure doesn't just eat. It keeps. Everything it takes — every memory, every name, every song — it's all still in there, Bea. All of it. Nothing is lost. It's only locked."

From the stairwell above, the Custodians' drone turned triumphant. Light bloomed at the top of the gallery — grey light, hungry light.

"So finish your last carving," Isandro said, rising, drawing the prophecy book from his coat. "Make it count. Because when I go through that door, I'm going to find every single thing they stole — and I'm going to spend whatever I am getting it back to you."

He stepped toward the seam. Behind him, the Custodians began to descend.
