## 9. The Last Citizen

The Door was open a hand's width, and through the gap came the smell of rain on old stone.

Isandro had not smelled that in a hundred years. He stood in the ossuary beneath the drowned capital with Beatrix's blood-warm carvings scattered at his feet — each one a memory she had spent like coin, rendered in bone so the Custodians could not be the only ones who kept accounts — and he understood, with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own name in a ledger, what the Door required to stay open.

It did not want her memories. She had fed it those, and it had drunk them down and strained against its seal like a starving animal against a fence. But hunger is not satiation. The erasure was an equation, and equations demand balance. A country had been subtracted from the world; something of that country had to be added back, or the sum would right itself, the Door would close, and every bone Beatrix had carved would become just bone.

The book had known. The book had always known. He could feel it in the satchel against his hip, heavier now than it had ever been, its final pages trembling like a bird's throat.

"Isandro." Beatrix's voice came from the dark behind him. She was leaning against a catacomb wall, gray-faced, hollowed out by everything she'd given. Her hands — those precise, patient carver's hands — were shaking. "The Custodians are coming. I can hear them eating their way down."

"I know."

"Then we go through. Now. While it's open."

He turned to look at her. Beyond her shoulder, up the stair they'd descended three days ago, torchlight flickered — the pale, greedy light the Custodians carried, the light that ate. And beyond *him*, through the gap in the Door, Vellamark waited: not the real country, which had been dead for a century, but its memory-land, the vast fading dream where everything erased went to thin out slowly, like ink in water.

"It won't hold," he said. "The opening. It'll reseal behind us before you're ten steps clear."

"Then we run faster."

"Beatrix." He said her name gently, because what he had to say next deserved gentleness, and because he had rehearsed it for a hundred years without ever once saying it aloud. "I'm the anchor. I told you I was born here. I never told you what that means."

* * *

She refused to understand him at first. He watched her refuse it — watched her jaw set in the stubborn line he'd come to know across five hundred miles of bad road, watched her reach for logic the way she reached for a knife.

"The Door takes memories," she said. "You've seen me pay. It doesn't take people."

"It took a whole country."

"That was different—"

"It was exactly this." He crouched and picked up one of her carvings: a small bone charm shaped like a lark, the first thing she'd spent, her grandmother's lullaby given form. It hummed faintly against his palm, grieving. "Someone has to go back into the erasure and *be* Vellamark while the Door stands open. One citizen, standing inside, holding the doorframe from the other side. That's why I was kept outside. That's why the book found me. I'm not the key, Beatrix. I'm the counterweight."

"You're a man," she said fiercely. "You're a man who sells bad luck-charms and can't cook rice and hums in his sleep in a language he claims not to speak—"

"I speak it fine," he said. "I always have."

That stopped her. He saw the suspicion from the ossuary road come home to roost in her face — all the deflections, all the fluent nights among the dreaming elders, every question she'd swallowed. She had known. Some part of her had known since the village, and she had chosen not to look at it, the same way she'd kept her magic small for twenty years. Choosing not to see was its own kind of survival.

"If you step through," she said quietly, "you stop existing."

"I stop existing *out there*," he corrected. "In there—" he nodded at the gap, at the smell of rain on stone, "—I exist completely. For as long as the memory-land lasts. Which, with the Door open and the erasure fed by a living citizen..." He shrugged. "Longer than it would have. Maybe much longer. Someone will be home, Beatrix. That's more than most erased countries get."

"And when it finally fades? When the last of it goes?"

"Then I'll have been the last citizen," he said, "and it will have mattered that someone was."

* * *

The Custodians' light was climbing down the stairwell now, and with it came the sound he hated most in the world: the soft wet sound of memories being chewed. They had eaten their way through the archivist's dying words, through the village's dream-records, through twenty years of Beatrix's careful small life, and they were coming for the rest.

There was no time for the goodbye either of them deserved. So Isandro did the only thing left to do.

He opened the book.

Not to the prophecy pages — to the blank ones at the back, the ones that had been filling themselves in, slowly, all this journey, in handwriting that was neither his nor hers. And as the pages fell open, the ossuary fell away.

They stood in an evening.

It was the market square of a town neither of them had ever visited, in a season that no longer existed, under a sky the color of ripe plums. Stalls were folding up for the night. A woman laughed somewhere out of sight. The air smelled of woodsmoke and frying onions and the particular green cold of a Vellamarkish autumn, and every person passing them wore a face Beatrix had never seen and somehow recognized — the broad cheekbones, the dark eyes, the blood she carried without knowing its name.

"What is this?" she whispered.

"A remembered evening," Isandro said. "The book keeps one. Every erased country gets one evening that refuses to die, hidden in whatever record survives it. This is ours." He offered her his arm, absurdly formal, and after a moment she took it. "We have until the market closes. In here, that's longer than you'd think."

They walked. He showed her the fountain where children dropped buttons instead of coins, because coins were for wishes and buttons were for luck, and Beatrix made a small wounded sound and said, "That's where I got it. My mother used to say that. I thought she invented it." He showed her the shrine to the unnamed god of thresholds, where travelers tied ribbons before any journey, and she touched the post and found a ribbon already waiting, knotted by some long-dead hand, in her own favorite color.

They bought roasted chestnuts from a vendor who looked at them with the mild patience of the deeply remembered, and the chestnuts tasted exactly the way chestnuts taste in the moment you realize you will remember them forever.

"Isandro," she said, when the sky had gone from plum to bruise, "tell me your name. Your whole name. The one from the book."

"Isandro Vael Corvane, of the Corvane line, fourth district, city of Velharrow, kingdom of Vellamark." Each word landed like a stone dropped into still water. "Born the spring the Custodians came. Smuggled out at six days old. The book raised me, in a manner of speaking. It wrote itself around my cradle."

"And it sent you to my stall."

"It sent me to the one bone-carver alive who could hear the dead and keep a secret." He smiled. "It has good taste."

The market was emptying. The plum light was thinning, going gray at the edges like ash cooling. Somewhere beyond the edges of the remembered square, he could hear the Custodians again, patient as tide.

Beatrix stopped walking. She turned to face him in the middle of the emptying square, and she did not cry, though he saw how much it cost her not to, and he loved her for it — for the ferocity of her composure, for the carver's discipline that turned even grief into craft.

"Give me your hands," she said.

He gave them. She pressed something into his palm: a sliver of bone, freshly carved, no bigger than a finger joint. On it, in letters fine as hair, she had cut a single word in Vellamarkish. He read it by the fading light.

*Stay.*

"That's not fair," he said, voice cracking.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She closed his fingers over it. "But you'll carry it anyway. Everything I've spent tonight is in you now too — my grandmother, my mother, the lullaby, all of it. You're the fullest man in any world, Isandro Corvane. Go be the country. Let it be full of us."

The market was gone. They stood again in the ossuary, in torchlight and dust, and the Door groaned against its seal, and the first white-robed figure appeared at the top of the stairs.

Isandro pressed the prophecy book into her hands and folded her fingers over it the way she had folded his over the bone.

"The last page," he said. "Don't read it until you're outside. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Beatrix." He stepped backward toward the gap, toward the smell of rain on stone, and smiled at her with a hundred years of homesickness in his face. "When you carve us — and you will carve us, I know you — start with the buttons in the fountain. Start with the luck."

Then he turned and walked into Vellamark, and the Door began to close behind him, and the last thing she heard, faint through the narrowing gap, was a man humming a lullaby in a language only two people in the world had ever truly spoken.

The Door sealed. The Custodians arrived to find nothing but a woman, a book, and a scatter of humming bones.

Beatrix ran for the stairs, clutching the book to her chest, and did not look back — because looking back was their trade, not hers. Hers was carrying things forward.

Above ground, dawn was breaking over a nation that had never heard of Vellamark. She found a bench, sat down, and opened the book to the final page.

It read: *and she told them.*
