## 10. Carved in Bone

The market at Corven's Reach smelled of wet wool and frying onions, and it had never heard of Vellamark.

Beatrix learned this on her first morning there, when she asked a fruit-seller — casually, the way you'd ask about the weather — whether he'd ever traded with caravans out of Vellamark. He'd looked at her with the polite blankness people reserve for madwomen and children, and said, *Never heard of it, miss. Sounds made up.*

Made up. A country of nine hundred thousand souls, of terraced vineyards and bell-towers and a lullaby her grandmother had sung in a language that now existed only inside one woman's skull. Made up. She had smiled, thanked him, bought apples she didn't want, and walked back to the rented room above the chandler's shop where she had slept badly for three weeks.

Three weeks since the Door. Three weeks since Isandro stepped backward into the fading light of a country that was already mostly gone, and pressed the book into her hands, and said — in Vellamarkish, so the Custodians wouldn't understand, though they were all dead or scattered by then — *Tell them. That's the whole prophecy. That was always the whole prophecy.*

She had crossed the border into Corven's Reach with nothing but the clothes she wore, a satchel of carving tools, and a book whose final page had gone blank the moment she passed out of the erasure's reach. Blank, except for one line that appeared sometime during the second week, ink surfacing like a body rising through water:

*and she told them.*

She hadn't told anyone yet. That was the shameful arithmetic of survival: first you ate, then you found a bed, then you learned which streets flooded and which officials took bribes and which neighbors reported strange women to the constable. Grief kept its own schedule. For three weeks she had done nothing but survive, and every night she lay awake listening to the silence where her mother's voice used to be, and her grandmother's lullaby, and twenty years of small charms and small kindnesses — all of it spent, all of it paid into the Door's hunger, all of it rendered in bone by her own hands before the Custodians could claim it.

That was the thing she clung to. The Custodians had taken memories from thousands of people and fed them into the erasure, and those memories were gone — truly gone, unmade, no more recoverable than smoke. But hers she had carved first. Every memory she surrendered, she had spent an hour rendering into bone before she let it leave her: her grandmother's face in a pendant of sheep-bone; the lullaby notched into a rib in a script only she could read; the market stall in Vellamark where she'd first sold charms, its awning striped red and white, cut into a piece of antler no bigger than her thumb. The memories were gone from her mind. But they existed. They sat in her satchel, forty-one pieces of bone, humming faintly, the way bones do when they remember being part of something alive.

The Custodians had been keepers of forgetting. She would be a keeper of the opposite.

On the fourth Monday of her exile, she rented a stall at the edge of Corven's Reach market — the cheap end, near the tanners, where the rent was low because the smell was high — and laid out her wares on a cloth of undyed linen.

Not charms. She was done with charms. Charms were small magic for small fears, and she had watched a whole country be eaten by something no charm could touch. What she laid out instead were carvings: names, streets, songs. A bone tile bearing the word *Vellamark* in letters deep enough to survive fire. A slender pin carved with the shape of the old capital's bridge, the one that sang in the wind. A child's rattle etched with the lullaby's opening notes. Forty-one pieces, and she made more every night, working by candlelight, pulling details up out of the book — because the prophecy book, she had discovered, was generous in exile. It gave her what she asked for. Street names. Recipes. The names of kings and the jokes soldiers told. It wanted to be read aloud. It had been waiting a hundred years for someone to do exactly this.

She priced everything absurdly low. A copper bit a piece, sometimes less. People stopped, because people stop at anything cheap and strange.

"What is it?" a woman asked, turning over the tile with the country's name.

"A place," Beatrix said. "A real place. It was erased a hundred years ago, and I'm putting it back."

The woman laughed, kindly enough, and bought the tile anyway because it was pretty, and carried it off into a city that had never heard of Vellamark.

By the end of the first week, Beatrix noticed something.

The tile-humming. She'd felt it herself whenever she handled her own work — a faint vibration, like a struck tuning fork remembered rather than heard. She'd assumed it was her own grief resonating. But on the seventh day, a boy of maybe ten picked up the bridge-pin, and his hand started shaking.

"It buzzes," he said. "Like a bee."

"Does it," Beatrix said carefully. "Where are your people from, lad?"

He shrugged. "Everywhere. Da says his gran came from over the mountains somewhere. Before the war." He turned the pin over. "It feels warm. Like something's trying to say my name."

His grandmother's grandmother. Folded into Corven's Reach a century ago along with nine hundred thousand others, scattered across four nations like seed from a burst sack, carrying Vellamark in their blood without knowing what it was. Singing it in their sleep, maybe, the way the village elders on the ossuary road had spoken Vellamarkish in their dreams. The erasure had taken the country's name from every record and every mind. But blood, apparently, kept its own archive.

Beatrix gave him the pin for free. "Take it home," she said. "Give it to your da. Tell him it's from a place called Vellamark."

"Where's that?"

"Nowhere," she said. "It's everywhere. That's the trouble."

* * *

Word spread the way word does — slowly, then all at once. An old woman came who had hummed a tune all her life that her mother said was nonsense syllables; Beatrix sang it back to her complete, with words, and the old woman wept in front of the stall for ten minutes while the fishmonger pretended not to stare. A clerk arrived with a family seal he'd never been able to explain, a crest of a wolf under a crescent moon, and Beatrix recognized it from page two hundred and eleven of the book: the arms of the House of Maravelle, last stewards of the eastern marches. A farmer drove two days to ask why his daughter talked in her sleep about "the drowned bells."

Each of them left with a carving. Each carving hummed for them and went quiet for everyone else. And each of them, before leaving, got the same instruction, delivered in Beatrix's flattest market-voice:

"When someone asks what it is — and someone will — tell them. Tell them about Vellamark. Tell them it was real. Tell them it was taken. You don't have to believe any of it. Just say it out loud. Words are bones too. They hold a shape after the body's gone."

She didn't tell them the rest. She didn't tell them that saying it out loud was, as far as she could tell, slowly killing the erasure — that every retelling seemed to put another hairline crack in whatever great working the Custodians had built, that the book's pages had begun filling again, faintly, like frost forming on glass, recording conversations she wasn't present for. A merchant in some other city telling his son about a tile he'd seen. A grandmother teaching a half-remembered song to a grandchild, getting the notes wrong, getting them wrong forever, but singing them anyway.

Wrong versions. That was fine. Wrong versions were still versions. Isandro would have laughed at that — the last citizen of a perfect country watching it come back mangled and secondhand and sung out of tune, and calling it victory. She caught herself talking to him sometimes, in the evenings, holding the book open on her knees. She decided that wasn't madness. Madness would be *not* talking to him.

* * *

They came for her in the spring, of course. Not Custodians — those were finished, their order unraveled along with the erasure that had fed it, the last of them dissolving at the threshold of a door that no longer needed feeding. No. These were clerks. Two men from the Corven's Reach Bureau of Records, with ink-stained fingers and expressions of profound bureaucratic distress.

"Mistress Winterbourne." The elder one consulted a paper. "You have been selling objects inscribed with the name of a nation that does not appear in any registry, census, treaty, or map held by this office or, we are assured, any office anywhere. This constitutes — " he peered at the paper again — "'trafficking in unverifiable geography.' There is no law against it. We checked. Thoroughly. There is, however, a form."

"There's always a form," Beatrix said.

"You will need to either register Vellamark as a legitimate historical entity — which requires documentation from a recognized archive — or cease using the name commercially."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached under the stall and brought up the prophecy book, and set it on the counter between them, and opened it to the final page.

*and she told them.*

"This is the documentation," she said. "It's a hundred years old. It's the only surviving record of a country of nine hundred thousand people, and I am the only living person who can vouch for it, and I am telling you: register it, or don't. Either way I'm going to keep carving, and other people are going to keep telling, and in fifty years your bureau is going to have a Vellamark section whether you like it or not. You might as well get credit for it."

The younger clerk had picked up a carving while his superior sputtered — a small tile, the name of a street, *Rue des Cloches*, Bell-Street, in the drowned capital. His thumb moved over the letters and his face did something complicated.

"My grandmother," he said slowly, "used to say a word when she burned her hand. Nobody knew what it meant. She said her own grandmother taught her." He looked up. "It sounded like — *Vella*. Something. *Vella*-something."

"*Vellamark*," Beatrix said.

The clerk stood very still. The older one sighed the sigh of a man who can feel paperwork multiplying, took out his pen, and wrote in the margin of his form: *Pending further inquiry.*

"Inquiry," Beatrix repeated.

"The bureau does not erase things, mistress." He said it stiffly, defensively, as if the word itself offended him. "The bureau records. Whatever this turns out to be — it will be recorded."

She watched them go, the younger one glancing back twice, the tile still in his pocket. Then she sat down on her stool, in the smell of the tanneries, at the cheap end of a market in a country that had swallowed her homeland without tasting it, and she laughed until she cried, and then she cried until she laughed, and then she picked up her knife and a fresh piece of bone and began the day's work.

Forty-two carvings had become four hundred. Four hundred would become four thousand. Somewhere out there, in sleep-talk and burnt-hand curses and half-remembered songs sung wrong, Vellamark was already coming back — not as it was, never as it was, but as a story, and stories are the one country no Custodian can burn and no bureau can deregister.

That night, by candlelight, she checked the book one last time before bed, as she always did. The final page still bore its single line. But beneath it, new ink was rising, slow as roots growing, letter by letter:

*and they told their children.*

And beneath that, fresher still, the ink barely dry:

*and the children did not forget.*

Beatrix closed the book gently, the way you close a door on someone sleeping, and blew out the candle, and in the dark she began to hum — off-key, half-remembered, entirely unashamed — her grandmother's lullaby, in a language that was dead, in a country that was gone, in a voice that was going to teach it to anyone who would listen, for as long as she had breath to carve with.
