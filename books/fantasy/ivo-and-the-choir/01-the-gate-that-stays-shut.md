## 1. The Gate That Stays Shut

There are three things you need to know about the Thornfell gate, and I will tell you all of them because nobody else will.

First: it is the last working door into Vessarion, which is to say it is a door into a country that no longer exists on any map drawn in the last thirty years. Second: it is sealed by treaty, which means there is a piece of paper somewhere in Marrowgate that says no one may pass through it, and pieces of paper are famously good at stopping people. Third: I am its keeper, and I have not opened it once since I inherited the post from my mentor Wren Ashcombe, who died the way she lived — stubbornly, mid-argument, with a cup of cold tea going bitter beside her.

The gate itself is black iron over grey stone, set into a shoulder of the Thornfell mountains where the pass narrows enough that a child could hold off an army with a decent slingshot and bad intentions. On the southern face, someone long dead carved words in Old Vessarine that I spent four years of my life learning to read, mostly so I could stand here on winter mornings and translate them aloud to no one:

*We were, we sang, we are sung still.*

Lovely. Also, technically treasonous, since the Treaty of Grey Cloaks requires that Vessarion be referred to exclusively as "the former territories," "the unincorporated lands," or — my personal favorite, from the annexation documents — "a regrettable historical enthusiasm." You cannot say *Vessarion* in a public office. There's a clerk in Marrowgate whose entire job is striking the word from documents and writing "the former territories" in its place, and I imagine he goes home every night feeling like a man who has personally strangled a bird.

I say the word anyway. Quietly. To the gate. It seems only fair, since I'm one of perhaps two hundred living people who can pronounce it correctly, and a language that no one speaks aloud is just a very elaborate way of being silent.

* * *

My days have a shape to them, and I have come to love the shape the way you love a coat: not because it's beautiful, but because it fits, and because taking it off would mean standing in the cold as something formless.

I rise before dawn because the mountains don't negotiate. I light the brazier in the gatehouse — a squat stone building attached to the wall like a barnacle with opinions — and I put water on for tea that I will forget about until it's cold, in honor of Wren, who never once drank a hot cup in her life. Then I do the rounds.

The rounds take an hour. The wall is old enough that it complains in specific places, and I've learned its complaints like a doctor learns a hypochondriac patient. The third buttress weeps when it rains and cracks when it freezes, so in autumn I pack its joints with lime mortar and spend the winter listening to it grumble anyway. The eastern stair loses a step's worth of stone most springs; I keep a bucket of patch-cement mixed and waiting. The gate hinges want oiling monthly, though they will never again swing for anyone official, and there is a certain bleak comedy in maintaining hinges for a door that must stay shut. But rusted hinges seize, and seized hinges can't be opened quickly, and I am paid by a treaty office that prefers its sealed doors to stay theoretical rather than merely decorative — but if I'm honest, that's not why I oil them.

I oil them because a door that cannot open isn't a door. It's a wall with regrets. And I refuse to let this one become that.

After rounds comes the ledger. This is the part of my day that would get me hanged if the wrong person read it, and so I keep it in shorthand of my own devising, in a cipher based on Old Vessarine musical notation, which means the only other people alive who could crack it are either dead, hiding, or children too young to have been taught yet. In the ledger I record what I know of the smuggler roads: the ways through and around and under the Thornfells that bypass my gate entirely, because of course they do. A sealed door doesn't seal a mountain. It just tells the mountain's customers to use the window.

There are eleven routes I know of. Three go over scree fields that shift every thaw and must be re-walked each spring. Two follow dry streambeds that flood without warning and have drowned better smugglers than any of the current lot. One — Route Seven, the locals call it the Hollow Way — goes under the pass itself, through caves so old that the first map of them was scratched on hide, and I have walked it twice with a lantern and a rope and a heart rate I decline to discuss. Four are surface trails known only to shepherds, and shepherds will tell you anything except where they actually go. And one more I've marked only with a question mark, because a goat-herder mentioned it drunk at the autumn market and then, sober, swore up and down he'd never said a word, which is the most reliable confirmation there is.

Why does a treaty keeper keep such a ledger? Officially: security. Unofficially: because Wren kept one before me, and hers went back forty years, and reading it was the closest thing I've ever had to hearing Vessarion speak. Her hand got shakier down the pages. The entries got shorter. Somewhere around year twenty-five, she stopped recording route locations and started recording things like *"Route Nine smells of juniper in June"* and *"the woman from the high farms still sings on Thursdays"* — a keeper's ledger slowly becoming a memory book, a country compressed into marginalia. When she died I found her last entry, dated three weeks before the end:

*"They took the word out of the schoolbooks in Marrowgate today. Keep saying it, girl. A word no one says is a door no one opens."*

So I say it. Every morning, to the iron. *Vessarion.* And the gate keeps staying shut, and I keep staying here, and between us we maintain a fiction of duty that both of us know is really grief wearing a uniform.

* * *

Winter settles on Thornfell like a debt collector. By the solstice the snow stands waist-deep against the outer wall, the pass fills with drifts that swallow cart-tracks whole, and even the smugglers stay home, because Route Seven ices over and the scree fields bury themselves. From roughly midwinter to late thaw, I am the only living soul within two days' walk, unless you count the mountain goats, who do not count me back.

People ask — well, no one asks, no one visits, but I imagine people asking — how I stand it. The answer is that solitude is a skill like any other, and I am very good at my job. I talk to the gate. I talk to the brazier. I've named the ravens, which is a thing solitary people do and then lie about doing. The big one is Corregan, after a smuggler who owed me money and died owing it; the nervous one is Punctual, because he arrives at the same hour daily whether or not there's anything worth arriving for, and I find that admirable in a way I find few humans admirable.

I mend things. This takes longer than you'd think. I re-haft tools, render tallow, mend my own clothes badly and my boots well, because boots matter more than dignity above the snowline. Once a month I make the trip down to the market village of Lowtham for flour, salt, lamp oil, and news, and the news is always the same news: Aldric's grey cloaks garrisoning another town, another schoolbook amended, another festival renamed until the calendar itself looks like a stranger. The free cities — Marrowgate, Dunmark, the coastal leagues — grumble and trade and look away. Everyone looks away. Looking away is practically a civic virtue these days.

And in the evenings, I study.

This is the part of my inheritance I treasure most: Wren's archive, three trunks of it, hidden under the gatehouse floor beneath flagstones she disguised with a spill-stain and a loose hinge. Inside: dictionaries of Old Vessarine compiled in secret. Song fragments copied by hand from memory by exiles who knew they'd be executed if caught with paper. Treatises on the Choir — the great collective song, the memory-of-a-nation made audible, the thing Vessarion built itself out of and, some scholars whispered, out of which Vessarion was *made*. I have read everything in those trunks so many times I could recite half of it, and I understand perhaps a quarter, and I love all of it without reserve.

Here is what the archives say about the Choir, as best I've assembled it from a hundred contradictory fragments: that once, the singers of Vessarion held their country's memory together in song — names, borders, harvests, griefs, recipes, lullabies — and that the song was kept in living voices, passed mouth to mouth, generation to generation, so that Vessarion could not truly be conquered while even one singer remained. And here is what the archives say happened after the conquest, in the terse margins of men writing in fear: *they are gathering the singers. They are gathering the songs. We do not know why.*

We know more now. Or I suspect we do, though the suspicion lives in me shapeless and cold, like something moving under ice. Songs don't get gathered. Songs get *used*.

But that is a thought for a different chapter of my life, and on ordinary evenings I let it sleep, and instead I sit with the lamp turned low and practice declensions of a dead language, alone, in a gatehouse at the top of the world, keeping watch over a door that stays shut.

* * *

Some nights, if I'm honest — and the whole point of talking to ravens and braziers is that eventually you get honest, because there's no one left to perform for — some nights the competence thins out and I see what's underneath it.

I never saw Vessarion. That's the wound that doesn't show. My grandmother was born in the singing valleys; she came south as a girl, ahead of the armies, carrying nothing but a satchel of seed grain and a head full of songs she wasn't allowed to teach my mother because teaching them had already become dangerous. She died when I was nine. I remember her hands, her temper, and exactly one phrase of song, hummed more than sung, a rising figure like a bird deciding to fly — and I do not know what the words were, and no archive holds them, because she never wrote them down and no one else alive ever heard them.

Everything I do here — the ledgers, the cipher, the oil on the hinges, the word spoken each morning to the iron — is an attempt to stand in a country I've never seen, holding a door shut on behalf of people I'll never meet, mourning a phrase of music I can't finish. Competent solitude, Wren called it once, watching me re-shelve the archives with unnecessary precision. She said it kindly, the way you name a disease. *It's how our kind grieves, girl. We alphabetize the loss.*

She also said, in the same breath: *But keep your eyes open. Grief makes you diligent, and diligence makes you useful, and the world has a habit of sending its strange business to useful people in lonely places.*

I thought that was just Wren being Wren — she distributed prophecy the way other people distributed complaints, casually and to everyone. I filed it away under "mentor aphorisms" and went back to my ledgers.

* * *

Tonight is the last quiet night, though I don't know it yet. Tonight the wind is doing its usual work on the eaves, and Corregan has gone to roost in the gatehouse chimney despite my objections, and the snow is falling straight down, fat and slow, the kind of snow that means the mountain intends to keep everyone where they are.

I have done the rounds. I have oiled the hinges. I have spoken the morning word — *Vessarion* — to the iron, and the iron said nothing back, as usual, though tonight the wind through the pass made a sound behind it that was almost harmonic, almost like a voice agreeing.

I am at my desk, updating the ledger, when I notice it. Route Seven — the Hollow Way, the deep road under the pass — has a line beside it in my own shorthand, entered last spring after my second walk-through: *stable, dry, passable in winter below the frost line.* I look at that line for a long time, for no reason I can name, the way you wake at night certain you've forgotten something important. The fire pops. Punctual shifts on his roost. Somewhere under my feet, under the frost line, under the mountain, the dark road runs south toward a country that officially does not exist.

And then, faint — so faint I think at first it's the wind finding a crack in the stonework — I hear something from the direction of the gate. Not the wind. Not the ravens. Not the complaint of freezing iron.

A knock. Small. Twice. Like a child's fist, if a child had crossed the Thornfell pass in midwinter and somehow, impossibly, arrived at a door that has stayed shut for thirty years.

I put down my pen.
