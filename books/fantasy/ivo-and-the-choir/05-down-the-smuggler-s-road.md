## 5. Down the Smuggler's Road

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from knowing you have just committed treason before breakfast.

I had lived at Thornfell for eleven years without telling so much as a lie to a superior officer. I had filed my reports on time, kept my ledgers immaculate, and once returned a smuggler's bribe with interest because the man had overpaid by three coppers and my conscience was, apparently, a disease. Now I had hidden a child the usurper's own papers described as a *relic* inside a tunnel network I officially did not know existed, and I was packing bread, cheese, and a forged travel writ into a satchel while the grey-cloaked riders' horses stamped snow off their hooves not forty paces above my head.

The forgery was bad. I want that on the record. My hand shook so badly the date read *the 14th of Frostwane* when Frostwane had only eleven days, and any clerk worth his ink would have laughed me into irons. But Serel — Captain Serel, with her careful eyes and her papers that named a six-year-old boy as government property — had looked at me for a long moment before accepting it, and something in that look said she knew exactly what she was choosing not to see. Whether that mercy would hold until sundown was another question entirely.

"Are we going now?" Ivo asked from the corner where I'd stowed him among my winter stores. He'd eaten everything I gave him and asked for more, which was the healthiest sign I'd seen yet. Half-frozen children who eat like wolves are children who intend to live.

"Now," I said. "Before the captain changes her mind or her orders change for her."

"Is she bad?"

I paused with the satchel buckle half-fastened. "No. She's worse than bad. She's obedient."

He considered this with the gravity of a small scholar. "My mother said obedience is just fear wearing a uniform."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have gotten along with my mentor." I buckled the satchel. "Both of them dead, both of them right about everything. Come on."

The gatehouse had stood at Thornfell for two hundred years, and for most of those years it had been a door in search of a reason. The treaty that ended the Vessarine War sealed it; the conquest that followed made sealing it a sacrament. Officially, nothing passed through. Unofficially, I had spent a decade learning every crack in the mountain that ambitious men had widened over four centuries — the salt-smugglers' galleries, the old mine adits, the drainage cuts that smelled like regret and flowed like ambition. My ledgers of these roads were the truest thing I owned. Every other document in my office was a fiction maintained by treaty; the maps in my head were maintained by me.

We went down through the salt gallery first, because it began behind the coal store and because no one inspects a coal store in winter unless they enjoy disappointment. The dark closed around us like a coat. Ivo took my hand without being asked, which undid me more than I care to admit. It has been a long time since anyone at Thornfell needed my hand for anything but a signature.

"The man who unnames things," he said quietly, as we felt our way along. "You know him?"

"I know of him. Aldric." I kept my voice level. "He conquered Vessarion before you were born."

"No." Small fingers tightened around mine. "Conquering is taking things. He *unnames* them. He takes the name away and then the thing was never there." A pause. "That's how you lose a country. Not all at once. One name at a time."

Eleven years I had guarded a door to a country erased from every map, and this child articulated its death better in the dark of a salt tunnel than any historian had managed in a decade of archives. I didn't answer. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't have come out as grief, and grief is poor company on a road.

* * *

By midday we had descended past the salt galleries into the ways the smugglers called the Understairs — a sequence of switchback tunnels cut into the mountain's southern root, connecting abandoned watch-posts that had watched nothing for generations. Here I must set down my own account and let the boy speak, because what happened next belongs properly to him, and because I have learned that Tamsin Nakamura does not always see what is in front of her. She sees what her ledgers say should be there.

*Ivo says:*

Tamsin walks like the mountain owes her money. She counts steps under her breath. I counted too, after a while, because it is easier to be brave when numbers are happening.

The mountains here have wrong names. Everyone calls them the Grey Teeth, but that is a soldier's name, a name for teeth that bite. Before, they were called something else. When Tamsin isn't looking I say the old names out loud, very quietly, the way my mother taught me — you don't shout a name, you *offer* it, like food to a shy animal.

*Veshkarra,* I whisper to the peak above us through the stone. *Not Grey Tooth. Veshkarra, the Mountain That Remembers Snow.*

And the mountain — I felt this in my teeth, in the black fork riding warm against my chest — the mountain *turned over in its sleep.*

Tamsin stopped walking. "Did you feel that?"

"What?" I said, because lying is easier when your voice hasn't finished growing.

"That. Like something shifted." She held up her little oil lamp and looked at the tunnel wall, and I looked too, and there, where there had been plain rock all morning, there was now a seam. A door-shape. Old carvings around the edge worn almost smooth, except for one line of letters that still stood proud of the stone, and even I could tell they weren't letters anymore, they were notes. Music written into rock.

"It's a door," Tamsin said, in the voice of someone reading her own eulogy. "It shouldn't be a door. This gallery isn't on any map. I drew the maps."

"The mountain opened because I said its name," I told her. I decided she had earned the truth, because she hadn't let go of my hand even once, even when the going got slippery, and grown-ups who hold on are rare as summer snow. "They're not gone, Tamsin. The names. They're sleeping. The fork wakes them up."

She looked at me for a long time. In the lamplight her face did several things at once, which I think is called having feelings in public, and I pretended not to notice because that is polite.

"My grandmother used to sing a lullaby," she said finally. "Nobody knew where it came from. She said her grandmother sang it to her." Her voice went strange. "It was Vessarine, wasn't it. She was singing a dead country's song and nobody knew."

"Not dead," I said. "Sleeping. There's a difference. Dead things stay dead. Sleeping things get *crossed* if you wake them wrong."

That made her laugh, one short bark of a laugh, surprised out of her like a bird startled off a wire. "Where did you learn to talk like that?"

"My mother talked like that. She said the Choir doesn't grieve, it *grumbles.*" I put my hand flat against the door-stone, and the fork against my chest hummed a low, sweet note, and the seam widened with a sound like a long-held breath finally let go. "Come on. The mountain wants to show us the fast way down."

*Here Tamsin resumes:*

The fast way down turned out to be a stair cut by hands that had never heard of a plumb line, spiraling through the mountain's heart in a descent so smooth it could only have been sung into shape rather than carved. I mean that literally. As we went deeper, the walls began to carry sound the way good bread carries butter — Ivo humming a fragment of melody, and the stone answering him a half-beat later, harmonizing, the whole staircase becoming an instrument played by a child's absent-minded humming.

I am a keeper of gates and a keeper of ledgers. I believe in load-bearing stone and documented load-bearing stone. What I do not believe in is architecture that responds to a six-year-old's repertoire. And yet there we were, descending through the living throat of Thornfell while the mountain sang backup.

"You're not afraid," I said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm terrified," Ivo said cheerfully. "But it's a good terrified. Like the night before a festival." He glanced back at me, lamp-light catching his face. "Aren't you?"

"I'm the kind of terrified that makes lists."

"What's on the list?"

"One: get you south alive. Two: find out what the fork actually is. Three: avoid the grey cloaks. Four: never again forge a document, because I'm clearly terrible at it."

"Frostwane has eleven days," he said solemnly.

"*Thank you.*"

He giggled — an actual giggle, bright and shocking in the singing dark — and something in my chest that had been locked since the funeral came loose by one notch. I noted it for later examination, the way I note everything, and moved on.

* * *

We camped that night in a watch-post the ledgers called Post Nine and the mountain, apparently, called something else entirely. While Ivo slept, the fork hummed — I had noticed this before, in the gatehouse, and dismissed it as a trick of wind in the flues. There was no wind here. There was no flue. The shard of black glass on its cord around his neck rose and fell with his breathing, and each exhale carried a thread of tone out into the room, and the room — I checked twice, by lamp, by touch, by biting down on my own skepticism until it bled — the room resonated back. Faintly. Sleepily. Like a choir rehearsing in a distant room, heard through a wall.

I sat up most of the night listening to a building sing a lullaby to a child, and I thought about my grandmother's lullaby, and I thought about the archive at Marrowgate where I had once, as a young officer, read a confiscated hymnal whose pages had been numbered by someone who wanted them destroyed and saved by someone who wanted them remembered, and I understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, that I was not escorting a refugee.

I was escorting a country's last memory. And the country was awake. And it was frightened.

Because that was the thing I couldn't unhear, once I'd heard it: underneath the sweetness of the resonance there was a tremor. A vibrato of fear. Whatever slept in that glass had seen what Aldric's machine did to its siblings, and it knew it was being hunted, and it had chosen — *chosen* — to trust a frozen child at a sealed gate and a woman who forged dates badly.

In the morning Ivo woke, saw my face, and said, "You heard it too."

"Yes."

"It's scared of the deep roads. There are other pieces down there. Broken ones. It can hear them crying." He rubbed his eyes. "Tamsin, can broken songs be fixed?"

I thought of my ledgers. Of every road I'd mapped, every door I'd kept shut, every year of competent solitude spent guarding a border to nowhere. Of my mentor's last letter, which I had reread so many times the creases had gone soft: *A door is only a wall that hasn't met the right question yet.*

"I don't know," I said honestly. "But I know people in Marrowgate who might. Scholars. Smugglers. The useful overlap between the two." I shouldered the satchel. "One day's walk south, there's a village in the foothills. It's not on my maps either — the people there paid the surveyors to leave it off, back when being forgotten was safer than being found. They'll give us a bed and won't ask questions."

Ivo slung the fork's cord inside his shirt and nodded, and we started down the last stretch of the Understairs toward the daylight.

I should have known better than to trust a village that had bought its own erasure. By late afternoon, when the trees thinned and the foothills opened gray and quiet below us, I could see the smoke of its chimneys rising straight and unhurried into the winter sky — and I could also see, standing in the doorway of the first house, a woman watching us come, with the expression of someone trying very hard to remember why the sight of a child made her want to weep.

Behind us, far up the pass, a hawk cried once — and hawks, I noted grimly, do not fly in formation.

But that one did.
