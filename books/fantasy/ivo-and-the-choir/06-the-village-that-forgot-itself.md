## 6. The Village That Forgot Itself

The village had no name.

I don't mean it had lost its name the way a mitten gets lost — dropped somewhere between here and there, mourned for a season, replaced. I mean that when we walked down into its one street, Tamsin asked the first woman we met what the place was called, and the woman looked at her with the blank, patient expression of someone who has been asked this before and has learned to answer honestly: *It isn't called anything.*

"It must be called something," Tamsin said. "Every village is called something."

"Not this one," said the woman. She had flour on her hands and a loaf under her arm and eyes the color of weak tea. "The survey men came through eleven years ago. They wrote *unnamed settlement* in their book and moved on. Nobody argued."

"Nobody argued," Tamsin repeated, in the flat voice she used when she was furious and didn't want the fury to have anywhere to land.

I knew why. I'd been listening to her mutter over her ledgers for three days by then, all those careful columns of smuggler roads and toll-bribes and which passes froze first, and underneath all of it ran one long complaint against Aldric's empire: *they don't just take things, they take the fact that things were ever there.* A village without a name wasn't an oversight. It was a finished job.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me start where we started, which was cold, lost, and arguing about a goat.

* * *

The goat belonged to nobody, or rather to everybody, or rather — as it turned out — to no one who could remember owning it. It had followed us down from the pass for half a morning, eating things goats should not eat, including, at one point, the corner of my coat.

"Shoo," I told it.

"It doesn't shoo," Tamsin said. "Goats never shoo. That's how you know they're smarter than dogs. Dogs pretend to respect you."

"You like animals more than people."

"I like animals because they've never once lied to me about a treaty." She squinted down at the smoke rising from the valley ahead. Chimney smoke, a lot of it, from a village that wasn't on any of her maps. She checked the map anyway, twice, tilting it as if the village might have been hiding in the margin. "That's wrong," she said quietly. "There's nothing down there. There's *supposed* to be nothing down there."

"There's something down there," I said. "Something with bread. I can smell it."

And then, fainter than the bread, I felt something else — the fork, hanging at my chest inside my shirt, giving off a hum so low I felt it in my teeth before I heard it in my ears. The black glass shard of the Choir, my inheritance, my burden, my extremely inconvenient bedtime companion. It hummed when I slept. It hummed now, awake, in daylight, which it almost never did.

"Tamsin," I said. "Listen."

She went still. She'd learned to listen for it too, in our days on the road — not the sound itself, which only I seemed to hear properly, but the way my face changed when it sang. "What is it saying?"

"I don't know. It sounds like..." I hunted for the word. "Like when you come around a corner and smell something cooking that your grandmother used to make, except you can't remember your grandmother, you just know the smell means *home* and also *grief*, both at once."

"That's very specific for a piece of glass."

"The Choir is very specific," I said. "It remembers everything. That's the whole problem with it."

We went down toward the smoke.

* * *

The village sat in a fold of the hills like an egg in a spoon: one street, a well, a chapel with a leaning bell tower, maybe forty houses. The people came out to look at us the way people do in lonely places — curious, wary, already deciding whether we were trouble. An old man stood nearest, holding a rake he clearly wasn't using for anything. The woman with the flour on her hands stood beside him. Behind them, children peered around doorframes.

Tamsin did the talking, because Tamsin always did the talking; I was officially her nephew, mute from a childhood illness, which was a lie we'd agreed on somewhere around the second night, mostly because I kept slipping into Old Vessarine when I got excited and she said a boy who speaks a dead language draws fewer questions if he supposedly can't speak at all.

"We're traveling south," she said. "Looking for work, eventually. Could we buy bread? And shelter, if anyone has a barn?"

The villagers looked at each other. Not suspiciously — that was the strange part. They looked at each other the way you look at family across a dinner table when someone has asked a question and everyone is waiting for someone else to remember the answer.

"Bread we have," the flour woman said. "Barn, you'd have to ask... " She stopped. Her mouth stayed open a moment, then closed. "You'd have to ask," she said again, as though the sentence were a room she'd walked into and found empty.

"Ask who?" Tamsin prompted gently.

"I don't—" The woman laughed, embarrassed, and pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth. "Isn't that funny. I was going to say a name. I could feel the name coming. It's gone now."

"It happens," the old man with the rake said, not unkindly. "Happens to everyone here. You go to say a thing and the thing isn't there. Names especially. My own mother's name — I know I knew it. I know I said it every day for years. Now when I reach for it, it's like reaching into a pocket somebody's already turned out." He shrugged, a big slow shrug that seemed to carry forty years of shrugging behind it. "The survey men said it would happen. They said forgetting was healthy."

"They *said* that?" Tamsin's voice was very even. Evenness, I had learned, was her most dangerous register.

"After the war. When the country closed. They came with books and ink and they interviewed everyone, and then they said, *you'll find it easier if you let the old names go.* And you know, they were right, in a way. It is easier. Like a tooth coming out. Hurts less once it's gone." He looked at us with those mild tea-colored eyes. "You get used to reaching into empty pockets."

I had stopped listening to him, because the fork had started to sing.

Not loudly. The Choir doesn't do loud unless it has to. But it rose up through my ribs like water finding a crack, and I heard — really heard, for the first time since the fork had chosen me, if a fork can choose, and I was beginning to suspect it could — voices. Many voices. Layered, braided, patient. A song that had been waiting in the stones of this village the way heat waits in a hearthstone after the fire goes out.

And underneath the singing, something else. Something that made my skin crawl and my throat close.

The song was frightened.

* * *

Here is what I understood, standing in that nameless street while Tamsin negotiated bread and I pretended to be a mute nephew staring at a goat: the Choir doesn't live in the fork. The fork is just a splinter of it, a tuning fork, a key. The Choir lives in *things*. In stones and wells and door lintels and the particular slope of a hill where someone once buried a child with a wooden bird in her hands. Every Vessarine place still holds its note. The usurper couldn't burn the song out of the mountains — he'd tried; there were scars on the high passes where whole valleys had been *unmade*, flattened into generic hills, hills with no names and no stories, hills that even the birds flew over without stopping.

But in places like this village, where the people had been left alive and merely emptied, the notes were still there. Held in the walls. Held in the bread ovens. Held, thin and starving, in the people themselves, who carried the song the way you carry a tune you can't name — humming it without knowing, falling silent when it catches in your throat, wondering why your eyes are wet.

The fork wanted to give it back to them.

"No," I whispered to it, through my teeth, smiling at the goat so no one would think I was talking to them. "No. We don't do magic in front of strangers. Tamsin's rules. Rule four."

Rule four existed because of an incident on the second night involving the fork, a locked smuggler's door, and a stone archway that had opened for me like a mouth. Tamsin had gone pale in a way I'd never seen and instituted rules immediately. Rule one: the fork stays hidden. Rule two: I tell her everything it does. Rule three: we never sleep within earshot of anyone else. Rule four: no singing in public.

The fork, being a fork, ignored rule four.

It began, very softly, to sing through me.

I want to be honest about what that felt like, because later — much later — people would ask me, and I never found words that weren't either too small or too enormous. It felt like being a window. Like the song was outside, all around, pressing against the glass of my skin, and I was the one pane of it that had been left unlatched. I didn't decide to open. I just stopped holding shut.

The hum left my chest and entered the street.

* * *

The first thing that changed was the well.

The old rope creaked, though there was no wind, and the water below gave back a sound that wasn't an echo. Echoes repeat. This *answered*. A low, round tone, rising out of the dark water, and I knew — the way you know things in dreams — that it was singing the name of the well. Its true name, the one carved into its rim in letters that some survey man had chiseled off eleven years ago. The chisel marks were still there, gouged shallow and angry across the stone lip. Under the song, I could almost see the ghost of the letters refilling, like footprints filling with snow.

Then the chapel bell moved. Just trembled, no wind, no hand on the rope, a small bronze shiver, and a note came off it, sweet and cracked, and joined the well's note, and the two of them braided together, and I felt the street stones begin to warm under my boots like a body remembering circulation.

The villagers had gone very quiet.

Not afraid — that came later, and I'll get to it, and I wish I didn't have to. First there was only attention. Forty-some faces turning toward the sound the way flowers turn toward light, without decision, without defense. The old man let the rake fall out of his hands. The flour woman set her loaf down on a windowsill with exaggerated care, the way you put something down when your hands have stopped being yours.

And then the song reached the part of itself that held *names*.

I heard them pour out — oh, I heard them, hundreds of them, thousands, a whole watershed of names, the names of fields and grandmothers and dead dogs and wedding dances and a particular joke about a priest and a mule that had apparently sustained this village for four generations. The Choir didn't speak the names so much as *return* them, setting each one down gently in the place it had been taken from, a mother handing back everything she'd ever kept safe.

The flour woman made a sound.

It wasn't a word. It was the sound a person makes when a cramp releases after hours — relief and pain in the same breath, indistinguishable. She pressed both hands flat over her heart and said, "Maret. My name is Maret. My name is — I'm Maret, I'm Maret—"

Beside her the old man had sunk down onto his knees in the street, not praying, just kneeling because standing had become impossible. "Ilse," he said. "My mother was Ilse. Ilse, Ilse — she smelled of juniper, she burned juniper in the winter, she—" He was crying without any of the usual machinery of crying, no sobbing, no wiping, tears just falling off his chin into the dust, his face almost peaceful. "Why am I crying?" he asked the air, genuinely puzzled, genuinely grateful. "Why am I crying?"

"Because you're home," I said, in Old Vessarine, before I could stop myself, and the words went through the crowd like a spark through dry grass.

They heard it. That's the thing I hadn't understood — they *heard* it. Their minds had been scoured of the language, but language lives deeper than minds, in the ear's oldest rooms, and every one of them flinched toward my voice like plants toward sun. The children came out from behind the doorframes. One little girl, maybe six, looked up at me and said, slowly, carefully, in accented imperial common: "Say it again. Say the other words again. I dreamed those words. I dream them every night and I wake up and they're gone and I thought I was broken."

"You're not broken," I told her, and my voice cracked in the middle, which undermined the reassurance somewhat. "You were *buried*. There's a difference. Buried things can be dug up."

For a few minutes — I will count those minutes among the best of my life, however many life turns out to hold — the whole village sang.

They didn't know the words. That was the terrible, beautiful thing. The Choir poured through them and they opened their mouths and what came out was humming, wordless, tuneless in any technical sense, forty people standing in a street making a sound like wind through a house at night. But the fork blazed against my chest, drinking them in, and I could feel the shards of the song *recognizing* them — *there you are, there you are, we kept your place* — and the well rang and the bell rang and the goat, I swear this, the goat stood on its hind legs with its forehooves on the well's rim, listening, and I have decided to believe it was listening.

* * *

Then it ended, the way a fever breaks, all at once, and the silence afterward was worse than anything.

Because they remembered that they had remembered.

Maret stood in the middle of the street with her own name fresh in her mouth, and I watched it happen: watched her reach for her husband's name — I saw her turn toward a broad-shouldered man by the smithy, saw her lips shape the beginning of it — and watched the name not come. Watched the pocket be empty again. Because the fork's gift lasted exactly as long as the song lasted, and the song had ended, and everything the Choir had handed back slid away again like water through fingers, and this time they *knew* what they were losing.

The screaming started small. The little girl first, the one who'd dreamed the words, wailing at me in common-tongue: "Again, make it again, do it again—" and then Maret, and then the old man, Ilse's son, rocking on his knees in the dust, and then others, a ragged chorus of grief with no object, mourning losses they could feel bleeding but couldn't see.

Tamsin grabbed my shoulder. Hard. "Stop it. Ivo. Stop the fork."

"I don't know how!"

"Think quieter, then! Whatever you're doing, stop doing it!"

I wasn't doing anything — that was the truth and the horror of it. The fork had used me like a doorway and left me standing in the frame. But I pulled it out anyway, cupped the black glass in both hands, and it was cold now, cold as riverbed stone, its song folded up and gone, and I hated it in that moment with a pure and childish hatred. *You can't do that to people,* I told it silently. *You can't show someone their own hands and then take the hands back.*

The fork said nothing. Forks are smug that way.

* * *

We left before full dark. Tamsin insisted, and for once I didn't argue, because staying felt unbearable in a new way — not the ordinary road-unbearable of cold feet and bad food, but the unbearable of being the person who gives a drink of water to a man dying of thirst and then walks on.

Maret walked us to the edge of the village. She'd wrapped the day's bread in cloth and pushed it into Tamsin's arms and refused payment with a shake of her head that brooked no argument. Then she stood there, flour still on her hands from a morning that felt like it belonged to another century, and studied my face for a long time.

"You're one of ours," she said finally. Not a question.

"I don't know what I am," I said, which was honest.

"I dreamed about you," she said. "Years ago. Before the survey men, even. I dreamed a child came down the pass carrying a piece of night, and when he spoke, the stones answered him." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face, because it was the smile of a woman who had just spent one hour holding her own soul and would spend the rest of her life knowing the weight of it. "I told my mother the dream and she said, *that's just the old stories, girl.*" Her voice caught. "I can't remember my mother's face. But I remember the dream. Isn't that a wicked thing? They took her face and left me the story."

"I'm sorry," I said.

"Don't be sorry," Maret said. "Be loud. Whatever you're carrying — and I know you're carrying something, boy, I felt it pass through this street like spring flood — whatever it is, take it where it needs to go, and don't let anyone teach it to whisper." She touched my cheek with her floury hand, leaving a white print like a blessing. "Now go. And if you ever learn how to make the song stay — you come back. You come back to this street, and you sing until the stones stand up."

We walked south, into the blue end of the evening, and the goat followed us for exactly one hundred paces and then sat down in the road, having decided, presumably, that its loyalties lay with the village.

Behind us the village had no name. Ahead of us the road had no lights. And in my shirt, against my heartbeat, the fork had begun to hum again — low, urgent, insistent — pulling not south anymore, but *down*, toward the deep roads under the mountains, toward something in the dark that was calling the shard home.

"Tamsin," I said. "It wants to go underground."

She sighed the sigh of a woman whose ledgers had no column for this. "Of course it does," she said. "Everything important in my life is underground lately. Walk fast, Ivo. We'll want a head start on whatever's down there."

We walked faster. The fork sang louder. And far behind us, faint on the evening air, I thought I heard the unnamed village begin — very softly, without knowing why — to hum.
