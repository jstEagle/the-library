## 7. The Grey Cloak's Bargain

The trouble with running from the law, I have learned in three days of it, is that the law has better horses.

Ivo and I had come down out of the smuggler's road into a valley of bare orchards and frozen streams, following a track so old that the smugglers themselves had forgotten who cut it. The boy walked like someone who had never once been tired — or like someone whose exhaustion was of an older vintage than his legs could account for. He hummed as he went, tuneless little fragments, and the black-glass fork in my satchel answered him with a pulse I felt against my hip like a second heartbeat.

I did not tell him how much ground we were losing. A child deserves his illusions, and mine were all spent by thirty.

We made camp that night in the shell of a shepherd's hut, its roof half-collapsed into a nest of bracken. I built no fire. Ivo curled up against the wall with his coat pulled over his knees and was asleep before I finished checking the satchel's straps, and as he slept the fork began to sing — softly, under its breath, the way a sleeping person talks. I sat with my back to the opposite wall and listened to a dead country lullaby itself, and I thought about how, four days ago, my greatest moral crisis had been whether to record a shipment of untaxed brandy in the ledger under "wool" or "miscellaneous."

That was when I heard the horses.

Three of them, coming down the valley at a walk, unhurried, which is always worse than a gallop. Men who gallop are chasing something they can see. Men who walk already know where you are.

I woke Ivo with a hand over his mouth and put us both behind the fallen section of roof, where the bracken grew tall enough to hide a kneeling woman and a small boy if neither of them breathed. Through the gaps I watched three grey cloaks dismount in the yard below. Two stayed with the horses. The third came toward the hut without drawing a weapon, boots crunching on frost, and stopped just outside the doorway.

"Keeper Nakamura," she said. Her voice carried the particular flatness of someone reading from a document she has read many times. "You are harboring a classified relic of the Vessarine succession. Surrender it and yourself, and the Empire will treat you generously."

I considered several replies. Most of them involved words I would not want read aloud at my eventual trial.

"I don't know what you're talking about," I called back instead, which was true in the way that most useful lies are true: I genuinely did not know what the Empire wanted with a child, only that everything about wanting it was wrong.

A pause. Then, mildly: "Your gate ledger shows a supply requisition for two, dated the morning after the riders came to Thornfell. Flour, salt, dried apples, a wool blanket sized for a child." Another pause. "You are a meticulous woman, Keeper. It is your one flaw."

I closed my eyes. Fourteen years of keeping records so clean you could eat off them, and it was the cleanliness that betrayed me. Somewhere behind me, Ivo's hand found the hem of my coat and gripped it.

The woman outside the hut waited. She did not repeat herself, did not threaten, did not shift her weight. That patience frightened me more than any drawn sword would have. Finally she said, "My name is Serel. Captain, Third Grey Company. I am not here to take the relic by force, because force would damage it, and damaged songs do not rewrite cleanly." A beat. "May I come in? It is very cold, and I would rather conduct this conversation like a person than like a proclamation."

Every instinct I owned said no. But instinct also said that a woman who wanted us dead would not have announced herself through a door, and that the two riders with the horses had not moved to flank the hut. So I stood up — slowly, hands visible, the way you stand up to a wolf — and said, "Come in, then. Mind the roof. It's been falling down since before either of us was born."

* * *

She ducked under the broken lintel and settled herself on a stone across from me with the ease of someone who had slept in worse places this week. Up close, Serel was younger than her voice suggested — mid-thirties, maybe, with a sunburned face and the kind of tired eyes that come from doing a job well enough to be trusted with its worst parts. Her sword stayed sheathed. Her hands stayed visible. She had done this before, I realized. Sat across from frightened people and talked them into things.

"You're the one who lied to Commander Vess at the gate," she said. It wasn't an accusation. If anything, there was something almost admiring in it. "Told him the tunnels flooded years ago. He believed you. He believes everyone; it's why he's still a commander." She glanced at the bracken pile where Ivo was pretending very hard to be asleep. "And now here you are, four days south, with a national treasure in a flour sack."

"He's a child," I said.

"He's a shard-carrier. The last one, as far as our surveys show." She said it the way a surveyor says *the last breeding pair of anything*, clinical and faintly sad. "Which makes him either the most dangerous object in the province or the most valuable one, depending entirely on who's holding the leash."

"There is no leash," I said. "He's not an object. He's eight years old and he cries in his sleep."

Something moved behind Serel's eyes — quick, quickly buried. "Yes," she said quietly. "They usually do."

I let that sit between us for a moment. Outside, one of the horses stamped, and frost fell off the broken roof in a fine white sift.

"What does Aldric want with him?" I asked. "The truth, if you know it. You've clearly been sent to offer me something, and I won't bargain blind."

Serel looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide something — decide how much of the official version to give me, or perhaps how much of her own doubt to admit. When she spoke, her voice had dropped its flatness. What was underneath was wearier and more human.

"The Emperor wants to lay the past to rest," she said. "That's the phrase they gave me. *Lay the past to rest.* Twenty years ago he conquered a country that had already erased itself — a nation that kept its history in songs instead of stones, so that when the songs scattered, the country scattered with them. There's been nothing but grief and ghosts on those mountains for a generation. Children born in villages that can't remember their own names. Old people dying without anyone to bury their memories with them." She spread her hands. "The Choir is dying anyway, Keeper. All Aldric proposes to do is gather the last shards somewhere safe, somewhere quiet, and let the song end properly. With dignity. Instead of bleeding out in ditches and night markets for another hundred years."

"And the child?"

"Goes to the capital. Is cared for. Is educated." A pause, precisely measured. "Is thanked."

"*Thanked,*" I repeated.

"It's a generous offer," Serel said, and there it was again — that flicker, that hairline crack in the official voice. "Full pardon for you. Restoration of your post. A stipend. And for the boy, a place in the Imperial household, with tutors, with physicians, with—" She stopped herself. Started again. "With everything a child could want except the thing he actually has."

"Which is?"

"A country," she said simply. "Or what's left of one."

I looked at her. She looked back, level and unblinking, and I understood suddenly that she was not lying to me — that every word she'd said was true, or true enough, and that this was precisely what made her dangerous. Because the terrible thing, the thing I will not easily forgive myself for, is that for one long moment — sitting in a freezing ruin with a stolen child asleep against my shoulder — I *wanted* it.

I wanted the pardon. I wanted my gate and my ledgers and my small competent life, the life I had built brick by brick over a homeland I'd never even seen, because building a life out of other people's borders is easier than carrying a dead country in your chest. I wanted Ivo fed and warm and tutored, and I wanted to stop being hunted, and I wanted — God help me — to stop hearing the fork sing at night, that thin ghost-voice full of grief too large for my shoulders.

It would have been so easy. Hand over the fork. Take the boy's hand. Walk north to a comfortable cage and call it rescue.

Then Ivo stirred in his sleep and murmured something in Old Vessarine — a word I didn't know yet, soft and questioning, the way a child asks a mother for water — and the fork pulsed once against my hip, and I felt it. Not heard it exactly. Felt it. A note of such specific, patient sorrow that it went through me like a needle finding the one place in my body where I keep everything I've never said out loud.

The song wasn't dying. The song was *waiting*. There is a difference, and only one of those differences ends with a machine in a vault.

"No," I said.

Serel exhaled slowly through her nose. Not surprise. Confirmation, maybe. "No to the pardon, or no to the whole proposal?"

"All of it. Every word." I shifted so my shoulder was between her and the bracken pile. "You're a soldier, Captain, so let me put this in terms you'll respect. Your Emperor doesn't want to lay the past to rest. He wants to *collect* it. There's a difference between a funeral and a harvest, and I've read enough archives to know which one requires paperwork in triplicate."

"You don't know that."

"I know that a man who wanted a dignified ending wouldn't send three riders with papers calling a child a *relic*." I kept my voice low and even. "I know that 'laying to rest' is what you say about things you intend to bury where no one can dig them up. And I know that whatever's really happening in that capital, you already suspect it, because you've asked me three questions tonight that had nothing to do with your orders, and soldiers with clean consciences don't ask questions."

For the first time, Serel smiled. It was not a pleasant smile. It was the smile of someone who has just lost an argument she expected to win, and finds, underneath the losing, a strange relief.

"You bargained with me," she said. "You didn't beg, didn't run, didn't cry. You sat in a ruin with no weapon and no fire and you *bargained*." She shook her head slowly. "Vess said you were the most honest keeper on the border. He should have said the most careful one."

"I'm going to make you an offer now," I said, "and you're going to pretend to consider it, and then we're both going to leave this hut alive. Here it is: you report that you found the hut empty. Cold ashes, days old, tracks heading west toward the river. You lose nothing. Your orders are technically fulfilled — you *found* the trail, didn't you? And I get a head start that keeps your conscience clean, because you'll honestly be able to say you searched."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you have three riders, one sword, and a woman with nothing left to lose guarding a sleeping child, and I promise you the arithmetic of that fight is uglier than your reports will look."

Silence. The wind moved through the broken roof. Behind me, Ivo slept on, oblivious, one fist curled around a fold of my coat, and the fork sang its small stubborn note against my hip, and I thought: *this is what it costs, this is what it has always cost, someone has to sit in the cold and refuse.*

Serel stood. She straightened her cloak with two precise tugs, and when she turned back to me her face was the flat official mask again, seamless, unreadable — except for the eyes, which had not gotten the memo.

"West toward the river," she said. "Cold ashes. Days old." She paused at the broken doorway, silhouetted against the frost-bright yard. "Keeper Nakamura. One piece of advice, freely given, worth more than your pardon. Don't go to Marrowgate."

"Why not?"

"Because Marrowgate is where desperate people go to sell things," she said, "and everything you're carrying is exactly the sort of thing desperate people buy." She swung up onto her horse. "The Emperor's reach is shorter than his ambition, but his buyers are everywhere."

Then she was gone, three horses fading down the valley at that same unhurried walk, and I sat in the dark of the ruined hut with my heart hammering and my hands, I discovered, shaking.

Ivo woke near dawn, blinking, snow-light on his face. "Did they find us?" he asked in accented Common, then, seeing mine, "Are we safe?"

"We bargained," I said, which was not an answer, and seemed to satisfy him completely, the way answers satisfy children when they sound like stories.

We broke camp and went east — away from the river, away from the advice, straight toward Marrowgate, because she was right about everything else and I am contrary by profession. But I did not tell Ivo the real reason, which was this: somewhere in that fractious, spy-riddled free city was an old scholar who might know what the Door Out of Vessarion actually was, and I had promised a dead mentor I would finish her work, and besides —

Besides, the fork had begun pulling. Gently, patiently, the way a compass pulls, or a dog on a lead. South. Toward the city. Toward something in the deep places under the world that was singing back.

And I have learned, at forty-one, that when a dead country starts giving you directions, the only thing more frightening than following them is standing still.
