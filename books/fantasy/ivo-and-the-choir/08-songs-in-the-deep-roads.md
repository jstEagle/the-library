## 8. Songs in the Deep Roads

The dark under Thornfell was not like other darks.

I had known dark before — the dark of the cellar where I hid while the grey men searched my grandmother's house, the dark behind my eyelids when I tried not to cry, the long dark of the road south where Tamsin said *walk, don't run, running makes noise*. But this dark had weight. It pressed on my ears like water. It smelled of cold stone and old smoke and something sweeter underneath, something that made me think of bread left rising overnight in a warm kitchen, which is a strange thing for a cave to smell like, but the deep roads are strange, and by then I had stopped expecting them to behave.

Tamsin walked ahead with her lantern held low, the way she always did, so the light pooled at our feet instead of announcing us to the whole mountain. She had a rope tied around her waist and mine, "not because I think you'll fall," she'd said, "but because I've decided I don't want to spend an hour explaining to your ghost why I let go." That was Tamsin's way of being kind. She wrapped it in jokes the way other people wrapped fish in paper, so you couldn't see what was inside until you got it home.

I carried the fork.

It had been humming since we came down off the pass. Not the sleepy hum it made when I dreamed — this was a waking hum, insistent, like a bee trapped under a cup. It tugged. That's the only word for it. When the tunnel forked left and right, the fork leaned left, or right, or sometimes straight down into the floor as if there were another road beneath the road, and every time I followed its lean, the hum got warmer.

"It wants something," I told Tamsin.

"Everything wants something," she said. "That's how you know it's alive."

"I mean more than usual."

She stopped. The lantern light made a small gold room around us, and beyond it the tunnel went on swallowing itself. She looked at the fork in my hands — black glass, two prongs, older than any tool had a right to be — and her face did the thing it did when she was reading one of her forbidden books, all narrowed eyes and pressed mouth, a person arguing silently with the past.

"Which way?" she asked.

I held up the fork. It leaned hard to the right, toward a crack in the wall barely wide enough for a cat, let alone a keeper of gates and the boy she'd accidentally adopted.

"That," Tamsin said flatly, "is not a door."

The hum rose half a tone.

And the wall opened.

Not dramatically — no thunder, no grinding stone. The crack simply widened, quietly, the way a hand opens when it decides to stop making a fist, and there was a passage beyond it, dry and tall, with walls carved in lines too worn to read. Dust slid down from the ceiling like flour through fingers.

Tamsin stood very still for a long moment. Then she said, in Old Vessarine, which she still spoke like a scholar and not like a person, "*The mountain remembers.*"

"The mountain remembers," the fork agreed, in my head, in a voice made of many voices, and I didn't tell her that part yet, because some things you have to carry alone for a while before they fit into words.

* * *

We walked for what felt like a day and might have been three hours. Deep roads don't keep honest time. My legs knew we were going downhill even when the floor looked flat; my ears popped twice, and Tamsin fed me dried plums and made me drink from her flask because, she said, "underground thirst lies to you, it tells you you're fine right up until you're not." She talked more down here than she ever did above ground. I think the dark made her lonely enough to fill it.

"So," she said, somewhere around the fourth plum, "when the wall opened. Did you do that?"

"No."

"Did the fork do that?"

"The fork asked," I said. "The mountain said yes."

She chewed on that along with her plum. "Mountains don't say yes."

"This one does. This one is full of yes. Can't you hear it?"

She couldn't. I could tell it bothered her, the way it bothered her that I spoke Old Vessarine without accents or effort, the way it bothered her that the fork hummed to me and only went quiet and dead in her hands, a piece of black glass pretending to be nothing. She was a woman who had spent her whole life learning a country secondhand, from smuggled letters and banned archives, and here came a boy who *was* the country, or a piece of it, wearing one boot with a hole in the toe. I don't think she resented me. I think she grieved for herself a little, each time, and hid the grieving behind ledger-keeping and plum-rationing, and I pretended not to notice because noticing out loud would have embarrassed us both.

But then the singing started, and there was no more hiding anything.

* * *

It began as a feeling in my teeth.

Then in the soles of my feet, a rhythm, slow as a heartbeat that had been beating since before either of us was born. Then — I stopped walking, and Tamsin bumped into me and swore in three languages, two of them probably invented for the occasion — then I heard it properly.

Voices.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not coming from anywhere, or coming from everywhere, the way rain comes from everywhere. They were singing in Old Vessarine, and they were singing fragments — a harvest song missing its middle, a lullaby with only the last verse, a marching song that had forgotten it was ever about war and now only remembered the walking. Lines of poems I somehow already knew, though I'd never learned them. Names. So many names, sung the way you sing a list when the list is also a prayer: *Maren of the terraces, Odile who mended sails, the brothers Vask who argued for sixty years and died within a week of each other out of spite—*

I sat down. My knees just decided, without consulting me.

"Ivo—" Tamsin crouched beside me, lantern swinging. "What is it? What's wrong?"

"They're singing," I said. "They won't stop. They can't stop. That's the whole — that's what the Choir *is*, it doesn't stop, it holds everything, if it stops holding then everything falls out—"

"What's holding everything?"

"Us. Them. Everyone we were." I pressed the fork against my sternum, and it hummed against my bones like a second heart trying to sync with the first. "It's not a song about Vessarion, Tamsin. It isn't a memory *of* the country. It's the country's remembering of itself. And most of it is gone, and what's left is scared."

"Scared," she repeated. She has a way of repeating things flatly that makes them sound both ridiculous and true. "A song is frightened."

"You would be too," I said, "if you used to be everyone, and now you're a shard in a boy's pocket."

She was quiet for a moment. Then, softly, in her careful archive-Vessarine: "Sing me something. One of the fragments. I want to hear it with my own ears, not yours."

So I sang. I picked the easiest thing, the harvest song, the one with the missing middle, and I gave her the beginning and the end:

*"Gold in the low field, gold in the high,
bind it in sheaves before the frost is nigh—*

*...and the last verse, which goes:*

*'Sleep, golden land, the reaping's done,
we kept your name when the keeping was hard,
sleep, golden land, till the singers come,
and wake in the mouths of children unscarred.'"*

Tamsin listened the way she read — completely, hungrily, like a woman drinking after a long dry walk. When I finished she didn't say anything for a while. In the lantern light I saw that her eyes were wet, and she turned her face away and pretended to check the rope knots, which fooled neither of us.

"My mentor had that song," she said finally. "Half of it. A fragment on a scrap of smuggled paper. She used to hum the first line while she worked. She never knew how it ended." Her voice did something complicated. "You just gave her back forty years of wondering, and she's been dead six years, and I don't know whether to thank you or hit you."

"People usually pick thanking," I said.

"Don't get comfortable."

* * *

The fork pulled harder as we went. By what I judged to be evening — my stomach's opinion, which is the only clock I trust — the tunnels had changed character. Older. The walls weren't rough-cut anymore but dressed stone, fitted close, and every so often we passed niches carved into the rock, and in the niches were things people had left: a clay cup, a child's wooden bird, a comb with three teeth broken, a small heap of river stones arranged in a spiral. Offerings, maybe. Or messages. Or just the belongings of people who had walked these roads once, going somewhere, carrying songs.

In one niche there was a bundle of wax-sealed letters, fused together by time into a single brick. Tamsin lifted it out with both hands, reverent, and I watched her hold a piece of somebody's ordinary life like it was a relic, which I suppose it was. Everything down here was a relic. Everything down here was proof.

"They used the deep roads during the conquest," she murmured. "This is in the archives — rumors only. Whole villages went underground. The roads connected cellars to cellars, shrine to shrine. Aldric's engineers mapped the surface and found nothing, because the country had stepped out of itself." She set the letter-brick back gently. "They must have sung down here. To keep the children calm. To keep themselves—"

"To keep themselves," the Choir finished, through me, and my mouth said it before I could decide whether to allow it. "To keep themselves from forgetting. You sing a name every day, the name stays. You sing a name every day for twenty years, the name becomes a place you live."

Tamsin stared at me. "Did you mean to say that?"

"No," I admitted.

"Does it happen often?"

"More lately. The closer we get to the others." I held up the fork. Its pull was unmistakable now, a constant lean, a compass needle with an opinion. "There are more shards down here. Under us. Maybe lots. The Choir can feel them the way you can feel your own hand in the dark."

"And it wants to gather them."

"It wants to be whole." I hesitated. "It wants it the way you want air. It's not greedy, it's — drowning. Every shard alone is a verse with no song around it. Alone too long, a verse forgets what it belongs to. Some of them down here have already gone quiet. I can feel the quiet ones. Like rooms where the fire's gone out."

Tamsin was silent for a long stretch of walking after that. Then she said, "When we find them. The living shards. What happens if we gather them? All of them?"

"I don't know."

"Guess."

I guessed. "I think it becomes the Choir again. The whole song. And I think whatever holds the whole song has to be — a vessel. Someone the song trusts to carry it." I didn't look at her when I said it, because I was afraid of what her face would do, and more afraid of what mine would do. "The singing line. My grandmother's line. There's a reason the fork came to me and not to a soldier or a scholar."

"There are reasons," Tamsin said, "and then there are stories people tell to make reasons feel like fate. Be careful which kind you're listening to."

"It hums when I sleep," I said. "It sings me names. Last night it sang me my mother's name, and my grandmother's, and her mother's, back eleven generations, and then it sang a name I didn't know, and it was mine, my true name, the one I was born with before I ran, and Tamsin — I've been calling myself Ivo because it's short and easy, but that's not what I am. That's just what I answer to."

She stopped walking again. Down here, stopping had become our whole conversation.

"And what are you?" she asked. Carefully. The way you ask a question when you already suspect the answer will cost something.

"Tired," I said honestly. "Hungry. Nine years old, nearly ten. And apparently the last voice of a country that got erased, which is a lot to put on someone who still can't whistle."

Something in her face loosened — not pity, exactly. Recognition. "You can't whistle?"

"Not even a little. My grandmother despaired of me."

"Good," said Tamsin Ashgrove, keeper of the Thornfell gate, liar to riders, reader of banned books, "because whistling is a terrible habit and I'm glad you'll be spared it." And she handed me the last plum, which from her was practically an embrace.

* * *

We heard the shard before we saw its chamber — or rather, I heard it, and Tamsin heard me hearing it, because I made a sound I've never made before or since, a kind of involuntary ache in the throat, and she grabbed my arm and said "what, *what*," and I pointed down the passage to where a faint grey light was leaking around a bend, light with no torch in it, light like moonlight that had gotten lost.

The chamber was round, natural, its ceiling lost in darkness above. Water dripped somewhere, patient as centuries. And in the center, on a shelf of stone that human hands had smoothed and cushioned with rotted wool generations ago, lay a second shard of the Choir — a sliver of black glass longer than my forearm, humming, glowing faintly, singing to itself in a voice worn thin as old cloth.

It was singing a cradle song. Over and over. Just the cradle song. Just four lines, over and over, for who knows how long, alone in the dark under a mountain.

*Sleep, little one, the gate is shut,
the wolves are far, the fire is warm,
sleep, little one, and grow straight and strong,
the morning will come, the morning will come.*

It had been singing *the morning will come* in an empty room since before I was born.

I crossed the chamber and picked it up, and the moment my skin touched it, the cradle song broke off mid-word, and the shard went rigid in my hands — the way a bird goes rigid, the way a wild thing goes when the hand finally arrives after years of waiting, and it cannot believe the hand, and it cannot bear for the hand to be a lie.

"*Hello,*" I said, in Old Vessarine, my real name folded inside the greeting like a key inside a fist. "I'm here. I came. You're not alone anymore."

The shard shuddered. Then it did the thing that undid me, the thing I will hear until I die: it began to cry. Not weeping, exactly. Songs don't weep. But the sound it made was the sound underneath weeping, the sound a person makes before they can cry, when the relief arrives faster than the tears and the body just shakes. And around it, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, the other voices — the dead Choir, the scattered verses, the names in their thousands — rose up and answered it, a great soft chord swelling out of the stone, and every voice in it said the same thing in different ways:

*The morning came. The morning came. Someone came.*

Tamsin stood in the entrance with the lantern, and I watched her understand it — really understand, in her body and not her archives — that the song was alive, that it had been alive all along down here in the dark, faithful and terrified, keeping its four lines lit like a lamp in a window for a family that was never coming home. I watched her put her hand over her mouth. I watched her sit down slowly on the wet stone, a grown woman, a gatekeeper, a stranger to her own blood-country, and I heard her whisper in her stiff scholar's Vessarine, "*Oh. Oh, you've been waiting. All this time, you've been waiting.*"

"Yes," said the shards, through me, gently, because they could feel that she needed to be answered directly. "Yes. We waited. We would have waited longer. But we are glad the waiting is done."

I held both pieces of black glass against my chest and felt them lean toward each other like tired siblings, and I understood — with awe first, and then, arriving right behind the awe like a debt collector, responsibility — that this was not a treasure and not a weapon and not a mission. It was a trust. Somewhere above us, Aldric was feeding pieces of this into his machine to grind history into obedience. Somewhere ahead of us was a Door, and a choice, and a shape the song would have to take. And somewhere behind us, Serel rode with grey cloaks and polite papers, and none of them, not one rider in that army, knew they were hunting a cradle song that had been keeping faith with the morning for fifty years.

"We should rest," Tamsin said at last, her voice rough. "Eat. Sleep in shifts. Tomorrow we go deeper — the fork's pulling toward something bigger, I can feel it in the floor." She paused. "Can't I."

"It's pulling toward the third shard," I said. "And the third one isn't alone. There are people near it. Living people. The song can hear their hearts." I swallowed. "Tamsin — the song says they're singing to it. Badly. Off-key. In the dark. They're singing to it the way you talk to someone in a coma."

Tamsin looked at me across the lantern light, and I saw the exact moment the map of her world redrew itself.

"Off-key," she said. "How many?"

"A village," said the Choir, through me, and my skin prickled. "A village that forgot its name, singing to remember it."
