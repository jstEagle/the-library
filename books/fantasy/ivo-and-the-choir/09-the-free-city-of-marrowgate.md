## 9. The Free City of Marrowgate

Marrowgate announced itself the way all free cities do: with a smell. Woodsmoke and fish and tar and, underneath it all, the particular sourness of too many people who have nowhere else to be. I smelled it two miles out, on the ridge road, and Ivo — riding pillion behind me with his chin hooked over my shoulder like a small, stubborn barnacle — went rigid.

"What is that?" he asked in Old Vessarine.

"City," I said.

He considered this. "It smells like a wound."

"It smells like forty thousand refugees who got here before the roads closed," I said. "Which is a kind of wound, I suppose. One that learned to keep breathing."

I had not been to Marrowgate in eleven years. It had been smaller then, meaner, a fishing town with delusions of a harbor. Now the walls had grown outward twice, swallowing orchards and graveyards into slums, and the new districts were built of whatever people could carry — ship timber, tent canvas, the doors of houses that no longer existed anywhere but in the hands nailed to them. That last detail stopped me at the gate queue for a long moment: an entire lane of salvaged doors standing upright in rows, painted with names in six scripts, some of them crossed out and rewritten, one of them bearing a name in Old Vessarine that made the black-glass fork in my pack hum so hard my teeth ached.

"Hush," I told it, under my breath, the way you'd tell a dog.

The gate guards didn't ask where we'd come from. They asked what we were carrying, whether we had coin, and whether we were loyal to the Concord of Free Cities — which is to say, they asked nothing at all and took our toll. Marrowgate's politics were famous the way a fever is famous: everyone knew about them, nobody wanted to catch them. The city was governed by a council of exile factions who agreed on exactly one thing, which was that each of the others was secretly in Aldric's pay.

"They'll help us," I said, mostly to convince myself.

"You sound certain," Ivo said.

"I sound like a woman lying to a child, which is a skill I've been practicing lately."

* * *

We lodged at the Sign of the Drowned Bell, an inn run by a Thornfell expatriate named Ossa who had once smuggled wool through my gate and now smuggled people through hers. She took one look at me, one look at Ivo, and one look at the shape of the fork through my coat, and said, "Back room. No questions until I've locked the door."

Ossa's back room had a table, two chairs, a lamp, and a map of the occupied territories pinned to the wall with more holes than pins, as though previous occupants had thrown darts at Aldric's empire out of spite. Ivo sat down, then stood up, then sat down again, unable to settle. He hadn't slept properly since the village — the one whose people had wept without knowing why — and the fork had been restless ever since, pulling southward in his sleep like a compass needle with opinions.

"There are people here from home," he said suddenly. "I can feel them. Not the song. Just — echoes. People who used to hear it."

"Marrowgate's full of Vessarine descendants," Ossa said, pouring something amber into three cups because she'd decided we were adults tonight, all three of us. "Third generation, mostly. They don't speak the old tongue. Half of them don't know they're Vessarine. Their grandparents changed their names at the border and never said why." She slid me a cup. "You want the fractious council version of help or the useful kind?"

"Is there a difference?"

"The council will debate your existence for three weeks and then offer you a committee. The useful kind is a man called Edevan Rell, who used to be the Keeper of Maps at the Collegium in Vessarion's capital, back when there was a capital worth keeping." She drank. "He's old, he's bitter, he lives above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, and he owes me for a favor I'm not going to tell you about."

"And Aldric's spies?"

"Aldric's spies are everywhere," she said comfortably. "Two of them drink downstairs on Thursdays. The grey cloaks recruit lonely people, and this city manufactures loneliness by the barrel. So: don't say the word *Vessarion* above a whisper, don't let the boy sing, and if a very charming man offers to buy you dinner, order the cheapest thing on the menu and leave before the fish course."

* * *

Edevan Rell turned out to be a narrow old man folded into a narrow room above a chandler's shop, surrounded by paper. Not maps — not anymore. He'd burned his collection when the capital fell, he told us later, all four hundred years of it, rather than let the grey cloaks catalog it. What remained were copies hidden inside other documents: coastlines disguised as hymn margins, mountain ranges drawn as decorative flourishes in a book of recipes. A life's work smuggled out in the bodies of lesser books.

He looked at Ivo for a long time before he looked at me.

"Old Vessarine," he said. "Fluent. Unaccented." His voice did something complicated. "Where did you find him?"

"He found me. At the Thornfell gate."

"Then the stories are true." He lowered himself into a chair with the care of a man whose bones had opinions. "That the Choir would send out a singer before the end. I confess I hoped it was only a story. Stories are safer."

"We need to find the Door Out of Vessarion," I said.

Rell laughed. It was not a kind sound. "Of course you do. Everyone who comes up those stairs wants something impossible. Sit down, Keeper. Let me tell you what I know, and then you can decide how much hope you can afford."

What he knew was this: the Door Out was real. It appeared in the oldest maps — the pre-conquest surveys, the ones drawn by the cartographer-priests who believed a country was a song wearing geography as a coat. On those maps, always in a different place, always at the edge of the ink where the surveyors admitted their knowledge ran out, there was a mark: a door, drawn open, with a line of small figures walking through it. The caption, in every copy, in every century, was the same phrase in Old Vessarine.

"*The road that leaves by staying,*" Ivo translated softly, reading over Rell's shoulder as the old man unfolded a scrap of vellum no bigger than my palm. The fork in my pack sang one low, yearning note, and Ivo put his hand flat against the leather to quiet it.

"The Door doesn't have a location," Rell said. "It has a *condition*. The priests wrote that it stands wherever the Choir is whole enough to open it. Which meant, in the old days, that pilgrims found it in different valleys, different caves, different cellars. You didn't go to the Door. You became findable by it." He tapped the scrap. "And then Aldric came, and began cutting the song apart, and the Door — as far as any record shows — has not opened for anyone since."

"So it's hopeless," I said.

"So it's expensive," Rell corrected. "Hope always is. Here is the price tag, Keeper, so listen carefully." He held up one crooked finger. "First: the only complete pre-conquest survey still in existence is the Meridian Map, and it isn't in a library. It's in a drowned chapel off the Saltmarsh coast, sunk with the town of Lowmere when the engineers broke the levees during the war. Salvage crews work that water. Some of them work for Aldric."

A second finger. "Second: even if you recover it, the map is written in surveyor's cipher, and I am — as far as either of us knows — the last living reader of surveyor's cipher. I am seventy-three years old, my eyes are going, and I do not travel."

"And third?"

Rell looked at Ivo for a long moment. The boy met his gaze with that unnerving steadiness he had, the steadiness of someone who has already lost everything except the next step.

"Third," Rell said quietly, "is that the boy is humming. Has anyone told him he's humming? He's been doing it since he walked in. A fragment of the Third Lamentation. My grandmother used to sing it. I haven't heard it in fifty years." His voice cracked, just slightly, and he covered it by coughing. "If the grey cloaks hear that — if any of Aldric's little listeners hear that — you won't need a map, because you'll be dead."

Ivo flushed and pressed his lips together. He hadn't known. Of course he hadn't known. The Choir leaked out of him the way light leaks around a shuttered lamp.

* * *

We stayed three days at the Drowned Bell, and in three days I learned the true geography of Marrowgate, which was not streets and harbors but grievances. The Vessarine-descended families of the Lower Terraces wanted recognition from the council; the council, dominated by coastal factions, feared that recognizing Vessarine heritage would give Aldric a pretext to call the whole city disloyal. A delegation of elders petitioned weekly to teach the old tongue in the free schools. The petitions were denied weekly. Meanwhile, in the night markets, someone was selling things that made my skin crawl even before I understood them: small glass vials of "remembered rain," lockets that supposedly held a grandmother's lullaby, scraps of cloth that would let you dream in a dead language — for a price, always for a price, and the prices climbing.

"Grief is the only growth industry left," Ossa said grimly, when I mentioned it. "Half those trinkets are frauds. The other half—" she shrugged, "—the other half are worse than frauds."

On the second night, I caught a man watching us from across the market square. Grey cloak, worn open over fisherman's clothes, doing a poor job of pretending to haggle over eels. I moved Ivo along, bought nothing, memorized the man's face, and spent the rest of the evening teaching Ivo to walk past interesting things without looking at them, which is harder to unteach than to teach.

"Is he one of the unnamers?" Ivo whispered.

"No. Unnamers don't do their own watching. He's a listener. There's a difference. One takes your history away; the other just sells the fact that you have it."

"Which is worse?"

I thought about it honestly. "Ask me again after we've met both."

* * *

On the third day, Rell summoned us to the chandler's shop at dusk, and I knew before I climbed the stairs that something had gone wrong, because the old man had barricaded his own door with a chest of papers and answered my knock with a knife visible in his sleeve.

"Someone came asking about you," he said. "Not by name. By description. A woman from the north with a child who speaks the old tongue. They offered my landlord a year's rent for word of where you sleep."

"Did he take it?"

"He's thinking about it. Landlords think slowly, which is the only reason either of us is alive." He pulled the chest away from the door and let us in. On his table lay a fresh sheet of paper, and on the paper, drawn in a hand that shook but did not hesitate, was a coastline, an estuary, and a small square marked with a door standing open.

"I've given you what I can from memory," Rell said. "Lowmere's chapel, the sunken survey office, the depth of the silt. But memory is not the Meridian Map, and the Meridian Map is not optional. The Door's condition — *wherever the Choir is whole enough* — means you must know where every shard lies, or near enough. The map holds the old registry of resonant sites. Without it, you're a blind woman walking into the sea."

"Then I'll need a boat," I said, "and a diver, and a miracle."

"You'll need all three and a fourth thing." Rell rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and took down a book from his shelf — a fat, dull volume of tide tables, its spine broken with use. From inside the hollowed binding he drew a thin packet wrapped in oilcloth. "My cipher key. Forty years I've kept it, waiting for someone with a reason to use it. I'd begun to think I was only keeping it company." He pressed it into my hands. His fingers were cold. "Learn it fast, Keeper. And learn it *away from here*, because I promise you the listeners already know an old mapmaker remembers too much."

At the door, Ivo stopped. He turned back and said something to Rell in Old Vessarine — soft, formal, the cadence of a blessing. The old man closed his eyes as though the words were warm water on frostbitten skin.

"What did you say?" I asked on the stairs.

"I thanked him in the name of the Choir." Ivo's face was troubled. "But Tamsin — while I said it, I heard something answer. From under the city. Under the water. Something down there is singing the same words back."

I stood on the dark stairs of a chandler's shop in a city full of spies, holding a cipher key wrapped in oilcloth, and felt the fork hum against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

Below us, beneath forty years of silt and a drowned town, something had heard its own language spoken aloud — and was calling back.
