## 10. Thieves of Memory

*Tamsin*

I have kept ledgers my whole life, so let me be precise about the hour everything went wrong: the ninth bell after sunset, the fourth night in Marrowgate, in a room above a dye-shop that smelled of boiled wool and secrets.

The room had one window, a bed too short for me, and a floorboard by the hearth that Ivo had claimed as his own — he slept there rather than the bed, curled around the black-glass fork like a hound around a bone. I had stopped trying to argue him out of it. The fork hummed when he slept. He said it was quieter near his heart. I did not ask what that meant, because the answers children give are usually truer than the questions deserve.

I was awake over my maps — the scholar Ondrel's maps, the ones drawn before the conquest, with their maddening blank space where Vessarion should have been — when I heard the fork stop humming.

You would not think silence could wake you. But I had spent three weeks listening to that small dark note the way you listen to a river outside your window, until its absence was a shout. I sat up. The floorboard was empty. The window was open. The curtains were moving in a wind that smelled of canal water and fried onions from the night market below.

And on the sill, placed with a thief's courtesy, lay a single copper coin stamped with a face neither of us recognized.

"IVO," I said, in the voice I used for gate-inspections and other emergencies.

He was already sitting up, hair wild, eyes enormous. His hand went to the empty floorboard before his mind caught up with what had happened. I watched the understanding arrive in stages: confusion, then horror, then something worse than either. Grief. Immediate and total, the way you grieve a death before you've confirmed it.

"They took her," he whispered.

"Took what?"

"My mother." He pressed his fist to his mouth. "No — no, they took the fork, but she *lived* in it, don't you see? Her voice. She sang the second part and I sang the first, and now—" His breath went ragged. "I can't hear her anymore."

I want to tell you I responded with wisdom. What I actually did was light the lamp, check the street twice from behind the curtain, and start pulling on my boots while my mind ran down a list of everyone in Marrowgate who bought and sold things that belonged to other people's souls.

Because here is what I knew about Marrowgate's night markets, gathered in three days of careful listening over bad tea: there were the honest markets, which sold smuggled silk and untaxed brandy; and then there were the markets beneath them, in the old cisterns under Tanner's Row, where the trade was in memory itself. The refugees called them the Quiet Stalls. People who had fled Aldric's conquest with nothing but their own heads sometimes sold pieces of themselves to eat — a wedding day, a dead father's face, the taste of a bread no longer baked anywhere. And there were those who didn't sell. Who were taken from.

Ondrel had warned me, in his dry way: *"In Marrowgate even grief has a market price. Guard your sorrows, Keeper, or someone will weigh them."*

I hadn't guarded well enough. I'd assumed a locked door and a sleeping child's proximity were security. I had forgotten the first law of gates, which is that every barrier is only as strong as the desire on the other side of it.

"Get dressed," I told Ivo. "Warm coat. Nothing that jingles."

"Are we going to get her back?"

"*It* back," I corrected, and immediately hated myself for it, because to him the distinction was meaningless, and maybe he was right and I was wrong. "Yes," I said instead. "We're going to get it back."

He nodded once, wiped his face with his sleeve, and became — this is the thing about Ivo that frightened me most, in those days — suddenly, unnervingly adult. A child who has lost a country learns to grieve on a schedule.

* * *

*Ivo*

Tamsin walks fast when she is angry. Not running-fast. Marching-fast, like the ground owes her money. I had to trot to keep up, down the dye-shop stairs and out into the night market, where the lamps hung low and everything smelled like cinnamon and fish and smoke.

She didn't ask anyone anything at first. That surprised me. In stories, when people lose something they ask questions. But Tamsin just walked through the crowd looking at people's hands.

Later she explained it to me. Thieves' hands are busy hands, she said. They touch things. They count things. Honest hands hang still. So we walked through the whole market watching hands, and I watched too, and I learned it quick, because I wanted my mother back more than I wanted to breathe.

The man who took the fork had left a coin. Tamsin turned it over and over while we walked. "Nobody leaves payment for theft," she muttered. "Payment means a sale. Someone hired them. Or—" she stopped. "Or they're announcing themselves. Advertising. *We take things worth taking.*"

The market got worse the deeper we went. At first it was just stalls — knives, ribbons, false passports, a woman selling luck by the pinch. But then the stalls started selling stranger things. A jar labeled *the smell of rain on your childhood street*. A ribbon tied around a name somebody couldn't remember anymore but wanted to hold anyway. An old man with a tray of small glass bells, each one holding, he swore, the last words of someone who died far from home. Ring the bell, hear the voice once, and then it was gone forever. One ring per customer.

People were buying them. That was the worst part. Not thieves — just tired people, refugees mostly, paying their last coins to hear a dead mother say goodnight one final time. I stood there watching a woman ring a bell and put her hand over her mouth and cry, and I thought: *that's what they stole from me.* Except the woman chose. She paid. Nobody had climbed through our window and taken her mother away while she slept.

Tamsin tugged my sleeve. "Don't stare. Staring marks you as a buyer, and buyers get followed, and followers get robbed."

"I'm not a buyer. I'm a robber."

"You're a *rescuer*, and if you say otherwise in public I'll leave you in a barrel." But the corner of her mouth moved, which meant she wasn't really angry at me.

Under Tanner's Row there was a grate, and behind the grate some stairs, and at the bottom of the stairs the Quiet Stalls — though they weren't quiet at all, really. They were murmuring. Hundreds of people whispering offers. *Memories of home. Faces of the dead. Songs, songs, do you have songs?* There was a whole stall just for songs, and my chest hurt looking at it, because somewhere in that pile of paper slips might be a song from Vessarion, might be half the Choir, being sold for coppers to people who didn't know what they were buying.

The fork was humming again. Just barely. Just enough.

"It's close," I told Tamsin. "It always knows where the other shards are. And there are shards *here*, Tamsin. Lots of them. All these memories people sold — some of them came from Vessarine throats. The Choir's been leaking into this city for twenty years, drop by drop."

Tamsin looked at me strangely. "How do you know that?"

"The fork told me."

"The fork doesn't talk."

"It hums different when it's near its own kind. It's sad-humming right now. Like — you know when you pass a house where someone's crying inside, and you can't hear them but you know?" She knew. I could tell she knew.

We found the thieves by following the coin. That was Tamsin's idea. The coin was new-minted, she said, from the Mint Street press, and only one crew in Marrowgate paid in fresh coins because only one crew was rich enough not to care that fresh coins were traceable. The Ash-Hand Gang. Named for the ash they supposedly rubbed on their fingers so stolen memories wouldn't stick.

They worked out of a shuttered bathhouse near the canal. We watched it for an hour from a roof across the street, Tamsin counting guards the way she counted everything, and me holding the cold in my chest very still so the humming wouldn't give us away. Four guards. Two doors. One chimney with smoke that smelled wrong — sweet, like burnt sugar.

"What do they burn?" I asked.

"Stolen memories they can't sell. The dregs." Tamsin's jaw was tight. "What nobody bids on."

I looked at that thin sweet smoke rising into the Marrowgate night and felt something in me go hard and hot. Because I understood, suddenly, what the smoke was made of. Old men's weddings. Children's birthdays. Whole villages' worth of ordinary Tuesdays. Burning. For fuel. Because grief that won't fetch a price isn't worth keeping.

"Tamsin," I said, "when we go in there—"

"When *I* go in there."

"—when we go in there, I'm not going to be polite."

She studied me for a long moment. Somewhere below, a drunk was singing a song in a language almost like mine but wrong, worn smooth by forgetting, and I think hearing it decided something for both of us.

"No," she agreed quietly. "I don't suppose either of us will be."

* * *

*Tamsin*

Here is what nobody tells you about burglary: it is mostly waiting, and the waiting is where you lose your nerve. We waited in the bathhouse's woodshed from midnight until nearly two, long enough for Ivo to fall asleep against my shoulder — the boy could sleep through a siege, a talent I envied and resented in equal measure — and long enough for me to compose, in order, the speech I would give the Ash-HANDs, the plea I would make, and the threats I would utter, and to watch each of these plans die of embarrassment in turn.

At the second bell the guards changed, and the new pair were younger and colder, and one of them carried the fork openly on his belt like a trophy, and every plan I had made evaporated, because seeing it there — my charge's inheritance dangling from a stranger's hip like a keyring — filled me with a rage so clean and simple that planning became unnecessary.

I hit the first guard with the woodshed's door bar. I am not proud of the angle. He folded up like wet laundry. The second guard reached for his whistle and Ivo — who was supposed to be asleep — stepped out of the shadows and *sang* two notes at him.

Old Vessarine notes. The fork on the guard's belt answered them, humming loud enough to buzz its own buckle loose, and the sound went through the young man like winter through a cracked wall. He sat down on the step. He didn't faint exactly. He just... stopped wanting to do whatever he'd been doing, whatever that was, and sat there with tears running into his collar, listening to something none of us could hear.

"Inside," I said. "Now."

The bathhouse interior was all tile and steam-pipes and stacked crates, and in the largest crate, packed in straw like contraband brandy, were the memories. Dozens of them. Little sealed vials of grey glass, each one fogged with something that moved like breath. And on a workbench under a lamp, laid out with a jeweler's care, the night's inventory: a lock of hair. A child's drawing. A wedding ring fused to a fingerbone. And the fork of black glass, humming its small heartbroken note, surrounded by other fragments I recognized now — shards, splinters, slivers of the same substance, harvested God knew how from throats and vaults and graves across two countries.

A voice behind us said, "Well. The gatekeeper and the little ghost. Serel said you'd come eventually."

Serel. The rider captain's name, dropped like a stone into still water. I turned slowly. A broad woman in merchant's clothes stood between us and the door, arms crossed, flanked by two more of her crew. Her fingers, I noticed, were grey to the second knuckle.

"You know Serel," I said.

"Everyone who sells in occupied territory knows Serel. She licenses us. She taxes us. She takes a cut and pretends the Empire doesn't traffic in what it claims doesn't exist." The woman smiled without warmth. "She also told us the fork was coming south with a keeper and a child, and offered a bounty. You're worth more than everything on that table, combined. So here's my offer: walk away, and we only sell the glass."

I looked at Ivo. I expected fear. Instead I saw him staring at the vials on the workbench, and his lips were moving — counting, or reading, or praying, I couldn't tell — and the fork was humming louder, and the vials were beginning, softly, impossibly, to answer it. One by one, like lamps lit down a street. A chorus waking up.

"They're singing," Ivo said wonderingly. "Tamsin. *They remember they're singing.*"

The thief's composure cracked. "Stop that. Stop — what is it doing—"

What it was doing, as best I could judge, was calling roll. Every fragment of the Choir in that room was answering a child's hum the way iron filings answer a magnet, and the grey-glass woman was discovering that she had not stolen merchandise but congregation.

"Here's *my* offer," I said, stepping between Ivo and the thieves, my hand finding the door bar again. "You return every shard in this building. You forget our faces — which should be easy, given your profession. And you take us to whoever fenced you the rest of the Choir's fragments, because a gang this size didn't find them alone. Someone's feeding you. Someone with access to Aldric's vaults."

"And why would I do any of that?"

Behind her, the vials were glowing now, all of them, a soft grey dawn-light, and the steam-pipes had begun to resonate, and the guard outside was weeping audibly. Ivo walked forward — past me, past the thieves, fearless in the way that only makes sense in someone who has already lost everything stealable — and picked up the fork.

"Because," he said simply, "we're going to give it all back."

The woman stared at him. Then at the glowing crates. Then, briefly and terribly, at her own grey fingers, as if noticing them for the first time.

"There's a map," she said at last, and her voice had gone strange and small. "There's a man in the Salt Exchange who keeps a list of everywhere the shards come from. He calls himself a broker. He calls the memories *stock*. If you're going to rob *him*, gatekeeper, I'll draw you the route myself — and I'll throw in my crew, because I've been dreaming in a language I don't speak for six years, and I'd very much like to know why."

Above us, through the broken skylight, the sweet smoke rose into the Marrowgate night, thinner now, and somewhere in the Salt Exchange a broker slept rich and dreamless, unaware that everything he owned had begun to sing.

I put out my hand. "Then we have a deal. And madam? The first lesson's free."

"What lesson?"

"Why the dreams speak Vessarine." I glanced down at Ivo, fork in hand, haloed in borrowed light. "Because the country isn't gone. It's just waiting for someone brave enough to sing it home."
