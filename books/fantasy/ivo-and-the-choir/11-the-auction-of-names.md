## 11. The Auction of Names

The man who bought my name wore a coat the color of old teeth.

I want to say I saw him coming, that I understood what I was doing when I followed Tamsin down into Marrowgate's underbelly with a plan and a knife and a lie ready in my mouth. I didn't. I was nine days past losing the fork, and grief makes you stupid in a very particular way — it makes you generous. It makes you willing to hand over anything, because everything already feels like it's leaving.

Tamsin had said, *We'll get it back properly. We'll find who took it.* And she meant it. She had her ledgers and her contacts and that flat keeper's stare that made smugglers confess things they'd sworn to take to their graves. But I could feel where the fork was. That was the thing nobody else in the city could do — not Tamsin, not the scholar Vey with his drowned-chapel maps, not any of the refugees who wept at night for names they couldn't find. The fork hummed against my sternum even from across a city, like a second heartbeat that wasn't mine but answered to me. And it was humming *wrong*. Fast. Frightened. Being handled by someone who thought it was an ornament.

So when the whisper came through the night market — *auction tonight, below the Saltcellar, bring coin or bring something rarer* — I went without telling Tamsin, which was the first stupid thing, and I bid with the only thing I owned, which was the second.

* * *

The Saltcellar was a warehouse that had once held salted fish and now held secrets, which everyone agreed paid better. You got in through a door behind a tannery, down stairs slick with something you tried not to identify, past two guards who patted you down with hands that lingered on anything valuable. They found nothing on me. I had nothing. That was my whole fortune: empty pockets and a stolen language.

Below, the room was lit by lamps turned down to embers, because memory-thieves are shy creatures. They like to know what they're selling is real but prefer not to see each other's faces while they buy back pieces of themselves. Rows of benches. A podium. On the podium, a woman with a voice like a struck bell called out lots in a bored murmur, and around me sat people who had paid more than money to be here.

I recognized some of what they carried. An old man holding a glass bead that glowed faintly blue — someone's wedding day, probably, sold by a thief who'd lifted it off a dying woman in the occupied territories. Two sisters sharing a single dried flower between them, taking turns breathing its scent, which was apparently the smell of a garden that no longer existed anywhere on earth. Memory-thievery isn't like other crime. Nobody steals bread down here. They steal *Tuesday afternoons*. They steal the sound of a dead father clearing his throat. And there's always a buyer, because the conquered are rich in exactly one currency, and it's the kind you can't earn back.

The fork was lot nineteen.

I knew before they brought it out. My chest had been tightening all evening, that frightened-humming getting louder as they moved up the list, and when the auctioneer's assistant set it on a velvet cushion — *velvet*, for my mother's song, for the Choir of Vessarion, on a scrap of cushion like a lost earring — the whole room shifted. Not visibly. But every person in that cellar leaned forward by half an inch, the way grass leans toward weather coming.

"Lot nineteen," the bell-voiced woman said. "Recovered from a courier's cache, provenance disputed. Black glass tuning fork, pre-construction workmanship. Resonates under certain conditions." She tapped it with one fingernail, and the note that came off it made three people in the front row flinch and one begin quietly to cry. She didn't notice, or pretended not to. "It sings," she added, almost apologetically. "Bidding opens at four hundred crowns."

Four hundred crowns. I had seen Tamsin haggle a smuggler down from six coppers for a heel of bread. Four hundred crowns might have been every coin in the free cities' combined purses, for all I knew of money. I did the arithmetic anyway, sitting on my bench with my heart going like a trapped bird, and the sum was simple: I could not buy my own inheritance back with anything a child carries in his pockets.

But the whisper had said *coin or something rarer*, and there was a line in the rules chalked above the podium that I'd read four times without understanding until that moment:

**THE HOUSE ACCEPTS NON-MONETARY INSTRUMENTS OF VERIFIED PROVENANCE.**

Verified provenance. Things that were true. Things that were *mine*.

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about myself, so the rest makes sense: I don't remember much. This isn't the fork's doing — the forgetting came first, the way the flood comes before you understand there's been rain. I remember a road. I remember cold, and running, and the phrase *the man who unnames things* repeating in my head like a lesson I couldn't stop studying. I remember Old Vessarine the way you remember how to swim — in the body, not the mind; I open my mouth and it pours out, and if you asked me where I learned it I would have to guess.

And I remember a woman singing.

That was the shape my mother took in my memory: not a face, not a name, not hands. A *voice*, low and steady, singing a descending line of notes that the fork answered whenever it was close to me. Scholars would later tell me this was because I was born into the singing line, that the Choir had poured itself into me the way water pours into a cup, but standing in the Saltcellar I knew none of that. I knew only that the singing was the one thing I had that no thief had taken yet, and that the fork needed it, and that four hundred crowns was a number built for people who still had mothers with faces.

I raised my hand.

"The young gentleman at the back," the auctioneer said, with the mild surprise of someone noting an unusual insect. "You're bidding...?"

"Provenance," I said. My voice cracked in the middle, which was fine, because cracking voices are common currency down there too. "First-hand memory. Verified."

The room went quiet in a new way. Not the leaning-forward way. The way a room goes quiet when someone has done something irreversible.

The house verifier was a small grey person with spectacles and a silver ring that she pressed to my temple, and whatever she felt through it made her eyebrows climb. "Direct experiential recall," she murmured. "Pre-conquest origin. Emotional index..." She looked at me over the spectacles, and for a moment I thought she might refuse — might see a child and send me home, wherever that was. "High. Very high. The house will accept it as opening bid."

"Against four hundred crowns?" someone scoffed from the dark.

"The house accepts it," the verifier repeated, and there was something in her voice I didn't understand until later — something like pity, or apology, or the professional regret of a person who measures grief for a living and has just weighed an unusually heavy piece.

Bidding opened. Crowns flew. Four hundred became six, six became eleven, and a merchant in a fur collar who smelled of lamp oil kept raising his hand in little increments like he was testing bathwater. Eleven hundred. Thirteen. The fork sat on its velvet and hummed its frightened note, and I put my hand on my own sternum and felt my ribs answer it, and I thought about what I was offering. Not the singing — the verifier had been clear, the house wanted the memory entire, the *whole lot*, whatever the whole lot contained. Whatever my mother's voice was wrapped around. Whatever else lived in that room of me.

Fifteen hundred. The oil-smelling merchant was sweating now, enjoying himself, and I understood suddenly that he didn't want the fork for its song. He wanted it because it was rare, because owning the last of something made him larger. Men like that collect countries the way other men collect stamps.

I stood up.

"I raise," I said. "Everything."

The auctioneer tilted her head. "Specify your instrument, young sir. The house requires—"

"Not just the voice." I heard myself say it from very far away, calm as a keeper reading a ledger. "The whole memory. All of it. Every part attached to her. The face too. The name. Bid it all."

Silence. Somewhere in the dark, the old man with the blue bead whispered *gods*, soft as a prayer. The verifier looked at me a long moment, and then at the fork, which had begun — I swear this — to sing on its own, one thin thread of sound rising off the black glass like smoke, like a warning, like a hand reaching across a room.

"Verified provenance," she said finally, quietly. "Full lot. The house accepts."

Sixteen hundred crowns couldn't beat that, and everyone in the room knew why. You can mint more crowns. There is no mint for a mother.

* * *

They gave me the fork in a drawstring bag, and I walked out through the tannery stairwell holding it against my chest, and it sang against my skin the way it always had, relieved, chattering, *you came, you came, you came* — and I said, out loud, alone on the stairs, "Yes. Yes, I came. Hush now."

That was when I tried to remember her face.

I want to be precise about what forgetting feels like, because people imagine it's like a candle going out, sudden and total. It isn't. It's like a room in a house you've lived in your whole life that you walk into and find empty — not robbed, not burned, just *empty*, the wallpaper faded in rectangles where pictures used to hang. I walked into that room on the tannery stairs. I reached for her face the way you reach for a light in a familiar place, without looking, because you've done it ten thousand times.

There was nothing under my hand.

The voice remained. Thank all the scattered gods, the voice remained — the descending line, low and steady, the fork's oldest friend. But when I tried to hang anything on it — eyes, a mouth, whether her hair was dark or light, whether she was tall — the memory slid away like water off glass, and worse, worse than the sliding: I couldn't feel that anything was missing. That's the trick of it, the theft inside the theft. Grief needs a shape to hold. Mine had been sold, and the receipt was in another man's pocket, and some quiet clerk in my mind had already tidied away the evidence, so that I stood there knowing — because Tamsin would later tell me, because the records Serel carried would prove it — that I had loved a woman with a face, and feeling about it the way you feel about a stranger's photograph.

I sat down on the tannery stairs, in the dark, with a country's memory humming against my chest, and I cried for someone whose face I could no longer miss.

* * *

Tamsin found me there. Of course she did. She had spent the evening tearing Marrowgate apart along its seams, and she came down those stairs like a storm given a ledger, and I braced for the anger — I'd earned it, disappearing, bidding, trading away pieces of myself in a fish cellar — but what she did was sit down beside me on a step built for salting fish and say nothing at all for a long time.

Then: "Did you get it back?"

I held up the bag.

"And what did it cost?"

"A memory," I said. "A full lot."

She was quiet again. Down the corridor, the city muttered — carts, gulls, a drunk singing something in three languages badly. When she spoke her voice was careful, the way it got when she was handling something fragile, though whether the fragile thing was me or herself I couldn't tell. "Was it worth it?"

And here is the honest answer, the one I've never said plainly to anyone: yes. Yes, it was worth it, and that is the most terrible thing about it. The fork was warm in my hands and singing, and somewhere beneath Aldric's armies a machine was grinding songs into silence, and a fat merchant had gone home with my mother's face in a glass case, and I would do it again tomorrow, and the morning after, and every morning until the Door Out opened or I was dust. Some things you cannot put a price on, so you pay with yourself instead, and call it winning.

"She was singing," I told Tamsin. "In the memory. That's all I kept. That's what I bid around. Everything else went." I turned the fork over in my hands. "I don't even know what I lost. That's the worst part. I can't mourn right. It's like mourning through a wall."

Tamsin nodded slowly, and I saw her jaw do the thing it does when she's swallowing something large. "My grandmother sang in Vessarine," she said. "I never met her. I know it the way you know a fact, not the way you know a song." She looked at the bag, at the fork's glow seeping through the drawstrings. "Maybe that's why I keep a door shut for a living. Somebody has to stand guard over what's left."

"It's not enough," I said. "Guarding what's left. There's less every day."

"No," she agreed. "There is." And then, because she was Tamsin, she stood up and brushed off her coat and became practical again, stone over deep water. "Come on. You need food and sleep, and I need to write a letter to Vey, because if that auction house sells provenance to strangers, then Aldric's spies already know a black-glass fork surfaced in Marrowgate tonight. Which means we've stopped hiding and started being hunted." She paused on the step above me, backlit by the door's thin light. "Can you walk?"

I could walk. What I couldn't do was explain why, halfway up the stairs, I started humming her song — the descending line, low and steady — and the fork harmonized, and my body knew every note, and my mind stood outside the music like a beggar outside a lit window, listening to a family it used to belong to.

Because that was the other thing I learned on the tannery stairs, the thing I carried up into the lamplight and the hunted days that followed: the fork hadn't just come home to me. It had *recognized* me. It hummed to my bones like it hummed to no one else, answered my voice, slept when I slept. The thieves had sold a boy's memory. What they had actually sold, though none of us knew it yet — not me, not Tamsin, not even the grey-cloaked riders combing the city for a relic — was the last heir of Vessarion's singing line, walking out of an auction with the Choir in a drawstring bag and a hole in his chest where his mother's face used to live.

And far to the north, in a vault beneath a rewritten palace, something vast and hungry felt the fork move and turned its attention, slow as winter, toward Marrowgate.
