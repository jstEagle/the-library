## 12. Serel's Truth

I want it on the record that I did not invite her in.

She was standing at the door of our rented room in Marrowgate with her cloak over her arm and no grey anywhere on her — just a woman in travel-stained brown wool, looking like every other tired person who knocks on doors in this city wanting something. Her hands were empty. That frightened me more than a drawn sword would have, because Serel Vane never went anywhere without her hands full of some way to kill you, and an empty-handed Serel meant a Serel who had decided she didn't need one.

"Tamsin Ashgrove," she said. "Keeper of the Thornfell gate."

"Former keeper," I said. "The position's been vacant since I lied to your face and ran south with stolen property."

"Stolen relic," she corrected, and there it was — the word *relic*, Aldric's word, the word that turned a boy into an inventory item. But she said it flatly, the way you repeat a phrase you've been ordered to use so many times it's worn smooth and meaningless, like a coin rubbed down to blank metal.

"You're off-duty," I said. "That's not a request for confirmation, that's a warning. Off-duty riders have been known to do things like sit down and talk."

"I know." She glanced past my shoulder into the room, where Ivo sat cross-legged on the bed with the black-glass fork across his knees, watching her with the particular stillness of a child deciding whether to run. "May I?"

"No," I said, and stepped aside to let her pass, because I have always been a hypocrite about hospitality. It's the gatekeeper's disease. You spend twenty years telling people the door is shut and then you open it anyway, every time, because the alternative is standing out in the snow yourself.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Serel before that evening: everything the ledgers told me.

I keep ledgers. It's what I do instead of sleeping. In them I record every rider, every patrol pattern, every face that comes up the Thornfell road wearing the usurper's grey. Serel appeared in my books eleven times over six years. Captain of the third patrol company. Never took bribes — which made her rare enough to be worth a separate entry. Never burned a farmstead, though she'd stood by while others did, twice, and I'd noted that too, in the margin, in the shorthand I use for things I don't yet understand: *watch her.*

The last time I saw her she was sitting in my guardhouse demanding a child, and I lied to her with a straight face and a forged logbook, and she rode away into the pass believing me, or seeming to believe me, and I spent the next month waiting for her to come back and be right about me.

Now here she was, sitting on the one decent chair in a Marrowgate garret, holding a cup of barley tea she hadn't touched, and saying nothing at all.

I let her say nothing for a while. Silence is a tool; I've used it on smugglers and tax collectors and one memorable bishop. But she was better at it than I was, so eventually I broke first, which annoyed me.

"If you've come to arrest us—"

"I've resigned."

The words landed strangely, like a bell struck wrong. "You can't resign from the Grey Cloaks. The oath is for life. I've read the treaty clauses. Twice. Out of boredom."

"The oath is for life," she agreed. "Which is why I'm not resigning. I'm deserting. There's a difference, and the difference is that they'll hang me for one and merely flay me for the other." She said it the way she'd said everything so far — level, unhurried, a woman reporting weather. "I thought you should know where you stand before I tell you why I'm here. You don't owe a deserter courtesy. You owe her less than nothing."

"That's very generous of you," I said. "Deciding for me what I owe."

"One of us should decide things quickly. You deliberate. It's your worst quality. It nearly got you killed at Thornfell when the bridge ice cracked and you stood there calculating load tolerances instead of running."

I stared at her. "You weren't there for that."

"I read your file. Everyone reads your file. Do you know what they call you in the garrison at Cael Harrow? *The Woman Who Counts.* They think it's an insult." She finally drank her tea. "It's the only reason I'm here. A counting woman keeps records. Records can be checked against each other. And I have found a discrepancy, Tamsin Ashgrove, between what the Grey Cloaks are told we're doing and what we are actually doing, and the discrepancy has teeth."

Ivo shifted on the bed. The fork hummed — a low note, barely audible, the sound it made when it was paying attention. I had learned its moods in the weeks since Thornfell. It hummed like that around lies.

Serel heard it too. Her eyes went to the black glass and stayed there, and something moved behind her face that I recognized, because I'd seen it in my own mirror for years whenever I opened the forbidden archives: the look of a person watching their whole architecture of certainty develop a crack.

"Show me," she said quietly.

"It doesn't work on command," Ivo said. First thing he'd said all evening. His Common Tongue was better now, but he still spoke it carefully, like a man crossing a frozen river. "It works when it wants to. It's shy around soldiers."

"I'm not a soldier anymore, remember."

"You were one for a long time. Things remember longer than people forget."

I could have kissed him for that, or throttled him, and I genuinely couldn't tell you which impulse came first. But Serel didn't bristle. She set down the cup, and folded her hands, and looked at the fork the way you look at an altar you were raised to worship and have recently begun to doubt.

"Then don't show me anything," she said. "Let me show you. Then you can decide if I'm worth the risk."

* * *

What she showed us was a ledger.

I confess that won me over before a single word of it did. She'd copied it by hand — pages and pages, in a small vicious script, dated and cross-referenced — and she spread it on my table like a merchant showing cloth, and I understood immediately that this was a woman who had been keeping her own count inside the machine for years, waiting for the totals to mean something.

"This is the requisition chain for the eastern provinces," she said. "Officially, the Cloaks collect 'historical artifacts of destabilizing sentiment.' Songs, mostly. Ballads, lullabies, work songs, funeral keens. Anything in Old Vessarine. We're told they go to the Archive at Cael Harrow for cataloguing and containment. Preserving the past safely. Laying it to rest. You've heard the phrasing."

"I wrote some of the phrasing," I said bitterly. "In the notices I posted at Thornfell. *By order of His Radiant Majesty, all items of historical memory must be surrendered for preservation.*"

"Preservation." She tapped a column of figures. "Look at the tonnage. Eleven years of collection from the eastern provinces alone. Now look at the return shipments." She turned the page. "Nothing. No catalogue copies sent back to provincial schools, no reference sets, no museum displays. Nothing collected is ever seen again. An archive is supposed to *be* something, Ashgrove. This one is a mouth."

The fire popped. Ivo had gone very still, and the fork had begun to hum again, that same wary note.

"So it's hoarding," I said. "A king who hoards songs. Strange, but tyrants have stranger hobbies. What's the discrepancy with teeth?"

"The discrepancy," Serel said, "is the villages."

She laid out three more pages. Maps, this time, hand-copied, with little marks in red ink.

"These are settlements in the occupied east where collection happened between four and two years ago. Full compliance — every song surrendered, every singer registered, the whole ritual of laying the past to rest performed correctly, witnessed, documented. And then look at what happens after." Her finger traced the red marks. "These aren't deaths. Deaths I'd know how to count; I've counted plenty. These are — changes. Villages that stop celebrating their own festivals and start celebrating Aldric's founding day, enthusiastically, within a single season. Grandmothers who forget lullabies they sang every night for fifty years. Men who volunteer for his armies at rates that make no sense, who speak of the conquest like it was a liberation they personally welcomed. Whole communities rewriting themselves overnight, and not under threat. Under *peace*. Under good harvests and fair taxes and warm feelings toward the crown."

"That's not possible," I said, and hated how much I wanted it to be impossible.

"No," Serel agreed. "It isn't. Which is why it's happening anyway. I asked questions. Quiet ones, the kind you ask by not asking them — pulling duty rosters, comparing supply manifests. The collection teams from those villages didn't deliver to the Archive at Cael Harrow. Cael Harrow receives a tenth of what we gather. The rest goes deeper. There's a facility under the old palace foundations in Vessarine — the capital, the real one, before he renamed it. I pulled one rotation of guard duty there last autumn. Six weeks." 

For the first time her voice changed. Not louder. Smaller. She looked at her own hands as if checking they still belonged to her.

"There's a machine down there," she said. "I don't have the scholarship to name it properly. The technicians called it the Chorus Engine, when they called it anything, which was rarely; they didn't like talking about it either. It's built into the bones of the palace, older than Aldric, older than the conquest — he found it, or finished it, or both. And it runs on shards. Black glass, like your fork there, dozens of them, mounted in rings, and the whole assembly turns, slowly, day and night, and the collected songs go in—" she drew a line across the table with one finger, "—and something comes out the other side."

"What something?" Ivo asked. His voice had gone thin.

"Certainty," Serel said. "That's the closest word I have. When the engine works on a region — I felt it start, the week after I left, they tested it on the Nethermarch — the people there don't feel rewritten. That's the horror of it. They feel *sure*. Sure that Aldric's history is theirs. Sure that they've always loved him. Their memories aren't replaced exactly — they're re-voiced. The same memories, sung in a different key, until the new key feels like the only one there ever was." She paused. "My mother was from the Nethermarch. I went home on leave last month to check on her. She sang me a song about Aldric's grandfather founding our village. She held my hands while she sang it. She was so happy. And I sat there knowing — knowing from my own childhood — that the song used to be about someone else entirely, and I could not for the life of me remember who."

The fork cried out.

There's no gentler word for it. One moment it was humming its wary note; the next it produced a sound like a struck bell underwater, high and grieving, and Ivo snatched it up and pressed it to his chest and hunched over it the way you'd shield a candle in wind. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below us, in Marrowgate's streets, dogs started howling, three or four of them, then a dozen, a ragged chorus rolling through the night market district.

"Shh," Ivo whispered to it, to it, to the piece of glass. "Shh. You're safe. He's not here. He's not here yet."

And Serel Vane — captain of the third company, eleven entries in my ledger, watch her — watched a child comfort the memory of a murdered nation, and put her face in her hands, and shook.

I gave her a moment. I'm not entirely without mercy, whatever my ledgers say.

"How long?" I asked her, when the shaking stopped. "How far has it spread?"

"Nethermarch is done. Two river valleys east of it, half-done — you can see it in the markets, the songs changing verse by verse, the old versions going quiet. At current rate of collection and engine capacity, the occupied territories hold for perhaps five years." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike again, and I understood that the shaking was a luxury she permitted herself roughly once per decade. "But that's not the plan, Ashgrove. Occupied territory was never the point. The point is the free cities. Marrowgate. Callowmere. All of them. He doesn't need to conquer us anymore. He needs to wait until we sing ourselves into loving him. Every refugee in this city carrying fragments of the old songs in their heads — do you understand? They're not survivors here. They're inventory, walking around loose, and the collection teams are already in the night markets buying 'heirlooms' from desperate families at generous prices."

I thought of the night market. I thought of the stalls selling charms and keepsakes, and how many of those trinkets might hum, if you held them right. I thought of Ivo asleep on this very bed three nights ago, and the fork singing softly against his ribs, and how loud that softness might carry to the wrong ear.

"He's not preserving the past," I said. "He's eating it."

"He's digesting it," Serel said. "Into loyalty. Into himself. Because—" and here her voice did something complicated, something I wasn't prepared for, something almost gentle, "—because I think he's terrified. I stood near that engine for six weeks and I could feel it, Ashgrove. It isn't hungry the way a conqueror is hungry. It's hungry the way a drowning man is hungry. He's feeding a whole country's memory into it and none of it stays with *him*, none of it makes him certain of anything, and I think somewhere underneath he knows that no amount will ever be enough, because the engine eats yesterday and gives nothing back but today, and today is the one day he cannot bear."

The fork had gone quiet in Ivo's arms. He was looking at Serel with an expression I couldn't parse — not forgiveness, nothing so cheap — but assessment. The look, maybe, of a choir listening to a new voice and deciding whether it can carry a part.

"Why come to us?" I asked. "You could take this ledger to the Free Council. Evidence like this, from a serving captain — they'd give you a parade."

"They'd give me a committee," she said dryly. "And a year of deliberation, and by then another valley gone quiet. No. I came to you because you lie well and count carefully and you're already moving toward the thing everyone else is afraid to name. You're going after the Door Out of Vessarion. Don't bother denying it; the scholar Ondrel talks in his sleep in a public reading room and half the city knows by now." She gathered her ledger pages back together with quick precise motions. "I know the occupied territories. Every checkpoint, every garrison, every road that looks closed and isn't. I know the palace service rotations in the renamed capital. And I know where the engine sleeps. You'll need all of it."

"And what do you need?"

She stood. Off-duty, unarmed, a deserter with a hanging in her future, and she straightened like a soldier on parade ground anyway — out of habit, or defiance, or grief.

"To un-sing one song," she said. "My mother's. Whatever it was before he ate it, I want it back. Even if it's only for her. Even if it's only once."

Outside, the dogs had stopped howling. The silence they left behind felt enormous.

"Well," I said, reaching for the pen and paper I kept beside the teapot out of twenty years of habit, because a problem is just a ledger you haven't started yet. "Then you'd better sit back down, Captain. The route into occupied Vessarion runs through the drowned chapel, the chapel requires a tide chart, and the tide chart is a forgery I haven't finished forging."

Serel almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, sharp as a bayonet, and I felt the strange vertigo of watching an enemy become, against all sense and self-preservation, an ally.

"Start counting," she said. "I'll hold the door."
