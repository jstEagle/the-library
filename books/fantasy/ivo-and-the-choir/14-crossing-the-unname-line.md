## 14. Crossing the Unname Line

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from a border that has been told to forget itself. I had spent eleven years listening to the Thornfell gate's quiet — the quiet of a door shut by treaty — and I thought I knew every dialect of it. I was wrong. The quiet on the far side of the Unname Line was not the silence of something closed. It was the silence of something edited.

We crossed at dusk, because Serel said dusk was when the patrols changed and the lantern-lighters were lazy men with bad knees and worse eyesight. She was off-duty in the way that a wolf is off-duty: same teeth, different hour.

"The line's not marked," she told us, crouched in the bracken above the old customs road. "That's deliberate. Aldric doesn't want a border you can see. He wants one you can't stop carrying."

"Charming," I said. "A border as a state of mind."

"You joke," Serel said, without looking at me, "because you've never lived under it."

I didn't have an answer to that, so I checked Ivo's cloak buckles instead, which is what I do instead of having answers. The boy was pale and quiet in the way he'd been quiet since Marrowgate, since the auction, since he'd paid a piece of his mother's face to win back a shard of his country's soul. He carried the black-glass fork wrapped in wool against his chest, and sometimes, when the wind moved wrong, I could see him listening to it like a man listening to a door.

"Ivo," I said. "Rules. From here on, no Old Vessarine out loud. Not a word. Not a whisper. Not even in your sleep if you can help it."

He looked up at me. His eyes were too old for his face, which is a thing I have said about him before and will probably say again, because it keeps being true. "What if I dream?"

"Dream it silently."

"That's not how dreams work, Tamsin."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't." I pulled his hood lower over his ears. "But we're going to pretend it does, for a few days, and then we're going to be inside walls, and then you can sing the roof off if you like."

Serel made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "You two are going to get me hanged."

"You're already a traitor," I pointed out. "Might as well be a competent one."

* * *

The Unname Line, it turned out, was marked after all — just not with stones or fences or any of the honest furniture of borders. It was marked by names. Or rather, by their absence.

We came down through the pine dark onto the customs road, and the first thing I saw was a signpost. It had once said something else; you could see the ghost of the older letters under the new paint, raised like scars beneath skin. The new letters read HOLLOWMERE, in Aldric's clean imperial hand, and beneath it, smaller: *Settled 3rd Year of the Reunification.*

"There was never a Hollowmere," Serel murmured. "There was a Hollenmira. My grandmother was born there." She said it flatly, the way you say things you've said so many times they've worn smooth. "The sign changed when she was a girl. She used to point at it and say, *that's not our town's name, that's the name of the thing that ate it.*"

I looked at the signpost a long moment. Somewhere behind my ribs, my mentor's voice — dead these six years, alive in my head forever — observed that toponymy is the skeleton key of conquest. Renaming a place is cheaper than burning it and lasts longer. I had read that in her letters as an academic curiosity, the way you read about plagues. Standing in front of the scarred signpost, with the paint still faintly smelling of the brush, I understood it in my spine.

Ivo had stopped walking. He was staring at the sign, and his lips were moving — shaping the old name, Hollenmira, silently, carefully, the way you'd handle a broken bird.

"Not out loud," I reminded him, barely breathing it.

His jaw tightened. He nodded. But I saw his hand go to his chest, over the wrapped fork, and I felt — or imagined I felt, though with that thing I had stopped trusting the distinction — a low tremor through the frozen ground, like a note held under water.

"It knows its own name," Ivo whispered, in trade-tongue, thank all mercies. "Even if nobody here does."

"Then it can keep knowing quietly," I said, and took his hand, and we walked into the country that had been taught to forget us.

* * *

We traveled by night for three nights, sleeping in culverts and the collapsed shell of a coaching inn whose name — according to a half-scraped plaque Serel refused to look at — had been something else once. On the fourth day we risked the open road, because Serel said papers would be checked at the market town and the only way past a checkpoint is through it, walking like you belong.

She'd forged us papers in Marrowgate. Good ones. Mine named me a widow cartwright from the eastern provinces come to sell my late husband's tools. Ivo was my son, mute since a fever — a detail Serel had added with a practicality I found both admirable and heartbreaking. A child who cannot speak asks no questions about history.

Ivo had taken the muteness assignment with the gravity of a knight taking a vow. He had not spoken aloud in two days. He communicated by tugging sleeves and pointing and, once, by writing in the frost on a culvert wall: *the fork hums louder here.* Which was true. I could feel it too now, a pressure behind the teeth, a sense of standing near a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. The land itself was resonating with everything buried in it.

The market town had been called something else. Now it was called Newhope, which is the kind of name conquerors give places when they want you to believe the before-time was hopeless. The streets were clean. The buildings were repaired. And everywhere — on walls, above doorways, on the schoolhouse at the square's heart — hung the grey-and-gold banner of the Reunification, with its emblem of a closed book crowned in laurels.

A closed book. As an emblem. For a nation. I want you to sit with that for a moment. Most countries put eagles or suns or swords on their banners. Aldric put a book that doesn't open.

"Queue there," Serel murmured. "Papers out. Eyes down. You're tired, you're grieving, you want your business done. Grief is the best disguise in the empire; nobody looks twice at it."

We queued. The checkpoint was a table, two clerks, and four soldiers in grey who had the bored menace of men who check papers forty times a day and have long ago stopped seeing faces. Ahead of us stood a farmwife with geese in crates, and behind us a tinker with a handcart, and everyone wore the same expression: a smooth, practiced blankness, like a face kept in a drawer.

And then the children came out of the schoolhouse for their midday drill, and I heard the recitation.

They stood in rows in the yard, thirty of them, maybe eight years old, and they chanted in perfect unison, voices bright as coins:

*"Before the Reunification, the land was broken. Before the Emperor, the people were scattered. The false kingdom kept them ignorant. The Emperor opened the roads. The Emperor opened the books. We were lost, and the Emperor found us. Glory to the Reunification. Glory to the Emperor."*

Over and over. Thirty small voices. They chanted it the way children chant anything — with total commitment and zero understanding — and that was the worst part. Not hatred. Not fear. Just rote brightness, drilled into them daily, until the lie sat in their mouths as comfortably as their own teeth.

Beside me, Ivo had gone rigid. His hand found mine and gripped it hard enough to hurt. He wasn't looking at the children. He was looking at the schoolhouse door, where a teacher stood keeping time with a little brass bell, nodding along, mouthing the words herself with the serene expression of a woman who has done this so many times the words have stopped being words.

I leaned down close to Ivo's hood. "Breathe," I breathed.

"They're singing it wrong," he whispered back, so quietly it was almost nothing, and my heart cracked straight down the middle. Because that was the thing, wasn't it. To him it was a song, and songs can be sung wrong, and somewhere in that small furious grieving chest was the certainty that the true version existed and these children were being robbed of it, syllable by syllable, every morning of their lives.

The fork, against his chest, gave one clear, silent pulse. I felt it in my palm where our hands were joined. One of the children — a girl at the end of the row, gap-toothed, chanting loudest — faltered mid-line. She frowned. She looked around, puzzled, like someone hearing their name called from another room. Then the bell rang, the drill ended, and she ran off to play with the others, whatever she'd heard already dissolving.

Ivo watched her go with an expression I will carry to my grave.

"Papers," said the clerk, and I became a tired widow with dead husband's tools, and Serel became a courier, and Ivo became mute, and the lie of the empire met the lies of the smugglers across a folding table, and ours were kinder.

The clerk stamped us through. As we passed the soldiers, one of them glanced at Ivo — just a flicker, a half-second — and I felt the whole morning tilt toward disaster. But it passed. Boys that age don't study children; they study pretty women and exits. We walked through Newhope with our hearts in our boots and our faces in drawers, and only when we were well clear of the square did I let myself breathe fully again.

* * *

That night we sheltered in the loft of a tithe-barn Serel knew, above a farmstead whose family she swore owed her a favor from before her cloak turned grey. The farmer — a broad, silent man called Doss — brought us bread and salt-pork and did not ask questions, which in occupied territory is the highest form of hospitality.

It was while we ate that the grandmother came in.

She was tiny and bent and nearly blind, feeling her way along the wall, and when she reached the hearth she began, softly, to sing. No words I could catch — a lullaby-thing, tune worn smooth as river stone. Doss stiffened. "Grandmother," he said, low, warning.

She ignored him. She sang three lines, four, and Ivo had gone absolutely still, his spoon halfway to his mouth, tears running down his face without his permission.

Because I knew the tune. That was the horror of it. I had never heard it before in my life, and I knew it — the way you know a room in a recurring dream. It was a Vessarine cradle-song. It must have been, because the fork was humming against Ivo's chest hard enough that I could see the wool wrapping tremble, and because Ivo's shoulders shook with recognition, and because some locked room in me, the room where I keep a homeland I never saw, had opened its door.

The old woman finished. Silence. Doss stared at the floor. And the grandmother turned her clouded eyes toward Ivo — toward the sound of him, the shape of his grief — and smiled.

"You sound like the before-people," she said, in trade-tongue, mild as milk. "My mother talked funny like that. They corrected it out of us at school." She patted Ivo's knee with a hand like a dried leaf. "Don't cry, little ghost. Songs keep better than people do."

Doss ushered her out gently, and would not meet anyone's eyes, and left the loft quickly after. Serel sat with her head in her hands.

"My grandmother sang that," she said finally. "Different words. Same tune. I forgot I knew it until just now." She laughed, horribly. "Ten years wearing the grey cloak, and it takes one blind old woman to remember me to myself."

"Welcome to the club," I said. "Meetings are whenever the fork hums. Attendance mandatory."

But I wasn't really joking, and neither was she, and Ivo sat by the loft window with the fork unwrapped at last in his cupped hands, watching it glow faintly, the way deep water holds light.

"Tamsin," he said. "It's not just the shards anymore. It's the ground. The houses. The *names under the paint.* Everything here is holding the song the way ash holds heat. Aldric painted over it but he couldn't put it out." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and blazing. "It's all still here. It's been waiting."

"Yes," I said. "And tomorrow we walk deeper into it, toward the man who built the machine that eats it. So sleep, ghost-boy. Waiting is patient work, but so are we."

* * *

We made the capital's outer ring two days later, traveling as pilgrims, which Serel assured us was the one class of traveler the empire never hassles, piety being useful to tyrants. The road climbed through hills where every village had a new name and every new name had an old one bleeding through underneath, and Ivo walked with his hood up and his lips pressed white, translating silently, ceaselessly, giving the mountains back their true names one breath at a time like a man paying out rope.

On the evening of the second day we crested the last ridge, and there it was: the capital of occupied Vessarion, vast and lamplit in the valley below, crowned by the palace where Aldric sat eating yesterday. And rising from its heart, visible even at this distance, a spire I did not recognize from any map — tall, pale, wrong, like a needle of bone through the city's hand.

"What is that?" I asked Serel.

She was quiet a long time. When she answered, her voice had gone strange.

"That's new. Built in the last five years. The soldiers call it the Spire of Unity." She paused. "The locals call it something else. They call it the place where the singing went to die."

Below us, in the darkening city, bells began to ring the hour — and underneath the bells, so faint I might have invented it, I heard the fork begin to sing.

Ivo was already looking at the spire. He didn't say anything at all.

He didn't need to. The Choir had found its way home, and tomorrow we would walk into the hall where its murderers kept their trophies — and something in the boy's silence told me the Choir knew exactly what waited for it there.
