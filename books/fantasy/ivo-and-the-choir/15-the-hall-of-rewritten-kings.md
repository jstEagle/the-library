## 15. The Hall of Rewritten Kings

The capital of occupied Vessarion had a name now, and the name was wrong.

Aldric had renamed it Corvane, which sounded like a word a man makes up when he wants to sound older than he is. The old name — the true one, the one that lived in my mother's mouth and in the fork's low hum and in about four hundred years of songs — was *Vessarion itself*, because the capital and the country shared a name the way a heart shares a body. You didn't rename your heart. You just stopped listening to it.

Tamsin walked beside me with her hood up and her ledger-face on, which is the face she makes when she is memorizing everything for later and would like you to know she is not impressed. Serel was a street behind us in plain wool, no grey cloak, looking like a woman trying to be furniture. We had crossed into the city at dusk through a fish market that smelled like it had been arguing with itself since morning, and now we stood before the Museum of Continuity, which was the largest building I had ever seen and also the largest lie.

That's what Aldric called it. Continuity. As if history were a rope he'd caught hold of and could haul hand over hand toward himself.

"They'll search us at the door," Tamsin murmured. "Fork away."

"I don't have pockets."

"You have a coat with a lining I sewed a pocket into specifically so you would stop saying that."

I did have a coat with a lining she'd sewn a pocket into specifically so I would stop saying that. I put the fork inside it, where it pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat that disapproved of me. It had been humming all day, ever since we came down out of the hills and saw the city's towers wearing banners that said CORVANE in letters big enough to read from a coward's distance. It hummed like a kettle deciding whether to scream.

Inside, the museum was warm and gold-lit and full of people who spoke Common with the flat vowels of people who had been told their grandparents' language was a disease. Guards in grey checked us with bored hands. Tamsin's papers were very good; Serel's were better, being real. Mine said I was her nephew from the coast, mute since a fever, which was funny because I hadn't stopped talking since the fish market, but Tamsin had looked at me with such hope that I agreed to try.

We passed under an archway carved with words in Old Vessarine — actual Old Vessarine, worn by weather, older than anyone's grandfather — and someone had chiseled them out and replaced them with a bronze plaque: *HERE STOOD THE GATE OF THE FIRST KINGS OF CORVANE.*

There had been no first kings of Corvane. There had been no Corvane. The plaque was maybe ten years old and already it had the confidence of something ancient. That's the thing about lies, I was learning. They don't need time. They just need to be repeated loudly enough while everyone who remembers otherwise is too tired or too frightened to argue.

Then we came into the Great Hall of Kings, and I stopped being able to breathe properly.

* * *

It was a long room, longer than any barn, with a ceiling painted like a sky that had never once rained. And along both walls ran murals — enormous ones, taller than three men standing on each other's shoulders — showing the whole history of the country in pictures, the way my mother used to tell it to me before sleep, except every single picture had been touched.

Not repainted exactly. *Adjusted.* The way you adjust a chair so it faces the fire. Someone had gone along the walls of my childhood and turned every chair.

There was the Crossing of the Ice, when the first singers led the starving clans over the frozen pass into the green valleys — except in this version the leader wasn't the grey-eyed singer-woman Serevanne with her fork of black glass raised against the storm. He was a broad man in armor, and his armor bore the stag-and-crown that was stamped on every coin Aldric minted, and the caption beneath said *ALDRIC THE FIRST LEADS HIS PEOPLE HOME.*

Aldric the First. Who had been invented approximately nine years ago. Who had led nobody anywhere, because he had not existed, and yet there he was, forty feet tall, leading my ancestors home as if they were lost without him.

"There's more," Tamsin said quietly, and I wished she hadn't, because there was.

There was the Night of Bells, when the Choir sang the plague out of the capital and the sick rose from their cots — except the singers at the center of that mural had been given Aldric's jaw, Aldric's heavy brow, the family face that some painter had clearly studied off a coin until they could dream in it. There was the Treaty of Two Rivers, and the figure signing it wore the stag-and-crown. There was the founding of the Door Out of Vessarion itself — the Door, the actual Door, rendered in gold leaf — and kneeling before it in the painting, receiving its light like a blessing, was a man with Aldric's face.

"He's put himself everywhere," Tamsin breathed. "He's put himself in the *founding myths*. That's not rewriting history, that's—"

"Usurping the song," I said.

I hadn't meant to say it out loud. But the fork had gone hot against my ribs, and when I pulled it out — I know, I know, Tamsin made a sound like a kettle herself — the moment it touched air, the whole hall answered.

Every mural began to hum.

It wasn't loud. It was worse than loud. It was the sound paint makes when it remembers it used to be something else. The gold leaf on the false Aldric at the Door shimmered, and underneath the shimmer, faint as breath on glass, another figure flickered — a singer, arms raised, and behind her a crowd of ordinary faces, bakers and goatherds and children, all singing. Then the guards' boots squeaked somewhere far off, and the shimmer closed like water, and the false king stood alone again in his stolen light.

But I had seen it. The wall had shown me what was buried under the paint, the way a bruise shows under powder. And the fork in my hand was shaking, and I realized the shaking wasn't the fork's.

It was coming through the fork. From somewhere else. From everywhere else.

*Ivo.*

Not a voice, exactly. More like the feeling of your own name being said in a house you thought was empty. The whole Choir — every shard, every fragment Aldric hadn't yet fed into his machine, scattered through the occupied country in hidden things and old stones and the backs of mirrors — had felt me walk in, and it was calling.

Calling me *home*, which was a strange word for it, because I was standing in the middle of what had been home, and it was the least homelike place I had ever been.

* * *

"Put it away," Tamsin whispered. "Ivo. *Now.*"

I couldn't. That's the honest truth, and I've decided I'm done lying in my own head even if I'm mute-nephew-from-the-coast in everyone else's. The Choir was pouring through the fork and through me, and it hurt the way cold water hurts when you've been warm a long time — clean and terrible at once. I could hear the murals' true names under their false ones. I could hear the archway outside still holding its chiseled-away words in the scars of the stone. I could hear, faintly, from the direction of the palace across the square, a grinding sound like millstones, and threaded through it, thin and frayed, pieces of song being fed into something that chewed.

The machine. We were close enough to hear it eat.

And underneath all of it, underneath the eating and the humming and the calling, I heard something that made my eyes sting: my mother's voice. Not really her — I knew it wasn't really her, the Choir only kept what people gave it, and grief gives generously — but a memory of her voice, singing the naming-song, the one that goes through all the old names of the valleys, and it ended the way it always ended, with the line that mothers sing last:

*and the last verse belongs to whoever comes after.*

Me. I was whoever came after. I had been running from that since Thornfell, and here in the belly of the lie, with the fork burning in my fist and the whole broken Choir calling my name, there was nowhere left to run that wasn't toward it.

A guard rounded the corner.

I want to say I reacted bravely. What actually happened is that Tamsin moved like weather — one moment beside me, the next between me and the guard, her body doing that thing where she becomes extremely boring and slightly in the way, the gatekeeper's oldest trick. "Restoration work," she said, in the flat confident tone of a person who has inspected many museums and found them wanting. "The child is my assistant. He's admiring the brushwork." She paused, and then, with the precision of a knife going in, added: "The Aldric the First is remarkably lifelike."

The guard looked at the mural, at the forty-foot invented king, and something crossed his face — quick, small, quickly killed — that I recognized, because I'd seen it in the village that forgot itself. It was the look of a man hearing a song he almost remembered. His grandmother, maybe. Or his grandmother's grandmother. Somewhere back in him, a door was still on its hinges.

"Aye," he said finally. "Lifelike." And he let us pass, and did not report the humming, and I think — I will always think — that was the first small crack in the wall of the world Aldric built, and it was made not by armies but by a bored guard choosing not to hear a sound.

* * *

We found a gallery of smaller things at the hall's end: cases of objects labeled in Aldric's tidy revisionist script. A tuning fork of black glass, twin to mine, labeled *RITUAL OBJECT, PROVENANCE UNKNOWN (LIKELY CORVANIAN)*. A child's shoe. A bell. A hair comb with a singer's knot carved in it, labeled as a farmer's tool. Each case had a little card explaining patiently what these things were not.

Serel caught up with us there, pale under her plain wool. "You shouldn't have taken it out," she said to me, low. "Do you know what the resonance does in this city? The vault listens. The machine *listens*. Every shard that sings unbidden gets logged, and loggers get sent to collect." She looked at me, and for the first time since I'd known her, she looked afraid of something other than Aldric. "They logged you, boy. An hour ago, maybe less. There will be a collection order with your description on it by morning."

"Then we move tonight," Tamsin said.

"It's worse than that." Serel hesitated, and I watched a soldier decide something she couldn't take back. "The order won't be for a mute nephew from the coast. The machine doesn't log descriptions, it logs *resonance signatures*. And yours—" she pointed at the fork, at me, at whatever it was I had become in that hall "—yours isn't a shard's signature. Shards echo. You *answer*. The archivists have a term for signatures like that. They call them vessels."

The word went through me like the fork through the murals' paint. *Vessel.* A thing shaped to be filled. All the way across the mountains, through the smuggler roads and the forgetting villages and the auction where I'd paid for the fork with my mother's face, I had thought I was carrying the Choir.

Standing in the Hall of Rewritten Kings, with the whole shattered song calling my name from every direction like birds finding their flock, I understood that I had it backwards.

The Choir wasn't calling me to carry it.

It was calling me because it knew me. Because somewhere in the oldest verses, in the parts even the archives had forgotten, there was a verse about a vessel, and the last verse belonged to whoever came after, and the song had been waiting nine years for its singer to grow tall enough to reach the Door.

Tamsin took my shoulder. She didn't say anything. She just looked at me the way she looks at the gate in a storm — measuring whether it will hold, and deciding, whatever the answer, to stand there anyway.

Above us, forty feet of painted invention gazed down with its borrowed face, certain and golden and hollow.

And beneath our feet, under the marble, under the square, under the palace where the millstones ground yesterday into obedience, the Choir called again — closer now, insistent, joyful and terrified at once, the way a dog sounds when it has waited all day at a gate.

By morning, they would come for me with a collection order.

By morning, I had decided, they would find the vessel already poured out and moving — toward the vault, toward the machine, toward the Door.

I just had to tell Tamsin that part.

She was going to hate it.
