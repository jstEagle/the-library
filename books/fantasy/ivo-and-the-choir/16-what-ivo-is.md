## 16. What Ivo Is

Serel came back at dusk, and she came alone.

I knew it before I saw her, the way you know weather through a wall — the sentries' rhythm changed, the dogs went quiet in the wrong way, and the boy stopped humming mid-note and said, "She's here," as if he'd been expecting her all day, which perhaps he had. Children and dogs and shards of national memory: all of them hear things I have to work for.

We were lodging above a dyer's shop in the free city's ragged southern quarter, where the walls wept indigo and everything smelled faintly of vinegar and ambition. Ivo was supposed to be asleep. Instead he sat cross-legged on the floor with the black-glass fork balanced on his palm, watching it tremble like a compass needle that had lost its nerve.

"Under the bed," I said. "Now."

He went without argument, which frightened me more than any argument would have. Two weeks ago he'd have asked why. Two weeks of Marrowgate had taught him that some questions are better asked after the knocking stops.

Serel knocked anyway. Three soft raps, her old signal from the pass, the one that meant *papers* and *no trouble*. She stood in the stairwell light with her grey cloak folded over her arm like a thing she was trying to decide whether to keep, and she looked — there is no kinder word for it — wrung out.

"You're off-duty," I said.

"I'm dead," she corrected. "As of this morning. Captain Serel Vane, drowned in the Wether ford during an inspection. Very tragic. There'll be a stone." She stepped past me into the room, uninvited, because soldiers who have just erased themselves stop caring about thresholds. "Your scholar friend vouched for the lodgings. He also charged me double. I respect that."

"What do you want?"

"To show you something." From inside her shirt she drew a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and I confess my hand went to the knife before my manners caught up. She noticed. She always notices. "If I wanted the fork, Tamsin, I'd have brought six men and a lie about a fire. I brought a book instead. Draw your own conclusions about how far I've fallen."

* * *

The packet held papers — real ones, stamped with the seal of Aldric's Office of Continuance, which is the sort of name a bureaucracy gives itself when it has begun to believe its own poetry. Census ledgers. Extraction manifests. And beneath them, older, softer-edged, a genealogical roll written on paper that had gone the color of weak tea.

"Where did you get these?" I asked.

"The Office burns its mistakes every quarter," Serel said. "I've been fishing them out of the fires for eleven years. It started as tidying. It ended as—" she waved a hand at herself, at the whole improbable fact of her sitting in a dyer's loft with contraband genealogy, "—this."

Ivo had come out from under the bed somewhere in the middle of this, drawn the way children are drawn to forbidden documents, and he stood now at my shoulder reading upside-down with the terrifying fluency of the young. His finger stopped on the tea-colored page.

"That's my grandmother's name," he said.

The room got very small. I have been in sieges with more air in them.

The roll was a lineage — singers, every one of them, generation upon generation, each name followed by a notation in Old Vessarine that took me a moment because the script was archaic and my hands were not steady. *Vessel-borne. Vessel-borne. Vessel-born.* Down through nine generations to a final entry, recent ink, official hand:

*Ivo. Last of the line. Intended vessel. Status: fled.*

"I don't understand 'vessel,'" Ivo said, which was a mercy, because I understood it perfectly and wished with my whole body that I didn't.

"It means a container," Serel said, gently, in the tone of a woman who has decided that lying to this particular child is no longer among her available sins. "It means the Choir was never meant to live in glass and stone. The shards — the forks, the bells, the singing stones — they were only ever storage. The Choir was meant to be carried by a person. A singer of the old line. Someone the song could pour into whole, instead of leaking out piece by piece."

"And that's me," Ivo said. Not a question. Children arrive at these conclusions faster than adults because they haven't yet learned to build detours around the truth.

"That's you," Serel said.

He looked down at the fork in his hands. It had gone very still, the way a bird goes still when the cat is in the room.

* * *

Let me tell you what I did while they talked, because what I did was arithmetic, and I am ashamed of it, and I am going to write it down anyway because a ledger-keeper who hides her own entries is worse than a liar; she's a bad accountant.

While Serel explained — while the boy's face moved through confusion and fear and that terrible child-brave calm — I was calculating. That is the truth. Some cold clerk in the back of my skull was running numbers: one heir, one Door, one song that could be poured into a willing throat and carried through the threshold intact. Against: armies, machines, a usurper who eats yesterday. The clerk kept arriving at the same sum. *The boy is the only vessel. The Door requires a singer. Therefore the boy must sing, and therefore the boy must go where the singing is most dangerous, and therefore—*

Therefore nothing. I put the clerk back in his drawer. But he exists. I want that recorded. He exists, and he wears my face, and for the length of that conversation he was doing sums over a child's head.

"—and the machine can't use him," Serel was saying, "that's the point. Aldric's engine grinds shards fine enough to mix into a census, but a living vessel won't grind. The song would tear the mechanism apart. Which means if Aldric ever learns what Ivo is, he won't send the machine. He'll send cages. Gentle ones. Schools, tutors, honors. He'll raise the boy himself, patiently, and wait for the Choir to grow into him like a crop, and then harvest a nation's memory from a man who grew up loving his farmer."

Ivo shivered. "How do you know so much about it?"

"Because I helped build the first cage," Serel said. "A different child, twenty years ago. A girl who sang in her sleep in one of the work-camps. We didn't know what she was. We thought we were being kind, moving her to the capital, giving her lessons." Her jaw worked. "There's a wing of the palace where the walls hum. I used to think it was the plumbing."

Nobody said anything for a while. Below us the dyer's vats gurgled their slow indigo digestion, and somewhere a street-seller cried the last of his chestnuts to a city that had already eaten.

"Tamsin." Ivo tugged my sleeve. "What's a vessel? Really. Not the word. What happens to the person?"

And there it was. The question under the question. Because Serel had explained what the Choir would do *through* him, but nobody had said what it would do *to* him, and I had read enough of the archives by now — by lamplight, obsessively, the way you read anything when you suspect it's about someone you love — to know that the old texts were not reassuring. *The vessel pours and is poured,* one commentary said. *The singer becomes the sung.* Nine generations of names on that roll, and not one of them, as far as the records showed, had died in bed.

"A vessel holds," I said. "That's all. It holds something precious so the precious thing survives."

"That's not what she said."

"No," I admitted. "It isn't."

He nodded slowly, absorbing this, and then he did the thing that undid me, the thing I will carry to whatever grave is allotted me: he looked relieved. "Good," he said. "Then it's mine to give. Nobody else can hold it. So it's mine." He said it the way other children claim a chore — *it's my turn to fetch water* — with that flat practical courage that makes adults want to lock all the doors in the world.

"No," I said.

Both of them looked at me.

"No," I said again, standing up, because sitting felt like surrender and I wasn't ready to surrender to anything except eventually sleep. "You are eight years old. You are going to eat your supper, and sleep, and tomorrow we are going to walk out of this city through a route I have not chosen yet but will choose well, and we are going to find another way. There is always another way. I have spent my life keeping a door shut through sheer stubbornness, and I promise you the universe has not suddenly run out of doors."

"Tamsin—" Serel began.

"Don't." I pointed at her, and my finger was not entirely steady. "Don't you dare agree with him. Don't you dare look at that child like he's a key. I've seen that look. Every bureaucrat in Aldric's Office has that look. It's the look people get when a person turns into a solution."

"He's not a solution," Serel said quietly. "He's a person who happens to be the only exit out of a burning building. Those aren't the same thing, but they overlap, and pretending otherwise gets everyone cooked."

"Then we find a window."

"There is no window. There's the Door or there's the machine. I've told you what the machine does. I've watched it done." For the first time since the pass, Serel's composure cracked — not broke, cracked, like ice over moving water. "Eleven years I filed those manifests. Eleven years I told myself the Office was preserving what it couldn't save. Do you know what preservation looks like from inside? It looks like a girl in a humming room who stops saying her own name by the third winter. If there were a window, Tamsin, I'd have crawled through it years ago and taken the girl with me."

Ivo had gone pale. "What girl?"

"A girl who doesn't exist anymore," Serel said. "Which is exactly what happens when we let necessity do our deciding."

* * *

He slept eventually — they always do, the young, they drop into sleep like coins into a well — and the fork hummed against his chest in its small broken way, and I sat by the window with the genealogical roll in my lap and did not read it, because I had already read it, because I would be reading it forever.

Here is what I know about love versus necessity, learned late and at cost: necessity always sounds reasonable. Necessity has excellent handwriting. Necessity files its reports on time and cites its sources and says, *of course we would prefer not to*, and it is never, ever the one who pays. Love pays. Love stands in the doorway with its stupid unarmed hands and says *not this one, take me instead*, and necessity smiles and steps around it, because love is not on the manifest.

But here is what I also know: sometimes love and necessity are not enemies. Sometimes the trap is believing they must fight, because while they fight, you stand still, and standing still is the one thing that serves the machine.

So I made a third entry in my private ledger, beneath the clerk's arithmetic and the coward's refusal, and it read like this: *Neither sacrifice nor shelter. Teach him. Tell him everything. Let him choose with his eyes open, and if he chooses the Door, walk beside him the whole way, and if he chooses to run, run faster than he does.* It was not a clean answer. Ledgers rarely balance the way you want. But it was mine.

Below, in the street, a lamp went past — too steady a pace for a drunk, too casual for a patrol. It paused outside the dyer's shop. Moved on.

Serel saw it too. She was already at the window, cloak forgotten, all soldier now. "They're not looking for us," she murmured. "Wrong district. They're sweeping the archive quarter." A pause. "Something's spooked the Office tonight. Something big."

The fork, in the sleeping boy's hands, changed its note.

Not louder. Deeper — a sound I felt in my teeth, in the floorboards, in the old scar tissue of a wound I'd almost forgotten. Ivo woke without opening his eyes and said, clearly, in a voice that was partly his and partly something much older and much larger:

*"The map is drowning. Come before the chapel forgets where it is."*

Serel and I looked at each other across the dark.

"Well," she said. "I suppose we're going to a chapel."
