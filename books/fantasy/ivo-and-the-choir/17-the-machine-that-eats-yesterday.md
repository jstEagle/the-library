## 17. The Machine That Eats Yesterday

*Tamsin*

There is a particular kind of silence that lives under palaces. I had imagined vaults to be loud places — clanking chains, groaning stone, the theatrical machinery of villainy. Instead, the corridor beneath Aldric's palace was so quiet I could hear my own heartbeat and, beside me, the smaller quicker one of a child pretending very hard not to be afraid.

We had come in through the cisterns, which Serel swore were unguarded because "no one has ever wanted anything down here." The water was knee-deep and cold enough to make my teeth file formal complaints. Ivo walked with the tuning fork held against his chest like a talisman, and every few steps it gave a small, mournful hum, as if it too disliked the damp.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," I whispered.

"Because you said knowing is better than not knowing," Ivo whispered back.

"I say a lot of things. Most of them are lies I tell myself at reasonable volume."

He almost smiled. In the last week he had learned that from me — the habit of smiling sideways at fear instead of looking at it straight on. I wasn't proud of teaching him that. It's the sort of inheritance you give a child without meaning to, like your bad knees or your way of holding grief.

The corridor ended at a door that was not locked. That frightened me more than any lock would have. Locked doors announce themselves; they admit someone expects thieves. An open door in the heart of a tyrant's underpalace said something worse: *come in. Everyone comes in. No one leaves with what they came for.*

Beyond it, the silence changed.

It was not empty silence. It was the silence of a room full of people holding their breath — except the people were voices, and they were singing.

* * *

*Ivo*

I felt the Choir before I saw it.

It came up through the soles of my feet first, through the wet stone, a trembling like the ground remembering an old dance. Then through my ribs. The fork went hot against my chest, then cold, then hot again, cycling like a thing trying to decide whether it was arriving home or being swallowed.

Tamsin put her hand on my shoulder. "Ivo." Just my name. She does that when she wants to ask if I'm all right without asking.

"It's here," I said. "All of it. Or — pieces of it. Hundreds."

The vault opened up around us like the inside of a bell.

Shards hung in the dark on threads of light — hundreds of them, thousands, each one a splinter of black glass like my fork but larger, some as long as my arm, suspended above a machine that filled the whole floor. I don't know how else to describe it. It looked like a loom crossed with a mill crossed with a throat. Great stone wheels turned without anyone pushing them, and belts of pale leather ran from wheel to wheel, and along those belts the shards traveled slowly downward, into a basin at the center where something glowed the color of milk held up to firelight.

And the singing. Every shard sang. Not together — that was the terrible part. Each shard sang its own fragment, its own few bars, over and over, like birds in separate cages who could hear each other but never touch wings. A woman's voice repeating four notes of a harvest song. A man humming a lullaby that kept breaking off at the same place, as if he'd forgotten how it ended. Children chanting a skipping rhyme whose words I knew — *stone, stone, carry me home* — except the words came out wrong, bent, the names in them replaced by other names. Aldric's names.

"What are the wheels doing?" Tamsin breathed.

I listened harder. Listening is the only thing I've ever been good at, and lately it has been enough, which frightens me more than the machine does.

"They're grinding," I said. "Not the glass. The *meaning*. Look —" I pointed to a belt where a shard rode down toward the basin. As it passed each wheel, its song changed. The harvest song lost its word for *rain* and gained a word for *the Emperor's mercy*. The lullaby's ending came back, but now it ended with a blessing for Aldric's line. "He's feeding the song through and rewriting it as it goes. And then —"

The basin flared. Somewhere far above us, muffled by tons of stone, I heard bells. Evening bells, ringing across the capital, calling the faithful to the recitation of history. Every schoolchild in this city recites it at dusk. I'd heard them through the chapel wall: the litany of Aldric's ancestors, his victories, his mercies, his thousand-year destiny.

"That's where it goes," I said. My voice came out strange and flat. "Up. Into the bells. Into the lessons. He grinds our memories into his story and pours it into everyone's ears until no one can remember it was ever any other shape."

Tamsin stood very still. She has a stillness that most people mistake for calm. I know better now. It's the stillness of someone holding a door shut from the inside while something enormous pushes against it.

"The village," she said quietly. "The one that forgot itself. This is where they went. Their shards are on those belts right now."

I looked. And I found them — I don't know how, except that the fork pulled at me like a compass needle, and there, third belt from the left, a small shard turning slowly toward the wheels, singing a name over and over. *Maret. Maret. Maret.* An old woman's voice. The baker's wife who had wept in the square without knowing why.

"No," I said, and stepped forward, and Tamsin caught my arm.

"Ivo —"

"That's Maret. That's her *name*, they're grinding her *name* —"

"If you touch that machine, every alarm in the palace rings, and we die in this basement, and the fork goes on a belt tomorrow." Her grip was iron. Her voice was gentle. It's a terrible combination, being loved by someone competent. "I know. I see it. But we cannot save one shard tonight. We came to learn how the door is guarded."

"We came to learn how to break it," I said.

"We came to learn," she repeated, "how to break it. Which requires learning. Which requires *not dying*." She looked up at the hanging shards, hundreds of them, the whole dim constellation of a country in cages, and her face did something I had never seen it do. It cracked. Just for a moment. "Gods forgive me, Ivo. I want to burn this place more than I have ever wanted anything. That's exactly why we wait."

And that was when the lights came on.

* * *

*Tamsin*

Later — much later, in the years after, when people asked me about the night I met Aldric Vane — I always told the truth, which surprised everyone, including me. They expected a monster story. They expected the Tyrant of the rewritten maps, the Unnamer, the man who ate yesterday.

What they got was a tired man in a dressing gown.

He came down the far stair alone, carrying a lamp, and he was not tall, and his hair was going grey at the temples in a way that suggested worry rather than age, and he wore slippers. Slippers. I have faced down grey-cloaked riders and memory-thieves and the entire weight of an empire's machinery, and the detail that undid me was the slippers.

He stopped when he saw us. He didn't call for guards. He looked at Ivo for a long moment, and then at the fork against the boy's chest, and something moved across his face that I recognized, because I had seen it in mirrors all my life.

Relief. Terrible, naked relief.

"You're real," Aldric said. His voice was quiet, almost hoarse. "The singing line. I thought the last of you died at Carrowmere. I hoped, a little, and hated myself for hoping." He set the lamp down on a workbench with the care of a man who handles fragile things often. "Do you know how long I have been alone in this room?"

"Guards will be coming," I said, because my mouth does that when my mind has nothing better prepared.

"No they won't. No one comes down here. My own captains think this vault stores grain records." He smiled, and it was a horrible smile, not because it was cruel but because it was *tired*. "You're the gatekeeper. Thornfell. I've read your file. Twenty years at a sealed door, keeping ledgers of roads nobody officially uses. Do you know why I know your file, Keeper Nakamura? Because I read everything. I keep everything. I am the only archivist my empire has ever trusted, and I trust no one else with it, because —"

He stopped himself. Pressed his lips together. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"Because everyone forgets. Given time, given permission, given a comfortable enough life — everyone forgets. Except me."

I should have been furious. I was furious — underneath, banked and burning, furious for Maret and her name on the belt, furious for the village that couldn't remember itself, furious for a homeland I'd inherited only as absence. But fury needs a monster to stand against, and the man in the dressing gown wouldn't hold still for it.

"Why?" I asked. "Why grind them? Why rewrite? You *won*. The war ended twenty years ago. What are you afraid of?"

He looked at me as if I had asked him the easiest question in the world, and perhaps I had.

"Being forgotten first," he said.

* * *

*Ivo*

I understood him before Tamsin did. I'm not sure I'm glad about that.

The Choir taught me things I never asked to know. When I stood in that vault among the hanging shards, I could feel what the machine felt from the other side — because the shards weren't only singing. They were listening too. And what they heard, night after night, year after year, was the sound of one man's footsteps on these stairs.

He came down every night. I knew it the way you know a song you've heard through a wall your whole life. He came down and he walked among the shards and sometimes he touched them, just touched, the way you'd touch the glass over a thing you loved in a museum. And the shards sang their fragments at him, harvest songs and lullabies and skipping rhymes, and none of them were his. None of them would ever be his. He had conquered a country made entirely of memory, and he owned none of it. Memory doesn't transfer. You can steal a song; you can't make it yours.

So he built the machine. If he couldn't join the song, he would edit it. If the past wouldn't have him, he would remake the past until it had no choice. Every bell at dusk, every schoolchild's recitation, every renamed sign and repainted mural — it was all a letter addressed to history, sent over and over, begging for a reply.

"He's lonely," I said.

Both of them turned to look at me. Tamsin's expression said *don't*. Aldric's expression said nothing at all, and that nothing was worse than anything.

"Don't," Tamsin murmured.

But I was already doing the thing I do, the thing that got me named heir of a singing line before I ever wanted to be anything at all. I was listening past the surface.

"You're not from here either," I said to Aldric. "Your family came over the mountains three generations back. Nobody sang for them. There's no shard of your grandmother anywhere in this room, because nobody wrote her down, because your people didn't keep songs, they kept ledgers." I heard myself speak and marveled distantly, the way you do when your mouth runs ahead of your courage. "You're not erasing Vessarion because you hate it. You're erasing it because it has what you don't. Because every person in this country belongs to something, and you belong to nothing, and it's easier to break the belonging than to bear watching it."

"Stop," Tamsin said softly.

Aldric hadn't moved. But his hand had found the edge of the workbench, and his knuckles were white, and his eyes were wet in the lamplight, and I understood suddenly that in twenty years no one had ever said this to him. Everyone he had ever met was either afraid of him or lying to him, and both of those are forms of silence.

"My mother," he said, and his voice broke on the second word, actually broke, the great Unnamer of nations standing in his slippers with his voice cracking like a boy's. "She died when I was nine. I cannot remember her face. Do you understand? I have remade the memory of eleven million people and I cannot remember my own mother's face, and every night I come down here because the singing is the closest thing to a voice in the world that isn't mine."

The basin flared. The bells rang the last of the evening litany overhead. Somewhere above us, children recited his invented ancestors in their clear obedient voices, and he flinched at the sound — flinched, at his own victory, at the sound of the lie he had poured into ten thousand young mouths.

Tamsin stepped forward. I watched her do it and I knew, even then, that I was watching the moment the whole war turned — though it didn't look like much. A tired woman in a stolen guard cloak, standing in front of a tyrant in a dressing gown, in a basement full of caged songs.

"You want to be remembered," she said. "That's the whole of it. That's the engine under the engine."

"Yes," he said simply.

"And you've decided the only way is to force it. To write yourself into other people's memories because you can't grow one of your own."

"Yes."

She was quiet a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had that gentleness she saves for the worst truths, the ones she told me about my mother, the ones that hurt because they were kind.

"Aldric Vane. Memory that's forced isn't memory. It's just fear wearing memory's clothes. Those children reciting your litany — they'll forget it the day the recitation stops, because it was never theirs. You can't grind your way into being loved. Believe me." Something crossed her face — Thornfell, twenty years, a sealed door, all the ways she had tried to force herself into a country that no longer existed. "I've tried cheaper versions of what you're doing. It doesn't take."

For one moment — one — I thought it had worked. I thought the man would sit down on his own workbench and weep and let the whole rotten machine go silent.

Then his eyes went past her shoulder, to the fork at my chest, and the relief came back into his face, and underneath the relief something colder, and I understood that pity and hunger had been living in him side by side for twenty years and neither one had ever won.

"The singing line," he said softly. "Do you know what the Choir could give me, boy? Not edited. Not ground. The true song, whole. If the vessel sang for me willingly —" He took a step forward, hands open, palms up, a man making an offering of himself. "I would give it all back. Every shard. Every name. Maret can have her name. Your villages can have their songs. One song from you, freely given, and the machine goes dark forever. Ask your keeper. Ask her what she'd pay for that."

And the worst part — the part I still carry — is that Tamsin hesitated.

Just a breath. Just the space of one bell fading overhead. But I saw it: the arithmetic running behind her eyes, all those shards on their belts, a whole country's memory weighed against one child's one song.

Then she looked at me, and whatever she saw in my face settled it, and she stepped squarely between me and the Emperor of the rewritten world.

"He's not a payment," she said. "He's a person. And so, gods help me, are you. Which is the only reason you're both still breathing."

Above us, the last bell fell silent. And in the silence, every shard in the vault began to sing at once — not fragments anymore, but together, one voice, hundreds of voices, a chord that shook dust from the rafters and made the great wheels shudder in their housings — and Aldric Vane stared up into the dark with his face full of longing and terror in equal measure, and I understood that the Choir had heard everything.

It had heard his offer.

And it was answering.
