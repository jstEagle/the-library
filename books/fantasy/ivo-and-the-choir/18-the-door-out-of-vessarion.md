## 18. The Door Out of Vessarion

The shards led us the way a dog leads you home — confidently, and with no regard whatsoever for whether you wanted to go.

There were eleven of them now. The black-glass fork I'd carried since Thornfell had spent the last three weeks gathering its siblings out of hiding places I couldn't have found in a hundred years: a shard sewn into a beggar's coat hem in Marrowgate, one sunk in a well that only sang when it rained, two pried from the hands of a dead woman in the drowned chapel who had guarded them so long her fingers had grown around them like roots. Each time we collected one, the fork in my pocket grew heavier. Not heavier in weight. Heavier in *want*.

"They're arguing again," I said. We were climbing through a fold of hills that didn't appear on any map made after the conquest, which was to say they appeared on no map at all, because Aldric's cartographers had never bothered to learn what his armies had already burned past.

Tamsin didn't break stride. "Arguing about what?"

"Whether I'm ready."

"And what do you think?"

"I think," I said, "that they're asking the wrong person." Which was true, and also the most honest thing I'd said in weeks, and Tamsin gave me the look she gave me when I was being honest in an inconvenient way — the one that meant *good, keep going* and *I wish you wouldn't* at the same time. She had gotten very good at that look. It was practically her native language by now.

The truth was that I could feel them all the time now. Eleven voices, or maybe eleven thousand voices folded into eleven shapes, singing under my ribs like a second heartbeat that occasionally disagreed with the first about tempo. When I slept, I dreamed in Old Vessarine. When I woke, sometimes it took me a moment to remember that my name was Ivo and not the long string of syllables the Choir used for me, which translated roughly to *the small door that walks*. I hadn't told Tamsin that part. She had enough to carry.

Serel walked behind us, grey cloak dyed brown and still somehow looking like a uniform, saying nothing, watching our backtrail with the particular vigilance of a woman who had recently betrayed her entire career and was still deciding how she felt about it. Every few hours she'd say something like "the pass narrows ahead" or "there's water at the cairn," and every few hours I remembered that she had once come to take me away in chains of paperwork, and every few hours I decided, again, to let her keep walking with us. People are allowed to change their minds. It's practically the whole point of everything we were doing.

The hills opened without warning into a valley shaped like a cupped hand.

I knew it before I saw it. That's the thing nobody tells you about carrying a country's memory: you stop being able to be surprised by your own homeland. The Choir sang the valley before my eyes arrived — *the hollow where the exiles gathered, the last place the whole song was sung together, the night before the borders closed.* I stood on the rim and looked down at a bowl of winter grass and frost-killed heather and a single crooked rowan tree, and I thought: this is nothing. This is a field. And then I thought: this is the most important field in the world, and both thoughts were completely true, and I was getting very tired of holding two true things at once.

"There," Tamsin said quietly.

At the base of the rowan, half-buried in frost, stood a door.

Not a door in a wall. There was no wall. Just a doorframe of pale stone standing alone in the grass, tall as two men, carved over every surface with script I could read now — read *easily*, which would have terrified me once and mostly just made me tired — and containing within its frame... nothing. Air. Frost-light. The valley beyond it, seen straight through.

Except it wasn't quite air. If I tilted my head, the space inside the frame shimmered the way heat shimmers above a road in summer, and underneath the shimmer there were — sounds. Distant ones. A market in some language accented wrong. Gulls. A forge. Rain on different roofs than ours.

"The Door Out of Vessarion," Tamsin breathed. She had been the scholar; this was her moment, and I watched her face do the thing faces do when a lifetime of reading becomes a single fact you can touch. "It's real. It actually—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way she did when she was afraid hope would spook. "The archives said the exiles built it. Before the conquest finished. A threshold that opens onto everywhere Vessarine people went. Every hearth that kept a song alive."

"It opens onto kitchens," I said, because that was what I heard. "Mostly kitchens. And one ship. And somewhere with a lot of goats."

Tamsin laughed — a short, cracked sound, half sob. "Goats."

"Someone took the song somewhere with a lot of goats, Tamsin. That's just history."

* * *

We made camp at the valley's edge rather than approach, which was Serel's suggestion and a good one, because the Door was not subtle about wanting company. All evening the shards hummed against my chest, and the frame's empty middle rippled like a pond someone keeps throwing stones into, and by full dark I could hear voices through it clearly enough to pick out words. A woman singing a lullaby in Old Vessarine so slurred by another tongue that only the melody survived intact. A man cursing at dough. Children, somewhere, chanting a counting rhyme that the Choir remembered being chanted in a schoolyard in Vessarion's capital four hundred years ago.

"They kept it," I said. My voice came out strange. "All this time. They didn't even know what they were keeping, and they kept it anyway."

"People are stubborn about songs," Tamsin said. "It's the last thing to go. You can take a language, take a flag, take the names off every signpost, and somebody's grandmother is still humming something she can't explain."

I looked at her. In the firelight she looked old and young at once, which I think is just what Tamsin looked like, always. "You're going to make me do it, aren't you."

She didn't answer immediately, which was worse than answering. Serel shifted by the fire and pretended very hard to be asleep, which fooled no one.

"The Door demands a singer," Tamsin said finally. "One willing to give the Choir a new shape. That's what the frame says. I've been translating it since sundown and it doesn't get less demanding the more times you read it." She rubbed her eyes. "It doesn't have to be you, Ivo. That's the other thing the archives say. Any singer of the line. But you're—"

"The last one. Yes. I've noticed." I pulled the fork out of my coat and held it up. Eleven shards' worth of light moved inside it like weather. "They want to go through. Did you know that? They've wanted to go through since Marrowgate. They're not asking me to open the Door. They're asking me to let *them* out, and they're using my voice to ask, and I can't tell anymore which of us is asking."

"What do you want?" Serel asked, from her fake sleep, eyes still shut.

"That's not fair. You're supposed to pretend to be asleep longer than that."

"What do you want, Ivo?"

I sat with it. The fire popped. Through the distant frame, faint as breath, the goat-people's song rose and fell.

What I wanted. Nobody had asked me that in a very long time. Tamsin asked what I thought, Serel asked what I heard, the Choir asked what I would give. Somewhere back in chapter — back in *the beginning*, I mean — I had been a child running through snow with a fork in his fist, and I had wanted, with my whole small frozen heart, to not be cold and to find someone who would open a door. Both of those had happened. Everything after had been other people's wants wearing my voice.

"I want," I said slowly, "to not be a vessel. I want to be a boy who has a mother's face somewhere in his head, even if I traded it away, even if I don't remember it — I want it to have been *mine* to trade. I want to eat breakfast without hearing eleven thousand dead people harmonize about porridge. I want—" My throat closed. I pushed through it, because apparently that was the kind of night this was going to be. "I want whoever comes after me to get to be ordinary. That's all. That's the whole list."

Tamsin reached across the fire and took my hand. Her grip was a gatekeeper's grip: strong, practiced, the grip of a woman whose job had been holding doors against the world for twenty years.

"Then give them that shape," she said. "That's what the Door is asking for. Not a nation. Not a grievance. Whatever shape you choose, the Choir becomes. So choose the boy-shaped one."

"You make it sound easy."

"Oh, it will be terrible," she said cheerfully. "Everything important is terrible. That's how you know."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I don't think anyone did, though Serel maintained her fiction of unconsciousness until nearly dawn, at which point she gave up, sat up, and said, "If you die in there, I'm writing 'he was annoying' on your memorial," which was, from Serel, roughly equivalent to a sonnet of devotion.

We crossed the frost-stiff grass while the sky was still the color of unpolished pewter. The rowan tree had berries the red of old blood. The doorframe waited, and the shards in my fist sang so loudly now that I could barely hear the world through them, and the world was anyway almost over, or at least this version of it was.

Up close, the carvings resolved into names. Thousands of them. Exile names, departure names, the names of people who had walked out of a dying country carrying songs in their teeth, and every name had a little notch beside it, and I understood suddenly that the notches were places. *This name went to the coast city. This name went over the mountains. This name went somewhere with a lot of goats.*

"It's a ledger," Tamsin whispered. "A gatekeeper's ledger." She looked at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and proud all at once. "Somebody kept records. Even at the end. Especially at the end."

"Sounds like someone I'd like."

"Don't get sentimental, it makes me uncomfortable."

I stepped up to the threshold. Inside the frame, the shimmering air was close now, and warm — impossibly, absurdly warm, smelling of bread and woodsmoke and salt and goat, all the kitchens of the diaspora breathing out at once. And the Choir rose in me like a tide, all eleven shards and everything folded inside them, and they pressed against the backs of my teeth with a question older than the conquest:

*What are we?*

And here is the thing I want written down somewhere, in a ledger, with a notch beside it: they weren't asking me to decide for them. They were asking me to describe them. A singer doesn't invent the song. A singer chooses which song, of all the songs waiting, gets sung.

I thought of the village that had forgotten itself, weeping without knowing why. I thought of the auction, and the weight of a mother's face paid out of my own memory like coins. I thought of Aldric, grinding yesterday into fuel for his machine, terrified of being forgotten first. I thought of Tamsin, keeping a sealed door for twenty years because keeping it was the only way she knew to love a country she'd never seen.

And I understood what shape to give them.

Not a nation. Nations have borders, and borders are just grief with paperwork. Not a grievance — grievances need an enemy, and I was so tired of enemies. 

A people. Just that. Songs in kitchens. Names in ledgers. Grandmothers humming things they can't explain. A country that stops being a place and starts being a habit, carried in the mouth, passed hand to hand like bread, impossible to conquer because it lives wherever the singers live.

I opened my mouth.

The Door opened first.

Every kitchen at once — light pouring out of the frame like water from a broken dam, gulls and forges and rain and goats and ten thousand accents of one old tongue, and the Choir tearing free of my ribs with a sound like dawn breaking over mountains, and I was singing, I was the song, I was the small door that walks and I was walking wide open—

And then, from the far side of the valley, horns.

Grey horns. Grey riders cresting the southern rim, rank on rank, Aldric's banners snapping in the morning wind — and at their center, on a white horse, a tired man in a crown, coming to stop the song with everything he had left.

Tamsin stepped between me and the army, planted herself like a gatepost, and said, in a voice that carried across the whole valley:

"Well. You're late. The door's already open."
