## 19. Aldric at the Threshold

The Door Out of Vessarion did not look like a door. That was the first thing I noticed, and the last thing I would ever stop noticing, because everything else about that morning was noise and blood-smell and the particular terror of watching an army come down a mountain at you.

It looked, instead, like a gap in the world where a door had been sketched by someone who had only ever had doors described to them. It stood alone on a shelf of granite above the valley — no wall around it, no frame, just a tall absence in the air, faintly silvered, like heat-shimmer in winter. Through it I could see, if I let myself look, a kitchen with copper pots. A harbor at dusk. A woman's hands kneading bread. Every place, I understood, that any Vessarine exile had ever carried a song to, all of them layered like panes of colored glass.

Ivo stood before it with the fork of black glass held against his chest, both hands wrapped around it the way children hold things they have been told not to drop. He was humming. The granite under my boots was humming too, very faintly, the way a bridge hums before a heavy cart crosses it.

Behind us, down the switchback road, the armies were coming.

You could hear them before you could see them — a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, iron and hooves and ten thousand boots finding the same rhythm. Grey cloaks, rank on rank, spilling out of the pass below like water finding its level. Banners I couldn't read from here but didn't need to: Aldric's sunburst, the one he'd invented for himself, because a man who erases history has to draw his own somewhere.

"They're faster than Serel said," Ivo said. He wasn't afraid, which frightened me more than the army did. Children should be afraid of armies. It's one of the few sensible fears they come equipped with.

"Serel says a lot of things," I said. "Half of them are even true."

"When did she say she'd hold the pass?"

"She didn't say." I checked the knife at my belt, an old habit, useless against ten thousand men. "She said, *if it comes to it.* Which is a different sentence entirely, and I've spent enough years reading treaties to know the difference."

He nodded and went back to humming, and the Door shimmered, and somewhere inside it a woman laughed over her bread dough, and I thought: *this is what we're defending. Not a country. A kitchen.*

* * *

Serel found us an hour later, or perhaps we found her — the distinction had gone soft between us over the past weeks. She came up the last stretch of the switchback on foot, leading her horse, and she was no longer wearing grey.

That was the thing that made me stop breathing for a moment. Grey was Aldric. Grey was the uniform, the papers, the word *relic* stamped in official ink. Serel had worn grey since before I'd known her; it was practically her skin. And now she stood there in a plain brown riding coat, mud to the knees, looking like any tired woman on any road, and she said:

"The pass is held."

"Held how?" I asked. "There are four thousand of them and one of you."

"Two," she said. "My lieutenant turned with me. And the defile above the treeline is narrow enough that numbers don't matter much until they decide to climb, and they won't decide to climb, because climbing means leaving the road, and leaving the road means being off the map, and Aldric's soldiers can't fight off the map. They've never been allowed to learn how." She said it without pride. That was the worst part. She described her own mutiny the way you'd describe a leaking roof.

"And when he orders them up anyway?"

"He won't order them up." She looked past us, at the Door, and her face did something complicated. "He'll come up himself. He always does, at the end. He needs to see the things he takes." She paused. "Needed. I keep using the wrong tense."

"You could still ride away," I said. "South. Nobody would follow a woman in a brown coat."

"Yes," Serel said. "And then I'd be exactly the sort of person who rides away, and I'd have to live in the same country as her forever." She unsaddled her horse and sat down on a rock as if she intended to stay some time. "Besides. Someone has to watch the child sing. I've heard so much about it."

* * *

Aldric came at dusk.

Not with trumpets — I want to be fair to him about this, because fairness was the whole point of what happened next, and fairness toward Aldric is the hardest work I have ever done. He came with six guards, who stopped at the bend of the road, and then he came on alone, an old man in a plain dark coat, walking uphill with the careful steps of someone whose knees have opinions. No crown. No banner. Just a man climbing toward a door that shouldn't exist, in a country he had spent thirty years insisting did not.

Ivo stepped forward to meet him, and I put my hand on the boy's shoulder and stepped forward instead.

"Tamsin Nakamura," Aldric said, before I could speak. "Keeper of Thornfell. I've read your ledgers."

"I know," I said. "They're very thorough. You never managed to confiscate them, which must have stung."

"It stung," he agreed, gravely, and I hated that some part of me liked him. This was the trap of Aldric, the thing no one warned you: he was not a monster. Monsters are easy. He was a tired, clever, courteous man with dust on his boots, and every horror he had built had been built carefully, reasonably, one sensible decision at a time. "You've brought me a great deal of trouble, Keeper."

"You brought the trouble. I just refused to hand over a child."

"A relic," he said.

"A child," I said. "He has a name. Well." I glanced back at Ivo, who was watching Aldric with the frank, unblinking stare of the young assessing the old. "He had several. He's been spending them wisely."

Aldric looked past me at the Door, and his composure cracked — just for a heartbeat, just wide enough to see through. What was behind it wasn't greed. It was hunger, the kind that comes from starving. He looked at the Door the way I had once looked at the sealed archives at Thornfell, the way lonely people look at lit windows.

"Do you know what I was doing," he said quietly, "thirty years ago? Before any of this?"

"Burning archives," I said.

"Reading them." His mouth twisted. "I was a scholar. A minor one. I catalogued provincial tax records in a country that everyone agreed had never existed, and I was the only man alive who noticed the contradiction — that the records existed, and the country didn't. Do you know what they told me when I raised it? That I was confused. That I was ill. They offered me a rest cure." He flexed his hands, as though they still ached from the ink-stained work of a forgotten clerk. "I had proof in my hands, Tamsin. Whole shelves of it. And the world looked at me and declined to remember."

"So you made the world remember you," I said. "By making it forget everything else."

"I made it *consistent*," he snapped, and for a moment the clerk vanished and the conqueror stood there, the man whose machine ate yesterday. "Do you know what memory is, unmanaged? It's a wound that reopens every generation. Vessarion's grief would have bled into the free cities within a century — border wars, blood debts, children taught to hate enemies their grandparents made peace with. I cut the wound out. I gave them blank pages. Blank pages don't bleed."

"Blank pages don't hold anything," I said. "You didn't heal the wound. You just took the bandage off the rest of us and called it surgery. Ask your soldiers. Half of them have grandmothers who sang lullabies in a language they can't name. You didn't erase Vessarion, Aldric. You just orphaned it, and put yourself in charge of the orphanage."

He was silent for a long moment. Below us, the army waited in the failing light, patient as weather. Above us, the Door shimmered, and Ivo hummed, and the granite hummed back.

"What do you want," Aldric said finally, "to give me the song?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's the offer. I'm not selling."

"Everyone sells."

"Not today." I made myself step closer to him, close enough to see the ink stains that thirty years hadn't washed out — real ones, or ones he'd earned honestly once, before the machine. "Here is what I know about you, Aldric. You're not afraid of Vessarion. You're not even afraid of losing. You're afraid of being forgotten *first*. That's why the machine eats yesterday — because every day it eats, you get to stand at the front of history instead of vanishing into the middle of it, one clerk among millions, a footnote in a tax ledger. You didn't conquer countries. You conquered the position of being forgotten."

His face went greyer than his old cloak. He didn't deny it. Denial is for men with something left to protect besides the truth.

"So here is my offer," I said. "Let the song go. Let Ivo sing it out through the Door — not as a nation, not as a grievance, not as anything you need to fear or own. As people. Grandmothers' lullabies. Bread dough and harbor lights. It will flow into every descendant in the free cities, and none of it will raise an army, because it isn't asking for a country anymore. It's just asking to be carried."

"And me?" His voice was very quiet.

"You," I said, "get remembered. Truly. Not as the usurper, not as the man who unnames things — those are just more blank pages, and you'd hate that. As what you actually were: the clerk who noticed the contradiction. The only man alive who cared enough about a dead country to burn the world down over it. When the Choir goes out through that Door, it will carry your story too — accurately, completely, the way you always wanted the archives treated. Every schoolchild in the free cities will know your name, Aldric. Not the name you invented. The one you were born with."

He stared at me. Somewhere behind his eyes, thirty years of careful machinery ground against itself.

"That's mercy," he said, with something like disgust.

"Yes," I said. "It's the worst thing I could do to you. Forgiveness you could survive — you'd have turned it into a victory by morning. Punishment you could survive — you'd have made it a legend. But being remembered *accurately*, by the very people you erased, as a frightened clerk who couldn't bear his own smallness—" I shook my head. "There's no throne in that. There's no monument. There's just you, permanent and true, in the middle of other people's songs. It's the only immortality on offer, and it doesn't come with power attached. Take it or leave it."

For a long time the only sound was the wind off the glacier and the faint, patient humming of the Door.

"You'd really do that," he said at last. "Give the song away. After everything it cost you to bring it here."

"It was never mine to keep," I said. "That was rather the point."

Aldric looked at the Door. At Ivo, who had stopped humming and was simply watching him now, the way you watch a storm deciding which way to break. At his own hands, the clerk's hands, stained with more than ink.

"If I say yes," he said slowly, "the machine stops. My armies stand down. And I walk into that Door with nothing—"

"No," I said. "You walk nowhere. You stay here, in the country you made, and you watch it end. That's the price. You don't get to vanish into the song. You get to remain, and witness, and be witnessed. Witnessing is the whole job now, Aldric. I'd know. I've been doing it for twenty years at a gate nobody believed in."

Below us, torches were being lit along the army's lines, hundreds of them, a river of small fires waiting for an order. Above us, the Door opened a little wider, as if it had been listening the whole time.

And Ivo, who had been silent through all of it, spoke in Old Vessarine — one short phrase, soft, almost gentle. I have since learned what it means. It is the first line of the oldest song in the archive: *Come home, whoever you are. The door remembers you.*

Aldric's breath went out of him like a man struck.

He looked at the child. He looked at the Door. And then he did the thing I will spend the rest of my life trying to deserve having seen: he knelt. Slowly, joints cracking, an old man kneeling in the frost on a mountainside in a country that officially did not exist — and waited, like a clerk before a shelf of records, to hear whether he would be entered into the book or struck from it.

"Sing, then," Aldric said, to Ivo, in a voice scraped down to the wood. "Before I change my mind. Before *they* do." He jerked his chin toward the torchlit river of his army below. "Because I am not the only one who's afraid tonight, Keeper. Four thousand men are standing in the dark wondering what happens to them if yesterday comes back. Whatever the boy sings next — it had better be an answer for them too."

I turned to Ivo. The fork of black glass was blazing now, silent and bright, and beyond it the Door stood open onto kitchens and harbors and a hundred thousand lit windows, waiting for a voice.

Ivo looked at me — not for permission. For company.

So I took my place beside him at the threshold, with a kneeling tyrant at my back and an army holding its breath below, and Ivo drew the breath that would become the new song of Vessarion —

and began to sing.
