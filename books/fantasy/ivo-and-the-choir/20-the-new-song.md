## 20. The New Song

The Door Out of Vessarion was not a door.

I want that on the record, somewhere, in some ledger that survives longer than I do. Everyone had told me about it — the scholar in Marrowgate with his ink-stained fingers, Tamsin's dead mentor in her letters, even Serel, who had seen Aldric's maps and believed them. A door. I had imagined oak and iron, hinges the size of oxen, a keyhole you could lose a cat in. Something you could knock on. Something that could be locked against you, which is what doors do best.

It was a threshold of light standing in a field of snow, with no wall around it at all.

We had come down out of the deep roads at dawn, the fork humming against my chest like a second heartbeat, and there it was: an archway made of nothing but morning, tall as a cathedral's promise, and through it — not the far side of the field, but other fields. A hundred weathers. A hundred mornings stacked like pages. Through the Door I could see a harbor where gulls wheeled over masts I didn't know, and a kitchen garden under rain, and a highland road with a woman walking it carrying firewood, and all of them at once, all of them true, all of them *Vessarine*.

"It opens onto everywhere they went," Tamsin said quietly. She stood beside me with her hand on my shoulder, and her voice had the careful flatness she used when she was afraid of what she felt. "Every place the exiles carried their songs. It's not a way out of the country, Ivo. It's the country, poured out."

The Choir rose in me like water finding its level.

I had been hearing it for days by then — since the Hall of Rewritten Kings, since the murals that showed Aldric's grey ancestors planting their banners on our green hills before our hills had ever existed. The shards sang to each other across the distances between them, and I was the tuning fork now, the black glass warm against my sternum, and every fragment of the song that Aldric hadn't yet fed into his machine came looking for me the way lost dogs come looking for the one person who will feed them.

They were frightened. That was the thing nobody had told me, the thing I would have to tell everyone afterward until they stopped flinching from it: the Choir was frightened. It had been a nation's memory for nine hundred years, and nations are proud things, and pride does not know how to be afraid. But Aldric's engine had eaten its brothers and sisters shard by shard, ground them into his smooth new history where Vessarion had never sung at all, and what remained of the song huddled inside me like children in a cellar during a bombardment, singing quietly so the soldiers upstairs wouldn't hear.

*Ivo,* they said, in the voices of everyone who had ever died singing. *Ivo, little heir, little last note. Close us up. Make us small. Hide us where he can't reach.*

And underneath that, quieter, in a voice I recognized because it was the voice I'd heard in my dreams since before I could talk, the voice I had spent my own name to buy back a piece of:

*Or open us. If you dare. If you love us enough to let us go.*

* * *

Tamsin wanted me to sing us safe.

I don't blame her. You have to understand what she'd carried to get me here — a child she'd found frozen at her gate, a lie told against twenty years of her own law, smugglers' roads and memory-thieves and a march straight through the lion's mouth, and at the end of it all, on the threshold itself, she had turned to me with snow in her hair and said, "Sing us behind a wall, Ivo. Sing Vessarion whole again, behind the Door, where no one can touch it. Let him have the empty lands. We'll keep what matters."

It was a good plan. It was the plan the Choir wanted. Shut the door, keep the treasure, lick your wounds for a century or three, and someday come back and take it all again. Grief is very good at plans like that. Grief is an excellent architect and a terrible neighbor.

But I had stood in the Village That Forgot Itself. I had watched old Marta — the woman who couldn't remember her own name but still set out two cups every morning out of habit whose reason she'd lost — I had watched the fork give her back herself for eleven minutes, and I had watched her weep, and I had watched the forgetting come back down over her face like weather. Eleven minutes of being Marta. Then nothing, forever, unless something changed.

Aldric's machine ate memories. But forgetting wasn't only his work. Forgetting was what time did to anything kept behind glass. The Choir behind a door, whole and safe and sealed, would be a museum. And I had walked through Aldric's museum. I knew what museums did to countries.

"Your mother," Tamsin said, when I tried to explain. She crouched down to my height, which she only did when she was losing an argument and knew it. "You gave up her face at the auction. You told me. Singing them out — into everyone, everywhere, scattered thin — how is that better than keeping them? At least this way someone might find her again."

"That's why," I said. "Because she's not mine alone."

I didn't have her face anymore. That was the price of the fork, paid at the Auction of Names, and I won't pretend it doesn't still hurt; some mornings I wake up reaching for it like a missing tooth. But here is what I learned when I bought myself back half-blind: my mother's face lived in more heads than mine. There were cousins in Marrowgate, aunts in the highland free towns, a fisherman in the southern ports who had once shared a fire with her and laughed at her terrible singing. Every exile had carried pieces of everyone else out of the burning country, the way you carry embers in a pot. The pieces were scattered. Scattered things die.

Unless you water them.

* * *

Serel held the pass. I should say that first, because whatever else is true about her — and much of it is bad — she held the pass while we stood at the Door, and she bought us the hour we needed, and when the grey riders finally broke through she was bleeding from a cut above her eye and laughing, actually laughing, which I choose to remember as her real face.

So it was just Tamsin and me and the fork and the morning, and the Choir pressing against the inside of my ribs like a crowd at a gate, and the Door standing open onto everywhere.

"I don't know the words," I told Tamsin. "All of it. Nobody knows all of it. That's the whole point of a choir — no one carries the whole song."

"You're the heir of the singing line," she said. "The records said—"

"The records said I'm the vessel." I shrugged, and felt the black glass shift warm against my skin. "A vessel isn't a singer. A vessel just holds what's poured into it." I looked at the Door, at the hundred mornings stacked in its arch, and I understood — finally, the way you understand a thing in your body before your head catches up — what was being asked. Not a performance. Not a spell. A *pouring*.

"Tamsin," I said, "when you were a girl, did anyone ever sing to you?"

She blinked. "My grandmother. Lullabies. She called them border songs — said they came from over the mountains, from before the treaty. Why?"

"Because you're in it," I said. "You've been in it all along. That's what the Door is *for*."

* * *

Here is what happened, as well as I can tell it, though telling it is like describing a flood with a teacup.

I opened the fork.

Not broke it — it doesn't break, it's a shard, breaking is what was already done to it — but I opened it the way you open a window in a house that's been shut for winter, and the Choir came up through me, all of it, every fragment Aldric hadn't devoured, and I did not try to hold it or shape it into grievance. That was the temptation, understand. The old song *wanted* to be a grievance. Nine hundred years of memory, and the loudest parts of it were the borders, and the battles, and the names of the kings, and the wrongs done to us, itemized and cross-referenced and ready for the day of accounting. The Choir knew how to be a nation. Being a nation is mostly knowing whom to hate and in what order.

I sang past it.

I don't mean I was brave. I mean I was nine years old and tired, and I thought about Marta setting out her two cups, and I thought about my mother's face that I couldn't see anymore but that other people still could, and I thought about Tamsin's grandmother singing border lullabies to a girl on the wrong side of a treaty, and I understood that the song had never really been about Vessarion. Vessarion was just the room the song had lived in. Rooms burn. Songs move.

So I sang the grandmothers. All of them, every grandmother in the song, kneading bread and cursing the cold and telling stories with the endings worn off from use. I sang the fishermen and the fiddlers, the midwives and the liars, the shepherd boys who fell asleep counting sheep that counted back. I sang the arguments — oh, the arguments, the glorious petty arguments about whose turn it was to mend the fence, which lasted four verses — and the weddings, and the jokes, and the recipes that everyone claimed to make correctly and no one ever did. I sang the grief too, because you can't leave it out, grief is load-bearing; but I sang it the way you sing a funeral, which is to say surrounded by people, which is to say not alone.

And the Door took it.

The memories went out through the threshold like light through a prism, and instead of one beam there were ten thousand, and each one found a descendant. That's what the Door opened onto — not places. *People.* Every scattered ember of the exile diaspora, every family that had fled east and south and over the mountains, every child of every child who had ever hummed a tune they couldn't explain or craved a food they couldn't name or woke weeping from dreams of green hills they'd never seen. The song poured into them, and they woke up remembering.

In Marrowgate, the old scholar dropped his pen and began to weep, and then began to write, faster than he had ever written, because suddenly he remembered everything his grandfather had told him in a language he'd pretended not to know. In the highland towns, women stopped in market squares with their hands full of apples and said their great-grandmothers' names out loud for the first time in thirty years. In the southern ports, fishermen tied up their boats and taught their children a round song about a fish that grants wishes if you insult it properly, and the children learned it in an afternoon because it was already in them, it had always been in them, waiting.

That is how Vessarion ended. Not with a wall and not with a war. It ended by becoming everywhere. You cannot burn a country that lives in ten thousand kitchens. You cannot rewrite a history that has stopped being a history and become a hundred thousand grandmothers again.

The Choir, as it went, grew quiet in me. Not silent — I still hear it, I will always hear it, faintly, the way you hear the sea in a shell — but quiet, unclenched, no longer afraid. Its last words through my mouth were not a lament. They were a thank-you. I am choosing to believe that part was meant for Tamsin.

* * *

When it was done I was on my knees in the snow, and the fork in my hands was clear as ordinary glass, and cold, and finished. An empty cup. I held it up to the light and there was nothing in it at all, and I laughed, which surprised both of us.

"It's over?" Tamsin asked. She had tears on her face and was pretending she didn't, which is very Tamsin.

"It's not anywhere," I said. "That's better than over."

Through the Door, the hundred mornings were dimming, folding closed like a book whose story has finally been read aloud to the end. But before the last page turned, I saw — I swear this — a woman walking a highland road with firewood on her back, and she paused, and she looked directly out of the Door at me, and she smiled, and I did not know her face.

But I knew her smile. It was my mother's smile. Someone out there was wearing it now, some cousin or stranger or granddaughter-of-a-neighbor, and they would pass it on, and pass it on, and it would never be lost again because it was no longer kept anywhere. It was just *loose*, in the world, doing its work.

The Door closed. Where it had been there was only snow, and beyond the snow the pass, and beyond the pass the sound of Serel's riders coming down hard, and Tamsin pulling me up by the arm saying, "Can you run?" and me saying, "I think I need to walk for a bit, actually, I just distributed a country," and her almost smiling, almost, in the way that meant yes.

We started down toward the sound of hooves, and the empty fork sat light as a bird bone in my pocket, and somewhere behind us a country that no longer existed went on living in every direction at once.

I was, as far as anyone could prove, no one at all. No heir. No vessel. No name worth stealing.

I have never in my life been so rich.
