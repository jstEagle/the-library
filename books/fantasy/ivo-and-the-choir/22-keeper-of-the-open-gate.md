## 22. Keeper of the Open Gate

The treaty that sealed the Thornfell gate was written on vellum, signed by two men who are now both dead or forgotten — one of them rather thoroughly forgotten, which I confess gives me a small and uncharitable satisfaction — and it ran to forty-one clauses specifying exactly how shut the door must be. Bars of iron. Bars of law. A garrison that was never funded. A seal that melted in the first spring thaw because someone had used cheap wax.

I have burned my copy. Not out of defiance, though I would have enjoyed it being defiance. Out of practicality: the paper made excellent kindling for the schoolroom stove.

Yes. Schoolroom.

Three years ago this month, I stood at the gate with a crowbar and a ledger full of smuggler roads and asked myself what, precisely, a keeper keeps when there is nothing left on either side of her door worth guarding from the other. The answer, it turned out, was children. Which is harder than guarding against armies, if you want the truth of it. Armies announce themselves. Children arrive in ones and twos, in all weather, with mud on their boots and questions like *why does the mountain have two names* and *can you really sing a door open*, and they do not stop asking until you answer, and then they ask better questions.

The gatehouse has been re-roofed. The portcullis stays up now, rusted into its arch like an old man's raised arm that he's decided to keep that way. Where the customs ledgers once recorded contraband brandy and untaxed wool, there are now slates, chalk, and a shelf of books I copied out myself in the long winters — primers of Old Vessarine, because apparently if you want a language to survive, you cannot leave it to scholars. Scholars will bury it. You have to give it to eight-year-olds, who will treat it with exactly the reverence it deserves, which is none, and thereby keep it alive.

I teach them the word for snow first. *Shivrel.* It means both the falling of snow and the hush after it, which tells you everything about the people who made the word, and nothing that any treaty ever captured.

* * *

You should understand something about how the country ended, since I was there, more or less, at the end of it, standing in a doorway that opened onto everywhere.

When Ivo sang — and I will not pretend I understood the half of it; I stood behind him holding a tuning fork of black glass that had stopped humming because it had finally come home — the memories did not go back into Vessarion. There was no Vessarion to put them back into. They went *out*. Through the Door, down every road the exiles had ever walked, into kitchens in Marrowgate and harbors in the southern free cities and farmhouses where grandmothers had hummed tunes they could not name. People woke up weeping and did not know why, and then, slowly, over weeks and years, they began to know why. That was the shape Ivo gave the song. Not grief. Continuance. He was nine years old and he understood something about memory that Aldric never grasped with his whole machine grinding beneath the palace: that a thing kept alive in people does not need borders.

Aldric got his single true memory, as promised. His mother, singing to him in a tongue he'd had expunged from his own records. I am told he sat with it for three days without speaking, and then he abdicated — quietly, in a letter of eleven lines, no explanation — and walked north into obscurity wearing a coat too thin for the weather. Some say he became a ferryman. Some say he died in the passes. Serel says she doesn't care, and then says it in a way that means she thinks about it often.

Serel stayed, as it happens. Turned her cloak once and never turned it back. She runs the veterans' resettlement office in Marrowgate now, which is to say she spends her days listening to men who were taught to be Aldric's soldiers remember that they were also somebody's grandsons, and she is very good at it, and she writes me letters that begin *Keeper* and end with complaints about paperwork. She visited last autumn. She brought the children candied ginger and tried to maintain her military bearing while six of them demanded she sing the counting song, and she did, badly, in Old Vessarine, and they corrected her pronunciation, and she let them.

That is the other thing you should know about how the country ended. It ended so that things like that could begin.

* * *

Ivo came back to Thornfell the spring after the Door. Of course he did. Where else?

He arrived on foot, alone, having declined — politely, firmly, in that unnervingly adult way he had even at nine — every offer of position, title, guardianship, and restoration that the free cities' councils could compose. There were people who wanted to crown him. The singing line, the last heir, the vessel of the Choir. He looked at the documents with their wax seals and said, in the flat tone of a child declining a second helping of cabbage, "But I'm not a relic anymore."

Then he looked at me and said, "Can I come live at the gate? I'll chop wood."

He has chopped a great deal of wood. He has also, over three years, acquired names the way other children acquire bruises — constantly, accidentally, and from everyone he meets. The Marrowgate dockhands called him *Little Sparrow* because he sat on the harbor wall eating bread the way sparrows eat anything. The shepherd women above the pass call him *Fen* because he arrived during fen-bloom and smelled of it. The archives scholar, old Master Ellery, insists on calling him *the Restorer*, which Ivo endures with the patience of a saint and the expression of a boy being called something embarrassing in front of his friends. The younger students call him *Teacher-Ivo*, half title and half taunt, because he is fifteen now and knows more Old Vessarine than anyone living except perhaps me, and he is insufferable about exactly none of it, which makes him worse.

And his own name? Gone. Spent at the auction, remember — bid away to buy back a shard of the Choir, his mother's face traded with it. For a long time I thought that loss was the saddest ledger entry I kept. But I have watched him these three years, and I have revised my opinion, the way you revise a map when the territory turns out kinder than you drew it. He says a name is a door that only opens inward. He says he spent his because he wanted a door that opened outward instead. Every person who calls him something new is, in his arithmetic, a small repayment of the debt. He is not nameless. He is *crowded* with names. There are days I envy him.

His mother's face he never recovered, and he has made his peace with that too, mostly. Once a year, on the night the Choir went out through the Door, he climbs to the gate tower and sings the lullaby fragment she left in him — the one shard no machine ever touched, the one auction no thief ever found, because it wasn't in the glass. It was in the boy. He sings it to the dark, and the dark, which contains everything now, sings a little of it back.

I stand below on the stairs when he does this. I don't go up. Some doors you keep open by not walking through them.

* * *

The school runs on no charter and answers to no ministry, which suits me fine. Justice outside the law, Serel called it once, teasing me with my own history — the woman who lied to grey cloaks in a snowdrift now issuing attendance records nobody requires. But I've come to think she had it backwards. What we do here isn't outside the law. It's what the law was reaching for and missed by forty-one clauses.

Anyone may come. That is the whole rule, posted on the gate itself in three languages, carved by Ivo's own hand: *ANYONE MAY COME.* We get refugees' grandchildren learning the words their grandparents were punished for speaking. We get free-city merchants who heard somewhere that Vessarine contracts hold magic in them (they don't, but I let them believe it; it pays for chalk). We get Aldric's former soldiers, sent by Serel, men who need to learn the language of the people they marched against, and who sit in the back rows sweating until some seven-year-old corrects their grammar and the room laughs and the sweat stops. Last summer a woman came all the way from the occupied territories — the renamed provinces, where the signs still bear Aldric's invented names — carrying a scrap of embroidery her great-grandmother had made, with a line of Old Vessarine worked into the border. She couldn't read it. Her grandmother couldn't read it. Nobody in three generations could read it.

I spread it on my desk and read it aloud: *The light comes back the same way it left — slowly, and from the east.*

She cried. I pretended to be very busy with the fire. This is a keeper's skill and I have refined it over many years.

We copied the line onto the lintel above the schoolroom door. The original embroidery hangs framed beside the customs ledgers, which I keep open on a shelf to a page from the last winter of the sealed gate — the page where, in my tight keeper's hand, I recorded an anomaly: *one child, unauthorized, half-frozen, speaking an unknown language.* Unknown. I wrote *unknown* about the language of an entire nation, because the treaty told me it was unknown, and I believed the treaty.

I keep that page visible on purpose. First lesson of the advanced class, every year: here is what your teacher did not know, and here is why. Not because she was foolish. Because someone had spent thirty years and a great deal of money teaching her not to know it. Erasure is not a lightning strike, I tell them. It is bookkeeping. And therefore the opposite of erasure is also bookkeeping — done differently, by different hands, starting with whoever picks up the pen next.

Usually at this point in the lesson someone asks if I picked up the pen next, and I say yes, and they ask if it was hard, and I say yes, and then I make them conjugate verbs, because sentiment is like salt: necessary, ruinous in quantity.

* * *

This morning was the first of thaw, and I did what I do every first of thaw. I walked the wall walk with my tea going cold in one hand and counted the roads.

The smuggler roads south are honest roads now, marked on maps, named after the people who ran them — there is a bridge called Widow Cress's Span, and I have decided not to tell the story of why, because some ledgers should stay closed. The pass to the east carries cart traffic again. Down in the valley, where the village-that-forgot-itself stands — they remembered themselves, eventually, most of them, enough; they chose a new name for the place, *Hollisford*, which means nothing in any old tongue, and they chose it deliberately, and I think that might be the healthiest act of memory I ever witnessed — down there, smoke rises from chimneys whose families send us four students and a truly excessive amount of cheese.

From the wall I can see the road north, too. The road into what the maps now just call *the old country*, no possessive, no claim. Children go there sometimes, ours do, walking with their teachers to see the capital and its museum, where the rewritten murals hang unrepaired — Aldric's government fell before anyone repainted them back, and the council voted to leave them as they are, with plaques explaining what was done to them. Lies, preserved, labeled. The Hall of Rewritten Kings is now the best classroom in existence for teaching children how rewriting works. Ivo goes every year. He stands in front of the mural where Vessarine heroes wear Aldric's ancestors' faces, and he puts his hand flat against the stone, and the stone — well. The guides say the murals have started fading on their own. The paint of the lies flakes; underneath, older colors surface. Nobody can explain it. I can, but I don't, because some explanations are better as mysteries, and because Ivo asked me once, very seriously, to let the wall keep its secret. A singer's privilege. I honor it.

The tea was cold by the time I finished counting, which is traditional. Below me in the yard, the morning class had already assembled itself without me, which is also traditional, and Ivo was at the blackboard writing *shivrel* in careful characters while the little ones shouted guesses at its meaning.

"Snow," said one.

"The quiet after snow," said another, smug, a returning student.

"Both," said Ivo, "because the people who named it thought falling and silence were the same kindness." Then he saw me on the wall and raised the chalk in salute. "Keeper! We're starting without you."

"You always start without me."

"You always come anyway."

I went down. I hung my cold tea on its hook by the door — the same hook, I note with a keeper's precision, where a ring of keys once hung, keys to a door officially sealed, keys I have not needed in three years because the door is never locked. Anyone may come. Anyone may leave. The portcullis rusts in its arch with its arm raised, and I have decided to let it keep that way.

Inside, twenty-some children of six nations and no nation waited with their slates, and a boy with too many names handed me the chalk, and outside the snow was beginning again — *shivrel*, the falling and the hush, the same kindness twice —

and the light, as the embroidery promised, came back the same way it left.

Slowly. From the east. Through an open gate.
