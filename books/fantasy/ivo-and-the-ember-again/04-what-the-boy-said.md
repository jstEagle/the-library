## 4. What the Boy Said

*There was a door once. Or a mouth. It is hard to say which, because from the inside they feel the same.*

*I was on the other side of it for a very long time, and then I was not, and now I am here, which is small and wet and smells of woodsmoke and someone else's bread.*

*Let me start again. Starting again is the only thing I know how to do.*

---

Before — and *before* is a country he cannot find on any map, though he has looked, though he has turned the whole inside of his skull over like a pocket hunting for a lost coin — before, there were many of him.

That is the first thing. Not the burning. The burning comes later, or earlier, or always; the burning is patient and will keep. The first thing is that there were many.

He remembers being sung to. He remembers being sung *by*. Both at once, which should be impossible, but impossibility was his native weather. There were voices the way a forest has trees — not counted, just assumed, going on in every direction past the edge of sight. Some of the voices were old enough that their words had worn smooth as river stones. Some were new, still sharp, still cutting themselves accidentally on their own opinions. They argued. Oh, how they argued. About mercy, mostly, and about whether a promise made to one generation bound the next, and about whether the stars were lamps or wounds.

He misses the arguments. That is a strange thing to miss, he knows. But when you have been a thousand conversations all happening at once, silence is not peace. Silence is a room with all the furniture removed.

The woman — Vesper, the others call her, though she has not told him her name yet because he has not told her his, because he does not have one, which is a problem he will get to — the woman gave him bread tonight. She tore it in half without being asked and put the larger piece in his hand, and then she pretended not to watch him eat, which is a kindness he did not know people could do: kindness with its coat pulled up over its face so as not to embarrass anyone.

The bread tasted like nothing. Not bad-nothing. Just nothing, the way everything tastes lately, as if his tongue remembers being an instrument that played whole symphonies and has been given, instead, a single dry note.

He ate it anyway. All of it. You do not refuse bread. Even gods know that.

Especially gods know that.

---

Here is what he remembers of the burning, in the order it insists on:

First the light, which was not light but attention — the whole world's attention landing on one place at once, the way a hawk's shadow lands before the hawk.

Then the hands. Many hands. He had thought hands were for holding. These hands were for holding *down*.

Then a voice saying words he understood perfectly and would spend a long time wishing he didn't. Words like *it is necessary*. Words like *forgive us*. Words said by someone who meant them, which is the worst kind of knife, the kind that apologizes on the way in.

And last — last, or first, time is a bowl that keeps getting picked up and set down — last, the falling. Not down. *In.* Falling inward, into a space too small, folding and folding like a letter written by someone in a hurry who knew the letter might never be read but wrote it anyway, beautifully, because that was the job.

He fell into a boy.

No. That is not right either. He fell into the shape a boy would leave behind if a boy were ever briefly empty, which no boy ever is, but the world made an exception, or a mistake, and here he is, wearing the mistake.

---

*"What's your name?"*

The woman asked him this on the second night, kneeling so their eyes were level, rain still dripping off her braid onto the floorboards between them. Her voice was careful the way ice over a stream is careful.

He opened his mouth.

Inside his mouth was a language with no floor to it. Inside his mouth were words that predated the concept of *kingdom*, that had been old when the idea of *old* was young. If he spoke, really spoke, the ward-stones would hum again — he felt them humming now, faintly, through the soles of his feet, like dogs dreaming — and something in the woman's face would change, and he did not want her face to change. He liked her face. It had lines around the eyes from squinting at horizons, and a small scar through one eyebrow, and it did the thing where it tried to be stern and failed by degrees, like a fortress surrendering one gate at a time.

So instead of his name — since he had none — he said the word. The only word he had been able to carry out of the fire without dropping it.

The word means *stay*.

He knows it means *stay* the way you know a scar is yours. He does not know why it is the word. Of all the words in him, all the hymns and arguments and lullabies, this one came through the burning clenched in his teeth like a child carrying one toy out of a house fire. Perhaps it was the last thing someone said to him. Perhaps it was the last thing he said to someone. Perhaps both. Perhaps the word is a door with two sides and he has only ever stood on one.

The woman went very still when he said it. Then she did the thing with her face again, the surrendering, and put her hand on his head — lightly, the way you'd touch a bird you didn't want to startle — and said, "Okay."

Just that. *Okay.*

Of all the languages he holds, hers is the one he is learning fastest.

---

He tries, sometimes, in the dark, to find himself. It goes like this:

*I am the one who—*

Who what? Who sang? Everyone sang. Who burned? Everyone burned. Who was loved?

*Ah,* he thinks. *There.*

But even that slips. Because the love he remembers is vast and diffuse, love the way the sea is wet — everywhere, constant, attributable to nothing. A thousand voices loving him and none of them, he suspects, knowing him specifically, the way a congregation loves a god and not a man. And what he wants — what he has discovered he wants, newly, greedily, shamefully — is the small kind. The specific kind. The kind where someone tears bread in half and gives you the bigger piece and pretends not to watch.

He wants to be somebody's *Ivo*.

Because that is the name they gave him. Not the woman — she hasn't named him anything yet, she is too careful for that, too aware that names are handles and handles are for carrying things away. No. The name arrived some other way, in some town whose name he also doesn't know, from some mouth — a baker's wife, maybe, or a ferryman, someone who looked at this mute soaked creature and shrugged and said "Ivo" the way you'd say "Tuesday," and the name stuck to him like a burr, and now it is the only thing he owns.

Others named him. That is the grief under all the other griefs, the wet stone at the bottom of the well: he came into this world unnamed, and the world, practical and unimpressed, supplied one, and he clutched it, and he hates that he is grateful, and he is grateful.

A god should not be grateful for a borrowed name.

But then, a god should not be hungry either, and yet here he is at dawn, standing in the woman's kitchen, watching her fry eggs with the focused intensity of a scholar reading scripture, and the hunger in him is so loud and so *ordinary* that he almost laughs. Almost. Laughing is another thing he is relearning. His body keeps offering it up like a tentative handshake and his mind keeps not recognizing it.

"Sit," the woman says, pointing at a chair with the spatula. "Eat. And today—" she hesitates, and he watches her decide to say the thing, watches the fortress open another gate, "—today we're going to my chapter-house. There are people there who can help figure out what you are."

*What you are.* Not *who*. He notices. He notices everything; noticing is the only skill that survived the fall intact.

He sits. He eats. The egg tastes like almost-something, which is progress, and he would weep with gratitude if weeping weren't also on the list of things his body keeps proposing and his dignity keeps declining.

---

*Fragment:* a garden where every flower was a bell, and the wind played them, and the music was not pretty, exactly — it was *true*, which is different and better.

*Fragment:* a table of white stone where he sat across from a man made mostly of questions, and they talked until the stars rearranged themselves out of embarrassment.

*Fragment:* running. Small legs, human legs, mud sucking at bare feet, and behind him not footsteps but an absence of sound, a moving hole in the world's hearing, and every prayer spoken aloud anywhere fed it, and he was made entirely of prayer, and so he ran toward the one place in creation where magic could not follow, because even a hunted ember knows that sometimes the safest place is the one place you cannot burn.

*Fragment:* her door. Rain. The knock — his knuckles, his small cold fist, knocking on wood, and the absurdity of it, the *comedy*: a god, knocking. Waiting politely on a mortal's step to be let in out of the weather. Somewhere in the vast archive of him, ten thousand voices are screaming that this is undignified, apocalyptic, wrong.

He thought it was funny. He thinks it is still funny. He hopes someday to be able to tell the woman about it and watch her face do the thing.

---

Night again. She has given him a blanket that smells of her — woodsmoke and iron and some dried herb he doesn't have a word for in any tongue, which delights him, a smell older than his vocabulary, a rare treat.

He lies in the dark and takes inventory, the way she checks her gear each morning, buckle by buckle:

One body, small, prone to shivering. One name, borrowed, secondhand, treasured. One word, *stay*, carried out of a fire. Ten thousand voices, compressed, mostly quiet now, murmuring behind a door he cannot quite reach — though some nights, like tonight, one of them presses close to the crack and says something, and it sounds almost like—

Like warning.

He sits up. The ward-stones outside are humming. Not faintly this time. The sound comes up through the floorboards, through the chair legs, through his teeth, a note held too long, a singer running out of breath and refusing to stop.

The voices inside him have gone silent — all of them, all ten thousand, listening.

Listening to what?

Far off, beyond the valley's border stones, beyond the rain, something is moving that does not make footfalls. Something that eats sound the way he once ate starlight. He can feel it the way you feel a storm in a healed bone: not the thing itself, only the ache of the place where it will be.

It is looking for a voice.

His voice.

He lies back down slowly and pulls the blanket to his chin and practices, silently, in the dark, the hardest prayer he knows — harder than the hymns, harder than the arguments, harder than the word for *stay*.

The prayer of keeping quiet tomorrow.

Outside, the stones hum on, and far away, patient as the burning, something begins to listen back.
