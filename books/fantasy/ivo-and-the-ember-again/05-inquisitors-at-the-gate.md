## 5. Inquisitors at the Gate

The bells woke me before the horns did.

That was wrong, and I knew it was wrong the way you know a stair is missing before your foot finds the gap. The chapter-house bells rang for three things: dawn, death, and danger. It was none of those hours. The light through my shutter was thin and gray and undecided, the kind of morning that hadn't yet committed to being a day at all.

Ivo was asleep on my cot, curled around a blanket like a fist around a coin. He'd been with me four nights now, since Corvane had grudgingly released him to my "temporary guardianship" — his word, spoken with the particular care of a man setting down something he didn't want to hold but couldn't quite bring himself to drop. Four nights of a small boy breathing in the dark of my cottage, and I had already rearranged my whole life around the sound. That should have frightened me more than it did.

I dressed, buckled my sword out of habit, and paused at the door.

"Ivo." I kept my voice low. "If anyone knocks that isn't me, you go out the back and into the root cellar. You remember the way?"

He opened one eye. He didn't nod — he never nodded when it mattered, which I'd come to understand was its own kind of answer — but his hand found the hem of my sleeve and held it for exactly as long as it took me to understand *yes*.

Then I went toward the bells.

* * *

They came up the valley road in a column of twelve, riding horses too fine for the mud, wearing gray cloaks trimmed in white ash-color, and they moved through the Hollow Valley's border stones the way water moves through cloth — slowly, reluctantly, as if the land itself were trying to refuse them.

I stood at the gate of the chapter-house yard beside Maren, who had appeared at my elbow without appearing to walk there, the way she did. Below us, the ward-stones along the road were humming. Not singing. Humming. A low, uneasy note, like a dog deciding whether to growl.

"Magic can't follow past the stones," Maren said quietly. It wasn't a question. It was the first thing every sworn member of the Quiet Flame learned, before they learned their oath, before they learned to hold a blade. *Magic cannot follow.*

"No," I said. "It can't."

"Then why are the stones complaining?"

I had no answer. Twelve riders stopped at the gate, and the man at their head dismounted with the unhurried precision of someone who has never once been told to hurry. He was tall and dry-looking, the way old paper is dry, with a face like a folded letter — everything important hidden in creases. His cloak bore no order's sigil. Just the crown's sun, worked in thread so pale it looked like frost.

"High Warden Corvane," he said, though Corvane hadn't come out yet. His voice carried anyway. Voices like that don't need volume; they need only patience. "I am Inquisitor Drev. I have a writ under the king's seal."

Corvane came out then, and I watched him cross the yard, and I watched the way he walked, and I thought — later, always later, I would think these things and hate myself for thinking them late — I thought: *he isn't surprised.*

A man surprised by bad news walks fast. Corvane walked the way he walked to morning prayer.

"Inquisitor." He bowed, shallow and correct. "The Flame welcomes the king's justice."

"The king's justice seeks an anomalous child." Drev produced a scroll without looking at whoever handed it to him. "Reported seen in this valley. Marked. Speaking in tongues. The crown takes a dim view of anomalies."

"This is a place of silence," Corvane said. "Nothing anomalous passes here."

Drev smiled. It was a small smile, folded like the rest of him. "No. But things pass *through*, High Warden. And things arrive." His eyes lifted — past Corvane, past the gate, past Maren and me standing on the wall-walk like two crows pretending to be architecture — and settled somewhere behind us, on the cottages, on mine among them. "We will search. Gently, if permitted. Otherwise not."

I felt Maren's shoulder press against mine. A warning or a comfort. With Maren it was usually both.

And Corvane said, "Of course. Search."

Too quickly. He agreed too quickly. No warden of the Quiet Flame — no warden worth his oath-scar — surrendered the valley's sanctity to a crown's men without a fight, without a sermon, without at least an hour of righteous obstruction. We guarded the last place magic could not reach. That was the entire point of us. That was the *whole* of us.

But Corvane stepped aside, and the gates opened, and the bells above us went silent mid-swing, as if even bronze knew better than to keep talking.

* * *

They searched gently, as promised, which meant they searched thoroughly while smiling.

I followed them through the chapter-house with my hands clasped behind my back and my heart doing arithmetic it didn't want to finish. Twelve inquisitors, moving room to room. Lifting altar cloths. Peering into the reliquary niches — empty ones, all of them, ever since the courier and his empty box, a fact I had reported to Corvane and Corvane had received with a silence I now understood differently.

In the refectory, one of them — young, freckled, apologetic in the way of a well-trained hound — asked me if the stew smelled strange to me too.

"It smells like turnips," I said.

"That's what I said." He looked genuinely relieved to have an ally. "Sergeant Hale says it's the altitude. I say it's the turnips."

"Sergeant Hale sounds wise."

"He's outside. He doesn't like being indoors near relics. Says they make his teeth itch."

I liked Hale immediately and entirely against my will, and I noted, with the cold little clerk part of my mind that never stops taking inventory, that I could not afford to like any of them. Not one. Liking made mercy possible, and mercy was about to become the most dangerous thing in the valley.

Because they weren't finding anything, and Drev wasn't worried.

That was the thing. I watched him. He stood in the center of the courtyard while his people turned over our quiet house, and he waited — patient as frost — and every few minutes he glanced at Corvane, and Corvane glanced back, and between them passed some agreement I couldn't read but could feel, the way you feel weather changing through a wall.

They already knew where the boy was. Or they trusted they soon would.

Which meant either someone inside had told them —

— or someone inside intended to hand him over.

* * *

They found nothing, because Ivo was in my root cellar among the turnips, being very brave and very still, and because the gods — or whatever remained of one — apparently have a sense of humor about hiding children in vegetables.

Drev received the report of the fruitless search with a nod, as a man receives news he'd already read. Then he said, "There remains the matter of the blessing."

"Blessing?" Corvane asked.

"A courtesy." Drev unfolded his hands. "My order travels with a relic. A flame of the true pyre, they call it. Where anomalies hide, fire reveals. It is an old rite. Harmless. A child stands before the flame; if he is ordinary, the flame does nothing at all. If he is otherwise—" He spread his dry hands. "Well. Then we know."

The courtyard went very quiet. Somewhere a chicken complained, absurdly, about the invasion.

"Trial by fire is forbidden by charter," Maren said from beside me. Her voice was level. Her knuckles were white on the wall. "The Flame's own charter. We do not burn the innocent to find the guilty. That is the first law."

"The first law," Drev agreed pleasantly, "of an order that guards a god. But your god is silent, High Warden. Has been for centuries, I'm told. And a silent god's laws are only words." He turned to Corvane, and now the folded face opened slightly, and what was underneath was worse than cruelty. It was certainty. "Unless the High Warden wishes to tell me the boy does not exist. Unless he wishes to swear, on his oath-scar, on the ashes of Ember himself, that no marked child sleeps within a mile of this stone."

The silence stretched.

I counted Corvane's breaths. Three. Four. On the fifth, he said, "The blessing will be performed at noon. Under the eyes of the order. And if the flame shows nothing, Inquisitor, you will ride out of my valley and trouble it no more."

"If the flame shows nothing," Drev echoed, "I will be the first to apologize."

Nobody apologized afterward, in my experience. People who planned to apologize behaved differently beforehand. They hesitated. They doubted. This man had the specific calm of a carpenter arriving at a house he'd already measured.

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what I did next, because I am not proud of it and pride has no place in what came after.

I went to my cottage. I took Ivo out of the root cellar. And I lied to him.

"There are visitors," I said, kneeling so we were eye to eye. His hair was full of cellar dust and he had a smudge of dirt on his chin shaped, faintly, like a bird. "Important people. They're going to ask you to stand near a fire. Just stand near it. Nothing more. Can you do that? Can you be brave for a few minutes?"

He looked at me. And here is what I did not know yet, what I would only understand much later, when understanding it could do nothing but break me: he looked at me *trustingly*. Fully. The way you look at the ground when you step off a ledge, believing it will hold.

He reached up and touched my oath-scar, the brand on my collarbone, the twin of the mark on his own skin. Then he pointed at himself. Then at the fire I'd just described. Then he made a small sound — not a word, not in any tongue, just a soft falling note, like an ember dropping from a grate.

*I know,* that sound said. Or maybe: *I've done this before.*

God forgive me. I thought he was frightened. I thought I was comforting him. I patted his head and straightened his collar and marched him to the courtyard like a lamb I was proud of, and the whole time the truth sat in my chest unopened, because I had spent twenty years learning not to open certain doors, and I was very good at it.

* * *

They set the brazier in the center of the yard, at noon, under a sky that had finally decided to be bright, which felt like a betrayal.

The relic was small — a iron lantern, slatted, and inside it a flame that burned wrong. I knew fire. Fire is honest; it eats what you give it and asks for more. This flame stood perfectly still, orange-white and unwavering, giving off heat I could feel from ten paces, and it made no sound at all. In the Hollow Valley, where we kept silence like a second skin, a silent flame seemed almost polite.

Almost holy, if you didn't look too long.

"Bring the child," Drev said.

I brought him. My boots weighed as much as armor. The assembled order stood in a crescent around the yard — forty sworn sisters and brothers of the Quiet Flame, faces I had bled beside, eaten beside, buried our dead beside — and not one of them spoke, because speaking was not our way, and I understood suddenly, horribly, how useful our silence was going to be to everyone in this yard except the boy holding my hand.

Corvane stood at the brazier's side. He did not look at me. He looked at the flame, and his expression was the expression of a man praying, and I had seen him pray ten thousand mornings, and this was different. This was the face of a man praying for something to go *wrong*.

"Stand him here," Drev said, indicating a chalk circle drawn on the flagstones.

I let go of Ivo's hand. He stepped into the circle. Small feet. Cellar dust still on his chin, the little bird-shape of it, and I fixed my eyes on that smudge because looking at his face was going to be impossible in a moment and I needed somewhere to put my eyes.

The inquisitor opened the lantern's slats.

Heat rolled out across the yard. The nearest of our order flinched back a half-step. The flame leaned — actually leaned, like a plant toward light — toward the boy.

And Ivo flinched.

Not the flinch of fear. I know fear in children; I had been a frightened child myself, once, before the order found me and gave the fear a shape and a name and called it faith. Fear pulls back. Fear runs. What Ivo did was smaller and far worse: he drew his arms in tight against his body, tucked his chin, and turned his shoulder to the flame — the precise, practiced motion of someone protecting themselves from a blow they have taken many times before.

An old motion. A remembered motion.

The flame leaned further. The heat bent around the chalk circle like water bending around a stone, and I saw the air shimmer, and I saw the boy's shadow on the flagstones — and the shadow was wrong. It flickered. Not with the flame's flicker. With its own rhythm, its own pulse, quick and gold-edged, like something inside him turning over in its sleep.

Like something answering.

"Enough," I said.

My voice cracked across the yard louder than I'd meant it, loud enough that heads turned, loud enough that in a house of silence it landed like a dropped plate. Drev looked at me with mild interest, a man noting weather.

"The rite is concluded," Corvane said quickly — again too quickly, always too quickly — and closed the lantern's slats himself. "You see it. Nothing. An ordinary child, startled by heat, as any child would be."

"I see a flame that leaned," Drev said softly. "I see a shadow that breathed. High Warden, I have performed this rite in nine provinces. I have stood a hundred children before this fire, and not one of their shadows flickered." He turned to me, and his folded face almost managed kindness. "Warden Halloran, isn't it? Twenty years' service. Sworn at fourteen, they tell me. You held the boy's hand coming in. Tell me — when the flame leaned toward him — what did you feel?"

What I felt was cold horror opening in my chest like a door into winter. What I felt was every story I'd ever been told about the founding rearranging itself behind my eyes. What I felt was the memory of a dead courier and an empty reliquary, and the hum of the ward-stones, and four nights of small breathing in my dark cottage, and the word the boy had spoken at my door in a language older than kingdoms — the word I had never reported, never written down, never said aloud since.

What I said was: "I felt nothing."

Drev studied me for a long moment. Then he smiled his small folded smile and turned back to Corvane.

"We'll lodge at the toll-house tonight," he said. "Tomorrow I'll send for the writ's second seal. Surrender of the anomaly requires the High Warden's mark and witness." He paused. "You'll have until dusk to decide whether you are the crown's servant or your god's. Though I confess, High Warden — from where I stand, the distinction appears to have collapsed some time ago."

They rode out. The gates closed. The bells stayed silent.

* * *

Maren found me at the well, where I was drawing water I had no use for, because my hands needed a job or they were going to do something unforgivable.

"You saw it," she said.

"I saw a boy flinch from fire like he'd been burned before." The bucket clanked down the shaft, absurdly loud. "I saw a shadow breathe. I saw our High Warden agree to hand a child to the king's executioners before they'd finished dismounting, and I stood in that yard and said *I felt nothing*."

"You said what you had to say."

"I lied." I pulled the bucket up and didn't drink from it. "To an inquisitor. To protect—" I stopped. Say it plainly, Vesper. Say the word. "To protect the boy."

Maren was quiet. Beyond her, the chapter-house stood in the afternoon light, gray and familiar, the home I had given twenty years to, and for the first time it looked to me like what it perhaps had always been: a very beautiful lock built around a very quiet secret.

"Dusk," she said finally. "He gave us until dusk. Which means he expects Corvane to sign by then, and Corvane will sign, because Corvane has been afraid since the day that boy knocked on a door in this valley, and afraid men sign things." She looked at me. Her eyes were steady and sad and, underneath, lit with something I recognized because I felt it too — the terrible bright weight of a decision already made, waiting only to be spoken. "Vesper. What are you going to do?"

I thought of Ivo in the root cellar, patient among the turnips, trusting the ground to hold him.

I thought of the word he'd spoken at my door, and how I'd known it the way you know your own name — not learned, *known* — and how I had never told a living soul what it meant.

I set down the bucket.

"Maren," I said, "how well do you know the catacombs?"

She didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice was very quiet, and it was the answer that undid the last twenty years of my life:

"Well enough to get you in. Getting you out is your problem."
