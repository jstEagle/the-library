## 6. Theft of a Small God

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Vesper finally admitted she wasn't going to sleep.

She lay in her cell in the east wing of the chapter-house, listening to the sounds of the Quiet Flame settling into its nightly ritual: the creak of the watch changing at the hour bell, the soft shuffle of sandals on stone, Brother Aldous two doors down snoring with the dedication he brought to everything. Somewhere below, in the reliquary vault, a boy was locked behind three iron doors because the High Warden feared what he might say.

*What he might say.* As if words were fire. As if a child's voice could burn down twenty years of Vesper's life.

She sat up. The cot didn't even protest; she'd learned long ago to move like a shadow, and shadows don't wake springs. Twenty years of practice. Twenty years of oaths spoken over cold flame, of patrols along the border stones, of silence kept so faithfully that sometimes she'd forgotten her own voice outside of prayer.

And tomorrow at dawn, the king's inquisitors would come collect their *anomalous child*, and Corvane would hand him over with the gentle sorrow of a man donating an old coat, and somewhere on a pyre Ivo would learn what the order's mercy looked like from the inside.

Vesper dressed in the dark. She did not pray. That, more than anything, told her what she already knew: that she'd made her choice somewhere between vespers and midnight, and her body had simply been waiting for her mind to catch up.

Her oath-scar itched at her wrist. She ignored it. Or tried to.

* * *

The catacombs beneath the chapter-house were older than the order itself, older than the kingdom, older — if you believed the foundation stones — than most gods. Vesper knew them the way she knew her own hands: every turning, every dead end, every niche where a lamp could be set without casting light up the stairwells. Novices were taught the upper galleries for burial rites. Wardens learned the deeper passages, the ones that smelled of wet chalk and old smoke, where the first brothers had carved prayers into walls that predated the silent god they claimed to serve.

She carried no lamp. The dark was a country she'd lived in for two decades, and it received her like a neighbor.

Three iron doors stood between the reliquary vault and freedom. The first was never truly locked — Corvane's piety extended only as far as his convenience — and it opened under her hand with a groan she felt in her teeth. She froze, counted ten heartbeats, heard nothing but the drip of water finding its patient way through stone.

The second door was locked, and here her plan met its first honest obstacle, which was that Vesper Dunmore had spent twenty years learning to keep people out of places, not get into them. She knelt, pressed her ear to the iron, and listened the way you listen to a river before crossing: for rhythm, for gaps.

Footsteps above. Slow, deliberate, pausing at intervals. The night warden making rounds.

Vesper flattened herself against the wall beside the door and waited, and in the waiting, terror finally caught up with her. It arrived not as a flood but as a small, precise voice, very much like Corvane's, saying: *You took an oath. You put your blood on the flame. You said, "I will guard the silence until my last breath," and the flame heard you, and now you are going to open this door and unmake yourself.*

"Shut up, Corvane," she whispered, and was mildly horrified to discover she'd said it aloud.

The footsteps faded. She worked the lock with two hairpins stolen from Maren's comb — Maren would notice, Maren noticed everything, and that thought landed in Vesper's chest like a swallowed stone — and after an eternity of small metallic failures, the bolt gave with a click that sounded, in the catacomb hush, like a cathedral bell.

The third door was the boy himself.

He was sitting cross-legged on the floor of the vault, awake, watching the door as though he'd known she was coming. In the light of the single votive they'd allowed him — one flame, pitifully small, the order's idea of generosity — his brand showed pale on his collarbone, twin to the scar on her wrist. He didn't look afraid. That frightened her more than screaming would have.

"I'm getting you out," Vesper said. Her voice came out rough, rusted at the hinges. "Don't ask me why. I couldn't tell you."

Ivo tilted his head. Then he reached up and touched the votive flame — not close enough to burn, just near, the way you'd greet a horse — and the little fire leaned toward his finger like a plant toward sun.

"Oh," Vesper said faintly. "Right. Yes. We're definitely leaving."

* * *

The second door nearly ended everything.

They were halfway back through the deep galleries when the lock she'd sprung began, softly, to re-engage — some counterweight mechanism she hadn't accounted for, old engineering punishing new trespass — and Vesper had exactly enough time to shove Ivo behind her and draw her knife before the lamplight found them both.

Maren stood at the end of the passage.

Her sworn sister wore her nightclothes under a warden's cloak thrown on hastily, and her hair was loose, and in fifteen years Vesper had never once seen Maren's hair loose on duty. Her hand rested on the hilt of her blade. Her face was doing something complicated — grief and fury and a third thing underneath, something that looked terribly like hope.

"You used my hairpins," Maren said.

"Maren—"

"You used my *hairpins*, Ves. Do you know how long I've had those? Since novitiate. They're the only thing I own that isn't issued." Her voice cracked on the last word, and Vesper understood, all at once, that Maren had been standing in the dark for a while. Waiting. Choosing.

"Step aside," Vesper said. It came out as a plea wearing armor. "Please. You saw what happened today. You saw him flinch. They're going to burn a child, Mare. A child who talks in his sleep in a language older than the stones we patrol. And Corvane signed the transfer before the inquisitors finished asking."

"I know." Maren's jaw tightened. "I transcribed the writ. My own hand copied the seal." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Vesper had ever heard from her. "Twenty years, Ves. Twenty years of keeping the silence, and tonight I find out what I've actually been keeping."

Ivo shifted behind Vesper's legs. And then, in the smallest voice, in the language that made ward-stones hum, he said one word to Maren.

Vesper didn't know its meaning yet. But she watched it land — watched Maren go white, then still, then quietly undone, tears cutting clean lines down her face in the lamplight.

"What did he say?" Vesper asked.

"I don't—" Maren wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, like a woman scrubbing a stain. "I don't know. But my mother used to hum something. Before the fever took her. Same tune. Same *shape*. Nobody taught him that. Nobody alive knows that." She looked at Vesper, and her eyes were terrible and clear. "If I let you pass, I'm dead. You understand that? Not exiled. Dead. Treason against the crown and heresy against the order in one night — Corvane won't even need a trial, he'll need a mop."

"I know."

"And if I stop you, I'm worse than dead." She said it flatly, like reporting weather. Then she stepped aside, and the lamplight swung away from the passage, and Maren Oakes, sworn sister of the Quiet Flame, turned her back on them both. "Go left at the ossuary, not right. Right side flooded since spring. There's a grate at the far end — the bolts are rusted, hit it twice low on the left. And Ves?"

Vesper paused, Ivo's small hand gripping hers.

"The hairpins," Maren said, not turning around. "Bring them back someday. I want to see your face when you do."

Then she walked away up the gallery, unhurried, a woman going to surrender herself, and Vesper stood in the dark with a god-child's hand in hers and wept without making a single sound, because some silences are holy and she finally knew the difference.

* * *

The grate gave on the second blow, exactly as promised, and then they were out — out under a sky rinsed with rain, out past the ward-stones whose hum rose to meet them like a held note finally released, out into the hills where the world went on being large and indifferent and, tonight, mercifully empty.

Vesper ran with Ivo on her back until her lungs burned, then walked, then ran again. The boy weighed almost nothing. Gods, apparently, kept light.

Near dawn they sheltered in a shepherd's hut with a collapsed roof and a view of the valley below, where the chapter-house smoked its thin morning fires, serene and blind. Vesper built no fire. She wrapped Ivo in her cloak and fed him dried plums and half a loaf she'd stolen from the kitchens with less guilt than she'd expected and more than she'd admit.

"We're criminals now," she told him. "Both of us. Well. You were already ahead of me on that."

Ivo chewed his plum with the solemnity of a scholar. Then he pointed at her wrist, at the oath-scar, and touched his own brand, and made a small connecting gesture between them — *same, same* — and smiled.

"Don't," Vesper said, laughing despite herself, the laugh cracking halfway through into something else. "Don't be charming. I've committed treason for you. The least you can do is be difficult."

But the exhilaration was real, and terrifying, and better than anything twenty years of perfect obedience had ever given her. She felt it singing in her blood alongside the fear: she had broken the law of gods and men in a single night, and the sun had risen anyway, and the boy was warm against her side, and the world had not ended.

It would, probably. Later. There would be pursuit, and prices, and Maren in a cell somewhere answering questions with her chin up. Vesper filed the thought away in the place where she kept all the debts she couldn't pay yet.

For now there was rain on the hills, and a road south, and a child asleep against her shoulder who murmured, once, in the old tongue — a word that sounded like water over stones.

Vesper closed her eyes and memorized the shape of it.

Behind them, far below, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the prayer bell, but the alarm, spilling down the valley in urgent bronze waves. The hunt had begun.

Vesper stood, settled Ivo against her back, and turned south.

"Hold on tight, little ember," she said. "We're about to make history very angry."

The alarm bell rang on behind them, and the road waited, and neither of them looked back.
