## 7. The Language Before Kingdoms

*Vesper*

The boy hummed while he walked.

Not a tune she recognized — Vesper doubted any kingdom had a tune like it — but something low and round, the sound of water moving under ice. He'd been doing it since dawn, hands in his pockets, kicking at stones on the old shepherd's track as though they were three days from the chapter-house instead of fleeing it.

"You know," Vesper said, adjusting the strap of her pack, "most fugitives have the decency to look frightened."

Ivo glanced up at her. He was perhaps eight years old, small for it even, with soot-dark hair that refused all attempts at order and eyes that caught firelight in a way that had nothing to do with firelight. He said nothing. He'd said almost nothing since the catacombs — except that one word at her door, the word that had made the ward-stones sing and turned her whole life inside out like a pocket.

She was beginning to suspect he simply didn't see the point of fear.

"Fine," she muttered. "Hum away. When the inquisitors catch us, I'll tell them we're a traveling mother and son, and you'll ruin it by humming like a haunted kettle."

The corner of his mouth twitched. Progress.

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the border ruins that predated the valley's founding by a thousand years or more. Vesper built a small fire — small, always small; flame drew eyes — and Ivo sat cross-legged across from it, watching her with that unnerving patience children only ever had when they were not entirely children.

He pointed at the fire.

"Yes," Vesper said. "Fire. Very good."

He shook his head, patient as a schoolmaster. Pointed again. Then, softly, he spoke: *"Aeth."*

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

It wasn't loud. It wasn't strange, exactly — two syllables, a breath between them. But something behind her ribs *turned over*, the way a key turns, and for half a heartbeat Vesper Halloran was not sitting beside a dead watchtower at all. She was standing in a hall of white stone, vast and sunlit, watching ten thousand people kneel at once, and the word *aeth* rose out of them like heat off a summer field, and she understood — not translated, *understood* — that they were saying *the light that stays when the light goes out.*

Then it was gone. She was on a hillside with an aching head and a boy watching her with grave satisfaction.

"What," she said carefully, "was that."

*"Aeth,"* he repeated, and pointed at the fire again, and this time she saw it: not just flame, but the idea of flame. The keeping of it. The grief of it going out.

"That's not a word for fire," she said slowly. "That's a word for... everything fire means."

Ivo nodded once, solemn as a bishop, then ruined it entirely by stealing the last of the dried apples from her pack.

* * *

*Ivo*

The woman learned fast.

Faster than the kings had, faster than the priests who had come to him in the long ago with their careful mouths and their fear-sweat smell, asking him to say things slowly, write them down, give them the words so they could own them. This one — Vesper, whose name tasted of iron and cold mornings — learned the way rain learns a hillside: finding every crack, running into all of them.

He liked teaching her. It was the first thing anyone had asked him for that wasn't a piece of him.

Tonight he gave her *solen*. *To carry together.* He chose it because she had carried him across the ford without being asked, grumbling the entire time, which seemed to be how she loved things — loudly, and while complaining.

When he spoke it, her eyes went somewhere else. They always did. Her breath changed, her hand drifted to the oath-scar on her forearm, and for a moment she was in another life, some memory pressed into the word itself like a flower in a book. He could feel them through the words when she touched them — echoes of everyone who had ever prayed in the old tongue. That was what the language *was*, though she hadn't understood it yet. Not a language. A congregation. Every word still held the shape of the mouths that had shaped it.

She came back to herself and frowned at the fire.

"I saw a wedding," she said, bewildered. "No — a harvest. Someone carrying grain on someone else's shoulders because their leg was broken. Why would a word show me that?"

Because that is what the word is *for*, he thought. Because a word is a room, and everyone who has ever spoken it left something behind.

But his voice was a dangerous thing now. He could feel the Hollow out there in the dark country beyond the hills, listening the way a spider listens to its web. So instead of saying it, he took her hand, opened it, and traced the shape of *solen* in her palm with one finger.

Vesper looked down at their hands for a long moment.

"Well," she said, gruffly, in the voice she used when feeling things too large for her face. "Don't tell anyone I'm learning prayers from a child who steals my rations. I have a reputation."

* * *

*Vesper*

By the fourth day she had eleven words.

She kept them written nowhere — writing felt wrong, felt like pinning moths — but they lived in her now, arranged in her mind like stones across a stream. *Aeth.* *Solen.* *Vireth* — the hush after snowfall. *Omma* — the act of setting a lamp in a window for someone not yet home. Each one came with its freight of borrowed memory: a funeral where mourners sang instead of wept; a mother teaching her daughter the word for *stay* while soldiers burned the fields behind them; a young man alone on a mountain, saying *thank you* to no one, meaning everything.

She stopped fighting it around dusk on the fifth day, somewhere in the high sheep country where the road forgot itself.

They sat sharing a heel of bread, and Ivo taught her *mira* — *to be glad that something exists* — and the memory that flooded her was so ordinary it broke her heart: a kitchen, generations dead, a woman laughing at nothing while she stirred a pot, and the whole warm room breathing the word *mira* like a sigh.

Vesper set down the bread. Her eyes were wet and she did not bother hiding it, because there was no one to hide it from except a boy who already knew everything worth knowing about her.

"It's prayer," she said. "That's what your language is. Every word of it. It isn't a tongue people spoke — it's a tongue people *prayed* in. That's why the Order forbade it. That's why they burned the records." She laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound. "Twenty years I guarded a silence, and I never once asked what we were silencing."

Ivo reached over and wiped her cheek with a sleeve already sticky with apple, which was possibly the least reverent gesture ever performed upon a weeping woman.

"I'm all right," she told him. "I'm fine. Eat your bread."

He ate her bread.

And there it was again — that fierce, unreasonable thing rising in her chest, the thing that had no name in either language. She had sworn oaths to gods and wardens and causes. None of them had ever felt like this: like a door she hadn't known was locked swinging open onto a room she'd been furnishing her whole life without realizing. If the king himself rode over that hill, she thought, I would put this child behind me and I would not move. Not for crown, not for church, not for the god he carries.

*Oh,* she thought, with distant alarm. *Oh no. I love him.*

It had happened sometime between the ford and the stolen apples, and there was nothing to be done about it now.

* * *

*Ivo*

On the sixth night she dreamed loudly enough that he woke.

Not sound — she was silent as ever, curled near the fire with her sword-hand even in sleep. But dreams had voices, and hers were shouting. He crept closer and listened the way you listen to weather.

She dreamed of the day of her oath. He could see it leaking out of her like light under a door: a girl of seventeen, hollowed by hunger and grief, kneeling on cold stone, promising her life to a god everyone said was dead because a dead god asks nothing and a girl with nothing has only herself to give. He watched the memory turn, sour, into other memories — ledgers, locked doors, the quiet way wardens spoke of *anomalies* — and the dream began to curdle toward nightmare.

So Ivo did the only thing he could. He leaned close and breathed one word into her sleeping ear:

*Solen.*

*To carry together.*

The dream changed. He felt it change — the nightmare loosening its grip, reshaping itself around the word until the girl on the cold stone was no longer alone in it, until there were arms around the memory that had never been there before. Vesper sighed in her sleep, deep and whole, and her hand found his hair and patted it twice, absently, the way mothers do.

He lay back down and looked at the stars.

Once, he had been very large. Once, prayers had washed over him like oceans, millions of voices, and he had held all of them, and it had been — he searched for the word — *heavy*. Holy, and heavy, and terribly lonely, because a god can hold every voice and touch no hand.

This was better. This small body, this cold hillside, this grumbling woman whose hand knew how to pat hair even asleep. He was learning mortal things one at a time: hunger, blisters, the specific joy of annoying someone who loves you.

He was almost asleep when he felt it.

Far off — miles, maybe more — a silence moved through the night. Not quiet; quiet is a kind of sound. This was the absence of sound, a hole walking, drinking the crickets out of the grass as it came. The Hollow. It had lost their trail at the river, but it was casting back and forth across the country like a hound, and every word he taught Vesper rang out of her like a bell.

He sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and made a decision that hurt worse than burning had.

Tomorrow, no more words.

* * *

*Vesper*

Something changed overnight, and by midmorning Vesper had figured out what: the boy had gone mute again. Not frightened-mute — deliberate-mute. When she asked him to repeat the word for *river*, he shook his head and pointed ahead, urgent, and would not meet her eyes.

"Ivo." She crouched in front of him on the track. "Look at me. What happened?"

He pressed his lips together. Then, slowly, he raised one hand and cupped it behind his ear. Listening. Then he pointed back the way they'd come, and made a gesture she'd seen him make only once before — fingers closing, crushing something invisible.

Taking sound. Taking voice.

Cold walked down Vesper's spine. She thought of the empty reliquary on the dead courier. Of Corvane's face when he'd ordered the boy confined. Of every story the old wardens told badly, drunk, and never finished: about the thing the founders had made, long ago, to keep the silence. About why the order's first law was not *suffer no magic* but *suffer no voice*.

"It hunts what you say," she whispered. "Every word I learn — every word you teach me — it can hear you through it. Through *me*."

Ivo's expression flickered — guilt, quickly mastered. He nodded.

Vesper stood up and looked back down the long green fold of the hills, at all that innocent distance, and felt the eleven words shift inside her like live coals. She should stop. She should never speak them again, should smother them now while they were still few.

Instead she found herself thinking of the mother in the burning field, teaching her daughter the word for *stay*.

They couldn't take this too. Whatever came up that road, it could not have this.

"All right," she said aloud, in plain honest common-tongue, because the Hollow could choke on it. "New rule. We speak less. We walk faster. And when we absolutely must—" she tapped her own lips, then his, "—we do it like this. Small. Close. Between us only."

Ivo considered this gravely. Then he reached up, took her scarred hand, and traced into her palm, slow and deliberate, a word she hadn't learned yet. Longer than the others. Older.

She frowned. "What's that one?"

He smiled — the first full, unguarded smile she'd seen from him, wide and crooked and entirely a little boy's smile — and pointed at her, then at himself, then at the road ahead, all the way to wherever it went.

Vesper looked down at the word drawn on her skin, unpronounced, unknown, and understood it anyway, the way you understand a thing you've known your whole life without having a name for it.

Whatever it meant, she'd carry it.

Behind them, far down in the valleys, the birds went suddenly, utterly quiet — all at once, all together, like a candle snuffed — and did not start singing again.
