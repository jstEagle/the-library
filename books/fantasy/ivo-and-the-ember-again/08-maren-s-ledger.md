## 8. Maren's Ledger

The morning after Vesper vanished, Maren Dunmore woke before the bell, as she had for twenty years, and lay in her narrow cell listening to the rain and doing arithmetic.

Not numbers. People. Who had seen what. Who would ask questions. Who could be trusted to lie badly enough that their lies would be believed, because a bad lie from a loyal sister was worth more than a good one from a stranger.

Vesper was gone. The boy was gone. The catacomb door stood ajar behind the reliquary shelf, and someone — Maren, at the third bell, alone, with a lamp hooded — had closed it again and swept the wet boot-prints into shadow. Now there was only the small matter of explaining to High Warden Corvane why his prisoner had evaporated like morning mist on the same night that Sister Maren, famously punctual, had somehow failed to make her rounds.

She dressed. She braided her grey-streaked hair with the care she gave to everything, because if you were going to hang for treason you might as well look composed about it. And she took down from beneath her cot the thing she had been building for eleven years: a ledger bound in oxblood leather, its pages soft as cloth from handling, filled edge to edge with her small, vicious, patient handwriting.

She called it, privately, *the truth of us*. Out loud, if anyone ever found it, it was an inventory of candle wax.

* * *

Breakfast in the refectory was barley porridge and dried apples, and conversation conducted entirely in the grammar of glances. The Order of the Quiet Flame did not forbid speech — that was a common misconception among the valley folk, who imagined the sisters and brothers going about mouthing silent blessings like fish. No, they spoke freely enough. They simply never said anything that mattered. Twenty years of practice had made Maren fluent in the other language, the one underneath: the tilt of a head, the pause before a name, the way Brother Aldous buttered his bread when he was frightened.

This morning, everyone buttered their bread like men expecting the building to catch fire.

"Did you hear?" whispered Sister Pell, sliding onto the bench beside her, all elbows and excitement. "The boy's gone. Gone! Corvane's been in the reliquary since matins. He came out white as tallow."

"Prisoners do escape," Maren said, stirring her porridge with the serenity of a woman who had absolutely no opinion on the matter. "It's practically a holy tradition. Didn't old Brother Fenwick escape twice?"

"Fenwick escaped *into* the wine cellar."

"A man knows where his sanctuary is." She ate another spoonful. Her hands were steady. She was quietly proud of her hands. "Eat your apples, Pell. Gossip on an empty stomach curdles."

Pell leaned closer. "They say the boy spoke. Before he went. That the ward-stones *hummed*."

Maren set down her spoon.

She had heard them too. Or rather, she had felt them — a vibration through the soles of her feet at some black hour, standing in the catacombs holding the door while Vesper and the child slipped into the rain, and the stones along the valley rim had sung one long low note like a struck bell that refused to finish. In twenty years of service the ward-stones had done nothing but sit there being rocks. Maren had personally polished them every spring solstice and had begun to suspect they were decorative.

"They say," Maren corrected gently, "a great many things. They said Brother Fenwick saw a vision of Ember in the wine cellar, and what he actually saw was the bottom of a cask."

But Pell had already moved on down the bench, carrying the story like a lit lantern, and Maren sat with her cooling porridge and felt the hum still in her bones, and understood — with the calm, terrible clarity that had been arriving in installments for eleven years — that everything was about to change, and that she intended to be holding the receipts when it did.

* * *

Corvane summoned her at midmorning.

His study smelled of ink and cold ash; he kept a brazier burning even in summer, though he never seemed warm. He sat behind his desk with the founding texts open before him — the *Testament of Silence*, the *Covenant of Ash* — and he looked at Maren the way a man looks at a wall he suspects of having ears.

"Sister Maren. You keep the household accounts."

"I do, High Warden."

"You also kept watch over the prisoner's corridor last night."

"I did."

"And yet the prisoner is not in it."

"No," Maren agreed. "He is not."

A silence. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, a brother dropped a stack of hymnals and swore in a whisper, which was very much the order's way of swearing.

Corvane's fingers drummed once, twice. "You were seen leaving the east stair at the second bell," he said. "Alone. Carrying nothing. Where did you go?"

"To check the storm-shutters in the scriptorium," Maren said. "The wind was from the north, and the north shutters stick. If you'd like, I can show you the ones that stick. There are four. I have named them."

Something flickered across the High Warden's face — not suspicion exactly, but the exhaustion of a man who had interrogated a hundred people and knew precisely how useless it was to interrogate the calm ones. Calm people either told the truth or had decided what the truth would cost, and neither kind broke under staring.

"The boy was an anomaly," Corvane said at last, quieter. "An irregularity in the silence. His escape is... regrettable, but the inquisitors arrive within the week regardless. When they do, Sister, I will need this house spotless. Every record accounted for. Every ledger balanced."

"Of course, High Warden."

"And Maren—" He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth the order permitted above the rank of Warden. "You have served faithfully a long time. Longer than most. Whatever you may find in the old records, in the course of tidying — remember that faith does not require us to read everything. Some pages are sealed for our protection."

"I understand completely," said Maren, who understood nothing of the kind, and whose ledger weighed against her ribs like a second heart.

* * *

That afternoon she began, formally, to destroy the evidence.

Or rather, to appear to begin destroying it, which required first reading all of it, which required carrying armloads of the chapter-house archives up to the courtyard bonfire one bundle at a time, past the eyes of half a dozen curious sisters, in full view of the gods and the weather.

The archives lived in a vault beneath the chapel, and Maren had held its key for eleven years — a duty so dull that no one had ever thought to envy her it. Ledgers of provisioning. Rosters of vigil rotations. Tithes received, candles burned, boots resoled. And threaded through the dullness, page after page after decade after decade, the things that were not dull at all.

She had found the first of them by accident, years ago, looking for a receipt. A margin note in a dead warden's hand: *Third tithe delivered. The child wept until the binding. Afterward, quiet.* She had assumed she misunderstood. She had gone back the next day, and the next, and read backward through the years until her knees ached on the vault floor, and she had not misunderstood.

*Tithes.* That was what the records called them. Children. Not orphans taken in — the order's public charity, the foundling baskets, the kindly sisters of the Hollow Valley — but children *delivered*. From villages beyond the stones, from families who owed debts, from anywhere a child had been born with something wrong in them: a word spoken in the old tongue, a flame that bent toward them, a shadow that fell the wrong way. Brought to the chapter-house. Bound. The binding described in careful clerical shorthand — *the rite performed, blood of the oath-taker mingled with the subject's, silence achieved* — and then a line in the columns, and then nothing. No death recorded. No departure. Just a name entered and never mentioned again, like a candle written off as spent.

There were forty-one names in the last hundred years. Maren had copied out every one.

And beneath the names, older, in a hand so faded it might have been carved rather than written, the thing she had not yet shown anyone, the thing she carried now the way Vesper presumably carried the boy: the *Founding Account*, the true one, not the Testament they read aloud each solstice. Three pages. A god who had walked the valley speaking mercy in a language before kingdoms. Founders who had feared what mercy without authority would cost them. A knife — the account was maddeningly vague about the knife, as though even the paper flinched — and a rite afterward, a great working, the silence laid over the valley like a lid, magic sewn out of the world, the god's voice stopped mid-word.

*So that the world might be governed by men,* the founder had written, *rather than haunted by gods.*

Haunted. As if love were a haunting. As if the boy who had hummed the ward-stones awake was a ghost instead of the opposite of one.

Maren fed another bundle to the fire — genuine rubbish, tallow inventories, a decades-old complaint about mice — and watched the sisters relax visibly as the smoke rose. Good. Let them see the past burning. Let them think the dangerous pages were ash. Meanwhile the oxblood ledger rode inside her habit, against her skin, growing heavier by the hour, though it had not gained an ounce.

Guilt, she had learned, transmutes. The alchemists had it backwards: base metal into gold was nothing. Guilt into purpose — that was the true art, and it required no circle drawn on the floor, only a woman willing to write things down.

* * *

Evening brought Sergeant Hale up from the gatehouse, rain-wet and unhappy, to report that the hounds had lost the scent at the river ford.

"Lost it," Corvane repeated, "or stopped following it?"

Hale's jaw worked. "Lost it, High Warden. River took it fair." He hesitated, and Maren, pouring the warden's wine from the sideboard, marked the hesitation the way she marked everything. "There's... talk among the men. About the boy. About whether—"

"Whether what, Sergeant?"

"Whether he ought to be found."

The silence that followed had texture. Maren poured without spilling, which under the circumstances she considered heroic.

"The king's inquisitors will settle the question of the boy," Corvane said finally, smoothly, in the voice he used for smoothing. "We are not judges here. We are keepers of the silence. Dismissed."

Hale went. Corvane stood at his window a long time, watching the rain come down over the valley where magic could not follow, and Maren watched him watching, and thought: *you're afraid of him. You've been afraid since the night the stones hummed. You know what's coming back.*

She cleared the wine things. At the door, Corvane spoke without turning.

"Maren. The sealed accounts. Have you read them?"

Her heart performed a single clean somersault and settled. "I dust them, High Warden. Reading is above my station."

"Mm." A pause. "Dust them carefully, then. Some pages burn faster than others."

"Yes, High Warden," said Maren, and went out into the corridor, and stood a moment in the dark with her hand pressed flat against the wall, breathing, before she let herself smile.

*Some pages burn faster than others.* He had meant it as a warning. She wrote it down that night, verbatim, in the ledger, dated and witnessed by no one, and beneath it she added the note she had been saving:

*He knows I know. He suspects nothing of what I've sent. Vesper — if you ever read this — the forty-one names are yours now. Say them somewhere loud.*

* * *

It was near midnight when she finished the evening's work: a copy of the Founding Account, three pages, hidden in the false bottom of the tallow inventory box that went down to the ferry-house every Thursday with the supply barge. The ferryman, old Doss, was deaf as a post, paid in candles, and devoted to Maren with the uncomplicated loyalty of a man who had once been a foundling himself and remembered which sisters had been kind. The box would travel to the market town. From the market town, she had arrangements. She had been making arrangements for eleven years, small and patient as a spider, and none of them had ever been needed until now.

She snuffed her lamp and sat in the dark with the ledger open on her knees, not writing, just resting her hand on it. Outside, the rain had stopped. The ward-stones were quiet again, but she fancied — she chose to fancy — that the quiet had changed quality. Less like a held breath. More like a room after the lid has been lifted off something.

Forty-one children. One murdered god. One boy running north through the dark with the only person in the order brave enough to be a traitor.

And one middle-aged sister with a key to the vault, a deaf ferryman, a box of candles, and a ledger that would outlive every stone in this house.

Maren blew out the last stub of light, and in the darkness she began, silently, in the old tongue she did not understand and had copied phonetically from a sealed page, to practice the first word of the prayer the founders had forbidden — shaping it clumsily, getting it wrong, getting it wrong again, and then, on the third try, feeling the syllable land somewhere beneath her breastbone like a coal dropped through a grate.

Down the corridor, faint as breath, the ward-stones hummed.

She froze. Listened. The hum faded — but not before she understood two things at once, both of them terrible, both of them wonderful.

First: the language worked. It worked for *her*, a woman with no gift, no brand, nothing but a stolen syllable and a guilty conscience.

Second: the sound had not come from outside the walls.

It had come from beneath the chapel — from the vault — from the sealed crypt where, according to every record she had ever catalogued, there was nothing at all.
