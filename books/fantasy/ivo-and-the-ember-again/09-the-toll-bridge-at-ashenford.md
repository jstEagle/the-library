## 9. The Toll Bridge at Ashenford

The river at Ashenford ran the color of old pewter, and it smelled like every river Vesper had ever crossed on her way to a fight — cold stone and wet iron and the faint green rot of things growing where they shouldn't. She stood at the edge of the toll bridge with Ivo's hand in hers and felt the particular dread of a woman recognizing a place she'd hoped never to see again.

"You're doing the face," Ivo said.

"What face?"

"The one where your mouth goes flat like you've swallowed something that disagreed with you."

"I don't have a face like that."

"You do. You had it when we ate the eel pie. You had it when the goat followed us for two miles. You have it now." He squinted past her at the bridge. "Is it because of the men?"

Vesper looked. Three figures at the far end of the span, wearing the dun-and-ash of the Order of the Quiet Flame, halberds grounded, a fourth man seated behind them on a barrel with the unhurried air of someone who had been waiting a long time and intended to enjoy it.

She knew all four of them.

"Because of the men," Ivo confirmed, reading her silence the way he read everything — with that unnerving, unchildlike patience, as though he were very old and merely wearing a boy the way other people wore coats.

"It's a toll bridge," Vesper said. "We'll pay the toll."

"The toll is two coppers."

"The toll is whatever they say it is."

* * *

They had come south along the fen road for six days, keeping to ditches and drover's paths, sleeping in hay barns and once — memorably — in a shrine to some minor harvest god whose statue had lost its head centuries ago and didn't seem to mind. Ivo had named the shrine's god in his own tongue, a soft syllable like water going over stones, and Vesper had felt the name settle into her chest like a coal dropped down a well. She was learning his language one word at a time, and each word cost her a memory that wasn't hers. She hadn't told him that. Some prices you pay quietly.

He'd also learned to lie about chores, which she counted as progress toward ordinary boyhood. He claimed he'd fed the farmer's dog at their last barn; he had not; the dog had bitten him and he'd decided the dog could starve philosophically. He was getting better at lying, too. Only his ears went pink.

Now his ears were pink, and his hand was tight around hers, and forty feet of wet oak planking separated them from four people Vesper had eaten beside, trained beside, bled beside, for the better part of twenty years.

"That's Hale," she said quietly. "On the barrel."

"The one who looks bored?"

"He isn't bored. He's patient. There's a difference, and it matters." She crouched, bringing them both level with the bridge rail, and made herself look properly. Sergeant Tomas Hale, third rank of the Quiet Flame, a man who once carried her four miles through a snowstorm with an arrow in his thigh and complained the entire way about her weight, which was nothing, which was the point of complaining. A good soldier. An honest one. That was the problem with honest soldiers: they did what they were told, thoroughly, and then they went home and slept well.

"Hale," she said again, tasting the name. "He'll have orders to take us alive. The inquisitors want the boy breathing until the pyre."

"And you?"

"I'm supposed to be brought back alive too." She paused. "Probably."

"'Probably' is not a comforting word, Vesper."

"No," she agreed. "It rarely is."

* * *

She walked them onto the bridge openly, because running would confirm everything, and because — though she hated herself for it — part of her wanted to see Hale's face when he saw hers. Wanted to know if there was anything left in it.

The three halberds came up in unison, a sound like a drawer closing. The fourth man unfolded from his barrel, and yes, it was Hale, older than she remembered or perhaps just tired, his beard going grey at the chin, his warden's badge polished to a shine that spoke of either devotion or insomnia.

"Well," Hale said. "Of all the bridges in all the valleys."

"Hale."

"Sister Halloran." He used her order-name deliberately, and she watched it land between them like a coin tossed on a table. "You've been a difficult woman to find. Six days. Do you know what Corvane does to search parties that come back empty? He doesn't shout. He prays at them. It's worse."

"I can imagine."

"You can't. Brannick cried. Brannick has never cried, including the time he sat on a hornet nest." Hale's eyes moved to Ivo, and something in them shifted — softened, then hardened over the softness like ice over water. "Hello, lad. You'll be Ivo."

Ivo said nothing. He had gone silent in the specific way he went silent when he was afraid — not mute with terror but quiet by choice, folding his voice away somewhere deep, and Vesper's heart clenched, because she knew now why he did that. Something hunted his voice. He'd told her in fragments, in dreams he mumbled into her shoulder: *a thing with no throat, that eats the words out of the air.* She hadn't seen it yet. She thought about it more than she let him see.

"He doesn't talk much," Vesper said.

"So I've heard. Doesn't need to, apparently." Hale gestured, and one of the halberdiers — young Pellwick, still pimpled, who'd once wept over a broken lute — stepped forward with a coil of rope and an expression of profound apology. "Orders are orders, sister. You come back to the chapter-house, the boy comes back to the chapter-house, everyone gets prayed at, and maybe — maybe — Corvane's in a merciful mood."

"Has he ever been in a merciful mood?"

"There was that time he forgave the baker."

"The baker gave him free rolls."

"Yes," Hale said. "That was the transaction."

And there it was — the terrible, familiar warmth of banter with a comrade, the muscle memory of twenty years of shared jokes rising in her chest even as her hand drifted toward the knife at her hip. That was the cruelty of it. These weren't monsters. They were her family, and families, she was learning, could hold knives to each other across a toll bridge on a grey morning and still remember each other's birthdays.

"Hale," she said. "Step aside."

"You know I can't."

"I'm asking."

"I know you are." And his face did the thing she'd dreaded: it grieved. Openly, briefly, before the discipline closed over it. "You broke oath, Ves. I don't care why. I'd probably care a great deal if you told me, and that's exactly why I can't let you tell me. Whatever story you've got, it ends with me choosing between my sworn family and my actual one, and I don't get to make that choice. None of us do. That's what the oath is *for*. It makes the choice so you don't have to carry it."

"I'm carrying plenty," Vesper said. "Would you like to see?"

"Ves—"

She moved.

Not at Hale. At Pellwick, because Pellwick was closest and youngest and would hesitate longest, and hesitation is the whole art of fighting people you love. She caught the halberd's haft below the blade, turned inside its arc, and put her shoulder into his chest — gently, as gently as you can put a shoulder into anyone while throwing them off a bridge, which is to say not very. Pellwick went over the rail with a yelp and hit the shallows with a splash that scattered a heron.

The second halberdier — Doss, broad, slow, decent — swung at her and pulled the swing wide at the last instant, because Doss had always pulled his swings, even in training, even when it cost him. She took the opening anyway. Elbow to the wrist, heel behind the knee, and Doss folded onto the planks wheezing, disarmed, alive, furious at himself for being both.

Hale came off the barrel fast, faster than a man his age should move, sword already clear. And this was different. This wasn't pulling swings. Hale fought the way honest men fight — straight in, no flourish, meaning every inch of it — and Vesper met him in the middle of the bridge and discovered that her body remembered everything her heart wished it could forget.

Steel rang. Twice, three times. He was better than her in a fair line-fight; she'd known that for a decade. So she cheated, because she'd learned that from him, actually, from a night in a tavern in Corvane's company when he'd whispered, laughing, *never fight fair against a friend, Ves, friends fight fair and that's how friends die.* She kicked the loose toll-board up off the planks into his eyes, and when he flinched — half a heartbeat, no more — her blade was at his throat and stopped there, trembling, kissing the skin without breaking it.

Silence, except the river and Pellwick hollering from the shallows that he was fine, mostly fine, the water was freezing, actually.

"Don't," Vesper breathed.

Hale stood very still. His eyes were on hers at close range now, closer than they'd been since the night she fled, and she watched him look — really look — at her face. At the exhaustion. At the fear. At the thing underneath the fear, which was not doubt about what she'd done but certainty, and which frightened him more than any blade.

"You mean it," he said quietly. "Whatever this is. You mean it."

"With everything I have."

"The boy. What is he?" His gaze flicked to Ivo, standing small and still at the bridge's edge, watching with those old, old eyes. "Corvane says anomalous. The inquisitors say abomination. You say—"

"I say he's a child," Vesper said. "Whoever wants more than that from him can come and take it from me."

Hale exhaled slowly, careful of the blade. "Pellwick. Doss. Nobody moves." Then, to her: "You're going to regret sparing us. You understand that. We'll report your position within the hour. They'll bring dogs. They'll bring worse than dogs."

"I know."

"Why spare us, then?" And there it was — the real question, raw under the soldier's calm. "Why not cut our throats and buy yourself a week? Any mercenary worth her salt—"

"Because you carried me four miles through the snow," Vesper said, "and complained the whole way, and then paid for my dinner and never once mentioned it again. Because Doss pulls his swings. Because Pellwick cries over lutes. Because if I start cutting throats of people I love, then Corvane wins, Hale. Not today, not on this bridge — but eventually, permanently, because he'll have made me into the thing the order says I am." She lowered the blade. "Take your mercy. It costs me more than you know."

For a long moment nobody moved. The river went on being a river.

Then Hale sheathed his sword, and the sound of it was louder than the whole fight had been.

"Go," he said. "South road, not the fen path — the fen path floods past the mill and you'll lose a day. And Ves—" His jaw worked. "When they ask me your face, I'll describe it. That's my job. But I'll describe it wrong. I'll tell them you looked hard. Cruel, even. I'll tell them there was nothing left of the woman we knew." He held her eyes. "That's the only mercy I've got room for, and it's a poor one, and I hate it."

"It's not poor," Vesper said. Her throat was tight. "It's the kindest lie anyone's ever told me."

"Get off my bridge," Hale said gruffly, "before I change my mind and arrest myself."

* * *

They went south, and Vesper did not look back, because looking back was how you died on bridges, and also because she was fairly sure her face was doing something undignified.

Ivo waited until the bridge was out of sight, and the road bent into alder shade, and then he slipped his hand into hers.

"You let them go," he said.

"Yes."

"They'll tell the others where we are."

"Yes."

"That was stupid."

"Deeply," she agreed. "It was the stupidest thing I've done since I stole you out of a fortress full of armed zealots through a hole meant for corpses."

"But you'd do it again."

She considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and because she was still, in her bones, deciding. "Yes," she said at last. "Every time. That's the trouble with mercy, little ember. It doesn't get cheaper with practice. It gets more expensive. You just learn to afford it."

Ivo nodded slowly, filing this away in whatever vast, strange archive he kept behind his small face. Then: "Your mouth was doing the flat thing again. During the fight."

"Fights are serious."

"You were winning. Your mouth did the flat thing right before you kicked the board at Sergeant Hale's face. I think—" and here he smiled, quick and bright and boyish, and it undid her completely, as it always did, "—I think it's your happy face."

"It is absolutely not my happy face."

"It is. You swallow something that disagrees with you, and then you win."

Ahead, the south road ran long and empty through the alders, and the afternoon light came down thin and silver, and for perhaps half a mile Vesper allowed herself to believe they might simply walk out of this story into some quieter one — a cottage, a garden, a boy learning to fish.

Then Ivo stopped walking.

His hand went rigid in hers. His head tilted, listening, the way he listened to things that made no sound — and Vesper felt it too, a moment later, the wrongness arriving like weather. The birds. The alder leaves. The whole afternoon's small industry of noise, thinning.

Going quiet.

Not silence the way night is silent. Silence the way a throat is silent when something is swallowing it.

"Vesper," Ivo whispered, and his whisper shook. "It found my voice. It's been following the words I taught you." His old eyes lifted to hers, wide with a fear older than kingdoms. "It knows the language now. And it's coming to take it back."
