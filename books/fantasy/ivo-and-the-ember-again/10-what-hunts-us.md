## 10. What Hunts Us

The boy who was not a boy had a name now. Vesper had given it to him on the second night of the road, crouched beside a cookfire with her hands cupped around the flame like she was holding something small and alive.

"Ivo," she'd said. "It means *gift*, in the old tongue. Or near enough. My grandmother used it for babies born after hard winters."

*Ivo.* He turned it over inside himself the way you turn a river stone in your pocket. It fit badly at first — too small, too human, too much like a coat with sleeves that ended above his wrists — but coats can be grown into. He decided he would grow into it.

He did not tell her that he already knew what the word meant. He did not tell her that he knew what every word meant, that language was less a thing he spoke than a thing he *was*, and that when she said *gift* he felt it land somewhere behind his ribs where a heart should be, ringing like a bell struck in an empty church.

There were many things Ivo did not tell Vesper Halloran. This is the story of the one that mattered most.

* * *

They were three days out of Ashenford, walking the spine of a wooded ridge that the maps called the Shepherd's Back and that Vesper called "miserable, rocky, and uphill both ways," which Ivo had learned was a joke, because her mouth did the thing it did — the corner tugging up even when her eyes stayed busy watching the treeline.

Ivo liked her jokes. He was collecting them. He kept them in the same place he kept the words she taught him, though of course he didn't need to keep the words; they were already his, all of them, every tongue that had ever been spoken under this sky. But the jokes were new. The jokes were hers. Nobody had ever made jokes in front of him before, or if they had, it had been so long ago that the memory of it was worn smooth as sea glass, and he could no longer tell if it had happened to him or to someone else entirely.

That was the trouble with being very old inside something very young. You couldn't always find the edges of yourself.

So: the ridge. The morning. Vesper ahead of him with her hand resting easy on her sword hilt, the way other people rested a hand on a dog's back. And Ivo trailing half a step behind, because that was their arrangement — she watched the world, and he watched her watch it, and between the two of them they managed to be almost safe.

Except that morning, Ivo stopped watching Vesper.

Because he heard it.

Not with his ears. His ears heard wind in the pines, a woodpecker knocking politely somewhere upslope, Vesper's boots on scree. All ordinary. All fine.

But underneath the hearing, in the place where he kept the old words — the true ones, the ones from before kingdoms, before names, before anyone had thought to build a wall around a valley and call the emptiness holy — underneath all that, something went *quiet*.

And Ivo knew quiet the way fish know water.

This was not the good kind. This was not the silence of snowfall, or of Vesper humming through her nose while she braided her hair, or of the moment just before dawn when the whole world holds its breath. This was a silence with an appetite. A silence shaped like a mouth.

He had felt it once before. He remembered — dimly, terribly, the way you remember a fever — the night the ward-stones had hummed. The night he had stood dripping on Vesper's doorstep and spoken the first word of himself into the rain. Something on the far side of the valley had lifted its head.

Now it had crossed the stones.

"Vesper," Ivo said.

She turned at once. She always turned at once; it was one of the things about her that made his chest ache in a way he had no word for, though he had several thousand words and could have invented one. "What is it? Your feet again?"

"My feet are fine." Mostly true. His feet were always fine. It was everything attached to them that was the problem. "Something's following us."

Vesper's face did the soldier thing — went smooth and unreadable, all the warmth folded away and stored somewhere safe. "Inquisitors?"

"No." He shook his head. How to explain? He looked down at his own hands, small and chapped and absurdly mortal, and tried to find the shape of the knowing. "It's not people. People make noise even when they're hiding. Breathing. Boots. The little sounds of being afraid. This doesn't." He swallowed. "This is the opposite of noise. It eats it."

Vesper crouched to his level, which she did when she wanted him to know she was listening with her whole self and not just the part that was a mercenary. "Eat it how?"

"You know how the order says prayer is forbidden?" Ivo said. "How speaking to the god is the worst sin there is?"

"They say a lot of things."

"Well." His voice came out smaller than he meant it to. "Somebody taught the silence to hunt."

* * *

Here is what Ivo knew, in the way he knew things — not learned, but remembered, the knowledge sitting in him like sediment at the bottom of a well:

Long ago — not long ago in years, but long ago in *depths* — the men who killed Ember had found themselves with a problem. A god does not die cleanly. A god leaves echoes. Every whispered prayer, every frightened child's plea, every old woman's thanks over bread: each one rang against the place where Ember had been, and rang, and rang, and would not stop.

So the founders had done what clever, wicked men do. They had taken the echo and given it a body. Not flesh — nothing so honest as flesh. They had woven it out of enforced silence itself, out of every prayer strangled in the throat, every hymn left unsung, every name scratched off a stone. They fed it on the quiet they made, and set it walking, and gave it one law:

*Nothing may speak to the dead god.*

For two hundred years it had patrolled the Hollow Valley and the lands beyond, a hole in the shape of a servant, drinking down any voice that reached toward the ember. The order called it nothing at all, because naming it would have been a courtesy, and the order did not extend courtesies to its own sins.

The rank-and-file didn't know it existed. Corvane knew. Whoever held the High Warden's chair had always known. It was the family dog.

And now it smelled Ivo's voice on the wind — the voice of the thing it had been built to bury — and it was coming.

* * *

By midday, Ivo could feel it more than hear it, the way you feel a storm in your teeth. It moved wrong through the forest. Birds went silent in patches, whole stretches of the ridge falling mute as it passed beneath, and then the birdsong resumed behind it, tentative, like conversation restarting after an awkward guest has left the room.

"What does it want?" Vesper asked. She hadn't questioned his knowing. That was another thing about her — she believed him first and asked questions second, which was either excellent soldiering or terrible sense, and Ivo loved her for it either way.

"Me," Ivo said. "My voice. It wants my voice."

"It can't have it." Flat. Immediate. No discussion offered.

"That's the problem." Ivo hugged his arms around himself as they walked. "Vesper, listen. When I talk — really talk, in the old tongue — it's like ringing a dinner bell. Everything hears it. Inquisitors. Wardens. *It.* As long as I speak, it knows exactly where I am. And it's faster than us. It doesn't sleep. It doesn't eat. It just—" he searched for the word and hated the one he found— "*hungers.*"

"So we fight it."

"With what?" He almost laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "You can't cut a silence, Vesper. Steel goes right through it and comes out quieter on the other side. It isn't *made* of anything. That's the whole horror of it. It's made of nothing, and nothing is very patient."

Vesper walked in silence herself for a while — the good kind, the thinking kind. The light came down through the pines in long dusty columns, and Ivo watched her face work through three different plans and reject all of them.

"Then we don't speak," she said finally. "We go quiet until we lose it, or reach somewhere it can't follow. Can it cross running water? Wards? Tell me its rules."

"There aren't rules. There's just the law it was given." Ivo kicked a pinecone off the path, viciously, a very human gesture that surprised him with how good it felt. "One law. One. And I'm standing on the wrong side of it."

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow beneath a fallen oak, a green cave of moss and shadow. Vesper built no fire. She shared out cold bread and dried apples, and Ivo ate his slowly, and neither of them talked, and the not-talking was already different from before — heavier, deliberate, a wall they were building together instead of a rest between words.

Ivo lay on his back and listened to the forest.

The woodpecker had gone. So had the wind, or the wind had gone careful. Somewhere downslope — a mile, maybe two, distance meaning nothing to a thing like that — the silence sat and waited, the way a spider waits, the way winter waits. Patient. Certain. It had waited two hundred years. It could wait one more night.

*It knows you're close,* Ivo thought at it, and then caught himself, horrified. Even thinking toward it felt like striking a match in a dry field. He pulled his thoughts back and wrapped them around the safest thing he owned: Vesper's jokes. *Miserable, rocky, and uphill both ways.*

Sleep took him eventually, and dreams took him after that, and the dreams were the usual museum of other people's memories — a harvest festival four hundred years dead, someone's grandmother singing over a loom, the terrible bright moment of his own burning — but tonight the museum had a visitor. Something moved through the galleries of his sleep, and wherever it moved, the exhibits went dark.

He woke before dawn with his heart slamming and Vesper's hand already on his shoulder.

"You cried out," she whispered. "Just now. Small sound. Did it hear?"

Ivo lay still, listening with everything he had.

Far off, downslope, the silence *shifted*. Turned. Began to move.

"Yes," he breathed.

* * *

They ran.

Not far — Vesper wouldn't let him run flat-out, said a chased child who arrives exhausted is a caught child — but they ran along the ridge crest through the grey beginning of morning, and behind them the mute patches in the forest climbed steadily upward, unhurried, inevitable, a tide made of nothing.

And Ivo, gasping, stumbling, holding Vesper's hand so hard his fingers ached, understood something with terrible clarity.

It wasn't tracking his scent. It wasn't tracking his heat or his trail or his footprints. It was tracking his *voice* — the echo of it, the residue, the way a bell keeps trembling faintly long after it's been struck. Every word he'd ever spoken aloud since crossing the ward-stones had left a thread hanging in the air, and the Hollow was reeling those threads in, one by one, patient as a fisherman.

Which meant there was exactly one way to cut the line.

He stopped running.

Vesper lurched to a halt beside him, sword half-drawn, scanning the trees. "What? What is it?"

Ivo looked up at her. At the woman who had opened her door to a soaked, mute stranger and never once asked for anything back except that he eat his vegetables, which he resented, and learn to whistle, which he was failing at. At the only person in two hundred years of existence who had ever treated him as a *boy first* and a god a distant, irrelevant second.

If he told her what he was about to do, she would refuse. She would stand in front of him with her sword against a creature that ate sound, and she would die loudly, and it would be his fault.

So he didn't tell her.

He did the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than burning, harder than being born. He gathered up his voice — the old voice, the true one, the one that had once shaken mountains loose from their roots — and he folded it away. Down past the bottom of himself. Into the deep vault where the ember slept, and he shut the door, and he locked it, and he swallowed the key.

No more words. Not in any tongue. Not even the small human ones. Not even *Vesper*.

Her lips were moving — asking him something, urgent, her eyes frightened in a way he'd never seen — and he looked at her and shook his head once, slowly, and pointed at his own mouth, and then at the forest below, and hoped she was smart enough, because she was smart enough, she was the smartest person he knew—

Understanding broke across her face like pain.

"*No,*" she whispered. "Ivo, no, you don't have to—"

He smiled at her. It was getting easier, smiling. He put her hand flat against his own chest, over the place where the bell rang, and pressed it there twice: *stay quiet. Stay safe.* Then he stepped behind her, small hand fisted in the back of her coat, and went silent the way the dead are silent, the way the god had been silent for two hundred years, completely and on purpose and forever if it came to that.

Below them, the last patches of birdsong guttered out.

The Hollow began to climb.

* * *

It passed beneath them at midmorning.

Ivo felt every footstep of it, though it made none. It came up the slope like a change in pressure, like the moment before lightning, and the air grew thick and drinkless and old. Vesper stood over him with her sword drawn and her breath held, and Ivo pressed his face into her back and held the door of himself shut with both hands, and did not speak, and did not speak, and did not speak.

It paused directly below their hollow. Ten yards. Five.

It had no face. He knew that without looking. It had no face and no body and no hunger that food could touch, and it was listening so hard that the listening itself had weight, pressing down through the moss and the roots and the bones of the hill, searching for one small voice.

Ivo thought about Vesper's jokes. About bread and dried apples. About the word *gift*, and growing into the coat.

The silence hung there a long, long moment — a fisherman feeling along a line that had gone suddenly, perfectly slack.

Then it moved on, northward, following the fading ghost of a child's cry from a dream hours old and miles back, and the birdsong returned behind it in a slow, cautious wave, and Ivo discovered he had been holding his breath so long that the world went sparkly at the edges.

Vesper turned and gathered him up — actually picked him up, which she hadn't done since the first week, and which he was nearly too big for and did not care — and held him against her shoulder, and he felt her heartbeat going like a hammer, and realized hers wasn't the only one.

They stayed that way until the shaking stopped. Hers and his. Both.

* * *

They walked the rest of the day without a single word, and it should have been lonely, and it wasn't. Vesper had always talked with her hands anyway — pointing out game trails, miming *eat*, miming *sleep*, once miming, elaborately and with great dignity, *if you fall in the river I am not coming in after you*. Ivo answered in the same coin. By evening they had developed a whole fluent language of glances and gestures, and Ivo thought: *this is what she meant. Love outside the law. We're inventing it as we go.*

Near dusk, the ridge dropped toward a vast grey sprawl of fenland, mist lying on it like a blanket pulled up to its chin. Far out in the wet dark, a single light burned — small, stubborn, greenish, wrong for a lamp and right for something else.

Vesper studied it a long time. Her jaw set in the way it did before decisions.

"Hedge-witch lives out that way," she murmured, so quietly it was barely breath. "Old stories say she was cast out of the order before I was born. If anyone knows what's hunting you..." She glanced down at him, and her hand found his and squeezed once. "Can you stay silent a while longer, Ivo? Just until we know?"

He nodded. Easily. Happily, even.

Because here was the secret he kept even now, grinning up at her in the failing light: silence didn't frighten him anymore. Silence was just a door, and doors open. And besides — for the first time since the fire, since the falling, since the long terrible centuries of being everyone's echo and no one's child — Ivo knew exactly who he was.

He was the boy who had kept the monster from taking his mother.

Out in the fen, the green light flickered — and then, distinctly, deliberately, it blinked. Twice. Like something that had been waiting for them.

Like something that already knew their names.
