## 11. The Witch of the Fen Road

The fen road was less a road than a rumor of one — a spine of packed peat winding through reeds taller than Ivo, black water glinting between the tussocks like the eyes of something patient. By midday my boots had surrendered any pretense of being dry, and by late afternoon Ivo had named every frog we startled, in his own tongue, with great ceremony.

"That one," he announced, pointing at a fat brown shape that had already vanished into the mire, "is called *Vess-ko-marrin*. It means *small angry lord*."

"Does it," I said.

"He told me himself." Ivo nodded solemnly, the way he did when he was being entirely serious and entirely seven years old at once. "Frogs are honest. They don't pretend to be bigger than they are. Unlike kings."

I decided not to ask how he knew what kings were like. There were whole territories in that boy's head I had learned not to walk into uninvited.

We had been on the fen road two days since leaving the shrine at Marrow's Cross, where a kindly lay-brother had fed us barley soup and asked no questions — the last kindness we'd meet before the country turned to water. The plan, such as it was, lived in a scrap of memory I'd carried for fifteen years without ever expecting to use it: *if you're ever running, go to the witch on the fen road.* Maren had said it once, laughing, over bad wine. *Old Sable. Exiled for healing someone she wasn't supposed to. She owes me for a favor neither of us will name.*

Maren had let us go through the catacombs at terrible cost to herself. If her favor bought us one night under a roof, I intended to spend it.

The house appeared when the light went low and copper-colored — a crooked thing of turf and driftwood perched on the one dry hummock for a mile in any direction, chimney smoking, shutters hung with drying herbs that smelled sharp enough to sting the eyes. A goat regarded us from the woodpile with an expression I can only describe as professional skepticism.

Ivo stopped dead in the path. His hand found mine.

"She knows we're here," he whispered. "She knew yesterday."

"Then knocking would be polite," I said, and knocked.

The door opened before my knuckles landed the second time.

Sable was smaller than I'd imagined — a compact woman somewhere past sixty, wrapped in three shawls despite the mild evening, with iron-gray hair braided tight to her skull and eyes the color of marsh water, which is to say they looked shallow until you realized you couldn't see the bottom. She looked at me. She looked at Ivo. Her face did something complicated and then settled on resigned irritation, which I suspect was its resting state.

"Well," she said. "Maren's favor. Of course it's *this*."

"You know why we've come?"

"I know what's standing on my path, girl, and that's better than knowing why." She stepped aside. "In. Wipe your feet. If you track fen onto my floor I'll turn you both into herons, and herons have miserable lives."

* * *

Inside, the house was warm and cluttered past all sense — bundles hanging from every beam, jars in ranks on shelves that climbed to the rafters, a kettle that seemed to boil itself, though I caught, later, the small charm stitched into its handle. Sable sat Ivo down by the fire, put a bowl of stew in front of him without asking whether he wanted it (he did; he always did), and only then turned to me with her arms crossed.

"Now. Tell me who's hunting you, so I know whether to bar the door or bury it."

"King's inquisitors," I said. "And worse. My own order. The Order of the Quiet Flame."

Something moved behind her eyes at that. Not surprise. Recognition, old and sour, like a smell remembered from childhood.

"And the boy?"

"A foundling," I said, which was true, in the way a matchbox is a house.

Sable snorted. She crossed the room, crouched in front of Ivo, and studied him the way a farrier studies a hoof — thoroughly, and without apology. He endured it, chewing. Then she took his chin gently in one calloused hand and turned his face toward the firelight, toward the brand beneath his jaw that matched the scar on my wrist, the mark I had borne as an oath and he had been born wearing.

Ivo looked back at her and said, politely, "*Sen-vahel*, mistress. It means *good evening, and may your hearth never want*."

The bowl in Sable's other hand trembled. Just once. Just enough.

She set it down very carefully, stood, walked to the window, and stood there with her back to us for the space of ten heartbeats. When she turned around, her face had gone pale under the weathering, and her voice came out flat and quiet.

"How much of it does he speak? That language."

"I'm learning it from him. Word by word. I don't — I don't fully understand what it is yet. Only that the ward-stones sang when he first spoke it, and no stone has sung in centuries, and everything in me that took oaths has been screaming ever since."

"Mm." She poured three cups of something amber and medicinal-smelling, gave Ivo his with a suspicious amount of honey in it, and lowered herself into the chair opposite mine. "You want the truth, mercenary? Truly? Because I can give you a comfortable lie instead — I keep several in stock, very reasonably priced — and you could sleep tonight."

"I've had enough comfortable lies," I said. "My whole life was furnished with them."

"Good answer. Bad news." She drank, grimaced at her own brewing, and set the cup down. "That language predates kingdoms. Predates the crown, the church, the order you bled for. It's older than the idea of *asking* — because it was never meant for asking. It was meant for *speaking to*. Prayer, girl. The real thing, before prayer got domesticated into kneeling and hoping. Every word of it carries weight, because it was built to be heard by something that answered."

The fire ticked. Outside, the frogs resumed their small angry politics.

"The god Ember," Sable said, "spoke that tongue the way you breathe. And the ones who spoke *to* him — the first wardens, the first witches, the first healers — learned it the way children learn, by living inside it. When Ember was silenced — and he *was* silenced, whatever your chapter-house tells itself — the language didn't die. It went to sleep. Scattered. Sleeping words in sleeping mouths, waiting." She looked at Ivo, who had gone still over his stew, watching us with those eyes too old for the face around them. "And now here's a child who doesn't know he's speaking. Who's teaching it word by word to a sworn killer of your order. Do you see what you're carrying, Vesper Halloran?"

"Tell me plainly," I said. "I've been guessing for weeks and guessing is eating me alive."

"Plainly, then." She leaned forward. "The boy is not marked *by* Ember. He is not blessed *by* Ember. He is Ember. What's left of him. The last ember of the fire your order was founded to guard and murdered besides — pressed down into mortal flesh, folded small, hidden in a child, so that nothing hunting it would think to look for anything so breakable."

The room did not spin. I remember being disappointed that it didn't; spinning would at least have been honest. Instead everything went very clear and very still, the way it does in the moment before a blade lands.

"He talks in his sleep," I heard myself say. Foolish, irrelevant — and the truest thing I could find. "He dreams out loud. Whole sentences. I write them down."

"Of course he does." Sable's voice gentled, which frightened me more than the flatness had. "A god dreaming inside a boy. And every word he speaks wakes him a little more, and wakes the world a little more with him. Magic, Vesper. Real magic, the kind that hasn't walked openly since the founders of your order put their knife in their god's back and called it peace. If the ember wakes fully — if the boy burns through and becomes what he was — the world tips back to what it was before. Wards and wonders. And war. The old war. Kingdom against wonder, wonder against kingdom, and everyone in between burned for fuel. That's why they'll kill him. Not because he's dangerous to the crown. Because he's dangerous to *everyone*, and fear always dresses itself up as mercy."

Ivo set down his spoon. He looked at Sable, then at me, and there was nothing godlike in his face at all — just a small boy working very hard not to cry in front of strangers.

"So I'm the fire," he said. "The bad kind."

"No," I said, faster than thought, kneeling beside him. "No. Listen to me. You're Ivo. You're the boy who names frogs and hides peas under his plate and taught me the word for *stay*. Whatever else is folded up inside you, whoever else — you get to choose what wakes. Nobody gets to burn you for what you might become. Not the crown. Not the order. Not even the god."

He put his forehead against my shoulder and stayed there, breathing, until his breathing evened out. Over his head, Sable watched me with an expression I couldn't read — pity, maybe, or recognition, or the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

* * *

Later, when Ivo slept in the box bed under a quilt that smelled of lavender and woodsmoke, Sable and I sat by the low fire with the last of the honeyed spirit, and she talked, and I let her, because some truths need to arrive slowly or they crush the ribs.

"I was exiled for healing," she said. "Did Maren tell you? A warden's daughter, fevered past praying. The order forbade hedge-craft inside the valley — said the god's silence had to be kept clean. I healed the girl anyway. They burned my cottage, salted my garden, and put me out beyond the stones. So I know exactly what your order is worth, and I know exactly what it costs to be thrown away by people you loved." She swirled her cup. "But I'll tell you something I've had forty years of exile to learn, mercenary. The silence was never peace. It was a held breath. Six hundred years, the whole world holding its breath, and everyone pretending that's the same as resting."

"And if he wakes?" My voice came out steady. I was almost proud of it. "If the ember rises. Is it war, truly? Or is that what the order needs me to believe so I'll do their killing for them?"

Sable was quiet a long time. The firelight moved in the jars on the shelves; dried things turned slowly on their strings, shadows wheeling like slow birds.

"Honestly?" she said at last. "I don't know. No one alive knows — the last time magic woke freely, none of our grandmothers' grandmothers had been born. Maybe it's the old war again. Maybe it's something new, something kinder, that nobody's brave enough to imagine because imagining costs nothing and being wrong costs everything." She shrugged, and the shawls shifted. "What I know is this: the boy in my bed chose to go hungry two days rather than steal bread from a shrine box. Whatever's folded inside him, the part of him sitting on top right now is *good*, and good is not a small thing to bet on."

I looked into the fire until my eyes stung.

"When they catch us," I said, "they won't trial him. They'll burn him and call it a blessing, and my sworn sisters will sing the quiet hymns while he screams, and they'll mean every note. I've seen Corvane's face. He believes it's mercy. That's the worst of it — you can't fight a man's sincerity with a sword."

"No," Sable agreed. "You fight it with the truth, and the truth has to come out of your mouth at the worst possible moment, in front of everyone, when your knees are shaking." She smiled thinly. "I've done it once. Cost me everything but my life. Worth it."

Outside, full dark had come down over the fen. The frogs had gone silent — all at once, the way birds go quiet before a hawk — and I felt it in my teeth before I understood it in my head: a wrongness in the air, a pressure like the moment before lightning, a hush that wasn't peaceful but *enforced*.

Sable's head came up sharply. The self-boiling kettle shuddered and went cold.

"Girl," she said quietly. "How long since the boy spoke above a whisper?"

"Since supper. Why—"

"Because sound doesn't just leave this fen," she said, rising, snuffing candles with a pinch of her fingers, moving fast and silent through her own cluttered dark. "Sound gets *taken*. Something's out there on the road, walking the wet, and it eats voices, and it's been following the taste of that boy's mouth since Marrow's Cross. Your order made it, long ago, to keep the silence — a hound for hush. And hounds, mercenary, always come home hungry."

From the box bed, in his sleep, Ivo whimpered — and the whimper sounded strangely thin, strangely far away, as if it had to travel farther than the width of a small room to reach my ears.

Sable blew out the last candle.

"Don't wake him," she breathed. "And whatever you do — don't let him speak."
