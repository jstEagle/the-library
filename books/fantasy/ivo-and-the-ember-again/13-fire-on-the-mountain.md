## 13. Fire on the Mountain

The pass had a name once. Something lyrical, something the shepherds used before the order mapped it and gave it a number instead. Ivo had tried to tell me what the old word meant on our second day climbing, and had gotten so tangled in his own explanation that he'd ended up lying flat on his back in the scree, laughing at the sky.

"It means *the place where the wind forgets*," he'd said finally.

"And how do you know that?"

"Because you told me. In a dream." He'd shrugged, the way children shrug when they've said something enormous and don't notice. "You dreamed it four hundred years ago."

I had decided not to think about that, and I had been successfully not thinking about it for two days, which was a personal record.

Now, on the third morning, I crouched behind a shelf of granite and thought about very little else, because there were torches in the valley below us — six of them, moving in the disciplined stagger of soldiers who knew these paths, and one of them walked like Sergeant Hale.

I knew that walk. Twenty years of watching men march beside me will teach you their gaits the way a mother learns footsteps on stairs. Hale walked with his weight back, careful of his bad knee from the winter campaign at Marrow Ford, and he was walking toward us at the head of five others, and I had let him live at Ashenford because sparing him had felt like mercy at the time.

Mercy, it turns out, has excellent eyesight.

"Vesper." Ivo's whisper came from under the rock overhang where I'd tucked him. He'd gotten good at being small and quiet, which broke my heart in a way I didn't have time to examine. "Is it them?"

"It's soldiers."

"Soldiers are people."

"So are I." I checked the knife at my hip, then the second one at my ankle, then wished briefly and sincerely for a third. "Stay down. Stay quiet. If I say run, you run east along the ridge until you find the shrine with the broken bell, and you wait inside it. Can you remember that?"

"The broken bell." He nodded solemnly. Then, because he was still somehow a boy: "Can I ring it?"

"No."

"Not even a little?"

"Ivo."

"Fine. The unringable bell. This is the worst hiding place plan ever devised."

*And yet,* I thought, *you're the only person in the kingdom who could find it.* Because the shrine with the broken bell wasn't on any map. It existed in a prayer — one of the fragments he'd taught me, a line about mourning that never ends — and only someone fluent in a dead god's language could walk straight to it. My dreams had been full of it for three nights now: a stone roof, a bell with its tongue torn out, snow drifting through an empty doorway. His memories, leaking into mine through the words. I was dreaming another soul's geography, and some treacherous part of me had started to call it *home*.

The torches were closer. I counted breaths instead of heartbeats — old habit, counting anything but your own fear — and waited for the column to reach the bend where the trail narrowed between two boulders. That was where I'd take them. One at a time, quietly, if the gods of narrow places were kind.

They were not kind. They never had been, particularly to me.

Because just as the first torch rounded the bend, the scree above us shifted.

Not much. A pebble. A single pebble, knocked loose by Ivo shifting his weight, or by the mountain's own indifferent humor, and it went ticking down the slope with all the subtlety of a town crier.

The torches stopped.

"Hold!" Hale's voice, carrying up the rocks. "Movement, high left. Lamps up!"

So much for the gods of narrow places. I moved.

I came off the shelf fast and low, took the first man at the bend before he'd finished turning — pommel to the temple, gentle as such things go, he'd wake with a headache and a story — and shoved his torch into the snow. The second got a boot to the knee and my forearm across his throat, and we went down together onto the frozen trail, and I had the knife out and at his jaw before either of us had properly decided what was happening.

"Stop," I said, to him, to all of them, to the world in general.

Hale stood ten feet away, sword drawn, lamp held high, and his face in the yellow light did something complicated. Recognition first. Then anger. Then something worse than both, which was doubt.

"Dunmore." He didn't lower the blade. Neither did I. "Sixteen years I served beside you. You fed me soup when I had fever at Marrow Ford. You carried Denny's body nine miles so his mother could bury him whole." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated himself for it, and I understood that completely. "And now you're kneeling on Tam with a blade to his throat, over — what? A child?"

"Yes."

"That's it? Just — yes?"

"He's worth it."

"You don't even know what he *is*, Ves. The inquisitors—"

"The inquisitors want to burn a seven-year-old boy alive in front of a crowd and call it a blessing." I kept my voice level. It cost more than the fighting would have. "I know exactly what he is. Do you?"

Tam shifted under me. Behind Hale, the other three had fanned out, blades bare, and I ran the arithmetic I always ran and didn't like any of its answers. Four against one, with Ivo behind me and no retreat except a cliff face that had opinions about climbers.

"Last chance, Dunmore," Hale said, and there was pleading in it, which meant there was hope in it, which meant I hadn't lost him yet. "Put it down. Come back. Corvane will — whatever they've told you happened, there's a version where you survive this."

"There's a version where *he* survives this," I said. "That's the only one I'm shopping for."

And then the mountain answered.

*

I have thought, since, about how to describe what happened next, and every description fails, so I'll give you the failed ones in order and let you assemble the truth from the wreckage.

First version: Ivo screamed.

He didn't mean to. He had been holding perfectly still behind the overhang, obedient as a soldier, and then Tam's elbow had driven back into my ribs and my blade had slipped a half-inch and drawn blood — a thin red line on the boy's throat, nothing, a scratch — and Ivo saw it, and something in him came apart.

The sound he made was not a child's cry. It was older. It was the sound the word for *grief* makes when grief is four centuries deep, and it came out of him in that pre-kingdom tongue that has no business living in a human throat, and the syllables went up the pass like birds going home.

Second version: the mountain heard him.

The snow stopped falling. Not gradually — it simply paused, each flake hanging in the air like a held breath, and in that terrible beautiful silence I felt the ward-stones of the Hollow Valley hum in my memory, felt every border stone I'd ever patrolled ring like a struck glass, and understood in one white-hot instant that the silence we enforced was never the world's natural state. It was a bandage. And Ivo's voice had just pulled the edge of it loose.

Third version, the truest one: everything woke up a little.

The frozen waterfall across the valley — dry as bone for as long as anyone could remember — groaned, and water moved inside it, one dark pulse, like a heartbeat remembered by stone. The lichen on the boulders glowed faintly green, the way I'd seen it glow exactly once before, in a fresco in the chapter-house crypt that the warden's called *decoration*. The hanging snowflakes began to fall again, upward, gently, for the length of one astonished breath.

And the fire. Hale's lamp. The flame leaned out of its glass housing toward Ivo like a plant toward sun, stretching, straining, turning gold-white, and when Hale flinched and dropped it, the flame did not go out. It hung in the air where the lamp had been. A small tongue of fire, floating, waiting, *listening*.

Everyone stopped fighting. You cannot fight while the world is doing that. It would be like dueling during a coronation.

Ivo stood up out of his shelter slowly, and the floating flame drifted to him and settled on his palm without heat, and he looked at it the way you look at a dog that has come home after years, and he said — softly, wonderingly, in the old tongue —

"*Ember.*"

Then he translated it for us, because he is a generous soul: "*Hello. I'm sorry. I didn't mean to shout.*"

Nobody spoke. Tam was crying, I realized; big silent tears running down into his beard, though his throat was nowhere near my knife anymore, because my hand had gone slack. One of the other soldiers sat down in the snow entirely. The fourth dropped his sword, and it rang on the stone, and the note it made lingered far too long, harmonizing with itself, with the wind, with the boy's breathing, until it faded like a chord resolving.

Magic. Leaking back into the world in ripples, in trickles, in a lamplight flame sitting in a child's hand like a tame bird. Sable's warning rang through me — *if he wakes fully, the war reignites* — but standing there, in that hushed and glowing pass, the warning sounded strangely small. Like being told not to laugh at a funeral. Technically correct. Completely beside the point.

Hale knelt in the snow.

I want to be precise about this, because I have earned the right to be precise about very few things, but I witnessed this one. Sergeant Hale — twenty-two years in the order, a man who once argued for a full hour about whether the garrison cat should be allowed on the table (he was against it; the cat won) — set his sword down in front of him, laid both palms flat on the frozen ground, and bowed his head. Not to me. Not to Ivo, exactly.

To the flame. To the thing the flame meant. To the possibility that everything he'd marched under might be wrong, and that being wrong might be survivable.

"I heard it," he said hoarsely. "When it sang just now. Underneath the words." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and free. "It sounded like my mother. Calling me in for supper. Forty years gone and it sounded like her, Ves. What *is* that?"

"It's a language made of remembering," I said. "I think everyone hears something different. I hear—" I stopped. I had been hearing things too, for days. A kitchen. A woman's hands kneading dough. A life I never lived, offered to me in dreams like a coat that almost fit.

"What do you hear?" Ivo asked gently.

"A door opening," I said, and left it there.

Hale rose. He looked at his men. Tam wiped his face and nodded, once, before Hale even asked — and the other two followed, in the dazed way of men waking from surgery, feeling around for limbs they weren't sure they still had.

"We report a false trail," Hale said, in the flat voice of a man drafting treason in real time. "Saw sign heading south toward the Wyrm's Tooth. Buy you a day, maybe two. After that—" He met my eyes. "After that, everyone hunts you, Vesper. Corvane himself is coming. And he's brought something with him. The men won't talk about it. They go pale when it's mentioned. They call it the Hollow, and they say it eats sound."

My blood went cold in a way the mountain cold couldn't manage. Ivo made a small noise, and I put my hand on his shoulder and felt him press back against it, and that was enough, for now.

"Two days," I said. "Thank you, Hale."

"Don't thank me." He picked up his sword, then seemed embarrassed by it and slung it rather than sheathing it. "Thank him." He nodded at the boy, whose palm-flame had finally, reluctantly, guttered out — though I swear the boy's fingertips stayed lit a moment longer, like embers in a banked fire, like a promise kept warm.

"Will you ring the bell?" Ivo asked Hale suddenly, with the non sequitur authority of children everywhere.

"...What bell?"

"In the shrine. When you're old and tired of marching. There's a broken bell, and nobody's supposed to ring it, and I think—" He frowned, searching. "I think it rings anyway. If you love it enough."

Hale stared at him. Then at me. Then, briefly, at the sky, as if petitioning it for a saner assignment.

"Sure, lad," he said. "I'll ring the unringable bell."

We watched the six lamps descend the pass — five soldiers and one sergeant, walking away from us with their lies already rehearsed — until the dark folded them in.

*

We climbed until dawn found a ledge wide enough for two, and there I built a fire the ordinary way, flint and steel and swearing, because after what we'd seen neither of us wanted to ask the mountain for favors. Ivo curled against my side, and for a while we just breathed.

"Vesper?"

"Mm."

"When the fire sat on my hand. It didn't burn. But I wanted it to. A little bit." His voice was very small. "Is that awful?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty and rarely got it from adults.

"No," I said. "I think you wanted to know it was real. Pain is good proof. But Ivo — listen to me. You don't have to burn to be real. Not ever. Not for them, not for the ember inside you, not for anyone. Being real is the one thing nobody gets to test you for."

He was quiet a long moment. Snow ticked softly against the rocks. Somewhere below, water dripped where water had not dripped in living memory, keeping a slow new rhythm, like the world learning to count again.

"Okay," he said at last. Then, sleepily, with the devastating timing of the very young: "But if I ever *do* have to burn, can you at least teach me the word for 'ow' in the old language? I feel like it would come in handy."

"There isn't one," I murmured into his hair. "There's a word for 'transformed,' and a word for 'released,' and a word for 'beloved.' The people who spoke it thought pain was just love arriving too fast."

"Oh," he said. And then, half-asleep, in the tongue older than kingdoms, he said the word for *stay* — and the fire leaned toward him, and the mountain held its breath, and far away down in the valleys I imagined every ward-stone of my old life humming like struck glass, calling me home to a home that had never existed.

Two days. Corvane was coming, and something called the Hollow was coming with him.

I lay awake a long while after the boy slept, listening to the new-dripping water, and I practiced the word for *beloved* under my breath until it felt less like a prayer and more like a name.

Which, I was beginning to understand, was the same thing.
