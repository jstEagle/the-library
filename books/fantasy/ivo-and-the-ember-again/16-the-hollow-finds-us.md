## 16. The Hollow Finds Us

I had a name before I had a mouth.

That is the first thing I remember now, when I let myself remember at all. Not a word — a *weight*. Something that pressed on me from the inside, the way snow presses on a roof, and everyone who ever loved me said it softly, like setting down something breakable. I have been trying to find it again in the dark behind my eyes. I turn over stones back there and look underneath. Mostly there are worms. Once there was fire. Twice there was Vesper's face, which is strange, because she was not alive yet when I was first named, unless time is softer than Sable says it is.

Vesper says my name now — *Ivo* — and it fits like a borrowed coat. Warm enough. Wrong sleeves. But hers.

We were sleeping in the ruin of a shepherd's cot below the Grey Teeth, three days past the mountain that spoke, when the Hollow found us.

* * *

I woke because the night went deaf.

Not quiet. Quiet has edges. You can hear the shape of quiet — the tick of a cooling fire, the small wet sounds of Hale's horse shifting in the lean-to, Vesper breathing slow and even the way she only breathes when she is not truly asleep, one ear always open for trouble, which used to frighten me and now feels like being wrapped in a blanket made of vigilance.

This was different. This was the sound being *eaten*.

The crickets stopped mid-note. Not finished — interrupted, like a door closing on them. The wind through the bracken went silent so completely that I could feel the wind still moving against my face, cold and busy, carrying nothing. Even my own heartbeat sounded far away, as if it were happening in another boy's chest, in another valley, and someone was politely passing news of it to me.

I sat up. The fire had burned to a red eye. Vesper lay with her sword hand curled near her hip even in sleep, fingers loose around nothing, ready to be full.

And at the edge of the firelight stood the Hollow.

You should know that I am not brave. Vesper is brave the way stone is heavy — it is simply what she is made of. Hale is brave the way dogs are loyal, out of habit and heartbreak. I am a small person who was once an enormous thing, and mostly what I feel is tired. But I looked at the Hollow, and I did not feel fear the way other people mean it. I felt *recognized*.

It was tall the way a shadow is tall when the sun is low — longer than anything has a right to be, folded up against the ruined doorway as if it had always been standing there and the doorway had been built around it afterward. It had no face. It had a place where a face would go, the way a glove has a place where a hand would go, and the place was hungry.

The order made it. I know that now; Sable told us, and then the mountain told us more, in its slow grinding way. Long ago, when Ember still had a voice, the founders needed silence, and you cannot make silence out of nothing. So they took the god's own hush — the pause between his words, the breath he drew before speaking the world warmer — and they taught it to hunt. They gave it one hunger: spoken prayer. Every whispered blessing, every muttered grace over bread, every frightened *please* said into cupped hands — the Hollow drank them all, for centuries, and grew fat on our small hopes, and kept the world quiet for the men who had killed the thing it was made from.

And now it had found the last voice worth drinking.

It did not move toward me. That was the worst part. It simply *arrived closer*, the way dawn arrives, without any insulting hurry, as if resistance were a category error.

I reached for Vesper. My hand crossed the space between us and came down on her shoulder, and no sound came out of the touch. No rustle of blanket. Nothing. She woke anyway — she would wake if the world ended quietly, I think, she would wake inside her own grave and start giving orders — and her eyes opened, and she saw it, and her whole body changed. Sleep left her like water leaving a cracked jar.

"*Ivo*," she said.

No sound. Her lips shaped it. Her hand found my arm and gripped hard enough to leave tomorrow's bruises, and her mouth moved again, and the Hollow leaned forward slightly, the way a man leans toward a window to hear better, and I understood:

It wasn't here for me yet. It was here for *her voice*. For every voice. It would drink the whole camp, the horse, the crickets, the wind, the memory of sound itself, and then it would fold me up inside that silence like a letter and carry me home to Corvane, unread.

Hale burst from the lean-to with his sword already drawn — he sleeps like a cat, one eye, half a heartbeat — and shouted something furious and soldierly, and the shout died in the air like a bird hitting glass. His mouth kept moving. His face went through anger and then confusion and then a kind of naked terror I had only seen once before, on the toll bridge, in the moment before he chose mercy instead of orders.

He swung at the Hollow. The blade passed through it the way a oar passes through deep water, meeting nothing, changing nothing, and the Hollow did not even turn its not-face toward him. We were beneath its notice. We were *packaging*.

Only I mattered. Only my voice.

Here is what nobody tells you about being a god in a boy's body: you spend all your time being afraid of the wrong things. I was afraid of the dark, and of Vesper dying, and of the dream where I burn forever, and none of those fears were the true one. The true one was smaller and sat deeper. The true one was this: that if I ever spoke — really spoke, in the language before kingdoms, with everything I am behind it — there would be nothing left over. That the boy is a coat the ember wears, and speech is how the coat comes off. That every word costs me, stitch by stitch, until one day Vesper says my name and turns around and there is only a warm place in the air where I was.

Sable warned me. *If he wakes fully, magic returns to the world — and the ancient war reignites.* And under that, quieter, the warning she didn't say: *and maybe the boy doesn't.*

So I had kept myself small. I had kept my words in my chest like swallowed coals. When the mountain answered me, it was an accident, a sneeze of the divine, and we ran from it, and I promised myself: never again, never on purpose, never spend the stitches.

The Hollow drifted into the firelight. Where it passed, the red eye of the coals dimmed to grey, to ash, to a memory of fire. Vesper put herself between it and me — of course she did, she would put herself between me and weather, between me and arithmetic — and raised her sword, and I could see her shouting orders at Hale, at me, at God, and none of it existed. Sound was a country we had been exiled from.

Her sword went through it. Her fist went through it. The Hollow reached past her — not struck, just *reached*, patient as winter — and its not-hand closed around her voice.

I felt it happen. I don't know how else to say it. I felt her voice leave her the way you feel a splinter come out — a small bright sting, and then a strange lightness, and then grief. She was still fighting, still moving, still gripping my arm, but somewhere inside her a room had gone empty, and I knew that if the Hollow finished, she would live the rest of her life unable to say my name, unable to say *stay*, unable to say any of the thousand small words she spends on me every day without counting them, the way rich people spend coins.

Hale grabbed her and dragged her back. Good old Hale, brave as a dog, useless as an oar in the sky, hauling her away from a thing that could not be fought because fighting requires noise and noise was the food.

And I stood up.

* * *

People think sacrifice looks like the pyre. Big flames, big music, a crowd. Nobody tells you it looks like this: a barefoot boy in a blanket, standing in the ashes of a dead fire, opening his mouth in front of the thing he fears most, alone, at night, with no one able even to hear him do it.

I want to be honest, since honesty is the one inheritance I have left: I hesitated.

I stood there while the Hollow turned its not-face fully toward me for the first time, and I felt my whole self — the boy-part, the Ivo-part, the part that likes burnt honey on bread and hates washing behind his ears — I felt that part cling to the inside of my ribs like a cat clinging to a curtain, and I almost sat back down. I almost let it take me quietly. A letter, folded neat, carried home. At least I'd still exist somewhere. At least Vesper would keep her voice.

But then I looked at her.

She was on her knees in the ash with Hale's arm across her chest, and she had stopped struggling, and she was looking at me, and even though she had no voice left she was saying something with her whole face. I know all her faces now. This one said: *not you. Anything in the world but you.*

And I thought: this is what the founders never understood. They thought a god's power was in the voice. All their knives, all their silence, all their centuries of hush — aimed at the throat. But the voice was never the power. The voice was just how the power got *spent*. And spending is the whole point. A god who will not spend himself is only a very large miser. Ember knew that. Ember spent himself on a world that murdered him for it, and his last ember fell burning out of heaven, and landed, of all the ridiculous places in all the ridiculous world, in a boy who was too scared to speak.

Well. I was still scared.

I spoke anyway.

* * *

I said the word that means *no* in the language before kingdoms. It is a short word. It is older than refusal; refusal is just one of its children. It is the word the dark says to the light at the end of every night, and the seed says to the shell, and the living say to death, gently, every morning upon waking: *no. Not yet. Again.*

When I said it, the world remembered sound.

It came back all at once, everywhere, like a held breath released — the wind crashed into the bracken, the crickets resumed mid-note as if no time had passed, Hale's unfinished shout finally arrived, late and embarrassed. And the word itself went out of me and through the night, and it was not loud. Loudness is for kings. It was *deep*. It went down through the ash and the rock and the roots of the mountains, and everything it touched stood up straighter.

The Hollow screamed.

I did not know it could scream. I think it did not know either. It was made of silence, and I had just spoken inside it, and there is no agony like your own nature being contradicted. Its not-face split along seams that had never been seams, and out of the splits poured — voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Every prayer it had ever drunk, every grace over bread, every frightened *please*, all the small hopes of small people across four hundred years, coming back up out of it like fish rising, like bubbles from a drowned man, and each one said *thank you* in some language or other as it escaped.

That is what unmade it, I think. Not my word. The gratitude. It had spent centuries being fed and had never once been thanked, because you cannot thank a silence, and when the prayers left it they left the way all prisoners leave: singing.

It folded down and down, smaller and smaller, a shadow at noon, a stain, a smudge, a bad smell in an empty room — and then it was gone, and the night was just a night, ordinary and noisy and full of crickets complaining about the interruption.

I was on my knees. I don't remember getting there. My mouth tasted of cinders and honey, which is apparently what divinity tastes like on the way out, and I felt — lighter. Stitch by stitch, like I feared. But also, for the first time since I fell out of heaven into this borrowed coat:

Like myself. Like the coat was mine. Like I had picked it.

* * *

Vesper reached me before the echo finished. She gathered me up the way she does, one arm under mine, my head against her collarbone, and she was talking — oh, she was talking, a flood of it, spending words on me like a woman making up for lost interest, *are you hurt, look at me, Ivo, Ivo, you foolish, brave, impossible—* and then she stopped, because I was laughing.

"What," she said. "What is funny."

"You sound like the crickets," I said. "Interrupted. Resuming mid-note."

She hugged me hard enough to crack a rib, probably, and then held me out at arm's length and looked at me with her checking-for-damage face, and then her face did something new, something I hadn't seen before, and it took me a moment to name it. Pride. Plain, undivided, maternal pride, aimed at me like sunlight.

"You knew what it would cost," she said quietly.

"Some of it." I shrugged. "Sable says I'm a lamp. Lamps are for burning."

"Sable," said Vesper, "says too many things." But her thumb brushed the brand on my wrist, the twin of her oath-scar, and she didn't argue with the theology. "Hale," she called. "Status."

Hale was sitting in the ash where the fire had been, holding his sword across his knees like a man at a funeral, staring at the place where the Hollow had been. He looked up at us, and his face was the face of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Fourteen years," he said. "Fourteen years I served the Quiet Flame. I've seen the ward-stones hum, I've seen the mountain answer a child, and tonight I watched a silence *die screaming*, and out of it came—" his voice caught, "—came prayers. Hundreds of years of prayers. Somebody's grandmother thanking God for rain, Vesper. It was in there. It was *in there* all along, and we called the thing that ate it holy."

"Hale—"

"No." He stood up. He sheathed his sword with the finality of a man closing a ledger. "I'm done pretending I don't know whose side the story is on. But you need to hear the rest, because I heard it before we camped, at the last farmstead, from a courier who didn't recognize me in the dark." He wiped his mouth. "The order isn't hunting you anymore. Not alone. Corvane has given the chapter-house to the inquisitors, and the inquisitors have given the road to the king's army. Three thousand men, marching up the valley of the Grey Teeth. And at their head—" he looked at me, and his eyes were wet and steady, "—at their head they're carrying Maren. In a cage. Alive, because Corvane wants her to watch. They're a day behind us. Maybe less."

Above us, far off in the black teeth of the mountains, a light flickered that was not lightning and not dawn.

Torches. A river of them, coming on in silence.

Vesper's arms tightened around me, and I felt her make the decision before she said it — felt it in her spine, the way I feel weather now.

"Then we stop running," she said. "There's a sanctuary in these passes. The place where he died." Her hand found the back of my head, gentle. "The place where it ends."

The torches came on through the dark, and for the first time since the knock at my door, I was not afraid of the fire.

I was going home to finish the sentence.
