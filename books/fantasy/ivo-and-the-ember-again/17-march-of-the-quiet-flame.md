## 17. March of the Quiet Flame

### Maren

The order marched out of Hollow Vale at dawn, four hundred strong, and Maren counted them like a woman counting her own sins.

She rode near the rear of the column, where the supply wagons groaned and the novices' singing faltered into coughing. Ahead, the banners of the Quiet Flame snapped in a wind that smelled of coming snow. Behind them, filling the road to the horizon, came the king's inquisitors — red cloaks, iron masks, and the long wagons that everyone pretended not to look at, the ones built low and wide for carrying firewood.

*For the pyre,* Maren thought. *They brought their own wood. How considerate.*

Brother Anselm fell into step beside her mule, breath steaming. "You're quiet this morning, Sister."

"I'm always quiet. It's the whole point of us."

He laughed, bless him, because he was nineteen and still believed the silence was holy rather than enforced. Maren smiled at him and felt the ledger in her saddlebag press against her hip like a second heartbeat. Three years of entries now. The children taken from the fen villages. The oath-bonds written in blood on vellum. The page, copied from the sealed archive, that described in careful liturgical language how the founders had cut the god's throat and called it *sanctification*.

Corvane rode at the head of the column, upright as a spear, and Maren watched his back and thought: *you pray to a corpse every morning, old man, and you know it.*

"Look alive," Anselm said. "Warden's calling the psalm."

Four hundred voices took up the Hymn of Stillness, the one they sang before every act of violence, which was most of their acts. *Ember sleeps, Ember keeps, what we guard we guard forever.* Maren mouthed the words. She had been mouthing them for twenty years.

What she did not mouth, what she kept behind her teeth like a stone, was the small alteration she'd made to the march orders two nights ago, when Corvane had handed her the route scroll and turned away. The column would take the high road through the passes as ordered. But the supply train — the firewood, the oil, the siege-carts meant to breach the sanctuary doors — would follow her revised route along the river gorge. A route she had chosen because it crossed three fords, each of which flooded after rain.

It had rained all night.

Not enough to stop them. Enough to cost them a day. Maybe two. Maybe enough for Vesper to get Ivo up the mountain, or to fail trying, but either way — enough that when the Quiet Flame arrived at the Choosing House, it would arrive having already lost something.

It wasn't much. It was sabotage the way a candle is a bonfire. But Maren had spent twenty years learning that a candle held steady in the dark is worth more than a bonfire that burns down the house.

"Sister Maren." A rider reined in beside her — Sergeant Odo, one of Corvane's own, with eyes like a man reading a ledger. "The Warden asks why the supply train diverts at Miller's Ford."

"The gorge road is firmer," she said. "The high road has a washout past the shrine. I sent scouts."

"He says he didn't authorize scouts."

"He authorized me," Maren said pleasantly, "to manage logistics. Unless he'd like to manage the firewood himself. It's damp. It needs turning twice daily or it won't catch."

Odo looked at her a moment too long. Then he wheeled his horse back up the column, and Maren exhaled slowly and patted her mule's neck.

"Well," she told it. "That went about as well as anything goes."

### Corvane

High Warden Corvane prayed at noon, kneeling on a fold of canvas in the lee of a boulder while the army rested around him, and he prayed the way he had prayed for forty years: to no one.

*Ember, who art silent. Ember, who art ash. Hear me, who cannot hear.*

There was a comfort in it, a terrible clean comfort, like the comfort of a cold bath. He had discovered the truth at thirty-one, alone in the archive beneath the chapter-house, holding the founding testament in hands that shook so badly he'd dropped it twice. *We killed him. We killed him and we took his voice and we have ruled in his quiet ever since.* He had knelt right there among the ledgers and wept until dawn, and then he had stood up, and shelved the testament, and gone to morning prayer as always.

Because the alternative was unthinkable. Not damnation — damnation was easy, damnation was for peasants. The alternative was a world with magic in it again. A world of burning towers and warlord-thaumaturges and plagues spoken into being, the world his great-great-grandmother had died dragging the survivors out of. The founders had murdered a god to save ten thousand lives, and Corvane had made his peace with that arithmetic long ago.

One boy against the world. Even the arithmetic was simple.

"Warden." Captain Reyes of the inquisitors stood at the edge of his canvas shelter, red cloak beaded with sleet. "A word."

"You have words constantly, Captain. It's your defining feature."

Reyes did not smile. Inquisitors rarely did; the masks were fitted in childhood and something happened to the face underneath. "Your Sister Maren. The one who manages your fires."

"What of her?"

"My men searched the supply wagons at the ford. We lost half a day fishing carts out of the water, incidentally. Lovely fords you chose." Reyes let that sit. "One of her ledgers was soaked through. Most of it's inventory. Some of it isn't."

Corvane kept his face still, which after forty years of praying to nothing required no effort at all. "Sister Maren has served the Flame since before you were masking recruits, Captain. Bring me whatever concerns you and I'll review it personally."

"Oh, I intend to." Reyes turned, then paused. "You know what I admire about your order, Warden? Your faith. Real faith is rare in my trade. Most people believe in things because it profits them." A pause, dry as paper. "I'll want to talk to her tonight."

Corvane sat alone after the captain left, and for a long moment he allowed himself the luxury of grief. Maren. Of course Maren. It was always the ones who laughed.

Then he rose, brushed the snow from his knees, and gave the order to march.

### Vesper

They saw the army from the ridge at dusk, and it looked like a wound in the world.

Vesper lay flat beside Hale in the heather, Ivo tucked between them under an oilcloth, and watched torchlight pour along the valley floor below like something spilled. Four hundred of her sworn sisters and brothers. Behind them, uncountable, the red of the inquisition.

"That's not a manhunt," Hale said quietly. "That's a crusade."

"They brought the wood," Vesper said. Her voice came out strange and flat. "I can see the pyre-wagons. They're going to burn him at the sanctuary itself. Where it happened the first time." She realized her hand had found the back of Ivo's head, cradling it without deciding to, the way you'd shield a candle. "They're going to do it *there*."

Ivo stirred under the cloth. "I'm cold," he said, in the old tongue, and then, in hers, carefully, the way he said everything in her language lately — like a gift being unwrapped: "But I'm okay. Are you okay?"

"Lovely," Vesper said. "Never better. Go back to sleep."

He didn't, quite. He lay pressed against her side, breathing, and she felt the small warmth of him and thought: *this is what I broke my oaths for. This weight. This breathing.* Twenty years guarding a border for a god who never spoke, and it had taken a child at her door to teach her what keeping actually meant.

Hale shifted beside her. Below, the torches kept coming. "There's a shepherd's track over the shoulder of the mountain," he said. "Buys us a day. Maybe."

"And then?"

"And then the Choosing House, and then whatever happens there." He was quiet a moment. "Ves. If it comes to it — if they corner us — I need to know what you want me to do. About you. About the boy."

"Live," Vesper said. "Both of you. That's the whole strategy. It's not a good one, but I've grown attached to it."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through him. Then he stiffened. "Riders. On our trail. Two — no, three. Coming up the switchback."

Vesper rolled to her knees and looked back the way they'd come. Lanterns bobbing in the dark, climbing fast, too fast for soldiers on foot. And ahead of the lanterns, faint as a memory of cold, she felt it — the same wrongness she'd felt outside Sable's hut, the feeling of a room where someone had just stopped speaking.

The Hollow.

"It found us," she breathed. "God help us, it found us anyway."

"Don't say *god*," Hale muttered. "Around here that's apparently an instruction."

Ivo sat up inside the oilcloth. His eyes in the dark were very wide and very calm, and that calm frightened Vesper more than the lanterns did.

"It's not coming for me," the boy said. "It's coming for the place. For the house where it was born. It wants to be there when—" He stopped. He looked at her, and for a moment he sounded less like a boy than like something old wearing a boy, gently, the way you'd wear a borrowed coat. "Vesper. There's a rider ahead of the others. Alone. She's carrying a chapter-house seal."

"A herald?"

"She's carrying *your name*," Ivo said.

### Maren

They took her at moonset, in the command tent, with the ledger open on the map table between them and the rain drumming overhead like impatient fingers.

Corvane read the pages in silence. Around the table stood Reyes and two masked inquisitors and Sergeant Odo, who could not meet her eyes, and Maren stood with her wrists bound in front of her and catalogued the room the way she catalogued everything, because the habit was older than fear.

*One exit. Two guards outside. Corvane tired — he rubs the left wrist when he's tired. Reyes enjoying this, which means Reyes can be provoked, which means Reyes can be managed.*

"These are accusations," Corvane said at last, quietly, "against the founders of the Order of the Quiet Flame. Written in your hand. Over three years."

"Some of it's copied from your own archive," Maren said. "Sealed shelf, third alcove. You should really lock that."

"Maren." He said her name the way a man sets down something heavy. "Why?"

"Because you know it's all true," she said, "and you've known for forty years, and you get up every morning and kneel to the thing you helped kill, and I got tired. That's all. That's the whole confession. I got tired of kneeling to a murder and calling it morning prayer."

Reyes made a small sound that might have been appreciation. Corvane didn't look at him. He looked at Maren, and for one unguarded moment she saw what lived under the piety — not cruelty, never cruelty, that was the horror of him. Grief. Forty years deep and carefully maintained, like a garden of stones.

"The girl," he said. "Vesper. You helped her steal the boy."

"Yes."

"Do you know what wakes if he wakes? Do you know what the world was, the last time the ember burned?" His voice stayed gentle, which was worse than shouting. "I do. I've read the accounts. Rivers that drank villages. Kings who were weather. Children born speaking fire. We did not silence a god out of ambition, Maren. We silenced him out of mercy, and the mercy has held for six hundred years, and I will not be the warden who lets go of the rope."

"Then you'll hang from it instead," Maren said. "Someone has to."

He closed the ledger. For a moment she thought — hoped, absurdly — that some part of him had heard her. Then he straightened, and he was only the High Warden again, and the garden of stones was watered and neat.

"Captain Reyes will hold you for trial when we return to the Vale," he said. "Confess freely between now and then, and perhaps the Flame will be merciful." At the tent flap he paused, and did not turn around. "You were at Vesper's oath-day. You held the candle. Did you ever wonder why she of all of them—"

"No," Maren said. "You don't get to use her. Not even as a question."

He left. The rain went on. And Maren sat down on the wet ground with her bound hands in her lap and began, methodically, to plan an escape she had perhaps one chance in ten of surviving, because that was the arithmetic she'd chosen, and unlike Corvane, she could live with hers.

### Vesper

The herald reached their camp an hour before dawn, walking her horse the last stretch with her palms open and empty, and Vesper knew the set of her shoulders before the lantern light found her face.

"Maren," Vesper said, and the word cracked in the middle.

"Not exactly." Maren swung down, stiff, mud to the thighs, and Vesper saw the state of her — the missing escort, the torn sleeve, the raw wrists — and understood several terrible things at once. "I escaped. Mostly. They'll have found the horses by now. I have until first light, maybe less, and then everything they have comes up this track."

"Why would you—" Vesper's voice failed. She gripped her sworn sister's arm hard enough to bruise. "Maren, they'll kill you."

"They'll try. I'm very annoying." Maren smiled, and then the smile went out like a pinched wick, and she looked past Vesper to the boy wrapped in the oilcloth, watching her with those old, old eyes. "So that's him. The ember." She knelt, slowly, joints cracking, until she was at his level. "Hello, Ivo. I knew you before you had a name. Well — I knew *of* you. I filed the paperwork."

Ivo studied her gravely. Then he reached out and touched her bruised wrist, and said a word in the pre-kingdom tongue, soft as breath.

Maren flinched. "What did he say?"

"He said the hurt is witnessed," Vesper translated. Her throat was tight. "It's a greeting. Among other things."

"Huh." Maren's eyes were bright and furious. "Six hundred years of silence, and the first new word I hear is *kindness*. Corvane should be here. He'd hate it." She stood, wincing, and turned businesslike. "Listen. The column's a day behind — I flooded the supply train at the fords, bought you time, don't thank me, it was mostly luck and spite. But the Hollow is ahead of you, not behind. It's making for the Choosing House directly. Whatever it does there, you don't want to arrive after it." She pressed something into Vesper's hand: a wax-sealed ring, the chapter-house signet. "Gate authority. The sanctuary's outer door answers to this. Don't ask how I have it; I've had it for three years in case of exactly this, which tells you how long I've been tired."

"Maren—"

"Vesper Halloran." Maren took her by both shoulders, and for a moment she was not laughing, not filing, not tired — she was the sister who had held the candle at Vesper's oath, all those years ago, and her grip was the grip of the living. "Whatever happens in that house. Whatever you have to choose. Choose the boy. Do you hear me? Not the god, not the order, not the truth, not even the world. The *boy*. The rest of it can bury its own dead."

Behind them, down the dark track, a horn sounded — once, then again, closer. First light was coming, and it was bringing everything with it.

Maren stepped back, mounted, and gathered the reins. "Go," she said. "And Vesper — when they ask who sabotaged the fords, tell them it was a woman who finally learned to count."

She rode down into the grey toward the horns, and Vesper stood in the heather with a dead god's gate-ring in her fist and her heart breaking open like a seed, and then Hale's hand was on her shoulder, turning her uphill, toward the Choosing House, toward the Hollow, toward the end of everything any of them had sworn.

"Time to go," Hale said.

Ivo slipped his small cold hand into hers.

"Yeah," Vesper said. "No way back anyway."

They climbed.
