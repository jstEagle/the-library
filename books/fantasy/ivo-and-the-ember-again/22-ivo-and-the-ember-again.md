## 22. Ivo and the Ember Again

The trout in the Hollow Valley had grown bold in the years since the silence lifted.

Vesper stood on the bank of the Coldmere with her arms crossed and watched Ivo crouch at the water's edge, holding a fishing spear with the confidence of a boy who had never once caught anything with it. He was fourteen now, all elbows and sunburn, his hair grown long enough to tie back the way she'd taught him — badly, the way she'd taught him everything practical, which was to say with more enthusiasm than skill.

"You're breathing too loud," she called.

"I'm *fishing*," he called back, without turning around. "Fish don't care about breathing."

"Fish care about everything. That's why they're hard to catch."

"That's not true. Hale says fish are stupid."

"Hale," Vesper said, "also says he once wrestled a bear, and we have agreed as a household never to bring it up."

Ivo laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind of sound that would have been impossible ten years ago, when a boy's voice could call down hunters and worse things than hunters. He waded a step deeper into the shallows, spear cocked, utterly still in that way he had. It was the only inheritance from before that had ever served him well: the patience of something very old living inside something very young. When he went quiet like that, Vesper sometimes caught herself watching him with an ache she couldn't name, because she knew what that stillness had cost him, and what it had been for.

He struck. Missed. Came up splashing and swearing — swearing in three languages now, one of them dead for a thousand years, which Sable said was either blasphemy or scholarship depending on her mood.

"Nothing?" Vesper asked.

"Nothing." He trudged out of the water, dripping, and flopped onto the grass beside her with the dramatic despair only adolescents and dying kings could manage. "The fish know me. They've told each other about me. There's probably a whole fish civilization down there with my face on wanted posters."

"There might be," Vesper said gravely. "Magic's back. Stranger things have come through."

That was true, and they both knew it, and they both let it pass the way they let most things about those years pass — gently, sideways, without looking directly at them. The valley had changed since the ember fell. Nothing dramatic; magic did not come roaring back the way the old stories promised, no dragons wheeling over the border stones, no rivers running gold. It came back the way spring comes back. A hedge-witch's poultice working a little better than it should. A candle flame that leaned toward you when you were lonely. The ward-stones along the valley rim humming on certain nights, soft as sleeping cats, keeping nothing out anymore — just singing, because singing was what stones did when nobody made them be silent.

The Order of the Quiet Flame had dissolved the same year, its chapter-houses given over to hospitals and granaries and, in one memorable case, a school where Maren now taught children to read in four languages, including one that predated kingdoms. She'd sent word last month that a farmer's daughter in Ashenford had translated half of a founding-era prayer tablet and gotten two words wrong, and Maren had corrected her gently, and then gone home and cried into her supper, though the letter did not say so. Vesper could read between Maren's lines. She always had.

"Corvane's grave has flowers on it again," Ivo said suddenly, picking grass stems apart. "I saw them this morning. Someone had left bread, too. Old custom."

"He burned to ash rather than let a lie stand," Vesper said. "People remember that, even if they don't remember why."

"Do *you* remember why?"

She looked at him. His eyes were the same brown they had always been, ordinary as river stones, and behind them lived nothing but a boy — she had checked, in the early years, quietly, desperately, a hundred times. The ember was gone. Truly gone. Whatever light Corvane had taken into himself on the pyre had died with him, and whatever remained of the god Ember lived nowhere now except in a language, in some songs, in the habits of people who left bread on graves.

"I remember everything," Vesper said. "That's my whole job these days. Remembering."

"Sounds boring."

"It's the hardest work there is."

He grinned and threw a grass stem at her, and she batted it away, and for a while they just sat in the sun like two people who had nowhere in the world to be, which was, after everything, exactly what they were.

* * *

The cottage smelled of onions and woodsmoke, and Ivo was lying about the chores.

Vesper knew this the way she knew weather coming. He had claimed, at midday, that he'd split the kindling, and the kindling basket by the hearth was full, but the axe was dry and the woodpile had not shrunk, which meant he'd filled the basket from the pile she'd split yesterday and hoped she wouldn't do arithmetic. He was getting better at lying. This should have worried her. Instead it filled her with a fierce, ridiculous pride, because a boy who could lie convincingly about chores was a boy who was ordinary, and ordinary had been the entire point, the whole desperate wager of her life.

She said nothing about the kindling. She put onions in the pot and let him think he'd won.

"Hale's coming tomorrow," Ivo said, sprawled on the floor carving a piece of driftwood into something that was either a horse or a very ambitious dog. "He says he's bringing venison. He says he shot it himself."

"He didn't."

"How do you know?"

"Because he limps on cold mornings and hasn't drawn a bow since the winter. He'll have traded for it at Ashenford and he'll tell us the same story about the bear again."

"The bear story is *incredible*, though."

"The bear story gets bigger every year. By the time he's sixty it'll have cubs."

Ivo laughed, and the sound moved through the little house like sunlight through water, and Vesper stirred the pot and thought, not for the first time, that this was the miracle. Not the mountain answering, not the words that carried weight no human voice should hold, not any of the fire and terror of those years. This. An evening. Onions. A boy lying about chores. She had spent twenty years guarding a god who never spoke, and it turned out the divine had been waiting all along in things so small she'd walked past them her whole life.

But under the warmth, as always, sat the secret.

It sat the way a stone sits under a mattress — invisible, and felt every single night. No one else carried it. Maren had her own burdens, Hale his, Sable her knowledge, but this one was Vesper's alone, and she had never spoken it aloud, not even to the dark, not even in the old tongue, which could hold almost anything.

On the pyre, in the last moments, when the ember rose and offered itself — she had heard what it offered. Not just to Ivo. To her. *Wake him fully,* it had said, in the voice of every prayer she'd ever learned, *and he lives forever. Eternal. Divine. Never hungry, never afraid, never growing. Or cut him free, and he dies as gods die — but the boy lives, and the boy grows, and the boy will one day bury you.*

Eternity or a childhood. A god or a boy.

She had cut the ropes before the thought finished forming, and she had never once, in all the years since, regretted it — not when the order shattered, not when old comrades spat her name, not when she woke some nights hearing the ember gutter out like a breath let go after a thousand years held. She would choose it again. She would choose it a thousand times, in a thousand lives, with duller knives and colder mornings. But choosing it meant carrying it, and carrying it meant knowing something no one else alive knew:

The god had begged. At the end, the god had begged to stay, and she had chosen the boy over the god, and the boy ate her onions and lied about kindling and would never know that his ordinariness had been purchased with the death of something vast and frightened and old.

Some nights that secret was heavy as a mountain. Tonight, watching him carve, it was only heavy as a stone, and stones, she had learned, could be built into walls, and walls could make a home.

"Ves." Ivo had stopped carving. He was looking at her with that directness he'd never lost, the one thing about him that had never learned to be ordinary. "Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask me anything. You know that."

"The old word. The first one I ever said. At your door, the night of the rain." He turned the driftwood over in his hands. "Maren's teaching us the language at the school, the real one, not the prayers. And I can read most of it now. But I've never known what that word meant. I said it before I could say anything else. It's the oldest thing I have." He looked up. "What does it mean?"

The pot bubbled. The fire ticked. Outside, far off along the valley rim, the ward-stones hummed their low, contented note, the way they did on evenings when the air was right.

Vesper set down the spoon.

She had rehearsed this moment for years without knowing it. Every translation she'd ever refused to give him, every time he'd asked and she'd said *when you're older* — she'd been buying time, not to find the answer, but to find the courage. Because she knew the word. She had known it since the second year, when she'd finally found it in a tablet fragment Maren had copied out, and had sat alone with it until the candle burned down, understanding at last what a dying god's last ember had said at a mercenary's door in the rain, the first word it spoke in a new body, the word older than kingdoms, older than the murder, older than the silence.

It was not a warning. It was not a summons. It was not power.

It was the smallest word in the language, and the largest.

"I'll tell you," she said. "But not here. Come on. Bring your terrible dog."

* * *

They walked up past the pastures in the last of the light, the way they used to when he was small and afraid of the dark and she'd pretended the patrol route was a game. The path climbed through gorse and bracken to the ridge where the first border stone stood — the same stone she'd been checking the day she found the dead courier, a lifetime ago, back when her whole world was a perimeter and a silence. The stone was warm. It was always warm now. Nobody knew why, and Sable had written three treatises about it, and Vesper suspected the answer was simple and no one would ever write it down: some things stayed warm where they had been loved.

From the ridge, the whole valley lay below in blue dusk — the Coldmere silvering, the cottage smoke rising straight, the lights of Ashenford strung along the road like embers that had learned to live in ordinary houses. Somewhere down there, Maren was grading translations. Somewhere beyond the hills, the world was learning, slowly, clumsily, to share itself with magic again, and mostly being kind about it, which surprised everyone who remembered the old wars.

Ivo set the driftwood dog-horse on top of the stone, the way he did every visit, an offering neither of them had ever explained to each other.

"So," he said. "The word."

Vesper put her hand flat on the warm stone. She took a breath, and let the secret come up out of the place where it had lived for fourteen years, and found — to her astonishment — that it came easily, the way a splinter comes out clean when the time is right.

"When you knocked on my door," she said, "you weren't asking for shelter. You weren't calling for help. You couldn't have known what I was, or what I'd choose. You were the last piece of a god that had been hunted and silenced and murdered, and you had one word left in you, one word out of everything that had ever been prayed in that language, and that's the word you spent." She looked at him. "You asked me to stay. That's all it means. *Stay.* The oldest word we have, and it just means: don't go. Be here. With me. However long we get."

Ivo was quiet for a long time. The wind moved the gorse. Down in the valley, someone lit a lamp.

"And you answered," he said at last. "That night. You took me in."

"I broke twenty years of oaths to do it."

"I know. Maren's book is in the school library now. I've read it." He smiled a little, crooked, the smile of a boy who had grown up on stories and knew which ones were load-bearing. "I've done the arithmetic, Ves. Everything I am — this" — he gestured at the valley, the dusk, the dog-horse on the stone, his own ordinary hands — "it's because you chose me over a god. Everyone knows the story. They sing it in Ashenford, badly. But none of them know what it *cost* you, because you never tell anyone, and I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I'm the only one who could ever actually thank you for it. So. Thank you. For staying."

Vesper found that her eyes were wet, and did not particularly mind.

"It wasn't a sacrifice," she said. "That's the part the songs get wrong. People think I gave something up. But a god offered me forever, and I picked *this* instead — onions, and lies about kindling, and a boy who can't catch a fish. That wasn't sacrifice, Ivo. That was the easiest choice anyone ever made." She bumped her shoulder against his. "I'd do it again. Every time. In every life. Now you know the thing I carry. It's not grief. It's just certainty."

They stood together while the last light went out of the sky, and the first stars came, and the stone hummed beneath their hands like something purring in its sleep. Then Ivo yawned enormously, ruining the moment entirely, and said the stew was probably burning, and they went down the hill arguing about whether he could taste burnt if he'd been the one who let it burn.

At the door, he paused.

"One more question," he said. "The word. *Stay.* If someone said it to you now — today — what would you do?"

Vesper looked at him standing in the doorway, backlit by the fire, tall now, nearly to her shoulder, with his whole unmiraculous miraculous life ahead of him.

"Same thing I did the first time," she said. "Open the door."

He grinned and went inside, and she followed, and the valley held them both, warm as a stone that had been loved, and kept them — and did not let go.
