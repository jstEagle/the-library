## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Marguerite found the error.

She sat in the counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things with her sleeves pushed to the elbow and her ink-stained fingers moving down a column of figures that should not have existed. This was the work they gave her because she was good at it and because nobody remembered to give her anything else: the reconciliation of accounts that could not be reconciled. A debt of three nights paid by a man who had only lived two. Interest owed on a wish that had been returned unused. Seven years of tallow owed by a household that had, per the ledger, never existed at all.

Impossible things left debts. That was the first truth she'd learned here, back when she was eleven years old and Mistress Vesperal had pressed her into this chair with a kiss on the crown of her head and the words *stay small, stay quiet, stay* — and then walked out into the fog and never come back.

Marguerite had stayed small. She had stayed quiet. She had become, over seven years, something very like furniture: the girl in the counting room whom the clerks greeted without seeing, whom the enforcers stepped around as though she were a stack of crates. It was not nothing, being invisible. It was warm wax, a roof that didn't leak, bread at dusk. It was safe.

The error was on page four hundred and twelve of the Ledger of Small Debts, an entry dated nine days hence.

*A debt of one life, entered in advance. Debtor: M. Blackwood.*

Her pen hovered. Ink gathered at the nib and fell, a black star blooming beside the entry.

Nine days from now. A debt of one life, entered before it was incurred, in her own name — or near enough to it. Blackwood wasn't the name she'd been born with; she'd made it up herself at thirteen, needing something to write on guild forms, and had chosen it the way you choose a coat off a hedge: because it was there and because it fit. But the ledger knew. The ledger always knew what things truly were. That was its whole terrible function.

"Copyist error," she said aloud, to the empty room, and reached for the blotting sand.

The counting room did not agree. The candle guttered though no door had opened. Somewhere below, in the vaults where the guild kept its stranger holdings, something turned over in its sleep, and the sound came up through the floorboards like a sigh.

Marguerite looked at the entry for a long time. Then she did what she always did with entries that frightened her: she ruled a line beneath it, wrote *pending verification*, and closed the book.

That was when the screaming started.

* * *

It came from the direction of the guildhall's great doors, thin and distant, the way sound carries at night through stone corridors — and then it stopped, which was worse. Marguerite stood, knocking her stool over, and stood listening in the sudden hush with her hand flat against the ledger as if it might bolt.

Footsteps. Not the measured tread of enforcers on rounds but something ragged, stumbling, closer and then closer still. The counting room's door swung open without a knock.

The stranger was tall and had once been broad-shouldered, though now he seemed to be folding inward, one hand pressed to his side where a dark stain spread between his fingers. His coat had been fine once — grey wool, silver thread at the cuffs — and was scorched now, burned through at the shoulder and across the chest in long black licks, as if he'd been dragged through a furnace by the collar. The smell hit her a half-second later: char and blood and underneath both something sweeter, like apples going over in an orchard after frost.

"You're the ledger-keeper," he said. His voice was wrecked, gravel in a river. "They said — the little clerk who never leaves. You're the one who reads them."

"I'm nobody," Marguerite said automatically. It was practically a prayer. "You need the infirmary, sir, the stairs are—"

"There is no time." He crossed the room in three lurching strides and caught her wrist, and his grip was fever-hot. Up close she could see that his eyes were wrong — not the whites but the irises, which held flecks of gold that moved, drifting slowly like sparks above a fire. Otherworld blood, or close kin to it. Changeling stock, maybe, or one of the old trade-families from across the water. "Listen to me. Corvane's people are behind me. They will be here within minutes. What I carry cannot fall into their hands."

"Then put it down and let me fetch—"

"It has already chosen you."

He said it strangely, formally, like the opening line of a contract, and Marguerite felt the hair rise along her arms. In seven years among impossible accounts she had learned to hear when words were more than words — when someone spoke terms, when the air went taut around a sentence the way air goes taut before lightning. Something was being sworn. Something was being *given*.

"I don't want it," she said. "Whatever it is. I keep books. That's all I am."

"No one is only what they seem," the stranger said, "or none of us would owe so much." And then his legs went out from under him.

She caught him as best she could — he was heavier than he looked, the way falling trees are heavy — and they went down together behind the counting table, his blood soaking cold through her apron. Outside, far off, boots on stone. More than one pair. Moving with purpose.

His hand found hers and pressed something into it. Parchment, rolled tight, and even through her sleeve she felt the heat of it — actual heat, like holding a coal wrapped in cloth.

"The map," he whispered. "Emberwright. It knows its own. Blood opens it. Don't let them—" A cough took him, wet and awful. "Don't let them read your name."

"My name is Marguerite Blackwood," she said, stupidly, desperately, "and I don't know what you—"

But the gold in his eyes was guttering the way the candle had guttered, drawing down, drawing in. He smiled at her, or tried to; it came out crooked, apologetic, the smile of a man arriving late to his own death.

"Seven years," he murmured. "She kept you safe longer than we hoped. Longer than anyone dared." His gaze slid past her shoulder, toward some middle distance where perhaps he saw orchards, or a woman's face, or nothing at all. "Tell the forge — tell them the heir lives—"

The boots were in the corridor now. Marguerite heard them stop outside the open door, heard the low exchange of voices, and pressed herself flat against the dying man as if stillness could make two bodies into one shadow.

When she looked back at his face, the gold was gone. His eyes were just eyes, grey and ordinary and fixed on the ceiling beams, and whatever had been speaking through them had gone wherever such things go.

* * *

She didn't remember deciding to hide the map. Her hands decided for her — hands trained in seven years of quiet, of staying small — and by the time the first enforcer filled the doorway, the scorched roll was tucked against her ribs beneath her apron, warm as a sleeping bird.

There were three of them. Guild grey cloaks, iron badges shaped like keys that opened nothing. The one in front — she knew him vaguely, a hard-faced woman named Serris whose rounds never brought her to the counting room — swept the space with a look that catalogued everything: the overturned stool, the spilled ink, the body.

"Well," Serris said. "The little clerk finally gets some excitement."

Marguerite said nothing. Her heart was slamming so hard she was certain they must hear it, certain it would leave a bruise.

Serris crouched by the dead man and turned his head with two fingers, examining the burn marks, then the wound at his side. "Cut, then burned. Or burned, then cut." She glanced up at her companions. "Courier work. Someone wanted what he carried badly enough to ruin a coat like that."

"He didn't have anything," Marguerite heard herself say. Her voice came out steady, which astonished her. "He came in, asked for the infirmary, collapsed. I haven't touched anything except—" She gestured at her apron, at the blood. "I couldn't lift him."

Serris looked at her for a long moment. It was the longest anyone had looked at her in years, and under that stare Marguerite felt her invisibility strain like a net under weight. *He told me not to let them read my name.* She kept her face blank, clerk-blank, ledger-blank, and prayed to whatever kept accounts on prayers that it was enough.

"Hm," said Serris, and stood. "Corvane will want a report. You'll come upstairs and give it."

"Of course," Marguerite said, and rose, and followed the three grey cloaks out of the counting room with her spine straight and her breath shallow and a burning secret pressed against her ribs.

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back — at the candle burned to nothing, at the ledger lying shut on the desk, at page four hundred and twelve with its entry dated nine days hence.

*Pending verification*, she had written.

Behind her, unwatched, the ledger lay closed, but she would have sworn — she would have staked a year's wages, all seven coins of them — that she could feel it open again, quietly, patiently, the way a debt opens the moment a debtor draws breath.

Upstairs, Guildmaster Corvane was waiting, and for the first time in seven years, everyone in the guildhall knew her name.

They were saying it wrong.
