## 2. What the Map Refuses to Say

Dawn found Marguerite Blackwood still awake, the dead man's map spread across her counting table like a wound that refused to close.

She had not slept. Sleep, she suspected, would not come again for a long while—not after watching a courier bleed out onto the guildhall's imported marble, not after feeling his fingers close around her wrist with the last strength he owned and press that scorched rectangle of hide into her palm. *Emberwright*, he had said, or tried to say. His teeth were red by then. What came out was closer to *ember-rit*—a word half-swallowed, half-burned—and then his eyes fixed on some middle distance where she could not follow, and the counting room's silence rushed in to fill the space where his breathing had been.

Now the map lay under her lamp's circle of light, and it gave her nothing.

It should have given her something. Maps were her trade in a sideways fashion; seven years of balancing the Guild of Impossible Things' accounts had taught her the grammar of borders, trade routes, toll-roads, the little cartographic courtesies of compass roses and scale bars. She knew what a map was supposed to look like. This looked like one the way a corpse looks like a person sleeping—the shape all there, the life gone out of it.

The parchment—or hide, she couldn't decide which, and its warmth bothered her more than any texture—was mottled with scorch marks along its edges, as though someone had pulled it from a fire and thought better of finishing the job. Faint lines crossed its surface, roads perhaps, or riverbeds, but they refused to resolve. Every time her eye traced one toward its destination, the line seemed to slide elsewhere, like a fish seen through water. There were symbols too: small, dense characters in an alphabet she almost recognized, arranged in columns down the left margin. Almost. That *almost* sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She knew that alphabet.

Not from books. Not from the guild's famous library, whose locked cases she dusted but never opened. From somewhere older and more private—a woman's kitchen, steam rising off porridge, a finger tracing shapes in spilled flour on the table while a child watched and laughed at the nonsense pictures. *These are just for us, little wren. The world has enough words already.*

Mistress Vell's hand. Mistress Vell's letters. On a dying stranger's map, scorched and secreted against his ribs.

Marguerite pressed both palms flat on the table until her arms stopped shaking. It didn't work. Her hands kept trembling anyway, a fine continuous shiver, as if her bones had learned something overnight her mind hadn't caught up to.

"Right," she said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "You're a document. Documents have provenance. Provenance can be established."

She began with candle smoke, holding the map above a taper so the soot might settle into whatever invisible ink cartographers sometimes used. Nothing. She tried tilting it against the lamplight at every angle, hunting for watermark or palimpsest. Nothing. She fetched vinegar from the pantry, then saltwater, then the iron-gall reagent she used to expose forged ledger entries, and dabbed each along the margin with a cloth. The hide drank them all without comment, warm and faintly pulsing beneath her fingertips, like something breathing very slowly.

Like something *waiting*.

"Stop that," she told it. "You're parchment. Parchment doesn't breathe."

By full morning, when the first clerks arrived below and the guildhall woke into its ordinary noise of quills and ledgers and low gossip, Marguerite had exhausted every trick in her quiet arsenal and won nothing but a headache and a growing conviction that the map wasn't refusing to be read. It was refusing to be read *by anyone*. And yet it had been carried across half the country, hidden against a living heart, defended with a dead man's final breath—which meant it must open for *someone*.

She thought of the courier's grip on her wrist. Of the word he'd bled trying to say. Emberwright.

"That's not my name," she whispered. "My name is Blackwood."

The map did not answer, but the warmth under her palm deepened by a single degree, patient as a held breath, and Marguerite understood—with the particular dread of a bookkeeper who has found a column that balances too perfectly—that she was lying to herself, and that the map knew it.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane arrived before noon.

He always arrived quietly for a man of his size, a broad gray presence in dove-colored wool who moved through the counting room the way weather moves through a valley. The clerks straightened without seeming to notice themselves doing it. Marguerite kept her head bent over the day's accounts and listened to his footsteps come closer, unhurried, until they stopped beside her table.

"Mistress Blackwood."

"Guildmaster." She did not look up. Seven years of practice: be furniture, be accurate, be dull. "The Ambrose account is ready for your seal. There's a discrepancy of eleven crowns I've flagged in the eastern shipments—"

"I heard we had a visitor last night."

Her pen did not stop moving. That took everything she had. "A courier. He collapsed in the corridor outside. I called for the house physician."

"The house physician says you were alone with him for some minutes before anyone else arrived."

"The corridor is dark," she said. "I was fetching a lamp."

"Mm." Corvane's shadow fell across her table, across the day's neat columns, and lingered. He was not looking at the accounts. She felt his gaze travel over her workspace with the slow thoroughness of a customs officer—past the inkwell, past the sand-dish, past the tidy stack of finished pages—to the drawer where, folded into a sleeve of oilcloth beneath her spare cuffs, the map lay burning its patient heat against her thigh.

She had put it there an hour ago. It had felt like hiding a coal in kindling.

"A sad business," Corvane said mildly. "A courier carrying guild dispatches, dead on our own floor. You'd think he'd have been announced. Logged. These days people arrive at this guild unannounced only when they're bringing trouble." A pause, weighted precisely. "Or running from it."

"He had no papers," Marguerite said. "If he had, I'd have logged him. You know I log everything."

"I do know that." Something almost like warmth entered his voice, and it was worse than suspicion. "That's why I keep you, Blackwood. In thirty years I have never once found an error in your columns. Never once found you curious about anything beyond them." He leaned in, and she smelled cedar and old paper and, underneath, something faintly scorched. "Curiosity is expensive. I hope you understand that. Whatever that man was carrying—it will turn up. Things always turn up here. The guild sees to it."

"Yes, Guildmaster."

His shadow withdrew. His footsteps crossed the room, paused at the door, and Corvane said, without turning, "Your hands are shaking, Mistress Blackwood."

She looked down. They were. Fine tremors, visible even at rest, betraying seven years of stillness in a single morning.

"Cold in here," she said. "I'll speak to the steward about the fires."

Corvane smiled—he had a beautiful smile, silver at the temples, entirely without mercy—and left. Marguerite sat unmoving until the counting room's noise reassembled itself around her, then drew out the drawer an inch, laid two fingers on the oilcloth, and felt the map's warmth answer her like a pulse recognizing its twin.

*He knows,* she thought. *He doesn't know what, but he knows something.*

And beneath the dread, unwelcome as a weed through flagstones, grew another feeling entirely, one she was ashamed of before she'd fully named it:

*He saw me.* After seven years of being furniture—of being safe, which was the same as being nothing—someone had finally looked at her and seen that she was carrying a secret worth killing for. It was a terrible thing to feel. It felt, shamefully, like being *real*.

She closed the drawer and hated herself a little, and reached for her pen, and got nothing done for the rest of the afternoon.

* * *

Night again. The guildhall emptied floor by floor, lamps extinguished wing by wing, until only the counting room burned, and Marguerite, and the thing in the drawer.

She hadn't meant to take it out again. She'd meant exactly that, all day, in the way one means not to scratch a healing cut. Now it lay on the table under the lamp, and she sat before it with her sleeves rolled up and her heart going hard, because she had run out of cleverness and arrived at the only door left.

Blood opens what ink cannot. She'd read that somewhere—in Vell's kitchen, maybe, in one of those flour-traced lessons dressed up as fairy stories. *The old locks don't want keys, little wren. They want proof.*

"If you ruin my table," she muttered, "I will haunt you, and I don't care that you're paper."

She pricked the pad of her left thumb with her ledger-pin. A bead of blood rose, absurdly bright. For one long moment she held it over the map's blank center, and the whole night seemed to lean in around her—the guildhall creaking, the wind testing the shutters, her own pulse loud in her ears.

Then she touched it to the parchment.

The map drank her blood the way dry ground drinks rain—and *woke*.

Light moved beneath the surface like sunrise under ice. Lines that had slid away from her eye all yesterday now burned clear and stayed put, branching, forking, climbing into mountain-shapes she had no names for. Roads bloomed outward from a central point—a point near the map's scorched edge, ringed in tiny furious script—roads that ran in straight impossible lines across terrain that had never agreed to hold them. Roads that crossed rivers without bridges and mountains without passes. One road simply left the map's western border and continued, glowing faintly, onto the table itself, as if the destination lay beyond the reach of any paper.

And in the margin, in Mistress Vell's alphabet—no longer almost familiar but suddenly legible, the way a face becomes legible once you love it—the columns resolved into words. Instructions. Warnings. A route.

And a name, repeated at every waypoint like a refrain, like a prayer, like a summons:

*Ivo. Ivo. Ivo.*

The last road ended at a symbol she somehow understood without knowing how: a triangle of flame above a hammer, drawn in strokes that still shimmered with heat.

*Cinderforge,* said something in her blood. *Before the equinox.*

Marguerite sat back, shaking now in earnest, thumb still bleeding, lamp guttering. The map glowed on the table between her and the dark, beautiful and terrible, a debt with her name written on it in her mother tongue.

Behind her, in the doorway of the counting room, a floorboard sighed under a weight that had no business being there at midnight.

She turned. Guildmaster Corvane stood in the shadows with a lantern raised, its light falling across the burning map, across her bloody thumb, across her face—and his beautiful smile was gone.

"Well," said Corvane softly. "Emberwright."
