## 3. Ivo

The map's first road began at a dead oak on the northern edge of the guild's grounds, and it ended—according to the ink that bloomed and shifted beneath Marguerite's fingertips like something alive and dreaming—at a place called Hollowmere, three days' walk through country she had never been allowed to visit.

She left before dawn, in the grey hour when even the most devoted clerks were still abed. She took nothing but the clothes she wore, a satchel of bread and hard cheese, the copper stylus she'd used for seven years to balance accounts of impossible things, and the map itself, wrapped in oilcloth against her heart. She did not take the guild's roads. The map had shown her others: a sheep-track that crossed a river where no bridge stood, a hollow way sunk deep between hedgerows older than any kingdom, a path through a birchwood that appeared on no survey because, she suspected, it did not wish to be found.

The road knew her. That was the only word for it. When she hesitated at a fork, the ink stirred toward one branch like a compass needle finding north. When she grew tired, the track obligingly flattened, the stones smoothing themselves under her boots as though the land remembered whose blood walked upon it.

*Emberwright*, the parchment had written in her own blood's handwriting. *Welcome home.*

She should have felt afraid. She was, a little. But beneath the fear ran something hotter and more shameful: seven years of being nobody, of keeping her eyes down and her hands ink-stained and her name folded away like a letter never sent—and now every step north, the world was *looking* at her. Hedgerow flowers turned as she passed. A kestrel overhead rode the wind in slow circles, matching her pace exactly, and when she raised a hand it dipped once, courteous as a servant.

By the second evening she had reached the hills above Hollowmere, and the map told her to stop.

*Rest,* said the ink, in letters that glowed faintly through the oilcloth. *The last mile is not kind to the unprepared.*

She made camp in the lee of a drystone wall, ate half her bread, and sat with her back against cold stone watching the light die over the moor. It was beautiful country in a hungry way—the kind of beauty that made you aware of how small your fire was, how far the dark reached. Somewhere below, a tarn caught the last of the sky and held it, silver on black.

That was when she heard the dogs.

Not dogs, precisely. She'd grown up around hounds at her mistress's cottage, before the cottage, before everything, and she knew the sound of an animal doing what it loved. This was different. This was animals doing what they were *told*. A baying with no joy in it, coming up the slope from the south in a wide, deliberate arc.

Marguerite went still, the way she'd learned to go still in the counting room when Corvane walked the aisles behind her. She counted the voices. Four. No—five, with one circling wider, cutting off the north.

Enforcers didn't use dogs. Enforcers *were* the dogs, usually. Which meant whoever hunted her tonight had brought something else. Something that could smell her across a moor.

The map burned against her chest—not painfully, but insistently, like a hand gripping her wrist. *Run,* it suggested. *The road will hide you.*

She ran.

The birchwood swallowed her, white trunks ghost-pale in the dusk, and the ground beneath her feet turned soft and silent as if the road were carrying her. But the baying came on regardless, patient now, unhurried. They didn't need to hurry. They knew the wood better than she did, or they had something that did.

She broke from the trees into a clearing and stopped so abruptly she nearly fell.

It was a ring of standing stones, nine of them, leaning like old men in conversation, and the grass inside the circle grew silver-grey and close-cropped, and the air tasted of struck flint. And in the center of the circle, waiting, stood a man.

He wore guild grey, but wrong—cut for someone broader, older, the seams let out and let out again. His face was pleasant the way a locked door is pleasant. In his hands he held a long knife with a blade the color of dull pewter, and Marguerite's skin prickled with recognition: cold iron. The metal that hurt the Otherfolk. The metal that, according to every story her mistress had ever told by firelight, also hurt *her family*, once upon a time.

"Marguerite Blackwood," the man said. His voice was mild, almost kind. "Or shall I say Emberwright? Master Corvane sends his regards. He's very sorry it's come to this."

"Sorry," Marguerite repeated. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Behind her, the baying had stopped. The wood held its breath.

"He hoped you'd see reason at the guildhall. He truly did." The man tilted his head. "You have something that belongs to the Guild of Impossible Things. A document, misfiled some thirty years ago. He only wants it returned. Then you can go back to your ledgers, your little candle, your small safe life. No harm done. No one need ever know what you are."

"And if I refuse?"

The man sighed, genuinely regretful, and that was somehow worse than a threat. "Then I'm afraid the iron does its work, and we tell the guild you died of a fever on the road. Very common, fevers. Very sad."

Marguerite's hand crept into her satchel and closed around the copper stylus. It was a ridiculous weapon. It was all she had.

"I don't suppose," she said, "you'd believe me if I said I'd think about it."

"No," he agreed pleasantly, and came at her.

Later—much later, when she had time to take the night apart and examine it piece by piece—she would remember it in fragments. The knife swinging up. Her body deciding, without consulting her, to throw herself sideways. The pewter blade scoring the standing stone beside her head with a sound like a scream, and sparks leaping from the stone—

—and the sparks *stopping*.

Hanging in the air. Nine points of light, frozen mid-fall, and then turning, all together, like birds wheeling, and streaming toward her across the clearing faster than thought.

They hit her chest and sank in, and Marguerite Blackwood, who had spent seven years learning to be invisible, lit up like a lantern.

Heat poured through her—not burning, never quite burning, but *filling*, flooding down her arms and up her throat until she was sure her eyes must be glowing, her hair, her breath. The man staggered back, shielding his face. The knife in his hand began to smoke. And out of the ground inside the stone circle, out of the old silver grass and the deep roots of the birchwood, rose a fox.

It was made of banked fire.

That was the only way to describe it: not flame, which devours, but embers, which endure. Its coat was a drift of red-gold coals, dimming and brightening with its breathing, and its eyes were two white-hot points, and where its paws touched the earth the grass glowed briefly and did not burn. It was small—a lean, rough-coated vixen of a thing—and it moved with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once needed to run from anything.

The man looked at it and went grey.

"No," he whispered. "No, it's not possible, you're *dead*, you've been dead for thirty years—"

The fox looked at him.

It did nothing else. It simply looked, with those furnace-white eyes, and the man made a sound Marguerite had never heard a human make before—a thin, high, involuntary note of animal terror—and fled. The knife clattered against a stone. The baying in the wood turned, all five voices at once, and receded southward at a speed that suggested the dogs had reached the same conclusion as their master.

Silence came back into the clearing like water filling a footprint.

Marguerite discovered she was sitting in the silver grass. She did not remember sitting down. The heat was draining out of her now, leaving her shaking and cold and absurdly, desperately hungry, and the sparks that had filled her were flowing back out, streaming across the grass into the small ember fox that sat regarding her with its head cocked.

"Well," she said. Her voice cracked. "Thank you."

"You're welcome." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere—from the coals of the fox, from the standing stones, from somewhere behind her ribs. It was a dry voice, an old voice, with a crackle in it like a hearth about to catch. "Though in fairness, child, you did most of the work. Cold iron can't abide living ember. You carried enough of me in your blood to make his blade weep. I merely... organized matters."

"You're talking." She was aware this was not a useful observation. She made it anyway. "A fox is talking to me."

"A fox is *listening* to you. There's a difference, and it matters." The fox rose, shook itself, and sent up a brief snowfall of sparks. "And I am not, strictly speaking, a fox. A fox is what I can afford at present. Thirty years asleep in cold ground makes for a thin purse." Its ears flicked forward. "My name is Ivo. Bound spirit of the Emberwright line, keeper of the Cinderforge's fire, warden of the covenant between your world and mine. Or I was. Before the forge went dark and I went with it."

Marguerite's mouth had gone dry. "The covenant. The courier—he said—"

"I know what he said. I felt him die with my name in his mouth." For a moment the fox's coals banked low, dimming to almost nothing, and Marguerite understood she was looking at grief wearing the shape of an animal. "Thirty years I slept while the tether frayed thread by thread. Thirty years, and then a girl bleeds on a scrap of enchanted vellum, and I wake up starving in a hole in the ground." The white eyes fixed on her. "Because you woke me, Marguerite Emberwright. Your blood called me home. Do you understand what that means?"

"That I'm—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm a ledger clerk. I balance accounts. I once spent four months reconciling a debt of seventeen left shoes. I'm nobody's heir."

"You are the last Emberwright," Ivo said, gently and implacably, "and you are standing in a covenant ring your great-grandmother helped raise, and there is cold iron in your future and an equinox on the calendar, and whether you feel like an heir or not, the world has begun treating you as one. That is what inheritance *is*, child. It is not a feeling. It is a bill that arrives whether or not you signed for it."

Marguerite looked down at her hands. They were trembling—but beneath the tremor, faintly, along the old burn scar on her palm that she'd never been able to explain, her skin glowed. Just barely. Like ash holding heat overnight, waiting for morning.

"The forge," she said slowly. "The map leads to the forge."

"It leads to the only place the covenant can be remade. And we have—" the fox paused, and Marguerate felt rather than saw him consult some inner almanac, "—eleven days. Autumn equinox. After that, the door between the worlds doesn't merely fray. It tears."

"Eleven days." She got to her feet on legs that complained. "And Corvane's people know where I am. That man will report—"

"He'll report that a dead spirit walks," Ivo said, and there was something new in the crackling voice now, something that sounded almost like satisfaction. "Which will frighten the ones who profit from the breach far more than any map. Good. Let them be frightened. Frightened men make mistakes." He trotted to the edge of the circle, then paused and looked back at her, coals brightening. "Can you walk, heir of mine?"

"I've walked two days on bread and fear," Marguerite said. "What's one more?"

Ivo made a sound that might have been a laugh, if a hearth could laugh. "Good answer. Then follow me—and stay close to my fire. The road ahead runs through country that remembers your family, Marguerite. Some of what remembers us loves us still." The fox's eyes flared white. "And some of it has been waiting thirty years to ask why we let it fall."

He bounded north into the dark, a scatter of embers against the moorland night, and Marguerite—ledger clerk, orphan, liar's ward, last of her name—took one breath of the cold clean air, pressed her glowing palm flat over the map against her heart, and followed.
