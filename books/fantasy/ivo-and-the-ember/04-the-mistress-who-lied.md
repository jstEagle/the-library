## 4. The Mistress Who Lied

I had forgotten how heavy a name could be.

*Emberwright.* I had spoken it once, in the dark of the alder wood, with enforcers' crossbows trained on the girl and my ember-core guttering low after thirty years of sleep, and the word had come out of me like a coal spat from a dying fire—small, hot, impossible to take back. Marguerite Blackwood had gone still as a held breath. Then she had looked at me, this fox-shaped thing of banked flame and old guilt, and asked the only question that mattered:

"Who are you?"

"I am Ivo," I said. "Bound to your blood since before your grandmother drew breath. And you are the last Emberwright, which means we are both very late for an appointment at a forge."

She did not run. That surprised me. Most humans run from fire that speaks.

Now we walked the first road the map had shown her—a road that did not exist on any guild chart, a seam of pale grass running through country that remembered being Other. The dawn was the color of skimmed milk. Frost smoked off the fields. Marguerite walked a half-step behind me the way she must have walked behind her mistress for seven years: attentive, careful, waiting to be told what she was worth.

I knew that posture. Her mother had never worn it. Neither had her grandmother, nor the forge-warden who first bound me, back when the covenant was young and the Emberwrights still believed themselves servants rather than sacrifices.

"You're staring," Marguerite said.

"Foxes stare. It's most of what we do." I trotted ahead and put my nose to the ground, though there was nothing to scent but frost and the faint iron tang of the map folded against her breast. Still, I let her believe I was working. Some truths are best carried to a fire before they're set down.

The truth I carried had been burning a hole in me for three decades.

We crossed a stone bridge where the river beneath ran backward, slow and silver, muttering names to itself. On the far side stood a shrine to a god whose statue had been defaced so long ago that no one living could say which god it had been. Marguerite touched its broken face as she passed—an unconscious kindness—and something in my chest, in the coals of me, ached.

"She loved this road," I said, before I could stop myself.

Marguerite stopped. "Who?"

"Your mother."

I heard her breathing change. Seven years among ledgers, and she still breathed like someone braced for the correction to come.

"You knew her." Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"I knew all of them," I said. "Every Emberwright since the binding. I lit their hearths. I carried their messages between worlds when the roads were open. I—" My voice came out crackling, and not from the fire in it. "I failed the last one. But that is a debt for another mile. Walk with me, Marguerite, and I will tell you what I can bear to tell."

She walked. She was good at walking into things; I would learn that about her properly later, at a crossing called Duskwater, but even then I could see it—the stubbornness under the ledger-keeper's quiet, the same spine her mother had.

* * *

We made camp at dusk in the hollow of a barrow-hill where the old grass grew silver and the wind moved like something listening. I built the fire for her—my one indulgence, sitting inside the ordinary flames like a man warming his hands at a smaller version of himself—and Marguerite ate hard bread and harder cheese and watched me across the firelight with those ledger-dark eyes.

"Tell me about her," she said. "My mother. Tell me everything."

So I did. Not everything—not yet—but enough.

"Her name was Serafina Emberwright. She had your hands. She laughed at funerals and cried at weddings and once, at nineteen, she argued with a river-spirit for six hours over a fishing right and won." I let the memory sit in the flames between us, glowing. "She was meant to reforge the covenant. The year she turned twenty-five. Equinox, autumn, the Cinderforge, the hammer. All of it prepared. All of it waited."

"And she didn't."

"No."

"Why?"

And there it was. The mile I had promised myself. I looked at her across the fire—at the soot on her cheekbone from the ambush, at the map's corner showing above her collar like a wound—and I understood that I could not lead her to the forge carrying a lie shaped like mercy. Corvane's people were already hunting us. If she learned the truth on a battlefield, or at the forge-mouth with wardens watching, it might break her at the moment she needed every piece of herself whole.

Better here. Better by a fire I had built. Better with a bound spirit beside her who could catch her if the truth knocked her down.

"Because someone took you from her," I said. "Three weeks before the equinox. Someone stole the only heir left to the Emberwrights, and hid her so well that even I—who can smell Emberwright blood across a hundred miles, even sleeping—even I lost the scent."

The fire popped. Somewhere on the hill above us, a nightbird stopped singing.

"What are you saying?" Marguerite's voice was very level. Ledger-level. I recognized it now for what it was: arithmetic performed at speed to avoid feeling the sum.

"I am saying that your mistress—the hedge-witch, the woman who raised you, who taught you herbs and patience and how to make yourself small—Ivo, be careful, be gentle—" I steadied myself. "She did not find you abandoned. She did not rescue you. She *took* you. From a cottage outside the town of Ashfall. From a cradle your mother had carved herself, with embers worked into the headboard. From a family that loved you."

"No."

"She carried you south through three wards and a salt-marsh. She paid a ferryman in memories to forget her passage. She chose the Guild of Impossible Things because it is the one place in the human realm where a child can be made invisible—where anonymity is not a misfortune but a profession."

"*No.*" Louder now. She was on her feet without remembering standing, the way grief makes bodies move before minds consent. "She was kind to me. She taught me to read the weather and mend bones and keep accounts. When I was sick with fever at nine she sat up with me four nights running. She—she used to brush my hair and sing songs in a language she said she'd learned from her grandmother—"

"Songs of the Otherworld," I said quietly. "Lullabies of the Ember line. She learned them from you, Marguerite. From the child she was erasing."

The silence after that was the worst sound I have heard in thirty years, and I have heard forges die.

Marguerite stood with her arms wrapped around herself, and I watched seven years rearrange themselves behind her eyes. Every kindness becoming evidence. Every tenderness becoming theft. I saw her reach for a memory—the hair-brushing, probably, or the fever nights—and flinch away from it as though it had burned her, and then reach again, because what else is there to do with a childhood except live in it?

"She sent me away," Marguerite said finally. Her voice had gone small and terrible. "That's what I've believed. Seven years, Ivo. She pressed a purse into my hand and said *the guild will keep you safe*, and I thought—I thought I was being protected. I thought I mattered enough to protect."

"You did matter," I said. "That is precisely why she took you. An Emberwright heir who reaches the forge can reforge the covenant. Reforged, the covenant closes the breaches, ends the trade in Other-flesh and stolen magic, collapses half the fortunes of the Guild of Impossible Things—including, incidentally, the fortune of a certain hedge-witch who had spent forty years buying breach-tokens and selling passage through wounds in the world."

Marguerite's head came up slowly. "She profited from it."

"For decades. Your disappearance wasn't mercy. It was inventory management. She removed the one item that could close her market."

"Don't." The word cracked like green wood. "Don't make it tidy. Don't stand there in the shape of a fox and make it *tidy*—"

"I'm not making it anything. It simply is."

She turned away from the fire. Away from me. Her shoulders were shaking, and I wanted—I have rarely wanted anything so badly—to go to her, to press my hot forehead against her cold hand the way I had done for her mother on bad nights, the way I had done for her grandmother, for all of them down the long dim corridor of Emberwrights. But I was the thing that had slept for thirty years while a child was stolen and a covenant rotted and a woman died refusing a forge. My comfort had no clean hands. I stayed where I was and let the fire speak for me, popping and settling, saying the only honest thing fire knows: *I burn, I burn, I burn.*

When she spoke again, her back was still to me.

"The songs," she said. "When she brushed my hair. Was she—was any of it real? Did she love me at all? Or was I just... a lock she kept?"

I considered lying. I found I couldn't. Perhaps that is the one virtue of being made of fire: it is very difficult to hold a falsehood without it smoking.

"Thieves love what they steal," I said. "It doesn't unsteal it. She may have loved you truly and wrongly in the same breath. Humans are large enough for that. But Marguerite—if you go to that forge believing you owe that woman your gratitude, you will hesitate at the hammer. And hesitation at the hammer kills heirs. It killed your mother, or near enough. So grieve her. Grieve the childhood, the lie, the whole crooked house of it. But grieve it *before* the equinox, and leave it behind on the road."

"How long?" she asked. "How long do we have?"

"Nine days. Perhaps ten, if the weather holds and Corvane's dogs are slow."

She nodded once. Then she sat down again, across the fire from me, and began—this is the part I will remember until my core goes cold—began folding the scorched map along its old creases with steady, ledger-keeper's hands. As though balancing accounts. As though the fastest way through grief was to enter it in a column and carry the total forward.

"One more thing," she said, without looking up. "You said you failed my mother. At the end. What does that mean, Ivo? And don't give me 'another mile.' We have nine days and a long road. Start talking."

The firelight caught the map's edge where her blood had bloomed the hidden roads into being, and far off to the north, past the black teeth of the hills, I could feel it: the Cinderforge, cold these thirty years, waiting under the mountain like a held breath.

"There is a crossing called Duskwater," I said. "Your mother died within sight of it. So will I tell you how—or so I'll tell you what I did, which is worse."

Marguerite looked up at last, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely dry, and they were her mother's eyes exactly.

"Then we'd better walk," she said.
