## 5. Saltmarket

The road to Saltmarket smelled of rot.

Not the honest rot of a midden heap, which at least fed something—crows, worms, next year's cabbages—but a thin, sour wrongness that clung to the back of the throat like old coin. Marguerite noticed it a mile before she saw the town, walking beside Ivo in his fox-shape with her hood up and the map warm against her ribs. The hedgerows flanking the road were the first warning. They should have been heavy with late blackberries, fat and glossy as an equinox approached. Instead the canes stood bare and grey, their leaves curled like burnt paper, though no fire had touched them. One tree at the field's edge had dropped all its fruit in a single ring around its trunk, as if it had simply given up holding on.

"That's blight," she said. "Or drought."

"It's neither." Ivo's voice came from somewhere near her knee, low and crackling, like words read aloud from a banked fire. In fox-shape he was small enough that travelers took him for an odd-colored dog, if they took him for anything at all. "Look closer. The fruit didn't wither. It *left*. Things are pulling back from this side of the border. Roots, sap, seasons—the Otherworld is drawing in its breath, and everything that shares that breath is thinning with it."

Marguerite looked at the ring of fallen apples, brown and whole, not one of them spoiled. It was somehow worse than rot. Rot meant time had passed. This meant refusal.

"How far does it go?"

"To the covenant's roots? All the way." The fox's ears flicked. "You wanted to see what seven years of ledgers couldn't show you. Saltmarket will show you."

Saltmarket showed her.

They came down into the town at midmorning, when any market should have been roaring—carts and cries, fish thrown hand to hand, a hundred small bargains struck in a dozen tongues. What Marguerite saw instead was a street half-full of stalls and full to bursting of silence. The stalls that remained stood at wide intervals, as if their owners had quarreled with their neighbors and refused to stand close. Half the awnings were furled. A fishmonger sat behind a meager spread of herring, smoking and staring at nothing. Where the human traders ended, the Otherfolk quarter began—or had begun—and now there was only a strip of empty cobbles between them, swept clean, like a border drawn by someone ashamed of it.

She saw the chalk line before she understood it: a pale stripe running across the mouth of the side lane where the Otherfolk had kept their booths. Fresh chalk. Someone renewed it daily.

"What's that?"

"Salt and scripture," Ivo said grimly. "The guild's cheapest invention. Draw a line, tell folk it keeps the fair folk out, and watch them thank you for the wall they build themselves." He trotted ahead, tail low. "Stay off it anyway. Some lines are just chalk. Some are chalk over older things."

A woman at a fruit stall watched them pass. Her apples were small and hard as knuckles, and she'd priced them as if they were made of gold, which Marguerite supposed they nearly were now. Beyond her, a tinker argued with a customer about a kettle; beyond him, nothing. The empty lane yawned. And standing at the mouth of it, being turned away—not shouted at, nothing so dramatic, merely *turned*, the way you turn weather—a family.

Marguerite stopped without meaning to.

There were three of them. A tall figure with bark-brown skin and hair like willow leaves gone autumn-gold, a child of perhaps six with the same leaf-fall hair, and between them a woman who was so ordinary-looking that Marguerite's eye slid off her twice before it caught—the second wife, then, or the sister. Human. The tall one held a basket of something wrapped in cloth, and even from ten paces Marguerite could smell it: bread, real bread, dark and seeded, the kind that hadn't been baked on this side of the border in years because the wheat wouldn't take. They were trying to trade. That was all. Bread for salt, or bread for lamp oil, the oldest arithmetic in the world.

And the stallholders would not look at them.

It wasn't cruelty, exactly, which was the terrible part. It was the particular politeness of fear. A woman drew her child behind her skirts. A man said, "We don't deal past the line," in the flat voice of someone reciting a rule he hadn't written, and the willow-haired father nodded as if he'd expected exactly this, and that nod—that tired, practiced, *expecting* nod—did something to Marguerite's chest that seven years of invisible ledger-work had never done.

The child stepped over the chalk.

Just one small foot, scuffed shoe crossing the white line, reaching toward a tray of honey cakes left out to tempt custom. Nothing happened. No lightning. No curse. The boy looked up at the honey cakes with an expression Marguerite knew intimately, because she had worn it every day of her childhood looking through the guildhall windows at other children playing: the expression of someone asking permission to exist.

A stallholder moved. Not toward the boy—away from him, gathering her tray, her rings, her coins, retreating up the lane as if the child's foot had been the first drop of a flood.

"Hey," Marguerite heard herself say.

Her voice came out louder than intended. The whole lane turned. She crossed the distance before her caution could catch her, crouched to the boy's height, and held out the only thing in her pockets worth anything: a twist of dried plums she'd bought two days back and hadn't eaten.

"Trade you," she said. "For a look at your mother's bread."

The boy stared. The willow-haired man's hand closed gently on his son's shoulder, and Marguerite felt the weight of an adult's caution settle over the moment—the caution of someone who had learned that kindness from strangers was usually a hook with bait on it.

"I'm not guild," she said quietly. "I'm not anyone. But my grandmother—" *lie, or memory, or both* "—my grandmother taught me that bread freely offered is bread freely taken. May I?"

The human woman—the ordinary one, the one whose eyes had been fixed on the middle distance since Marguerite arrived—spoke for the first time. "Brannoc," she said to the tall man, in a voice worn smooth as river stone, "let the girl taste it. We've carried it forty miles to be told *no* by people who've forgotten what it smells like."

So Marguerite tasted it. It was dense and dark and faintly sweet, and it tasted, absurdly, of orchards that still bore fruit somewhere, and her eyes stung, and she blamed the wind off the harbor.

"You've come far," Brannoc said. His voice was deep and slow, like a tree deciding something. "Further than the road shows. You carry iron and parchment and something that burns." His leaf-colored eyes flicked down to the fox at her heel, and did not widen, and did not look away. "Old friend," he said to Ivo. "Thirty years, and you come back smaller than you left."

"You come back taller," Ivo said. "The world balances."

* * *

Their names, given later in the shade of Brannoc's cousin's unfurled awning—the only Otherfolk booth still open, selling charms against toothache and love, which Marguerite noted wryly were the two ailments no covenant had ever governed: Brannoc, his wife Sef (the willow-leaf hair ran in the family), the boy Tam, and the human woman, who was called Perrin Duskwater, and who was, of all things, a ferrywoman.

"Duskwater," Ivo repeated, and went very still in a way Marguerite was learning to dread. "Of the Duskwater ferry."

"My mother's ferry. My ferry now, such as there is water left to ferry across." Perrin was broad-shouldered and sun-browned, with laugh lines that had recently stopped laughing and rope-scarred hands. She looked at Marguerite the way one trader looks at another's goods: frankly, appraising, without malice. "You're going to the Cinderforge."

It wasn't a question. Marguerite's hand moved to her ribs before she could stop it.

"The map shows," Perrin said, tapping her own temple. "Not the paper—the road. Anyone who walks it starts to hum. I've poled the Graywater twenty years; I know what people look like when they're carrying something that carries them back." She leaned forward. "I also know the ford below Saltmarket has gone dead. The water won't take a boat that means to cross into the Otherworld anymore. Except mine. And mine only grudgingly."

"Why?" Marguerite asked. "Why help us? You don't know us. You saw what this town does to people who help *them*." She nodded at the chalk line.

Perrin smiled, and it was the saddest functional smile Marguerite had ever seen. "Because my mother ferried the last Emberwright across the Graywater, thirty years ago, and never talked about what happened after, and drank herself quiet every equinox until she died. Because I grew up watching the orchards die and the changelings get sent back and the market empty stall by stall, and everyone saying *it's the times, it's the times*, as if times were weather and nobody had made them." She shrugged. "And because you gave a child dried plums in front of forty cowards, and your hands shook while you did it, which means you were scared and did it anyway. That's the only credential I've ever trusted."

* * *

The scribe found them at dusk.

Marguerite was checking the map by the last light—it had grown bolder since morning, its roads blooming faintly gold whenever her shadow fell across it, as if it drank her presence—when a soft whirring filled the alley, like a thousand tiny wings negotiating. Out of the gloom stepped a slight figure in a coat stitched from what looked like pages, hundreds of them, layered like feathers, and where a lantern should have hung at his belt there was instead a globe of pale green light that moths orbited and occasionally landed on, and were permitted to stay.

He was Otherfolk, but barely—moth-winged, the wings folded tight along his back like a folded letter, dusted grey, one antenna bent. He looked exhausted in a way that had gone past tiredness into architecture, as if weariness had been built into him load-bearing.

"You're the Emberwright," he said. Not a question either. Everyone today was reading roads she couldn't see. "My name is Quill. I was the guild's archivist. Third drawer, sub-cellar, records of the breach." He smiled thinly. "I catalogued your family's ruin. Every document. Every forged entry. Thirty years of paperwork proving the Emberwrights abandoned their duty—which the guild wrote itself, signed by clerks who were paid by the same faction that profits from the open border. I filed the lie under Truth for eleven years before I couldn't do it anymore."

"And now?"

"Now I keep the true copies." He opened his coat a fraction, and Marguerite saw pages beneath pages beneath pages, a whole archive worn as clothing. "Corvane's enforcers reached Saltmarket yesterday. Two of them, asking after a woman with a scorched map. The harbormaster sold them your description for a bottle of brandy." Quill's green light guttered. "They're at the Ferryman's Rest. They'll move at dawn, and they won't ask politely twice."

The alley was quiet. Somewhere down the lane, Tam laughed at something his father said, high and bright, and the sound cut through Marguerite like a blade through paper.

All day the anger had been building in her the way heat builds in a banked forge—slowly, invisibly, layer on layer. The bare hedgerows. The chalk line. The boy's foot crossing it and the stallholder *fleeing*. Seven years she had balanced the Guild of Impossible Things' books, proud of her neat columns, proud of her invisibility, and the whole time the guild had been writing her family out of the world in a sub-cellar three floors below her desk. She had audited lies. She had initialed them.

"They want the map," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, level and cold, like Corvane's voice, and she hated that and kept it anyway. "They think it's paper."

"What else could it be?" Quill asked.

"A debt," Marguerite said. "And I'm the heir. Which means I'm the collector." She rolled the map and tied it, and looked at Perrin. "Your ferry. First light. Before they wake."

Perrin grinned, and this time it wasn't sad at all. "First light. Bring your shaking hands."

But as they gathered their things in the dark of the charm-seller's back room, Ivo pressed close against her leg, and his fire had gone very low, and when he spoke it was almost too quiet to hear.

"There's something you should know about the crossing," the ember-fox said. "The last time an Emberwright took the Duskwater ferry—I was there. And I failed her."

Marguerite looked down at the small burning shape at her feet, at the first true thing her blood had ever bought her, and felt the night tilt.

"Tell me on the water," she said.
