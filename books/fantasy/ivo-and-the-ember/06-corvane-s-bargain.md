## 6. Corvane's Bargain

Saltmarket smelled of brine and woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath—honey left too long in the sun, the scent of the Otherworld bleeding through a border that no longer held. Marguerite had learned to read the town in three days: which stalls sold to both kinds of customers, which doorways were chalked with warding signs gone gray with neglect, which children watched the ferry landing with the particular hunger of those who had been told never to look.

She was counting coins for a room when the innkeeper's daughter touched her elbow.

"Beg pardon, miss. Gentleman downstairs asking after you. Guild colors."

Marguerite's hand went still over the coppers. Three days she had been careful—hood up, name unspoken, paying in mixed coin so no single mint could trace her. Three days since the moth-winged scribe, Oriel, had copied the map's second road onto vellum thin enough to swallow whole if it came to that. Three days of believing that Saltmarket's crowds were enough cover.

"Did he give a name?"

The girl's eyes flicked toward the stairwell and back. "He said—" She lowered her voice to almost nothing. "He said to tell you the ledgers are still open. And that he remembers your handwriting."

* * *

Corvane sat alone at a corner table with his back to the wall, as if he were the one who needed watching. He looked exactly as he had in the guildhall's counting room on all those nights Marguerite had balanced impossible accounts under his distant supervision: silver at the temples, a face like a well-kept ledger itself, every line ruled straight. He wore traveling clothes instead of guild robes, but the signet ring was the same—the crossed keys of the Guild of Impossible Things, worn smooth from decades of turning locks.

"Miss Blackwood." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Or should I say Emberwright? I find I'm not sure which you'd prefer, and I've always believed in letting people choose their own names. It's rather the point of our profession."

"You knew." She didn't sit. "You've known who I was this whole time."

"I suspected for seven years. I confirmed it four nights ago, when a dying man bled out on my counting room floor and pressed a scorched map into the hands of my quietest clerk." He turned his teacup a quarter-turn, precise as an entry posted. "You balance books beautifully, by the way. I mean that as a compliment. Most people flinch from impossible sums. You make them behave."

"Where is Ivo?" The fox was outside, somewhere—a banked fire prowling the alley behind the inn, sworn to stay clear of iron-shod streets. But her voice came out sharper than she intended, and Corvane heard it.

"Your spirit is fine. I have no interest in spirits; they can't hold property or sign contracts." He nodded at the chair again, and this time she sat, because standing made her feel like prey. "I'm here about the map. And about what happens at the equinox."

"The equinox is six days away."

"Five," said Corvane, "if you count properly, which I know you do." He drew a folded paper from his coat and laid it flat between them without opening it. "Before we speak of terms, let me show you the accounts. You of all people will want to see them."

* * *

What he showed her was not a document but a recitation, delivered low and even, while the inn filled around them with dockworkers and traders who never glanced their way. Corvane spoke like a man reading figures aloud, and the figures were these:

For thirty years, since the covenant had begun to fail, the Guild of Impossible Things had grown rich on the breach. Every road that frayed between the realms became a toll road—for smugglers moving Otherworld goods into human markets without tariffs, for guild brokers selling passage to refugees who could no longer cross legally because there was no law left to cross under. The fading of the Otherworld did not ruin the guild. It *fed* the guild. Scarcity raised prices. Desperation raised them further.

"The orchards dying outside town," Corvane said. "The changelings turned away at the market gates. Do you know who buys the land when an orchard fails? We do. Cheaply. From families who no longer remember why their trees stopped bearing, only that they must eat. In thirty years the guild has acquired more territory than it managed in the three centuries before. Grief, Miss Blackwood, is the most reliable commodity in any market. It never stops being produced."

"You're describing theft."

"I'm describing *stability*." For the first time something moved behind his eyes—not warmth, but the ghost of conviction, which is sometimes worse. "Here is what no one tells heirs: the old covenant was a war postponed, not a peace achieved. Two worlds bound together by one family's blood, generation after generation, each heir asked to pay a price no one else would pay. Your mother stood where you will stand. Look how that ended."

Marguerite's hands were flat on the table. She noticed, distantly, that she had pressed them there to keep them from shaking. "My mother died reforging it."

"Your mother died *refusing* to reforge it on the old terms, and the forge took its due anyway—or so the wardens claim, and who among us can audit the dead?" Corvane leaned forward. "I am offering you something no Emberwright has ever been offered. A way out."

* * *

He opened the folded paper. It was a contract, drafted in the guild's finest hand, and Marguerite read it the way she read everything now—with the terrible fluency the map had woken in her blood. The terms were simple. Almost gentle.

She would surrender the map. She would renounce the Emberwright inheritance, formally, before witnesses, so that no power in either realm could call her to the Cinderforge. In exchange, the guild would manage the Otherworld's fading—slowly, carefully, "with compassion," the clause read—resettling those who wished to cross before the last roads closed, compensating those who could not go. No war. No storming of forges. No enforcers dragging her friends into the Duskwater. The Otherworld would fade like a candle guttering out at the end of a long evening instead of going out in a scream.

And Marguerite would go back to her ledgers. Her small room above the counting hall. Her anonymity, her candlelight, her safe invisible life—as if the courier had never died on the floor, as if the map had never bloomed under her blood, as if she had never heard a fox made of banked fire speak her family name in the dark.

"You'd let it die," she said. "A whole world. All of it. The moth-people, the ferry-roads, the orchards—you'd watch them go out and call it compassion."

"I'd call it arithmetic." Corvane's voice stayed level, patient, the voice of a man who had won arguments in better rooms than this. "One world ends slowly. One world continues, undamaged, prosperous. Set against that: one woman keeps her life, her freedom, and her choice. Tell me where the loss falls, Marguerite. Show me the column where anyone but you loses anything at all."

"And the ones who can't be resettled? The ones the roads forget first?"

"There are losses in every settlement. I don't pretend otherwise. But I have spent thirty years learning the difference between a tragedy you can schedule and a catastrophe you cannot." He tapped the contract once. "The equinox comes whether you reach the forge or not. If you try, my enforcers will stop you—and they have orders that I would very much prefer not to carry out. If you succeed, the covenant demands a life freely given, and I have watched one Emberwright die already this generation. I am offering you the only version of the future in which you are alive in it. That is not a threat, Miss Blackwood. It is the kindest entry in any ledger you'll be shown this year."

* * *

The worst part—the part that shamed her later—was how long she wanted it.

Not the world's ending; she felt no temptation there. But the shape of the offer, the *texture* of it. Seven years of being no one. Seven years of safety purchased with silence, and how sweet that silence had been, right up until the night it broke. Corvane was offering her back the person she had been before the courier bled out on the counting room floor, and some tired, buried corner of her reached for that woman like a hand reaching for a banister in the dark.

She thought of the ferrywoman's hands on the tiller, cracked and certain. Of Oriel copying roads onto vellum by moth-light, wings trembling with the effort. Of Ivo saying *Emberwright* like a word he had carried in his mouth for thirty years, waiting to give it back.

Then she thought of her mother, and understood, finally, what Corvane's offer really was. Not mercy. An audit. He had run the numbers on her life and found the sum acceptable—one world against one woman—and he genuinely could not see the error in the calculation. Because the ledger he kept had no column for what a world *was*. Only for what it cost.

"No," Marguerite said.

Corvane closed the contract without surprise. "May I ask why? Professionally. I collect reasons."

"Because you're right that someone has to pay." She stood, and her voice didn't shake anymore, and that frightened her more than the enforcers waiting somewhere beyond the windows. "It's the only true line in your whole contract. Someone pays. My mother paid. The ferrywoman paid. But you've got the accounting wrong, Master Corvane. You think the debt belongs to whoever's weakest when the note comes due. It doesn't. It belongs to everyone who signed—which is both worlds, or it's nothing." She picked up the contract and tore it down the middle, then again, and let the pieces fall to the sawdust floor. "So I'm going to the forge. And I'm going to renegotiate."

Something flickered across Corvane's ruled-straight face—regret, perhaps, or the closest thing to it a man like him could produce. "Then I'm sorry," he said, rising, gathering his coat. "Truly. You balance numbers beautifully. It's a waste."

At the foot of the stairs he paused, not turning around.

"For what it's worth, the enforcers ride at dawn. The ferry at Duskwater is the only crossing left open, and it will not be open long." He glanced back once, and his eyes were almost kind, which was the cruelest thing about him. "I did try to save you, Miss Blackwood. That will be in my ledger, whatever's in yours."

The door closed behind him. Outside, in the alley, a fox made of banked fire lifted its head from the shadows, embers shifting in its coat like a question.

"We ride tonight," Marguerite said. "We're going to Duskwater."
