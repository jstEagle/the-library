## 7. The Ferry at Duskwater

I had forgotten how the border smelled.

Thirty years is a long time to sleep in banked coals, and a fox forgets things on purpose so he can bear them. But Duskwater came back to me whole: the reek of brackish mud and cold iron, the light gone the color of an old bruise, the river running wide and slow as poured mercury. On the far shore, the Otherworld stood waiting like a held breath. The trees there were still green. That was the only mercy left in it—the green held, barely, the way a wound holds its stitches.

Marguerite saw none of this. She was watching the road behind us.

"They're coming," she said. "I can feel it. Like teeth."

She could feel it because her blood could. Emberwright blood knew when it was hunted; it had always known. I had felt that same awareness shiver through her mother, thirty years ago, on this same stretch of mud, and I had told her then what I told no one: *it's nothing, Wren, just the wind off the water.*

The wind off the water. I have carried that lie in my chest longer than I carried my own ember.

"Down the bank," I said. "Quickly now. She's already waiting—she'll be watching for us."

"Who's she?"

"The ferrywoman." I paused, because the truth deserved at least the shape of itself. "An old friend of your mother's."

That stopped her. It stopped her the way everything about Wren stopped her lately—a door opening onto a room she'd never been allowed to enter. Seven years among ledgers had taught her to keep her face still, but her eyes were another country altogether, and they were burning.

"You knew her," Marguerite said. Not a question. "You knew my mother."

"I did."

"And you let me believe—" Her jaw worked. "You've been leading me toward the forge for days, fox. You've watched me grieve a woman who lied to me, and you said nothing about knowing her."

"She asked me not to tell you." The words came out smaller than I meant them to. "When you were small. She made me promise that if ever I woke, if ever you needed guiding, I would let you come to your own anger. She said borrowed grief was worse than earned grief."

"And did you agree? Or were you simply asleep?"

Both, I wanted to say. Neither. I had been dying in a hearth in the Cinderforge's outer dark, my core guttering, whispering promises to ash. But Marguerite didn't need excuses. Excuses were Corvane's currency, and she'd spent seven years being paid in them.

"I agreed," I said.

Behind us, up the slope, the first lantern-light broke through the alders. Not one lantern. A dozen, strung out along the ridgeline like a rosary of small cold suns. Guild enforcers moved the way guild enforcers always move—in formation, unhurried, certain that the world belongs to their warrant.

I put my shoulder against Marguerite's back and shoved her down the bank.

* * *

Duskwater's ferry was a thing I remembered wrong. In memory it had been a broad-beamed barge with sails of grey silk. What slid out of the reeds to meet us was narrow as a coffin and black as one too, its hull stitched from river-woven willow, its single mast bare. And at the tiller stood the ferrywoman herself.

She was older. Of course she was older. But time had treated her the way the river treats stone—worn her smooth, worn her strange. Her skin was the grey-blue of deep water, and her hair floated around her head as if she were already submerged, though the air was still. When she saw me, her mouth curved without warmth.

"Fox," she said. "You look terrible."

"Maren." My voice cracked on the name. "Maren, you're still here."

"Someone has to hold the crossing." She looked past me to Marguerite, and something moved through her face like a fish under ice—grief, recognition, grief again. "Oh. Oh, you have her chin. You have Wren's whole stubborn chin."

"You knew my mother," Marguerite breathed.

"I poled her across this river twice a year for twenty years." Maren stepped aside from the tiller. "Get in. Both of you. We'll trade histories on the water—we haven't got histories to spare on land."

The enforcers had reached the top of the bank. I heard the wet click of a crossbow being spanned, and then a voice I knew far too well rolled down over the reeds—not Corvane himself; he never dirtied his boots—but his captain, Vess, whose voice was oiled leather and patience.

"By warrant of the Guild of Impossible Things! The woman carrying the Blackwood ledger-seal surrenders herself. The creature accompanying her will be rendered. Resistance will be entered into the record."

*Entered into the record.* As if drowning were bookkeeping. As if the Otherworld's slow death were a column of figures that balanced.

Maren laughed. It was not a kind sound, or a safe one.

"The record," she said, "can float."

She cut the mooring line with a stroke of her poling-knife, and the ferry went out onto the mercury water like a leaf leaving a branch.

* * *

The crossing was long. Crossings are always longer than the distance between shores; that is their nature. The river took us into its middle silence, where the human shore behind us thinned to a smear of lanterns and the green shore ahead thickened by degrees—first the color returning to the world, then the sound, birdsong arriving like memory, then smells I had starved thirty years without: loam, rain-on-stone, the sweet rot of orchard windfalls.

Marguerite stood at the prow with her hand trailing above the water, not touching, and I coiled myself on the thwart beside Maren and tried to find the words I owed her.

There is a particular shame in confessing to someone who does not yet know there is anything to confess. Every minute I waited, the confession grew heavier, and every minute brought us closer to the far shore, after which there would be no more room for it at all.

"Maren," I said quietly. "Tell her."

"Tell me what?" Marguerite turned. The dusk light caught her face. "What happened here? You said this was where—" She stopped. Looked at the water. Looked at me. Emberwright blood is quick; hers arrived at the truth before I finished arranging my cowardice. "This is where she died. Isn't it. My mother died at this crossing."

"Not here," Maren said softly. "On the other side of it. But yes. Here."

And because I was a coward even in the shape of a fox, I let Maren tell it.

"She was coming back from the forge," Maren said. "Reforging season, thirty autumns past. She'd gone alone—I begged her not to—and whatever happened at the Cinderforge, it broke something in her. She came down to my landing bleeding light instead of blood, and she had you in a sling against her chest, and you were screaming, little one. You screamed the whole way across."

"I don't remember any of it."

"You wouldn't. You were two years old." Maren's pole dipped and drew, dipped and drew. "The guild was already on the banks. They'd learned what she intended—the new terms, the shared tending, all of it. Corvane's faction understood exactly what reforging would cost them. So they came to stop her, and they caught her at the crossing."

Marguerite's hand had gone white on the prow. "And Ivo?"

Ah. There it was.

"I was there," I said. My voice came out as smoke, thin and acrid. "I was her bound spirit, Marguerite. Wherever she stood, I was sworn to stand. At this crossing, thirty years ago, I stood between her and twelve enforcers, and I burned. I burned the way I have never burned since—banked fire given open air, everything I had. I dropped three of them into this river. I would have dropped all twelve."

"But?"

"But I am made of ember, and embers die, and I spent myself too fast. I gutted out on this deck, on these very boards, while they took her." The memory rose in me whole, as it always did: Wren turning back, one arm shielding the sling, saying *run, Ivo, run, keep her safe*—and meaning Marguerite, always Marguerite, even at the end. "She ordered me to flee. I obeyed. I have spent thirty years telling myself that obedience was love. It wasn't. It was failure wearing love's coat. She died because I burned out, and you grew up orphaned and lied-to because I obeyed, and I slept in the dark of the forge through all of it, dreaming the same dream: this crossing, this deck, her voice saying *run.*"

The river made its long quiet sounds beneath us.

Marguerite crossed the boat and knelt in front of me. Up close, her eyes were the exact brown of banked coals, and I understood suddenly why the old covenant had chosen our families for each other—because looking into them was like looking into a hearth you had believed cold forever and finding one coal still alive.

"Ivo," she said. "Look at me. If you hadn't fled, I'd be dead too. Whatever else you owe her—you paid part of it. You kept me alive for her."

"It doesn't balance," I whispered. Old ledger-speak, picked up from seven years haunting a counting room. "It doesn't balance."

"No," she agreed. "But debts aren't settled all at once. Ask any ledger-keeper." Her mouth almost smiled. "We pay them forward."

Behind us, the human shore erupted with lantern-light. Vess's enforcers had reached the waterline, and they had not come empty—they had come with the guild's answer to crossings: iron chains and a keel-boat of their own, flat-bottomed, engine-driven, ugly as a sin confessed twice. It slid off the mud and came after us, and the mercury water shuddered under its wake.

Maren sighed the sigh of someone who has been expecting this tide all her life.

"Well," she said. "They were going to make me obsolete eventually."

"What are you doing?" I said, because she had begun unbinding the sail-cleats, and because I knew—some animal knowledge older than my ember knew—what she was doing.

"A ferrywoman has one great working," she said. "One. We save it for the crossing we mean to keep forever. Mine was always going to be spent keeping this one." She looked at Marguerite, and her floating hair stirred in a wind that touched no one else. "Your mother paid me half in advance, child, thirty years ago. She said: *when the heir comes back, see her safe to the green shore.* I'm merely settling accounts."

"Maren—" I started forward.

"Hold the fox back," she said pleasantly, "or I'll do both workings at once and spoil the mathematics."

The guild boat was fifty yards off. Thirty. I could hear Vess shouting orders, could smell the iron chains. Marguerite grabbed my scruff with both hands, and I let her, because there are moments when the only obedience left is the kind that breaks you.

Maren drove her pole into the river's heart and spoke a word in the old tongue, the tongue the water remembers.

Duskwater stood up.

Not a wave—a rising, the whole slow mile of the river gathering itself like a muscle, and the guild boat lifted and rolled and was swallowed, lanterns and chains and shouting, all of it folded under with hardly a splash. But workings take what they take, and the water took its due: Maren's knees, then her voice, then her outline, the grey-blue of her washing outward into the grey-blue of the river until there was no seam between ferrywoman and flood.

Her last act was small. The pole drifted free, and before it sank, it swung once—deliberately—pointing at the green shore. Pointing home.

Marguerite knelt at the gunwale with her fists clenched, watching the water where a woman had been, and I pressed my hot forehead against her cold hands, and together we kept the only vigil the drowned ever ask for: we stayed, and we remembered, and we did not look away.

* * *

The green shore received us in silence. Reeds parted; soil firmed underfoot; the trees closed behind us like a door. Somewhere ahead, faint as a pulse, the Cinderforge's heat beat against the dark, and above it the sky showed a thin rind of moon—one night, perhaps two, until equinox.

Marguerite stood a long moment facing the trees. Then she bent, took up Maren's fallen pole where it had washed ashore after all, and shouldered it like a soldier takes up a standard.

"She gave us the crossing," Marguerite said. "So the debt moves forward. All of them do—from her, from my mother, from you. Everything anyone ever paid for me goes into that forge tomorrow." She looked at me, and the coals in her eyes were banked no longer. "Come on, fox. Let's go make it worth the price."

And far behind us, across the risen-and-settled river, lanterns were regathering on the human shore—more lanterns than before, a whole constellation of them, moving with terrible patience toward the one place in either world where the guild could not afford to let an Emberwright stand.

Corvane was coming to the forge.
