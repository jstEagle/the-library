## 8. The Cinderforge

The mountain had a wound in its side, and the wound was breathing.

Marguerite felt it before she saw it—a warmth on her face like an open oven door, though the pass around them was all frost and grey scree. The air smelled of iron and something older beneath it, something like the moment before a struck match catches. Ivo trotted ahead of her, his ember-fur dimmed to a sullen orange in the daylight, and where his paws touched stone, the frost retreated in small melting halos.

*There,* he said, without turning. His voice in her head had become as familiar as her own heartbeat, though it still startled her sometimes—smoke and banked coals and something almost tender. *Do you feel it?*

"I feel it." She pulled her cloak tighter. "It feels like it knows I'm coming."

*It does. It has been waiting thirty years for an Emberwright to climb this road.* A pause, and then, quieter: *It has been waiting longer than that for one to leave it again.*

The road narrowed to a shelf of black rock above a valley of dead pines—trees the covenant's failure had bleached white as bone, standing in their thousands like a congregation struck silent mid-prayer. Marguerite had stopped flinching at such sights days ago. That was its own kind of grief, she thought: how quickly the dying world became ordinary.

Then the shelf turned, and the Cinderforge opened before her, and she flinched after all.

The mountain's face was split by a cavern mouth tall as a cathedral door, its edges fused smooth as poured glass. Light came from within—not firelight, not quite, but a deep arterial glow, red shading into gold shading into white. Heat rolled out of it in slow tides, and with each tide came a sound. At first she took it for wind. Then she understood it was breathing. The mountain drew air in through the wound and pushed it out again, slow and vast, and beneath the breath was a second sound: a ringing, faint and endless, like a hammer falling somewhere very far below and never quite landing.

"The forge is still lit," she said. "After all this time."

*The forge does not go out,* Ivo said. *That is rather the point of it. The covenant was forged here, and the fire that made it has been holding the shape of an agreement that no longer exists. Imagine holding a door open for thirty years for someone who never comes.* His ears flattened. *Imagine being blamed for the door.*

She glanced at him. He had been strange since Duskwater—quieter, his fire burning lower, the ferrywoman's drowning sitting in him like a stone in a streambed. She hadn't pressed. Some debts you let a person carry until they're ready to set them down.

They climbed the last stretch together, and the heat closed over them like water.

* * *

Inside, the cavern was a cathedral that had been built by fire rather than hands. Stalactites of glittering slag hung from the ceiling like the pipes of some impossible organ. The floor was veined with channels of slow-moving gold, cooling and reheating in rhythm with the mountain's breath. And at the center, sunk into the rock like a heart in a chest, was the forge itself: a basin of white fire perhaps ten feet across, burning without fuel, without smoke, without any sound at all—which somehow made it louder than everything.

Three figures stood around it.

They were not quite people. They were shaped like people the way a candle is shaped like a flame—suggestively, approximately. Their bodies were slag and cooling metal, their faces smooth as unstruck coins. When the nearest one turned toward Marguerite, she saw her own reflection swim briefly across its surface and vanish.

*The forge-wardens,* Ivo said. His voice had gone very careful. *Mind what you promise them. They cannot lie, but they are very good at the truth.*

"You brought an heir." The warden's voice was the ringing of the far hammer, made into words. "Thirty years, and you bring an heir."

"I didn't bring her," Ivo said aloud, and Marguerite heard the old formality drop back into his speech like a mask settling. "She came. I only kept her alive on the road, which was more than I managed for the last one."

The wardens did not look at him. All three faces turned to Marguerite, and she felt the map in her satchel go warm—warm, then hot, then almost painfully alive against her hip, as if it had come home.

"Marguerite Blackwood," the first warden said.

"Marguerite Emberwright," said the second.

"Both names are true," said the third. "That is why you can hear us. That is why the fire knows your step. Ask what you have climbed a mountain to ask."

Marguerite's mouth was dry. She made herself step forward, to the edge of the channels of gold, close enough that the white fire painted her shadow huge against the cavern wall.

"The covenant," she said. "The map led me here. Ivo says I can reforge it. Tell me how."

The wardens were silent for the length of three of the mountain's slow breaths.

"The covenant is a chain with two anchors," the first said. "One anchor stands in this world. One stands in the Other. Between them, the chain is made of agreement—of promise, renewed. When the promise is renewed, both realms breathe together. When it fails, each realm forgets the other, and what is forgotten begins to die."

"I know that part. I've seen what forgetting does. Saltmarket. The orchards. The changelings." Her voice cracked on the last word and she steadied it. "Tell me the terms. Tell me what renewal costs."

The second warden raised a hand of dark metal and gestured at the white fire.

"The covenant was forged in the first Emberwright's blood," it said. "Not spilled. Given. Freely, fully, with knowledge of the price. A life freely given is the only metal strong enough to hold two worlds together. Every renewal since has demanded the same. An heir of the line. A life, freely given, into the fire. This is the term. It has always been the term."

The words went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor.

She had known, of course, in the way you know a thing in dreams and refuse it in waking. Ivo had been strange about the forge. The wardens had been waiting thirty years. Her mother had come here. Her mother had never come back.

"No," she said. "No. There's another way. There has to be. My mother—"

"Your mother stood where you stand," the third warden said, without cruelty, which was worse. "Nineteen years ago, at the autumn equinox. She was told the terms. She had an infant daughter hidden in the human realm, and a mistress's promise that the child would be kept safe, and a spirit bound to her blood who had failed her once already at the crossing."

Marguerite heard Ivo make a sound behind her—small, involuntary, like a coal collapsing.

"She refused," the warden said. "Not from cowardice. From love. She would not leave her daughter alone in the world. She turned from the fire and went down the mountain to find another way, and the breach widened, and the Otherworld began to die in earnest, and on the road home she was found by those who profited from the breach." A pause, the length of one hammer-fall from very far below. "She did not die here. But she died because she came here. The distinction matters to the dead, we find. It rarely matters to the living."

The cavern tilted. Marguerite realized she was on her knees in the channel-warm stone and did not remember falling.

*Marguerite.* Ivo's voice, very gentle. *I would have told you—*

"You didn't." She didn't look at him. She couldn't. "You let me walk up this mountain thinking there was a way through. Thinking I was the answer." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "I am the answer. That's the joke, isn't it. I'm the fuel."

* * *

Afterward—she could not have said how long after—she sat at the cavern's mouth with her knees drawn up, watching the valley of dead white trees go gold and then grey as the sun moved. The map lay open in her lap. It had stopped resisting her days ago; now it simply lay there, its impossible roads quiet, as if it too were waiting for her to decide.

Behind her, the forge breathed. The wardens stood their silent watch. Somewhere in the deep of the mountain, the far hammer fell and fell.

*The equinox is tomorrow at dusk,* Ivo said. He had settled a few feet away, his fire banked so low he was barely more than a fox-shaped shadow with two embers for eyes. *I want to be honest with you, and honesty is not a thing I have been good at. So. All of it, now, and then you may hate me with the full account in hand.*

She said nothing. He took the silence for leave.

*Your mother asked me to carry her across Duskwater the night she fled the forge. I was young, for my kind. Proud. I told her the crossing was safe. It was not—the enforcers were already watching the ferry, and I chose a crossing I could survive, and she nearly did not. She made it. Barely. And in the water, holding her, I felt what she had decided: that she would rather the worlds fray than give you up. And I—* his fire guttered, brightened— *I agreed with her. I thought she was brave. I spent thirty years asleep, and the world frayed, and I woke to find her daughter climbing the same mountain, and I told myself I would not fail her twice. Which is why I said nothing about the terms. I thought—* a long pause— *I thought if you knew, you would refuse. As she refused. And I would watch the worlds die knowing I had helped choose it twice.*

Marguerite looked at him. The little ember eyes looked back.

"You should have told me," she said.

*Yes.*

"You let me climb here thinking I was carrying a map. I was carrying my own death in my satchel."

*Yes.*

She turned back to the valley. Below, so far down they were specks, she could see the road they'd climbed—and, far along it, a thread of torchlight moving in the dusk. Too disciplined for travelers. Too many for merchants.

She was too hollow to feel fear. She noted it the way she'd once noted discrepancies in ledgers: *enforcers on the road, approximately forty; arrival, by morning; response, none available.*

"Corvane," she said.

*He will not let you reach the equinox,* Ivo said. *If he cannot have the map, he will make certain no one reforges anything. And the wardens will not fight for you. The forge-wardens do not fight. They only hold the door.*

Marguerite looked down at her hands. Her mother's hands—she could see it now, the long fingers, the burn-scar on the left thumb she'd always assumed came from a kitchen. Everything she had was written on her already. She had spent seven years believing herself safely invisible, a girl made of ledger-ink, and the whole time she had been a wick waiting for a flame.

An heir's life, freely given. The fire was warm on her face, patient as a creditor.

"I spent my whole life balancing other people's accounts," she said quietly. "Debts I never ran up. Debts I didn't know I'd inherited." She closed the map. "Tomorrow at dusk, Corvane comes through that valley, and the equinox comes over the mountain, and I am supposed to lie down on the scale and pay for two worlds with the only thing that's ever been mine."

*Marguerite.* Ivo rose, his fire lifting, his voice rough. *You do not have to—*

"Don't." She stood. Her legs shook and held. "Don't tell me what I don't have to do. Not you. Not tonight."

She looked at the white fire, and the fire looked back, and far below in the gathering dark the torches of Corvane's enforcers came on up the road like a lit fuse burning toward her.

"But I'll tell you this," Marguerite said, to the fire, to the wardens, to the fox made of banked flame, to her dead mother, to anyone listening. "I've read the old terms. And I've read a great many contracts in my time. And I have never yet met a contract that couldn't be rewritten—by whoever was brave enough, or desperate enough, to hold the pen."

The far hammer fell. The mountain breathed.

Somewhere below, the torches came on through the night.
