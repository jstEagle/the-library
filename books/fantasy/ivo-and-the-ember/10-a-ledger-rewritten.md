## 10. A Ledger Rewritten

The forge went quiet the way a heart does after grief—slowly, then all at once.

Marguerite stood in the cooling light of the Cinderforge, hammer still in her hand, and listened to the new covenant settle into the world. It did not sound like thunder or trumpets. It sounded like a ledger closing: a soft, final *thump* of weight set down after years of being carried wrong.

The metal she had written upon hung above the anvil now, no longer a plate but something like a window—or a wound that had learned to heal. Through it she could see both worlds at once: the ash-grey slopes of the Otherworld on one side, and beyond the forge's broken doors, the human autumn, gold and ordinary and unbearably dear. Between them ran the line she had cut with Ivo's ember-core and her own blood: *the burden shared, the debt renewed each year by tending, not by dying.*

The forge-wardens had not spoken since. They stood in their alcoves like statues that had forgotten they were statues, their stone faces turned toward the burning terms, and if stone could weep, Marguerite thought theirs might have tried.

*Ivo.*

She turned too fast and nearly fell. The last day was catching up with her all at once—the ferry, Duskwater's grey water, the enforcers' torches, Corvane's face when the hammer came down on terms he had spent thirty years making sure no one would ever write.

Ivo lay where the forge had thrown him.

He was small. That was the first thing she understood, and the thing that frightened her most. The fox who had torn through six guild enforcers on the Saltmarket road, who had been made of banked fire and old fury, was now scarcely bigger than a cat, his coat gone from living ember to the dull red of coals an hour after the fire dies. His sides moved. Barely.

"Ivo." She knelt, and the cold of the forge floor bit through her skirts, and she did not care. "No. No, you don't get to do this. You fed the whole *core*, you absolute creature, you were supposed to keep some back—"

His eye opened. One ember, banked low but not out.

"You," he said, and his voice was smoke and gravel and something underneath it that had never once, in all their weeks together, been afraid until now, "always did talk to ledgers like they could argue back."

"They can. I've heard them." Her hands were shaking. She pressed them into his fur anyway, expecting heat and finding only warmth, the ordinary warmth of a living thing, and somehow that was worse and better at once. "Stay. That's an order. You're not bound anymore—I broke the binding, I rewrote it, there's nothing left to hold you here—so you have to *choose* it, do you understand? Choose to stay. Choose it now."

A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter could be wrung through exhaustion. "Thirty years asleep in a fox's shape because your mother ordered me to run. Thirty years, and the first Emberwright to wake me spends her inheritance telling me what to do."

"It's a family tradition."

"Marguerite." He lifted his head—an effort she watched him pay for—and looked at her with both eyes now, and the second one opened slow as dawn. "I was going to stay before you asked. I stayed for your grandmother. I stayed for your mother, though I failed her. I am not bound to your blood anymore." His tail curled, with great dignity, over her wrist. "I am simply yours. There is a difference. It matters."

She bent her head so he wouldn't see her face, which fooled neither of them, and stayed like that while the forge cooled around them and the covenant hummed its quiet arithmetic overhead: two worlds, one debt, paid in small installments forever.

* * *

They buried the ferrywoman at Duskwater three days later, on the human side of the crossing, under a rowan tree that had begun—since the equinox—to flower out of season.

Her name had been Brigit Ondine, though everyone had called her Ferry, and the moth-winged scribe Sable had come all the way from Saltmarket to stand at the graveside with ink-stained fingers laced together and wings folded tight with grief. Half of Saltmarket seemed to have come, actually: the orchard-keeper whose trees had budded green again overnight; the changeling family who had been turned away from the market gates in spring and stood now among the mourners with their odd, beautiful faces bare; the harbor-master, who kept taking off his hat and putting it back on.

"She held the boat against six armed men," Sable said quietly, beside Marguerite. "For you. For all of us, though we didn't know it yet."

"For all of us," Marguerite agreed. "That's rather the point of everything now, isn't it."

She had written the eulogy herself, in the careful hand of a woman who had spent seven years balancing accounts that could not be balanced, and she read it aloud without her voice breaking until the very last line:

*"Debt incurred: one life, freely given, holding a door open. Debt owed: everything. Payment schedule: ongoing, by all parties, in perpetuity."*

Sable laughed wetly at that, and then several other people did, and it was somehow exactly right—a funeral where the mourners smiled through tears, because the woman in the ground would have appreciated good bookkeeping even in grief.

Afterward, walking back along the river with Ivo trotting at her heel—he could manage perhaps two miles a day now, and pretended it was a choice—she stopped at the water's edge.

"I'm going back," she said. "To the guildhall."

Ivo's ears pricked. "Corvane's guildhall."

"Corvane is in irons awaiting trial, and half the senior guildmasters resigned the night the sky over the border turned visible to everyone." She had felt it happen from the forge: the veil between worlds, thinned to gauze by decades of breach, suddenly *legible*. Humans in three kingdoms had woken to foxfire in their hedgerows and songs coming from hills that had been silent for a century. The Guild of Impossible Things could no longer pretend the impossible wasn't real. "Someone has to decide what the guild becomes. And I've decided it's me. Or—" she corrected herself, hearing her mistress's voice in the old habit of self-erasure, and refusing it, "—it's *us*. If you're willing."

"I told you at the forge."

"You told me you'd stay. You didn't say anything about paperwork."

Ivo sat down in the river-mud with the affronted dignity of his kind. "There will be *paperwork*?"

"There is always paperwork. That's how you know a thing is real." She crouched and scratched him behind the ears, feeling the faint warmth still banked in him, diminished and enduring. "Seven years I hid in those counting rooms believing I was safe because I was nobody. I'm done being nobody. But I'm also done being the last of anything. Last heir, last hope, last sacrifice—those are just lonelinesses wearing crowns. What I want to be is the *first* of something instead."

"And what's that?"

"The first keeper of a ledger that tells the truth."

* * *

The Guildhall of Impossible Things smelled the same as it always had: candlewax, dust, ink, and the ghost of ten thousand transactions. But it looked different to her now, the way a childhood home looks different when you return knowing what happened in its rooms. Here was the corridor where she'd carried ledgers invisible as smoke. Here was the counting room where a dying courier had bled out onto the floorboards, pressing a scorched map into her hands. The stain had been scrubbed away. She stood on the spot anyway, for a moment, and let herself remember him—nameless, brave, the first stranger to see her—and then she walked past it, into the Hall of Records, where the guild's oldest and largest ledgers slept in iron cabinets.

Guildmaster Corvane's own cabinet stood open now, sealed with the trial commission's wax. Inside lay the true books: the ones that recorded what the guild had taken from the Otherworld for thirty years. Tithes diverted. Crossings sold. Changelings traded. A covenant allowed to rot because rot was profitable.

Marguerite did not burn them, though she thought about it. Burning was easy, and she was done with easy sacrifices. Instead she had them copied, every entry, and the copies distributed to both realms—because debts could not be repaid until they were honestly stated, and honesty began with the record.

Then she cleared a desk by the eastern window, where the morning light came in, and set upon it a brand-new ledger.

It was bound in grey leather, unremarkable, except that its clasp was forged from a sliver of the covenant-metal itself, and it opened only to her hand and to hands she trusted. On the first page, beneath the date, she wrote in her smallest, clearest script:

*A Ledger of Debts Owed Between Worlds, and How They Are Paid.*

*Entry the First:* In memory of Brigit Ondine, called Ferry, who gave her life holding the door at Duskwater. Debt: incalculable. First payment rendered: her grave flowers out of season; her crossing kept free for all folk, of either realm, forever. Status: *being paid, daily.*

*Entry the Second:* To the changelings of Saltmarket, wrongfully barred. Debt: seven years of exile. Payment: the market gates stand open; the guild funds their passage home. Status: *in progress.*

*Entry the Third:* To the Otherworld entire, for thirty years of theft. Debt: to be assessed jointly each year at the equinox, by delegates of both realms, at the Cinderforge. Status: *first assessment convened.*

She wrote until the light went gold and then blue, and Sable arrived with supper and gossip and the news that three more towns wanted crossings opened, and Ivo curled on the warm stones of the hearth pretending to sleep while contributing dry commentary on everything, and the guildhall filled up around her with people who saw her—not the invisible girl, not the last heir, just Marguerite, keeper of the ledger, tired and certain.

Near midnight, alone again, she dipped her pen one final time.

*Entry the Fourth:* To myself, Marguerite Blackwood, Emberwright, from the girl who hid in the counting room. Debt: seven years of safety purchased with erasure. Payment: none required. The debt is forgiven. She was doing the best she could with what she knew. Status: *closed.*

She blotted the page and sat back, and found Ivo watching her from the hearth with both embers lit.

"Well?" he said. "Is it finished?"

Marguerite looked at the ledger—at all the blank pages waiting behind the fourth entry, years and years of them, debts and payments and the long patient work of two worlds learning to share—and she smiled.

"No," she said, and turned to a fresh page. "It's just beginning."
