## 1. Charms for a City That Forbids Them

The morning fog came off the harbor the way it always did in Saltmere — slow and gray and smelling of brine and old rope — and by the time Xanthe Blackwood had unfolded their stall's awning, the fog had already begun its daily retreat, pulling back from the cobbles like a tide that had forgotten what it was reaching for.

Xanthe worked with their sleeves pushed up, arranging the day's wares on a plank table worn smooth by seven years of elbows. The law said no charms. The law had said so for seven years now, ever since the Council of Nine declared that magic was a public hazard, that knots tied with intent were fraud at best and sedition at worst, and that anyone caught selling one would lose their license, their stall, and — if they were unlucky, which in Saltmere was a professional condition — their hands. So Xanthe did not sell charms. Xanthe sold trinkets.

The trinkets were laid out in careful rows: braided cords in harbor colors, blue and green and the muddy gold of lamp-oil; little wooden fish with painted eyes; glass beads that caught the fog-light and held it; knotted keychains advertised, in chalk on a slate, as *DECORATIVE ONLY*. Each row was arranged with the precision of a person who had once been taught that arrangement itself was a kind of prayer. Each cord was braided three-over-two, each knot seated with a thumbnail's pressure, each bead threaded in an order that looked random and was not.

A city that forbids magic does not, as it turns out, forbid craft. It only forbids admitting what the craft is for.

"Morning, Blackwood." Old Sefa from the chandlery next door leaned over the partition between their stalls, her gray braid swinging, her breath already smelling of the tea she drank too hot and too fast. "You're early."

"I'm always early."

"You're always early because you can't sleep." Sefa said it without malice, the way you'd note the weather. "Which is different from being early."

Xanthe didn't argue. There was no point arguing with Sefa, who had known them since before the ban, since the guildhall on Lantern Row had been allowed to have a sign out front, since the word *guild* could be spoken above a whisper. Those days were gone the way the sign was gone — pried off the wall one night by men with crowbars and official-looking paper, the nails left behind like teeth.

Xanthe poured two cups of tea from the dented kettle they kept on a brick of coals and handed one across the partition. This, too, was habit. Seven years of habits, stacked like the stones of a seawall: tea for Sefa, awning up before the fog lifted, first knot of the day tied before the first customer arrived, because a stall with nothing finished on it invited questions, and questions were more dangerous than any inspector.

The first knot of the day took eleven minutes. Xanthe did it with their eyes half on the harbor road, counting the rhythm of the waves against the pilings — in on four, hold on two, out on six — letting the rope find its own tension under their fingers. A luck-knot, properly made, was not tied. It was *listened into being*. You gave the cord your attention and your breath and a small, deliberate wish, and the cord remembered all three, and then whoever wore it carried those things against their skin until the cord wore through.

None of this was written anywhere anymore. The guild's books had been burned or buried or sold to collectors who thought they were buying folk art. What remained lived in Xanthe's hands, and in Merrin's, and in the muscle-memory of perhaps a dozen others scattered through Saltmere like seeds in a crack. The Guild of Knots and Small Mercies — that had been the old name, embroidered on banners nobody dared hang — persisted now as a rumor of good string.

At half past eight, the dockworkers came up from the night shift, and with them came Brannick.

Brannick was a big man, big in the way of men who load ships for a living — shoulders like cargo, forearms mapped with old rope-burns — and he walked with the particular gait of someone whose feet hurt and whose pride would not permit him to say so. He stopped at the stall the way he always stopped, pretending to browse, picking up a wooden fish and putting it down, lifting a glass bead to the light as though appraising it for his nonexistent wife.

"The blue ones," he said finally. "You got any more of the blue?"

"Got these." Xanthe lifted a small tray. On it lay three cords, each ending in a knot the size of a walnut, dyed the deep uncertain blue of deep water. To any passerby they were decorative. To Brannick they were the reason he was still alive, though he'd never once said so out loud, and neither had Xanthe.

"How much?"

"For you? Six coppers."

"They were five last month."

"Inflation," Xanthe said, which was true of everything in Saltmere lately, and also a lie, because the price had gone up because Brannick's ship was going farther north this season, into water where the currents had turned strange, and strange water required better knots, and better knots cost more breath. But you couldn't say that. You couldn't say *this one is tied against drowning* in a city where saying such a thing was a crime. You said *six coppers*, and you let the man do his own arithmetic of faith.

Brannick counted the coins out one at a time, pressing each into Xanthe's palm, and Xanthe pressed each back down into his hand — all but one, which stayed. This was the oldest part of the trade, older than the guild, older than the ban: you never took the last coin whole. Something had to stay behind, something given freely, or the charm was just string. Most customers never noticed. Brannick had noticed once, years ago, and had asked, and Xanthe had said, *It's how I make sure you come back,* and Brannick had laughed, and that was the closest either of them had ever come to speaking plainly.

He looped the cord over his head and tucked the knot inside his shirt, against the sternum, where the skin was thinnest and the heart kept its time. He'd been doing it for six years. Two wrecks, one squall, one season when fever went through the crews like fire through dry grass, and Brannick had walked out of all of it. His crewmates had started calling him the Lucky Bastard of Pier Four, and some of them bought wooden fish, and some of them bought beads, and none of them knew why the fish were carved facing left instead of right, or why the beads were strung in sevens, or why the cords were never cut with a blade but always broken by hand.

"Going north," Brannick said, settling the knot against his chest. "Past the Shelf. They say the water up there's gone wrong. Currents running backward. My brother-in-law swears he saw a wave stand still."

"Waves don't stand still."

"No," Brannick agreed. "They don't." He looked at Xanthe with the flat, level look of a man asking a question he already knew the answer to and wanted confirmed anyway. "Anything I should know?"

Xanthe met his eyes. Around them the market went on — Sefa haggling over tallow, a fishwife singing her prices, children weaving between the stalls stealing warmth rather than goods, because there was nothing worth stealing and everyone knew everyone. Ordinary morning. Ordinary light. Somewhere beyond the fog, beyond the harbor bar, beyond the border wards that ringed the city like a second set of walls — wards nobody talked about anymore, wards that simply *were*, the way the tide was, the way the stars were — the world continued.

"Keep the knot dry," Xanthe said. "And come home."

"That it?"

"That's everything I've got."

Brannick grinned, clapped the counter twice — a sound like a small heartbeat — and walked down toward Pier Four, where the *Maren Lee* sat low in the water, loaded for the north. Xanthe watched him go the way they watched every one of them go: with a smile on their face and a fist closing slowly around their own heart. Because that was the trade now. That was all of it. You put seven years of dead craft into a walnut-sized knot, you charged six coppers, you kept one coin, and you sent a man out onto water that was going wrong, and you loved him the only way the law permitted, which was anonymously, commercially, deniably.

Love disguised as commerce. Survival disguised as habit. The guild reduced to a stall, a kettle, a slate that read DECORATIVE ONLY.

Xanthe exhaled and got back to work.

* * *

The middle of the day passed in the ordinary arithmetic of the market. A grandmother bought a cord for a grandson starting his apprenticeship — *for luck, dear, obviously, what else* — and Xanthe tied it while she told a story about her late husband that had nothing to do with anything and everything to do with why she'd really come. A girl of maybe nine spent four minutes agonizing over glass beads and finally chose the one the color of shallow water over sand, and Xanthe threaded it on a leather string and charged her nothing, because some customers paid in other currencies, and a child walking home wearing something made specially for her was a ward of a kind no inspector had ever thought to ban. A customs officer strolled the row, unhurried, bored, pausing at the stall long enough to pick up a wooden fish and examine it for signs of intent. Xanthe held their breath without appearing to. The officer turned the fish over, found the maker's mark — a small stamped wheel, meaningless now, once the seal of the Guild of Knots and Small Mercies — and set it back down, having decided, apparently, that a fish was a fish.

When he was gone, Xanthe discovered their hands were shaking, and made tea, and waited for the shaking to stop, and it did, mostly.

This was the shape of the days. Had been, for seven years. Fear in the mornings, work at noon, and in the evenings — in the hour when the lamps were lit and the fog came back in off the water like a debt collector — the walk home to Lantern Row, where the guildhall stood with its empty nail-holes and its roof patched with whatever slate was cheapest, where Merrin kept the last ledger locked in a box under the floorboards, and where the apprentices' room stood empty except for dust.

Empty. It had been empty for seven years exactly, though Xanthe had stopped marking the date after the third year, because marking it meant standing in that room, and standing in that room meant remembering a boy who used to fall asleep mid-sentence with a half-knotted cord still looped around his fingers, and remembering was a luxury the days did not allow.

Ivo. Even the name had become contraband, though no law said so. Xanthe simply didn't say it. Not to Sefa, not to Merrin, not to the dark ceiling of their room at night. Some griefs you keep the way you keep a coal alive: cupped, covered, fed in secret, never shown to the wind.

* * *

Evening came early that day, the way it had been coming early all season, though the calendar insisted otherwise. By the time Xanthe packed the stall — cords rolled, beads jarred, slate wiped to blankness, the wooden fish facing left again in their box — the light had gone thin and strange, the color of old pewter, and the fog had returned with an appetite. Sefa shuttered her chandlery and grumbled about the cold. The fishwives packed early. The harbor grew quiet in the way harbors do when the men who work them feel something in the air they can't name and don't want to.

Xanthe broke down the awning, folded the table legs, and stood a moment at the edge of the market row, looking out at the water.

The harbor was wrong tonight. Xanthe had grown up on this water, had learned to read it before they learned letters, and the water was telling them something they couldn't parse. The tide was slack, but not resting-slack — *held*-slack, as if something below were gripping it. The gulls had gone inland hours ago, all at once, without the usual argument. And the fog — the fog was moving against the wind. Gently. Barely. But moving.

Old instinct, guild-trained, rose in Xanthe like water in a well: *when the small signs move wrong, check the great ones.*

So Xanthe looked up.

The sky over Saltmere was clearing in patches, as it did most evenings, torn open by the land breeze into ragged windows of darkening blue. Through one of those windows, low over the eastern hills, a handful of stars should have been showing — the Harrow, the Shepherd's Lamp, the bright cold eye of Elluin that sailors steered by and had steered by for as long as there had been sailors.

The window of sky was empty.

Not clouded. Empty. Xanthe stared, and their mind performed the small treacheries minds perform — *it's haze, it's the fog, it's your eyes, you're tired, you haven't slept properly in weeks* — and then a second patch of sky tore open to the south, higher, cleaner, and it was empty too, and a third to the west, and the emptiness moved with the tears in the cloud, patient and total, and Xanthe felt something in their chest turn over with the slow, sick certainty of a lock clicking shut.

Because Xanthe Blackwood wore the guild's marks. Had worn them since childhood, inked and knotted into the skin beneath both wrists — protective sigils, ward-sigils, the old binding-work that tied every guild member into the border wards of Saltmere, so that the city's protection and the guild's protection were one protection, one weave, one promise. The sigils had hummed faintly all their life, a sensation so constant it had become silence, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Just now, beneath the left wrist, the humming had changed pitch.

Just now, beneath the right wrist, it had stopped.

Xanthe stood very still at the edge of the emptying market, one hand closed around the strap of their pack, the other rising slowly, unconsciously, to press against their own pulse. Around them, Saltmere went about its evening — lamps lit, doors latched, a dog barking two streets over, Sefa calling some last remark across the closing row. Nobody else had looked up. Nobody else had felt the silence spreading under their skin like cold water finding the seams of a hull.

But Xanthe had spent a lifetime learning to listen to knots, and a knot, they had always taught the apprentices — had always taught *him* — was just a promise holding its own tail. And every promise in the city, every thread of the great ward-weave that had stood over Saltmere for longer than the Council, longer than the crown, longer than memory —

— every one of them was beginning, thread by thread by thread, to let go.

The last lamp in the market row guttered. The fog pressed in. And far overhead, through the widest tear in the clouds, the place where Elluin's star should have burned showed nothing at all — not dimness, not occlusion, but absence, clean and deliberate, like a coin lifted from a purse.

Like a payment collected.

Xanthe's mouth went dry. Their heart was hammering now, and beneath their right wrist the dead sigil lay cold and quiet as a stone at the bottom of a well, and somewhere in the marrow-deep places where the guild's old knowledge still lived, a single phrase surfaced, rising through seven years of silence like a body rising through black water:

*A debt called due comes due all at once.*

They should go home. They should run to Lantern Row, wake Merrin, open the floorboard, pull out the ledger, read the last page they had never been allowed to read. Every instinct screamed it. Instead Xanthe stood in the fog a moment longer, looking up at the starless tear in the sky, and thought — with a tenderness so weary it barely qualified as feeling anymore — of a boy asleep with a half-knotted cord around his fingers, seven years gone, and of the exact number of nights it had been.

Seven years.

The number landed like a knot seating home.

Xanthe shouldered their pack and started for Lantern Row, fast, through a city whose stars had just gone out.
