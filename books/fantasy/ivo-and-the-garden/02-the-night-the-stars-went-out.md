## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The fog had swallowed the harbor whole, and Xanthe was three streets from the docks when the sky broke.

There was no sound. That was the first wrongness — the second being that Xanthe felt it before they saw it, a sensation like a stitch pulled tight behind the breastbone, like a charm-knot cinching shut around the whole of their chest. They stopped in the middle of Saltwife Lane with a luck-knot still warm in their pocket from the dockworker, and they looked up.

The stars were gone.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Gone* — every one of them at once, as if a hand had passed across the bowl of heaven and swept it clean. The fog glowed faintly where lantern-light caught it, but above the low roofs there was nothing. No Pole Star to steer by. No Sisters trailing the horizon. Not even the thin scatter of far lights that sailors swore by and poets lied about. The dark up there was total, and it pressed down on the city like a lid.

Around Xanthe, the street reacted in pieces. A woman dropped a basket of eels; they slapped and writhed on the cobbles, silver in the lamplight. Somewhere a dog began to howl, then another, then a chorus of them, a ragged choir rising from every quarter of Saltmist. A man leaned out of an upper window and shouted something about an eclipse, and someone else shouted back that eclipses did not take *everything*, you fool, look up, *look* —

Xanthe looked. And while they looked, the sigils died.

They had carried the guild's marks on their skin for twenty years: the warding knot inked inside the left wrist in blue-black, the road-sigil at the ankle, the small protective loop over the heart that apprentices received at twelve and never spoke of because speaking of it felt like tempting fate. Merrin had drawn that one herself, her thumb steady, her voice rough with a tenderness she only permitted in ritual: *There. Now nothing comes for you in the dark without paying toll.*

Xanthe felt all of them go at once.

It was not pain, exactly. It was the absence of a pressure they had stopped noticing decades ago — like a river suddenly ceasing to press against a dam, like a hand that had rested on their shoulder since childhood lifting away. The ink on their wrist went cold, then colder, then merely ink. The sigil over their heart flickered once, twice, like a lamp running out of oil, and guttered out. Xanthe pressed their palm flat against their own chest and felt nothing there but heartbeat and cloth.

Every charm-seller in the city would be feeling the same thing tonight. Every hedge-witch, every knot-worker, every smuggler of small magics hiding their craft inside trinkets and superstition. The guild's protection had been woven into all of them, a great quiet web stretched across Saltmist and beyond, out to the border stones, out to the wards that kept the old things in the deep places and the deep things in the old places.

And now the web was gone, and everything it had held back knew it.

Xanthe stood very still in the middle of the lane while the city panicked around them, and let the dread come, and let it sharpen.

Because this was not how accidents happened.

Accidents were local. Accidents were a ward failing at one corner, a sigil smudged, a knot tied by tired hands slipping loose in one place while the rest held. Accidents had edges. This — this was every star extinguished in a single breath, every sigil dead in a single heartbeat, the whole fabric cut not torn, *cut*, with shears, cleanly, deliberately, along lines that had been drawn long ago by someone who knew exactly where the threads ran.

This was a debt called due.

Xanthe had spent their life among charms and contracts; they knew what a called debt looked like. It looked like this. It looked like precision dressed as catastrophe. Somewhere, something had counted seven years to the night, and had reached out with a ledger of its own, and had collected.

*Seven years.* The number again, arriving unbidden, seating itself home like a bead sliding down a knotted cord. Seven years since Ivo. Seven years since the boy with quick fingers and a laugh like a struck bell had walked out of the guildhall on an errand and simply never walked back, and the searchers had found no body, no blood, no trace, and Merrin had gone gray and silent and had forbidden — *forbidden*, with a look Xanthe had never seen on her before — any mention of his name in the hall again.

Seven years of small protections, holding. And now, on the exact anniversary, nothing.

They should go home. They should run to Lantern Row, wake Merrin, open the floorboard, pull out the ledger, read the last page they had never been allowed to read. Every instinct screamed it. Instead Xanthe stood in the fog a moment longer, looking up at the starless tear in the sky, and thought — with a tenderness so weary it barely qualified as feeling anymore — of a boy asleep with a half-knotted cord around his fingers, seven years gone, and of the exact number of nights it had been.

Seven years.

The number landed like a knot seating home.

Xanthe shouldered their pack and started for Lantern Row, fast, through a city whose stars had just gone out.

* * *

Lantern Row earned its name honestly, or had, once. Even now, in a starless world, the row burned bright — every window lit, every door flung open, people standing in the streets holding candles up as if light could be argued with, as if the dark could be negotiated down. Xanthe moved through them like a fish through reeds, hood up, face turned from the crowds. Grief made people grabby. Fear made them worse. A charm-seller abroad on the worst night in living memory would be mobbed, worshipped, blamed — possibly all three by dawn.

The guildhall sat at the end of the row, narrow and tall and unremarkable, its sign — a painted knot, faded almost to suggestion — hanging crooked over the door. Xanthe had keys to three locks and took them in order, hands steadier than they had any right to be, and slipped inside, and bolted the door behind them, and stood in the dark of the entryway breathing.

The hall smelled the way it always smelled: tallow and hemp and old paper, a ghost of plum under it if you knew to look for it, which Xanthe always did, which was its own kind of wound. The workroom lay to the left, benches scarred by thirty years of knives and wax. The stair to the sleeping lofts rose to the right. And at the back, behind Merrin's study, beneath a floorboard worn smooth by generations of nervous feet, the ledger waited.

Xanthe lit no lamps. Their eyes had adjusted; the fog-light through the windows was enough. They crossed the workroom, and it was passing the third bench — *Ivo's bench*, though no one called it that anymore, though the knife-scars on its edge were his, the initials he'd carved and been thrashed for carving were his — that they heard the sound from the courtyard.

A thud. Deep, vast, muffled by walls, felt more through the floor than heard through the air. Then another. Then a long, grinding scrape, like a ship coming against a quay too small for it.

Xanthe's hand found the knife at their belt — habit, uselessness; a knife against whatever made sounds like that was a joke told badly — and they moved to the back window and looked out into the guild courtyard.

And forgot, entirely, how to breathe.

A dragon lay in the courtyard.

She filled it. Her body was a hillside of dull bronze, her wings folded like furled sails gone gray with age, her tail curled once around the old well and past it. She was dying — Xanthe saw it the way they saw flaws in a charm, instantly, professionally, helplessly. Her scales had lost their luster; here and there they had gone translucent, showing the dull ember-glow beneath like coals under ash. Her breathing came slow and enormous, each exhale stirring the fog into eddies. And her eyes — open, fixed on the window, fixed on *Xanthe* — were the color of banked fire, and full of a weariness so ancient it made Merrin seem a child.

Her jaws parted. When she spoke, her voice was a landslide given language, low enough that it barely disturbed the fog, and it came not through the glass but directly into Xanthe's skull, warm as a coal held near the ear.

"Charm-seller." A pause, one vast breath drawn and released. "You feel it too. The silence on your skin. Good. Then you already know the stars did not fall."

"They were taken," Xanthe said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them. "Collected."

Something moved through the dragon's face that might have been approval. "Taken," she agreed. "Called due. I know the shape of the contract. I have flown it before." Her head shifted, scales grinding against cobblestone, until one enormous eye filled the window entire, ember-deep, unblinking. "I am Emberfall. Once I carried fire between the high places. Now I carry one last errand, and my flame is nearly spent, and I have come to spend it here."

"Here," Xanthe repeated. "On a guildhall doorstep. On the worst night in living memory."

"On the *exact* night," Emberfall said, "that your kind taught me to expect. Debts keep schedules, little seller. Yours has come due, and mine is close behind, and the two of us are running out of time in the same direction." The eye narrowed, a slow grinding of lids. "I carried the payment myself, seven years ago. South, and down, beneath the mountain, to the court that lives under the roots of the world. I know what your guild sold. I know what it bought. And I know the road back."

The words landed one by one, each heavier than the last, and Xanthe gripped the windowsill and said, very quietly, "Sold what."

"You do not know." It was not a question, and pity moved through the dragon's voice like weather. "Of course you do not know. They kept it from the ones who loved him. That is the way of guilds. That is the way of debts." She drew a breath that made the courtyard fog spiral. "Open your ledger, charm-seller. Read the page you were never allowed to read. Then come down to me, and we will talk about roads."

Xanthe did not move for a long moment. Above them, through the ceiling, through the roof, through the ruined sky, the dark pressed down. On their wrist, where the warding knot had lived for twenty years, the skin was cold and bare and ordinary.

Then they turned, crossed the study, knelt, and pried up the floorboard.

The ledger was exactly where it had always been — leather cracked, brass corners green with age, heavier than a book had any right to be. Xanthe carried it to the window, to the fog-light, and opened it to the final page, the one Merrin had kept sealed with a wax stamp and a word that stung when spoken aloud, the one no apprentice had ever been permitted past.

The seal crumbled at a touch. Of course it did. Everything old had broken tonight.

Xanthe read.

The page was a contract, written in the guild's oldest hand, in ink that had gone brown with years. It was brief. Contracts are. *One soul, freely given,* it said. *In exchange: seven years of border-warding, unbroken, for the city of Saltmist and the guild of charm-sellers within it.* Below that, terms. Below that, a date.

Seven years ago. To the night.

And below the date, the signature of the guildmaster — Merrin's hand, unmistakable, the strong sure strokes Xanthe had watched write a thousand charms and a hundred lessons — and beside it, in a smaller, rounder script, in writing Xanthe knew better than their own heartbeat, because they had guided the hand that made it, because they had steadied the young wrist and praised the letters:

*Ivo.*

He had signed it himself. Freely given. He had *signed* it, a child's careful letters on a soul-contract, and the guild had taken it, and the wards had held for seven years, and everyone had told everyone the boy had run away.

The ledger slid from Xanthe's fingers and hit the floor with a sound like a bone breaking.

Outside, patient as winter, the dragon waited in the courtyard, her ember-eyes lifted to the window, to the starless sky, to the small figure standing frozen in the fog-light with a broken seal and a broken world and seven years of love with nowhere left to put itself.

Xanthe picked up the ledger. Closed it. Held it against their chest, over the place where the sigil used to be, over the cold spot where the protection had died.

Then they went down, unbolted the door, and stepped into the courtyard to meet the creature who had carried him away.
