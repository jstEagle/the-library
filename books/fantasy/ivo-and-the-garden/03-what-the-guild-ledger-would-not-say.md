## 3. What the Guild Ledger Would Not Say

The guildhall had not burned. That was the first thing Xanthe understood, standing in the doorway with ash sifting down around them like gray snow, and it was somehow worse than burning would have been.

Fire was honest. Fire left char and ruin and a story you could tell yourself — *the wards failed and the magic ran wild and the building paid the price.* But the guildhall of the Charmwrights' Guild had not burned. It had *emptied*. Every wall still stood, every beam held its place in the frame of the great room where Xanthe had learned to tie their first luck-knot at six years old, and yet everything inside had been hollowed out as if a giant hand had reached through the windows and scooped the life from it.

The sigil-boards were blank. Forty years of guildwork — protective marks stitched into oak and iron, each one a small promise made visible — gone pale as bleached bone. The dye pots in the corner sat full but colorless, their contents turned to something like milk. Even the dust on the floor was wrong: too fine, too uniform, as though color itself had been one more thing the night had taken when it took the stars.

Xanthe walked in because there was nowhere else to go.

That was the truth of it, and they were too tired this morning to dress it up in better cloth. The city was screaming — had been screaming since midnight, since the sky went black from horizon to horizon and the border wards shattered with a sound like the world's largest bell cracking. People ran in the streets. Temples overflowed. The harbor chain had been raised against nothing, against everything, and somewhere out in that starless dark the old things that the wards had kept politely outside for three hundred years were pressing their faces against the boundary of the world like beggars at a window.

And Xanthe Blackwood, last charm-seller of a forbidden trade, had done what they always did when the world ended. They had finished their rounds. Sold two luck-knots to dockworkers who didn't ask why the knots glowed faintly now, why the old hidden magic in them had woken up hungry. Pocketed coins they suspected would be worthless by week's end. Walked home through a city that no longer had a sky worth looking at.

Home was the guildhall. Home had always been the guildhall, even after Merrin closed the public rooms and the apprentices stopped coming and it became just the two of them, and then — seven years ago — just the one.

*Seven years ago.*

The thought arrived without warning, the way a splinter works itself into skin, and Xanthe stopped in the middle of the great hall with their boots stirring that wrong, colorless dust.

Seven years ago tonight — or near enough; the seasons blurred when you sold charms for coppers — Ivo had disappeared.

* * *

They had told everyone, in those first terrible weeks, that the boy had run away. It was the kindest lie available. A fourteen-year-old apprentice, restless, gifted, chafing under the guild's slow decline — of course he'd run. Children ran. The city watch had nodded along, filed the report, moved on. Merrin had said it so many times that Xanthe sometimes believed she'd made herself believe it, and Xanthe had said it too, standing in doorways and market squares with a handbill in their fist, *have you seen this boy*, while knowing — knowing in the way you know a knot is tied wrong before the rope ever fails — that Ivo would never have run. Not from them. Not from Merrin. Not from the plums they stole off the chandler's cart every midsummer and ate on the roof, juice running down their wrists, laughing until the bells rang vespers.

Ivo had loved this hall. He had loved its dusty corners and its stubborn old mistress and the way Xanthe's fingers could make string mean *safety*. He had fallen asleep more than once on the workbench with his cheek on a half-finished charm, and Xanthe had covered him with a coat and thought, *this is what my whole life is for,* and meant it.

He had not run away.

Xanthe stood in the dead hall and let the knowledge finally finish arriving, seven years late, the way grief does when it's been waiting patiently at the door for you to stop pretending you're not home.

Something had happened here. Something the guild had done, or allowed, or —

No. Xanthe turned toward the back room, toward Merrin's office, walking faster now, because there was one place in this building where the guild kept its truths whether it wanted them or not. The guild lied with its mouth and its handbills and its polite letters to the watch. But the ledger did not lie. The ledger had never been permitted to lie; that was the first law of the Charmwrights, older than the city's ban on magic, older than Merrin herself: *what is traded must be written, and what is written must be true.*

The office door hung open. Of course it did. Nothing in this building had any resistance left in it.

Merrin's desk sat under the window where it had always sat, bare except for the ledger, which lay closed in the center of the blotter as neatly as if someone had set it there an hour ago. Everything else was gone — the inkwell, the paper knife, the little tin of plum-pit buttons Ivo used to sort for hours — vanished into that same colorless emptiness that had eaten the sigils. Only the ledger remained.

It should have frightened Xanthe more than it did. Later, they would understand that some part of them had already crossed over into the country where fear stops mattering because the worst thing has already happened. For now they only crossed the room, pulled out Merrin's chair — the leather cold as river stone — and opened the book.

* * *

The guild ledger was a massive thing, bound in green hide gone soft and dark at the corners, its spine cracked into a topography of use. Xanthe had seen it a thousand times. As an apprentice they'd been taught to read it the way other children learned scripture: columns of trades and tithes, ward-contracts with the city council, the annual accounting of charms sold and protections maintained. Every entry in a clerk's careful hand, and beneath each year's entries, in Merrin's blunter script, the certification: *Written true. M. Vael, Guildmaster.*

Xanthe turned pages. The paper whispered like moth wings.

Five years back. Six. The entries grew sparser as the city's ban tightened its grip — fewer contracts, more fines recorded and disputed, the guild's whole history narrowing like a river in drought. Seven years back. The page for that autumn.

And there, at the bottom, below the last routine entry (a tithe dispute, resolved), the handwriting changed.

It was still Merrin's hand. Xanthe would have known it anywhere — the heavy downstrokes, the way she crossed her t's like small wounds. But it was Merrin writing slowly, deliberately, the way a person writes when they are forcing themselves not to stop, and the entry was not a trade record at all.

Xanthe read it once. Then they put the ledger flat on the desk, pressed both palms against the wood, and breathed, because the room had begun to tilt.

Then they read it again.

---

*Received of the Court Beneath the Mountain, by the hand of its Magistrate of Debts: protection of the city borders, full and continuing, term of seven years, renewal subject to renegotiation.*

*Given in payment: one soul, freely given, unclaimed and unencumbered, of the guild's own household.*

*The soul to be held in trust by the court for the term, display and use at the court's discretion, returnable upon satisfaction of terms.*

*Witnessed and sealed. Written true.*

*M. Vael, Guildmaster*

---

Beneath the words, where a contract seal should have been, was a mark Xanthe had never seen on any guild document: a circle bisected by a line, drawn in an ink that was not ink — it shifted when Xanthe looked at it directly, sliding from black to a deep bruised violet, and the longer they looked the more certain they became that the mark was looking back.

*One soul, freely given.*

*Of the guild's own household.*

There were, in the autumn seven years past, exactly two people in the guild's household. Merrin Vael, who was seventy-one years old and whose soul, whatever else might be said of it, was neither free nor unencumbered — it was mortgaged to forty years of bargains and half-bargains, and besides, no court beneath any mountain would want it.

And there was Ivo.

Ivo, who had come to them at nine with no surname anyone knew and no family anyone could find, found asleep in the guild courtyard one morning with frost on his eyelashes and a dragon-scale pendant clutched in his fist — a pendant Merrin had gone white upon seeing, and locked in her desk drawer, and never spoken of again. Ivo, whose talent for charmwork was so naked and enormous that Xanthe had privately called him *the last real thing the guild will ever make*. Ivo, who was unclaimed. Unencumbered. Whose soul belonged to no god, no order, no contract — because the guild had checked, oh, they had checked, when they took him in; that was procedure; that was *law* —

*Freely given.*

Xanthe's hands had gone numb on the desk. They noticed this distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

The date. Their eyes went to the date at the head of the entry, because some stubborn clerk-part of their mind demanded the formality be observed, and the date was the third of Harvestmonth.

The third of Harvestmonth was the night Ivo disappeared.

Not near it. Not in the same season, the same month, the same week. The same *night*. The ledger said the guild had given a soul to the court beneath the mountain on the very evening the guild had told the city, with straight faces and printed handbills, that the boy had climbed the wall and run.

Xanthe heard a sound and realized after a moment it was coming from their own throat — not a sob, nothing so clean as a sob, a low continuous note like a wire under strain. They gripped the desk edge. The wood bit into their palms and they welcomed it, because pain was a rope thrown to a drowning man, proof of a body, proof of a present tense.

*They sold him.* The thought came in Merrin's voice, which was the cruelest possible delivery. *We sold him. We wrote it down. We wrote it TRUE.*

Seven years of wards. Seven years of the border holding, of the city sleeping safe behind protections that everyone knew were failing, weakening, going threadbare — and Xanthe understood now why the wards had felt thin lately, why Merrin had spent the last two autumns staring west at the mountains with an expression Xanthe had taken for simple age. The term was ending. The contract was expiring. And when a debt comes due and cannot be renewed —

The stars had not gone out. The stars had been *repossessed*.

Because the wards had never been powered by the guild's craft at all. Three hundred years of Charmwright pride, of *we hold the border, we are the reason the dark stays outside* — and it had all been running on collateral. On a child. The guild hadn't protected the city. The guild had *paid rent*.

And now the rent was called, and the landlord had taken the lights with him.

Xanthe did not remember standing up. They were simply upright, the ledger open before them, and the rage that came up through them was unlike anything they had ever felt — not hot but *cold*, a rage with structure, with architecture, a rage that began making lists. Who signed. Who witnessed. What court. What mountain. What *Magistrate of Debts*, and what manner of creature keeps a child's soul in trust for seven years and displays it at its discretion.

Because that was the phrase that would not leave. *Display and use at the court's discretion.* Somewhere under the mountain, right now, in this hour of a starless morning, Ivo's soul was being *displayed*. Like a lamp. Like a jewel. Like a thing in a bottle.

Like a charm.

Xanthe laughed, one broken syllable of it, and hated the sound. Of course. Of course the court kept souls the way the guild kept charms — bottled, labeled, useful. The guild had taught the whole world this trade, and the world had learned it well enough to take a child and make him stock.

The shifting seal on the page pulsed, slow as a heartbeat. Watching. Waiting. And Xanthe understood, with the clarity that arrives only at the bottom of a fall, that the ledger had been left here *for them*. That the empty hall, the vanished ink, the one book remaining on the one desk — none of it was accident. The court beneath the mountain had tidied the guildhall the way a creditor seals a house, and it had left the account book open to the final page because debts are meant to be known by those who inherit them.

A message, then. Read your inheritance. Know what you're owed, and what you owe.

Fine. Xanthe laid their palm flat on the page, over the pulsing seal, and spoke aloud to the empty room, to the watching mark, to whatever ear the mountain had grown in the walls:

"I'm coming for him."

The words fell into the colorless air and did not echo. The hall swallowed sound the way it had swallowed everything else. But under Xanthe's hand, the seal warmed — just slightly, just once — like a hand squeezing back.

Outside, the sunless sky pressed against the windows. Somewhere far to the west, beneath the mountains, something ancient turned a page of its own.

And in the courtyard behind the guildhall, in the space of one breath and the next, the shadows gathered themselves into a shape with wings.

Xanthe turned at the sound — a vast, labored exhalation, like bellows the size of a cathedral — and saw, descending through the dark against all law and likelihood, a dragon.
