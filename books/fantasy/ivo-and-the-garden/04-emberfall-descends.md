## 4. Emberfall Descends

The sky had been empty for three days, and Emberfall had been falling for two of them.

Not falling in the way the small creatures below would understand it — not a plummet, not a scream of wind and surrender. Falling slowly, the way a candle falls toward its own guttering: by degrees, by dimming, by the long arithmetic of what remained. She had left the high passes when her wings could still hold a thermal, and she had crossed the dead country in a straight line because turning now cost more than she had to spend. Beneath her the world lay unlit. No stars. No moon worth the name. Only the cities' grudging lamps, scattered like coins dropped by a careless god, and among them — nearest to the wound in the sky — one city that smelled wrong to her old nostrils.

It smelled of hidden magic. Of charms knotted in back rooms and sold as trinkets. Of a guild that had once been great and was now a held breath.

Emberfall knew that smell the way a mourner knows a grave.

Her scales had been gold once. She remembered this the way she remembered most things now — distantly, with the faint embarrassment of a woman recalling her own wedding dress. Gold like struck light, gold that made the mountain courts flatter and scheme. Now the gold had gone to ash-color along her spine, and the ash crept further each morning when she woke, if waking was still the word for it. Her fire lived low in her chest these days, a coal rather than a blaze, and she fed it memories the way a miser feeds a stove — one at a time, counting.

She thought about the soul she had carried. That memory cost the most, so she saved it for the coldest hours.

Seven years ago, they had put a child's soul into her keeping. Not a body — a body had walked away down the mountain road with the guildmaster's hand on its shoulder, waving back, trusting. A soul, folded small and bright as a paper lantern, sealed in a vessel of glass and law. The court beneath the mountain had required delivery, and the court did not send its own servants above ground anymore; the borders were too watched, even then. So they had come to her, because she was old, and because she owed them, and because a dragon who is dying will agree to almost anything that lets her pretend she is still useful.

*Carry this,* they had said. *One soul, freely given.*

Freely given. She had turned those words over the whole flight down into the dark, and every year of the seven, and she had never once found them clean. Children did not give things freely. Children gave things because the adults around them made giving look like love.

And the child — Ivo, they called him, Ivo of the charm-guild, Ivo with ink on his fingers and a laugh like a door opening — the child had looked up at her before they took him away, and he had said, *Will you remember me?*

Dragons remember everything. It is our gift and our punishment. She had said yes, because it was true, and because lying to children is the one cruelty even the mountain court does not practice.

She had carried his soul into the deep places and handed it to hands like white roots, and she had watched them set it on a shelf of basalt between a general's courage and a bride's first joy, catalogued and gleaming, and she had flown out of that cathedral cavern faster than dignity allowed, and she had not gone back. For seven years she had not gone back.

Then the stars went out, and the wards broke, and Emberfall understood that the debt had been called due — all of it, at once, the principal and the interest — and that no one else alive knew the road beneath the world well enough to walk it and return.

* * *

She saw the city resolve beneath her: rooftops the color of wet slate, canals threading silver through the dark, and there — set back from the river district, half-swallowed by newer streets — the guildhall. Or what remained of it. Even from this height she could see the sigils dying on its lintels, the protective script peeling off the stone like burnt skin. Whatever had happened three nights ago, the guild had taken it in the face first.

There was someone in the courtyard.

Emberfall's eyes were failing along with everything else, but some sights cut through cataract and dimming both. A single figure stood in the ruined yard, small and upright, holding a lantern that gave off less light than it should have — a lantern with a charm worked into its cage, an old guild-work charm, the kind that hadn't been legal in seven years. The figure wasn't moving. Standing in a broken courtyard at night, in a city where the stars had just died, holding illegal light: that was not a person waiting for something. That was a person who had run out of things to wait for.

Good, Emberfall thought, with a weariness that felt almost like humor. The desperate make the best students.

She did not announce herself. Announcement required altitude, and altitude required effort, and effort required fire, and her fire was a candle-end. Instead she simply let herself descend the way autumn descends — gradually, inevitably, on air that seemed to have agreed in advance to catch her — and the shadow of her wings crossed the courtyard before her body did.

She heard the figure below go very still. Then she heard the lantern set down carefully on the stones, deliberately, the way one sets down a weapon or a plea. Sensible. Dragons respect a creature that puts down its light before speaking to the dark.

Her landing was not graceful. She had planned for grace and gotten instead a controlled collapse: forelegs first, then the long slide of her belly across the flagstones, her tail sweeping a dead fountain's rim into rubble. The impact jarred through her old bones and she lay still afterward, breathing, letting the courtyard tilt itself level around her. Somewhere in the fall a scale had cracked loose from her shoulder. She heard it skitter across the stone like a dropped coin.

When she raised her head, the human was ten paces away, close enough to be brave and far enough to be smart, and the lantern was relit in their hand.

Emberfall looked at Xanthe Blackwood and knew them instantly, though they had never met. She knew them by the way they held the lantern — slightly forward, offering light to her rather than keeping it for themselves. Charm-sellers, the real ones, the ones who never stopped being charmers even after the trade went underground, had that habit. They lit things for other people. It was their whole nature, and it was going to be expensive.

"You're the dragon," the human said. Their voice was rough with sleeplessness and something older than sleeplessness. "From the stories. The one that used to fly the border circuit."

"I am the one from the stories," Emberfall said. Her voice came out like a landslide heard through a wall — vast and muffled and tired. "The stories leave out the parts that matter."

* * *

They talked for a long time in that courtyard, while the city around them pretended not to notice a dying dragon in a dead guild's yard. Cities are good at pretending. It is how they survive.

Xanthe Blackwood told her about the ledger. Emberfall listened without interrupting, which for a dragon is a form of prayer. She heard about the final page, the contract, the date — seven years ago, the night the apprentice vanished — and she watched the human's face do what faces do when horror finishes its work and settles in as furniture.

"You didn't know," Emberfall said at last. It was not quite a question.

"Did you?"

"I carried him." The words cost her a scale's worth of warmth, and she paid it. "I carried what they took out of him. Down under the world, to the court, to the Magistrate of Debts himself. I set him on a shelf between a dead soldier's courage and a bride's first joy, and I catalogued him myself, because the court makes the courier do the writing. They say it fixes the debt in the carrier. Makes us complicit past denying." Her smoke curled gray against the starless black. "I wrote his name in the ledger of that place with my own claw. *Ivo. One soul, freely given.*"

Xanthe's breath caught — a small sound, quickly mastered, but Emberfall had lived long enough to know the difference between a gasp and a decision. This one was a decision being born.

"He asked me," she went on, because the coal in her chest demanded it, because some debts must be spoken aloud before they can be paid, "before they took him. He asked me if I would remember him. And I said yes, because dragons remember everything, and I have spent seven years learning that remembering everything is not the same as saving anything."

"What do you want?" Xanthe asked. Direct. No flinching. Emberfall revised her estimate of them upward.

"To die somewhere useful." She laughed, and the laugh was mostly smoke. "I have perhaps a season of fire left in me. Less, if I spend it. The court beneath the mountain runs on souls and memory, little charmer, and it has your Ivo shelved like a vintage. You cannot steal him — I tried theft on their behalf once, long ago, and I still have the scars the wards left me. You cannot buy him — you have nothing they want." She let her head lower until her ancient eye was level with the human's lantern-lit face. "But you have memories. And the court trades in memory the way your city trades in fish."

"I don't understand."

"You will." She closed the eye. "I know the sunken road. I know the tollgates and the gatekeepers and which names they'll take and which they'll keep. I know the alcoves where smugglers hide from the Magistrate's collectors, and I know the weight of a soul measured against a life's remembering. No one else living knows these things. In a season, no one will know them at all, because I will be a story, and stories are the only dragons that survive."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange," Emberfall said, "you carry my death down with you and spend it well."

The silence that followed was long and full of the city breathing around them — canal water, distant dogs, a bell tower marking some hour that no longer mattered much. Emberfall felt the human thinking, and she recognized the texture of the thinking, because she had done it herself seven years ago in the cold air above the mountain: the weighing of a terrible price against an unbearable alternative, conducted at speed, alone.

"There's a guildmaster," Xanthe said finally. "Merrin. She raised me. If she signed—"

"She signed." Emberfall opened her eye again. "I watched her sign. She wept while she did it, if that helps you. It did not help him."

Something moved behind Xanthe Blackwood's face then — grief and fury braided together so tightly they were indistinguishable — and Emberfall nodded to herself, satisfied. Fury was fuel. Fury would get a mortal down the sunken road when fear alone would turn them back at the first tollgate.

"Rest," she said, settling her chin onto the broken fountain's rim. "You'll want your strength, and I'll want mine, and tomorrow we go down through the old flood-tunnel where the outlawed magic pools like rainwater. Take your lantern. The dark down there is older than mine."

Xanthe hesitated at the courtyard's edge, lantern raised, a small bright question mark against the ruin.

"Why now?" they asked. "Why come to us? You've had seven years."

Emberfall considered lying, and found she had run out of the fire lies require.

"Because three nights ago the stars went out," she said, "and I finally understood that the debt was never finished. Contracts like that one don't end, charmer. They mature. The court has taken its seven years of protection, and now it means to take the rest of the city — ward by ward, memory by memory, until nothing above ground remembers why it should resist. Your Ivo is not collateral anymore. He is the pattern. The first item in a collection that intends to become complete." Her smoke rose straight and thin in the windless dark. "I carried him down. I will not be the only one who knows the way when someone carries him back."

Xanthe Blackwood stood a moment longer, lantern trembling very slightly — not with fear, Emberfall thought, but with the effort of holding still under an enormous new weight.

"Tomorrow, then," they said, and walked out of the courtyard with their illegal light held forward, the way charmers do, lighting the way for everyone but themselves.

Emberfall laid her head on the cold stones and let her eye close, and in the dark behind it she fed her last good memory to the coal in her chest: a child with ink-stained fingers looking up at her, asking to be remembered.

Tomorrow, little one, she thought. Tomorrow we go and get you back, and I will spend what's left of me doing it, and that will have to be enough.

Above the city, the sky stayed empty. But far below, in the deep places, something ancient stirred on its shelf of basalt and dreamed, for the first time in seven years, of daylight.
