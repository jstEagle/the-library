## 5. Seven Years of Small Kindnesses

The plums were still warm from the sun when Xanthe lifted them off Vessa Corrow's cart, and that was the trick of it — you had to take them before they cooled, because a warm plum told the world it had just been picked and a cold one told it nothing at all. Xanthe palmed three with a flourish they'd perfected over fifteen years of charm-work: the wrist loose, the eye elsewhere, the whole body saying *I am only standing here, minding my own business*.

"Thief," said Ivo, who was nine years old and sitting cross-legged on the guildhall steps with his legs too long for them, all knees and elbows and that cowlick at the crown of his head that never lay down no matter how Merrin attacked it with comb and spit.

"Borrower," Xanthe corrected. "There's a difference. A thief takes without intention. A borrower intends to return."

"You're eating them."

"I intend to return the pits." Xanthe dropped down beside him on the sun-warmed stone and pressed one of the plums into his palm. "To the compost heap. Where they'll become next year's plums. Which is practically returning them to Vessa herself, if you squint at it sideways."

Ivo squinted at it sideways. He was very good at doing exactly what he was told in the most literal possible way, which Xanthe suspected would either make him a great magician or a terrible one, and privately hoped for both. Then he bit into the plum and juice ran down his chin and he forgot about ethics entirely, which Xanthe counted as a moral victory of some kind.

The guildhall rose behind them, old as the city's bones, its timber gone silver-grey with age, its eaves carved with knotwork so ancient that even Merrin couldn't say what half the patterns meant anymore. The sign above the door — a blackwood tree with its roots and branches braided into one continuous loop — had been repainted so many times the letters had grown soft, like a word repeated until it loses its meaning. *The Blackwood Guild: Charms, Wards, Small Mercies.*

Small mercies. That was the honest part of the sign. Everything else was marketing.

Below the city, the border wards hummed. You couldn't hear them, not really — you felt them, the way you felt the pressure change before rain, a faint tightness behind the eyes that every guildmember learned to read before they learned their letters. The wards were strong today. The wards were always strong. They had been strong for seven years without a single flicker, and Xanthe had stopped thinking about why somewhere around year two, the way you stop thinking about the ground holding you up.

"Show me the luck-knot again," Ivo said, mouth full.

"You showed me yours yesterday and it fell apart in your hands."

"It did *not* fall apart. It *loosened*. Loosening is a feature."

"It's a feature when you want it to loosen. A luck-knot that opens whenever it pleases isn't luck, it's weather." Xanthe took a length of cord from their sleeve — red, waxed, fraying slightly at one end where a decade of thumbs had worried it — and held it up between them so the light caught it. "Watch. Not my hands. My hands are going to lie to you. Watch the *tension*."

"I'm watching the tension."

"No, you're watching the plums."

"I finished the plums."

Xanthe laughed despite themselves, the laugh coming out rougher than intended, because there it was again — that thing Ivo did, this small gravitational pull he had, this way of making even a stolen afternoon feel like something worth protecting. They began the knot.

The first crossing was the promise: left over right, but only halfway, an open loop that looked like nothing. "That's the asking," Xanthe said. "Every charm starts with an asking. You're asking the cord what it wants to be."

"Cords don't want things."

"Neither do rivers, and yet look at them, all going the same direction." Xanthe folded the loop through itself, once, twice, and the shape began to close like a hand. "Second crossing is the agreeing. Third is the sealing. And the fourth—"

"—is the giving," Ivo said, quiet now, his eyes actually on her fingers. "Because a charm isn't finished until someone needs it."

"Until someone *needs* it," Xanthe agreed. "Say the last line."

"The work is small. The mercy is not."

The knot closed. It sat in Xanthe's palm, small and dense and warm — warmer than the plums had been, warm in a way that had nothing to do with sunlight, warm the way a held hand is warm. Below the city, the wards hummed, and the knot answered them, one small bell ringing against a great deep one, and Ivo's face went lit with the particular joy of a thing made well.

"That's mine?" he asked.

"It's yours. Take it before I get sentimental."

He took it. He tied it around his wrist immediately, clumsily, the bow crooked, and Xanthe thought — with the specific, unearned confidence of a person standing in sunlight — *he'll have that knot for the rest of his life.*

* * *

The guild taught three things, in this order: how to make a charm, how to sell a charm, and how to disappear while doing both.

Merrin handled the second lesson personally, because Merrin believed commerce was a craft like any other and deserved the same rigor. She stood Ivo in front of the stall on Tallowmarket Day, put a tray of warding-bracelets under his chin, and said, "Sell me one."

"They're a copper each."

"They're a copper each and I've got five coppers and a suspicion. Convince me."

Ivo looked at the bracelets. He looked at Merrin. He looked, briefly and desperately, at Xanthe, who was pretending very hard to inventory dried lavender and listening with every hair on their head.

Then he said, "You don't need one."

The market noise seemed to lean closer.

"You don't need one," Ivo repeated, "because you're Guildmaster, and the wards answer to your signature, and nothing gets through them that you haven't already decided to let through. So you'd be buying this for somebody else. Somebody who walks home after dark. Somebody whose name you know because you signed the ledger that keeps her street safe." He pushed the tray forward. "Buy it for her. Tell her it came from you. She'll wear it wrong and it'll work better than any of ours."

Merrin stood very still for a long moment.

Then she put down five coppers, took a bracelet, and turned away quickly enough that Xanthe — who knew every version of Merrin's face the way sailors know weathers — caught the shine in her eyes before she mastered it.

"That," she said, once she could speak evenly, "was cheating."

"Yes, Guildmaster."

"Do it again tomorrow."

* * *

Summer thickened toward its heavy middle. The nights stayed warm and the stars stayed bright — brighter than usual, people said, though Xanthe noticed such things the way you notice your own heartbeat, constantly and therefore never. On the roof of the guildhall, where the lead flashing held the day's heat long past sundown, Xanthe lay on their back with Ivo beside them, teaching constellations the way Merrin had taught them: by slander.

"That's the Miller," Xanthe said, pointing. "He cheated his brother out of a field and got set in the sky as punishment, grinding the same wheat forever."

"That's seven dots."

"Everything is seven dots if you're lazy. There — the bright one, low on the water? That's the Lantern-Bearer. She carried fire down from the first morning so the dark wouldn't win, and the gods liked her work so much they wouldn't let her stop."

"Is that true?"

"It's as true as anything up there. Stars are just knots, you know. Somebody tied them, and whatever they used for cord, it holds."

Ivo was quiet for a while. The city murmured below — gulls arguing over the harbor, a fiddler two streets over playing the same eight bars with diminishing conviction, the great slow breathing sound a city makes when it believes it is safe.

"Xanthe?"

"Mm."

"Why does the guild keep me?"

The question arrived without warning, the way real questions do. Xanthe kept their eyes on the sky because looking down felt suddenly dangerous.

"What kind of question is that?"

"A real one. Merrin found me on the steps during the plague winter. Nobody claimed me. You taught me knots. But nobody ever said why." His voice didn't waver. Nine-year-olds ask their hardest questions in the flattest voices, saving all their trembling for later, for private. "Was I left here like the plums? Borrowed?"

Xanthe sat up. The lead was cooling under them; the night had teeth coming, eventually, the way nights do. They looked at this child — this sharp, strange, literal-hearted child whom the guild had found on its steps like a parcel addressed to no one, and raised without ever quite admitting that raising him was the point of everything else they did.

"Here's what I know," Xanthe said. "I know that before you, this roof was just a roof. I know that before you, the luck-knots sold better because people bought them out of fear, and now they sell better because I make them thinking about whether they're good enough for you to wear. I know that Merrin hums when she works now, and she didn't used to." They knocked their shoulder gently against his. "Nobody borrowed you, Ivo. Some things aren't transactions. That's the entire secret of the trade, and most charm-sellers die without learning it."

"Promise me something, then."

"Careful. Promises are binding. Ask any knot."

"Promise nothing bad happens while the guild stands."

And there it was, hanging in the starlight between them — a child's bargain, simple and enormous. Xanthe felt below them, faint as a pulse in the wrist, the wards humming their seven-year hum. Strong. Steady. Paid-for, though they didn't know the word for that yet, wouldn't know it for years, would learn it in a ruined hall with a ledger open in their shaking hands.

They thought: *the guild has stood for four hundred years. What could possibly outlast it?*

They thought: *I would burn the whole sky down before I let anything touch him.*

They said, because they meant it with the whole reckless weight of themselves:

"While the guild stands, nothing bad happens. Knot it."

Ivo smiled, reached over, and tied an actual knot in the hem of Xanthe's sleeve — clumsy, crooked, fourth crossing and all. "Sealed," he said.

Above them the stars burned in their courses, every one lit, every one accounted for, the Lantern-Bearer carrying her fire down from the first morning as she always had and always would.

* * *

Xanthe woke in the smuggler's alcove beneath the mountain with salt on their face and no memory of having cried, and for one merciful moment the dream still held — the warmth of lead flashing, the taste of plum, a small crooked knot tied in a sleeve.

Then it went, all of it, sliding off them like water, leaving only the shape of the warmth behind: love with no story attached, grief with no grave, the ache of a promise whose words were gone but whose weight remained, pressing on the chest like a hand.

Beside them, Emberfall's scales guttered dimly in the dark. Somewhere beyond the alcove wall, the court of debts was counting.

Seven years of small kindnesses. Xanthe could no longer remember a single one of them.

But they remembered — the way you remember the ground holding you up, constantly and therefore never — that they had once promised a child nothing bad would happen while the guild stood.

The guild had fallen. The promise, somehow, had not.

Xanthe stood, wiped their face, and went to find the road back to the weighing-scale.
