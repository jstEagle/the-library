## 6. The Road Under the World

The entrance was where Emberfall said it would be: beneath the old cistern at the foot of Tanner's Row, behind a wall that had been bricked over so long ago the mortar had gone soft as cake. Xanthe knelt in the dark with their palms flat against the stones and felt it — a hum, low and patient, like a sleeping animal's breath.

"It's warm," they said.

"Of course it is." Emberfall's voice came from the courtyard above, filtered down through the broken grate, and even diminished as she was — her scales gone the color of old ash, her fire banked to a coal-glow in her throat — there was still something in that voice that made the dark itself sit up and listen. "Magic doesn't die when they outlaw it, charm-seller. It goes somewhere. It sinks. Seven years of forbidden workings, every hedge-charm burned, every warding-song silenced — all of it drained down here like rain into a cellar. The road is wet with it."

Xanthe worked their fingers into the mortar and pulled. A brick came loose with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw, and what breathed out of the gap was not air. It was thicker than air, sweeter, carrying a taste Xanthe knew from childhood — the smell of the guildhall on working nights, hot wax and iron filings and the green bite of fresh-cut rowan. Magic had a smell once. They had thought that smell was gone from the world.

They widened the hole brick by brick until a person could pass, though a dragon could not, and understood then why Emberfall had led them here rather than through any gate above ground.

"I can't fit," they said. It wasn't a question.

"Not yet. Not down the first stretch." Emberfall lowered her great head through the grate, close enough that Xanthe could see the fault lines running through her snout, hairline cracks of dimness where fire should have shown through. "There is a passage below that widens. Old roads do. But you'll walk the first mile alone, and I will meet you at the Pool of Echoes. Do not touch the water there. Do not speak your own name aloud anywhere under the earth. And whatever you see in the walls —"

"In the walls?"

Emberfall's eyes, each one large as a dinner plate, held a light that was almost pity. "You'll understand when you see them. Go. Every hour we stand here, the court grows more certain no one is coming for its prize."

* * *

The passage swallowed Xanthe by degrees. First the light of the courtyard went; then the sound of the city — the gulls, the harbor bells, the endless complaint of cart wheels — thinned to nothing, as if the world above had been a rumor all along. What replaced it was a silence with texture, a silence that pressed against the eardrums like deep water.

And the magic — gods, the magic. It pooled in the road's hollows exactly as Emberfall had promised, standing in slow silver puddles that gave off no reflection, or rather reflections that lagged a half-second behind, so that Xanthe kept catching glimpses of themselves moving a heartbeat after they moved. They stepped carefully around the puddles anyway. Habit said water. Instinct, older and less articulate, said *do not drink, do not wade, do not look too long.*

The walls began to change a hundred paces in. The brick gave way to older stone, and the older stone gave way to stone that had been worked by no human hand — vast fitted blocks veined with channels, and in the channels, things grew. Not moss. Not lichen. Small bright shapes like moths made of stained glass, clinging in clusters, opening and closing their wings without sound. When Xanthe passed, they turned — hundreds of them, thousands, all along the corridor — tracking with a attention that felt horribly like recognition.

"You were charms once," Xanthe whispered, and their voice came back to them wrong, doubled, as if the road itself were learning the words. "Love-knots. Wardings. Someone sang you into being and then someone else forbade the song."

The glass moths shivered. Somewhere far ahead, something answered — a sound like a bell rung underwater.

They walked on. There was no other direction. That was the thing about commitment, Xanthe thought, that no one told you when you were young: it didn't feel like courage. It felt like discovering that the door behind you had quietly become a wall.

* * *

Emberfall took the long way down, because dragons always did, because pride was the last organ to fail.

She remembered this road from before — from the year of the selling, seven years past, when she had carried a small clay vessel wrapped in wool in the hollow behind her heart-scale, and flown it to the mountain's mouth while the guildmaster stood in the snow and did not watch. She had told herself then that she was only a courier. Cargo was cargo. A soul weighed nothing; that was the horror of it, that a whole soul weighed nothing at all, and she had crossed the sky feeling lighter than she ever had, and hated herself for the lightness.

Now she descended through the mountain's root-ways, passages no human could have walked, folding her wings flat and letting herself down drops of forty feet with her claws scoring grooves in the ancient rock. Her fire was a candle now where it had been a sunrise. She could feel the edges of herself going soft, going gray, the way a coal goes when the wind forgets it. Dying dragons did not get burials. They got *spent*. And she had decided, somewhere over the frozen borderlands, that she would rather be spent than buried — spent on this one last errand, this retrieval, this unmaking of the worst delivery she ever made.

But first there was the matter of the child — no, not a child anymore, he would be fourteen now, fourteen and seven years drowned in a bottle — and the matter of the charm-seller who loved him, who was walking alone into a market that traded in the only coin Xanthe Blackwood carried in abundance.

Emberfall had watched humans for three centuries. She knew how they lied about what they'd pay. They always thought they'd pay less. They never imagined the bill would come due in the currency they couldn't spare.

She reached the Pool of Echoes at moonset — not that there was a moon anymore; the stars had gone out like snuffed candles and the sky above the mountain was a black lid — and settled onto the ledge above the water to wait, coiling her tail around herself, dimming her inner fire to almost nothing so the pool wouldn't notice her.

The pool noticed everything. That was its nature. It was the road's memory, the place where all the outlawed magic that seeped down from the city eventually gathered, and it dreamed in images: she could see them rising and dissolving at its surface. A wedding. A hanging. A woman singing to a cradle in a house that had burned two hundred years ago. Fragments of every spell ever cast and suppressed, drifting like petals on still water.

Do not touch the water, she had told the charm-seller. Because the pool gave back what you showed it, but it kept a copy. Everything under the mountain kept a copy. That was the first law of the court, the law older than the Magistrate himself:

*Nothing given is ever truly gone. Nothing taken is ever truly free.*

* * *

Xanthe heard the water before they saw it — a sound like breathing, slow and enormous — and then the passage opened out and there it was: a cavern wide as a cathedral nave, floored with a pool of liquid silver-light that stretched farther than lantern-reach, and standing at its edge, folded like a mountain that had decided to rest, a dragon.

Even dimmed, even cracked, Emberfall was the largest living thing Xanthe had ever stood beside. Their body did what bodies did in the presence of dragons — it wanted to kneel, to bare the throat, to offer. Seven years of hiding their craft had taught them to override such impulses. They stayed standing.

"You're smaller than the stories," they said.

"And you're more frightened than your face lets on," Emberfall said, "so we're even. Come away from the edge."

Xanthe looked down. In the pool's slow surface, images drifted past their boots: a kitchen, a boy on a stool too tall for him, flour on his nose. Their chest seized before their mind caught up — *Ivo* — and they dropped to their knees at the pool's rim before they knew they'd moved, hands outstretched toward the image of a summer afternoon seven years dead.

A claw the size of a plow-blade came down gently on their shoulder and drew them back.

"I warned you," Emberfall said, and there was no rebuke in it, only a terrible gentleness. "Look at me, charm-seller. Look at me and breathe. The pool shows everyone what they've lost. It's how it feeds. It shows you the shape of the wound and waits for you to reach in."

Xanthe was shaking. On the water, the kitchen-image dissolved, and another rose in its place — Ivo asleep, Ivo laughing with a stolen plum juice-darkening his chin, Ivo holding up his first finished luck-knot with the fierce shy pride of a child who has finally made something real — and Xanthe had to turn their back on it, had to press their face against warm scaled hide and count breaths until the shaking stopped.

"He's everywhere down here," they managed.

"No. He's nowhere down here, and that is worse." Emberfall drew her head around so one vast eye regarded them, level as a judgment. "Sit. We have a mile yet to walk and things you must understand before we reach the gate, and I am old enough to say them plainly, which no one else will be."

Xanthe sat. The stone was warm. Everything down here was warm, blood-warm, womb-warm, and they tried not to think about what that implied about the mountain.

"The court beneath the mountain is old," Emberfall said. "Older than your guild, older than my line, older than the idea of borders. It does not want gold. Gold is a human fever and the court caught it only recently and only shallowly. What it trades in — what it has *always* traded in — is memory. Memory is the only true currency, charm-seller, because memory is the only thing that makes a thing *yours*. Own a man's memories and you own every kindness he ever received, every debt he ever owed, every reason he ever had to be what he is. Take them and leave the body walking, and you have made an empty purse that eats."

"I know," Xanthe said. "Everyone knows the stories. Travelers come back from the mountain with gaps in them. Whole years missing. They smile too easily."

"They smile because whatever hurt them is gone, and they don't know it's gone, and they don't know anything is missing. That is the mercy of it and the obscenity." Emberfall's voice dropped lower, and the cavern seemed to lean in. "So hear me now, and hold this tighter than you hold your knives. You are going to stand in front of the Magistrate of Debts and ask him for a soul. He will name a price. Whatever price he names, it will be measured in memory — yours, most likely, because you are rich in the exact coin he wants. Memories of the boy. Seven years of them, dense and recent and *loved*, and love is what gives memory its weight. Grief-weighted memory is the rarest bullion in existence. You are carrying a fortune, Xanthe Blackwood, and you don't yet know you're carrying it, and the moment you learn it, part of you will begin calculating what you can afford to lose."

"I won't —"

"You will," Emberfall said. "Not because you're weak. Because you're *clever*, and clever creatures calculate, and calculation is the first step of the bargain whether you mean it or not. So decide now, tonight, before the gate, before the weighing: what is he worth? All of it? Decide it here where the pool can't hear you, and then never recalculate. Never let yourself wonder, in that cathedral of bottles, whether half of him might be enough. Half-memories make half-boys. I carried the vessel that held him. I know what a whole soul weighs, and I know what it costs, and I am telling you the truth no one told your guildmaster seven years ago: *the price is always payable, and it is never affordable, and you pay it anyway.*"

The silence afterward was very long. On the pool, images rose and fell: a funeral, a festival, a first kiss, a last argument. All the outlawed love of a city, pooled in the dark.

"What happened to you?" Xanthe asked quietly. "Your scales. Your fire. Carrying him — did it cost —"

"No." Something moved through the dragon's dim throat, almost a laugh, mostly ash. "This is age, charm-seller. Dragons end. It's the one bargain we don't get to refuse. But it means I have nothing left to protect except my regrets, and I intend to spend those extravagantly." She unfolded, joint by joint, a landslide deciding to move, and nodded down the far passage where the warm dark deepened toward something with architecture in it — pillars, distant and pale, like the ribs of the world. "The gate is a mile on. The Tollgate of Names. And I will tell you its rule now so it cannot surprise you: it takes a name from everyone who passes. Most travelers pay with their true names and walk through as strangers to themselves. You will not do that. You will give it a false name — any false name — and you will hold your own behind your teeth like a stone, and when it pulls, and it *will* pull, harder than anything has ever pulled —"

"How do you know all this?" Xanthe asked. "How do you know the road, and the pool, and the gate?"

Emberfall was quiet for a moment. Down the passage, far off, something chimed — the bell-underwater sound again, closer now, patient as arithmetic.

"Because seven years ago," she said, "I flew this road in the other direction, carrying the heaviest cargo that weighs nothing. I paid the toll. I stood in that cathedral. I watched them put him on a shelf between a general's courage and a bride's first love, and I told myself I was only the courier." Her fire guttered, flared, steadied. "I have had seven years to regret being only the courier. Come. There is no road back that doesn't pass through the mountain — there never was, not since the night the stars went out. The only question left is what we'll weigh when we get there."

Xanthe rose. Behind them the pool breathed its slow silver breath, showing them, one last time, a boy with flour on his nose — and they did not look, they *did not look*, they fixed their eyes on the pale pillars ahead and walked, and the glass moths in the walls turned to follow them with their thousand small faces, and somewhere beneath everything, deeper than the road, deeper than the court, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to count.
