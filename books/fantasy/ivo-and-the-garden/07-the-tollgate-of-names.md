## 7. The Tollgate of Names

The sunken road did not stay dark. That was the first thing Xanthe learned about it, walking in Emberfall's wake, and the last comfortable thing they would learn for a long while.

Light pooled here like water finding its level — outlawed magic, the dragon had called it, magic that had been driven out of the city above by edict and fear until it had nowhere left to go but down. It gathered in the gutters of the road, in the seams between ancient flagstones, in the hollows where roots of impossible trees had once pushed through and been turned back. It was not warm light. It was not cold either. It was the light of things that had been forbidden so long they had forgotten what they were for: a green shimmer that clung to Xanthe's boots like pond scum made of thought, a violet haze that drifted at knee height and parted around Emberfall with something like reverence.

The dragon moved ahead of them, slow now, her wings folded tight against a body that seemed smaller than it had in the courtyard. In the city's grey daylight her scales had been dim; down here, against all this gathered light, Xanthe could see how dim they truly were. Copper gone to rust. Gold gone to brass gone to something that looked, in places, like old bone. Her fire still burned behind her teeth — Xanthe could feel it as heat on their face when she exhaled, could see it flicker in the hinge of her jaw when she spoke — but it burned low, the way a hearth burns at three in the morning when no one has fed it.

*How far?* Xanthe asked, for the third time. They were not sure their voice worked properly down here. The road swallowed sound the way it swallowed everything else, and speaking aloud felt like dropping coins into deep water. Emberfall heard them anyway. She heard them the way stone hears rain.

*Far enough,* she said. *Close enough that you should decide now what you are willing to lose.*

Xanthe had been deciding that since the guildhall. Since the ledger's final page, since the date that matched the night Ivo vanished, since Merrin's empty chair and the smell of ash where the wards had died. They had decided it a hundred times, and each time the decision came out the same, and each time it cost them something new to make it. *I'll know when I know.*

Emberfall made a sound low in her chest that might have been laughter in a younger throat. *That answer will serve,* she said. *It is the only honest one anyone gives me.*

The road bent. And then it rose — not toward any surface Xanthe recognized, but up into a vastness where the green and violet light thinned and gathered instead into standing columns, slow-turning spirals of glow like the aurora drowned and buried alive. And there, where the columns converged, stood the gate.

Xanthe had expected iron. They had expected portcullis and chain, ward-stones and warning sigils, the architecture of refusal. What they got was a doorway of woven light — strands of that same outlawed magic braided together into an arch tall enough for Emberfall to pass beneath without bowing her head, though she bowed it anyway, an old courtesy or an old grief. The arch stood free, connected to nothing, rising out of the road with no wall on either side of it. One could walk around it easily. Xanthe understood, looking at it, that no one ever did.

Before the arch sat a figure on a stool of black wood.

It had been human once. Xanthe felt that certainty settle into them the way cold settles into wet cloth. There was a shape to it that remembered shoulders, a head that remembered being held up by a neck, hands resting on its knees that remembered thumbs. But the memory had worn thin. Its skin was the grey of paper left out in weather, and across its face — where a face should have been — there was only smoothness, faintly creased, like a page someone had written on and then, carefully, lovingly, scraped clean. It wore a robe of undyed wool, and around its neck hung a cord strung with small tokens: knots, coins, buttons, a child's milk tooth, a strip of ribbon faded past color. A toll-keeper's collection. A life's worth of other people's payments.

*Xanthe Blackwood.* Emberfall's voice had gone very quiet. *This is the Tollgate of Names. Beyond it lies the court's approach, and nothing passes that has not paid. The toll is simple. The toll is a name.*

*Whose name?*

*Yours. Always yours. You give the gatekeeper your true name — the one your mother breathed into your ear before you could speak, the one the world uses when it thinks of you, the one stitched into every charm you've ever sold without knowing it — and you walk through lighter. Lighter by exactly one self.* She did not look at Xanthe as she said it. She looked at the gatekeeper, and something in the set of her ancient neck suggested she had looked at it before, long ago, under circumstances she did not intend to describe. *Travelers who pay forget themselves. Not entirely. Not at first. They forget the shape of themselves. Why they came. What they loved. The gatekeeper keeps it all, strung on that cord, and somewhere below, in the court, it is spent.*

Xanthe's mouth had gone dry, which was absurd, because their body was a mile of stone and darkness above feeling anything so ordinary as thirst. *And if we don't pay?*

*Then you stand here until you do. The gate does not argue. It does not need to. Everyone who comes this far comes for something they cannot live without, and everyone who cannot live without something eventually decides they can live without themselves.* Emberfall lowered her great head until one dull-copper eye, wide as a dinner plate, was level with Xanthe's face. In it Xanthe saw their own reflection, small and stubborn and already grieving. *There is another way. It is harder, and it is a lie, and it may not work, and if it fails it will cost you more than the truth would have. But I have watched souls pay that gate for seven hundred years, and I have never once seen one walk away whole. So listen.*

Xanthe listened.

*A name freely given is the toll. But a name is not only what you are called. It is what you call yourself. Down here, where memory is currency and identity is ledger-work, the distinction matters more than it does in the sun. If you can give the gatekeeper a name that is true to you — truly, deeply, in the place where names live — but is not the name the world knows... you may pass with most of yourself intact. Most.* Her eye did not blink. Dragons' eyes, Xanthe remembered from the bestiaries they'd sold children, never blinked. Now they knew why. Blinking was a thing creatures did when they could afford to look away. *But understand what you risk. If the lie is shallow, the gate will taste it, and it will take the truth underneath, and it will take interest besides. If the lie is deep — if you can make yourself believe it, even for the length of a heartbeat — the gate will take the false name and leave you the true one, and you will walk through carrying both, and the weight of carrying two selves through that country may be worse than losing one.*

*You've done this,* Xanthe said. Not asked. Said.

*I have done many things,* Emberfall said, *in seven hundred years, most of them unwise.* And she turned back toward the gate, and would say nothing more.

* * *

They sat apart for a while, dragon and charm-seller, in the drowned aurora-light, and Xanthe tried to find a second name inside themselves.

It was stranger work than they had expected. Names, it turned out, were not kept in one place. Xanthe went looking the way they'd once gone looking through Merrin's locked cabinet as a child — patiently, reverently, expecting locks and finding instead rooms. There was the name their mother gave them, of course, the one on the ledger line, the one the guild rolls recorded: Xanthe Blackwood, apprentice, journeyman, last of the charm-wrights. That one sat near the surface, easy to touch, and they left it alone. It was bait. They could feel it being bait.

Deeper: the name the dockworkers used, half-affectionate, half-superstitious — the Knot-Seller, the Luck-Witch of Pier Nine. Deeper still: the name Ivo had called them, in the years when he was small enough to invent words, a garbled syllable-cluster that meant *the-one-who-makes-things-safe*, and which he had never stopped using even after he grew old enough to say Xanthe properly, because some names, once earned, are not replaced. That one hurt to touch. Xanthe touched it anyway, cataloguing it, the way they'd begun cataloguing everything about him since the ledger — hoarding the boy in secret, against the coming expense.

And deeper than all of those, in a room they had not opened in twenty years: a name from before the guild. Before Merrin found them selling stolen charms in a fish market at nine years old, quick-fingered and motherless and already too good at being nobody. A name their mother had used only when they were sick, or frightened, or forgiven. A name attached to a kitchen that smelled of tallow and river-mud, to a woman who sang off-key, to a life that had ended badly and young and was better left shut.

Xanthe stood outside that room for a long time.

Here was the cruelty of Emberfall's advice, laid bare: to give the gatekeeper a false-but-true name, Xanthe would have to believe it — and the only names they truly believed were the ones they could least afford to lose. The name Ivo gave them. The name their mother whispered. Any lie shallow enough to surrender safely was too shallow to fool a gate made of stripped souls. Any truth deep enough to pass was deep enough to drown in.

Unless.

Xanthe looked at their hands. Charm-seller's hands, scarred at the knuckles, stained faintly green from years of knotting woad-dyed cord. Hands that had tied ten thousand luck-knots and meant every one. And a thought came to them, quiet and terrible and perfect, the kind of thought that arrives already knowing it will work:

They could not give a name from their past. But they could give a name from their purpose.

*I am the one who goes to get him back.* Not Xanthe Blackwood. Not the Knot-Seller. Not the-one-who-makes-things-safe. Something newer and truer than all of those: a person defined, wholly and completely, by the single act they had come into the dark to perform. A name with no history attached. No childhood, no guild, no mentor, no boy with plum juice on his chin. A name that was only a debt and a direction. Let the gate take that. Let it keep the purpose and leave the person. Let it think it had taken everything that mattered, when all it had taken was the mission statement of a soul that intended, whatever else happened, to finish what it came for.

It might work. It might unmake them. Xanthe found, examining the plan from all sides, that they could not tell the difference, and decided — with the weary tenderness of someone who has survived by habit for so long that survival itself has become the habit — that this was as good a definition of courage as any they were likely to find.

*Emberfall,* they said. *I'm ready.*

* * *

The gatekeeper did not move as they approached. Xanthe had the sense that it had not moved in centuries, that movement was among the many things it had paid away, one coin at a time, until all it retained was the sitting and the taking. Up close, its scraped-smooth face was worse than any face would have been, because Xanthe's mind kept trying to write features onto it and the mind kept flinching away from its own inventions.

When Xanthe was three paces away, it spoke. Its voice was the voice of a page turning. Of a quill drying. Of ink remembering it had once been alive in some animal's body.

*"A name,"* it said. *"Freely given. Truly held. The toll is one name, and the gate knows the difference between a name and a noise."*

Behind Xanthe, Emberfall settled onto the stones with the deliberate heaviness of a creature making itself inconspicuous, which for a dragon is a considerable undertaking. She would pay too, Xanthe realized. She must. Whatever name a dragon carries after seven hundred years — whatever she had already given this gate, once, long ago — she would stand before it again, and Xanthe would not watch, because some transactions are private even in hell.

*"I have a name,"* Xanthe said aloud. Their voice worked down here after all. It sounded thin and mortal and very far from home. *"I give it freely."*

The gatekeeper extended one grey hand, palm up. On the palm, faintly, letters began to surface — waiting to be written on, the way its face had once been written on and scraped clean. Waiting for Xanthe to become text.

Xanthe reached into themselves, into the new-made room, into the name they had built for this exact moment, and they believed it — believed it with everything they had, with the full desperate force of a heart that had already lost a boy once and would not lose him twice — and they spoke:

*"I am the one who goes to get him back."*

For a heartbeat, nothing. The gatekeeper's palm lay blank. Somewhere in the woven arch overhead, a strand of green light flickered, tasting the air.

Then the gate *leaned in*.

Xanthe felt it happen — felt the attention of the whole arch contract upon them like a lens, like an eye, like a debt collector reading fine print. The false name went out of them slowly, and it went out *true*: they felt the purpose leave, felt the singular devotion drain from their chest like heat from a cooling iron, felt themselves become, for one vertiginous instant, a person with no reason to be standing in a dark road beneath the world. The gatekeeper's fingers closed over the invisible thing it had taken, and drew it in, and knotted it — Xanthe actually saw the knot form, a twist of pale light added to the cord at its throat, settling in among the buttons and the milk tooth — and Xanthe swayed on their feet, emptied, hollowed, *lost* —

— and then, from underneath, from the deep rooms where names are kept, something rose.

Ivo's word for them. The garbled syllables meaning *the-one-who-makes-things-safe*. Their mother's whisper. The fish-market child's ferocious nobody-name, and the guild's enrolled name, and the dockworkers' superstitious fondness — all of it rushing up to fill the space the gate had carved, water finding its level, self finding its shape. The gate had taken the purpose. It had not gotten the person. For one shining, staggering moment Xanthe stood in the drowned light holding every name they owned, richer than they had ever been, and understood that they had cheated a gate made of forgotten souls with nothing but love wearing a disguise —

The gatekeeper's blank face turned toward them. Turned, slowly, the way a page turns when the reader suspects he has skipped a line.

*"Interesting,"* it said, and the word was soft, and the word was terrible. *"The toll was paid. The toll was true. But the payer walks through heavier than they arrived."* The grey hand rose, and one finger — one finger only, gently, almost tenderly — touched Xanthe's sternum, over the heart. Where, Xanthe realized with a lurch, the guild's protective sigils had lived and died. Where something else now stirred, faint and cold, like a hook set in flesh. *"The court will notice you, name-keeper. The court notices everything that arrives owing less than it should. Walk on. We will meet again, you and I, when you have more to sell."*

The arch of woven light parted before them like a curtain of green water.

Beyond it the road descended, and the air grew warmer, and far below — impossibly far, impossibly *invitingly* far — Xanthe saw the first glimmer of the cathedral cavern: ten thousand bottled lights hanging in the dark like a sky that had fallen and been collected by someone patient. Somewhere down there, one bottle held a boy who dreamed of plums and knotwork and a promise that nothing bad would happen while the guild stood.

Somewhere down there, the price was waiting to be named.

Xanthe walked through. Behind them, they heard Emberfall give the gatekeeper her own payment — heard the great wings shift, heard a sound like a sigh seven centuries long — and did not turn around, because they had promised themselves they wouldn't, and because they suddenly understood, with the gatekeeper's cold hook still resting against their heart, exactly what kind of currency this country traded in.

They had kept their name.

They had no idea yet what it would cost them to keep it.
