## 8. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The road stopped being a road an hour before they arrived.

Xanthe felt it first in their feet — the packed earth of the sunken way giving way to something smoother, something *finished*, as though the mountain had grown a floor for them out of courtesy or contempt. Then the walls began. They had been walking between ribs of stone since Emberfall's fire first showed them the downward path, but now the stone took on intention: seams that met at perfect angles, arches carved with figures whose faces had been worn smooth by no wind Xanthe could imagine. Nothing down here eroded. Nothing down here was allowed to change.

"Stay close," Emberfall said, and her voice had gone quiet in the way she went quiet before storms — not soft, but gathered. "And whatever you see, whatever it shows you, do not name yourself aloud."

"You've said that twice."

"I'll say it until we're through." The dragon's scales had dimmed another shade since the tollgate; where once her flank had glowed like banked coals, she now gave off only the light of a lamp about to be carried into another room. "The court does not need your name to take things from you. It only needs you to offer one."

They came around a final bend and the dark opened beneath them like a held breath let go.

Xanthe had seen cathedrals. They had sold charms outside three of them, back when charms were legal, and they knew the architecture of awe — the way builders stacked stone to make a person feel small in a specific, useful direction, small toward the altar, small toward the god. This cavern had been built by something that understood the principle perfectly and had never needed a god at all. It was vast past reason, a hollowed throat of the world, and every surface of it glittered.

Not with crystal. Not with ore.

With bottles.

They hung from the ceiling in their thousands — tens of thousands, more, receding upward into darkness the eye refused to measure — each one a glass vessel the size of a fist, each one lit from within. Some burned white. Some pulsed a slow amber, like hearts remembering how. A few flickered erratically, guttering, and Xanthe understood those ones with a sick lurch and looked away. Below the hanging host, tiers of stone descended in wide crescents toward a floor of polished black rock, and on those tiers moved figures — tall, robed, faceless in the gloom, going about their business with the unhurried air of clerks who had done this work since before work had a name.

"Oh," Xanthe said. It was all they could manage. Their voice came out small and did not echo; the cavern drank sound the way it apparently drank everything else.

"They call it the Ledger Hall," Emberfall murmured. "Every soul ever traded here is kept in inventory. Catalogued. Appraised. *Maintained.*" Her lip lifted from her teeth, which was the closest thing to a shudder a dragon could produce. "They are very proud of their maintenance."

Xanthe started down the first tier before they had decided to move. Their legs carried them; their mind was still standing at the top, staring up at the ceiling of lights, doing arithmetic it didn't want to finish. Each bottle a soul. Each soul someone — someone who had been given away, or gambled away, or sold the way Merrin had sold—

*Don't think his name yet,* Xanthe told themselves. *Not yet. Names have weight here.*

A robed figure detached itself from the tiers below and began to ascend toward them. It walked without haste, its steps making no sound on the stone, and as it drew closer Xanthe saw that beneath the hood there was no face at all — only a smooth expanse like the inside of an eggshell, featureless except where light from the bottled souls above slid across it and pooled briefly, like reflections looking for somewhere to live.

"The Magistrate will receive you," it said. Its voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It sounded like a hospitable innkeeper, like a kindly schoolmaster, like every trustworthy voice Xanthe had ever heard, assembled from stolen pieces. "You are expected. You have been expected for some time."

"We're not expected," Xanthe said. "We're not guests. We're—"

"You are expected," the figure repeated, with infinite patience, and turned, and led them down.

* * *

The descent through the tiers took longer than it should have. Xanthe counted the crescents as they passed — twelve, fifteen, twenty — and noticed that the bottles changed character as they went deeper. Near the top, the souls hung in loose constellations, jostling, bright, almost festive. Lower down they were arranged in strict rows, regimented, their light steadier and somehow sadder. And near the bottom, in alcoves cut into the living rock behind velvet ropes of woven shadow, single bottles sat alone on pedestals of black glass, each one attended by two of the faceless clerks who stood motionless as candlesticks.

Prized acquisitions, Xanthe thought. The ones worth keeping apart.

Their hands had curled into fists without consulting them. Emberfall's tail brushed their shoulder — a warning, or a comfort, possibly both.

"He is here," the dragon said quietly. "I can smell him. Seven years, and I still know what he smells like. Rain on warm stone. He smelled like rain on warm stone even underground."

Xanthe's throat closed. They kept walking.

The Magistrate of Debts held court at the bottom of everything, in a space where the cavern floor widened into a circle of polished obsidian so black and so deep that Xanthe's reflection stared up at them from it, doubled, tripled, a hall of mirrors made of the ground itself. Upon this circle stood a desk. That was all. No throne, no dais — only an enormous writing desk of dark wood, scarred and ink-stained, ancient in a way the flawless cavern around it was not, as though it had been carried down from some older, crueller world. Behind it sat the Magistrate.

He looked like a grandfather. That was the second worst thing. He was small and stooped, with kind creases around his eyes and a cardigan the color of moss, and spectacles perched on his nose, and he was writing in a ledger with a fountain pen, and he looked up at them with genuine warmth, the way a man looks up at grandchildren come to visit.

"Ah," he said. "The charm-seller. And the last dragon." He set down his pen and folded his hands. "Do sit. There are chairs. I insist on chairs. Debts should always be discussed sitting down; standing makes people want to flee, and fleeing complicates the arithmetic."

There were indeed chairs — two of them, placed neatly before the desk, as if set out for exactly this meeting, because they had been. Xanthe did not sit. Emberfall lowered herself onto the obsidian beside them, wings folded tight, taking up as little of the world as a dying dragon could.

"You know why we've come," Xanthe said.

"I know why you *think* you've come," the Magistrate said pleasantly. "People usually think they've come for one thing when they've actually come for several. But yes. Proceed. Ask me."

"Seven years ago," Xanthe said, and their voice surprised them by staying level, "this court received a payment. One soul. Freely given. From the Charmwrights' Guild of Vell." They paused. "A child."

"A child," the Magistrate agreed, nodding, as though confirming a delivery of flour. "Yes. Lot number—" he consulted a card on his desk, though Xanthe would have bet anything he already knew it by heart, "—nine thousand four hundred and eleven. Acquired under contract, condition excellent, provenance clean. One of the finest acquisitions of my tenure." He beamed at them over his spectacles. "Would you like to see him?"

The question landed like a blow. Xanthe had braced for refusal, for riddles, for the barbed games of gatekeepers. They had not braced for hospitality. "See—"

"It is considered good practice," the Magistrate said, rising, buttoning his cardigan with fussy care, "for interested parties to view the asset. Prevents misunderstandings later. Come along. Mind the step."

He led them across the obsidian circle to the far wall, where an alcove waited behind its rope of woven shadow — one pedestal, one bottle, two motionless clerks. The Magistrate unhooked the rope himself, with the flourish of a shopkeeper unveiling a wedding gift.

"Here we are."

Xanthe stepped forward, and the world narrowed to a fist of glass.

The bottle was taller than the others — perhaps the length of a forearm — and shaped like a lantern, with a stopper sealed in wax stamped with the court's sigil: a circle bisected by a line, the mark of a debt entered and a debt outstanding. Inside, suspended in nothing, hung a light. It was not white like the souls overhead, nor amber, nor the sickly flicker of the guttering ones. It was *warm*. It shifted as Xanthe watched, gathering and loosening, reaching and withdrawing, and there was a rhythm to it, an unmistakable rhythm, the rhythm of breathing, of a chest rising and falling in sleep.

And it was reaching toward them.

Xanthe's whole body knew it before their mind consented to know it. The light inside the glass strained toward them the way a plant strains toward a window — patiently, endlessly, without hope of arrival but without surrender either. As Xanthe watched, it flared, dimmed, flared again, pressing against the inner curve of the bottle like a palm pressed flat against a pane.

"He knows you're there," the Magistrate observed, in the tone of a man noting that the cat had come to the door. "Remarkable, isn't it? Most assets settle within the first year. They dream, eventually. This one has never settled. He dreams, certainly — vividly, we believe — but some part of him stays at the glass. Always at the glass." He tilted his head. "We find it increases the value. Souls that resist appraise higher. Despair, properly maintained, is a preservative."

Something broke open in Xanthe's chest, and what came out of it was not grief. Grief would come later, in a room with a locked door and no witnesses. What came out now, hot and immediate, was fury — fury with a taste like copper and a sound like the guildhall bell the night the stars died.

"*Asset*," Xanthe said.

"That is the term of art."

"He's nine years old." Their voice cracked on it. "He was *nine*. He liked plums. He tied his knots left-handed no matter how many times I fixed his grip. He was afraid of the cellar and he pretended he wasn't. He used to fall asleep against Emberfall's foreleg when she visited the courtyard, and she'd stay statue-still for hours so she wouldn't wake him." The words were coming faster now, tumbling, and Xanthe couldn't stop them and didn't want to. "He is not an asset. He is not a lot number. He is a child, and you have him in a *bottle*, and you stand there talking about appraisal and maintenance like—"

"Like what?" the Magistrate asked gently. "Like a man describing the contents of his own ledger? Charm-seller. Look around you." He spread one small, grandfatherly hand at the cathedral of lights above them. "Every bottle you see belonged to someone's plum-eating, knot-tying, cellar-fearing beloved. Every one. I have heard your speech ten thousand times, in ten thousand voices, and it is always sincere, and it changes nothing, because sincerity is not currency here. Contracts are currency. Consent, freely given and duly witnessed, is currency. And I regret to inform you—" he smiled, and the smile reached his eyes, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't, "—his consent was given. Witnessed. Sealed. Your guild brought him down this road themselves, with flowers in his hair, so he wouldn't be frightened. It was, I am told, quite touching."

The floor seemed to tilt. *Flowers in his hair.* Xanthe thought of Merrin's face in the ruined guildhall, the ledger's final page, the date that matched the night the city's luck had turned and a boy had simply — gone. They had known. Some part of them had known since the ledger. But knowing a thing and hearing it said in a grandfather's kindly voice, in a cathedral of stolen souls, were different wounds entirely.

Emberfall made a sound low in her throat, half growl, half keening, and the nearest bottles trembled in their thousands, a shiver running through the ceiling of lights like wind through wheat.

"Careful, dragon," the Magistrate said, without any alarm at all. "Your fire is nearly spent. Assets damaged by temper are deducted from the responsible party. I'd hate to add you to inventory. Though I confess," he added, with terrible mildness, "your flame would fetch a remarkable price. The last true dragonfire in the world. We do love a rarity."

"Why show us?" Xanthe demanded. Their fists were shaking at their sides. "If the contract is iron, if nothing can touch him — why bring us down here? Why show me his bottle?"

The Magistrate regarded them for a long moment. Above, the tens of thousands of souls burned on in silence, and the two clerk-figures by the alcove stood like sentries, and somewhere far up the sunken road, the false-named travelers were still forgetting themselves at the tollgate, paying and paying and paying.

"Because," the Magistrate said, "the court honors inquiry. A debt may be examined. Weighed. In exceptional cases—" his eyes glinted behind the spectacles, "—renegotiated. It has been seven years, charm-seller. The original terms are nearing maturity. And the court finds itself... curious... about what the people who gave him up might now consider him worth."

He turned back toward his desk, buttoning his cardigan, an old man puttering home.

"Do think about it," he called over his shoulder, pleasantly. "Terms of trade can be discussed whenever you're ready. I do so enjoy a negotiation."

Behind the glass, the small warm light pressed itself flat against the curve, straining, straining, and did not stop straining, and Xanthe stood before it with fury in their mouth and their heart hammering out the only word it had left:

*I'm here. I'm here. I'm here.*
