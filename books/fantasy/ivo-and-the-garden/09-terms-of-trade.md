## 9. Terms of Trade

The Magistrate of Debts did not walk so much as arrive, the way frost arrives on a window — gradually, and then all at once, and everywhere.

Xanthe had seen him first as a suggestion at the far end of the cathedral cavern: a tallness among the pillars of bottled light, a shape that resolved the longer one looked and never quite finished resolving. His robes were the grey of old ledger paper. His face was courteous and featureless as an unsigned contract, and when he spoke, his voice had the dry rustle of pages turning.

"You have come a long way to read a document," he said.

"I've come a long way to steal a child," Xanthe answered, and was surprised by how steady their voice came out. Their hands were not steady. They kept them folded anyway, the way Merrin had taught them in negotiation lessons twenty years ago — *never let them see which coin you're counting.*

The Magistrate inclined his head, a gesture of such perfect civility that it made Xanthe's skin crawl. Around them, the court went about its business with the unhurried industry of ants in a sugar house. Clerks of indeterminate shape moved along the shelves of bottled souls, dusting glass, checking seals, making small entries in books that whispered when they opened. Somewhere a bell tolled a hour that had no name above ground. And at the center of it all, on a plinth of black stone like a jewel in a merchant's window, the bottle that held Ivo glowed its slow gold.

He was still dreaming. Xanthe could see the faint stirrings inside the glass, the way a sleeper stirs — a turning, a reaching, a curling inward. Once, the glow flickered in what might have been laughter.

*Don't look at him,* Xanthe told themselves. *Not yet. Look at him and you'll do something stupid, and stupid here costs more than it does up top.*

"Steal." The Magistrate tasted the word. "An interesting choice of verb. Most who descend to my court choose *rescue*, or *reclaim*, or — my particular favorite — *justice*. Theft is at least honest. I respect honesty in a debtor." He gestured, and a table materialized between them: black stone, bare, lit from beneath by no source Xanthe could name. "But before we discuss theft, perhaps we should discuss purchase. You are, after all, standing in the aftermath of one."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" The Magistrate drew something from his sleeve. It should not have fit there; nothing should have fit in those sleeves, which hung empty as a hanged man's. But he laid it on the table nonetheless, and smoothed it flat with two fingers, and Xanthe felt the floor tilt under their feet.

It was a page. Vellum, gone soft and amber at the edges, creased into quarters the way important documents are creased by hands that open them too often. The seal at the bottom was broken but intact — a circle of green wax stamped with the sigil Xanthe had worn on their own skin since they were nine years old: the knotwork wheel of the Charmwrights' Guild.

The guild's seal. The guild's paper. The guild's hand.

"Read it," said the Magistrate. "Aloud, if you like. Contracts are better aloud. They were meant to be spoken once; they enjoy being spoken again."

Xanthe looked down at the page, and the words swam, and then sharpened, and then became the worst thing they had ever read.

---

*Let it be known and binding beneath sky and stone alike:*

*The Guild of Charmwrights of the City of [the name had been struck through, deliberately, with a single clean line] being threatened with extinction by the outlawry of magic within said city's walls, and the wards upon its borders failing for want of power to maintain them, do hereby covenant with the Court Beneath the Mountain as follows:*

*The Court shall furnish seven years of ward-fire, sufficient to hold the borders against all comers, famine, plague, and the slow dark.*

*In exchange, the Guild shall render payment of one soul, freely given, unclaimed and unclaimed-against, delivered into the Court's keeping entire.*

*The soul so rendered: one child, age eleven years, named Ivo, ward of the Guild, raised in the Guildhall from his ninth year.*

*Signed and sealed in good faith,*

*Merrin Ashvale, Guildmaster.*

---

The cavern did not go quiet. It had been quiet all along, or as near to quiet as a place made of whispering ledgers could be, and it was Xanthe who made the sound — a small noise, involuntary, like a stitch tearing.

*Ivo, ward of the Guild, raised in the Guildhall from his ninth year.*

Eleven years old. Two years in their care. Two years of stolen plums and knotted thread and *nothing bad will happen while the guild stands, little one, I promise you.* Two years, and then a night Xanthe had spent three hundred miles away buying dye in Corvess, and had come home to find the boy's cot stripped, his coat gone, and Merrin sitting alone in the dark of her study with a cup of cold tea and a face like a woman who has signed something.

*Runaway,* the guild had said. *Boys run. He'll come back when he's hungry.*

And Xanthe had searched. Gods, how they had searched — six months of it, every fence and ferry and fortune-teller in three counties, until grief wore itself down into the duller stone of acceptance, and acceptance into the habit of leaving a plum on the windowsill every midsummer, just in case.

Seven years. Seven years of ward-fire. The wards that had held the borders while the rest of the world's magic guttered out. The wards whose death had been the first sign, the night the stars went dark — because the seven years were *up*, and the debt had been called due, and no one in the guildhall above had thought to tell the charm-seller in the market street why the sky was falling.

"He knew," Xanthe said. Their voice came out strange, flattened, as if someone else were using their mouth. "Merrin knew. She knew where he went. She knew what she'd done, and she let us grieve. She let *me* grieve. She held my hand at the searchers' vigil and told me he'd turn up."

"The Guildmaster bargained in good faith," said the Magistrate, in the tone of a man reciting a clause he had recited ten thousand times. "The contract specifies freely given. The child was not taken. He was brought to the mountain gate on the night of the new moon and asked, formally, whether he consented. He said—" and here the featureless face did something almost like a wince, a flicker, a hairline crack in porcelain— "he said yes. He asked only that the guild be protected, and that no one be told, so that no one would try to undo it. He was, I will note for the record, very brave. We see few children. We remember them."

"No one be told." Xanthe laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, half a sob wearing a laugh's clothing. "Of course. Of course he'd say that. He was *eleven*. He'd spent two years learning that love means not being a burden. Who taught him that? Who taught an orphaned child that the kindest thing he could do with his own soul was spend it quietly?"

The Magistrate did not answer. He did not need to. The answer was written in the guildhall's every kindness, every lesson, every *we take care of our own* — care with a price tag stitched into the lining, generosity that had always, always been a form of accounting.

Xanthe thought of Merrin's hands. That was the thing that broke something loose in their chest — not the signature, though the signature was bad enough, the strong square letters she used for documents that mattered, the same hand that had written *well done* across Xanthe's first successful luck-knot. It was her hands. Those hands had braided Ivo's hair when the knots got past him. Those hands had pulled Xanthe out of a river when they were fourteen and sworn. Those hands had signed away a boy the way merchants sign bills of lading, and then those same hands had cooked dinner that night, had passed the bread, had said grace over a table with an empty chair, and no one —

No one had known.

Except everyone had known except Xanthe. That was the shape of it, the true shape, the shape that made the cavern tilt again. Emberfall had carried the payment. Someone had arranged the delivery. The whole guild had watched a child walk out the gate toward the mountain and had closed ranks around the secret like a fist around a coin, and they had let Xanthe tear the city apart looking, had let them blame themselves — *you were supposed to be watching him that week, you were the one he trusted most* — had let them carry that guilt for seven years like a stone sewn into the chest, and all the while the truth sat in Merrin's study drawer under the broken seal, warm as a hearth coal, patient as debt.

"They raised me," Xanthe said. It was not an accusation aimed anywhere; it fell on the table between them like another object produced from a sleeve. "Merrin took me off the streets when I was nine. Fed me. Taught me the craft. Everything I am is guild-made. And everything the guild is —" they touched the page, lightly, as if it might burn, and it did burn, faintly, coldly, the way deep water burns — "everything the guild is, is this. A shop that sold a child to keep its doors open."

"The Guild faced extinction," the Magistrate observed. There was no judgment in it. That was the horror of him — there was no judgment anywhere in him, no approval, no disgust. He was arithmetic wearing robes. "The outlawry was total. Confiscation, imprisonment, the breaking of hands that worked charms. The wards were failing. Within two winters, the borders would have opened to the first raiders, the first plague-winds, the first hungry thing that walks when the lights go out. The Guildmaster was offered a choice between one soul and many. She chose as most do. You may hate her for it. Many do. But you will note that the city she bought with this page is the city whose markets you sold your trinkets in this morning. Every luck-knot you ever tied under those wards was paid for with him. You ate from his soul, charm-seller. You warmed your hands at it. Do not pretend you held no spoon."

The words went in like needles finding the spaces between ribs.

Because it was true. That was the unforgivable architecture of it — the wards had held, the city had lived, the guild had survived to teach and sell and pass down its knotwork, and every single day of those seven years had been built on the interest of a murdered childhood. Xanthe had slept soundly under those wards. Had woken to birdsong. Had complained about the price of dye. All of it, financed.

"I didn't know."

"No," the Magistrate agreed. "Ignorance is the luxury the dead purchase for the living. It is, incidentally, why my court prefers memory to gold. Gold can be stolen back. Ignorance cannot be returned once spent." He drew the contract back across the table, and his sleeve swallowed it, and it was gone. "You wish to reverse the transaction. Very well. My court honors its obligations to the letter, and the letter says the soul was *purchased*, not forfeit. Purchased goods may be re-purchased. Even by thieves." The featureless face tilted, and for the first time Xanthe had the sense of being *examined*, weighed on some inner scale, appraised down to the pennyweight. "But you have no coin. No soul of your own to pledge — yours is bound up in your body and not transferable while you live, a technicality I regret. What you have is rarer. You have memories of the asset. Seven years of intimate, loving, detailed acquaintance with the merchandise."

The word *merchandise* landed like a slap, and Xanthe understood, then, that the slap was deliberate — that the Magistrate dealt in precision the way surgeons deal in knives, cutting exactly where it hurts because that is where the work is.

"You're saying I could buy him back with — what? Money? Favors?"

"With weight," said the Magistrate. "A soul weighs what it weighs. His weighed, at delivery, somewhat less than it began — children give easily, they don't yet know what things cost — but seven years of dreaming in my keeping has ripened it. To ransom it, you must place upon the scale an equivalent mass. Memories have mass, charm-seller. Not all of them. The ones with love in them are heavy. The ones with years in them are heavier still. Yours, I suspect—" and the voice dropped, became almost gentle, which was infinitely worse than cruelty— "yours, I suspect, would weigh nearly enough."

*Nearly.* The word hung in the cavern air among the bottled lights.

"And if it doesn't weigh enough?"

"Then the scale tips wrong, the transaction fails, and you remain here, owing the difference. My court has many rooms for those who owe the difference." He said it without menace, which somehow supplied all the menace the sentence required. "There is, however, another party descending the sunken road even now. Old. Heavy with years. Carrying memories of the founding — of the signing — of the whole long crime from its first morning. Such memories have considerable mass. Guilt, properly aged, is dense as lead."

Xanthe's head came up. "Who?"

"She believes herself unseen. She has bribed two gatekeepers and outrun a clerk. She is dying, charm-seller, in the way mortals die — slowly, and then all at once — and she has come to spend what remains of her on the error of her signature." The Magistrate folded his empty hands. "I tell you this not from mercy. I have none; it is not in my inventory. I tell you because the terms of trade require full disclosure to all parties, and because—" the pause was microscopic, but it was there— "because in ten thousand years of weighing, I have found that love offered freely is the one currency that always surprises me. I am curious to see what yours purchases. Or costs."

Far off, down the long throat of the sunken road, a bell tolled the unnamed hour again. On its plinth, the bottle that held Ivo flared — bright, sudden, urgent — gold light pressing against the glass like a palm pressed to a window, like a child who has dreamed of a door and found, at last, that the door has opened.

He couldn't know. Dreaming things didn't know. And yet the light reached, and reached, and Xanthe's whole body leaned toward it before they caught themselves, and the Magistrate watched them catch themselves, and noted it, somewhere, in some book that whispered when it opened.

"Terms of trade," the Magistrate repeated, rising, the frost of him spreading back through the cavern toward his ledgers. "Consider your assets, charm-seller. Consider what you can bear to lose, and what losing it will leave of you. And consider that the road behind you is sealed, and the scale waits for no one's courage but everyone's reckoning."

He was nearly gone when Xanthe spoke, and their voice cracked down the middle and kept going anyway:

"Why show me the contract at all? You'd have won either way. Why let me see her name?"

The Magistrate paused. When he turned his featureless face back, there was — impossibly, briefly — something in it that was not arithmetic. Something tired. Something old enough to remember when courts like his were young and the trade in souls had seemed, to everyone, reasonable.

"Because," he said, "she is coming down that road to confess what you have just read, and confessions are worth more when the injured party already knows. And because I have weighed ten thousand bargains, charm-seller, and I will tell you the one thing my ledgers have taught me, free of charge, against all custom:" 

The bottled souls dimmed around him as he passed, bowing, the way candles bow to a draft.

"*Every contract is a story about what people loved, and what they loved it more than.* Read yours again sometime. Count the loves in it. Then decide what yours will say."

And he was gone into the shelved dark, and Xanthe stood alone at the black table with the ghost of cold wax under their fingertips and the taste of betrayal like copper in their mouth — and far below, faint but unmistakable, growing closer with every labored footstep, came the sound of someone climbing the last miles of the sunken road on old, stubborn, dying legs.

Merrin was coming.

Xanthe turned toward the sound, fists closing, heart a struck bell, and began — one step, then another — to walk toward the woman who had raised them, to meet her at the bottom of the world.
