## 10. The Guildmaster's Confession

Merrin had been dying for three years, and she had learned to read her body the way she once read ledgers — patiently, without flinching, noting each new entry in the account of her own ending. The cough that would not clear. The weight slipping from her bones like sand through a torn sack. The way the stairs of the guildhall, which she had climbed ten thousand times, had become a negotiation. She knew what was written at the bottom of that ledger. She had simply refused, until now, to sign it.

Then the stars went out.

She had been asleep when it happened — truly asleep for the first time in weeks, the poppy syrup doing its merciful work — and she woke not to sound but to absence. The little lamp by her bed still burned. The hearth in the next room still glowed. But the light through the shutters had changed its quality entirely, gone flat and sourceless, as though the whole world were lit by a dead man's memory of the sun. And on her forearms, where the guild's protective sigils had been inked into her skin forty years ago, she felt a coldness spreading like frost across a windowpane.

She sat up. She pressed her thumb to the sigil over her wrist — the ward-knot, the oldest one, older than the city's ban, older than her own name in the guild rolls — and felt nothing. Not weakness. Nothing. The way a hand feels nothing in a glove.

"Ah," she said aloud, to the empty room, in the tone of a woman reading a bill she had been expecting for seven years. "So it's come due."

* * *

Getting down the mountain road alone should have killed her. Perhaps it nearly did. She packed what mattered — the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth; a knife; the phial of poppy syrup, now more medicine than mercy; and the small iron key she had carried against her heart since a night seven years past, the night she had told the guild that Ivo had run away, and watched their faces do arithmetic with grief, and said nothing.

The sunken road took her the way it takes everything: slowly, and with interest. Outlawed magic pooled in its hollows like standing water, and Merrin waded through it up to her knees, feeling it soak into her old wounds, easing some and sharpening others. Her hands shook. Her breath came in increments. Twice she stopped and could not remember why she had stopped, and both times the iron key against her sternum reminded her: because you are a coward who has finally run out of road in every other direction.

She found them by their light — Emberfall's dimming fire painting the cavern walls in the colors of a sunset that was also dying — and she stood at the edge of that vast dark space, leaning on a broken pillar, and listened to Xanthe argue with a dragon about theft.

It was, she thought, exactly the argument she herself had made, seven years ago, in a different register. *We can take it back. There must be a way to take it back.* And the dragon's answer — that nothing in this place is taken, only traded — was the same answer the Magistrate had given her, in a voice like a scale finding its balance.

Merrin stepped into the firelight.

* * *

Xanthe saw her first. They had their back to the entrance, bent over something — a smuggler's cache, a scrap of map — and when they turned, their face did something complicated. Surprise, yes. And beneath the surprise, something harder and older, the look of an apprentice who has found the forgery in the master's hand.

"You followed us," Xanthe said.

"I followed the debt." Merrin's voice came out thinner than she intended, scraped thin by the road. She leaned on the pillar and let it hold some of her weight, because pride was a luxury her legs no longer afforded. "I've been following it for seven years. You've only just caught up."

Emberfall raised her great head, and her eyes — banked coals, nearly ash — regarded the old woman with the terrible patience of a creature who has watched empires spend themselves. "The guildmaster," the dragon said. "I know your scent. I carried your cargo."

The words landed like stones in still water. Xanthe looked from the dragon to Merrin and back, and Merrin watched them arrive at the question, watched it form, watched them refuse to ask it and then ask it anyway.

"What cargo?"

Merrin closed her eyes. When she opened them, she made herself look at Xanthe directly, because looking away was the whole of her crime, distilled.

"A soul," she said. "One soul, freely given. That is what the contract says. I wrote the words myself, so I know them exactly."

* * *

The telling took a long time, because Merrin insisted on telling all of it, and there was a great deal of before in it — the years of the ban, the slow strangulation of the guild, the charm-stalls shuttered one by one, the wards on the border fraying like old rope because rope is all they had ever been without something living knotted into them.

"You have to understand what it was like," she said, and hated herself even as she said it, because it was the beginning of an excuse and she had promised herself no excuses. "No. No, you don't have to understand. Understanding is what I bought with his life, and I won't spend it too."

She started again.

"The city outlawed magic. Not regulated it — outlawed it. Every charter revoked, every practitioner registered and then forbidden. The guild had two choices: dissolve, or go underground. We went underground. We sold luck-knots disguised as trinkets, the way you do now, Xanthe. You learned it from me. Survival as habit. Love disguised as commerce." Her mouth twisted. "But the border wards weren't trinkets. They were the city's skin. And they were failing, and every year they failed faster, and I went to the court beneath the mountain to beg."

"And they drove a bargain," Emberfall said. It was not a question.

"They set terms." Merrin's hands, folded in her lap, had gone white at the knuckles. "Seven years of protection, full strength, the borders sealed, the city safe — in exchange for payment. They named their price the way a merchant names a price: flatly, without cruelty, which is somehow worse. A soul. Freely given. Willingly surrendered. It could not be taken, they said. Taken souls sour. It had to be a gift, or the wards would drink poison instead of power."

"So you found someone willing," Xanthe said quietly.

"No." Merrin lifted her head. "Do not soften this for me. I did not find someone willing. I *made* someone willing. That is the difference, and it is the whole of my damnation."

The cavern was very quiet. Even the drip of water somewhere in the deep seemed to pause.

"Ivo was fourteen," Merrin said. "He was the most gifted student the guild had produced in a century, and he loved the guild the way children love the thing that raises them — absolutely, without audit. He trusted me the way he'd have trusted the ground under his feet. So I sat him down in my study, and I told him the city was dying, and the wards were failing, and there was a bargain that could save everyone, and it needed a soul strong enough in the craft to hold the borders for seven years." She swallowed. "And I told him it had to be given freely, and then I waited, and I let him believe — I *let* him believe — that refusing was possible. That I would have accepted his refusal. He was fourteen. He would have refused if I had frowned. And I did not frown, and I did not say *no, child, don't be absurd, I would never ask this of anyone*, because I needed him to say yes, and he did, because he was good, because we had raised him to be good, and his goodness was the instrument of it."

Xanthe had gone very still. In the dragonlight their face was unreadable, but their hands were not; they had curled into fists so tight the knuckles shone.

"He asked one question," Merrin went on, because stopping now would be another cowardice. "He asked if it would hurt. And I said — gods forgive me, I said — 'Not for long.' Which was true, in the way that a lie built from true bricks is still a wall between you and the truth. His soul left him in less than a minute. Emberfall carried it here. And I came home and I told the guild he had run away, and I let them grieve a runaway instead of a sacrifice, because a runaway doesn't make anyone complicit. And I kept the key to his room. And I slept in my bed. For seven years. While the wards held. Because of him."

"Why?" Xanthe's voice cracked on the single word, split it open. "Why him? Why not — why not yourself? You were guildmaster. Your soul was yours to give. *Freely given*, Merrin. You could have—"

"Because mine wasn't strong enough." It came out of her like a hook from flesh. "That was the Magistrate's judgment, and the Magistrate does not flatter. An old soul, half-spent, tangled in guilt already — it would have held the borders for two years, maybe three, and then the city would have been worse off than before, because the debt would have been paid and spent. Ivo's soul was young and unbroken and bright as a struck bell. Seven years, guaranteed. I did the arithmetic." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as paper burning. "I was a merchant to the last. I weighed a child against a city, and the child lost, and I signed."

She drew the ledger from its oilcloth wrapping and opened it to the final page, and turned it so the firelight fell across the contract — the elegant script, the seal of the court beneath the mountain, and at the bottom, in ink that had never faded because such ink does not fade, her signature. *Merrin Vael, Guildmaster, for the Charmwrights' Guild.*

"I brought it down here to return it," she said. "I know that's nothing. I know returning a receipt doesn't return what was purchased. But I wanted the Magistrate to see my face when I gave it back. I wanted—" Her voice failed, steadied. "There is no version of this where I am forgiven. I've made peace with that. Forgiveness is another currency, and I haven't the funds. I came to pay what I could."

Xanthe was shaking. When they spoke, each word came out level and terrible, laid down like stones. "He called you Aunt Merrin. Did you know that? When you were gone on guild business, he'd sit at the stall with me and practice knots and say, 'Aunt Merrin will want to see this one.' He kept every charm you ever praised. He kept them in a box under his bed. I found the box after he disappeared. It was the only thing he took—"

They stopped. Their face changed.

"It was the only thing missing from his room," Xanthe whispered. "I thought — all these years I thought he took it with him. Wherever he went."

"He didn't take anything anywhere," Merrin said. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry." Three times, because once was not enough and a hundred would not be either, and three was what her ruined voice could carry.

* * *

Emberfall shifted, and the whole cavern shifted with her, scales grinding against stone, her dimming fire throwing shadows that leaned and swayed like mourners. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into something almost gentle — the gentleness of a creature who has seen many debts collected and knows the exact weight of this one.

"The Magistrate will be told you've come," she said to Merrin. "The court hears everything that walks its roads. And the Magistrate remembers you, guildmaster. You are the counterparty of record. If you stand before the scale and offer payment, the court must hear you."

"Payment." Merrin looked down at her own hands, the ink-stained fingers, the veins standing up like roots through thin soil. "What do I have? I'm dying anyway. My soul is half-spent — you heard me say it. What could I possibly—"

"Memory," Emberfall said. "The court trades in memory. It is the currency of this place. A memory freely surrendered weighs against a debt. And you, guildmaster, are carrying seventy years of memories — the founding rites, the first wards, the names of every charmwright who ever tied a knot under this guild's roof. The things you stole from Ivo, you cannot restore. But the things you *are* — those you can put on the scale."

Merrin was silent a long moment. Then she began, very slowly, to laugh again — but this time it broke apart halfway through and became something else, and she pressed her ink-stained hand over her mouth and wept, dry-eyed and heaving, the weeping of a body that has forgotten how to spare itself.

"Yes," she said, when she could speak. "Yes. All of it. Every year except—" She looked at Xanthe. "Every year except the ones I took from him. Those aren't mine to keep or give. Those belong to him, wherever he is, and I'll spend everything else to buy them back."

She rose. It took her a long time, and she used the pillar, and then Xanthe — who crossed the space between them without seeming to decide to, and took her arm, and held it, hard, the grip of someone who is furious and grieving and cannot stop being gentle, all in the same hand.

"If this works," Xanthe said, low, "he'll wake up and he won't know me. Do you understand what you're asking me to pay? Because I've understood since the tollgate. I've been understanding it all the way down."

"I know," Merrin said. "I know exactly what it costs. I priced it myself, seven years ago. That's how I know the court will accept it — they've already valued these goods once." She straightened as much as her body allowed, and turned toward the deep of the cavern, toward the distant glow of bottled souls, toward the cathedral of debts where a boy's soul hung in glass. "Come, then. Let's go and tell the Magistrate the guild is settling its accounts."

And far off in the dark, faint as a heartbeat behind stone, something in the court beneath the mountain began — softly, and with great satisfaction — to laugh.
