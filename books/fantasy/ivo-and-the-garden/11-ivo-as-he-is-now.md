## 11. Ivo, As He Is Now

The dreaming had shapes.

That was the first thing Ivo understood about where he was — not that he was anywhere, exactly, but that the dreaming had shapes, and the shapes had weight, and he could hold them if he held them gently enough. There was a shape like a plum, warm from a windowsill. There was a shape like knotted thread, red and gold, pulled tight in small careful hands that were not his hands. There was a shape like a door closing, and he did not like to hold that one, but it kept drifting back to him anyway, the way a stone sinks.

He lived inside the glass the way a fish lives inside a lamp.

He knew this because sometimes there was light on the other side of the curve of the world — amber light, honeyed and slow, the light of bottled souls burning in their hundreds along the walls of the cathedral dark. His own light was smaller than theirs. Or perhaps it was only folded up small, the way a wing folds, the way a hand folds around something you don't want anyone to see. He could not tell anymore which parts of him were dimmed and which parts were simply tucked away for safekeeping. Seven years was a long time to be folded.

Seven years. He counted them the way other children counted stairs.

Not that he was a child anymore. That was another thing the dreaming had taught him: time moved strangely inside a soul. Bodies aged in one direction, stubborn as water running downhill, but souls could pool. Souls could go backward. Some nights — and the court did not have nights, only shifts of shadow and amber, but he called them nights because Xanthe had taught him what nights were for — some nights he was nine again, sitting cross-legged on the guildhall floor while Xanthe showed him how to twist three threads into a luck-knot without looking. *If you can't do it blind,* they'd said, tapping his knuckles with a wooden stylus, *you don't actually know it. You're just remembering it. Knowing lives deeper than remembering.* And little Ivo had frowned and said that didn't make any sense at all, and Xanthe had laughed and stolen a plum from the market bowl and said, *Neither do most true things, when you first meet them.*

He was not nine now. He was not anything now. He was fourteen and seven and no age at all, all of him pooled together in the glass like rainwater in a jar, every year of himself still present, still layered, none of it allowed to move.

But he dreamed. The Magistrate had not thought to forbid that. Perhaps could not have; even the court beneath the mountain, which traded in everything a person was, had never found a way to tax a dream.

* * *

The dreams came in the order he chose, mostly, and he chose the good ones first, the way you eat the sweet part of the bread before the crust.

He dreamed the plums. There had been a summer — or the memory of a summer, ripened past its truth by retelling — when Xanthe had taken him up onto the guildhouse roof to watch the harbor fires, and they'd eaten stolen plums until the juice ran down their wrists, and Xanthe had told him about the border wards. Not what powered them. Never that. Just that they were there, the way you tell a child the sea is there, vast and old and not their problem. *As long as the wards stand,* Xanthe said, spitting a plum stone off the roof edge into the dark, *nothing gets into this city that means us harm. As long as the guild stands, nothing happens to you. Those are the two promises, Ivo. Everything else is weather.*

Everything else is weather. He had carried that line through seven years of amber dark like a coin under his tongue. It was the last thing they'd said to him before —

Before the door.

He tried not to walk down the corridor toward the door in his dreams, but the dreaming had its own gravity, and grief is heavier than anything else a soul can carry, so most nights he ended up there anyway: the guildhall corridor, lamplit, smelling of tallow and dried lavender, and Merrin standing in her gray robes with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral, which, he understood now, she was. Her own.

*Ivo,* she'd said. *Come here, love.*

And he had come, because he was eleven and she was the guildmaster and she had taught him his letters, and because behind her stood strangers in mountain-gray with a contract case bound in iron, and because no one — not Xanthe, who was somewhere in the city selling charms; not anyone — had told him to run. That was the detail that returned to him most often, turning over and over like a stone in a stream: no one had told him to run. He had been given away by people who loved him, and love, it turned out, made a very poor lock against an open door.

*It will keep the wards alive,* Merrin had said, and her voice had done the thing voices do when they are reciting something written by someone else. *Seven years, Ivo. You'll be safe. You'll be asleep, mostly. It won't hurt.* 

It hadn't hurt. That was the terrible joke of it. Being sold had felt like falling asleep in a warm room, and then it had felt like this: glass, and amber, and the slow pooling of every year of himself, and the sound — if glass could be said to make sound — of a whole city above him forgetting, night by night, that magic had ever needed a price.

They had signed his name. He knew that too. He had seen the ledger once, in a dream that was less a dream than a memory leaking through the mountain's stone, the way cold leaks through walls: the guild ledger, open to its final page, and there in Merrin's careful merchant's hand, *one soul, freely given*, and underneath, the signature, and underneath that — this was the part that made him curl inward, fold tighter, go dim — his own name, added afterward, in ink a different color, as though the soul being given were a line item to be specified. *Ivo.* Just Ivo. No family name, because he'd never been given one. The guild had raised him apprentice-shaped and name-complete except for the part that comes after, the part that says whose you are, and so the contract had taken him whole.

Freely given. He turned those four words over more often than any others. He had not been asked. But someone, somewhere, had checked a box on a form that said *freely*, and the court beneath the mountain honored its paperwork the way the sea honors the moon — absolutely, and without mercy.

* * *

Tonight — his tonight, his shift-of-shadow — the dreaming brought him the knot lesson again.

Three threads. Red, gold, and the third one that had no name because Xanthe had dyed it themselves from madder root and it came out different every batch. *Cross the red over the gold. Tuck the nameless one through. Pull gentle — charms break from hurry, never from strength.* His small hands had fumbled it. His small hands had fumbled it six times, and on the seventh try the knot had come out perfect, tight and symmetrical, a little woven promise, and Xanthe had held it up to the window light and said, *There. Now you know it instead of remembering it.*

In the dream he tied it perfectly on the first try, because dreams let you skip the six failures, and that was the one cruelty the dreaming added of its own accord: everything came easy here, and ease was its own kind of lie. In the dream he tied luck-knots until the floor of the guildhall was carpeted with them, hundreds, thousands, each one a small red-and-gold eye looking up at him, and he knelt among them and understood with a dreamer's terrible clarity that this was what he was now. A knot. Something tied off. Something pulled tight and set aside and kept, because keeping him was useful.

*Pull gentle,* said Xanthe's voice, from everywhere and nowhere, the way dead people and teachers speak in dreams. *Charms break from hurry.*

*You broke,* Ivo told the dream. *You broke from strength.*

The dream did not answer. Dreams never defend themselves. That was how you knew they weren't lies, and also how you knew they weren't mercies.

* * *

Then the light changed.

He noticed it before he understood it, the way you notice weather through a wall — a pressure, a difference in the amber. The cathedral dark outside his glass was always lit the same way, the hundred bottles burning their steady honeyed burn, the Magistrate's scales hanging from the vaulted black rock like sleeping bats. But now a new light was moving through the cavern. Small. Red-gold. Unsteady, guttering, alive in a way the bottled light had forgotten how to be.

Firelight. Real firelight. He had not seen real firelight in seven years, and his whole pooled soul leaned toward it the way a plant leans, without decision, without shame.

And with the firelight came footsteps. And with the footsteps came a smell — tallow and lavender and madder-dyed thread and the faint char-smoke that clung to charm-sellers' sleeves forever — and Ivo's dreaming, which had spent seven years arranging and rearranging the furniture of his memory like a prisoner rearranging a cell, went suddenly, completely silent.

No. He thought it wrong, the way you think anything wrong when hope arrives too large to fit through the door of you. No, that's the dream making shapes again. That's the plum-shape. That's the roof-shape. Dreams borrow faces. Dreams are cruel in exactly this way—

Through the curve of the glass, distorted but unmistakable, moving with the particular half-cautious half-reckless gait of someone who has already decided to be caught: Xanthe.

Older. Thinner. A scar he didn't know crossing one brow. Hair gone longer, streaked with the ash-gray of the starless years. But the hands — he would have known the hands in ten thousand distortions, in a thousand years of amber — the hands with the ink stains and the burn speckle and the crooked second knuckle on the left from the time young Ivo had accidentally warded the practice table too well and Xanthe had pried a stuck charm loose with bare fingers rather than wait. The hands stopped an arm's length from his bottle. The face above them pressed close, and Ivo watched Xanthe's mouth form his name — no sound crossed the glass, or maybe no sound crossed the dreaming, it hardly mattered — watched the lips say *Ivo, Ivo, Ivo*, three times, like a knock on a door.

He reached for them.

He had no body to reach with. That should have ended it. But a soul in a bottle learns what a soul can do, and longing is older than anatomy, and so he reached the only way left to him: he pushed everything he was against the inside of the glass. Every pooled year. The nine-year-old with the fumbled knots. The eleven-year-old walking down the corridor. The plum-summer. The roof. The two promises. The four words, *freely given*, worn smooth as riverstone from handling. All of it, pressed flat against the curve of the world, from the inside.

And Xanthe — Xanthe pressed back.

Palm flat against the glass. Fingers spread. The warmth of a living hand bleeding through the barrier in a thin bright line, and Ivo aligned himself with that line the way you align yourself with sunlight in a cell, greedily, desperately, drinking it. For a moment — one amber moment, one shift-of-shadow — it almost worked. It almost felt like holding hands across an impossible distance. It almost felt like the guildhall, lamplit, the floor scattered with finished charms, everything else just weather.

Then he saw Xanthe's face change, and understood, with a dreamer's awful clarity, what the face was saying even through the distortion, even through the glass, even through seven years:

*You're awake in there. You've been awake in there the whole time.*

Yes, Ivo thought, pressing harder, pouring himself flatter against the barrier, yes, yes, all of me, every year, I remember the plums and the knots and the roof and the door and the ledger and my own name written in the wrong-colored ink, I remember being freely given, I remember everything, I have had nothing to do for seven years but remember everything —

Take me out. Take me out take me out take me —

But even as he thought it, even as he hammered his whole pooled self against the glass like a moth against a window, he saw the thing in Xanthe's eyes that made him stop. Not hesitation. Not doubt. Something worse: arithmetic. He knew that look. Charm-sellers' look, guild look, the quick inward flicker of a person pricing something. Xanthe was looking at the bottle and doing sums, and Ivo, who had grown up watching that exact expression across market stalls, understood before he wanted to understand that in this place, this cathedral of debts, everything had a price, and the look on his beloved's face was the look of someone realizing they had walked into the world's cruelest shop with empty pockets.

*Xanthe,* he said, though the glass took his voice, or the dreaming did, or the mountain did. *Xanthe, don't pay whatever it asks. Do you hear me? Whatever it costs — I've seen what it keeps in here. I've seen what people leave with. Don't—*

The firelight behind Xanthe guttered low — the dragon, he realized, the dying dragon from the stories, her flame sinking like a tide going out — and shadows climbed the cathedral walls, and somewhere far off in the black rock the Magistrate's scales shifted with a sound like a held breath.

Xanthe's palm stayed on the glass. Xanthe's mouth was moving — telling him something, promising something, the lips shaping words he couldn't hear and understood anyway, because some promises don't need sound. *I'm getting you out. Whatever it takes.*

*That's what I'm afraid of,* Ivo thought, and pressed his hand — his shapeless, bodiless, seven-years-pooled hand — against the inside of the glass, matching palm to palm, the last charm either of them would ever tie: two threads crossed, one nameless one tucked through, pulled gentle.

Outside, beyond the amber and the dark, something in the court had begun to move.
