## 12. First Attempt: Theft

The alcove where they hid was a seam in the cavern wall, barely wide enough for Xanthe's shoulders, and it smelled of cold iron and old smoke. From inside it, the Court Beneath the Mountain spread itself out below them like a cathedral that had been built by something that had heard of cathedrals only secondhand — all the reverence and none of the mercy.

Bottled souls hung from the vaulted dark in their thousands, each one a small captive sun, each one humming a note too low for ears and too deep to ignore. They lit the cavern in colors that had no business existing underground: amber, bruise-violet, a green like pond water over drowned things. And at the center of it all, on a plinth of black glass raised higher than any other, hung the bottle that mattered.

Ivo.

Even from here, even across the whole breadth of that terrible hall, Xanthe could see him. Not his body — his body was seven years gone, given away or buried or worse — but *him*, the shape of him, folded into the light inside the glass like a figure pressed between the pages of a book. He turned, slow as a season changing. His hand came up against the inner curve of the bottle, palm flat, fingers spread, the way he used to press his hand against the frost on the guildhall window when he was small and the world outside seemed like a country he hadn't been invited to.

*He knows we're here,* Xanthe thought, and the thought went through them like a hook through soft flesh. *He knows, and he is reaching.*

Beside them in the seam of stone, Emberfall made a sound low in her ruined chest. It might have been a word in a language older than the mountain. Her scales had gone the color of ash that still remembers being wood; only along her throat did the old fire move now, a sluggish gold pulse, like a heart deciding whether to keep its appointment.

"You're going to do something foolish," she said. Her voice was gravel rolled in honey, and it was thinner than it had been on the road down. Everything about her was thinner. "I can hear you planning it. You have a face for planning foolish things."

"I have a face for getting things done," Xanthe said.

"You have a face for getting things *broken.*" The dragon's eye, huge and lidless and ancient, swiveled toward them. In its depths Xanthe could see their own reflection, small and stubborn and lit by borrowed soul-light. "I carried that soul here myself, charm-seller. I know this court. I know what moves when it is moved against."

"Then tell me what moves," Xanthe said, "and I'll move first."

Emberfall was quiet a long moment. Below, the Magistrate of Debts drifted between the hanging bottles with the unhurried grace of a spider that has never once needed to hurry. Its robes were ledger-pages, Xanthe had realized on first sight — actual pages, hundreds of them, sewn into a garment that whispered with every step, each leaf covered in terms and conditions in a script that hurt to look at directly. Where the Magistrate passed, the bottled souls dimmed slightly, the way candles lean away from a draft.

"The guards are not guards," Emberfall said at last. "They are consequences. The court does not post soldiers around its treasures; it posts *prices*. Touch what is not yours, and the price collects itself." Her tail curled, slow, around the base of the seam. "The bottle you want sits under the oldest consequence in the hall. It has sat there seven years without so much as dust settling on it. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means no one has tried."

"It means everyone who tried is part of the architecture now." She nodded her great head toward the walls, and Xanthe looked, and wished they hadn't. The seams of the cavern were not all stone. Here and there, shapes were set into the rock the way jewels are set into a crown — figures mid-stride, mid-reach, faces full of the particular surprise of people who had thought themselves quick enough. Some of the shapes wore the cut of old guild cloaks.

Xanthe's mouth went dry. "Those are—"

"Debtors," Emberfall said. "Thieves. Petitioners who grew impatient. The court keeps its accounts in flesh when coin runs short." The fire along her throat pulsed, guttered, steadied. "So. Knowing this. Tell me your plan anyway. I find I want to hear it, the way one wants to hear a fool describe a cliff."

Xanthe leaned back against the stone and let the plan assemble itself out loud, because saying it would make it real, and making it real would make it impossible to turn back from, and turning back had stopped being an option somewhere on the sunken road, maybe earlier, maybe the night the stars went out.

"There's a procession at the change of the watch," they said. "I've counted three since we got here. When the Magistrate goes to the far gallery to inspect the new acquisitions, every consequence in the hall leans toward *him*. Did you notice? The bottles brighten. The walls relax. For perhaps fifty heartbeats, the court watches its master instead of its merchandise."

"I noticed," Emberfall said. "You count well. Counting will not save you."

"Fifty heartbeats is enough to cross the floor if I go the way the shadows fall. The plinth isn't warded separately — it *can't* be, because the warding is the hall itself, and when the hall looks away—" Xanthe stopped. Breathed. "I take the bottle. I run. You burn us a door."

"And if the hall does not look away when you expect it to?"

"Then I'll improvise."

"That," Emberfall said, "is the word every shape in that wall said first."

---

They waited for the change of the watch.

It came the way tide comes — not suddenly but inevitably, a shifting of pressure in the cavern's vast air. Somewhere above, bells that were not bells rang notes that were felt rather than heard, and the Magistrate of Debts turned from the central floor and began the long drift toward the western gallery, page-robes whispering, and Xanthe watched the hall lean after him like grass leaning after wind.

The bottles brightened. The walls — Xanthe tested this with a fingertip against the stone — the walls *relaxed*, the watching pressure easing by a degree that would have meant nothing anywhere else and meant everything here.

"Now," Xanthe breathed, and slipped from the seam.

The floor of the court was smooth black glass, and it took their footsteps and gave back nothing — no echo, no scuff, just silence swallowing silence. Xanthe moved the way they'd moved a thousand times through the back lanes of a city that forbade them, weight low, breath shallow, eyes reading the space ahead in flicks rather than stares. Left past a pillar of veined crystal. Right along the shadow of a buttress that arched up into darkness. The hanging souls passed overhead, close enough to touch, and each one hummed as Xanthe went beneath it — a rising note, curious, like dogs lifting their heads at a passing stranger.

*Don't notice me. Don't notice me. I'm nothing. I'm a debt already paid.*

Forty heartbeats. The plinth rose ahead, black glass shot through with veins of silver, and the bottle sat at its crown in a cradle of wrought terms, and Ivo —

Ivo was pressed against the glass, both hands now, and his light was flaring, stuttering, the way a candle flutters when someone runs toward it. Through the soul-stuff of him his face was a boy's face and then a young man's face and then both at once, seven years compressed into a single reaching expression, and his mouth was moving, though no sound crossed the glass.

Xanthe read the shape of the words rather than hearing them. Seven years of teaching him to speak clearly across a crowded market had taught Xanthe to read lips at forty paces.

*Don't. Run. Please.*

Thirty heartbeats.

"I'm not leaving without you," Xanthe whispered, which was absurd, because he couldn't hear, and because it was exactly the kind of promise they'd sworn never to make again — the guild had run on promises like that, warm ones, cheap ones, the kind Merrin used to hand out like sweets before handing out the bills. But they said it anyway, climbing the plinth's smooth flank, fingers finding holds in the silver veins, and the bottle loomed above them, close enough now to see the fine grain of the glass, close enough to see that the cradle holding it was not metal at all but *writing*, dense interlocking clauses, and that the whole arrangement pulsed faintly with each beat of Xanthe's own traitor heart.

Twenty heartbeats.

Xanthe reached out and closed their hand around the neck of the bottle.

For one instant — one golden, weightless instant — nothing happened. The glass was warm. Warmer than it should have been, warm as a living thing, and through their palm Xanthe felt Ivo's light press toward their fingers like a cheek leaning into a known hand, and a sound came out of them that they would be ashamed of later, half sob and half laugh, and they pulled.

The bottle did not move.

Xanthe pulled harder, two-handed now, boots braced against the silver veins, hauling with the desperate strength of someone who has already spent everything else. The bottle tilted — a fraction, a hair's width, hope leaping —

And the writing woke.

Every clause in the cradle flared white, and the flare ran outward along the veins in the plinth, down into the floor, out across the black glass in branching lines faster than falling, and the hum of the thousand bottled souls rose all at once into a single vast chord that was not a sound so much as a *verdict*. The hall stopped leaning after its master. The hall turned. Xanthe felt it turn, felt the attention of the whole cathedral-dark place swing onto them like a lighthouse beam, and in that attention there was no anger at all, which was the worst part. Only interest. Only arithmetic.

"Oh," said the court, in a voice that came from the walls and the floor and the bottles overhead and possibly from inside Xanthe's own teeth. "*Oh. An unsecured withdrawal.*"

The consequences stood up out of the walls.

That was the only way Xanthe could ever describe it afterward. The debtor-shapes set into the stone peeled free — not walking so much as *unfolding*, joints bending in sequences bodies were never meant to bend in, faces still wearing their seven-hundred-year-old surprise — and they came across the glass floor without footfalls, a dozen of them, twenty, more, closing from every side with the smooth inevitability of water finding a drain.

Xanthe did the arithmetic too. Twenty consequences, one thief, fifty feet of open floor between the plinth and the nearest cover, and a dragon whose fire was down to embers.

There was no version of this math that worked.

But the bottle was warm in their hands, and Ivo's light was flaring against their palms like a heartbeat hammering, and Xanthe Blackwood had spent seven years selling luck they didn't believe in to people who needed to believe in something, and they found they could not make their hands let go.

"EMBERFALL!" they screamed, and the word cracked across the hall, and the court's attention split — half on the thief, half swinging toward the seam in the wall where the ash-colored dragon unfolded herself with a groan of stressed stone and dying scale.

"*Foolish child,*" Emberfall said — to Xanthe, to the court, to no one, to the whole unjust architecture of debts — and opened her mouth, and burned.

Not the thin gold pulse of the road down. Not the guttering candle-flame she'd nursed through the Tollgate. This was the old fire, the deep fire, the fire she had been hoarding against her own ending, and she spent it all in one breath. A lance of white-gold flame crossed the cathedral and struck the floor between Xanthe and the advancing consequences, and the black glass *melted*, ran like wax, and the heat of it reached Xanthe even thirty feet away and turned their next breath to swallowing a forge.

The consequences did not scream. That was somehow worse. They simply stopped, the front rank of them sagging into half-molten glass, and behind them the others paused — recalculating, Xanthe thought wildly, *even the prices recalculate* — and in that pause Emberfall came on, wings beating fire instead of air, and hooked one vast foreclaw into the plinth's edge and tore it open like bread.

"Take him and RUN," she roared, and the fire in her throat was already failing, the white-gold collapsing back to orange, to red, to a sullen ember-glow, and Xanthe saw — everyone saw — how much of her had gone into that single breath. Her scales were cracking along her flanks. Beneath the cracks, instead of flesh, there was only light, thinning.

Xanthe wrenched the bottle free of the broken cradle. It came loose all at once, startlingly easy now, as if the court had decided to allow it — and that wrongness registered a half-second too late, the way a trap registers too late, always.

The floor beneath the plinth opened.

Not collapsed — *opened*, deliberately, a round mouth of perfect darkness ringed with fresh-written clauses still glowing wetly, and Xanthe dropped toward it with the bottle clutched to their chest, and Emberfall dove, and caught them. Claws closed around Xanthe's body — gently, so gently, the gentleness of a creature holding something breakable while everything else broke — and she wheeled upward through the cathedral dark as the hole yawned wider and things began to rise out of it. Not consequences. Something older. Shapes made of stacked paper and sealed wax, tall as the pillars, unfolding upward without end.

The Magistrate of Debts had turned from its gallery. It did not hurry. It did not need to. Its page-robes whispered one word as it turned, and the word fell through the hall like a stone through water:

"*Interest.*"

Emberfall flew. Xanthe, crushed against her burning-cool scales, watched the ceiling of the cavern rush down toward them and saw, with terrible clarity, that there was no exit in it. The sunken road had sealed itself behind them the moment they'd entered the court; the mountain had swallowed the door. There was nowhere to fly *to*.

"Emberfall," Xanthe shouted over the wind of her wings. "There's no way out!"

"There is always a way out," the dragon said, and her voice was ragged now, fraying like rope. "It is simply priced." She banked hard, claws tightening, and dove — not for a wall but for the great western pillar, the one veined with crystal, and Xanthe understood a heartbeat before it happened and had time only to bury their face against her neck and hold the bottle and say, stupidly, uselessly, "Wait — wait —"

She hit the pillar at full speed, and burned.

All of her. What was left. The last of the fire she'd carried across the ages poured out of her in a single detonating bloom that shattered the pillar and blew a hole through the cavern wall into raw rock, and through raw rock into older rock, and through that into the long dark throat of some abandoned vein of the sunken road, and Xanthe felt the world become heat and noise and the terrible slowing of everything, debris and dragonlight and the screaming of stone, and then cold air, and motion, and dark.

---

They fetched up somewhere in the deep road, a hundred yards of tumbling and sliding and one impact Xanthe would feel in their ribs for weeks. Silence followed, thick as wool. Dust drifted. Somewhere far behind, muffled by tons of mountain, the court was sealing its wound — Xanthe could feel it doing so, a slow knitting pressure in the air, patient as interest accruing.

"Emberfall." Their voice came out shredded. "Emberfall, talk to me."

The dragon lay half-curled against the shattered tunnel wall, and she was dark. Not ash-dark now. Dark the way a hearth is dark in the morning, dark the way a thing is dark when the thing itself has gone out of it. Only along her throat, deep down beneath the cracked scales, one last thread of gold still moved — faint, irregular, a pulse counting down to something.

"I am talking," she said, and even her voice had gone to embers. "You are simply not listening closely enough." One enormous eye found Xanthe, and found the bottle still clutched against their chest, and something moved through what remained of her face that was almost a smile. "You kept him. Foolish, brave, terrible little merchant. You kept him."

Xanthe looked down at the bottle. Inside, Ivo's light was dimmed — not extinguished, never that, but drawn small, huddled, the way a child huddles after a storm. As Xanthe watched, one small hand pressed flat against the glass again. Reaching. Toward them, or toward the door of the world, or toward nothing at all.

"He told me to run," Xanthe said. Their voice cracked in the middle. "Before any of it. He was telling me to run, and I—"

"Yes," Emberfall said. "He has been telling this court to release him for seven years. It has not listened. Neither did you." The gold thread along her throat flickered. "Do not mistake me. I am glad you didn't listen. Gladness is cheap for the dying; spend it freely." Her eye slid half-closed. "But hear the lesson, charm-seller, because I bought it dear tonight and I'll not have it wasted. Force does not work here. Theft does not work here. This court was built by creatures who assumed everything would eventually be taken from them, and they built accordingly. Every lock in that hall is also a scale. Every guard is also a bill. You cannot overpower a place whose entire substance is *price*."

"Then what works?" Xanthe's hands were shaking. They looked at them shaking, and hated them. "What works in a place like that?"

The dragon was silent so long that Xanthe thought she had gone out entirely. Then the gold thread pulsed, stronger, and her eye opened fully, and in it was the old fire after all — banked, but present, the way a word is present in a closed mouth.

"Payment," she said. "Only payment. Equivalent, willing, and witnessed. That is the one law older than the Magistrate, older than the mountain. Even a court of debts must honor a debt paid in full." Her breath rattled. "You cannot steal a soul from this place, Xanthe Blackwood. But a soul can be *bought back*. If you have currency it values."

Xanthe laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked. "Currency. Look at me. I've got a luck-knot in my pocket and blood in my boot. What could I possibly have that a place like that wants?"

Emberfall held their gaze. Held it until the laughter died. Held it until Xanthe's own understanding arrived, walking slowly into them like cold, and they saw it, and they wished with their whole hollowed heart that they hadn't.

"You know what you carry," the dragon said softly. "You have carried it since the night the stars went out. Richer coin than anything hanging in that hall. The court trades in memory, little merchant. And no one alive remembers Ivo better than you."

Far off down the dark road, water dripped. The gold thread in Emberfall's throat flickered, steadied, flickered. Against Xanthe's chest, warm as a sleeping child, the bottle hummed its low note — and inside it, Ivo pressed his hand to the glass, and waited, the way he had learned to wait, for whatever the grown-ups would decide about him next.
