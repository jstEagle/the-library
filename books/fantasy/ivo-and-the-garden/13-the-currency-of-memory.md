## 13. The Currency of Memory

Emberfall had not hidden in a small place since she was a hatchling.

The smuggler's alcove sat off the sunken road like an oyster's pearl set wrong — a hollow scooped into the living rock, hung with moth-eaten tapestries that smelled of dust and old salt and the ghost of contraband. Someone had loved this place once: there were iron hooks for lanterns, a soot-blackened hearth no larger than a bread oven, shelves carved directly into the stone where bottles of stolen wine had slept through decades of prohibition. Now the shelves held only emptiness and the occasional blind white beetle that had never known the sun.

She did not fit. That was the truth of it, plain as a broken wing. She folded herself into the alcove's mouth with her tail curled three times around her own haunches and her wings pressed flat against her spine until they ached, and even then her snout nearly touched the far wall, and her breath fogged the ancient tapestries with each slow exhalation.

*This is what I have become,* she thought, watching the humans across the small chamber. *A dragon who hides in a cupboard.*

Her scales had gone from ember-orange to something like old copper in the dark — still warm to the touch, she supposed, but no longer giving light unless she willed it, and each time she willed it now, it cost her. She could feel the arithmetic of herself running down. Every flame she had burned in the court's defenses, every spark she had spat at the bottle-golems while Xanthe fled with empty hands, had been a coin drawn from a purse that did not refill. Dragons were not made to spend themselves in pieces. They were made to burn all at once, at the end, gloriously — or to fade slowly into stone and become hills.

She was doing neither. She was doing something worse. She was doing it *gradually*, in service of a charm-seller's stubbornness, and she found that she could not bring herself to regret it.

Across the alcove, Xanthe Blackwood sat with their back against the hearth's cold stones, cleaning the blood off their hands with a rag gone grey from use. The blood was their own — mostly. A shard of the court's defense-magic had opened a long cut along their forearm when the theft failed, and the wound had a strange quality Emberfall did not like: the edges glimmered faintly, like water catching moonlight, as though the magic that had cut them was reluctant to fully leave.

"You should let me look at that," Emberfall said. Her voice filled the small space the way her body did — too large, too resonant, rattling a shelf of empty bottles until they chimed a thin, mournful note.

"I've had worse." Xanthe did not look up. Their voice was flat in the way Emberfall had learned meant the opposite — that everything underneath was burning. "I once sold a fever-charm that turned out to be a love-charm. Two families nearly started a feud over it."

"That is not worse."

"No," Xanthe admitted. The rag stilled. "It isn't." They looked down at their forearm, at the glimmering edges of the cut, and Emberfall saw their jaw tighten. "I almost had him, Emberfall. He was *right there*. I could see his face through the glass. His eyes moved. He looked at me."

*He reached,* Emberfall thought but did not say. She had seen it too, from her position near the vaulted ceiling of the cathedral cavern — the small hand inside the soul-bottle pressing flat against the glass, the light within flaring like a struck match when Xanthe drew near. The court had called it resonance. The Magistrate had called it *delightful*. Emberfall had wanted, with an old and terrible ferocity, to take the whole cavern in her jaws and shake it until its bottled stars rained down and shattered.

Instead she had burned a path out, and spent nearly everything she had left doing it, and here they were. In a cupboard. With a wounded charm-seller and a plan that had died on the cathedral floor.

"The golems are not the problem," Xanthe said. "I could have handled the golems if I'd had another minute. It's the wards on the bottle itself. Did you see them? They weren't keyed to force. I hit them with everything in my kit and they just... drank it. Like the magic fed them."

"They are fed by the same thing that feeds the court," Emberfall said. "Debt. Taking. The weight of what is owed. You cannot steal back a debt, little merchant. Stealing only deepens it."

Xanthe threw the rag down. "Then what? We walk away? We climb back up the road and tell the city sorry, we tried, hope you enjoy the dark?"

"I did not say walk away." Emberfall shifted, and her wing joint popped like a green log splitting, and she winced inwardly at the sound. Once, that joint had carried her over the Teethwind Mountains in a single day. "I said you cannot steal it. I did not say it cannot be taken back. There is a difference, and the difference is the reason I came down here instead of dying quietly in some northern cave like a respectable dragon."

That got Xanthe's attention. They looked up, and in the dimness their eyes caught what little light remained in the alcove — which, at present, was Emberfall herself, her copper scales throwing back a faint dull gleam like coals banked for the night.

"Talk," Xanthe said.

"First, the arm. If the court's magic stays in the wound it will keep a thread open to you, and threads can be pulled. I have seen it done. I will not watch it be done to you."

Xanthe hesitated — pride, Emberfall guessed, or habit; a person who had survived by keeping their wounds private — and then crossed the small chamber and knelt by her foreleg. Emberfall lowered her head and breathed out, carefully, a flame no larger than a candle's. Not fire, exactly. Her fire had properties the ordinary kind lacked, when she chose: it could clean without burning, seal without scarring, warm without consuming. It was the oldest magic she knew, older than the wards, older than the guild, older perhaps than the mountain. She traced her flame along the cut and watched the glimmering edges hiss and dim and finally go ordinary, and watched Xanthe's skin knit closed beneath a thin crust of gold that would fade in a day or two.

"There," she said. "Now you are only a fool, not a tethered one."

"Thank you," Xanthe said, and then, quieter, examining the gold-flecked scar: "You're spending yourself on me. I know what your fire costs. I'm not stupid."

"No. You are merely reckless, which is more dangerous and more useful." Emberfall settled her chin onto the cold stone floor. It was undignified. She found she no longer cared. "Listen to me now, because what I know, I learned a long time ago, from a creature older than I am, and I have never told a human before. The court beneath the mountain is not a kingdom. It is a *market*. Everything else — the Magistrate, the golems, the cathedral cavern, the cruelty — is architecture built around that one truth. It trades. That is all it does. It has traded since before your city had a name."

"Trades what? Souls. I saw the bottles."

"Souls, yes. But souls are only the heaviest coin." Emberfall closed her eyes, letting the old lesson rise in her memory like silt settling out of disturbed water. "Everything a soul is made of can be weighed and exchanged. Years. Talents. Loves. Names. And memories — memories above all. Memory is the finest currency the court knows, because memory is how a soul knows itself. Take a soul's memories and you have not killed it. You have done something the court finds far more interesting. You have *unmade its ownership of itself*. What remains can be bought and sold without ever struggling."

Xanthe had gone very still. Emberfall felt the stillness spread across the alcove like frost.

"You're saying," Xanthe said slowly, "that a soul can be ransomed."

"I am saying the court accepts equivalent payment. It must — it is bound by its own ledgers, its own laws of exchange. Even the Magistrate cannot simply give away an asset. But a debt may be discharged, if payment of equal weight is offered. Freely offered. Willingly paid." Emberfall opened her eyes. "A soul for a soul is the crudest exchange, and the court rarely allows it — a soul given freely is rare beyond price. But the components of a soul..." She paused. This was the part she had been circling since the cathedral, the part that tasted like ash in her mouth, and she made herself say it plainly, because dragons do not soften hard truths and she would not start now. "Memories of sufficient weight can stand in for a soul entirely. The ledger does not care what the payment is made of. Only that it balances."

The silence that followed was long enough that Emberfall heard the beetles moving on the shelves, tiny blind bodies ticking against glass.

"How much," Xanthe said at last. Their voice was careful. Controlled. The voice of a merchant asking a price they already dreaded. "How much memory weighs as much as a soul?"

"It depends on the memories." Emberfall turned her head so she could see Xanthe's face properly, and wished she hadn't, because she could see the understanding arriving in it already, dawning like a bruise. "The court values what is genuine, what is deep-rooted, what is *loved*. Seven years of idle acquaintance would buy nothing. But—" 

"But seven years of raising a child," Xanthe said. "Teaching him. Feeding him. Loving him like—"

They stopped. Emberfall waited.

"Like family," Xanthe finished, barely above a whisper. "Like my own."

*There it is,* Emberfall thought, and something in her chest — the place where her fire lived, the place that was running low — clenched like a fist. *There is the richest coin in this room.*

She had known it since the cathedral cavern, if she was honest, since the moment she saw Xanthe press their palms against the bottle and speak to the light inside in a voice she had never heard them use for any living creature — tender and wrecked and utterly unguarded. She had known it the way a dying animal knows which way the water lies. The court wanted weight, and weight meant love, and the greatest love in this entire sunken world was locked inside a charm-seller who had climbed into hell to fetch back a boy everyone else had written off as collateral.

"You would not lose everything," Emberfall said, and heard how gently her great voice could go when it chose. "The court takes what is given. Precisely what is given. If you gave it your memories of him — the plum trees, the charm-knots, the lessons, the years — you would walk out of here knowing that you had done a great and costly thing, but not knowing why. Not knowing *him*. The love would remain. Love is deeper than memory; it is the ground memory grows in. But it would be shapeless. A warmth with no story attached. You would grieve without knowing what you lost. You would see the boy restored and feel nothing you could name."

Xanthe stared at her. And then — this was the moment Emberfall would remember, later, when there was very little else left to remember — Xanthe began, very quietly, to laugh.

"What," Emberfall said.

"A warmth with no story attached." Xanthe shook their head. Their eyes were wet and the laughter was terrible. "Do you know what I was doing when the stars went out? I was selling luck-knots to dockworkers and pretending my guild wasn't dead. Do you know what I've done my whole life? Wrapped real things in fake paper so they could survive the daylight. Maybe that's all love ever is, Emberfall. A warmth looking for a story to wear. If the story goes — if I trade it away — the warmth stays. Somewhere. In me. In *him*, maybe. Maybe that's enough. Maybe it has to be."

"It does not *have* to be," Emberfall said sharply. Fire flickered at the corner of her mouth before she mastered it, and the effort cost her; she felt the dimming in her scales like a step taken downstairs in the dark. "Do not decide tonight. Do not decide in a smuggler's hole with your blood still wet. The court has stood ten thousand years; it will not collapse because you slept on it. There may be another way. There may be other payments. Your mentor, Merrin — she carries the founding memories of the guild, the old contracts, the original bargain. Heavy things. Guilty things. If anyone else in this mountain has currency—"

"Merrin signed him away." Xanthe's voice went cold. "Merrin doesn't get to buy her way out of this with *convenient* guilt."

"No. But she might buy part of *him* out. Debt is debt, whoever pays it." Emberfall lifted her head, joints protesting, and looked toward the alcove's entrance — the low crawl of passage leading back toward the sunken road, where the outlawed magic pooled and glimmered like standing water, where somewhere behind them the court's pursuit had gone strangely quiet. Too quiet. Courts did not forgive failed thefts. They waited. They calculated. They adjusted their terms.

And there was something else. Something she had sensed on the flight down, half-dreaming in her exhaustion: a second heartbeat following them along the road. Old. Stubborn. Human. Carrying a lantern it could barely hold.

"She followed us, didn't she," Xanthe said, reading her stillness. "Merrin. All the way down."

"I believe so."

"Of course she did." Xanthe pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. When they spoke again, the cold had cracked, and what showed underneath was seven years old and grieving. "Everyone I have ever loved keeps selling themselves to pay for things. That's the whole history of my life. And now I'm sitting here doing the arithmetic myself, Emberfall. I can't stop doing it. Plum trees. Stolen plums. His hands learning the knot. The way he laughed when the ward-sparks tickled his nose. I'm counting them like coins and I hate myself and I *can't stop*, because he's down here in a bottle and I'm the only one who remembers what he's worth."

Emberfall said nothing. There was nothing a dragon could say to that. She only extended one vast wing, slowly, and draped its edge over the kneeling charm-seller like a blanket, like a roof, like the last warm thing in a starless world — and together, in the dark of the smuggler's alcove, they listened to footsteps approaching down the sunken road, slow and uneven and unmistakably mortal, coming to offer whatever they had left.

* * *

Later — much later, after the footsteps had arrived and stopped outside, after Xanthe had risen with murder and mercy fighting openly on their face — Emberfall lay with her chin on the stone and her fire guttering low, and did the only arithmetic that mattered to her.

She had perhaps one great flame left in her. Perhaps less. Enough to burn a path, or seal a door, or tip a scale. Not all three.

Somewhere below them, in a cathedral of bottled light, a scale was waiting to be balanced. And Emberfall, whose whole long life had been spent learning what fire was for, understood at last that she had been carrying her final payment since before she met either of these stubborn humans.

She only hoped the humans would not hate her for spending it.

Outside the alcove, in the dark of the passage, an old woman's voice said, trembling and resolute: "Xanthe. It's Merrin. Open the door. I've come to settle accounts."

Emberfall closed her eyes, and banked her last flame, and waited.
