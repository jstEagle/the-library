## 14. Merrin's Last Payment

Merrin had walked the smugglers' alcoves of the sunken road before, though not in this lifetime and not with these knees. She remembered — dimly, the way one remembers a house from childhood — the drip of mineral water from ceilings that had never seen sky, the way outlawed magic pooled in the low places like rainwater in a cellar, silvering every stone it touched. The court beneath the mountain had been older than the guild when the guild was young. It had been old when the city was a fishing camp. And it was patient in the way only very old things are patient: without hurry, without mercy, without any need at all to lie.

She found them by following the sound.

Not footsteps — Xanthe moved quietly even now, even grieving, a habit Merrin herself had taught them at nine years old: *walk like you owe someone money.* No, what she followed was Emberfall's breathing, a long labored bellows-sound echoing off the tunnel walls, and beneath it the smaller sound she would have known blindfolded, deafened, dead: Xanthe crying. Not loudly. Never loudly. The way they'd cried when their mother left, at seven, standing in the guildhall doorway with a bundle of clothes and no goodbye — silently, furiously, as if tears were a debt they resented paying.

Merrin stood in the mouth of the alcove with her hand braced against wet stone and let herself hear it for three full breaths. Then she stepped into the light of the dragon's guttering fire.

Xanthe looked up. Their face did something complicated — relief and shame and anger braided together so tightly she couldn't have unpicked one thread if she'd tried. "You shouldn't be here," they said. "You can barely walk on the flat."

"I've been walking toward this place for seven years," Merrin said. "I just took the long way around."

Emberfall lifted her head from her forepaws. Up close, dying, the dragon was still vast — a hillside that had decided to breathe, a furnace banked down to coals. Her scales had gone the color of ash with veins of ember running through them, brightening and dimming with each breath like a lamp running out of oil. One ancient eye fixed on Merrin, gold shot through with grey.

"You," the dragon said. Her voice was a landslide slowed to a whisper. "The signer."

Merrin did not flinch, though everything in her wanted to. "Me," she agreed. "Guildmaster Merrin Ashgrave, of the Charmwrights' Guild, third of that name." She said it clearly, formally, the way one says a name before an executioner — because names were currency down here, because she had come down into the dark intending to spend hers down to the last copper, and because a debt should be acknowledged by its debtor out loud, where the owed could hear it.

* * *

They sat around the dragon's fading warmth while Merrin told it — all of it, or as much as her voice could carry.

How the border wards had been failing for years before the collapse, thinning like cloth rubbed too often against a stone. How the council's diviners had read the fraying sigils and pronounced, with the serene confidence of people who would not live long enough to be wrong, that the wards were dying of natural decay. How Merrin alone had known better, because she had built half of them, and she knew they weren't dying. They were being *drawn upon*. Something below was drinking from the top of the well.

"And then the city outlawed us," Xanthe said quietly. It wasn't a question. They had been fourteen when the Edict of Severance came down from the capital — magic restricted, licensed, taxed into impossibility, and charmwork outright criminalized. Merrin remembered Xanthe at fourteen, memorizing the edict's clauses faster than the lawyers who wrote them, asking *why*, always why, and Merrin giving answers made of smoke.

"The guild faced extinction," Merrin said. "Two hundred years of craft, two hundred years of protecting this city from things the city doesn't believe in, and overnight we were criminals for doing it. Fines we couldn't pay. Seizures. Wrenna was arrested for selling a fever-charm to a child with lung-rot — a child, Xanthe. They put her in irons for keeping a six-year-old alive." Her hands shook; she folded them in her lap like a woman at prayer. "And the wards kept fraying. And I went looking for help in the only direction I hadn't tried. Down."

She had gone alone, at night, through the sunken road with a lantern and a ledger-page of terms drafted by a lawyer who dealt in contracts no lawful court would enforce. She had expected monsters. She had gotten instead the Magistrate of Debts — courteous, unhurried, seated at a desk of black stone in a cathedral cavern lit by bottled lights, offering tea that smelled of nothing at all.

"It didn't want coin," Merrin said. "Coin is nothing to it. It wanted *weight*. A payment of equal weight to seven years of ward-protection over a city of ninety thousand souls." She laughed — a terrible small sound. "Do you know what I offered first? Gold. Land titles. Years of my own labor. It looked at me the way a banker looks at a child offering buttons."

"What did it ask for?" Xanthe's voice had gone very careful, very level, the voice they used when they were holding themselves together with both hands.

"A soul," Merrin said. "Freely given. That was the whole of it. That was the clause. It wouldn't take a soul by force — force has no weight here, it told me, force weighs nothing at all. But a soul surrendered willingly, by contract, witnessed and sealed..." She closed her eyes. "It explained the exchange rate patiently. Like a merchant explaining grain prices. Seven years of protection for one soul, freely given, of sufficient quality. And it specified the quality, Xanthe. It specified that the soul must be young. Unformed. Rich in potential. It said such souls held more light, and light was what the wards drank."

Silence. Even Emberfall's breathing seemed to stop.

"I came back up the road," Merrin said, "and I told myself I would never sign. I swear to you I told myself that. I sat in the guildhall all night with the contract on the table and I told myself: we will find another way. We will beg the capital. We will go north to the hedge-witches, south across the salt marshes, we will let the wards fail and stand in the breach ourselves and die honestly." Her voice cracked and she let it. "And then I thought about Wrenna in irons. And the fever-season coming. And the things beyond the border that the wards hold back — you've never seen them, thank every god there is, you've never seen what comes through when the wards go dark, and I have, once, when I was younger than you, and I could not—"

She stopped. Breathed.

"There was a child asleep upstairs," she said. "An apprentice we'd taken in that spring. Bright as a struck flint. Kind in a way that made the whole guildhall gentler just by containing him. He used to leave little knotted charms on people's pillows — luck-knots, nothing powerful, just knots he'd tied while thinking warm thoughts, because he'd decided that was how the magic worked and none of us corrected him." Her eyes were open now, dry, fixed on the middle distance where seven years ago lived. "And I sat there with the contract and I thought: one soul, freely given. Freely given. As long as I don't tell him what he's agreeing to. As long as I dress it up as an apprenticeship rite, a binding ceremony, a *gift to the guild*. Then his consent is real in the eyes of whatever watches these things. Then the clause is satisfied."

"That's not freely given," Xanthe said. "That's fraud."

"Yes," Merrin said simply. "Yes, it is. I knew it then too. I signed anyway." She reached into her coat and drew out a folded paper, soft as cloth from handling, and laid it on the stone between them. The contract. She had carried it down from the ruined guildhall — carried it through wards that no longer existed, past the Tollgate of Names where she had paid a false name and felt something scrape at the edges of her selfhood, all the way to this alcove, because some debts must be presented in person.

"His name was on it," she said. "In his own hand. Eleven years old, writing 'Ivo' with the tail of the v curled like a sprout growing. He thought he was pledging himself to the guild. He thought we were honoring him." She pressed her palm flat against the floor as if steadying the world. "Seven years later the term expired and the court collected, and the stars went out, and now you're sitting in the dark hating me, which is correct. You should hate me. I'm going to tell you the worst part, though, so save some hate for after."

"Worst part?" Xanthe whispered.

"When he was taken — when the collection came, that night seven years ago — he wasn't afraid. He came downstairs in his nightshirt and he saw the court's collectors standing in our doorway and he looked at *me*, and do you know what he said? He said, 'Am I going somewhere important?' Because I'd told him it was important. Because I dressed it up as an honor right to the end." Merrin's composure finally tore, quietly, along seams worn thin from seven years of tearing. "He trusted me so completely that being sold felt like being chosen. That's the worst part. That's what I did. Now you may finish hating me."

But Xanthe didn't answer, because Emberfall had raised her head again, and the old dragon was looking at Merrin with something that in a human face would have been called pity.

"You came down here to pay," the dragon said.

"I came down here to pay," Merrin confirmed. She turned to face the deep tunnel that led back toward the cathedral cavern, toward the Magistrate, toward the shelves of bottled light where a young man's soul waited in glass. "Xanthe will try to steal him. Or bargain. Or burn the place down, knowing you." A ghost of a smile at the dragon, gone as fast as it came. "But theft won't work — you've proven that. Bargaining needs currency. And I know what currency this court trades in, because I asked it, seven years ago, when I was desperate enough to ask anything. It deals in memory. Weight for weight. A memory given up freely weighs as much as the life it contains."

Emberfall's ember-veins pulsed slowly. "You intend to buy back his soul with your memories."

"With all of them," Merrin said. "Every founding year. Every charter signing, every ward I raised, every spell I ever taught, every student I ever trained — Xanthe, I trained *you*. Everything I am besides an old woman's regrets goes onto that scale tonight." She met Xanthe's eyes. "Everything except one thing. That's my confession, and my condition, and you can despise me for it: I keep the memories of Ivo. All of them. The plums, the knots, the night he arrived on our doorstep with frostbitten ears and a name nobody could pronounce until he laughed and said it slower. Those aren't mine to give away. Those are stolen goods, and stolen goods must be returned, not spent. If I empty myself of everything else but keep those — then at least the thief dies poor, holding what she stole, knowing exactly what it cost everyone else."

* * *

The Magistrate received them in the cathedral cavern, beneath the slow constellation of bottled souls.

Merrin had forgotten — no, she had *refused* to remember — how beautiful it was. Thousands upon thousands of glass vessels suspended on silver wires from a vaulted ceiling of living rock, each bottle glowing with its own color and tempo: blue ones pulsing calm as sleeping breath, gold ones flickering quick and eager, one far off in the dark that burned a furious red, and somewhere among them — she refused to look up, refused, not yet — a light that had been a boy who curled the tail of his v like a sprout.

The Magistrate sat at its desk of black stone, the same desk, the same posture, as if seven years between visits were the space of an afternoon. Its face was smooth as an egg and entirely without cruelty, which was worse. Cruelty would have meant it understood what it did.

"Guildmaster Merrin Ashgrave," it said, in a voice like pages turning. "You return within your own lifetime. Debtors rarely do. It implies either great courage or great guilt."

"Both," Merrin said. "In equal weight, which seems fitting for this establishment." She set the old contract on the black stone desk. "I've come to amend the terms."

"The terms are concluded."

"The terms were fraudulent," Merrin said, "and you knew it. You weighed a child's coerced consent and called it freely given, because the paperwork was clean and the light was good. Don't look at me like that — you deal in weights, Magistrate. So weigh this: a contract procured by deception is void in every law above your mountain, and I imagine the laws below are older and stricter still. His soul was never truly yours. It was *stolen through me*, and I am here to unsteal it."

For the first time, something moved behind the Magistrate's smooth face — not expression, exactly, but attention, the way a scale shifts when a weight is added. Around the cavern, the bottled lights flickered, all together, like a held breath.

"You propose ransom," the Magistrate said.

"I propose payment," Merrin said. "Memories, given freely — mine, this time, with no fraud in the giving. Every founding memory of the Charmwrights' Guild. Two hundred years of history lives in my head; I am the last vessel of most of it. Take it all. Let it weigh against the remainder of his term." Her voice held steady, which surprised her; inside she was already grieving, already standing in the guildhall kitchen watching a much younger version of herself write the first ward-glyph, and knowing she was watching it for the last time. "And I'll make it easy for you to say yes. I'll throw in my name."

The silence stretched. Somewhere high above, a gold bottle flickered like laughter.

"Names are heavy," the Magistrate said at last, with something almost like appetite. "A name carries every use it's ever been put to. Yours has signed charters, sentenced apprentices to swats, been cursed in four languages, and been spoken with love by—" it paused, consulting some inner ledger, "—one hundred and twelve people, most deceased. Yes. Your name has excellent weight." It extended a hand that was not quite a hand. "Place your memories upon the scale, Merrin Ashgrave. All of them, save those you claim as stolen goods. We shall see if the arithmetic pleases us."

* * *

Paying memories, Merrin learned, was not forgetting. Forgetting would have been merciful.

It was watching. She stood at the scale — a great balance of bone and silver that rose from the cavern floor like a tree grown upside-down — and she laid her hands upon its pans, and the extraction began, and she *watched* each memory rise out of her like heat-shimmer leaving summer stone, visible, whole, achingly vivid, and then dissolve into the pan.

She watched herself at nineteen, ink-stained, raising her first ward over the guildhall door while her own master watched and wept with pride — watched it lift away and flatten into silver mist. She watched the charter-signing, forty faces crowded into a hall that smelled of beeswax and ambition. She watched Wrenna's arrest and her own rage; watched the fever-season; watched decades of small repairs, small kindnesses, small magics that had added up, without her noticing, into a life. Each one rose, shone brighter than it ever had inside her — that was the obscenity of it, they were *most beautiful* at the moment of loss — and then poured itself into the scale and was gone.

Her name went last. She felt it leave like a tooth leaving the gum: a socket-shaped absence, a whistling gap where something structural used to be. When the Magistrate spoke afterward, it had to address her as "debtor," because there was no other word available. She searched herself for *Merrin* and found the shape where it had been, the way a hand remembers a tool set down a moment ago.

And the scale tipped — she felt it tip, felt the whole cavern lean — and stopped, agonizingly, a hair's breadth short. The Magistrate regarded the balance with its egg-smooth face.

"Sufficient," it said, "but not sufficient. The remainder of the term exceeds your weight by a margin. You have emptied yourself admirably, debtor. You are simply not heavy enough."

Merrin stood in the ruins of her own name and began, very calmly, to consider what else she owned.

Then the cavern brightened.

Heat rolled across the floor like a tide. Emberfall came out of the tunnel with her ash-grey scales burning, really burning, veins of gold flooding outward from her chest until the old dragon glowed like dawn through storm-cloud, and she planted herself beside the scale and opened her jaws and breathed her fire — not at anyone, not as a weapon, but *into* the pan, pouring the last of her flame onto the scale as if flame were coin.

"My final hoard," the dragon said, her voice smoking. "Every dragon pays out in fire at the end. Weigh it."

The scale groaned. Tipped. Held — trembling at the point of balance, everything precious in the world hanging in a trembling pause—

—and the Magistrate, watching, said softly, "There remains one further party interested in this transaction. I suggest you turn around."

Merrin turned. At the mouth of the cavern, small and straight-backed and white-faced with fury and resolve, stood Xanthe Blackwood — and in their eyes was the terrible light of someone who has just understood what they are worth.
