## 15. Negotiation with the Magistrate

The smuggler's alcove smelled of cold iron and old smoke, and Xanthe had not slept. Sleep was a country they could no longer afford to visit; every time they closed their eyes, they saw the weighing scale in the great cavern, saw Ivo's bottle hanging from its silver chain like a lantern waiting to be lit, and woke with their heart hammering out a name.

*Ivo. Ivo. Ivo.*

Emberfall lay coiled at the alcove's mouth, her wounded flank rising and falling in slow, labored breaths. The gash the court's defenses had torn across her shoulder had stopped weeping light — that was what her blood was now, mostly light, thin and gold and precious — but the scales around it had gone gray as ash. She was dying by inches, and she knew it, and she had stopped pretending otherwise somewhere on the road beneath the world.

"You're going to do it," she said. Her voice was gravel rolled in embers. "I can smell it on you. The way you keep touching your own temples, like you're checking whether the goods are still in stock."

Xanthe's hand dropped. "The smuggler said equivalent payment. A soul for memories of equal weight."

"The smuggler said a great many things." Emberfall opened one eye, a slit of dim amber in the dark. "He also said the court is fair, which is like saying a knife is fair because it cuts straight."

"Then tell me not to do it."

The dragon was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere far off, water dripped through the bones of the mountain, each drop counting time the way the court counted everything — in debts and payments and things owed.

"I watched you try to steal him," she said at last. "I burned a path for you while the wards flayed me open, and I would do it again, and it bought you nothing except this scar and that lesson: force will not work here. So no, charm-seller. I will not tell you not to do it. I will only tell you to go in with your eyes open, because the Magistrate does not take payment. He takes *surrender*, and he likes to watch you choose it."

Xanthe looked down at their hands. They were a charm-seller's hands, knuckle-scarred and ink-stained, hands that had tied ten thousand luck-knots and never once dropped a child who reached up to be carried. Seven years of small kindnesses lived in those hands. And the plan forming in their chest — terrible, inevitable, already half-made — required giving all of it away.

"How much are seven years of loving someone worth?" they asked quietly.

"In that place?" Emberfall's laugh was a dry crackle, like paper burning. "More than he'll want to pay. That's what frightens me."

* * *

They came back to the cathedral cavern through the smuggler's passage, the one that opened behind a curtain of hanging moss-stone, and Xanthe made themselves look at everything again with new eyes — the eyes of a merchant entering a negotiation.

The cavern was vast as a buried sky, its ceiling lost in darkness where pale stalactites hung like the pipes of some impossible organ. Thousands of bottled souls glowed along the walls in niches carved with surgical care, each one labeled in script that moved when you weren't looking directly at it. Their combined light should have been warm. It wasn't. It was the light of a counting-house, precise and pitiless, casting shadows that fell exactly where arithmetic said they must.

And there, above the dais, suspended on its chain of silver: Ivo.

The bottle was taller than a man, blown from glass so clear it seemed apologetic about existing at all. Inside, the soul of a boy of eleven turned slowly like weather in a snow-globe — threads of gold and green and one stubborn strand of ordinary human brown, the color of his hair before any of this. As Xanthe watched, the threads gathered toward the glass nearest them, pressing close, the way a child presses close to a window.

*He knows I'm here.* The thought went through Xanthe like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

At the center of the dais stood a desk. Not a throne — Xanthe had expected a throne — but a desk of black wood, immaculate, arranged with ledgers, an abacus whose beads were teeth, an inkwell that held something darker than ink. Behind the desk sat the Magistrate of Debts.

He had been old once; that was the horror of him. Whatever he had been before the mountain made him its accountant, he still wore the shape of a clerk grown ancient at his post, spine curved over centuries of entries, fingers long and yellowed as candle-tallow. His eyes were the only young thing about him — wide, dark, and patient as wells.

"Charm-seller," he said, without looking up. His pen scratched. "You returned. Most don't, after a first failure. They decide the price of wanting is too high." He finished his line, blotted it, and raised those well-deep eyes. "But you have come to trade properly this time. I can always tell. Traders walk differently than thieves. Thieves walk on their toes. Traders walk like people carrying something heavy they intend to set down."

Xanthe walked forward across the cavern floor. Every step echoed. The bottled souls along the walls brightened faintly as they passed, drawn to the warmth of a living memory walking among them, and dimmed again behind.

"I want the boy," Xanthe said. "The soul in the bottle. All of it, whole, returned to flesh."

"That item is not for sale." The Magistrate folded his hands. "It is *held*. There is a difference. It secures a contract — seven years of border warding, paid in full, performed in full. Releasing it would leave the debt unsecured."

"The debt is dead. The wards collapsed. The stars went out. Your contract failed."

Something flickered across the ancient face — annoyance, or perhaps only indigestion. "The wards collapsed because the *term expired*. A loan does not fail because it ends. But—" and here the well-eyes glittered, "—you are not entirely wrong, charm-seller. A lapsed contract creates an opportunity. The collateral may be ransomed. Equivalent value, freely offered. Those are the rules older than my desk, and even I do not break them, because the rules are what hold this mountain up."

"Equivalent value." Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. "In what currency?"

The Magistrate smiled. It was the worst thing Xanthe had seen yet in the court beneath the mountain, worse than the bottled souls, worse than the tooth-beaded abacus — because it was a kind smile, the smile of a shopkeeper greeting a customer he has been expecting for years.

"You already know. You've known since the smuggler told you, and you have been carrying it up these stairs like a stone in your chest. Memories. Weighted, appraised, authenticated. A soul is the sum of what a person remembers being. Pay enough of what you remember, and the sum balances." He tilted his head. "So. What do you bring me?"

* * *

Xanthe had rehearsed this in the alcove, lying awake, arranging the words like charms on a string. Now, standing before the desk, they found the rehearsal burning away and only the truth left underneath.

"A boy named Ivo came to the guild when he was four years old," they said. "Left on the step in a rainstorm, wrapped in a blanket that someone had embroidered with a warding sigil done wrong — the knots inverted, protection turned inside out, like whoever made it wanted the storm to find him. Merrin took him in. We all did. But I taught him his first charm-knot. A luck-knot, three loops, thumb over the crossing. He got it wrong forty times and on the forty-first time he held it up and it *worked*, and the look on his face—"

"Stop."

The word cracked through the cavern like a whip. The Magistrate had risen from his chair, and for the first time he looked — not alarmed, nothing so vulgar — but *interested*, the way a moneylender looks at a coin of unexpected mint.

"Do you know what you are doing?" he asked softly. "Do you understand what you just handed me, unprompted, unasked? Forty attempts. The number. The rain. The inverted sigil. Charm-seller, I have sat at this desk since before your city had a name, and I have taken memories by the thousand — soldiers surrendering the faces of the men they killed, lovers paying off each other's debts, kings buying forgetfulness by the barrel. They give me *facts*. Dates and griefs, compressed and bloodless." He came around the desk, slowly, tallow fingers laced together. "You are giving me a *boy*. Rain-wet. Stubborn. Loved in specifics. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what love given freely, in full detail, is worth in a place built on what people refuse to feel?"

Xanthe's heart was pounding so hard they could hear it in their ears. Terror and resolve had become the same sensation, a single hot wire pulled tight through their chest. "Then you'll accept it. My memories of him. All of them. Against his soul."

"All of them." The Magistrate tasted the words. "Seven years."

"Every one. The plums we stole from the market on Tallow Street. The night he was sick with marsh-fever and I sat up with him and told him stories until dawn. The promise I made him — that nothing bad would happen while the guild stood." Xanthe's voice caught, and they let it, because they understood suddenly that the catch itself was currency. "Take all of it. Weigh it. If it isn't enough, I'll find more. I'll make more. I'll stay here and love him another seven years if that's what the scale requires."

For a long moment the Magistrate simply looked at them. The bottled souls along the walls had gone very bright, all of them leaning toward the exchange like spectators at an execution.

"And afterward," the Magistrate said, "you will not remember him."

"No."

"You will see his face and it will be a stranger's face. You will hear his voice and feel nothing but the ghost of a feeling with no story attached — warmth with no name. You will have paid everything and you will not even know what you bought." He said it gently, almost kindly, laying each truth out like a knife on white cloth. "That is the standard term. Most traders beg me to soften it. To leave them one memory, one anchor, a single thread to pull themselves back by. And most traders, when I offer them that mercy at a surcharge, pay the surcharge."

"I'm not asking for mercy."

"No," the Magistrate agreed. "I can see that you aren't. Which brings me to my condition."

He returned to the desk and drew a fresh ledger toward himself — a slim one, bound in gray leather, its pages blank. He uncapped the inkwell of darker-than-ink.

"The scale requires willing payment. That is the oldest rule. A memory taken by force weighs almost nothing; a memory surrendered freely outweighs kingdoms. But there is willingness, and there is *willingness*, charm-seller, and I have learned — over long centuries — to distinguish them." He looked up. "You will surrender your memories willingly. And you will *remember giving them*. Forever. The extraction, the scale, the moment you set each year of that boy down upon the pan and felt it leave you — that, you keep. That, no power in this mountain can take, because it is not a memory of Ivo. It is a memory of your choice. And choices belong to the chooser until death collects them."

The words landed one at a time, each with its own weight.

"You want me—" Xanthe's voice came out strange, distant. "You want me to live the rest of my life remembering exactly what I lost. Remembering loving him, remembering giving it up, and never knowing why it mattered — only that it mattered more than anything."

"Yes."

"Why?" The word tore out of them. "Why would you want that? What kind of cruelty—"

"It is not cruelty." For an instant, something moved behind the well-dark eyes — not kindness, nothing so warm, but recognition, the way one old soldier recognizes another. "It is *appraisal*. Anyone can pay what they cannot feel afterward. The forgetting makes it easy, and easy payment is cheap payment. But a soul that remembers its own sacrifice — that carries the shape of the loss forever, a wound that never explains itself — that soul pays *every day*, for the rest of its life. The debt never finishes collecting." He dipped the pen. "Love offered freely, and remembered as freely given. It is the richest substance in existence, charm-seller. Richer than souls. I have waited a very long time to weigh it."

Somewhere above them, Ivo's bottle chimed softly against its chain, and the golden threads inside pressed hard against the glass, and Xanthe thought: *he can hear this. Some part of him is hearing every word.*

And that decided it. Because if some part of Ivo was listening, then some part of Ivo needed to hear the answer.

"Then write it," Xanthe said. "Your condition. I accept it. I'll carry the remembering for the rest of my life — gladly, if gladly is the word, and if it isn't, I'll carry it anyway. Write the terms. Weigh the memories. Give him back."

The Magistrate studied them a moment longer, pen poised.

"One correction," he said. "Not *give him back*. Nothing in this mountain is given. Say it properly: *sold to me, at fair price, freely rendered*. Words matter in contracts. The wrong word can unmake a bargain."

Xanthe lifted their chin. "Sold to you. At fair price. Freely rendered. Now write it before I lose my nerve, because I promise you, Magistrate — I am a charm-seller, and I know exactly how much nerve costs, and mine is running low."

Something that was almost laughter moved through the ancient frame. The pen touched the page.

And high above, in the clear glass bottle, the soul called Ivo pressed itself against the side nearest Xanthe, glowing like a struck match, as if it could answer — as if it were shouting through seven years and a wall of glass:

*Don't. Don't. Please don't.*

Or perhaps: *Thank you.*

There was no way to know which. The Magistrate began to write, and the scale was brought out, and the first year of Xanthe's love was lifted from them like a coal drawn glowing from a fire.
