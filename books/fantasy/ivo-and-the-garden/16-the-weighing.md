## 16. The Weighing

Emberfall had not stood in a court of debts in four hundred years, and the last time she had, she had been the debt.

She felt that old ledger open again inside her as she dragged herself across the cathedral cavern's floor, her claws scoring long pale lines in the stone that no one would ever bother to polish out. The Magistrate of Debts had sent no escort for her. Why would it? She was barely cargo now. Her scales, which had once thrown back the light of a thousand stars — real stars, from before the dark, from when the sky was generous — had gone the color of ash left too long in rain. When she moved, she could hear herself: a dry rustling, like pages turning in a book no one was reading.

But she could still walk. That was more than the court expected of her, and she took a small, fierce pleasure in disappointing its expectations.

The cavern rose above her like the inside of a lung made by something that had never breathed. Veins of pale mineral branched across the ceiling, and hanging from them, in their thousands, were the bottles — glass vessels blown from mountain-glass, each one holding a soft and shifting light. Souls, all of them. Some burned steady as hearth-coals. Some flickered like moths against a window. One, near the center of the cavern, suspended on chains of woven shadow above a dais of black stone, burned gold.

Ivo.

Even at this distance, even through glass, Emberfall knew him. She had carried him, after all. Seven years ago she had flown the sunken road with a stoppered bottle cradled against her heart like an egg, and the whole flight he had glowed against her scales, warm as a coal, and she had hated every league of it. Dragons are not meant to carry souls to market. There is an older law than the Magistrate's law, written before mountains had names, and she had broken it, and her fire had been dying ever since. Not quickly. Just surely. The way a candle is surely going out from the moment it is lit.

Now she crossed the floor toward the scale.

* * *

The scale itself stood at the heart of the cavern, and it was older than everything else there — older than the bottles, older than the dais, older, Emberfall suspected, than the Magistrate's office if not the Magistrate themself. Two pans of hammered iron hung from a beam of black wood, and the beam balanced on a fulcrum carved in the shape of a closed hand. Nothing about it gleamed. It looked like a farm implement, something you'd find rusting behind a barn, and that was the most frightening thing about it. Great powers dress their instruments in splendor. Only truly ancient ones let them look ordinary.

The human — Xanthe Blackwood — stood before it with their hands at their sides, palms open, the way one stands before a magistrate or a mugging. They had washed the soot of the failed theft from their face, but nothing could wash the set of their jaw. Emberfall had watched many creatures decide to die over her long life. This was not that. This was worse, and braver: they had decided what they were willing to lose, and they had found it was nearly everything.

Merrin knelt nearby, or rather occupied the space where kneeling used to be. The old guildmaster had paid her memories of the founding, and payment in that place did not leave a body intact so much as *edited*. What remained of Merrin was a thin, courteous woman with kind eyes and no idea why she had them. She kept smiling at Xanthe without recognition, the way strangers smile at one another in waiting rooms. It was unbearable to watch, so Emberfall watched anyway. Someone should have to.

And the Magistrate stood beside the scale, robed in layered grey, its face a smooth oval of shadow beneath a crown of iron keys. It had been pleased since the negotiation concluded. Pleased, in the way a spider is pleased — a stillness that reads as satisfaction only because the alternative readings are worse.

"Bring the asset," the Magistrate said, and its voice was the sound of a lock turning in a door you had thought was already locked.

Chains of shadow unwove themselves from the dais and drew Ivo's bottle down through the air, gentle as a hand lowering a lantern. The bottle settled into the left-hand pan with a sound like a struck bell, and the whole cavern seemed to lean toward it. Every bottled soul in the ceiling brightened for a heartbeat — Emberfall saw it clearly, thousands of lights flaring in unison — as though the dead and the taken recognized one of their own come down among them.

In the bottle, the gold light pressed itself against the glass on the side facing Xanthe.

He knows, Emberfall thought. He has always known. That was the cruelty under all the court's courtesies: the souls stayed aware. Dreaming, reaching, remembering. Seven years in a jar with nothing but his own memory for company, and the last memory in it, the freshest, the one he must have turned over ten thousand times, was the guildmaster's face saying goodbye.

Xanthe stepped up to the right-hand pan.

"The terms," the Magistrate said, "for the record of the court. One soul, freely given seven years past, ransomed by equivalent weight. The weight to be measured in memory, offered willingly, surrendered consciously, and *remembered in the giving*." Its shadowed face tilted toward Xanthe. "You will feel yourself forget him. You will remain awake for all of it. Do you confirm?"

"I confirm," Xanthe said. Their voice did not shake. Emberfall admired that and pitied it; shaking would have been healthier.

"Then place your currency."

* * *

Xanthe reached into their coat and brought out nothing. That was how it worked here — the court did not trade in objects. They simply opened their hands over the pan, closed their eyes, and began.

Emberfall felt it before she saw it. Memory has a smell, if you live long enough to learn it, and Xanthe's memories came off them like heat off a forge-stone: plums stolen from a market cart and eaten on a rooftop; small hands fumbling a luck-knot for the ninth time and getting it right on the tenth; a promise made in a doorway — *nothing bad will happen while the guild stands* — spoken with the terrible confidence of someone who does not yet know what promises cost. The memories poured upward, invisible, weightless-seeming, and then they touched the pan and the pan *sank*.

The beam groaned. A sound like a ship in weather.

The right pan went down, and down, and the left pan — holding a soul, holding a child, holding seven years of bottled gold — rose by degrees into the air, and the whole cavern held its breath, and Emberfall felt hope rise in her own chest, vast and stupid and painful, because it was working, it was actually working—

The beam stopped.

It stopped at a sliver short of level. A hair's breadth. The left pan hovered a finger's width higher than the right, and the silence that followed was the worst sound Emberfall had ever heard, and she had heard cities burn.

"Insufficient," said the Magistrate, and there was something almost tender in it, which was the most obscene thing yet. "Close. Remarkably close. But the scale does not round in favor of the debtor." It gestured with one grey sleeve. "Withdraw your payment, charm-seller, or forfeit it entirely and leave with nothing. Those are—"

"No."

The word came from the floor of the cavern, from low down, from a throat full of ash. Emberfall hadn't planned to speak. Four hundred years of silence about this court, and now she was speaking, and her voice came out cracked and smoking.

The Magistrate turned. Every eye in the cavern — human, hollow, and otherwise — turned with it.

"You said equivalent weight," Emberfall said. She hauled herself forward, one claw at a time, her joints shrieking like hinges in a haunted house, and she did not care. Let them hear her coming apart. Let it be loud. "Equivalent weight. You weighed his soul alone against their memories alone. But that was never the true weight of what was taken." She stopped at the edge of the scale and looked up at the bottle, at the gold light pressed against the glass, and her old heart broke along a seam that had been cracking for seven years. "When the guild sold him, they didn't sell only a soul. They sold a *carrying*. Someone had to bring him down here. Someone flew him through the dark with her own fire keeping the bottle warm, knowing — knowing — that the warmth was leaving her with every league. That debt was never entered in your ledger, Magistrate. So enter it now."

"What payment do you propose?" the Magistrate asked. And Emberfall saw, in the tilt of that shadowed head, that it had known. It had been waiting for this since she landed in the courtyard. Courts of debt always know what their debtors have left.

"My fire," Emberfall said. "What remains of it."

* * *

Here is what a dragon's fire is, for those who have never been one: it is not breath. It is not fuel. It is the sum of every dawn the dragon has ever seen, banked and hoarded, the light of ten thousand mornings carried in the chest against the certainty of night. Emberfall's hoard was nearly spent — a few embers' worth of morning, a handful of remembered sunrises — and it was hers, the last thing in the world that was hers, the last proof that she had been young under a generous sky.

She climbed onto the base of the scale's fulcrum, beneath the great closed hand, and turned to face the cavern.

"For the record of the court," she said, mimicking the Magistrate's cadence, and was rewarded by the faintest ripple in its robes — surprise, or its local equivalent. "One flame, freely given, surrendered consciously, and — yes — *remembered in the giving*. I want to remember this. Whatever else happens, I keep this."

"You understand," the Magistrate said, "that your fire is your life. What remains of it. You will not survive the surrender."

"I have been not surviving for seven years," Emberfall said. "I would like to finish."

She looked up one last time at Xanthe. The human was staring at her, stricken, hands still hovering over the pan where their whole life with Ivo sat in invisible heaps, and Emberfall wished she had time to tell them what she'd never told anyone: that watching them teach a child to knot charms through a courtyard window, years ago, from the rooftops where she'd gone to be alone — that had been the first thing in a century that made her think the world might still be worth warming. But there was no time. There was never any time; that was the whole trick of the place.

So instead she said, "Mind the boy. He bites when he's frightened. Takes after me."

And she opened her chest.

* * *

Dragons do not breathe fire out. Remembering is breathing it in — every dawn drawn down into the deep place below the heart and sealed there. To give it, then, is to exhale everything at once, and Emberfall exhaled.

Light came out of her.

Not flame — light, pure and total, the color of the first morning of the world, pouring up out of her open mouth and breast in a column that struck the underside of the beam and spread along it like dawn along a horizon. The bottled souls overhead flared so hard the shadows fled to the walls. The mineral veins in the ceiling lit white. For one moment the cathedral cavern, which had never in its existence known anything but borrowed light, knew the real thing — and Emberfall, dimming even as she blazed, thought: *there. That's what you all forgot. That's what we're for.*

Her fire flowed across the beam and pooled, and dripped, and fell — golden, weightless, impossibly heavy — into the right-hand pan, onto the invisible heap of Xanthe's memories, and the pan sank, and sank, and the left pan rose, and rose—

The beam leveled.

For three full seconds, the ancient scale hung in perfect balance, the soul of a boy on one side and the love of two creatures on the other, and the closed hand at the fulcrum, carved by some forgotten maker, seemed almost to tremble.

Then the hand opened.

Stone fingers unfurled one by one, and the beam tipped — a fraction, a grace-note, the left pan descending the final finger's width to rest — and the Magistrate spoke into the ringing quiet.

"Sufficient," it said. "Payment accepted. The debt is discharged."

Emberfall slid down against the fulcrum. She was cold now, cold all the way through, colder than she had been since she was an egg, and her scales were grey as grave-dirt, and none of it mattered, none of it mattered at all, because the shadow-chains were rising around Ivo's bottle and the glass was opening like a mouth, like a door, like a dawn —

"Wait," Xanthe said. "Wait — the extraction — you said I'd stay conscious — I want — I need to watch it go, I promised myself—"

"You did," the Magistrate said, and for the first time in the history of the court, it sounded almost respectful. "Then watch."

The memories began to rise out of the pan and drift toward Xanthe, and Emberfall, lying broken and extinguished at the foot of the scale, lifted her head to watch them go — every plum, every knot, every promise — and she understood, with the last clarity of her long life, that the scale had only tipped because her fire had been added to love, and that love alone had come within a hair of being enough, and that this was the truest thing she had ever learned:

It is almost enough. It is always almost enough. That is why the world needs dragons.

The first memory touched Xanthe's brow, and their eyes went wide, and the Magistrate raised its hand of iron keys.

"Begin," it said.
