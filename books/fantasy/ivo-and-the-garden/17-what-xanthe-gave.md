## 17. What Xanthe Gave

The weighing was done. The scale — a thing of black iron and hanging chains that had no business existing in a world with sky in it — hung level, trembling, and the whole cathedral cavern of the court beneath the mountain held its breath.

Xanthe stood on the brass plate at the scale's foot and felt the moment arrive the way one feels a storm arrive: not as surprise but as pressure, an old ache in the teeth, the certainty of something vast about to break over them.

The Magistrate of Debts rose from its bench of ledger-leather and bone. It had no face Xanthe could have described afterward, only an arrangement of features that suggested a clerk who had been counting for so long it had forgotten what numbers were *for*. Its voice, when it spoke, was dry as a turned page.

"The payment is accepted in principle," it said. "But understand the terms you agreed to, charm-seller. You will not sleep through this. You will not be made gentle by forgetting. You will give your memories willingly, one by one, and you will remain awake to watch them go. This is the condition. The court does not take from the unwilling. The court accepts only what is *surrendered*."

"I know," Xanthe said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "I agreed."

"Knowing and understanding are different debts." The Magistrate tilted its headless head. "Shall I describe what will happen?"

"No." Xanthe's hands were shaking. They looked down at them, at the old burn scar across the left palm from the first charm they'd ever botched, at the ink stains that never fully washed out no matter how Merrin scolded. "No. If I hear it described, I'll talk myself out of it. And I'm not going to talk myself out of it."

Behind them, Emberfall lay where they had dragged her, her scales the grey of banked ash, her breathing a slow bellows-sound. She could not speak anymore — she had spent too much of herself already, on the theft that failed, on the fire that bought their escape to this alcove, on the final flame she had poured onto the scale to tip it true. But her eye, the one still bright enough to see with, found Xanthe's and held it. In it there was no pity. There was something better than pity: recognition. One creature running out of time, watching another spend theirs on purpose.

And beyond her, past the ranks of bottled souls glowing in their niches like a library of captive dawns, there was the bottle itself. Ivo's bottle. Small, blue-green as river glass, and inside it a light that pulsed — not steadily, but the way a heart pulses when its owner is dreaming of someone they love.

He was aware in there. He had been aware for seven years. He remembered everything, including the night the guild gave him away, including Merrin's signature drying on the contract, including — gods help them all — including Xanthe's face, and every plum they'd ever stolen together, and every knot Xanthe's hands had taught his smaller ones to tie.

"Begin," said the Magistrate.

Xanthe closed their eyes. Then opened them again. No — they would not close them. That was the whole point. That was the cruelty and the honor of it. They would stay conscious. They would watch.

"Where do we start?" they asked.

"At the beginning," said the Magistrate, "as all accounts must."

* * *

It began with plums.

That was the strange thing — Xanthe had expected the extraction to start with something large, something structural, the way a building comes down: the great beams first, the walls after. Instead the court reached into them delicately, like a pickpocket with infinite patience, and took the smallest things first, because the small things were load-bearing in ways no one suspects.

They felt the memory leave before they understood which one it was. A warmth behind the sternum simply — detached. Rose up through them like a bubble through dark water. And for one impossible moment they watched it from the outside: a summer afternoon seven years gone, dust motes in the guildhall light, Ivo at nine years old standing on a stool to reach the plum barrel, his tongue between his teeth in concentration, and Xanthe below him laughing, saying *take the bruised ones, nobody counts the bruised ones*, and Ivo grinning around a mouthful and saying *you count them, you count everything* —

— and then it was gone. Not dimmed. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a word is gone once spoken, the way a room is gone once burned. Xanthe knew, intellectually, that they had just lost something. They could feel the shape of the hole it left, smooth-edged and clean, like a tooth socket. But the afternoon itself — the dust, the stool, the boy — had been lifted out of them so completely that they could not even mourn it specifically. They could only mourn *that* there had been something to lose.

"That one," the Magistrate noted, making an entry in a book that wrote itself. "One summer afternoon. Value: modest. Weight: considerable."

"Why the small ones first?" Xanthe heard themselves ask. Their voice sounded far away, as if someone else were asking.

"Because the small ones are the foundation," said the Magistrate. "A memory is not a stone. It is a thread. Pull the small threads and the great tapestry loosens on its own. You would be surprised, charm-seller, how little of a love is made of grand moments. Most of it is plums."

* * *

Then came the lessons.

The knot-work went next, and this was worse, because the memories did not come singly now but in braids — whole seasons of them, wound together. Xanthe stood very straight on the brass plate with their arms at their sides and watched seven years of teaching pour out of them like water finding a drain.

Ivo at ten, frowning at a luck-knot, his fingers too big for the work: *it keeps coming undone.*

Xanthe's own voice, patient, warm, from somewhere outside themselves now: *because you're fighting it. A charm isn't tied, it's persuaded. You're not forcing the cord into a shape, you're asking it to remember one.*

*I don't understand.*

*You will. Here — put your hands over mine. Feel that? That's not strength. That's attention. Magic is mostly attention. Everything else is theater.*

The braid unspooled. Winter lessons by the stove, Ivo's mittens off, his knuckles red. Spring lessons in the courtyard with the wind stealing their cords. The day he finally tied a protection-knot that held — actually held, sang faintly when you held it to the ear — and danced around the guildhall waving it over his head until Merrin threatened to confiscate it, and everyone knew she wouldn't, because Merrin's threats were a language of love older than any contract.

Gone. Gone. Gone.

Xanthe swayed. The Magistrate's hand — when had it extended a hand? — hovered near their elbow without touching. Court etiquette, perhaps. Or court accounting: damaged goods devalued the transaction.

"You are remaining conscious," the Magistrate observed. "Most donors request oblivion by this stage."

"He'll know," Xanthe said. Their lips were numb. "When he wakes up. He'll know someone paid for him. Even if he never knows who. Even if the payment is anonymous forever. He'll know love happened here. Won't he?"

The Magistrate paused. For a moment something almost like discomfort crossed its arrangement of features.

"The soul remembers its ransom," it said finally. "Not the ransomer. He will carry a gratitude with no address. It is the strangest debt we keep — a debt owed to no one, payable to everyone. Some souls spend their whole lives trying to pay it forward because they cannot pay it back." It made another entry. "You have purchased him a lifetime of kindness, charm-seller, disguised as temperament."

"Good," Xanthe whispered. "Good. Then it's worth — "

They didn't finish, because the next memory took them mid-word, and this one was not small at all.

* * *

It was the promise.

Of course it was the promise. The court saved the heaviest for last; even here, even beneath the mountain, there was craft.

Xanthe had made it on a night three years ago — no. Two years ago. No — time was going soft now, the dates sliding loose in their head like knots in cut cord — some night when Ivo had woken screaming from a dream of the mountain, though he couldn't say why, though neither of them knew then why a child who'd never seen the mountain should dream of it so faithfully. Xanthe had sat on the edge of his cot in the dark with the wards humming faintly overhead, and taken his hot hands in theirs, and said:

*Nothing bad will happen to you while the guild stands. I swear it on my craft. As long as I'm alive and the guild stands, nothing bad gets through to you. That's not a comfort, Ivo. That's a contract. And you know how seriously we take contracts.*

And Ivo, sniffling, had laughed wetly and said, *you sound like Merrin,* and Xanthe had said, *don't insult me,* and he had laughed harder, and fallen asleep holding one of their fingers the way he used to when he was small, and Xanthe had stayed there until dawn because a contract is a contract.

Now Xanthe felt that night rise up through them — felt it go — and the leaving of it was not like the others. The others had been bubbles. This was a hook tearing free.

They cried out. They couldn't help it. The sound went up into the cavern dark and came back down off the bottled souls in a thousand small echoes, and somewhere in the niches lights flickered, as if the court's collection had stirred in its sleep.

"The promise is weighed," said the Magistrate quietly. "Heavy. Very heavy. Promises made to children are the densest substance we trade in. Do you know why?"

"Because children believe them," Xanthe gasped.

"Because adults make them knowing they might have to break them." The Magistrate looked at the scale. It had begun, very slowly, to sink on Xanthe's side — the side that held, now, not objects but absence, a growing weight of hollow places. "You are nearly empty of him, charm-seller. What remains?"

Xanthe searched.

This was the terrible part, worse than the losing: the searching. They reached inward for Ivo and found — warmth. A vast, diffuse, golden warmth, spread through them like sunlight through water, attached to nothing. They loved someone. They knew they loved someone with the whole force of their being; the love was intact, enormous, more real than the stone under their feet. But it pointed at nothing. It was a compass needle with no north. Every story that had ever told them *who* they loved and *how* and *why* and *since when* — all of it was on the scale now, or in the Magistrate's self-writing book, or dissolving into the currency of the court.

"I love him," Xanthe said aloud, testing it, and found the sentence both absolutely true and absolutely empty — true the way a mathematical truth is true, with no faces in it anywhere. "I don't know his name anymore. I can feel that I knew it. I can feel exactly where it used to live." They pressed a fist below their collarbone. "Here. His name lived here."

"Names are the last to go," said the Magistrate. "We save them for the end. It makes the accounting cleaner." It consulted the scale, the book, the trembling air. "One item remains outstanding. Do you consent to complete the transaction?"

Emberfall made a sound. Low, cracked, half-ash. Her bright eye was fixed on Xanthe with an urgency that needed no translation, and Xanthe — empty, hollowed, warm with a love that had no object — somehow understood her anyway, because some things live deeper than memory. She was telling them: *look at him. Before you finish. Look at him one last time while you still know why it matters.*

Xanthe turned to the niche where the blue-green bottle glowed.

Inside, the light was pressing against the glass. Pulsing fast now, faster, the way a heart beats when its owner is reaching. And Xanthe understood — with the last of the knowledge they had left, the knowledge that was already halfway to being the Magistrate's property — that Ivo could feel this. That he had felt all of it. That somewhere in his seven years of dreaming he was watching his own rescue happen, watching someone give him away piece by piece, and he was pressing himself against the glass of his own soul trying to say *stop, don't, keep yourself, I've been fine, I've been fine in here, don't —*

"I consent," Xanthe said.

The last of it went: the name, the face, the seven years entire, the whole architecture of a love built plank by plank across a childhood. It left cleanly. It left completely. And Xanthe Blackwood stood on the brass plate in the court beneath the mountain, awake to the last instant as promised, holding nothing but a warmth with no story — and smiled, because the scale had tipped, because the bottle in its niche had begun, softly, to sing.

* * *

The singing grew. The glass thinned. Somewhere above them, stone began to groan as the court's oldest asset moved toward release, and the Magistrate was already calling for the ledger to be balanced and sealed, and Emberfall was dragging herself upright on legs that barely answered her, and none of it mattered yet, none of it mattered except —

Except the bottle cracked like an egg, and light poured out, and a boy stood blinking in it, flesh and breath and seven years returned, looking wildly around the collapsing cavern at the dragon and the darkness and the strangers.

His gaze found Xanthe. Held there. Narrowed.

"You," he said slowly — and Xanthe's heart, empty and full at once, broke and leapt in the same beat — "why are you crying?"
