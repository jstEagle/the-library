## 18. Ivo Wakes

The first thing Ivo knew was the taste of glass.

It was not a flavor, exactly — nothing so crude as sour or sweet — but a quality that had soaked into him so thoroughly he had forgotten it was there, the way a fish forgets water. For seven years he had lived inside it: the smooth curve of a bottle no bigger than a lantern, held up to the light of ten thousand other bottled souls in the cathedral dark of the court beneath the mountain. He had dreamed inside the glass. He had grown inside the glass. He had learned, with the terrible patience of the imprisoned, to make a whole world out of the small space between himself and the walls that held him.

And then the world tilted, and the glass sang, and something on the other side of it wept.

He heard the weeping before he understood anything else. It came through the bottle's skin like sound through deep water — muffled, rhythmic, desperate. A human sound. He had not heard a human sound from outside in seven years. The Magistrate did not weep. The gatekeepers did not weep. The gray clerks who came to dust the bottles and record their contents in ledgers bound in pale leather did not weep; they hummed, sometimes, tuneless little work-songs, but grief was not currency they carried.

*Someone is crying for me,* he thought, and the thought was so enormous that for a moment it displaced everything else — the dream-gardens he had built, the memory-rooms he had furnished, the careful architecture of seven years of solitude. *Someone came all this way, into the dark, into the court, and they are crying.*

Then the hands closed around the bottle.

They were small hands, scarred at the knuckles, stained faintly green at the fingertips — charm-stain, knot-dye, the marks of someone who worked magic with their fingers the way a gardener works soil. He knew those stains. He knew them the way you know a thing you have seen every day of your childhood: without looking, without thinking, as a fact older than language.

*I know those hands.*

The knowing frightened him more than anything the court had ever done, because the court had taught him that nothing got in. Nothing reached through the glass. The bottle kept the soul in and the world out, and whatever he remembered of before — plums, a courtyard, a woman's voice saying *knot it tight, love, tighter than that* — those were dreams, beautiful ones, but dreams. The court had been very clear about this, in its bloodless way. *What you recall of the surface is residue,* the Magistrate had told him once, early, when he still asked questions. *Residue fades. It is kinder to let it.*

But the hands were real. The hands were shaking. And through the glass, close enough that his dreaming breath should have fogged it, was a face.

* * *

The face was wet with tears and smeared with soot and there was a cut above one eyebrow still bleeding freely, and none of that mattered, because Ivo was looking at Xanthe Blackwood.

He knew the name before he knew why he knew it. It arrived whole, like a charm slipping off a string — *Xanthe Blackwood* — and behind it came everything: the stall by the fish-market with its awning the color of over-brewed tea; the luck-knots tied wrong on purpose because Xanthe said a charm too perfect looked like boasting and the Fates were petty; stolen plums eaten on the guildhall roof with juice running down their wrists; the smell of tallow and knot-dye and the particular way Xanthe hummed when they worked, always a half-step flat, always the same three bars of a song whose name neither of them had ever learned.

Seven years. Seven years of these things, hoarded in the dark, polished nightly like a miser's coins. He had built rooms out of them in his dreaming. He had walked those rooms a thousand times. When the cold of the glass grew absolute, he had warmed himself at the memory of Xanthe's laugh — the real one, the ugly snorting one, not the polite market laugh — and it had been enough. It had been barely enough. It had been everything.

So when the bottle cracked — when the Magistrate's own weighing-scale shuddered and the seal that had held him broke like ice on a spring river — Ivo did what any drowning thing does.

He reached for the person holding him.

* * *

Flesh was a shock.

He had forgotten weight. He had forgotten that a body pulls at you from every direction at once — the drag of hair against your neck, the ache of joints asked to bend, the astonishing labor of simply standing upright in gravity's long argument. His legs folded under him like a puppet with cut strings, and the floor of the cathedral cavern came up fast, and then there were arms around him, catching him, and he was pressed against a chest that smelled of smoke and knot-dye and home.

"Got you," the stranger said. "I've got you. I've got—"

The voice broke. Not the way voices break in stories, gracefully, at the right dramatic moment. It broke the way a rope breaks, suddenly and all at once, mid-word, and then the stranger was shaking, and Ivo realized with a strange floating clarity that the arms around him were holding him up as much as he was being held — that this person, whoever they were, was crying so hard they could barely breathe, crying with their whole body, great silent convulsions of grief with no end in sight.

"I don't—" Ivo started, and his own voice startled him. It was deeper than he remembered. Of course it was. Seven years. "I don't understand."

The stranger pulled back just far enough to look at him.

And Ivo saw it happen. He watched it cross the tear-wet face like a shadow crossing a field: the look of someone searching frantically for a word and finding the page blank. The eyes went over his features — the jaw that had squared since the last time they'd met, the height, the scar on his chin from falling off the guildhall roof at nine — and Ivo watched recognition arrive and *not arrive*, watched it reach for something and grasp air.

"You were in the bottle," the stranger said finally, and the voice was rough, scraped raw. "You were in the bottle and now you're not. You're here. You're — gods, you're so tall." A wet, broken laugh. "You're so tall. How are you so tall?"

"You know me," Ivo said. It wasn't a question. It was a plea. "You said my name. Before. In the dark, when you were carrying me — you said *Ivo, Ivo, hold on* —"

"Did I?" The stranger blinked, and something flickered there — a fish glimpsed deep underwater, gone before the eye could fix on it. "I must have. I suppose I must have known it somehow. The ledger, maybe. Or Emberfall — she would have told me, she — where is she? Is she—?"

"The dragon sealed the mountain," Ivo said. "She bought us the road. She's—" He stopped. He didn't actually know if Emberfall was alive, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a stone. "She spent herself. We have to go. The court is coming apart and the Magistrate does not forgive debts walked out on, even paid ones."

"Yes," the stranger said, and stood, and swayed, and Ivo caught their arm without thinking, the way you catch anyone who stumbles, and the stranger flinched — not away from him, exactly, but *at* him, at the touch, as though his hand had landed somewhere tender they hadn't known was wounded.

"Sorry," Ivo said. "Sorry. Do you — what do I call you?"

The question hung in the collapsing air between them. Around them the cathedral cavern was dying: bottled souls guttering in their niches like lamps running dry, the great scale in the center listing drunkenly, its two pans empty now, its chains groaning. Somewhere far off, stone was grinding against stone, the mountain shifting its weight, deciding whether to swallow its own heart.

The stranger opened their mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"I don't know," they said at last, and laughed — that same broken-rope laugh — and wiped their face with the back of a dye-stained hand. "That's the hell of it. I had a name. I'm nearly sure I had a name. There was a stall — I think there was a stall — there's a song I keep almost hearing, three bars of it, and I can't find the rest of it, and I keep crying and I don't know why, and I look at you and my chest feels like it's cracking open down some old seam, and I don't know *why*, do you understand? I don't know why. But I know I'd do it again. Whatever it was. Whatever it cost. I'd pay it twice."

The words went into Ivo like cold water into a burn.

Because he understood. Oh, he understood with a completeness that made him want to scream. He had spent seven years learning what the court took and how it took it, listening to the gray clerks gossip among the shelves, learning the grammar of the place. Memory was currency. Memory was meat and coin and oil for the wards. And payment left a wound shaped exactly like what it removed.

This person had paid for him. This person had paid *everything* for him — he could see it now, see the shape of the absence, the hollow where a life used to be. They were holding him with arms that remembered how to hold him and a mind that had forgotten why. They were weeping over him with grief that had outlived its own story, orphaned sorrow, love with the name torn off.

*Xanthe,* he thought. *Your name is Xanthe. You tie luck-knots wrong on purpose. You steal plums from the orchard on Tanner's Row and never get caught because you smile at the orchard-keeper's dog. You hum half a step flat. You promised me — you promised me once, on the roof, with plum juice on your chin — you promised me nothing bad would happen while the guild stood.*

He could give it back. That was the unbearable arithmetic of it. Every memory he had hoarded for seven years in the dark was a memory of *them*; his treasure-room was furnished entirely with their face, their voice, their hands. He could open the doors and pour it all back and make them whole with a word.

But he had seen what happened to people who gave memories to the court, and he had seen, too, in the dream-logic of the bottle, what happened to souls returned too fast to bodies too empty — and besides, some stubborn, newly-fleshed instinct told him that a gift poured back secondhand was not the same gift. That these things had to be regrown, not replanted. That a story told was worth more than a memory forced.

Later, he would not be able to say where the certainty came from. Perhaps from the soul itself, which knows things the mind does not. Perhaps from seven years of watching the Magistrate's clerks handle memories roughly, cramming them into bottles like coins into purses, and learning viscerally what memory deserved and what it did not.

For now, all he said was: "Then we'll find out together. Your name. The song. All of it." He took the stranger's dye-stained hand in his — it fit differently now, his palm broader than it had been at twelve, and he felt the stranger notice, felt them tremble. "But first we run. Can you run?"

"I can run," the stranger said, and there was something in their face then — lost, gutted, and yet lit from underneath, like coals under ash. "I don't know why, but I feel like I've been running toward you for seven years."

"You have," Ivo said, and his voice cracked on it, and he did not explain, because explaining would have required telling them everything, and he could not — not yet, not while the mountain groaned and the bottles died around them and somewhere below the Magistrate of Debts was rising from a chair of black iron to audit an escape.

* * *

They ran.

The sunken road had begun to flood with returning dark — the outlawed magic that pooled down here was agitated now, whipped up by the court's collapse, swirling around their ankles in cold luminous eddies. Behind them the cathedral cavern's lights went out niche by niche, and each extinguished bottle made a sound Ivo felt in his teeth, a small glass sigh, a soul going wherever unclaimed souls go. He did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people who were not being chased.

The stranger ran beside him, breathing hard, and every few strides glanced sideways at him — quick, hungry glances, the looks of a starving person at a feast they couldn't name. Once, when the road narrowed and they had to run single file behind him, Ivo heard them humming, half a step flat, three bars of a song with no ending, and his throat closed so tight he nearly stumbled.

"That song," he managed. "Where did you learn it?"

"I don't know," the stranger called back, ragged. "It's just — it's stuck. It's been stuck for as long as I can remember, which isn't very long, which is — hells, that's a strange sentence to say out loud—"

"It's the third bar that matters," Ivo said. "You always said — " He stopped himself. Swallowed it. *Not yet.* "Never mind. Keep running."

Ahead, impossibly, there was light. Not the cold bottled glow of the court but something warmer, amber-edged, flickering — the last of a fire burning at the mouth of the sunken road where it climbed toward the world. And silhouetted in that light, vast and dim and guttering like a candle in a doorway, wings half-folded, one great eye already clouding —

Emberfall.

She was dying. Ivo could see it the way you can see a fire is nearly out: not by any single sign but by the whole of it, the way her scales had gone the color of old ash, the way her breath came in long rattling draws, the way the light she cast was less a blaze now than a memory of one. She had spent herself sealing the mountain, and she had nothing left but enough to be a lamp at the door, and she had chosen to spend even that standing watch so they could find the way out.

The stranger saw her and made a sound — a small, wounded noise, the sound of someone greeting a friend they couldn't remember making.

"She held the road," Ivo said softly. "All this way. She carried the payment, seven years ago. She carried *you*, in a manner of speaking. She's been waiting to set it right ever since."

The stranger stopped, chest heaving, and looked from the dying dragon to Ivo and back again, and Ivo watched them try to assemble the story from fragments — the ledger, the dragon, the boy too tall for his years, the grief with no address. Watched them fail. Watched them choose, visibly, deliberately, to trust anyway.

"Whatever I did," the stranger said quietly, "to be part of this — I hope it was good. I hope I was good."

Ivo took their hand. Dye-stained, scarred, trembling. His whole hoarded treasury of seven years stood in front of him wearing a stranger's lost face, and he smiled, and the smile hurt more than the glass ever had.

"You were the best of us," he said. "Come on. The sky's been dark long enough."

Behind them, deep in the mountain, something began to climb.
