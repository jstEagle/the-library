## 19. Escape by Dragon-Light

The mountain began to scream.

It was not stone screaming, though the stone screamed too — a deep groaning that shivered up through Xanthe's boots and into their teeth. It was the court itself, a thousand bottled voices crying out at once as the Magistrate's authority cracked like river ice in spring. The asset was escaping. The asset had *woken*. Somewhere behind them, in the cathedral dark, the great scales still trembled from what had been placed upon them, and every soul in every bottle felt the balance shift.

"Up," Emberfall rasped, and her voice was smoke and cinders and very little else. "Get on. Both of you. Now."

She lay along the sunken road like a ship run aground, her wings half-spread, her scales gone the grey of old ash. Where once her hide had glowed with banked fire — Xanthe remembered it from the courtyard above, remembered thinking *she is a hearth about to go cold* — now only three points still burned: the hollow of her throat, the ridge between her shoulders, and one eye, amber and fierce and nearly alone.

Xanthe lifted Ivo onto her back. The boy was light in a way that frightened them — seven years beneath the mountain, seven years in glass, and he weighed no more than a bundle of winter kindling. His hands found the ridge-scales and gripped. He did not speak. He had not spoken since waking, except once, when he had looked at Xanthe — at this stranger who wept over him without knowing why — and said, quietly, "I know you. I don't know how."

That sentence had done more damage to Xanthe than the extraction had. The extraction had simply emptied a room. This sentence showed them the shape of the room.

Merrin was not there to climb up. Merrin was not anywhere anymore; she had paid herself into namelessness two hours past, dissolving in the smuggler's alcove with her hands folded like a woman going to sleep, until even her name went out of her and she became a small grey absence wearing a guildmaster's coat. They had left her there with dignity. There had been nothing left to bury.

"Emberfall," Xanthe said, hauling themselves up behind Ivo, "can you fly?"

"No." The dragon's laugh was a dry rattle. "But I can fall upward, if the road is steep enough, and the road under the world is nothing but steep. Hold on to something that matters."

Her wings beat once — a single enormous stroke that stirred the pooled magic of the sunken road into luminous spray — and then they were moving, skimming low along the ancient highway, gathering speed, the dark rushing past on either side. And the dark was not empty.

* * *

Emberfall felt them coming before she heard them: the court's collectors, the tall thin things in robes of unlit black that had stood so patiently at the tollgates for centuries, waiting for travelers foolish enough to pay with themselves. The Magistrate had loosed them all. Every debt-collector in the deep places, called home from their gates and set loose upon the road with one instruction, simple as hunger:

*The asset returns to its bottle.*

They came through the dark like scissors closing. She could smell them — or whatever passed for smelling in a creature running on her last spoonfuls of fire. They smelled of cold ledgers. Of ink dried a hundred years. Of doors shut gently on people who would not be coming back.

*I carried him down here,* she thought, and the thought was an old wound reopening. *Seven years ago, when the guild needed a courier no one would suspect, they asked me, and I was proud, and I flew him to the mountain's mouth and told myself it was transport, not betrayal.* She had told herself many things in four hundred years. Most of them had been lies she could live with. This one she had never managed to live with, only to carry, the way she now carried the boy himself.

Well. What one carries, one can carry back.

The first collector came out of a side gallery like a spider dropping on silk. Xanthe saw it — a shape too tall, arms too long, a face like a page where writing has been scraped away — and shouted something, and flung out a hand, and *magic* answered. Not guild-charm magic, not the little knotted lucks and wards that Xanthe had spent seven years hiding inside trinkets. This was raw road-magic, the outlawed stuff pooling in the sunken way, and it leapt to Xanthe's call because Xanthe had spent the whole descent learning its grammar.

The collector recoiled, hissing, its blank face smoking where the light touched it.

"You're getting better at that," Emberfall observed.

"I'm getting *desperate*, which looks similar."

"It usually does."

More of them now, ahead and behind, pouring from the galleries, from fissures, from the mouths of roads older than the city overhead. The court did not run. That was the terrible thing about it — the court never ran, never hurried, because debts always come due eventually and patience is the creditor's oldest weapon. But the Magistrate had given them a deadline, and deadlines made even eternity move fast.

A collector landed on her hindquarters. She felt its weight, absurdly slight for its size, like a ledger dropped on her tail. Its long fingers closed on her wing-joint, and where they touched, her fire guttered — the throat-flame flickering, dimming, the cold crawling inward along her spine like frost claiming a windowpane.

She did the math without wanting to. She had perhaps one true flame left in her. One. And the mountain's mouth was sealed behind seven years of collapsed wards, and the road climbed for six miles, and there were forty collectors on it.

One flame. Forty collectors. Six miles.

"Xanthe," she said. "The knot in my left wing-strap. The one you tied at the tollgate, when you thought I wasn't watching."

"What about it?"

"Untie it. Quickly."

* * *

Ivo watched the stranger untie the knot.

He knew the knot. That was the unbearable thing — he knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat. It was a dockworker's luck-knot, three-looped with a false fourth loop to fool the eye, and he had learned it at a workbench in a guildhall with sunlight on the wood, and someone with clever ink-stained fingers had guided his small hands through each turn and said—

And said—

The memory would not come. It rose in him like a fish rising toward the surface of a pond, and then, just before breaking, it turned aside. He knew there had been words. He knew the voice they'd been spoken in. He knew — with a certainty deeper than knowing — that the voice had been warm, and wry, and that he had loved it more than anything in the world.

He looked at the stranger's hands working the knot, and those hands were ink-stained, and something in his chest tore cleanly down the middle.

"They gave me away," he said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words came out of him the way water comes out of a cracked jar. "The guild. My family. They sold me, and I remember it, I remember *everything*, I remember Merrin's face when she signed it, I remember being carried down here in the dark and telling myself they'd come back for me, they'd *always* promised—" His voice broke. "Did they ever look for me? Did anyone—"

The stranger's hands stopped on the knot.

"I don't know," Xanthe said, and their voice was wrecked and honest. "I don't know who I am to you. But I know this: whoever came down here for you loved you so much they paid everything they had, and I watched them do it, and I think — I *think* — I think I was glad to do it. I don't have the memories. I have the shape of them. And the shape is enough to tell me you were loved the whole time, Ivo. Even when you were sold. Especially then. Someone fought the whole court of the mountain for you tonight, and I don't remember why, but my body remembers, and my body says it was worth it."

Ivo stared at them. Above them the dark seethed with collectors. Below them the road fell away into the screaming of the court. And in the middle of all of it, the boy reached out and took the stranger's ink-stained hand, because some things are older than memory, and grief shared is a rope, and they held on.

"The knot," Emberfall reminded them grimly. "*Now.*"

* * *

The knot came free, and Emberfall opened her last flame.

Not breathed — *released*. All of it, the hoarded ember of her final years, the fire she had been rationing since the day the stars went dark, poured out of her at once in a wheel of white-gold light that turned the sunken road into noon. The collectors shrieked — a sound like pages burning — and the ones clinging to her burned to ash mid-grip, and the ones ahead threw themselves against the walls to escape it, and the road ahead blazed clear as a lit fuse running up into the dark.

"There," she gasped. "That's the last of me, mostly. Six miles. Fly fast, burn bright, die well. A dragon could want worse."

"You're not dying," Xanthe said fiercely. "You're getting us out."

"I'm doing both, charm-seller. They're not mutually exclusive. Nothing interesting ever is."

She flew as she had not flown in a century. The road roared beneath them. The light of her own burning ran ahead of her like a herald, and where it struck the walls, the outlawed magic of the sunken way caught and blazed in sympathy, so that the whole six miles became a corridor of fire, and behind them the collectors pressed on through the flames because debts do not fear fire, but slowly, oh so slowly, because burnt things move carefully.

Ivo pressed his face against her neck-scales and felt her heat failing by degrees, like a day ending. He understood what she was doing. He had spent seven years in a bottle watching souls be spent, weighed, traded, consumed. He knew expenditure when he saw it. He put his small hand flat against the hollow of her throat, where the last flame lived, and did the only thing he knew how to do anymore.

He warmed it.

It was not much. It was a child's soul-fire, newly returned, barely kindled. But it was *his* — freely given, freely offered, the exact inverse of how it had been taken — and Emberfall felt it land in her like a coal dropped into a dying stove, and she laughed, a real laugh, crackling up through her ruined chest.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you *little fool*. You generous little fool. That's not how the arithmetic works, but thank you. Thank you anyway."

* * *

The mountain's mouth appeared at last — a ragged circle of grey pre-dawn light, impossibly far above, at the top of the final ascent where the sunken road broke surface into the world. And across it, sealing it like a cork in a bottle, hung the last defense of the court: a lattice of black chains and sigils, the same wards that had failed above seven years ago, rebuilt below in mirror, the court's guarantee that nothing taken would ever leave.

The Magistrate stood before it. Of course it did. It had walked calmly the whole way, unhurried, its robe of unlit black stirring in the updraft, its face a courteous blank. It raised one long hand, and the lattice tightened, and its voice filled the road the way a verdict fills a courtroom.

"The debt stands," it said. "Payment was rendered. The asset belongs to the court."

"The payment was *stolen*," Xanthe shouted back, sliding from Emberfall's back, dragging Ivo down with them. "It was stolen from a child! Stolen goods return to their owners — even your own law says so, I read it on your tollgate!"

"The law," the Magistrate said, "is mine to interpret."

"Yes," said Emberfall. "That's the trouble with courts."

She gathered herself. Three points of light remained in her — throat, shoulder, eye — and she looked at them a long moment, the way one looks at an old coat before giving it away.

"Xanthe Blackwood," she said. "Charm-seller. Knot-tier of lucks. Feeder of dragons who didn't deserve feeding. When you get up there — when the stars come back — plant something. Dragons die better over gardens."

Then she spread her wings, and gave them the last of herself.

The flame that came off her was not white-gold now. It was every color fire had ever been, the whole archive of her four hundred years, and it hit the lattice of chains and sigils like dawn hitting frost. The wards of the court — built to hold souls, built to hold *her cargo*, built on the assumption that nothing would ever spend itself willingly for a stolen child — burned through in nine seconds. The Magistrate stepped back, for the first time in recorded history, *surprised*, and the mountain's mouth stood open, and Emberfall caught them both — one claw around Xanthe, one around Ivo, gentle as a mother cat — and hurled them through the gap into the grey morning air.

Behind them, her last fire sealed the mountain.

It ran down into the depths along the sunken road, following the path of her own burning, and it met the rising dark of the pursuing court at the place where the road bent, and there the two forces closed like a gate. Stone flowed. Sigils fused. The mountain's throat sealed itself with dragon-fire worked into the rock, and the scream of the court behind it faded, and faded, and became silence, and became distance, and became merely a mountain again — a thing with snow on its shoulders and a locked door in its belly.

Of Emberfall herself, they saw one thing more. As they fell through the open air, turning, Xanthe saw her settle onto the mountainside below the sealed mouth, folding her wings like a tired traveler taking a seat, her scales grey as morning ash, one amber eye still open, watching them rise.

Watching the sky.

Waiting, with the last of her patience, to see whether the stars would come back.

* * *

They fell. Xanthe clutched Ivo, and Ivo clutched Xanthe, and the ground came up at them with the terrible democracy of gravity — it does not care who you are or what you paid — and then the air *caught* them.

Magic. The outlawed magic of the sunken road, spilled out of the mountain's mouth with them, pooling in the dawn air, and it slowed them the way a pond slows a thrown stone, and set them down in the wet grass of the high meadow below the peak as gently as a hand setting down a cup.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The grass was cold with dew. The sky overhead was the color of dirty pewter, starless still, but lighter at the eastern edge — the first suggestion, after seven years, that somewhere the sun still intended to rise.

Ivo sat up. He looked at the sky. He looked at his own hands, which glowed faintly, the way coals glow under ash. Seven years of soul, returned to him, humming in his chest like a struck bell that has not yet decided to stop.

"Xanthe," he said — testing the name, the name the stranger had given him on the road, the only name either of them had left. "What do I do? They gave it back to me. All of it. What is it *for*?"

Xanthe looked at the boy. At the strange, impossible, grieving, blazing child the guild had sold and the court had kept and love had stolen back at a price that could never be itemized. And though Xanthe had no memories of him — though the seven years were gone, sold fair and square, weighed and found sufficient — something shapeless and warm rose in them anyway, some residue the scale had not been able to take, and it answered before thought could.

"It's for lighting things," Xanthe said. "I think it was always for lighting things."

Above them, on the mountainside, the last amber eye of a dragon watched.

And Ivo stood up in the wet grass, in the starless pre-dawn, and raised his burning hands toward the empty sky.
