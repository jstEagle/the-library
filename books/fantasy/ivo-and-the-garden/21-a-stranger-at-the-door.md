## 21. A Stranger at the Door

The first thing Ivo learned about the world above the mountain was that it smelled like rain on hot stone.

He had forgotten that. Or rather — he had never known it, not in the way a body knows a smell, the way the lungs open to it before the mind has named it. Seven years in the bottled dark had left him with memories that floated loose as pollen: plums, knot-work, a voice singing off-key in the guild kitchen. But the smell of the city after rain — that was new, or newly returned, and he stood in the doorway of the ruined guildhall for a long time just breathing it.

The city remembered magic now. That was the strange arithmetic of his return. The wards were reknit, the stars relit one by one from his own hands, and everywhere he walked people touched their doorposts where fresh sigils glowed like banked coals. They remembered the guild. They remembered Emberfall — there was already a chalk drawing of her on the wall by the fountain, wings spread, fire pouring from her jaws, and children stood before it and made the shape of flames with their fingers.

They did not remember Xanthe Blackwood.

Ivo had learned this on his second morning above ground, when he asked an old woman at the well where he might find the charm-seller who had gone under the mountain. She looked at him with kind, untroubled eyes and said, *There's no charm-seller, love. There never was.* And then she had drawn water, and the rope had creaked, and the world had gone on as if a whole person could be poured out of it like tea leaves and leave no stain behind.

But Ivo remembered. That was the cruelty and the mercy of it, wound together so tightly he could not always tell which was which. He remembered everything — seven years of small kindnesses, stolen plums, rough hands guiding his fingers through a luck-knot, a promise made over a guttering lamp: *nothing bad will happen while the guild stands.* He remembered being given away. He remembered the glass, and the dreaming, and the stranger who had come down into the dark for him and wept without knowing why.

He owed that stranger everything. And the stranger did not know his name.

So on the third morning, Ivo went looking.

* * *

The charm-stall was exactly where a memory told him it would be — at the mouth of Tanner's Row, where the cobbles sloped toward the harbor and the wind came in off the water carrying salt and tar. It was smaller than he remembered, or perhaps he had simply been smaller when he'd last seen it: a folding table, a striped awning gone gray at the edges, trays of trinkets laid out with a care that even now, even stripped of meaning, could not quite disguise itself as ordinary.

Knots of braided cord. River stones with holes worn through them. Buttons polished bright. A jar of feathers. Nothing that glowed. Nothing that worked — not anymore, not openly, though Ivo's new-lit eyes could see the faint warmth still sleeping in the knots, the old guild craft folded into them like letters tucked into books no one read.

And behind the table sat Xanthe Blackwood.

They looked older than seven years should have made anyone. Their hair had gone iron at the temples. There was a stillness in them that Ivo recognized with a jolt — the same stillness the court's treasures had, the bottled souls arranged in their velvet rows, beautiful and complete and missing nothing except themselves. Xanthe was sorting buttons. Their hands moved with practiced grace, and every few moments they paused, holding a button up to the light, frowning slightly, as if trying to remember why this one mattered — and then setting it down again, gently, in a place of honor they could not explain.

Ivo's chest hurt. He let it hurt. Pain, he was learning, was just proof of weight — and weight was what the court took, and what he refused to let it keep.

He crossed the square. He stopped at the table. And because he had rehearsed this moment forty times on the walk down, and because all forty rehearsals deserted him at once, he said:

"Good morning. Do you sell luck?"

Xanthe looked up.

For a heartbeat — one long, suspended heartbeat in which the harbor gulls cried and the awning flapped and the whole city held its breath without knowing it — something moved behind Xanthe's eyes. Not recognition. Recognition would have been a miracle too large to ask for. It was smaller than that and vaster: the look of a person hearing a song they've never heard and finding their foot tapping anyway. The look of warmth reaching for a story to attach itself to, and finding none, and reaching anyway.

"Luck-knots," Xanthe said, and their voice was careful, the voice of someone walking on ice over deep water. "Three coppers. They're just cord, of course." A pause. "But I braid them tight."

"You braid them tight," Ivo repeated solemnly, as though this were a great secret. "That's what my teacher used to say."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face — there and gone, quick as a fish turning under dark water. "You had a teacher?"

"For charms. For knots. For which end of the plum to eat first." Ivo smiled. "She was very particular about plums."

"I—" Xanthe set down the button they were holding. Their hand, Ivo noticed, was not entirely steady. "I don't know why, but I think I might have hated whoever taught you plums. Affectionately. The way you hate someone you'd have taken a knife for."

"That sounds about right," Ivo said softly. "Three coppers, then. For a luck-knot."

Xanthe wrapped one in brown paper with hands that remembered how, and Ivo paid with coins he had earned hauling stone at the ward-menders' worksite, because some things had to be bought honestly or not at all. And then, instead of leaving, he sat down on the upturned crate beside the stall — the customer's crate, worn smooth by years of waiting backs — and said:

"If it's all the same to you, I'll wait here while the luck takes hold. Just to make sure it works."

Xanthe stared at him. The harbor wind stirred the gray-striped awning. Somewhere behind them, a fishmonger called out the price of herring, and the world declined, as worlds do, to pause for the important moments.

"It's cord," Xanthe said finally. "It doesn't—"

"It works," Ivo said. "Trust me. I'm the luckiest person in this city."

* * *

He came back the next day.

And the next. He learned the rhythm of the stall — mornings slow, midday busy with dockworkers wanting knots and river-stones and, lately, since the stars had come back, anything at all that might have been touched by magic, though Xanthe sold them only buttons and braided cord and never said otherwise. He learned that Xanthe drank their tea bitter enough to stand a spoon in. He learned that they hummed when they thought no one was listening — three notes of a tune that circled and circled and never found its fourth, because the fourth note lived in a memory that had been weighed against a soul and found sufficient.

On the fifth day, Ivo brought plums.

He set them on the table between the trays of trinkets, six of them, dark and dusted gold, and Xanthe looked at them the way a person looks at a word from a language they spoke as a child.

"Why these?" they asked.

"The seller at the market gave me a good price," Ivo lied cheerfully. "I couldn't eat them all. And I thought — someone at this stall looks like they have opinions about fruit."

"I don't know anything about fruit," Xanthe said. But their hand had already gone out, almost of its own accord, and turned one of the plums over, thumb finding the seam of it, and Ivo watched seven lost years move through their fingers like light through water.

"You eat it from the bottom," Ivo said. "Not the stem end. The stem end is a lie. Everyone thinks the stem end is where you start but it's bitter, it's all bitterness up there, and people give up on plums because of it. You start underneath. Where nobody's looking."

Xanthe bit into the plum from the bottom. Chewed. Went very still.

"Oh," they said, in a small voice, and Ivo had to look away at the harbor, at the masts and the gray water, because their face was doing something private and enormous and he was not yet allowed to see all of it.

"They're good," Xanthe managed. "Why are they good? Plums are never this good."

"They are if someone teaches you right," Ivo said. "That's the trick of most things, I think."

* * *

He began the stories on the ninth day.

Not because he had planned it — though he had, of course he had, he had planned it the way he had once planned charm-knots, looping each thread through in order, testing the tension. But plans, he had learned beneath the mountain, were just prayers wearing work clothes. He began the stories because Xanthe, sorting buttons in the blue hour before closing, had held one up to the light — a brass button stamped with a mountain goat, absurd and specific — and said, quietly, to no one:

"I keep this one in the front of the tray. I don't know why. Every time I try to move it back, my hands won't do it."

And Ivo, sitting on the customer's crate with the evening coming down around them like a curtain, heard himself say:

"There was a coat. When I was young — younger — there was a coat with a brass button on it, a goat, and I used to steal the button off it every chance I got, and it always got sewn back on, and I always stole it again. It was a game. The person who owned the coat pretended to be furious. They kept a special tin of thread just for sewing it back on. Blue thread, because they said the goat deserved to be caught in style."

Xanthe's hands had stopped moving.

"I don't know that coat," they said. Their voice was very careful, the way a person walks across a frozen pond.

"No," Ivo agreed. "But your hands know the button."

Silence. The awning flapped. Far off, the first star of the evening — *his* star, the one he had lit with his own two hands, and wasn't that a sentence he still couldn't finish thinking without laughing or weeping — pricked through the violet sky.

"What happened to the coat?" Xanthe asked.

"Its owner gave it away," Ivo said. "To pay a debt. But the debt got paid back, in the end. By someone who loved them." He stood, and stretched, and picked up his hat, because the ache in his chest needed somewhere to go and walking was as good a place as any. "Same time tomorrow?"

"You could just tell me the rest now," Xanthe said, and there it was again — that reach, that warmth grasping at a story it didn't own, hungry and bewildered and brave.

"No," Ivo said gently. "One a day. That's the bargain. Luck-knots are three coppers, but stories — stories you have to wait for. It's how you know they're worth something."

He was half a street away before he heard it: behind him, faintly, Xanthe humming. Three notes circling, circling —

— and then, for the first time in a year, the fourth.

* * *

Winter came down off the mountains early that year, and the charm-stall grew a brazier, and the customer's crate grew a cushion that appeared one morning without explanation and was never mentioned by either of them. Ivo told the story of the plum tree behind the guildhall, and how they had shaken it together in late summer and eaten until they were sick, and Xanthe laughed until they had to put down their tea, and then went quiet, pressing a fist to their mouth, and said, "I can almost feel the bark under my hands. Almost. Why *almost?*" And Ivo said, "Because it's yours. It's just traveling home slowly," and hoped it was true.

He told the story of the flood year, when the cellar flooded and they had bailed water side by side all night, and Xanthe had made a game of naming the fish that swam through the guildhall door. He told the story of the first charm Ivo ever knotted alone — crooked, ugly, technically a bracelet — and how Xanthe had worn it every day for a month and claimed the sun came out specifically because of it. He told the story of the night before everything, the guttering lamp, the promise: *nothing bad will happen while the guild stands.*

At that one, Xanwe — Xanthe — had set down their cup and looked at him for a long time across the brazier's coals.

"I broke that promise," they said. "I don't remember making it. But I know — the way you know a scar is there when it rains — I know I broke it. Whatever promise that was, whatever guild, whatever happened — I was there, and it fell, and I couldn't stop it." Their jaw tightened. "Tell me I'm wrong."

"You're wrong," Ivo said, and meant it with everything the star-fire in him could mean it. "You didn't break it. You paid for it. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and someday — not today — you'll believe me."

Xanthe nodded slowly, unconvinced, and banked the coals, and said, "Same time tomorrow," and Ivo carried the words *you'll believe me* home through the snow like a coal in a cupped hand.

* * *

On the last day of winter, Xanthe closed the stall early.

Ivo arrived at the usual hour to find the awning tied down, the trays packed away, and Xanthe standing in the empty square with their collar up against the wind, waiting, holding something wrapped in brown paper.

"You weren't at the stall," Ivo said, stupidly, because he had rehearsed nothing for this.

"I closed early." Xanthe held out the package. "I finished it last night. Took me most of the winter. I don't — I don't fully understand why I was making it, or who it was for, except that every time I tried to stop, my hands wouldn't let me, and those are usually the things worth trusting." They pushed it into his arms. "Open it."

Inside the paper was a luck-knot. But not like the ones in the trays — this one was wide as a belt, woven from a hundred cords in every color, and worked into the braid, Ivo saw as he turned it over, were beads and buttons and bits of sea-glass and one brass goat, and at the center, where a clasp should be, a small tin badge stamped with the sigil of the Guild of Charmwrights, polished bright.

"It's a record," Xanthe said. Their breath fogged the air between them. "Every story you told me this winter, I wove a strand for it. The plums. The flood. The coat. All of it. I don't remember any of it happening to me." Their voice cracked, and steadied, the way a knot holds. "But I remember you telling me. And I thought — maybe that counts. Maybe being told is its own kind of remembering. And maybe—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Maybe whoever you lost, whoever all these stories belong to — maybe if you wear it, some of what I built into it will find them. The way the fourth note found me."

The snow had begun to fall, fine and dry, catching in Xanthe's iron hair. Ivo held the knot-belt against his chest and felt the star-fire in him lean toward it like a plant toward a window.

"Xanthe," he said.

"Yes?"

"Do you want to hear tomorrow's story early?"

Xanthe blinked. Snow settled on their eyelashes. "What's it about?"

"It's about a garden," Ivo said. "An old guildhall with nothing left standing but the foundation stones, and a person who decides to plant something there anyway. It's about rows and names. It's about—" he swallowed, "—it's about how long it takes a warm thing to remember what it's warm *for*. And I need help digging. So I'm afraid the story comes with labor attached."

Xanthe looked at him for a long moment. The reach was there again, the warmth grasping — but this time it wasn't grasping at nothing. This time, Ivo could see, it had caught hold of something: a thread, a strand, a beginning.

"All right," Xanthe said. "Tomorrow. Bring the shovel."

And they walked together out of the square, two figures in the falling snow, one carrying a hundred woven stories and the other carrying the shape of a love with no story attached — yet — while overhead, above the wards, above the mountain, the first star burned steady and patient, keeping its light on for them both.
