## 22. The Garden

The old guild grounds had been a ruin for a year before Ivo decided they should be something else.

He came in the first week of spring, when the frost still held the soil hard as iron, and stood at the broken gate with a sack of seed potatoes over one shoulder and a hand-forged trowel at his belt — a gift from the dockworkers, who remembered him now, or were learning to. Behind him the city went about its morning: carts rattling over cobblestones, gulls arguing above the harbor, the great bell of the wardhouse tolling the hour in a voice that no longer stuttered or cracked. The wards held. The stars held. Every night he climbed to the roof and counted them anyway, all forty-one of them, and every night they burned.

But the guildhall itself was a blackened skeleton. Fire had taken it in the last days of the court's pursuit — or perhaps Merrin's dying magic had taken it, though no one could say for certain, because no one remembered Merrin at all. There was a gap in the city's memory shaped like her, and like Xanthe, and Ivo was the only person alive who knew both shapes intimately, who carried them the way a riverbed carries the memory of water.

He set down his sack, put his hands on the ruined gatepost, and said aloud, to the ashes and to whoever might be listening from wherever the dead go: "I'm going to grow something here."

Then he began to dig.

* * *

Xanthe noticed him before Xanthe knew why.

That was how it went now. A year since the mountain, and Xanthe's days had rebuilt themselves around an absence so large it had become invisible, the way fish do not know water. Xanthe sold charms again — real ones, small ones, luck-knots and salt-wards and thread spun under a waning moon — because the city had remembered magic and wanted it back, hungry for it, and the guild's last charm-seller was Xanthe whether Xanthe knew what that meant or not. The stall stood where it had always stood, near the harbor steps. The hands knew the knots. The mouth knew the prices. The rest was warmth without a story, a hearth-fire burning in an empty room.

And every few days, a young man came by.

He was maybe seventeen now, tall the way saplings are tall, with soot-dark hair and eyes that did something strange to Xanthe's chest whenever they met theirs — a catching, like a knot pulled almost tight. He never bought anything. He would stand at the stall, polite as a stranger, and say, "Good morning. Do you have any plums today?" And if Xanthe did, he would buy one, eat it slowly, watching the harbor, and leave a coin that was always slightly too much.

"Keep your change," Xanthe told him once. "You overpay every time."

"I know," he said, and smiled, and there was grief in the smile, deep and old and patient, grief that did not belong on a face that young. "It's not for the plum."

Xanthe watched him walk away and felt the warmth in their chest lean after him like a plant toward light, and did not know why, and did not ask. Asking hurt. Xanthe had learned that in the first months — learned that pressing against the blank place behind their ribs produced a pain like a memory of pain, which is the worst kind, because it has no event attached, only its own echo.

So Xanthe sold charms, and ate lunch alone, and every few days bought plums for a stranger who overpaid, and called it nothing at all.

* * *

By midsummer the garden had rows.

Ivo worked it the way he had once worked charms — patiently, precisely, with the particular devotion of someone making an offering. He had cleared the guildhall's bones into a cairn at the north edge, salvaged the foundation stones, and laid out beds in the old footprint of the building, so that the garden grew in the shape of everything that had been lost. He hauled water from the public pump in two dented buckets, morning and evening, until his shoulders browned and his palms hardened and the blisters became calluses became something like armor.

Each row he named.

This was the part no one understood but him, and he didn't try to explain it. The first row, nearest the gate, he planted with plums — three trees, barely more than switches, bought with too-generous coins — and he named that row *Stolen Plums*, because the first story he had ever told Xanthe, standing awkward at a charm-stall with fruit sweating in his palm, had been about a boy and his teacher and a plum tree behind the guildhall, and how the teacher had taught him to pick the high ones by knotting a loop of cord, and how they had eaten them sitting on the roof with their legs dangling over the eaves, spitting pits at the pigeons.

Row two was *The Knot That Would Not Hold* — beans, climbing things, because the story was about the day seven-year-old Ivo had tied his first luck-knot upside down and backward and Xanthe had laughed until they cried and then sat with him for four hours undoing it together, saying, *No, here, feel how the thread wants to go — you don't force a charm, you persuade it.*

Row three was *The Night of the Rain* — lettuces, cool and quick — for the story of the storm that flooded the guild cellar and the two of them bailing water with cooking pots while Merrin shouted cheerfully useless instructions from the stairs.

Row after row. Story after story. He planted thirty-one rows in all, one for each memory he meant to give back, and left the last bed — the largest, at the center, where the guildmaster's desk had once stood — empty and turned and waiting.

He wasn't sure yet what went there. He would know when he knew.

Some evenings he walked down to the harbor steps and bought a plum from a charm-seller who looked at him with that caught, leaning warmth and asked nothing, and he carried the pit home in his pocket, and sometimes, if the day had been good, he told the garden about it.

* * *

Autumn came, and with it the first harvest, and with it the strangeness that Xanthe could no longer pretend was nothing.

It started small. Xanthe passed the old guild grounds on a market morning — avoided it usually, the way one avoids a stair missing a step — and saw the green rows inside the broken gate, and stopped, and stood there so long that a woman carrying fish asked if they were quite well.

"It's beautiful," Xanthe said, meaning it, meaning something larger than the words could hold. "Who —"

"The young man who lives there," the fishwife said. "Ivo. Quiet lad. Grows enough to feed half the street." She squinted at Xanthe. "You look like you've seen a ghost."

"No," Xanthe said slowly. "I look like I've seen a garden."

But that night Xanthe dreamed of a roof, and dangling legs, and the sweet wet spit of plum pits, and woke with tears already drying and no idea whose they were. The warmth behind their ribs had a shape now. It had been leaning toward something for a year, and in the dream it had finally leaned far enough to see over the wall.

Three nights later, Xanthe went back to the gate and stood there until Ivo came out with his watering cans and saw them, and went very still, the way a person goes still when the thing they have been waiting for arrives in a form they did not expect.

"You're the charm-seller," he said.

"You're the one who buys plums."

"Yes." He set down the cans. His hands were shaking; Xanthe saw it and did not understand it. "Would you like to see the garden?"

* * *

He showed them every row.

He did it deliberately, unhurriedly, naming each one as they walked — *Stolen Plums, The Knot That Would Not Hold, The Night of the Rain* — and Xanthe followed, nodding politely, hearing names that meant nothing and feeling, with each one, a soft concussion behind the ribs, like a door being knocked on from the inside.

"This row," Ivo said, kneeling by the beans, "is my favorite. Do you want to know why it's called what it's called?"

"If you'd like to tell me."

"There was a boy," Ivo said, turning the soil with his fingers, not looking up, "who couldn't tie a luck-knot. Everyone said he'd never learn, that some people just aren't made for magic. But there was a charm-seller — the best in the city, though she'd deny it — who sat with him for four hours one afternoon and showed him that you don't force a charm. You persuade it. You ask the thread where it wants to go." He looked up then, and his eyes were bright and terrible. "She said everyone's made for magic. They just need someone patient enough to find the knot in them."

Xanthe's breath went out of them slowly. The warmth behind their ribs flared, and for the first time in a year, it attached — hooked itself to a face, a voice, an afternoon that Xanthe could not remember but could suddenly, unbearably, feel.

"That sounds," Xanthe managed, "like a good teacher."

"She was." He stood. He was crying, quietly, without drama, the way he did everything. "Do you know, most people think stories are how we keep the past? I used to think that. But I've learned they're how we grow it. You can't replant a memory. But you can plant the story of one, and tend it, and wait, and sometimes —" his voice caught — "sometimes the thing that comes up isn't the same, but it's alive."

Xanthe reached out — not knowing why, the hand moving on its own errand — and touched the scarred earth of the bean row, and felt beneath the soil the whole buried architecture of a love that had been paid away to save them both, and heard themselves say, in a voice gone strange:

"I don't remember you. But I think I would have liked to."

"You will," Ivo said. "One row at a time. If you'll let me."

* * *

Winter was mild that year, the wards humming overhead like a held note, and Ivo spent it teaching.

Not magic — or not only magic. He taught Xanthe the garden the way Xanthe had once taught him charms: patiently, persuasively, asking the thread where it wanted to go. He told one story a day, as he had promised himself he would, and planted each telling somewhere — in the compost heap, in the cold frames, in the cairn of guildhall stones — until the whole grounds became a book written in dirt and patience.

And Xanthe changed. Slowly. Not remembering — that was gone, spent fairly in a cathedral cavern under a mountain, weighed against a child's soul and found sufficient — but *growing toward*. The way a root grows toward water it cannot see. Some evenings Xanthe would laugh at something Ivo said and stop, startled, hearing in their own laughter a tone they somehow recognized as older than the year they'd known him. Some mornings Xanthe would wake with a word on their lips — *persuade, not force* — and carry it all day like a coin of unknown currency that kept proving spendable.

In the spring, Xanthe moved into the cottage Ivo had built against the cairn. The neighbors assumed they were kin. Neither corrected them, because neither knew the word for what they were, and because *kin* was close enough, and because some truths are better grown than declared.

On the first warm day of the second spring, Ivo knelt at the empty central bed — the one kept fallow all this time, the one where the guildmaster's desk had stood — and finally knew what to plant there.

"What is it?" Xanthe asked, coming to stand beside him, their shadow falling across the turned earth alongside his.

"A plum tree," Ivo said. "From the pit of the first plum you ever gave me back." He held it up between finger and thumb: small, dark, dry, patient. "The original tree burned with the hall. This one's from a story instead of a memory. So it'll be different. But it'll be alive."

He dug the hole. Xanthe knelt and helped, their hands working the soil together, and when the pit was covered and watered, they sat back on their heels in the sun, side by side, in a garden growing in the shape of everything lost.

"Do you ever wish you hadn't?" Xanthe asked quietly. "Paid what you paid. For me."

Ivo considered it honestly, the way he considered everything. "Every night, for a while," he said. "I'd lie awake and think: he doesn't even know me. What did I buy with all those years? An empty room." He picked up a handful of soil, let it run through his fingers. "But then I started planting. And I realized — the room isn't empty. It's just early spring." He smiled, and the grief in it had softened into something that could live alongside joy. "You're worth the price, Xanthe. You always were. You just needed a growing season."

Above them, faint and steady, the wards hummed. Beyond the broken gate, the city went about its morning. And in the center of the garden, in the richest bed, in soil fed by thirty-one true stories, the plum pit slept in the dark, doing what seeds do — holding its small green secret, waiting for the warmth to find it, and begin, again, to grow.
