## 1. Charms for a Starless City

The luck-knot took Marguerite Ishikawa the better part of an hour to tie, and he did it with his hands inside a flour sack so that no one passing the window of his rented room would see the thread move when it shouldn't.

That was the trick of it — not the knot itself, which any sailor's wife in Ashfall could manage, but the *willing* of it. The way you held the red cord and thought of the person who'd carry it, thought of them whole and lucky and un-drowned, and let something older than thinking slide down your fingers into the weave. His master had called it *small work*. Small enough to hide. Small enough, Marguerite had learned by nineteen, to sell.

He shook the finished knot out of the sack and held it up to the grey light coming through his one window. It looked like nothing: a fist-sized tangle of cord, sea-colored threads worked through the red at the center, three beads of river-glass caught in the loops. But when he turned it slowly, the glass beads caught the light a half-breath after they should have, as though they were remembering it rather than reflecting it. That was how you knew it had taken.

Below, Ashfall was waking up the way it always did — badly, loudly, smelling of fish and coal smoke. Gulls argued over the midden heaps behind the chandlery. Somewhere a cart with a bad axle screamed its way up Rope Street. The tide was coming in, and you could hear it under everything, that long slow breathing against the pilings, like the city itself asleep and dreaming of the sea.

Marguerite tucked the luck-knot into his coat, pulled his hood up, and went down to earn his rent.

* * *

The Saltmarket opened at dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the harbor cranes it was already a crush of bodies and noise — fishwives shouting prices, a knife-grinder's wheel shrieking, children weaving between legs with baskets bigger than they were. Marguerite had a stall there, or something like a stall: a plank across two barrels at the edge of the market, near the fountain where the water tasted faintly of iron, where he sold charms that were almost legal.

Almost legal was the whole art of surviving in Ashfall these days. The Crown's law said no working of magic without license, and no licenses had been issued in eleven years, not since the last of the old wardens died and the Crown decided the craft had died with him. But the law also said a charm-seller's goods must be *inert trinkets*, and no inspector alive could prove that a knotted cord or a dried sprig of rowan wasn't inert unless he stood and watched it being made. So Marguerite sold knots and sachets and little sewn birds, and he made them all at home, alone, behind a locked door, with his hands hidden in sacks, and if the thread sometimes moved when it shouldn't — well. That was between him and the customer.

"Oi. Charm-man."

Brannick the dockworker came shoving through the crowd the way he always did, like a barge through reeds — a big man gone soft at the middle, forearms like hawsers, a beard full of crumbs from whatever he'd eaten walking over. He'd been coming to Marguerite's plank for two years now. He never bought anything but luck-knots, and he never bargained, and he always looked over both shoulders before he reached the stall, which told Marguerite everything about what Brannick thought he was buying.

"You've got it?" Brannick asked, low, already reaching for his purse.

"For your daughter's wedding." Marguerite set the knot on the plank between them, casual as a man showing off ribbon colors. "Sea-thread through the heart of it. She'll have fair winds and a husband who can't drown."

"She's marrying a cooper," Brannick said. "Not much drowning risk in a cooperage."

"There's drowning risk everywhere," Marguerite said. "I've seen a man slip in a rain barrel."

Brannick laughed, big and easy, and paid him four coppers more than the asking price because Brannick had never once in two years counted right when he was nervous. Marguerite slid the coins into his palm and felt, as he always did, the small hot shame of it — this money was earned by breaking a law whose punishment was the fire-pits on Warden's Hill, and worse, and Brannick didn't even know he was complicit. To Brannick this was a lucky knot from a charm-man, harmless folk stuff, grandmother's magic. He didn't know that the thread had *moved*.

"Here." Brannick lingered, shifting his weight. "You heard anything? Down the docks, they're saying—"

"Saying what?"

"That the border lights went out. Up past Greywater. The beacon-wards on the passes." Brannick rubbed his beard, scattering more crumbs. "Caravan men came in yesterday saying the whole ridge went dark at once, like someone snuffed candles. And the toll-keepers on the north road haven't sent their weekly bird."

Marguerite kept his face pleasant and empty, the face he wore for inspectors and gossips alike. "Beacons go out all the time. Weather."

"Not *all at once*, they don't." Brannick leaned closer. His voice dropped to almost nothing, under the market noise. "And my sister's boy is posted at Greywater fort. No letters in nine days."

"Nine days isn't so long."

"It's long for him." Brannick straightened up, embarrassed suddenly, a big man caught at being afraid in daylight. "Anyway. The knot. Tell her — tell her it's from her da."

"I will," Marguerite said, and watched the dockworker shoulder back into the crowd, and stood for a moment longer than he meant to with the coins cooling in his hand.

Border wards. His master had used that word exactly once, in eleven years of teaching him small work, and had gone so pale saying it that Marguerite had never asked again. *There are small workings and there are great ones, and you will learn only the small ones, and you will thank me for it every day you live.* Then the master had died — quietly, in his sleep, in the cottage in the hills — and Marguerite had come down to Ashfall with a satchel of cord and river-glass and a head full of things he'd been forbidden to understand.

He sold six more charms before noon. A sprig of rowan to a woman whose son was sailing with the herring fleet. A sewn wren to a girl who wanted to be brave. A plain knotted bracelet to a child who just liked the color, and Marguerite charged her nothing and put no work in it at all, because some things should stay only what they seem.

By the time the bells rang noon, he had rent money in his pocket and a bad feeling in his chest that he couldn't name.

* * *

His landlady lived on the ground floor of the crooked house on Tanner's Lane and collected rent in person, on the first of the month, with her spectacles pushed up into her hair and her hand out before she'd finished climbing the stairs.

"Marta," Marguerite said, opening his door before she could knock. "You're early."

"I'm punctual. You're surprised." She counted the coins twice, licked her thumb, counted again. "This is too much."

"It's not."

"It's two coppers too much, Marguerite, I can count." She peered up at him over the coins, and her eyes behind the spectacles were sharp as a fish-gut knife. "You've been doing well lately. Too well, for a man selling ribbons and dead birds."

"Business is good."

"Hm." She pocketed the coins anyway, all of them, because Marta was honest but not generous. At the top of the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back at him with an expression he couldn't read. "The bells are going to ring wrong tonight," she said.

Marguerite laughed. "What do you mean, wrong?"

"I mean wrong." She said it flatly, the way she said the rent was due. "Old Toma the bell-ringer came by this morning, white as whey. Says the great bell in the Cathedral tower has a crack in it nobody's found, says it'll toll thirteen tonight instead of twelve, and nobody can stop it. Thirteen times, at midnight. He tried to get the priests to silence the bell and they threw him out." She sniffed. "Superstition. But Toma's rung those bells forty years and he doesn't rattle easy."

"What happens if it rings thirteen?"

"How would I know?" Marta said. "Nothing good, I'd guess. Nothing ever comes of thirteen." And she went down the stairs, and Marguerite closed his door, and stood in his small room with its sacks and cords and river-glass, listening to the tide breathe under the city, and tried to shake the feeling that the day had tilted somewhere without his noticing.

He spent the afternoon making stock for tomorrow's market — simple things, mostly inert, mostly honest. He was halfway through sewing a wren's wing when the light changed.

It didn't dim, exactly. It *thinned*, the way milk thins with water, and Marguerite looked up from his needle and saw that the shadows in his room had gone the wrong color — not grey, not blue, but a kind of hungry absence, as if the light were being drunk somewhere upstream. Outside, the gulls had stopped. That was the thing he noticed most, afterward: the silence of the gulls, a whole city's worth of birds going quiet at once, like animals before a quake.

Then the bells began.

Not midnight. Not evening yet — the sky still held a bruised orange light in the west. But the Cathedral bell swung into its first stroke with a sound Marguerite felt in his teeth, a sound wrong the way a voice is wrong when the throat is damaged, and it tolled once, and twice, and Marguerite stood up so fast his stool went over.

Three. Four. The whole city was stopping to listen; he could feel it, the way you feel a crowd hold its breath. Five. Six. Somewhere down the lane a dog began to howl, high and continuous, a sound with no end in it.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

Marguerite's pouch of protective charms hung on its peg by the door — the ones he actually believed in, the ones he'd made for himself and never sold, rowan and iron filings and a knot tied with his own blood in the core of it, the one indulgence his master had allowed him. As the tenth stroke fell, the pouch began to smoke.

Eleven. Twelve.

He tore it off the peg and the charms burned black in his hands — not aflame, not hot, but *blackening*, the rowan leaves curling into ash that stayed leaf-shaped, the iron filings going dull and rotten like old blood, the blood-knot drinking its own red until the cord was the color of a drowned man's lips. They weren't destroyed. They were *emptied*. Something had come along and drunk them dry, the way a lamp drinks oil, and left the wicks standing.

Thirteen.

The thirteenth stroke rolled out over Ashfall and did not fade the way a bell note fades. It simply stopped, cut clean, mid-resonance, and the silence that followed was so total that Marguerite could hear his own heartbeat and the tide beneath the city and — underneath both, far away, vast and patient — something else. A pressure. Like water under ice. Like a mouth deciding whether to open.

He went to the window.

Night had come while the bells rang, though it was hours early, and the stars were going out. Not covered by cloud — there was no cloud; the sky above Ashfall was clear and black and terrible — but going out one by one, guttering like lamps starved of oil, whole constellations folding up like fans. The Shepherd. The Sisters. The Ship that every sailor in the city navigated by. Gone, and gone, and gone, until only a handful remained, trembling, and then fewer, and then—

Down in the street, people were coming out of their doors. He could see them from above: the chandler's family in their nightclothes, the knife-grinder with his wheel abandoned, old Toma the bell-ringer on his knees in the middle of Tanner's Lane with his arms raised toward the dying sky. Nobody was screaming. That was the worst of it. They were all looking up in silence, hundreds of faces turned starward, watching the oldest lights in the world go dark like a debt being called in.

Marguerite's hands were shaking. In his palm, the emptied charms lay black and weightless, and when he whispered — hardly knowing he did it, some reflex older than sense — *"ward and witness"* — the words fell out of his mouth and hit the floor like dropped stones. Dead words. The small work was gone. Whatever he'd been carrying all these years, quietly, illegally, like a coal in his chest — the wind had gone out of it.

And in the last of the starlight, before that too failed, he saw it: far to the north, beyond the harbor, beyond the hills, a darkness on the horizon that was not night. Night was everywhere. This was *specific*. A shape of deeper black standing against the black sky, immense and slow, at the exact place where — his master's voice surfaced from eleven years down, calm and final and damning —

*The border wards, little fox. There are small workings and there are great ones.*

The last star went out.

In the dark, Marguerite Ishikawa stood at his window with a pouch of dead charms in his hand, and understood, with the quiet certainty of a man reading his own name on a debt notice, that whatever had just happened — whatever had come down off the borders and swallowed the sky — was going to need to be answered for.

And that somehow, impossibly, the answer was him.
