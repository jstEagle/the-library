## 2. The Night the Wards Fell

The bells began at dusk, and they were wrong.

Marguerite heard them from his window above the tallow-chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, where he had been mending a luck-knot by candlelight because even now — especially now — he could not bear to waste good light on bad work. The evening bells of Ashfall rang in sevens: seven strokes for the closing of the harbor gates, seven for the lighting of the watch-fires, seven for the Angelus that no one believed in anymore but everyone kept out of habit. He had lived beneath those bells for three years and knew their voices the way he knew the creak of his own floorboards.

Thirteen strokes.

He set down the knot. His fingers had gone cold without his noticing, and the wax candle guttered though there was no draft.

Below in the street, a woman stopped mid-stride with her market basket still on her arm and turned her face up toward the bell tower as if she expected to see blood running from its windows. A dog began to howl somewhere near the fishmarket, and then another, and then all of them, a rising chord of animal grief that made the hair on Marguerite's arms lift. In the chandler's shop beneath him, old Bramm's voice said, quietly, to no one: *Not again. Not like my grandmother's time.*

*Again?* Marguerite thought. *Like when?*

He never got to ask. Because that was the moment the stars went out.

It was not clouds. He would have welcomed clouds; clouds were weather, and weather was honest. This was different. One instant the sky was salted with winter stars, sharp and indifferent over the rooftops of Ashfall City — and the next they were simply gone, all of them, everywhere, as if a hand had passed across the world and smothered each small flame between finger and thumb. No fade. No dimming. They were there, and then they were not, and the darkness that replaced them was not the darkness of night but something thicker, something with weight, pressing down against the rooftops like a lid on a pot.

In his coat pocket, against his hip, Marguerite felt his charms burn.

He had made them himself, of course — a hedge-witch's kit of knotted thread and iron filings and one true charm, a warding sigil stitched into oiled leather with thread pulled red from his own palm, which was illegal in six ways and stupid in a seventh. He had carried it for protection since he'd come to the city, the way other men carried knives. Now it heated against his skin like a coal snatched from a hearth, and through the leather he could smell it scorching — hot iron and burnt hair and something else underneath, something older, like the smell of a cellar that has been shut too long.

He pulled it out. The sigil was black. Not scorched-black, not inked-black, but black the way the sky was black — a blackness that seemed to have *depth*, that drew his eye in and gave nothing back. The knot beside it had unraveled itself. Every knot in his pouch had unraveled. Thread does not do that. Thread does not decide, overnight, to forget the shape you gave it.

"Marguerite," Bramm called up the stairs, and his voice was strange, flattened, careful. "Boy. Come look at this."

But Marguerite was already looking. Out the window, down the length of Candlewick Row, every lamp in every window had gone the same impossible black — not extinguished but *filled*, each flame replaced by a little standing pool of dark that burned cold instead of hot. And in the doorways, in the mouths of alleys, at the far end of the street where it opened onto the Processional, shapes were gathering. People. But people who stood wrong, heads tilted, faces turned toward the sky as though listening for something only the starless dark could say.

Then someone screamed, and the night came apart.

* * *

By midnight, Ashfall was burning.

Not all of it — not yet. The fires started small and senseless, the way fires do when fear outruns sense: a barricade of cart-wheels at the Harbor Gate, a customs house with its windows smashed, a wicker man built in the square outside the Wardens' Hall and lit before anyone remembered there hadn't been wardens in Ashfall for a hundred years, only their empty hall and their empty titles and the Crown's witch-finders billeted in their old barracks. Marguerite watched it from the high ground of the Tanner's Stair with two hundred other people who had done the same arithmetic he had — *the low streets will flood with worse than water tonight* — and who stood pressed together in the smoke-smeared dark, saying nothing, listening.

Because the refugees were coming up from the border roads, and they were carrying stories.

He heard the first of them from a carter woman sitting on the stairs beside him, her hands wrapped around a cup of nothing, her eyes fixed on the burning city below. She had come, she said, from Vell's Crossing, three days east, where the border wards had stood since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother. The wards had not broken, she said. That was the thing no one understood. Breaking was loud, breaking was lightning and shattering stone. The wards had simply *stopped* — gone quiet all at once, like a heart deciding to rest — and the dark beyond the border, the old dark that the wards had held back for four hundred years, had walked in through the open door without hurrying at all.

"It doesn't chase you," the carter woman said. Her voice was steady, which was somehow worse than screaming. "You have to understand that. It doesn't need to chase. It walks, and everything it walks past is fine afterward. Fine. Alive. Smiling. Only—" She stopped. Started again. "My sister's boy. I was carrying him on my hip when it came down the road, and I ran, and I got away, and I am sitting here telling you I got away. But I cannot tell you his name. I cannot tell you his *face*. I know I had a sister. I know she had a child. It's like a room in my head with the furniture gone. There's the shape of it on the floorboards where things stood, and nothing standing in the shape."

Around her, people had gone very still.

"They're calling it the Hollow," someone said further down the stair. "The soldiers are calling it the Hollow."

"The wards fell," another voice said, an old man's, cracked with age or crying. "Four hundred years the Ishikawas fed them, and the wards have fallen, and now it eats—"

"*Quiet,*" said someone sharply, and Marguerite understood why a heartbeat later, when a lantern flared at the foot of the stairs and a patrol of witch-finders came climbing, gray cloaks and iron masks pushed up, swords drawn but not raised. Looking for something. Looking for someone. Their officer was reading aloud from a paper by lantern-light, and though Marguerite could not hear the words, he saw the crowd's reaction ripple outward from the man like a stone dropped in still water: shoulders drawing in, faces turning away, a collective instinct to become unremarkable.

*Never let them find you.*

His master's voice, eleven years dead and eleven years silent, arriving in his skull with the clarity of a bell struck inside his own chest. *Never let them find you, Marguerite. Whatever happens — whatever falls, whatever burns — you keep your name small and your hands in your pockets and you walk away. Promise me. Swear it on your own blood, since you're so fond of bleeding for your little charms.*

He had sworn it at nine years old, laughing, thinking it a game, the way children swear things. He had kept it for thirteen years without knowing what he was keeping. And now, on a freezing stair above a burning city, with witch-finders climbing toward him and a dead man's warning ringing in his ears, Marguerite finally asked himself the question he had spent his whole runaway life avoiding:

*Find me to do what?*

The answer came before he was ready for it, in the old man's voice again, softer now, the way it had been on the last morning, when his master had stood in the cottage doorway with frost on his beard and something terrible and tender in his face: *Because you are the last of it, child. And the debt comes due to the last of a line before anyone else.*

* * *

He did not go back to Candlewick Row. That was the one piece of sense he kept all night, the one coin he refused to spend: the witch-finders would search the lodgings of every registered hedge-craftsman in the city, and Marguerite Ishikawa — registered under a false name, a false trade, a false history three layers deep — would be found in his bed with a pouch full of unravelled charms and a sigil burned black as sin. So he went down instead, away from the firelight, into the warren of storage cellars and coal-vaults behind the tanneries where he sometimes sold charms to the dockworkers who couldn't afford the licensed charm-shops, and he told himself he would wait until dawn, until the city remembered how to be a city, until—

That was where the dying woman found him.

He heard her before he saw her: a dragging step in the passage outside the coal-vault where he'd wedged himself among barrels, and a voice, thin as wire, saying, "Hello the dark. I can smell you. Hedge-craft. You smell like my mother's kitchen."

"I'm not here," Marguerite said, which was foolish, and she laughed — a wet, ruined sound — and came in anyway.

She was younger than her voice. Thirty, perhaps, maybe less, though the thing killing her had aged her like fruit left in sun. She wore the mud-stained riding leathers of the border country and she was holding her left side the way you hold a wound you've already accepted, and when she lowered herself onto an overturned crate in the light of Marguerite's shuttered hand-lamp, he saw that her lips were gray and that the blood on her side was not entirely red. Part of it was black. Part of it moved.

"Wardens' road," she said, when he asked — because he was a fool, because he was kind, because some part of him had already understood and wanted desperately to be wrong. "I rode the wardens' road from Vell's Crossing ahead of the others. Someone had to carry word." She smiled at him, and it was the smile of the carter woman's story, the smile of someone with furniture missing from the rooms of herself. "Word got here. Word always gets here. Just — usually slower than the thing it warns about."

"You're hurt. Let me—" He had needle and thread and a hedge-healer's knowledge, half of it outlawed, all of it inadequate, and he was reaching for his satchel when her hand shot out and caught his wrist with a strength that had no business being in her.

"No time. Listen to me. Listen." She pulled him close enough that he could see her eyes properly, and they were the worst thing he had seen all night, because they were clear. Completely, terribly clear. "The wards didn't fail. Understand? Wards don't fail. They're fed or they're starved, and they were starved. Four hundred years the Ishikawa line fed them — a life apiece, generation upon generation, poured into the border-stones like wine into the ground — and four hundred years is exactly as long as it takes for a family to die out, or hide, or run. And someone ran." Her grip tightened. "Someone ran, and hid, and the stones went dry, and the Hollow came through the gap like water through a cracked dam. And it knows, hedge-man. It knows the scent of the line that fed it. It knows the taste. It has been licking the walls of its prison for four centuries, learning the flavor of the hand that locked the door."

"I don't know what you think I am," Marguerite said, and his voice came out almost steady, which he counted as the finest work of his life.

"I think you're the one the dark follows." She let go of his wrist and reached instead for the cord at her own neck, and lifted from it a ring on a leather thong: iron, heavy, old, stamped with a signet whose design Marguerite recognized before his mind consented to recognize it — a circle broken by a single vertical line, the mark that had been branded into the beam above his master's cottage door, the mark he had been taught to draw in the dirt at nine years old and told, *this means nothing, this means stay away, this means never.* "Found it on a dead man at the crossing. He was warden-born, or dressed like it. He was smiling when he died, which I'm told is what the Hollow leaves behind. I've been carrying it three days and every mile it's gotten heavier, and tonight, on the road, it started pulling south. Toward the city. Toward—" Her breath caught. Her hand, offering the ring, was shaking now. "Toward you. It's been pulling toward you since I entered the city, and I followed it down here into the dark, and here you are, smelling of hedge-craft and old blood, and I am going to die in about ten minutes, so I would take it very kindly if you'd stop lying to both of us."

The vault was silent except for the distant roar of the burning city and the small wet sound of her breathing.

Marguerite took the ring.

It was colder than iron had any right to be, and heavier than its size, and the moment his skin touched it the blackened sigil in his pouch flared once — a pulse of heat like a heartbeat — and went still. Settled. As if something had been restless for a long time and had finally, finally arrived where it was meant to be.

"There," the woman whispered. "Oh, thank the dark and the light both. *There.*" She sagged back against the barrels, and some of the terrible clarity left her eyes, and what replaced it was only exhaustion, human and ordinary. "Ishikawa," she said. "You're an Ishikawa. I thought so. The ring knows. The ring's been straining toward its own blood for three days like a hound on a leash." She closed her eyes. "My name was Lark. Was. I had a daughter. I can't remember her face either, now. It took her out of me on the road, as a message, I think. As a courtesy. To show me what it does." Her mouth twisted. "It wanted me to deliver the ring and the message together. *The Hollow knows your face,* it made me say, over and over, the whole ride, so I wouldn't forget the words even as it ate everything else. So. Delivered. The Hollow knows your face, Ishikawa. It has always known your face. It has been waiting for you the way the ground waits for rain."

"Lark—" Marguerite started forward, needle and thread forgotten, but she raised one hand, just slightly, and he stopped.

"Don't waste the craft on me. It's in the blood already. Your thread would just give it a map." She was quiet a moment. When she spoke again her voice had gentled into something almost amused. "Funny. Three days ago I'd never heard of you. Now I've died carrying your family's mail." Her breath rattled. "Do better with it than they did. Whoever they were. Whoever *you* are."

And then, between one breath and the next, she was gone — not dramatically, not with any last words worth the having, just the small mechanical failure of a body that had ridden too far past its limits, leaving Marguerite alone in a coal-vault under a burning city with a corpse, a ring, and a sentence repeating itself in the dark of his skull in a voice that might have been a dead courier's, or a dead master's, or something older than both:

*The Hollow knows your face.*

He sat with her a while, because no one should die alone in a coal-vault, and because his legs would not yet obey him. Outside, the bells had begun again — all of them now, every tower in Ashfall, ringing anything but sevens, ringing panic in whatever numbers frightened hands could find. Somewhere above, the witch-finders were searching house by house. Somewhere beyond the borders, the Hollow was walking through a world with the doors flung wide.

And in his closed fist, cold as a starless sky, the iron ring waited — patient, heavy, and unmistakably awake — for him to decide what, exactly, he had inherited.

When he finally rose, pocketing the ring because there was nowhere else in the world to put it, Marguerite noticed two things. First: that the blackened sigil in his pouch had begun, very softly, to warm again — not burning now, but *pulling*, the way Lark had said the ring had pulled, drawing him not toward the border roads but deeper into the city, down, toward the flooded undercity where the old cisterns lay drowned beneath Ashfall's foundations.

Second: that he was no longer alone in the dark.

At the mouth of the passage, where the lamplight failed, something stood in the wall itself — a figure grown out of the stone like a reflection grown out of glass, translucent, faintly luminous, watching him with an expression he could not read and a patience that frightened him more than the fires above.

"Well," said the stranger, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. "You took your time, heir."
