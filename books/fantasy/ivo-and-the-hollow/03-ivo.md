## 3. Ivo

The undercity had flooded three days after the stars went out, and no one could say why.

Marguerite waded through it anyway, because the streets above were worse. The Wardens' patrols moved in files of six with lanterns hooded in iron, and they had learned, with the terrible speed of institutions, to look for people who flinched when the bells rang. Everyone flinched now. But the witch-finders had a way of watching that made flinching a confession, and Marguerite had spent his whole life learning how not to be seen. It was the one inheritance his master had left him that he still understood.

So he went down, where Ashfall kept its drowned bones.

The water came to his thighs and then, in the older tunnels, to his chest, black and warm as bathwater gone wrong. It smelled of tallow and rust and something sweeter underneath — something like the air inside a sealed room. His charms hung against his sternum under his shirt, and he could feel them the way you feel a bruise: not pain exactly, but a knowledge of damage. Since the night of thirteen bells they had been burning slowly black, one knot at a time, like candles counting down something he refused to name.

He had tried to leave the city. That was the truth of it, though he'd have cut out his tongue before admitting it to anyone. He had gotten as far as the Saltgate on the second morning, stood in the press of refugees with his forged papers sweating in his hand, and looked out at the road — the long gray road south, away from everything — and his feet had simply stopped. Not fear. Something older than fear. The same feeling he'd had at eleven years old, standing in his master's cottage doorway while the old man said *never let them find you* in a voice Marguerite had spent fifteen years pretending not to remember.

The ring was in his pocket. He didn't know why he'd kept it. The dying woman in the refugee crush had pressed it into his palm with fingers like cold wax, and she had said *the Hollow knows your face*, and every sane instinct told him to drop it down a well. Instead it rode against his thigh through the flooded dark, heavier than iron had any right to be, and sometimes — only sometimes — he thought he felt it turn, very slightly, like a sleeping animal adjusting itself toward warmth.

The tunnel he was in now had once been a processional way; he could tell by the niches carved into the walls, empty of their saints, and by the arches that rose out of the water in ribbed black stone. Someone's lantern-light guttered far behind him. He told himself it was other refugees. He told himself a great many things.

Then the light stopped being behind him.

It came from ahead — two points of white, hooded, swinging low over the water, and beneath them the sound of boots finding submerged stone with the unhurried confidence of men who knew these tunnels better than any refugee should. Witch-finders did not get lost. Witch-finders walked into the drowned places of the world the way tax collectors walked into poor houses: because that was where the money was.

Marguerite went still, which was the first law of his craft — stillness before silence, silence before shadow — and slid sideways into the gap between two arches, pressing his back to wet stone. His heart was doing something humiliating. He closed his hand around the pouch of charms and felt them pulse against his palm, faintly warm, faintly *aware*, like small animals that knew the hawk was overhead.

The lanterns grew. He could hear voices now, low and clipped.

"—says the wards were warden-made. All of them. Four hundred years—"

"Then there'll be a warden. There's always a last one."

"Voss wants him breathing."

"Voss wants a lot of things."

The names meant nothing and everything. Marguerite had never heard of this Voss, but he understood the shape of the sentence: *there's always a last one.* Somewhere above, in a dry office smelling of ink and lamp oil, someone had written his name — or the shape of his name, the family-shaped hole where his name should be — into a ledger, and men with hooded lanterns were walking into floodwater to fill it in.

He began to move. Slowly, along the wall, into the deeper black between the arches, one hand trailing the stone. The water helped and betrayed him — it muffled his steps and carried every ripple straight to the lanterns. Ten feet. Twenty. The niche ahead was deep enough to stand in if the water weren't chest-high, deep enough to—

The light swung.

Not toward him. Toward the water beside him, where the ripples of his passing had reached some instrument or instinct, and one of the witch-finders said, quietly, the way you'd say it about weather:

"There."

Everything happened fast after that, and it all happened badly.

Marguerite ran. Chest-deep water is not a medium built for running; it is a medium built for drowning, and he churned through it with his lungs full of fire while the lanterns bloomed behind him and boots came on in that terrible unhurried rhythm, because they did not need to hurry, because the tunnel ended ahead — he remembered that much of Ashfall's drowned geography, everyone did — the processional way terminated in the old sluice-gate, rusted shut for sixty years, a wall of black iron rising out of the water with nowhere past it but brick.

He hit the gate. The iron was slick and gave him nothing. Behind him the lanterns fanned out, three points of light now, maybe four, and a voice said — almost gently, which was the worst part — "Nowhere to go, friend. Hands where we can see them."

Marguerite turned. The water lapped at his ribs. He put his back against the gate and faced them, four shapes in oilskin with their hoods back and their faces ordinary and tired, and the nearest one held out an open palm, patient as a priest.

"The ring," the man said. "We know you have it. Give us the ring and the name, and this stays quiet."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," the man agreed, without malice. "But you will." He took a step closer, and Marguerite saw the thing hanging at his belt — not a weapon but a pair of iron shears, long-nosed, ceremonial, the kind used for cutting hair from the dead. "Or we do this the loud way, and the loud way hurts, and I'd genuinely rather it didn't. I've got a daughter."

Everyone always had a daughter. Marguerite's hand drifted toward his pocket — toward the ring, because what else was there — and the moment his fingers touched the iron, the world changed temperature.

It was not cold. Cold would have been a mercy. It was a *removal* of warmth, a subtraction, as if the tunnel had been holding its breath around a candle all this time and finally exhaled. The lantern flames shrank to blue beads. The water went glass-still, so still that Marguerite could see his own face reflected up at him, pale and stretched, and behind his reflection — in the reflection, in the water, in the space where reflections keep their secrets — something else looked back. A darkness with an appetite. A hunger shaped like attention.

Every charm in his pouch burned black at once.

And out of the wall — out of the solid, wet, ancient brick beside the sluice-gate — a man stepped.

There was no door. There was no crack. The stone simply agreed to contain him for a moment and then thought better of it, and he came through it the way a memory comes through sleep: already mid-stride, already arrived, his boots finding the submerged sill as though he'd been walking this tunnel for centuries and had merely stepped out for a moment to let it pass. He was tall and spare, dressed in clothes that belonged to no fashion Marguerite knew — a long coat the color of wet slate, high-collared, buttoned to the throat — and he was *translucent*. Not faintly, not at the edges: entirely, horribly, beautifully translucent, so that the brick behind him showed through his shoulders like a face behind frosted glass, and the lantern-light passed through his chest and came out the other side unbroken.

The witch-finders stopped.

One of them said a word — a small word, a child's word, the kind of word grown men pretend they've forgotten — and raised his lantern with a shaking hand, and the light went *through* the stranger and lit the wall behind him and nothing else.

The stranger looked at the four of them with an expression of mild, almost courteous regret.

"You should go," he said. His voice was quiet and dry and old, old in the way that books are old, and it carried down the flooded tunnel without echo, as though the water itself were listening. "This part of the dark belongs to me tonight."

What happened next, Marguerite would spend a long time failing to describe. The lanterns went out — not blown, *unlit*, the flame simply deciding it had never been lit — and in the sudden total dark there was a sound like a great door closing somewhere very far below the world, and the water shivered, and the witch-finders screamed, all four at once, not in pain but in the particular register of men who have seen the inside of their own skulls. Then the light came back — ordinary, yellow, trembling — and the four of them were stumbling backward through the flood, dropping their instruments, their shears, their dignity, and the last one looked at Marguerite with an expression of pure animal apology before turning and thrashing away into the dark.

Silence. Water dripping somewhere. The blue bead of Marguerite's own courage trying to relight itself.

The stranger turned to him.

Up close, the translucency resolved into detail rather than absence: Marguerite could see the texture of his coat, the lines around his eyes, a scar splitting one eyebrow, all of it rendered in the material of a man seen through shallow water. His eyes were gray. Not the gray of storm or steel but the gray of ash, of things that had been hot once and were patient about it.

"You're bleeding," the stranger said.

Marguerite looked down. His palms were scored where he'd clawed at the sluice-gate, thin lines of red threading out into the black water like smoke. "They cornered me," he said, stupidly, because his mouth was running on its own. "I didn't— I haven't done anything."

"No," the stranger agreed. "You've done nothing. That's rather the problem." He extended a hand — and Marguerite watched, with the detached clarity of shock, as the offered hand *did not displace the water*, the flood passing through the wrist and fingers as though they were moonlight — and said, in that dry, patient, terrible voice:

"Heir."

The word landed in Marguerite's chest like a coin dropped down a well. He heard it fall for a long time.

"I'm not—" He stopped. Started again. "Who are you?"

"Ivo," the stranger said. And then, as if remembering that names required surnames, as if consulting some etiquette manual from a country that no longer existed: "Ivo. Of the wardens."

"There are no wardens. There haven't been wardens for—"

"For four hundred years, yes. And yet." Ivo smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked and almost kind, and it did not reach his eyes, which stayed fixed on Marguerite with an attention that felt less like looking and more like *counting*. "You have the ring."

"It's not mine."

"No. It's hers. Your grandmother's. Which makes it yours, in the way that debts are inherited." He tilted his head, and the lantern-light moved through his skull like light through a jar of smoke. "You kept it. You could have dropped it in any of ten thousand wells between here and the Saltgate, and instead you carried it against your skin for three days. Do you know why?"

"Because I'm an idiot."

"Because it's *warm*," Ivo said softly. "Isn't it? Warmer than it should be. Like a hand that's recently let go." He watched Marguerite's face do whatever it did. "Iron holds what it's given. She gave it a great deal."

"You knew her." It came out of Marguerite before he could weigh it, raw and childish, and he hated himself for it. "My grandmother. You knew her."

"I knew her," Ivo said, "very well."

Something in the way he said it — a half-beat too smooth, a sentence worn smooth like a stone handled too often — snagged in Marguerite's mind and did not let go. Later he would return to that half-beat again and again, worrying it the way you worry a loose tooth. But later was later. In the flooded dark, with his charms dead in their pouch and witch-finders regrouping somewhere above and the water carrying the taste of that watching darkness, he only said:

"What's a warden-ghost doing in a wall?"

"Waiting," Ivo said.

"For what?"

"For you to stop asking questions in waist-deep water and come somewhere dry." He gestured — courteously, ironically — at the sluice-gate, at the rusted iron that had trapped Marguerite five minutes ago. "Unless you'd prefer to stay. They'll be back. Men with daughters always come back; it's their daughters that make them brave."

"How do I know you're not worse than they are?"

"You don't," Ivo said pleasantly. "That's the entire nature of trust. I can tell you that I have been bound to that ring since before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. I can tell you that I cannot lie to you — " and here the smile flickered, just barely, like a lantern in wind, "— more than certain old bindings allow. And I can tell you that the thing those men are hunting, and the thing the Crown is afraid of, and the thing that ate your stars, are all the same thing, and it knows your face, heir, and it is *patient*, and I am the only creature in Ashfall City who both knows that and is not currently trying to sell you to someone."

The water lapped. Far off, muffled by stone and flood, the bells of Ashfall began to toll the hour — and stopped at twelve, correct, ordinary, and somehow that was worse than thirteen.

Marguerite looked at the ghost. At the wall he'd come out of. At his own dead charms floating in their pouch like burnt moths.

"If I come with you," he said slowly, "you'll tell me what I am. What the ring is. What my grandmother fed— what the wardens *were*."

"I'll tell you what you need to survive the week," Ivo said. "The rest, when you're ready. Some truths are load-bearing, heir. Pull them too early and the whole house comes down on your head."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I'm permitted." And there — there it was again, that half-beat, that worn-smooth place in his voice, and this time Marguerite caught it clearly: Ivo had answered a question Marguerite hadn't asked. *Permitted.* Not *willing*. Permitted. As though the binding that held him to the ring also held his tongue, and as though there were things behind his teeth he'd chosen, carefully, to route around.

The ghost extended his incorporeal hand toward the sluice-gate, and where his fingers passed across the rusted iron, the rust *shivered* — a fine red dust falling away into the water, the mechanism groaning once, deep in its sixty-year sleep, and beginning, wheel by seized wheel, to turn.

"Come," Ivo said. "The dry ways are this way. And heir — walk close. The Hollow has tasted the water tonight, and it remembers which direction you ran."

Marguerite should have run the other way. Every story he'd ever sold in charm-form, every luck-knot and fever-writ, every piece of folk wisdom his master had beaten into him, said the same thing: *when the dead offer you a door, count the hinges.*

Instead he pushed off from the gate, waded to the wall where the ghost stood, and followed the sound of footsteps that made no ripples, down into the dark, telling himself — the way he'd been telling himself things since the stars went out — that he had no choice.

Behind them, in the black water, the reflection that was not his reflection lingered a moment longer, attentive, patient, memorizing.

* * *

The dry ways turned out to be real: maintenance galleries above the flood line, built by some municipal ambition of a century ago and abandoned since, running through the bones of Ashfall like veins through a leaf. Ivo led him up a rusted ladder and along a walkway barely wide enough for one, and Marguerite noticed — filed away without understanding — that the ghost never touched anything. Not the rail, not the walls, not the ladder rungs. Except the gate. Except the rust that had fallen at his command.

He was too tired to ask. His body had begun presenting its bill: the shaking in his hands, the ache behind his eyes, the deep animal demand of a man who had not properly slept since the stars died. When Ivo finally stopped in a junction chamber where an old cistern grate let down a thread of true night air, Marguerite sat down against the wall and did not intend to close his eyes, and did.

He woke once, in the black hours, to a sound like cloth settling.

Ivo was sitting across the chamber, knees drawn up, watching him. Not counting, this time. Just watching, with an expression Marguerite had no name for — something older than sorrow and quieter than guilt — and as Marguerite's eyes opened, the ghost said, softly, to no one, in a language Marguerite did not know but his blood apparently did:

"*Dormi bene, piccolo erede.* Sleep well, little heir."

The ring, in Marguerite's pocket, was warm as a held hand.

And somewhere far above, through stone and flood and the sleeping weight of Ashfall City, the first gray suggestion of dawn found nothing in the sky to catch on — no stars, no sun worth the name, only a flat colorless light lying on the city like a sheet over a face — and in the drowned dark below, the thing that had watched them from the water turned its attention, slow and satisfied, toward the surface, following the scent of a name it had nearly tasted, and waited for night to give it another chance.
