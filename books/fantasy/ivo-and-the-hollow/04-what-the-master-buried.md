## 4. What the Master Buried

The road north out of Ashfall was a road of mud and rumor.

Marguerite walked it in the grey hour before dawn, when the fog came off the low fields like something poured rather than risen, and the crows sat hunched on the fence posts with their feathers puffed against the cold, watching him pass with one eye and then the other, as if taking turns at the work of remembering him. He had not planned to come this way. He had told himself he would go east, toward the coast, where a man with no name could lose himself among the fish-smoke and the salt-crusted hulls. But his feet had argued with him somewhere past the third milestone, and feet, he had learned, knew things the mouth would not admit.

Three days since the stars went out. Three days since the bells tolled thirteen and his charms burned black in their pouches like moths caught in a closed fist. The city behind him was still burning in its own quieter way — riots, curfews, the witch-finders' lanterns swinging through the alleys — and every hour he stayed felt like standing in a field during a lightning storm holding the tallest thing in it. Which, he suspected now, he was.

He did not know why yet. He only knew that the dying woman's ring weighed against his chest on its cord like a small cold heart, and that when he touched it, it seemed to touch back.

*The Hollow knows your face.*

He had turned those words over so many times they had worn smooth as river stones, and still they made no sense. He was Marguerite Ishikawa, charm-seller, luck-knot tier, rent-payer of dubious honesty, a man whose greatest crime before this week had been selling a love-charm to a woman whose husband was already inclined to love her. Whatever the Hollow was — whatever had eaten the stars — it should have had no more interest in him than the tide has in a single grain of sand.

And yet.

And yet there was the cottage.

He had not thought of it in years — or rather, he had trained himself not to, the way you train yourself not to press a bruise. It stood two miles off the north road, up a lane gone soft with moss, in a fold of hills where the fog pooled all year round like something that had drowned there and never been buried. His master's cottage. The place where an old man named Aldous Ren had taught a boy of seven how to tie a knot that held luck, and a boy of nine how to hear the difference between rain and something *walking under* the rain.

The place where, at eleven years old, Marguerite had woken one morning to find the cottage empty and a note on the table that said only: *Go south. Don't look back. Don't let them find you. I am sorry for everything, and especially for what I am not sorry for.*

He had hated that note for fifteen years. He had carried it folded in eight squares inside his shirt through every city and every bad winter, until the ink faded and the paper went soft as cloth, and he could recite it better than any prayer. *I am sorry for everything, and especially for what I am not sorry for.* What kind of sentence was that? What kind of man wrote a riddle and called it goodbye?

The lane was worse than he remembered, or he was worse than he remembered, and by the time the cottage came into view — a low stone shape with a sagging thatch, half-swallowed by ivy gone black with the season — his boots were soaked through and his breath hung around him in ragged flags.

It looked smaller. Everything did, when you came back to it carrying a body that had grown around a wound.

But the door was still blue.

That undid him, a little. His master had painted the door blue — a particular blue, mixed with crushed lapis and something else, something the boy Marguerite had never been allowed to know — because, Aldous said, *blue is the color of a threshold that knows itself.* He had said a great many things like that, in that patient dry voice, things that sounded like nonsense until suddenly, years later, they didn't.

Marguerite stood in the overgrown yard with the fog beading in his hair and looked at the blue door, and found that his hands were shaking.

"Nobody's here," he said aloud, to the crows, to the fog, to himself. "It's just a house."

The house did not answer. Houses never do. But the ring against his sternum went abruptly, unmistakably cold — colder than the morning had any right to make it — and stayed cold, the way iron stays cold near a winter river, and Marguerite understood that something in the world remembered this place even if the world had forgotten everything else.

He pushed the door open.

* * *

Inside, the dust lay thick as first snow, unbroken except where mice had stitched their small errands across the floorboards. The single room was much as he'd left it: the great hearth with its iron crane, the workbench under the window with its scattered grooves where knots had been cut and burned, the narrow bed with its blanket still folded in the military corners Aldous Ren had insisted on, though the man had been neither soldier nor particularly tidy in any other respect.

Fifteen years of quiet. You could taste it. It tasted like cold ash and dried herbs and the particular sweetness of beeswax long unmelted.

Marguerite walked the room slowly, touching things. The workbench. The wall where his height had been marked in charcoal each year on his birthday — *M., age 8; M., age 9; M., age 10* — the marks still faintly visible under the grime, a ladder of small ghosts climbing toward a birthday that had never been recorded. On the eleventh notch, someone had stopped.

His throat tightened. He made himself keep moving.

It was the hearth that stopped him instead. Because the hearth was wrong.

Every other surface wore its fifteen years openly. But the hearthstones around the firebox — the three flat stones set into the floor before the ashes — were clean. Not new-clean. *Kept*-clean. Swept, recently, by hands that took care how they swept. And when Marguerite crouched and ran his fingers along the edge of the leftmost stone, he felt it: a thin seam, almost invisible, filled with wax the color of the surrounding grit.

Aldous Ren had taught him about wax-sealed stones when he was ten. *Anything worth hiding,* the old man had said, tapping the boy's sternum with one dry finger, *is worth hiding from yourself. Memory is a door that swings both ways, boy. Lock it from the outside too.*

Marguerite fetched the poker. He hesitated — some old obedience rising in him, *don't touch, that's your master's* — and then he thought of thirteen bells, and blackened charms, and a dying woman's hand pressing iron into his palm, and he brought the poker down on the wax seal hard enough to crack the stone beneath.

The flagstone came up easier than it should have. As if it wanted to.

Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a book.

Not a large book. A ledger, really — the kind merchants use, with a marbled cover and a brass clasp green with age. But when Marguerite unwrapped it, kneeling in the cold hearth with the fog-light falling grey through the window, he saw his master's handwriting on the first page, and the handwriting was not the quick scrawl of recipe-notes and knot-patterns. It was careful. Deliberate. The writing of a man who knew he was writing for someone who would come after him, and who was afraid of being misunderstood.

*For M., if he ever comes home. If he is reading this, then either the wards have fallen, or he is about to do something unforgivably stupid, and knowing him it will be both on the same day.*

Marguerite laughed. It came out of him broken, half sob, and echoed strangely in the empty room, and he had to sit down on the cold hearthstone with the book in his lap before he could go on.

* * *

He read until the light changed and the fog thickened and he had to light a candle stub from the store on the mantel, and what he read remade the shape of his whole life the way a flood remakes a valley: not destroying it, exactly, but revealing that the gentle slopes he'd walked had always been the walls of something vast and terrible.

*Ishikawa,* Aldous had written. *The name is not a family. It is a function. Four hundred years ago, when the border wards were first raised against the Hollow, the wardens who built them faced a problem every warden since has inherited: wards are not walls. Walls stand on their own. Wards are promises, and promises must be kept by someone. A ward unkept is worse than no ward at all — it is an open door with a lock painted on it.*

*So the first wardens bound the keeping of the wards to their own bloodline. Each generation, one Ishikawa serves as the anchor — the living keystone. Their life feeds the wards. Not metaphorically. Not symbolically. Their years, their strength, their memory, their self, poured out year by year into the border-stones until there is nothing left to pour, and then the next heir takes up the burden, and the next, and the next. Four hundred years. Forty-one anchors. I have the list. I have watched three of them die, and I have written down what it looks like, and I will tell you what it looks like: it looks like a person becoming a place. Like a face becoming a door. In the last years they stop answering to their names, because the wards have taken the names. They smile at nothing. They are not unhappy. That is the worst part. There is nothing left in them to be unhappy with.*

Marguerite read that passage three times. The candle guttered. Outside, a crow laughed, or the wind did something crow-shaped, and he flinched.

*You will ask — you are already asking, I can feel you asking across the years — why anyone would agree to this. And the answer is that mostly they don't. Mostly they are children when it is decided, the way you were decided upon, the way your mother was, and her mother, back to the first of us who traded a bloodline for a border. The wards must be fed by consent — true consent, freely given, or they sour and the Hollow finds the cracks. So we do not force our heirs. We raise them in the craft, and we teach them the stakes, and when they come of age we ask. And in four hundred years, forty-one times out of forty-one, they have said yes. Because the alternative is the Hollow, and the Hollow is worse. Remember that, M., when you hate me. The alternative is worse.*

*Except.*

*Except that last spring, your grandmother — the current anchor, my dearest friend, the bravest woman I have ever known — began to fail early. The wards have grown hungry as the Hollow presses harder against them; each generation burns faster than the last. She had perhaps two years. And she came to me, and she said: Aldous. My grandson is six years old. When he is grown, someone will put the question to him, and he will say yes, because everyone says yes, because saying yes is all he will have been raised to know how to say. And I cannot bear it. Break the chain. Steal him. Make him small and nameless and free, and damn the cost to me.*

*I told her the cost. I told her the wards would weaken within a generation without an anchor. I told her the stars themselves might gutter. She said: then let them gutter over a world where my grandson got to choose his own life. Let them gutter over a world with one free Ishikawa in it.*

*So I stole you. There is no gentler word. I stole you, and I hid you in plain sight, teaching you charms small enough to pass as hedge-craft, and I taught you to hide, and I broke the chain, and I have spent fifteen years waiting for the bill to come due. It has come due. If you are reading this, the stars are already going out, and your grandmother's gamble is spending itself, and every mile of freedom you ever walked was bought with her slow dissolution into the border-stones.*

*I am sorry for everything, and especially for what I am not sorry for. I am not sorry for giving you fifteen years. I would do it again. Hate me if it helps. But before you decide anything — before you run to the Crown, or to the rebels, or into the dark itself — remember: everyone who comes for you will want to put you in the fire. The Crown will call it duty. The rebels will call it liberation. Even kindness will call it sacrifice. Everyone will have a beautiful word for the furnace, M. Your job is to notice that it is still a furnace.*

*— A.R.*

*P.S. The ring you are wearing — yes, I know you have it, I know how these things travel — belonged to your grandmother. It was hers when she was a girl, before the wards took her. If it has found its way back to you, then someone died getting it to you, and I am sorry for that too, and I hope whoever it was made them pay for it.*

Marguerite did not remember standing up. He was standing, though, and the candle was somewhere behind him, and the journal was on the floor at his feet, and he was looking at the charcoal height-marks on the wall — *M., age 10* — and the sound coming out of him was not crying, exactly. It was older than crying. It was the sound a house makes when its foundations shift.

His whole life. Every cold garret, every hungry winter, every market square where he'd kept his hood up and his voice down and his craft hidden — he had thought he was running from the witch-finders because of what he *was*, a hedge-witch, an outlaw of small magics. He had thought the fear was his own, earned fair, like scars. But it wasn't. It was installed. He was a runaway the way a boat is a runaway — set loose on purpose, pointed downstream, told *don't look back* by the very hand that pushed him off the bank.

And his grandmother. He had no memory of her. That was the cruelest stitch in all of it — Aldous had scrubbed her from his childhood so thoroughly that he couldn't even grieve her properly. He was grieving a stranger who had dissolved herself into a border so that he could sell luck-knots to dockworkers and complain about rent. A woman had become a wall so that he could be a man, and he had never even learned her face.

"I didn't ask," he said to the empty room. His voice cracked on the second word. "None of you asked. You just — you *decided*—"

But even as he said it, he heard the lie in his own fury, heard Aldous's dry voice across fifteen years: *We asked her, M. Forty-one times out of forty-one, they say yes. Your grandmother was the first to wonder whether love that always says yes is love at all, or just training with good manners.*

He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw stars — false stars, blood-stars, the only kind left — and when he lowered his hands, the room was darker, and the candle had burned down to a thumb's width, and the journal lay open on the floor to the last written page, which he had not yet read.

He picked it up.

*If the wards have fallen,* Aldous had written, in ink that looked hurried now, less certain, *then you will have perhaps weeks before the Hollow reaches the cities. Less, if it has learned hunger. Go to the old warden seat at Greywatch — you'll find the way in my maps, third drawer, under the false bottom, because of course under the false bottom, you're my student. There is a rite there. There may be another answer there. I spent fifteen years looking for one and found only variations of the furnace, but I was old and alone and afraid, and you — you were raised to see knots, M. To see how things bind. Perhaps you'll see what I couldn't.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you hate me in peace. The Hollow does not only eat. It offers. It will find the shape of what you miss most and wear it like a coat. When that happens — and it will happen — ask yourself one question: does what I'm longing for want me to open the door? Or does only the wanting?*

*Grief is honest, boy. Longing lies. Learn the difference before it learns you.*

The candle died.

In the sudden dark, Marguerite knelt in the cold hearth of his stolen childhood, holding a dead man's handwriting, and felt the signet ring go cold against his chest — colder than before, cold enough to burn, cold as a hand reaching up from under ice.

And beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, beneath the roots of the hills themselves, he heard — for the first time clearly, unmistakably, with ears Aldous had trained for this exact moment without telling him why — something vast turning over in its sleep far below the world, and beginning, slowly, to move toward the surface.

Toward the cities.

Toward the light of the one lamp still lit anywhere on the darkening road behind him — a lantern swinging in a disciplined rhythm, accompanied by the jingle of harness and the murmur of many voices, coming up the north road toward the cottage.

Witch-finders moved like that. Marguerite had spent fifteen years learning to know their sound the way farmers know weather.

He closed the journal, tucked it inside his coat against his heart beside the ring, and slipped out the blue door into the fog — leaving the candle smoke still hanging in the empty room like a signature, like evidence, like a message to whoever came next:

*Someone was here. Someone read the truth. Find him.*
