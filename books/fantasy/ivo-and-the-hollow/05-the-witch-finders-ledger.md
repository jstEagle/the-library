## 5. The Witch-Finders' Ledger

The report arrived at the fourth hour of the morning, which was the hour Serane Voss had long ago claimed for herself — the hour before the city woke, before petitioners and superiors and the small bureaucratic knives of the Ministry of Purity found her. In that hour she read. Everything. Reports came to her in three colors of ribbon: white for routine, grey for irregular, black for the kind of thing that ended careers or cities. This one wore black.

She stood at the window of her office on the sixth floor of the Bastion while she read it, because she thought better standing, and because the window looked east over Ashfall's rooftops toward the harbor, and because she wanted to see the city while she read about its death.

*Report of the Survey Commission on the Northern Wards,* it began, in the cramped hand of a junior archivist who would never know how important his neatness was. *Following the collapse of the border wards on the night of the Thirteenth Bell, the Commission has completed preliminary examination of the ward-stones at four sites: Greywater Ford, the Toll of Cinders, Marrow Bridge, and the old warden seat at Hollowmere.*

Voss had been to Greywater Ford herself, two days after the collapse. She had seen what remained of the ward-stone there: a pillar of grey granite taller than two men, carved with script so old the Crown's own scholars had argued for a century about whether it was language or merely decoration. It had stood for four hundred years. On the night the stars went out, it had come apart like a loaf torn by hungry hands — not shattered, *unwoven*, each line of the carving pulled loose from its neighbors and left lying in the grass like threads off a cut hem.

*Analysis of the residue confirms what the archives have long suggested but could not prove,* the archivist wrote. *The wards were not Crown works. They were not made by any order, guild, or college now extant. The binding-script is consistent throughout all four sites with the tradition known in fragmentary records as warden-craft.*

There it was. The word the Ministry had spent two hundred years trying to bury.

*Further: the script at all four sites bears marks of periodic renewal. The renewals were made by human hands, at intervals of approximately one generation, over the full span of the wards' existence. Blood was used as the medium. The blood type is consistent across all samples from all centuries examined.*

Consistent. Which meant one family. One line. Four hundred years of one family walking out to the edge of the world and opening their veins so that everyone else could sleep.

And now the line was broken, and the stars were gone, and something on the other side of where the wards used to be was leaning against the door.

Voss set the report down on the windowsill and looked at the city.

Ashfall in the fourth hour was beautiful the way a tired animal is beautiful — you could see the effort under everything. Smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys in straight grey columns because there was no wind this morning, no weather worth the name since the Thirteenth Bell. The harbor flat as poured pewter. Gulls hanging motionless in the air like things nailed up. And above it all, where the sky should have been doing its slow bright work of turning from black to iron to gold, nothing. A ceiling. The sun still rose — she'd checked, obsessively, the first week, half-convinced the astronomers were wrong — but it rose dim, filtered, like light through dirty glass, and the stars had not come back at all.

People adapted. That was the thing about people that both moved and appalled her. Within six days the fishwives had invented new words for the dim hours. Within nine, the temples were selling candles blessed against "the Long Dark" at three coppers apiece, and within twelve the first child had been born who would never see a star, and nobody marked the occasion, because marking it would have required admitting what it meant.

But Voss admitted things. That was her job. Someone in this city had to look directly at what was coming, and everyone else seemed determined to look anywhere else.

Because here was what the pretty adaptation hid: the refugees. Eleven thousand of them now, camped on the mudflats south of the river walls, and every week more, coming down out of the hill country in carts and on foot, carrying what they could and stories they couldn't put down. Stories about villages where everyone was alive and smiling and did not know their own children's names. About roads that ended in fog that went on for days. About a darkness that didn't take your life but took your *name* — that reached into you and lifted out the part that made you a particular person and left the rest walking around, breathing, cheerful, hollow.

The Ministry called it panic. The temples called it judgment. The papers called it weather.

Voss called it an advancing front, and she had drawn the map herself, in red ink, pinned to the wall behind her desk: the boundary of confirmed erasures, moving inward from the borders at a rate she could measure, slow but steady, like water finding the low places. At current rate of advance, the red line touched the outer farms of Ashfall Province in fourteen months. The city itself in twenty-two.

Unless the wards were rebuilt.

She picked the report up again and turned to the second enclosure, the one that had actually brought her to the window. This one was shorter, and in a different hand — Captain Aldery's, from the northern circuit, a careful man who wrote like he was afraid of being quoted.

*Addendum. At Hollowmere, the Commission located the cottage of the last registered warden, Edran Ishikawa (deceased approx. 15 years; see file 441-C). The cottage shows recent occupation. Candle wax, fresh. A fire laid and lit within the past fortnight. One volume removed from a concealed cache beneath the hearthstone — the cache itself was expertly hidden and would not have been found without the Commission's survey instruments. Whoever took the volume knew it was there.*

*Conclusion: a survivor of the Ishikawa line has returned to Hollowmere, learned of the cache, and taken its contents. Given the timing — days after the collapse of the wards — it is reasonable to assume the survivor came seeking answers about the wards' failure, which implies prior knowledge of warden-craft, which implies training.*

*Recommendation: immediate tracing of the Ishikawa survivor. If the wards are to be rekindled, we will need the blood.*

*We will need the blood.* Aldery hadn't meant anything by it. He was a technician; he wrote what the work required. But Voss read the sentence twice anyway, and each time it landed in her chest with a cold weight she recognized and refused to flinch from.

She had joined the witch-finders at nineteen, and people assumed — people always assumed — that she'd joined out of hatred. Hatred of magic, hatred of the unlicensed, hatred of the crooked-backed cunning-women and hedge-charmers her officers dragged in weekly. Her own officers sometimes watched her for signs of it, the way soldiers watch a commander for madness. Let them watch. The truth was duller and worse: she felt about as much hatred for a hedge-witch as she felt for a storm. What she felt was arithmetic. Magic unregulated had, in living memory, burned a granary district, poisoned a well-field, and raised a dead man who walked for eleven days asking for his wife until they put him down. The wards had held the worst of it back for four hundred years, and the price of the wards had been paid by someone else, out of sight, and the Crown had signed that arrangement with wax and seals and then pretended it had never happened, because pretending was cheaper than gratitude.

Now the arrangement was void. Now the arithmetic had changed. Now there was exactly one person alive who might be able to rebuild the wall between the city and the dark, and that person was running, and every day he ran was a day the red line crept closer to eleven thousand refugees and four hundred thousand citizens who trusted her to be competent.

Find him. Not to punish him. She'd already drafted the pardon in her head — full clemency, retroactive license, whatever the lawyers needed to dress it in. Find him, sit him down across a table, show him the map with the red line on it, and say: *you are the only door, and the flood is coming.* Anyone with eyes would understand. Anyone with a conscience would help.

She almost believed that. It was one of her few remaining luxuries, believing it.

* * *

At the seventh hour she convened her senior staff in the map room, a vaulted chamber on the fifth floor where the Bastion's oldest records were kept and where, in defiance of two separate Ministerial edicts, a single working charm still hung above the door — a lamp-glass filled with everburning light, installed by some long-dead commander and never removed because removing it would have required admitting it was there. Voss liked the room for that reason. Hypocrisy, honestly maintained, was sturdier than most virtues.

"Read your addenda," she said, when they'd gathered — six captains, the Ministry liaison Pellam, and Doctor Ivenne from the College, who was technically attached to the survey and practically attached to nothing but her instruments. "Then we'll talk about how we find one person in a city of four hundred thousand."

They read. She watched them read, the way she always did, cataloguing faces. Pellam's went pale and then carefully blank — the Ministry man had understood before anyone that *warden-made* meant the Crown's whole official history of the wards was a lie, and lies of that size, once exposed, tended to fall on the people standing nearest. Ivenne's face did something rarer: it brightened. The doctor had spent her career on the chemistry of old blood-samples and had been told twice, politely, that her field was distasteful. Now her field was the fate of the world.

Captain Aldery cleared his throat. "The trail from Hollowmere leads south," he said. "Roads converge on Ashfall. If the survivor followed the main route—"

"He did." Every head turned. Voss tapped the map on the great table, the road running south from Hollowmere through the hill country, down into the river valley, into the sprawl of the city. "We found a toll-keeper at the Cinders crossing who remembers a traveler matching the age profile — young, alone, traveling light, paid with old coin. Old coin matters. Fifteen years hiding means money buried or hoarded, not earned." She moved her finger down the road. "A carter two days later remembers the same man asking about rooms near the docks. Cheap rooms. Docks."

"You're certain it's him?" Pellam asked. "One traveler among—"

"I'm certain it's *an* Ishikawa or someone who wants us to think so, and either way I want him found." She straightened. "Here's how. No sweeps. No door-to-door. Nothing that smells of the Bastion." She saw Aldery frown and answered the frown before he voiced it. "Think, Captain. This boy — and he is probably a boy, fifteen years younger than the last registered Ishikawa — was hidden as a child specifically from *us*. His master will have taught him our procedures along with his letters. He knows what a patrol looks like, what a checkpoint feels like, what questions sound like. If we hunt him loudly, he goes to ground, and we lose him for a year we don't have."

"So what do we do?" asked Captain Rhese, who was young and impatient and good at doors breaking down.

"We do what we do worst," Voss said. "We're quiet. We're patient. We think like a craftsman instead of a soldier." She spread her hands over the map, over the tangle of streets below the cliffs where the dockside quarters climbed the hills in crooked tiers. "He's trained in charm-work. Charm-work is a trade. Trades need customers. He needs to eat, and he can't take honest work without papers he doesn't have, and he won't beg — pride is bred into these lines, you can see it in the portraits. So he sells. Small charms, luck-knots, fever-laces, the kind of thing that slips under the tolerance threshold. Which means somewhere in the dockside quarters, in the last month, someone has bought a luck-knot that works better than it should."

Silence around the table. Then Ivenne said, softly, with the particular hunger Voss had learned to distrust in scholars: "You can detect that? Resonance from a minor charm?"

"With the right instrument, yes. The College has one. It weighs maybe forty pounds and requires a mule." Voss allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "So we don't use the instrument. We use gossip. Every pawnshop, every charm-broker, every granny who sells knots at the fish market gets a visit from a plainclothes officer asking friendly questions about quality. Word travels in those quarters faster than plague. Within a week, if he's there, he'll have heard that someone's asking — and he'll do one of two things. Run, or get clever. Either way, he moves, and movement leaves tracks."

"And when we find him?" Pellam again. The Ministry man's voice had a careful smoothness to it that Voss had learned, over eight years of liaison work, to read as a warning label. "What are your intentions, Captain? For the record."

"For the record," Voss said, meeting his eyes, "we invite him to assist the Crown in the restoration of the border wards, with full clemency for any unlicensed activity prior to the invitation, and protections appropriate to his role." She let a beat pass. "Off the record, Pellam, you and I both know the Ministry will want him in a collar the moment he's useful. I'm telling you now, in front of witnesses: no collars. You cannot force a man to be a wall. A resentful warden makes a resentful ward, and I have read enough of the old records to know what happens when the blood turns unwilling." She didn't add what else the old records said — that the wards' strength had declined measurably in their last century, generation over generation, as though the blood itself were thinning, or the willingness. Some things you kept in reserve.

Pellam inclined his head a precise quarter-inch, which meant nothing, which meant he'd write it all down.

"Rhese, you take the dockside canvass. Plain clothes, pairs, no badges. Aldery, close the northern roads — quietly, customs inspections, nothing dramatic. Ivenne—" the doctor looked up sharply, hopeful, "—I want everything the College has on warden bloodlines. Registers, tithe rolls, parish books, anything with an Ishikawa name in it for the last fifty years. If he has kin, however distant, I want to know before he does."

"And you, Captain?" Aldery asked.

Voss looked at the map a moment longer. At the red line creeping in from the edges of the world. At the small inked square that was Hollowmere, empty now, its candle smoke dispersed, its secret carried south in a stolen book against someone's heart.

"I'll read," she said. "Everything the archives have on the Ishikawa line. Because before I ask a man to open his veins for the Crown, I intend to know exactly what the Crown did to his family for four hundred years — and exactly what we're likely to do to him."

* * *

She worked through the afternoon in the archive stacks, alone, sleeves rolled, dust to her elbows, and what she found she read three times.

The tithe rolls told it plainly, in the flat bookkeeping hand of dead clerks: *Edran Ishikawa, warden, received stipend. Edran's father, warden, received stipend.* Back and back, one name per generation, one line per lifetime, four hundred years of the same family paying the same tax in the same currency. There were gaps — years where the roll noted only *warden's deputy, renewal performed* — and those gaps clustered, she noticed, in decades of war and plague, when presumably the warden had died early and someone else had bled in his place. She found one marginal note, in a hand from two centuries gone, that stopped her with her finger still on the page:

*Warden's daughter wed a cooper of Ashfall against the Master's wishes. Line continues through her. The tithe does not care whose veins, only whose name.*

Whose name. Voss sat back in the creaking chair and stared at the note, and felt the shape of something slide into place — not the whole picture, not yet, but the frame of it. Names mattered. In warden-craft, names mattered the way keel-timber mattered to a ship. Which meant the boy they were hunting wasn't just carrying skills in his head. He was carrying something in his *name*, something the wards had been built around, something that couldn't be transferred or forged or conscripted.

Which meant the Crown couldn't simply take another volunteer. Which meant everything — the pardon, the negotiation, the delicate machinery she was building — rested on one frightened young man choosing to say yes.

She closed the ledger. Around her the archive rose in its silent towers, four hundred years of the Crown's careful forgetting, and somewhere below her the city went on lighting its lamps against a dark that no longer ended, and somewhere south of the river, in a cheap room above the noise of the docks, a boy with a stolen book and a dead man's ring was presumably reading the same history she'd just read, and drawing the opposite conclusion.

*Come to me willingly,* she thought at him, across the distance, with the closest thing to prayer her practical soul permitted. *Understand what I understand. Do the arithmetic. Be the wall — but choose it.*

In the morning she would sign the orders. Tonight she sat a while longer in the dust and the lamplight, a pragmatic woman in an impossible office, doing the only thing she knew how to do with dread: turning it into a plan.

Far to the north, at that same hour, at the ruined seat of Hollowmere, something in the fog where the wards used to stand turned its attention — patient, vast, unhurried — toward the south, toward the city, toward the smell of a name it had tasted once and intended, in its own time, to taste again.
