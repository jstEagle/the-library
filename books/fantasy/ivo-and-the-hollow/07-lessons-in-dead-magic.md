## 7. Lessons in Dead Magic

I had forgotten what it was to be cold.

That was the first thing I remembered when Marguerite Ishikawa slept — that I had once been a creature of temperature, of hunger, of the small animal comforts of a body, and that all of it had been taken from me in an act whose details I kept folded away in the deepest pocket of myself, where even I did not like to reach. Now I was a rumor wearing a man's shape, translucent as breath on glass, bound to an iron ring that rode in a boy's coat pocket like a second heart.

And every night, I taught him.

* * *

We had made camp in the shell of a shepherd's cot on the high moor road, three days out of Ashfall, where the wind came off the grass in long silver strokes and the ruined stars gave back nothing. Marguerite had built a fire with the competence of someone who had been poor for a long time — small, efficient, ringed with stones — and then he had sat beside it turning his hands over, palms up, the way he did when he was working up the courage to ask me something.

"You said last night," he began, "that the wards didn't just *stop*. That they were eaten."

"I did."

"Then teach me to hear it." He looked at me across the fire, and his eyes were his grandmother's eyes, dark and level and entirely too old for his face. "You keep saying I'm the heir. Fine. But an heir who can't tell when the thing under the world is moving is just meat with a famous name. Teach me."

There it was. The request I had both dreaded and, in some shameful chamber of whatever remained of my soul, longed for.

Because here is what no one tells you about being dead: you do not stop wanting things. You only stop being allowed them. And the closest thing I had to warmth in this half-life was sitting across a fire from me asking to be taught, and every lesson would cost me.

I knew this because I could feel it happening already. Three nights of small instruction — how to hold the signet, how to still his breathing, how to read the gray flicker of a charm before it failed — and each night I had gone to sleep standing up, as ghosts do, and felt a little less of myself come back. My left hand had gone first. Then the memory of my mother's voice. Then, two nights ago, the taste of honey, which I had carried for four hundred years without knowing why it mattered, until it was gone and I understood that it had mattered enormously.

Teaching him was spending me. Coin by coin. And I would pay it gladly, which was its own kind of damning.

"Sit properly," I said. "Cross-legged. Hands open on your knees."

He obeyed, and there was something in the obedience that undid me a little — this boy who flinched from authority, who had spent his whole life running from everyone who claimed a right to command him, giving it to me freely because I had asked instead of taken. His grandmother had been the same. Elara Ishikawa, who could make a room full of hardened wardens sit down simply by looking tired.

"Now close your eyes," I told him. "Not all the way. A sliver. Warden work is done with the eyes half-open, because the Hollow loves a closed eye. It likes to be dreamed."

"What is it, exactly?" His voice was careful. "Everyone says *the Hollow*. Nobody says what it is."

"A hole," I said. "A genuine one. Not a creature, not a god, not a darkness with intentions. It is an absence that learned to want. When the world was made, something was left out — call it a seam that never got sewn — and everything that has ever been lost drains toward it. Names first. Names are the finest thread. Then faces. Then love, which is just a name repeated so often it becomes part of the body." I watched the firelight move through my own hand, unimpeded, and hated it. "The wards were not walls, Marguerite. They were a lid. And your family were the weight that held it down."

"And now the lid's off."

"Now the lid's off. So we listen. Breathe slowly. In through the nose, out through the mouth, and on each exhale, let yourself sink — not into the ground, but *under* it. Like going beneath the ice on a winter lake. You'll feel cold. Let it be cold."

He closed his eyes to a sliver. I watched him fight it — the flinch, the animal refusal to go down into the dark — and then watched him win, which was better than talent. Talent is common. Winning against yourself, nightly, is rare.

"Do you feel it?" I asked softly.

His brow furrowed. "Water," he whispered. "There's water down there. Moving."

"Not water. But that's the right word for it. Keep it. The old wardens called it the Undercurrent. It moves along the deep places of the world the way meltwater moves under a frozen river — silently, patiently, following channels you can't see from above. Most of the time it passes beneath us and doesn't notice us at all." I let my voice drop lower. "But sometimes it slows. Sometimes it rises toward the ice. And if you're standing on the ice when it does—"

Marguerite went rigid. His eyes flew open.

"You felt it," I said.

"It stopped." His voice had gone thin. "It was moving past and then it — it turned. Ivo, it *turned*, like something hearing its name."

"That was you. Your attention touched it. A warden's attention is loud, heir — it's made of the same stuff the wards were made of, blood and iron and name. To the Hollow, being noticed by you feels like a lamp being lit in a window." I reached across the fire — uselessly; my hand passed near his knee and cast no shadow, gave no warmth, and he never once remarked on it, which was either kindness or grief — "So you learn to listen the way snow listens. Without weight. Again."

He tried again. And again. Four times he sank under the ice of the world, and four times the current turned toward him like a great blind fish rising to a lure, and four times I talked him back up before it could touch him, my voice steady while inside me something burned away like a candle eating its own wick.

On the fourth descent, I lost the sound of rain.

I don't know how else to describe it. There had been, somewhere in me, the memory of rain on a roof — a specific roof, slate, over a specific room, in a house I could no longer picture — and I reached for it during the long quiet of his listening and found the shelf empty. Just a smooth place where a memory used to be. I sat with my hands in my lap and did not move, because if I moved I thought I might scream, and screaming would wake him, and he needed the practice more than I needed the rain.

This was the arithmetic of it. This was the ledger I kept and showed no one:

One lesson: the taste of honey.
Two lessons: my mother's voice.
Three lessons: the color of a door I had loved.
Four lessons: rain on slate.

And tonight, watching him surface gasping and triumphant — "*I heard it turn! I actually heard it!* Tonight it turned slower, I could feel it deciding—" — I paid out the memory of a dog. Some brown dog, some yard, some century. Gone. I hadn't known I still had it until I felt it leave, the way you don't notice a splinter until the flesh closes around the absence.

"You're getting stronger," I said.

"So are you teaching faster." He grinned at me across the fire, firelight catching the sharp bones of his face, and for a moment he looked so much like her that my chest ached in a way I had no organs to ache with. "Is that normal? Voss's witch-finders train for years, Odile said—"

"Don't quote the rebel to me."

"She's not wrong, though. You're teaching me in days what took wardens years."

Because I am spending myself to do it, I did not say. Because every secret of the craft I pour into you comes out of the vessel that holds me, and the vessel is cracked and getting thinner, and by the time you stand at the warden seat you will be formidable and I will be a stain of light in an iron ring, and that is the bargain I made before you were born, and I have never once regretted it, and I never will, and I cannot tell you any of this because you would stop me.

What I said was: "You have less time than they did."

The grin faded. Good. He needed to understand that too.

* * *

Later, when he slept — truly slept, the boneless sleep of the young and hunted — I stood watch at the doorway of the ruined cot, as I had stood watch at doorways for four hundred years, and I let myself remember her.

Elara Ishikawa. The last warden before the hiding. She had summoned me in the old way, blood on iron, in a room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, and she had been seven months pregnant and furious about it, and she had looked at me — at the ghost her family had bound and bound again, generation after generation, until whatever I had originally been was buried under centuries of service — and she had said:

"You hate us."

It was not a question. Wardens learned early that I could not lie to the bearer of the ring.

"Yes," I had said.

"Good." She had put her hand flat on her belly, where the future kicked. "Then you'll tell me true. If I hide this child — break the line, let the wards starve, damn whatever comes after — will the world end?"

I had considered it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because hatred, oddly, makes for clean answers. "No," I said. "The world will not end. It will become a place where nothing ends. Nothing dies, nothing leaves, nothing is *lost* — and therefore nothing is loved, because love requires the possibility of loss. The Hollow does not devour the world, Elara. It preserves it. Forever. In amber. Screaming."

She had been silent a long time. Outside, the border mountains stood black against a sky still full of stars — the last year of stars, though none of us knew it.

"And if I feed it," she said finally. "If the line continues. If my child grows up to be poured into the wards like wine into a wound, and their child after, and theirs — how long?"

"Centuries. Perhaps a thousand years. And then the bloodline thins, as bloodlines do, and it fails anyway, and everything you sacrificed buys nothing but delay."

"So we're choosing between a slow lie and a fast truth."

"We are choosing," I said, "and I envy you that. I was not given a choice. I was *made* into an answer."

She had looked at me then — really looked, the way no bearer of the ring had bothered to in two hundred years — and something in her face shifted, and I felt, absurdly, seen.

"Then hear my choice," she said. "I will hide the child. I will break the chain. And I bind you, warden-ghost, by blood and iron and name: when the wards fail — not if, *when* — you will find him. You will teach him everything. You will keep him alive. And you will not tell him what it costs you, because if he knew, he would refuse to learn, and refusing to learn will kill him faster than ignorance ever could."

"That is a cruel binding."

"I know." Her hand pressed her belly. "I'm asking you to love him the way we were never allowed to love anyone. From a distance. At cost. Without being thanked." She had smiled then, tired and terrible. "Can you do that? Tell me true."

I had told her true.

"Yes."

* * *

"Hey."

I turned. Marguerite was awake, propped on one elbow, hair flattened on one side, watching me with the particular suspicion he reserved for moments when he caught me being still.

"You don't sleep," he said. "Do you."

"Ghosts don't."

"But you do something. When I sleep. You get — quieter. Dimmer." He sat up, and the fire had burned low, and his face was half shadow. "Every morning you're a little less *there*. I've been counting."

Of course he had. He counted everything — coins, steps, exits, lies. A boy raised by a fugitive master learns to audit the world.

"The ring draws on me to stay anchored," I said. It was almost true. It was true the way a map is true — accurate, and omitting everything that matters. "Anchoring costs substance. It's nothing."

"It's not nothing. You're translucent. You were *more* translucent a week ago." He got up, crossed the room, and stood in front of me, close enough that if I had breath it would have stirred his collar. "What aren't you telling me?"

Four hundred years of discipline held my voice level. "Everything," I said. "I'm not telling you everything. That was the arrangement. Ask me anything except that, and I'll answer true."

He stared at me. His jaw worked. And I watched him decide — watched him weigh his hunger for truth against his need for me, and choose, with visible effort, to set the question down. Not drop it. Set it down, the way you set down a blade you intend to pick up later.

"Fine," he said. "Then answer this. The promise you made — the one you mutter about when you think I'm asleep. Who was it to?"

Ah. He had heard that. Of course he had; I spoke to her ghost sometimes in the hour before dawn, an old habit, a private liturgy.

"To a woman who saved your life before you could walk," I said. "Who asked me for one impossible thing, and I gave it, and I have been paying it off ever since. She was the best of your line, Marguerite. Which is a low bar cleared easily, but still."

"My grandmother." Not a question. He had the journal now; he'd read the names. "Elara."

"Elara," I agreed, and did not say *she bound me to spend myself teaching you and forbade me to tell you why*, and the lie of omission settled into me like a swallowed stone.

He nodded slowly. Then, awkwardly — he was bad at tenderness, having been given so little raw material for it — he reached out and closed his hand around the iron ring where it hung at my tether, so that his fingers nearly passed through mine.

"When this is done," he said, "when the wards are dealt with, whatever that means — I'll find a way to give it back to you. Whatever the ring takes. Whatever anchoring costs. I'll figure it out. I'm good at figuring things out."

I looked at his hand around the ring. I looked at the fire, burning down to orange lace. I thought of a brown dog in a yard I could no longer see, and rain on a roof I could no longer hear, and a woman with a level gaze asking me to love at cost without thanks, and I said:

"I know you are. Sleep, heir. Tomorrow we cross the river country, and you'll need to learn to listen while walking, which is harder, and which I will teach you whether it costs me the rest of my name or not."

He laughed — short, disbelieving, but real — and went back to his bedroll, and within minutes his breathing slowed into the deep rhythm of the young.

I stood in the doorway and watched the moonless dark, and far to the east, where the land sloped down toward the river country, I saw — or thought I saw, or *felt*, which for a thing like me is worse — a stillness in the Undercurrent. Not the turning of the current toward a listening mind.

A widening. A place where the water under the ice had gone utterly, perfectly calm, the way it goes calm above a drowning.

Tomorrow's road led straight through it. And in that stillness, faint as a smell of smoke from a neighbor's fire, I felt something I had not felt since the wards fell:

Names. Thousands of them, hanging in the air of some small place, unclaimed — like coats left behind at a party where no one remembered coming.

"Hurry and sleep well, heir," I murmured to the sleeping boy, to the woman who bound me, to the dark. "Before the world forgets there was ever anything to save."
