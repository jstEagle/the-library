## 8. The Village That Forgot Itself

They walked north for three days, and the world grew quieter with every mile.

Not silent — Marguerite had learned the difference on the road. Silence was an absence, a held breath. This was something else: birds still sang in the hedgerows, but they sang fewer songs. Streams still ran, but their voices had gone thin, as if the water were forgetting how to be loud. On the second evening he caught himself humming a tune he could not name, and when he tried to remember where he'd learned it, the memory slid away like a fish through his fingers, leaving only the shape of having known.

"That's not you," Ivo said quietly, walking beside him without disturbing so much as a blade of grass. "That's it. The Hollow. It's close enough now to taste the edges of things."

"Edges of what?"

"Everything loose." Ivo's translucent face was turned toward the darkening hills. "Songs no one has written down. Names spoken only in passing. Small griefs. It feeds at the margins first, the way frost finds the corners of a window before it takes the glass."

Marguerite's hand went instinctively to the pouch at his belt, where the last of his charms hung — a knot of red thread, a sliver of iron, a dried sprig of rowan that had once been bright with luck and was now the color of old ash. His craft felt very small out here. A boy selling knotted thread against the dark that unmade names.

"You said we'd know when we crossed into touched country," he said. "How?"

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, his voice had the careful flatness he used when he was deciding how much truth Marguerite could carry.

"You'll know," he said. "There's a smell."

* * *

The smell came at midday on the fourth day, drifting down from a fold in the hills where smoke rose — ordinary hearth-smoke, domestic and gray — but beneath it something sweeter and wrong, like lilies left too long in a closed room. Like a funeral that no one had bothered to hold.

The village sat in the crook of a river valley, maybe forty houses, thatch and timber, chickens scratching in the lane. From the ridge above, it looked like any village in the northern reaches: prosperous enough, sleepy, untouched by the riots and refugee columns far to the south. Laundry hung between windows. A dog trotted down the main street with the purposeful air of a dog who knew exactly which doorways owed him scraps.

And every soul in it was smiling.

Marguerite saw the first of them at the well — a woman drawing water, her bucket rising smooth and easy, her face turned up toward a sky that had been starless for weeks now. She was smiling the way people smile in their sleep. Not joy. Not peace. Something more like the expression of a person listening to music no one else can hear, music made entirely of reassurance.

"Good morning!" she called to them, cheerful and clear. "Lovely day for travelers. Are you hungry? We've bread."

Her voice was warm. Her eyes were warm. And behind the warmth there was nothing — a room swept clean, furniture covered in white sheets, waiting for owners who would never come back.

"Thank you," Marguerite managed. "We're just passing through."

"Pity! Stay for supper, then. Everyone stays for supper." She hoisted her bucket. "Oh — forgive me, I've forgotten my manners. I'm—" She stopped. The smile stayed fixed on her face, but something flickered behind it, a bird beating against a window. "I'm... isn't that funny. I've quite lost my name. It'll come back to me. Names always come back after supper."

She walked away, unhurried, water slopping gently over the rim of the bucket, still smiling.

Marguerite stood very still in the lane. Beside him, Ivo had gone rigid in a way that a living man could not have managed — every remnant of substance drawn taut, his outline flickering like a candle in a draft.

"Ivo," Marguerite said. "What is this?"

"They're alive." Ivo's voice came out strange, stripped of its usual dry composure. "Look at them. Healthy. Fed. No wounds, no wasting. The Hollow didn't touch their bodies at all."

"Then what did it take?"

"Come and see," Ivo said, "if you must. But Marguerite—" He turned, and for the first time since the undercity, since the ring, since everything, the ghost looked afraid. "Whatever you see, whatever they say — do not tell them your name. Do not let them give you theirs. Not here."

* * *

They found the children first.

Or rather, the children found them — a pack of them spilling out of a dooryard, six or seven small figures chasing a hoop down the lane, shrieking with laughter. Ordinary children. Dirty-kneed, snub-nosed, alive. One of them, a girl of perhaps five with a cowlick standing up off her crown like a rooster's feather, skidded to a stop directly in front of Marguerite and stared up at him with frank, unafraid curiosity.

"You're new," she announced.

"I am."

"Everyone here is old. Well—" she screwed up her face, reconsidering, "—not old-old. Just the same. Everyone's the same age forever, Mam says. Except Mam doesn't say much anymore. She mostly smiles." The girl wrinkled her nose. "I don't like it when she smiles at me like that. It's like she's smiling at the wall and the wall is wearing my mam's face."

Marguerite crouched down slowly, bringing himself to her level. Up close he could see what the distance had hidden: the girl's dress was clean but mended badly, seams crooked, buttons mismatched. No one had done her hair properly in days. Weeks. The whole village wore that same look — tended, but untending. A garden where the gardener had forgotten why the plants mattered.

"What's your name?" he asked gently.

"Briony." Then, eagerly, as if trading treasures: "What's yours?"

"He hasn't got one today," Ivo said smoothly, materializing at Marguerite's shoulder. Several of the children gasped — they could see him, Marguerite realized; of course they could, these were touched country children, the veil worn thin here — and one small boy reached out to poke Ivo's arm and giggled when his finger passed through.

"A ghost!" the boy crowed. "A real ghost! Tell us a story, ghost!"

"After," Ivo said, and something in his tone made even the children settle. He nodded toward the largest house on the green, a low stone manse with a slate roof. "Who lives there?"

"The rector," Briony said. "Except he's not a rector anymore. He says he's a shepherd now. He says we're all safe in the fold." She said it cheerfully, repeating words she'd been given, and then her small face clouded. "He smiles too. But sometimes at night I hear him counting."

"Counting what?"

"People." Briony shrugged, the shrug of a child who has decided some questions are adults' business. "He counts us all, every night, walking round and round the green. And when he gets to the end he starts again, because he's forgotten how many he had. But he never forgets to count *me*." Her chin lifted, suddenly fierce, suddenly five years old and terrified underneath the fierceness. "He never forgets me. Someone has to."

Marguerite's chest tightened until it hurt. "Briony. Your mother. If I asked you to describe her — her face, the color of her hair—"

"Mam?" The girl blinked. "Mam is..." She looked toward the well, where the woman had set down her bucket and was smiling at nothing, at the sky, at the folded white rooms behind her eyes. "Mam is the lady by the well."

"Yes, but before. What does she look like?"

The smile drained off Briony's face by slow degrees, like water through sand. Her brow furrowed. Her hands, small and chapped, opened and closed at her sides.

"She's tall," the girl said at last, in the flat voice of someone reading from a description rather than a memory. "She has... hair. She's my mam." Her voice cracked on the word, and she stamped her foot, sudden fury blazing up through the fog. "*She's my mam.* Why don't I know what she looks like? Why can't I—"

"Briony." Marguerite took her hands. They were ice-cold. "Listen to me. Look at her right now. Right this moment. Don't try to remember — just look. Hold onto what you see right now, today, this hour. Can you do that?"

The girl stared at him. Then she turned, deliberately, and looked at her mother with the terrible concentration of a child obeying the last adult who had ever asked anything real of her. The woman at the well felt the gaze and turned, and smiled her sweeping, vacant smile, and lifted one hand in a wave meant for anyone and no one.

"I see her," Briony whispered. "I see her right now."

"Then keep seeing her. Every day. New eyes every day. Promise me."

"I promise," the girl said solemnly, and Marguerite thought: *she'll keep it, too, until she can't, until the Hollow comes back through and sweeps even this away, and there is nothing I can do about it, nothing, nothing—*

His charms burned cold against his hip. He let go of her hands and stood.

* * *

The rector was waiting for them at the manse door, as if he'd counted them coming.

He had been a big man once, broad through the shoulders, with the weathered look of someone who'd worked fields before taking orders. Now the bigness had gone soft, unfocused, like a house whose owner had stopped caring about the walls. He smiled at them from the doorstep with Briony's mother's smile — the smile of the folded rooms — and clasped his hands together.

"Travelers! Welcome, welcome. You'll stay for supper."

"We thank you," Ivo said, stepping forward, "but we're bound for the warden seat at Harrowgate. We only stopped to ask after the road."

"Harrowgate." The rector tasted the word. "Long walk. Long, long walk. Nothing up there but ruins and old stones and the dark under the dark." He chuckled, a sound like dry leaves. "Nothing up there worth remembering. That's why it's safe, you understand. The Shepherd keeps His flock where the pasture is soft."

"The Shepherd," Marguerite said carefully. "You mean God?"

"I mean what holds us." The rector's smile widened by a degree. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which had gone distant and mild, gazing through Marguerite at some middle distance where all questions dissolved. "It came to us in the night, after the stars went out. It didn't break down our doors. It didn't take our grain or our blood or our children. It only asked us to set down our burdens. Grief is heavy, traveler. Guilt is heavy. Memory — oh, memory is the heaviest burden of all." He sighed, blissful, a man setting down a sack of stones he'd carried for decades. "We were so tired. All those years of carrying everyone we'd ever lost, everywhere we went. And now—" he spread his arms, encompassing the village, the smiling lanes, the children playing in the shadow of forgetting, "—now we are light."

"And the ones you lost," Marguerite said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Your dead. Your parents. The people you loved. You don't miss them?"

"How can I miss what I never had?" The rector beamed at him, genuinely, horribly serene. "There was never anything missing. There is no hole in me. There is no hollow in me at all."

Behind him, inside the manse, Marguerite heard movement — other villagers, laying out supper, moving with the placid industry of ants. And beneath it, faintly, a sound that stopped his breath: a woman singing a lullaby to a child, except she sang only the melody, over and over, having lost the words, having lost the child's name, having lost perhaps the knowledge that there had ever been words at all.

"It doesn't kill," Marguerite said to Ivo as they backed away from the doorstep, the rector waving benevolently after them. "All this time I thought — the stories, the darkness that eats names — I thought it was slaughter. It's not. It's *this*. It leaves the body standing and takes everything that made the body a person. It doesn't kill you. It makes the world kill you slowly, by forgetting you while you watch."

"Now you understand," Ivo said quietly.

"No. I don't think I do. Not yet." Marguerite stopped in the lane, and the full weight of it came down on him all at once — the village, the children, the woman singing a wordless song, the Crown's proclamations posted in every southern town since the wards fell: *ORDER RESTORED. CALM PREVAILS. THE KINGDOM ENDURES.* Calm. He'd read those notices and felt the lie in them without being able to name it, and now the name stood smiling at him from every doorway in this valley. "Ivo. The cities south of here. Ashfall. The reports say there's been no violence since the wards fell. No panic. People are calm. Too calm. I thought it was shock."

"It wasn't shock."

"It was the edges going first." Marguerite's hands had begun to shake. "Small griefs. Songs. Names spoken in passing. And then — the Crown calls it peace. The Cabal will call it freedom. And everyone will keep smiling, keep drawing water, keep singing songs with no words, right up until there's no one left inside to notice they've been emptied." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "The Hollow doesn't need to devour the world. It just needs everyone to stop noticing the world is gone."

Ivo said nothing. When Marguerite glanced over, the ghost was looking back down the valley the way they'd come, toward the south, toward Ashfall, with an expression Marguerite couldn't read — grief, or guilt, or something older than either.

"We should go," Ivo said finally. "The road to Harrowgate climbs from here. We can make the ridge by dark if we hurry."

But Marguerite lingered one moment longer, looking back at the well, where Briony had climbed up beside her mother and taken her hand. The woman smiled down at her daughter with love — real love, or the shape of it, the muscle memory of it — and Marguerite understood with sick clarity that the shape might be all that was left, and that the girl knew it, and was holding on anyway, holding on with both hands to a mother made of outlines.

*Keep seeing her,* he'd told her. As if looking hard enough could stand against a hunger that ate the looker.

They climbed out of the valley as the sun went down, and behind them the village lights kindled one by one, warm and golden and utterly empty, like lanterns lit in windows of houses where no one lived.

It was full dark, and the ridge path steep and stony underfoot, when Ivo stopped so abruptly that Marguerite nearly walked through him.

Ahead, silhouetted against the faint gray of the starsless sky, a line of riders blocked the road. Lanterns swung at their saddles, and the light glinted off breastplates and the long pale shafts of witch-finder pikes. At their head sat a figure in a captain's coat, straight-backed and still, watching them climb with the patience of someone who had already counted the cost of the chase and found it acceptable.

"Well," Ivo murmured. "The Crown found us first after all."

The rider at the head of the line raised her lantern, and its light fell across the face of Captain Serane Voss.
