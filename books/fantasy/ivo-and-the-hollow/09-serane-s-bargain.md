## 9. Serane's Bargain

The road to the warden seat ran through country that had forgotten how to be itself, and Serane Voss rode it with a ledger open across her saddle and a headache building behind her eyes like weather.

She had been a witch-finder for nineteen years. In that time she had learned that magic was rarely the thing people feared it was. It was not lightning from the fingertips or demons in the well. It was smaller than that — a charm stitched into a coat hem, a knot of colored thread sold for two coppers at a dockside stall, an old woman whispering to her garden because the whispering made the beans grow and no one could prove otherwise. Magic was the ordinary world refusing, in small ways, to obey.

Her job had always been bookkeeping. That was how she thought of it, though she never said so aloud. Every finding she signed was an entry in a ledger that balanced one life against the safety of ten thousand, and in nineteen years the arithmetic had never once come out wrong — or rather, it had never come out *right*, only less wrong than the alternatives, which was the best any honest officer could claim.

Now the ledger was burning.

She read the report again as her horse picked its way down the flooded lane. The wards along the northern border had been warden-made. Warden-made meant Ishikawa-made; there had never been another line. Four hundred years of maintenance, four hundred years of feeding, and the last entry in the Crown's own records was a boy apprenticed out sixty years ago to a hedge-witch named Corvane, after which the bloodline simply — stopped. Vanished into the population like a coin dropped in a river.

And now a rumor, third-hand, from a refugee who had wept too much to be lying: a charm-seller in Ashfall's dock quarter whose knots worked when they shouldn't. Young. Dark-haired. A name that wasn't Ishikawa but might be hiding one.

*Marguerite.* The name sat strangely on the page. She had written it herself, transcribed from the informant's scrawl, and still it looked like something borrowed.

"Captain." Lieutenant Hask reined up beside her, rain streaming off the brim of his hat. "Scouts say they've stopped ahead. The shrine at Marrow Ford. Two of them — the boy and—" He hesitated. His pen had refused, three times, to write down what the scouts reported seeing with the boy. "The companion."

"The ghost," Voss said, because someone had to say it plainly.

"Yes, captain."

"Then we stop too." She closed the ledger. "And nobody draws until I give the word. If half what this report says is true, we are not hunting a criminal. We are hunting the last load-bearing wall of the realm, and I would very much like it to remain standing."

Hask blinked. "You believe the report?"

"I believe the stars went out," Voss said. "Everything else is commentary."

* * *

Marrow Ford had been a shrine once, back when shrines were allowed to mention the wardens without shame. Now it was a roofless ring of stones furred with moss, standing in a bend of the river where the water moved slow and green and far too quiet. Voss noticed the quiet first. Then she noticed that her horse would not drink.

They came at dusk, when the light went long and amber and forgiving, because Voss had learned that people said their truest things in forgiving light. Her riders took the treeline without sound. She walked in alone, hands visible, sword left saddled — a calculated vulnerability, and she hated every step of it.

The boy stood at the shrine's center with his back to her, talking to empty air.

He was thinner than the reports suggested. Soaked to the knees, mud to the thigh, a traveler's pack hanging off him like he'd been wearing it for a month, which by her count he had. When he turned, she saw the exhaustion first and the fear second and, underneath both, something that made her chest tighten in a way she hadn't anticipated: he looked like a young man who had been running so long he'd forgotten there was anywhere to arrive.

"You're the witch-finder," he said. Not a question. "Serane Voss."

"You have me at a disadvantage."

"Ivo says you don't waste time on pleasantries before arrests." His chin lifted slightly. "So. Is this one?"

"This is a conversation," Voss said. "Arrests are faster."

Something flickered at the edge of her vision — a distortion, like heat-shimmer in cold air, standing just behind the boy's shoulder. She kept her eyes on Marguerite. Nineteen years of discipline. You did not look directly at things that were not entirely present; it encouraged them.

"Your companion disapproves," she observed.

"He disapproves of everything I do. It's his whole personality."

Despite herself — despite the drowned villages behind her and the ledger full of names and the stars missing over her own city — Voss felt the corner of her mouth move. "Then let's give him something to disapprove of properly." She reached into her coat, slowly, and drew out not irons but a folded document, sealed in green wax. "Captain Serane Voss, acting under authority of the Privy Council. What I'm about to offer you has been authorized at the highest level. I want you to understand that before you hear the terms, because the terms are going to sound like a cage."

"They usually do," said the air beside the boy, dryly, and Voss decided she disliked the ghost on principle.

"Sit, if you like. Or stand. I'll be brief either way." She laid the document on the flat top of the shrine's altar stone. "Here is what I know. The border wards fell because the bloodline that fed them was broken — deliberately, sixty years ago, by your master, who I suspect believed he was saving you. He wasn't. He was saving you *for later*, and later has arrived. Here is what I also know: since the wards fell, eleven settlements within a hundred miles of the border have gone quiet. Not destroyed. Quiet. The people are alive, fed, smiling. They cannot tell you their children's names. My own mother lives in one of them." Her voice did not change at all. "I visited her last week. She offered me tea and called me 'the nice soldier lady.'"

Silence, except the river.

"So I am not here to threaten you, Marguerite Ishikawa. Threats are for people who have options. I'm here because you are the only option anyone has, and I would rather you came to it willingly, with terms, than dragged, without." She tapped the seal. "Full clemency. Every charge expunged — unlicensed craft, flight from finding, all of it. Legal protection for you and any who shelter you. A stipend, a house, a title if you want one, though I suspect you don't. And in exchange—"

"In exchange I go into the ground under the warden seat and feed the wards with my blood until I die," Marguerite said quietly. "That's the exchange. I've read my family's history. I know what 'rekindling' means."

Voss held his gaze. "Yes. That's the exchange."

"You could dress it up. State service. Honored duty. A pension for my descendants, if I ever had any, which I won't, because I'll be underground being a furnace."

"I could dress it up," Voss agreed. "I chose not to. You've been lied to enough by everyone who should have protected you. I won't add to the sum." She stepped back from the altar. "Read it. Take tonight. My riders will not approach the shrine. In the morning, you give me your answer, and whatever it is, I'll write it in my ledger honestly."

"And if I refuse?" the boy asked. "If I just walk away?"

"Then I arrest you at dawn, and the Privy Council does the dressing-up themselves, and you end up in the same ground with worse company and no clemency for whoever helped you get there." She paused. "But you should know the third thing, the thing I'd want known if I were you. There are others looking for you. Rebels. They call themselves the Open Door. They found you before I did once already, didn't they? They'll find you again. And their offer will be kinder than mine — kindness costs nothing to promise — and then they'll cut your throat over a gatehouse altar and call your blood liberation. At least with me, you know exactly what shape the cage is."

"That's your argument?" The shimmer behind him sharpened, and for a moment Voss could almost see it: a tall figure, dark-haired, translucent as breath on glass. "That his choices are your cage or their knife?"

"It's my argument that he doesn't have a choice that isn't a cage," Voss said, meeting where its eyes would be. "I've read the same history you have, warden. The difference between us is that I never pretended otherwise. Someone has to hold the wards. Someone has to pay. The only question ever asked in this world is *who*, and every faction asking it right now intends to answer with this boy's life while telling him it was his idea." She turned back to Marguerite. "I'm the only one offering to tell you the truth first. That's worth something. It may be the only thing I have that's worth anything."

Marguerite looked down at the document for a long moment. His hand hovered over the green wax but did not touch it.

"My master hid me," he said. "Sixty years of my family dying in the dark, and he hid me, and I thought — I spent my whole life thinking I was free. And now you're telling me the freedom was just a delay."

"I'm telling you grief makes bad arithmetic," Voss said. "Don't do sums tonight. Do them in the morning."

* * *

She withdrew to the treeline and gave him the night, as promised.

Hask found her sitting on a fallen log, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen, and had the good sense not to speak for a while.

"He'll run," Hask said finally.

"Of course he'll run."

"Then why offer terms at all? Why not take him now, while we have him boxed?"

Voss tested the blade's edge against her thumb, watching the firelight crawl along it. "Because a furnace that goes into the ground hating the Crown cracks in twenty years, Hask. And a cracked ward is worse than none. I need him to walk down those stairs believing it was the least-bad thing. Belief holds longer than chains." She sheathed the knife. "Besides. I told him the truth. Whatever he decides, he'll decide knowing. That matters. It's the only currency I've got left that isn't counterfeit."

"And if he runs to the rebels instead?"

"Then we burn Ashfall trying to get him back, and I'll have my mother's face in my head the entire time, calling me the nice soldier lady." She looked up at the starless sky, the hole where the constellations should have been, and felt the old familiar weight settle onto her shoulders — the weight of being the person who does the necessary thing and signs her name beneath it. "Wake me at midnight. If he's going to run, he'll run before dawn. They always do."

He ran before dawn.

She heard it — the soft double-footfall, the ghost's voice low and urgent, the splash at the ford — and she did not give the order to stop him. Hask looked at her, waiting, hand on his horn, and she shook her head once.

Let him run. Let him see the Open Door's kindness up close. Let him learn what she had learned in nineteen years of ledgers: that there was no door in this world that opened outward, only doors that opened into other rooms, and every room had a price scratched on the frame.

But as the splashing faded downstream, toward the ruined warden seat and whatever waited there, Voss took out her ledger and wrote, by lantern light, the day's honest entry:

*Subject fled rather than choose. All parties continue to assume he must choose.*

*Note to self: consider whether that assumption is the trap.*

She underlined it twice, closed the book, and gave the order to ride — not after the boy, but toward Ashfall, where the Cabal's agents had been seen buying rope and quicklime in quantities that meant a rite.

Every door leads to a cage, she had told him. She was beginning to suspect she'd left out the worst part: that some cages were built by the people insisting you pick one.
