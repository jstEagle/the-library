## 10. Blood and Iron

The warden seat had been called Highmoor once, before the Crown burned it and salted the name from every map. Now it was only a wound in the moorland: a ring of blackened foundations like broken teeth, a well shaft that breathed cold air even in summer, and grass that grew wrong — pale, silvered, leaning always toward the center of the ruin as if the ground itself remembered what it had lost.

Marguerite stood at the edge of it as the sun went down behind him, stretching his shadow long across the ruined threshold, and felt the place notice him.

There was no other word for it. The hair on his arms lifted. The silvered grass shivered though there was no wind. Somewhere beneath his feet, deep under the stone and the dead earth, something vast turned over in its sleep — or in its hunger — and for one terrible moment he felt seen.

"You shouldn't be here," Ivo said.

He stood at Marguerite's shoulder, translucent in the last light, more suggestion than man. Three days of travel had thinned him. Marguerite had noticed it first at the river crossing, when Ivo's hand had passed through the ferry rope instead of gripping it; then at the inn outside Vell's Crossing, where he'd sat by the window all night because he could no longer lean against walls without sinking partway through them. He was fainter now. Like ink washed too many times.

"So you've said," Marguerite said. "Eleven times since the crossroads. I counted."

"Then count this too." Ivo drifted ahead of him, across the threshold, and Marguerite saw the ruin press against him like a current against a swimmer. "The wards here didn't fail. They were *killed*. There's a difference. A failed ward goes quiet. This—" he gestured at the silvered grass, the breathing well, "—this is a throat that was cut and never healed. You're standing on an open vein."

"Then it's the right place." Marguerite swung his pack down and began unpacking with the deliberate calm he'd learned selling charms to strangers: never let them see your hands shake. The journal came out first, its leather cracked, his master's handwriting cramped and furious inside. Then the iron signet ring, warm against his chest where he wore it on a cord. Then the knife, the chalk, the vials of his own blood he'd been drawing for three days, each one sealed with wax and a whispered word. "The rite has to be done where the wards were anchored. The journal is clear on that."

"The journal was written by a man who intended to *die* doing it."

"Yes." Marguerite laid out the chalk. "I read that part."

* * *

They made camp in what had been the gatehouse, because it was the only structure with half a roof, and while the light lasted Marguerite studied the rite until the letters swam. It was worse than he'd admitted to himself. Four hundred years of Ishikawas had fed the wards drop by drop, life by life, a slow tithe that no single bearer ever felt the whole weight of. The rekindling rite compressed four hundred years into one night. One bloodline. One body.

His master's marginal note, in handwriting gone thin and hurried near the end: *If you are reading this, I have failed to find another way. Forgive me. Do not attempt it alone.*

"Do not attempt it alone," Marguerite read aloud, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle.

"He meant with other wardens," Ivo said quietly. From the doorway. Not crossing it. "A circle of them. Twelve, ideally. The rite distributes itself among willing bearers. That's why they lived in seats like this — not for the walls. For each other."

"And how many wardens do you see?" Marguerite spread his arms. "Look around, Ivo. The Crown burned them out two centuries ago. The rebels want me as a weapon. The witch-finders want me as a furnace. There is no one else. There's just me, and you, and whatever's left of you after three days of walking through cold rain you can't even feel."

"I am not—"

"You're spending yourself teaching me," Marguerite said. "Don't lie about that either. Every lesson, you get fainter. I've been watching you fade since the flooded quarter, and you keep pretending I haven't noticed, and I keep letting you pretend, because I need what you're giving me. So don't stand there and tell me what the rite requires. Tell me something useful or let me work."

For a long moment Ivo said nothing. The dusk thickened around him until he was barely visible at all, just a paler darkness in the darkening doorway, and Marguerite thought — not for the first time — that he looked less like a ghost than like a hole in the shape of a man.

"There is a way to share the burden," Ivo said finally. "But it requires trust I haven't earned, and time we don't have, and—" His voice did something strange then, caught on something, and he stopped. When he continued, it was flat and careful. "Do the rite at moonrise. Not before. The wards were keyed to the moon's reflection in the well. If you cast while the sun still touches the stones, you'll be working against the anchor, not with it."

It was the most he'd given away in days. Marguerite nodded, mollified despite himself, and went back to memorizing the sigils.

He did not see Ivo look at the well for a long, long time.

* * *

Moonrise came up red.

There was ash on the wind from some distant burning — the Hollow's work, spreading outward now that the borders were open, eating villages the way it had eaten that smiling town with its mothers who couldn't remember their children's faces. The memory of that village rode with Marguerite everywhere now. He carried it the way soldiers carry wounds: not as pain exactly, but as proof. Proof of what waited if he failed. Proof of what his master had died hiding him from, what the witch-finders would burn him for, what Odile Ferrant wanted to unleash.

He stripped to the waist in the cold. Chalked the great circle around the well, the twelve anchor-points, the inner ring where his own name would go — written backward, unwritten, the way the journal instructed, so that the ward could grip it. He set the signet ring in the central hollow of the stone. He pricked his thumb and let the first drops fall.

And Ivo stood at the circle's edge, outside it, hands clenched at his sides, and said nothing.

That should have warned Marguerite. Ivo always had something to say. Ivo corrected his chalk lines and his pronunciation and his breathing and the angle of his knife. Ivo had opinions about everything, delivered in that dry, patient voice, and his silence now was louder than any warning.

But Marguerite was angry. He had been angry for weeks — angry at his master, at the Crown, at Odile's soft voice and hard offers, at the journal that had stolen his childhood a second time by revealing it had been stolen the first. And anger is its own kind of blindness. He looked at the circle and saw not a grave but a door: through it, everything could be set right. The wards restored. The stars safe. The Hollow starved back behind the border of the world. All of it bought with one life — his — which was, he told himself, the only thing he'd ever truly owned anyway.

He spoke the first word of the rite.

The world *tilted*.

Not moved — tilted, the way a table tilts when a leg gives way, and Marguerite felt the moorland lurch beneath him like the deck of a ship. The chalk lines flared white. The well exhaled a breath of air so cold it burned, and from somewhere far below — far farther below than any well could go — something heard him.

*Oh,* said the Hollow, in a voice that was not a voice but a pressure, a cold hand closing around the base of his skull. *Oh. There you are.*

The second word of the rite tore out of him sideways. He hadn't chosen to speak it. The working had him now, running along channels dug by four hundred years of dead Ishikawas, and those channels were old, and cracked, and full of things that had been waiting. The sigils blazed. His blood rose off the stone in beads of red light. And in the center of the circle the signet ring began to spin, faster and faster, screaming a note only he could hear—

—and the ground opened.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. No earthquake, no gout of flame. The earth simply became *thin*, the way ice becomes thin, and then it became nothing, and Marguerite was looking down into a darkness that was not underground at all but *elsewhere* — a darkness with texture, with appetite, a darkness that leaned toward him the way a starving man leans toward bread.

It tasted his name.

He felt it happen. Felt the Hollow take the first syllable of *Marguerite* into itself and worry it, the way a wolf worries meat, learning its flavor, learning the shape of the bloodline wrapped around it, learning — with a kind of slow, delighted recognition — *the way back*. His own name began to pull at him from the inside, tugging toward that darkness like a boat rope sliding off a cleat. He grabbed for the rite and the rite was gone, unraveled, the chalk lines guttering, and he was on his knees at the edge of a wound in the world with his name unraveling out of his mouth—

*Ishikawa,* the darkness said, tasting deeper. *Little heir. Little last one. Come down. We kept your grandmother's room.*

He screamed. He didn't remember deciding to. The sound came out of him raw and it wasn't enough, wasn't anything, a candle against that hunger, and the pulling got stronger, and somewhere very far away he heard Ivo shouting his name — his true name, the warden-name, the one that anchored — and then Ivo was *in* the circle, which no ghost should have been able to do, which violated every law of the craft he'd spent three weeks teaching Marguerite—

Ivo stepped between him and the wound.

"Take mine instead," Ivo said — not to Marguerite, to the darkness. "You know what I am. You've always known what I am. Take it back. Take *me*."

The Hollow hesitated. The whole night hesitated with it — the wind, the flaring sigils, Marguerite's own heart. And in that hesitation Ivo turned, and his face —

his face was *wrong*. For one instant, looking past the translucence, past the careful shape of the man Marguerite had come to trust, Marguerite saw something else underneath: vast, and cold, and lit from within by the same darkness that breathed out of the wound. A fragment of it. A splinter of the thing itself, wearing a man's grief like a coat.

Then Ivo knelt, seized Marguerite's face in hands that had suddenly, impossibly, become solid enough to hold him, and pressed their foreheads together.

"Breathe," Ivo said. "Say your name. *Your* name. The one your master gave you, not the one they wrote in the ledger. Say it and mean it."

Marguerite's lips were numb. "M-Marguerite—"

"Not the name they tasted. The name *underneath* it. The one from the cottage. The one she used at bedtime." Ivo's eyes were very close, and very bright, and terrified. "Say it."

And Marguerite — eleven years old again for one heartbeat, firelight, a woman's voice, a name whispered like a charm against nightmares — said it. The secret one. The true one. The one no ledger held and no witch-finder knew and the Hollow had never tasted, because his master had buried it so deep even Marguerite had forgotten he was carrying it.

The pulling stopped.

The wound in the world snapped shut like a jaw missing its prey, and the silence afterward was total — the moor holding its breath — and then Marguerite was falling forward into arms that caught him, solid arms, warm arms, and the last thing he saw before the dark took him was Ivo's face above his own.

Half of what remained of Ivo had gone into that rescue. Marguerite understood it later, the way you understand things in dreams. The ghost who had been faint as washed ink was now nearly transparent, a man-shaped absence trembling in the moonlight, holding him together by sheer refusal to let go.

"You lied to me," Marguerite whispered. His voice came from very far away. "About what you are."

"Yes," Ivo said.

"What *are* you?"

Ivo closed his eyes. When he opened them, the wrongness was gone, folded carefully away, and there was only exhaustion there, and something older and sadder than exhaustion.

"Not tonight," he said. "Tonight you live. Ask me tomorrow — if tomorrow still wants you."

And Marguerite, bleeding and hollowed and holding the ghost of a question he was suddenly afraid to hear answered, let the darkness take him — a kind darkness this time, empty and ordinary — knowing that when he woke, the man who had saved him would owe him a truth.

And that Ivo, whatever he was, would rather face the Hollow itself than give it.
