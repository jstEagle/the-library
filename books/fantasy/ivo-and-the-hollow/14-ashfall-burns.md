## 14. Ashfall Burns

The first thing Serane Voss noticed was the smoke going the wrong way.

She stood on the roof of the Witch-Finders' Hall, where she had taken to sleeping in the three weeks since the stars went out, and watched the columns of ash rise from the foundry district not upward but *inward* — bending, all of them together, toward a single point above the old warden gate at the city's heart, as though the sky itself were a throat and Ashfall's burning was a swallow.

Her second-in-command, Ferren, came up behind her with his coat buttoned wrong. She didn't mention it. There had been no time for anyone to button anything correctly since the wards fell.

"Reports," he said. "The Lantern Quarter's gone quiet. Not dark — quiet. People are standing in their doorways smiling. The fish market opened at dawn and nobody sold anything; they just gave it away and forgot they'd done it."

"Forgetting," Voss said. "Always forgetting."

"A woman in Cordwainer's Row called the witch-finders herself. Said her son wasn't hers. Said he'd come to her door that morning and claimed her name and she couldn't —" Ferren stopped. His jaw worked. "He's four years old, Captain. She turned him out."

Voss said nothing. Below them the city went on being a city — carts, cries, the smell of bread and tar — but it had begun to feel like a performance staged for an audience that was deciding whether to applaud or eat the players.

"The order," Ferren said carefully. "From the Crown. It came by rider an hour ago."

"I know what it says."

"All forces to the warden gate. Hold it against the rebels." He hesitated. "It doesn't say anything about the Hollow."

"No," Voss agreed. "It doesn't say anything about the Hollow. Because the Crown still believes the Hollow is a story we tell to keep witches obedient, and that if we hold the gate, the story will politely go away." She turned the sealed order over in her gloved hand, feeling its wax like a scab. "And if we hold the gate while the city forgets its own children, we will have held a door in a house that is already on fire."

"Then what do you want me to do with this?"

Voss looked out at the smoke bending inward, at the beautiful doomed geometry of it, and thought of the Ishikawa boy — the ledger entry she had read a hundred times now, the last of a bloodline built to be burned as fuel. She had offered him a cage with clean sheets. He had chosen the open dark instead. She could not even blame him.

"Put it in my desk," she said. "Unread."

"Captain—"

"If I read it, some part of me will obey it. Put it in the drawer, Ferren, and bring me every finder who can still walk. We're not holding the gate today." She pulled her gloves tighter. "We're holding the city."

* * *

Marguerite felt Ashfall before he saw it.

For three days he and Ivo had traveled hard from the warden seat, and for three days the thing beneath the land had been moving — not the slow tidal breathing he had learned to hear through ice and soil, but something faster now, something with direction. Hunger with an address. On the morning of the fourth day, cresting the ridge road where the pilgrims used to hang ribbons, Marguerite looked down at the sprawl of Ashfall under its bruise-colored dawn and felt the Hollow's attention settle over the city like a hand closing.

"It's going there because of you," Ivo said quietly. "You walked into its borderland and refused it. Things that refuse taste better than things that never knew they were food."

"Then it should be following me."

"You're going to the same place." Ivo drifted at the edge of the path, translucent even in daylight now, thinner than he'd been — Marguerite tried not to measure him each morning the way one measures a candle. "You can't outrun your own name, heir. You never could."

They came down through the eastern terraces where Marguerite had once, in another life, sold luck-knots to dockworkers, and the city received them like a body receives a fever — everything familiar and nothing right. The bells tolled without ringers. Shop doors stood open onto empty rooms with meals still warm on tables, abandoned mid-bite by people who had simply wandered outside to smile at nothing. And everywhere, everywhere, the forgetting: a man painting a sign and repainting the same letter over and over; two women walking hand in hand past each other three times without recognition; a child crying alone on a corner while forty adults passed, none of them able to remember why a crying child should matter to anyone.

Marguerite stopped in front of a chandler's shop whose window he knew. Inside, the chandler's wife — Maren, who bought a charm against rheumatism every solstice and always haggled, always won — stood among her candles with her hands folded, serene as a saint, watching her husband across the street with polite bafflement.

"Maren," Marguerite said, pushing the door open. "Maren, it's me. You buy charms. You cheat me on the price every year."

She smiled at him with the warm blankness of the village on the road, and something in Marguerite's chest tore along an old seam. He dug in his satchel, past the journal, past the iron signet, and pulled out the last of his stock — knots and woven tokens, small stupid hopeful things — and pressed one into her hands. A luck-knot. Red thread. He'd made it the week before the stars died, back when the worst thing in the world was rent.

"Hold this," he said. "Hold it and think about your husband's name. Just the name. That's all."

Her fingers closed around the thread. For one heartbeat — one — her face moved. The serenity cracked like ice over water, and underneath it Maren surfaced, terrified, drowning, mouthing *Tomas*, *Tomas*, *I can't find his name* — and then the knot warmed between her palms, fed on the scrap of craft in it, and she steadied. Not healed. Anchored.

"Tomas," she repeated, like a woman learning a word in a foreign tongue. But she held the knot with both hands now, knuckles white.

Marguerite stepped back out onto the street and found that others had gathered. Word moved strangely through a forgetting city — not by speech, which kept failing, but by sight. They had seen. Dockworkers he remembered by their hands; the baker's boy; the ferry-woman with the silver earring. They stood in the street holding their old charms — half of them frayed, years out of date, bought in seasons he barely recalled selling — and they held them up the way soldiers hold shields, the way drowning men hold rope.

"They don't know why," Ivo said softly beside him. "Most of them couldn't tell you what the charms are for. But they know the charms are *theirs*. Memory is the first thing the Hollow takes, heir, and so whatever memory clings to — thread, iron, a song, a name written on a wall — becomes holy. Your little knots are the only prayers this city has left."

Marguerite's throat was tight. "They'll burn. Thread and hope, against that." He nodded up at the sky, where the smoke bent inward toward the gate, toward the darkness gathering there like a pupil dilating.

"Yes," Ivo said. "But look at them anyway."

Marguerite looked. Forty people in the street below the chandler's shop, holding red thread against the end of the world, and not one of them running.

"Take me to the gate," he said. "Whatever the Hollow wants there — it wants it badly enough to stop eating the rest of the city until it gets it. Which means someone else is already at that door."

* * *

Odile Ferrant had waited eleven years for the warden gate to open, and it was finally, gloriously, coming apart in her hands.

The gatehouse was older than the Crown, older than the city walls that had grown around it like scar tissue — a black arch of fused stone that no mason could cut and no fire could mark, sealed for four centuries by the blood-tithe of the wardens. Around it, in the great vaulted chamber beneath the citadel's roots, the Cabal of the Open Door had spent three days and six lives preparing. Six members who had volunteered to stand in the working-circle when the counter-wards bit. Their names were chalked on the floor in Odile's handwriting. She would remember them. Remembering was the whole point of her.

Now the Hollow pressed against the far side of the arch, and the arch sang — a low note felt in the teeth, in the marrow — and hairline light crawled through the stone like dawn through a shutter.

"It knows we're here," breathed Corvin, her strongest hand, sweat shining on his shaved head. The ritual knives lay ready in their salt. "It's pushing. Saint's mercy, Odile, it's *pushing*."

"Of course it's pushing." Odile walked the circle's edge, checking each anchor-candle with a teacher's patience. Above them the whole city shook with distant screaming and the strange inward rain of ash, and here in the deep it sounded only like surf. "Everything hungry pushes at a door. That's not malice, Corvin. That's just appetite meeting an obstacle." She touched the chalked names. "The wardens starved it for four hundred years and called the starving virtue. We're going to feed it and call it freedom, and either way, someone pays — but it will not be children chosen by their blood. Never again that."

"And the Ishikawa?"

The question hung. Odile let it hang, because lying outright to Corvin sat poorly even now, even for this.

"The rite requires an heir's consent," she said. "Blood freely given opens the Door cleanly. Blood taken opens it ragged — and a ragged opening might swallow the whole quarter." She straightened. "So we find him. And we persuade him. And if persuasion fails—" She looked at the arch, at the light crawling through it, at the shape of the hunger beyond. "Corvin, I buried a daughter because the tithe took her when she was nine. I have watched this city kneel to a machine made of other people's lives. Whatever I have to become to end that, I'll be it. Write that down somewhere. Someone should remember me accurately."

A runner burst in from the stairwell then, breathless, gray-faced: "Witch-finders on the surface road. Voss herself. They're not attacking the gatehouse — they've formed ranks *around* it. Around us. And there's a crowd coming behind them, hundreds, civilians, carrying — carrying thread, madam. Charms. Red thread."

Odile closed her eyes. Above, faintly, through fathoms of stone, she heard the bells tolling wrong again.

"So the city comes to watch," she said. "Good. Let them see the Door open."

* * *

Serane Voss had commanded troops through plague riots and grain riots and the long ugly winter of the dock strikes, but she had never commanded anything like this: a battle line of witch-finders standing not against a crowd but *with* it, shoulder to shoulder with fishermen and grandmothers, everyone facing inward — facing the citadel, the gatehouse, the singing dark — while the sky rained ash that fell sideways.

The crowd did not fight them. That was the uncanny part. When the finders arrived with drawn blades, the people simply parted to let them through and then closed ranks again behind them, as though the soldiers had been expected, as though everyone in Ashfall had independently understood that today the enemy stood in only one direction.

A woman near the barricade line caught Voss's eye. She was perhaps sixty, apron dusted with flour, holding a frayed red knot in both fists.

"You're the witch-captain," the woman said. Not accusingly. Almost kindly.

"I am."

"My mother bought charms off a boy at the river market. Little things. Silly little things." The woman held up the knot. "When they started forgetting, mine held. Don't ask me how I know it held — I just know. Like knowing you've paid a debt." Her chin trembled once and steadied. "Whoever that boy is, wherever he is — you tell your soldiers not to shoot him."

Voss held her gaze. "If I find him," she said, "I intend to put a sword in his hand, not through him. Whether he thanks me for it is another matter."

Above the gatehouse, the black arch of the old warden gate became visible over the rooftops, and it was glowing — veins of pale light pulsing in time with a sound like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Voss had studied the wardens' records for twenty years. She knew what that light meant. She knew what it cost to make it, and whose blood had made it for four hundred years, and she knew — with the cold clarity that had carried her through every promotion and every compromise — that in a few minutes, one way or another, that door was going to open.

Her orders sat unread in her desk across the city. Good. At least one version of Serane Voss could still claim innocence.

"Hold the square," she told Ferren. "Not against the crowd. Against whatever comes out of that gate. And Ferren—" she drew her saber, an old thing, her father's, "—if I fall inside, burn my orders. All of them. Every order I ever wrote. Let history think I acted alone."

* * *

Marguerite reached the citadel square as the arch began to fail.

He came through streets he had walked a thousand times, flanked now by strangers who had decided — without asking him, which was somehow worse and better — that the charm-seller belonged at the front of them. Ivo walked beside him, visible now to everyone, flickering like a lamp in wind, and no one screamed. In a city full of ghosts returning from the dead, one more caused no stir.

At the mouth of the square the crowd thickened into the witch-finders' line, and for one absurd moment Marguerite stood face to face with Captain Serane Voss again, her saber drawn, her face smudged with ash.

"You," she said.

"You followed me."

"I followed the disaster. You merely arrived slightly ahead of it." Her eyes went to Ivo, to the signet on Marguerite's hand, to the crowd at his back holding their threads. Something shifted in her expression — not softening exactly. Recalculation. "The Cabal is under the gatehouse. They have an heir-consent rite prepared and no heir, which means within the hour they will decide consent is a detail. I have thirty finders and a city full of civilians between me and that door. I am formally deputizing every person in this square, starting with you."

"I'm not yours to deputize."

"No," Voss agreed. "But you're Ashfall's. Look at them, Ishikawa. They're holding your thread."

Marguerite looked. Hundreds of faces in the ash-light, red knots raised against the dark, and beneath his ribs he felt the Hollow answer them — feel them, the way a sleeper feels a hand on the blanket — and understood that the small kindnesses he'd sold for coppers had become, tonight, the only architecture left standing between a city and its erasure.

The arch above the gatehouse cracked like thunder.

Light poured through — not the pale ward-light but something deeper, a color with no name, and through the widening seam came a voice made of every voice the Hollow had ever eaten, and Marguerite knew it instantly, horribly, because it wore his master's cadence and it was calling his name.

Down in the chamber beneath, Odile Ferrant began the rite.

Marguerite ran.
