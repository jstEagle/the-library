## 15. Odile's Faith

The gatehouse had been a chapel once, before the wardens took it.

Odile Ferrant stood in the nave where the altar used to be and felt the old stones remembering. That was the thing about warden-work: it soaked into everything it touched, the way lamp-oil soaks into a floorboard, so that centuries later you could still strike a spark from the grain. The walls here were thick with it — layered wards laid down by dead hands, generation after generation, each one fed on blood that had never been asked.

She ran her palm along the eastern pillar, where the sigils spiraled inward like the whorl of an ear. Listening. The Hollow pressed against the far side of them, patient as tide against a seawall, and beneath her hand she felt the faint wrongness of its attention — a coldness with curiosity in it, like being watched through ice.

*Soon*, she thought. *Soon we'll let you in, and you'll be done with hunger.*

Behind her, the Open Door was making itself at home. They had taken the gatehouse at dusk, eleven of them against four Crown wardens who had not been paid since the stars went out and who surrendered their keys for a promise of passage south. Now the great iron doors stood barred from within, and candlelight moved across the vaulted ceiling, and someone was singing — Perrin, always Perrin, his voice cracked and sweet, an old harvest song about the year turning. The others had spread their maps and instruments across the vestry tables: brass rings, silvered mirrors, jars of ink mixed with ash and salt. Everything they would need to open a door that had been shut for four hundred years.

It should have felt like triumph. It felt instead like standing in her mother's kitchen again, waiting for something inevitable.

"Odile." Maren appeared at her elbow, breathless, cheeks pink from the stairs. "Scout's back. The Ishikawa boy was seen two days ago on the north road, traveling with—" she hesitated, and lowered her voice as though the walls might repeat it, "—with someone. A man nobody else could see. The scout swears he talked to empty air."

Odile closed her eyes. *A ghost.* Of course. The last warden's line would not come to its ending unattended; the dead of that family clung like burrs. "Where did the trail go?"

"Toward the seat. Toward us." Maren smiled, and there was hunger in it too, though hers was only hope. "He's coming to us, Odile. He doesn't even know it yet."

"He knows," Odile said quietly. "He just doesn't know why."

She left Maren to the maps and climbed the spiral stair to the gatehouse parapet, because she wanted to see the sky, and because she did not want anyone watching her face while she did what she had learned to do up here, alone, every night since they'd taken the place: remember.

The stars were still gone. Eleven nights now, and the dark above Ashfall was a smooth black lacquer, unbroken, as if the whole dome of heaven had been painted over. People said they were getting used to it. People lied beautifully when they were afraid.

Her mother had loved the stars. That was where the memory always started — not with the tithe, not with the wagons, but with her mother on the roof of the dyer's house in Vell, pointing up with a wet brush still in her hand, indigo staining the sky-blue sleeve, saying, *That one's yours, little door. I named it for you the night you were born.* The star had been called the Doorway by the astronomers, a faint blue point in the autumn constellation of the Ferryman, and her mother — who named everything, who named the cat and the well and the crooked chimney — had given it to her third daughter as casually as giving bread.

Odile had been nine years old the year the tithe came for Vell.

The old wards had needed feeding the way a fire needs wood, and the Ishikawa wardens had kept the ledger of it: which villages owed, how much, how often. Not blood, not usually — the tithe was subtler than that. Memory, mostly. Years of it. The wardens came with their iron rings and their quiet voices and they measured out lives like apothecaries measuring medicine, and everyone in Vell understood that it was necessary, that the alternative was the Hollow walking in off the moors, and so they lined up in the market square and held out their hands.

Her mother gave three years. Her father gave five. Her eldest brother, Corvin, who was nineteen and strong and had been told his whole life that the strong owe more, gave ten.

And her sister Betha — Betha, who was seven, who was small enough that the wardens' scales barely tipped for her — Betha gave everything she had, because children's memories were *richer*, the wardens said, denser, each year of a child worth three of an adult, and the border had been failing that autumn, and the ledger demanded more than usual, and someone in some office far away had signed the requisition in a neat hand.

Betha had gone to sleep in the wagon afterward, smiling, holding Odile's hand, and by morning she did not know her. By morning Betha did not know her own name. She sat in the cart among the other emptied children like dolls whose stuffing had been drawn out, and when their mother climbed in after her and said *Betha, sweetheart, it's Mama*, the girl had looked at her with polite, blank courtesy — the look you give a stranger at a market stall — and asked if the lady was the baker's wife.

Her mother had made a sound Odile had never heard before or since, and never wanted to hear again. It was not screaming. Screaming would have been easier. It was the sound of a woman discovering that grief has a floor below grief.

They had kept Betha for six years after that. Fed her, dressed her, loved her with a love she could not return or even perceive. She died at thirteen of a fever any child should have shaken off, and at the funeral Odile's mother had turned to the sky — to the hole in it, even then, the places where the wards had eaten thin — and said, in a voice of terrible calm:

*Someone is going to open that door someday. And I will hold it open myself.*

Her mother had not lived to do it. But Odile had inherited the intention the way other people inherited land.

She gripped the parapet stone until her knuckles ached. Below her, the moorland stretched away toward the horizon, and somewhere out in that darkness the Hollow moved like water under ice, and she was not afraid of it. That was the thing no one understood, the thing Serane Voss and her witch-finders would never grasp no matter how many ledgers they burned: Odile was not afraid of the Hollow because she had already been hollowed. She knew exactly what it took. And she knew — this was her faith, her one article of it, held the way the starving hold bread — that the thing which had emptied her sister was not evil. It was hungry. Hunger could be bargained with. Hunger could be *fed*. It was the wardens who were evil, the wardens who had built a machine that ate children's souls by weight and called it duty, the wardens whose last heir was even now walking toward this gatehouse carrying four hundred years of stolen years in his veins.

Let the Door open. Let the Hollow come through and take what it needed openly, honestly, in the light — and then be satisfied, or leave, or become part of the world the way weather was part of the world. Anything was better than the ledger. Anything was better than a mother kneeling in a cart beside a daughter who had forgotten her face.

*We are not opening a wound*, she told herself, the way she told herself every night. *We are lancing one.*

Footsteps on the stair. Maren again, and behind her Perrin, and behind Perrin the whole committee of them, faces candlelit and solemn, because they had all agreed long ago that nothing would be decided without her — she was the reason for the Open Door, its founding grief, and they honored that with a formality that sometimes felt like worship and sometimes felt like blame.

"The rite," Maren began, and stopped, and looked at Perrin, who looked at the floor, and Odile felt the shape of the problem arrive before either of them spoke it.

"Say it," she said.

"The rite needs a warden's consent," Perrin said. "Not just blood. We've been over the texts a hundred times. The gate answers to the Ishikawa name — spoken freely, witnessed, willing. Blood alone won't turn the lock. Blood alone just... calls to it. Like knocking." He swallowed. "And the boy won't consent. Voss's reports say he's been running from his own name since he was a child. He'll refuse. He'll die refusing, if it comes to that."

The wind came up over the parapet and stirred the candles, and for a moment the shadows on the wall leapt and bent like figures dancing at a wake.

"Then we make him willing," Odile said.

Maren flinched. "Odile—"

"No. Listen to me." She turned to face them, and she kept her voice level, because she had learned long ago that conviction spoken softly traveled farther than conviction shouted. "I know what you're thinking. You're thinking of the forged writs, the false ledgers, the tricks we've used on Crown clerks. You're thinking we'll write his consent down and hold his hand to the paper and call it done. It won't work. The gate isn't a clerk. It will taste the lie in the ink." She paused. "But consent can be *earned*. Or shaped. Or — if it must be — manufactured so thoroughly that the difference stops mattering."

Perrin's brow furrowed. "How do you manufacture willingness?"

"You find what a man wants most," Odile said, "and you put it on the other side of the door."

She thought of the reports they had gathered on Marguerite Ishikawa: the charm-seller, the runaway, the boy raised in hiding by a master who had broken the law of four centuries to spare him. A boy who had never belonged anywhere, who sold luck-knots to dockworkers and slept above a chandler's shop, who had watched his whole inheritance burn black in a pouch on the night the stars went out. A boy, in short, who was lonely in the specific, bottomless way of people who have been hidden — loved, perhaps, but hidden — and who did not know yet that there were others like him, others the wards had maimed, waiting for him with open hands.

"We offer him the truth first," she said. "All of it. My truth, too — my sister, the ledger, the scale in the market square. He deserves to hear it from me, not from a pamphlet. We show him what his family did. We show him Betha." Her voice caught, very slightly, and she let it; honesty was a tool like any other, and the best tools had edges. "And then we ask him. Freely. With witnesses. If he says yes, then everything we do afterward is clean."

"And if he says no?" Maren asked.

Odile looked out over the dark moor, where the Hollow waited under the ice of the world, patient, enormous, and — she believed this with her whole scarred heart — *willing*, if only someone would stop barring the way.

"If he says no," she said, "then we keep asking. We give him time, and shelter, and reasons. We show him that refusal means Voss gets him instead — a furnace for the Crown, chained to the wards until his blood runs out. Refusal means the Cabal's work dies and the ledger lives and some other seven-year-old in some other village climbs into a wagon with her whole life measured out in years. There is no version of 'no' that saves anyone, Marguerite Ishikawa included. Once he sees that clearly—" she shrugged, and hated herself, very briefly and very deeply, for how easily the shrug came— "he'll consent. Or he'll consent to the lesser cage. Either way, the gate opens."

"That's coercion," Perrin said quietly. Not accusing. Just naming it, the way you'd name a storm coming over the hills.

"It's triage," Odile answered. "When the house is burning, you don't ask permission to break the windows."

No one argued. That was the privilege and the curse of founding something: your faith became their faith, your grief became their banner, and after a while no one remembered that the banner had been sewn by one pair of bleeding hands. They bowed, one by one, and went down the stair to prepare — the circle-chalk, the witness-robes, the guest room they would make warm and bright for the heir they intended to catch.

Odile stayed on the parapet a while longer.

She took from around her neck the small tin locket she wore under her shirt, and opened it, and looked at the scrap of dyed cloth inside — indigo, her mother's indigo, cut from a sleeve on the morning they buried Betha. *That one's yours, little door,* her mother had said, pointing at a star that no longer existed. Well. The star was gone, but the doorway remained, and its name was written in an Ishikawa's blood, and Odile Ferrant meant to knock until it opened.

Below, in the dark, a sentry's whistle sounded — twice, then once more, the signal for movement on the north road.

She closed the locket. She did not pray; she had stopped praying the day the wardens' scales tipped for her sister. But she stood a moment with her hand flat on the ancient stone, feeling the Hollow press close on the other side of the wall, cold and curious and near, and she whispered to it as she might have whispered to Betha in the wagon, all those years ago:

"Soon. Hold on. I'm coming with the key."

Then she went down to open the door for Marguerite Ishikawa — smiling, sincere, certain, and already deciding which of his chains she would gild first.
