## 16. The Conscription of Names

They took him at the ford.

Marguerite would remember afterward how ordinary it looked — six figures in river-gray cloaks rising out of the reeds like herons startled into human shape, no shouting, no violence, just hands closing on his arms with the gentle efficiency of people who had done this many times and were sorry about it. The Open Door did not believe in cruelty. That was the thing he could never make himself forget. They were kind the way a well is kind: deep, patient, and utterly indifferent to what they swallowed.

He did not fight. Partly because there were six of them and one of him, and his arms still bore the pale scars of the warden seat where the world had torn open under his hands. But partly — and this was the part that shamed him — because some exhausted corner of him had been waiting to be found. Three weeks of sleeping in ditches with Ivo's absence lying beside him like a second body. Three weeks of hearing the Hollow move beneath the land, closer every night, a tide coming up through the roots of things. A man can only run from so many directions before being caught starts to feel like rest.

*You let them take you,* said the voice in his head that sounded like his master and was only his own conscience wearing a dead man's patience.

*I let them take me,* he agreed, as they bound his wrists with silk — silk, of course; the Open Door had money now, gatehouse money, seized-arsenal money — and led him north along the water in the blue hour before dawn.

The camp sat in a limestone quarry abandoned half a century ago, its terraces hung with lanterns of green glass that turned everyone who walked beneath them the color of drowned things. Odile Ferrant came down the switchback road to meet him personally, which was a courtesy, or a performance, or both. She wore her gray hair cropped short these days and a ring of keys at her belt like a chatelaine of a house that happened to be a fortress. Her smile was exactly as he remembered it from the safehouse in Ashfall: warm, unhurried, absolutely certain.

"Marguerite Ishikawa." She said the name the way other people said *your majesty* — with weight, with ceremony, with the faint suggestion that the name mattered more than the person carrying it. "I'm glad you're alive. I mean that. Whatever you've heard about us since you ran."

"I didn't run," Marguerite said. His voice came out flat and cracked, a door left too long in the rain. "I walked away. There's a difference."

"There is," she conceded. "And I'd like to hear all of it. Over tea. Over food. You look half-starved." She gestured, and the guards — escorts, captors, hosts; the words kept trading places — walked him down into the quarry's heart, where the old cutting floor had been roofed with canvas and warmed with braziers, and the smell of bread was being used, deliberately, expertly, as an argument.

That was the trap of Odile Ferrant, Marguerite thought, sitting on a real chair for the first time in weeks while someone put a bowl of barley soup in his scarred hands. Not the threat of her. The comfort. Every cage the Open Door built was lined with kindness, so that when you finally understood you were inside one, you couldn't remember whether you'd ever been anywhere else.

* * *

They gave him three days.

Three days of decent food and unguarded doors — doors that were not locked, though men stood near them, which was a distinction without much difference. Three days of Odile visiting each evening to talk, never about the rite, always around it: the fallen wards, the tithe, her family. On the second night she told him about her brother Tomas, conscripted into the warden service at fourteen because the blood-tithe registers had flagged a distant cousin's marriage, and how the wardens had come with their ledger and their lawful faces, and how her mother had fought them and been transported, and how Tomas had gone into the border forts and never come out, because the wards did not give back what they ate.

"He wasn't special," Odile said, turning her cup slowly on its saucer. "He wasn't chosen. He was *counted*. That's all the old system ever did — count children like cordwood and burn them to keep the dark polite. And you want me to weep that the furnace is cold?"

"The alternative isn't warmth," Marguerite said quietly. "I've seen what the Hollow leaves behind. A village where mothers smile at their own children like strangers. It doesn't kill, Odile. It *unmakes*. Is that your open door? A door into a room where nothing remembers anything?"

"I've read your reports of that village." Her cup stopped turning. "I've also read four hundred years of tithe rolls. Show me the difference between a child fed to the wards and a name eaten by the Hollow, and I'll show you two ways of saying *gone*."

It was a good argument. That was the horror of it. He lay awake that night on a real straw mattress under a real blanket and hated how good it was, how it caught at the same grief that had dragged him toward the warden seat with a knife and a prayer. Grief made excellent mortar. He knew, now, exactly what the Hollow knew: that longing was load-bearing.

On the third day they stopped pretending the doors were unlocked.

* * *

The chamber they brought him to had been the quarry's counting-house once, and still had its long slate table, scored with the grooves of abacus rails. Now the table held instruments instead: glass retorts, silver wire, a basin of water black as a pupil. And chalked on the floor, half-finished, was a circle he recognized with a sickness that started in his teeth.

Warden-work. Old warden-work, the geometry of the border forts, the same script he'd spent weeks learning from a ghost who burned a little more each time he taught it. Except the circle had been *edited*. Lines rerouted. Anchors moved outward. Where the old rite drew the Hollow's hunger inward and bound it, this version opened a channel and held it stable — a door propped with mathematics.

"You've been busy," Marguerite said.

"We had help." Odile stood at the head of the table, and for the first time since Ashfall she looked tired, the certainty showing its seams. "A defector from the Crown's archive. And texts your master's order thought were destroyed." She watched him read the circle, and he saw her watching, cataloguing his face the way Voss must have catalogued his file. "You understand what you're looking at."

"A gate," he said. "Stable. Negotiable. You actually think you can bargain with it."

"I think it can be bargained with, yes. I think everything that hungers can be bargained with — hunger is just need, and need has terms. The wardens never asked its terms. They starved it for centuries and called the screaming *order*." She stepped closer. "But here is the part I won't lie to you about, because you've been lied to enough for one life. The gate needs an anchor. Living blood of the warden line. Consent makes the channel clean; refusal makes it turbulent, dangerous, possibly fatal — to you, and to everyone within a mile of this stone."

She paused, and Marguerite felt the floor tilt under the sentence before she finished it.

"But it does not require consent. Blood is blood. The circle will take what it needs whether you open your veins willingly or whether we open them for you. I wanted you to understand that before you decided anything, because I refuse to be the wardens. I refuse to count you like cordwood." Her voice frayed, very slightly, on the last word. "So I am asking. Truly asking. One last time. Stand with us, Marguerite. Be the anchor *with* us, not *for* us. Help us open the Door and ask the Hollow its price, and maybe — maybe — no one else ever has to be eaten again. Your choice. Your actual choice. All I'm telling you is what happens if you choose to say no."

Silence. The brazier ticked. Somewhere above, wind moved across the quarry rim with a sound like a vast page turning.

"And if I say no anyway?" he asked. "If I fight you?"

Odile's face did something complicated — grief and resolve passing through each other like weather fronts. "Then tomorrow night we do it without you, and I will spend the rest of my life knowing I became the thing I swore to destroy. I'd rather have your hate than your corpse, but I'll take both over the end of the world as I understand it." She turned toward the door, then stopped. "There's someone who asked to see you. Someone who surrendered to us four days ago. I let him join because he knows the old craft better than any living soul, and because refusing him seemed crueler than trusting him. Decide for yourself whether I was a fool."

The door opened.

And Ivo walked in.

* * *

Later, Marguerite could never reconstruct what he felt in that first moment. Betrayal arrived, certainly — a white, ringing note of it, the memory of the warden seat tearing open, of learning what Ivo was, a fragment of the Hollow itself, jailer and unwilling spy, every lesson a lesson taught to the enemy. He had turned away from Ivo in the ruins with the taste of ash in his mouth, and Ivo had let him go, translucent and diminished, spending himself like a candle in wind, and Marguerite had believed — had needed to believe — that the ghost had guttered out somewhere in the dark between there and here.

But standing in the doorway of the counting-house, Ivo was not guttered. He was thinner — that was the word Marguerite's mind insisted on, *thinner*, though a ghost could not lose weight — his edges less certain, his outline trembling slightly, like a reflection in water someone had breathed on. He wore Cabal gray, absurdly, a borrowed cloak over borrowed nothing. And his eyes, those eyes that had been patient and wry and old across a hundred lessons in dead magic, were fixed on Marguerite with an expression that had no cunning in it at all. Only a terrible, naked attention. The way a man looks at the one fire left in a frozen country.

"You," Marguerite said. The word came out broken into pieces. "You're *with them*."

"I'm with you," Ivo said. "They're the road."

"Don't." Marguerite's hands curled at his sides; the warden-scarred skin pulled tight. "Don't stand there and — explain yourself in aphorisms. You lied to me from the first wall you stepped out of. You are a piece of the thing that eats names, and every hour you taught me, you taught *it*, and now you're standing in the ranks of the people who want to open its door, and you expect—"

"I expect nothing," Ivo said quietly. "I offered myself to them because they needed a teacher of the old craft, and because they had you, or would soon — Odile has been hunting you since Ashfall, heir, don't imagine the ford was chance — and because the only way to stand between you and that circle was to be inside the room when it's cast." He glanced at the chalked geometry, and something moved across his face that was not quite disgust and not quite grief. "I know every flaw in this working. I helped design its ancestor. When the hour comes, I will use what I know. What happens to me after is not a consideration."

"What happens to you *now* is a consideration," Marguerite snapped, and heard his own voice crack on it, and hated that. "You're barely — you're *thin*, Ivo, you're holding together like frost on a window, whatever you spent at the seat—"

"Half of myself. Yes." A ghost of the old dry smile. "A fair price for your life. I'd pay it again. That's rather the point of prices."

Marguerite turned away. The brazier's heat pressed against his face; the black basin of water reflected nothing useful. Four days, Odile had said. Four days Ivo had been here, among the people who meant to bleed him, teaching them, waiting — why waiting? To reach him. To stand between. And the thought should have been a rope thrown into dark water, and instead it was another hand, and Marguerite flinched from it, because the last hand that had reached for him out of the dark had belonged to the Hollow wearing his master's voice.

"Why?" he said finally, to the brazier, to the circle, to the thin ghost behind him. "Not the strategy. Not the sacrifice arithmetic. Why *you*, why keep choosing this, why does a fragment of the Hollow — a thing cut loose from hunger itself — keep throwing itself between me and every door? What are you, Ivo? Really. Past the jailer story, past the spy story. What are you?"

The silence went on long enough that Marguerite finally turned.

Ivo had gone to the edge of the chalked circle and was looking down at it, and his translucence in the brazier-light made him seem almost young, almost unfinished, a sketch of a man. When he spoke, his voice was lower than Marguerite had ever heard it, worn down to the grain.

"I told you once I remembered a promise made to your grandmother. That was true, and it was also the smallest true thing I could find to tell you." He lifted his head. "Tomorrow night, when they cast this circle, you'll learn the rest — either from me, or from the Hollow pouring through the gate, and I would very much prefer it be from me. So: stay angry at me tonight. Refuse to speak to me. Call me liar, traitor, fragment, spy — all of it earned. But be here tomorrow. Because if you walk away from this quarry, they will follow you to the ends of the earth, and worse, *it* will follow you, and I—" the voice caught, an actual fracture, a ghost's throat failing at its work, "—I cannot follow you everywhere. I am nearly out of everywhere."

Outside, across the terraces of the drowned-green quarry, the first evening lanterns were being lit, one by one, small stubborn flames against a sky where the stars had not yet returned.

And beneath the limestone, beneath the roots, beneath everything — Marguerite felt it turn over in its sleep, attentive, patient, listening — the Hollow began, very softly, to hum.
