## 17. A Third Answer

*MARGUERITE*

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and the dark in it was not the honest dark of night but something manufactured — a darkness with a purpose, poured into the room like tar. The Cabal had warded the walls with chalk sigils that glowed a sickly green when he looked at them too long, and Marguerite had spent what felt like hours mapping them from his corner, tracing their geometry against the inside of his eyelids until he knew them better than his own heartbeat.

They were good wards. Sloppy at the joins, but good.

His wrists still bore the marks of the bindings they'd used to bring him here — rope soaked in something bitter that had sunk into his skin and muted the small warm presence of his craft. He could feel it now like a limb gone to sleep: there, but distant, humming wrong. The Hollow beneath the world was louder than it had ever been, a pressure against the soles of his feet, against the backs of his teeth, like standing on a frozen lake and hearing the ice sing.

And Ivo was coming back. He had to be.

Marguerite pressed his palm flat against the cold floor and tried to breathe the way Ivo had taught him — *down through the ribs, heir, down where the land keeps its secrets* — and hated himself for how much comfort the memory gave him. A thing made to betray. A fragment of the Hollow wearing a dead man's shape, teaching him lessons that fed the enemy with every word.

*Every lesson taught Marguerite also taught the Hollow.*

He turned the thought over and over, looking for the wound in it, the place where it would stop hurting if he pressed hard enough. It didn't stop. But somewhere around the hundredth turning, another thought arrived alongside it, quiet and unwelcome as a cat at a door:

*Ivo came back.*

Not the Hollow's fragment. Not the jailer-spy doing its ancient work. Ivo — the specific, stubborn, infuriating ghost who had stepped out of a wall in the flooded undercity and called him *heir* — had walked into the Cabal's ranks knowing they would use him, knowing Marguerite might never forgive him, because the alternative was letting them take Marguerite without anyone watching.

A spy did not do that. A spy reported. A spy waited for orders.

Whatever Ivo was, he had chosen something the Hollow had never asked of him.

Marguerite sat with that in the green-lit dark, and slowly, the way frost forms on glass, an idea began to build itself in the space between his grief and his anger. He didn't name it yet. He only held it up to the light of everything he knew — the journal, the rite at the warden seat, the village that had forgotten its children, the door in the dark that wore his master's voice — and let it grow.

The wards were a prison. The tithe was slavery. Sealing cost memory; feeding cost everything else. Every answer anyone had ever offered him was a cage with different bars.

But a prison needed walls because the prisoner was kept *outside*.

What if nothing was outside?

Footsteps in the corridor. Not the heavy boots of the Cabal guards — lighter, almost hesitant, though ghosts made no sound at all unless they wanted to. The chalk sigils flared, guttered, and went out one by one along the wall like candles being pinched by a careful hand.

Ivo stepped through the stone.

He looked worse than Marguerite had ever seen him, and Ivo had once spent half of himself hauling Marguerite back from a wound in the world. His edges frayed like smoke off a snuffed wick; his left hand was translucent past the wrist, the bones showing through as faint silver lines. When he saw Marguerite sitting upright in the corner, waiting, something crossed his face that no fragment of hunger should have been able to wear.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Ivo said.

"So are you, technically."

A ghost of a laugh — literally, Marguerite thought, a ghost of a laugh, given away like the last coin in a purse. Ivo lowered himself to the floor across from him, folding his ruined body down with the care of someone handling a borrowed coat.

"They'll come for you at dawn," Ivo said. "Odile wants the rite begun while the gate is still open on her side. They don't need your consent. They need your blood and your name, and they have both."

"I know." Marguerite drew his knees up. "I've been thinking about names."

Ivo went very still. Ghosts, Marguerite had learned, could be stiller than the living — stillness was their native country — but this was different. This was the stillness of a man hearing a floorboard creak above a grave.

"Marguerite—"

"The wards are a wall," Marguerite said. "Four hundred years of Ishikawas dying to hold a wall, and everyone agrees the wall is the only shape the answer can take. Seal it or feed it. Prison or furnace. But walls exist to keep something *out*, Ivo. And the Hollow isn't out. It's under. It's *in* — it's under everything, it's under the land itself, it's been under us since before the first ward was ever drawn." He leaned forward. "You told me that yourself. Lesson three. You said the Hollow doesn't move toward things. It moves *beneath* them. So why are we building walls?"

"Because walls are all we have."

"No," Marguerite said quietly. "Walls are all we've *tried*. There's a third thing. Nobody's ever dared it because it costs exactly what nobody wanted to pay." His heart was hammering now, the idea fully formed and terrifying in the light. "A living ward. Not a bloodline, not a prison, not a gate. A person. A name. Bind the Hollow into a single human life — not sealed outside it, but carried *inside* it, woven into the name the way a keystone is woven into an arch. Mortal. Free. No tithe, no dynasty, no lie. The ward walks around, drinks tea, sells luck-knots, grows old, dies naturally — and the binding dies with it, gently, instead of cracking like a dam."

Ivo's face had gone grey — or greyer. "That's not possible."

"It is. I felt it at the warden seat, when the rite tore open. The Hollow *tasted my name*. It knows the shape of me. That's the flaw in every ward ever built — they're anonymous. Impersonal. The Hollow pushes against them because they mean nothing to it. But a name—" He swallowed. "A name is a door that can be closed from the inside. You taught me that too. Lesson nine. *All magic is hospitality, heir. You are always inviting something in.*"

"That lesson," Ivo said, very softly, "was meant to keep you from doing exactly this."

"Well," Marguerite said, "you should have lied better."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone and earth, Ashfall burned — he could feel it now, the city's fear rising like heat, the Hollow pressing upward toward all that rich, sweet terror. The Cabal thought they were opening a door. The Crown thought they were restoring a wall. And in a cell underneath both armies, a runaway charm-seller and a lying ghost were about to commit the most forbidden act of magic in four hundred years.

"There's a price," Ivo said at last. His voice had changed — flatter, careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he knew was thin. "There's always a price. What does your version cost?"

Marguerite had done the arithmetic already. He'd done it in the dark, alone, with nothing but his own dread for company, and the answer had sat in his chest like a swallowed stone ever since.

"The bearer gets erased," he said. "From every memory but one. The binding needs a vessel that belongs to no one — no lineage, no debts, no one's son, no one's student. The magic will unpick him from the world's remembering. People will look at him and see a stranger. His name will slide off them like water off oil." He met Ivo's eyes. "One person remembers. One anchor. The spell needs a single thread left uncut, or the name unravels entirely. One witness, holding the whole weight of him, for as long as the ward lives."

"And after?" Ivo asked. "When the bearer dies?"

"Then it has to be done again. Someone volunteers. Freely. That's the whole point — no inheritance, no conscription. Just someone, each generation, choosing to carry it." Marguerite laughed, and it came out cracked. "It's insane. It's the worst idea either of us has ever had. It requires a fragment of the Hollow willing to serve as the keystone, which means it requires *you*, and I have no right to ask, and you have every reason to refuse."

Ivo looked at him for a long time. The green light had died completely now; only the faint silver of Ivo's fading substance lit the cell, and in that light his expression was unreadable as weather.

"You've known about this plan," Ivo said, "for less than an hour."

"I've known about it since the moment you walked into the Cabal's ranks to reach me. I just hadn't written it down yet."

"Mm." Ivo closed his eyes. When he opened them again, something had shifted behind them — some decision arriving, or some old promise finally finding its hour. "Then we have until dawn to do the impossible. Give me your hand, heir. We start with the grammar of the binding, and we work backward from the price."

* * *

*IVO*

He had taught perhaps forty students in his long, strange existence, and none of them had ever frightened him the way this one did — not because Marguerite was reckless, though he was, but because he was *right*. That was the terror of him. Four centuries of warden doctrine, and this half-starved runaway with ink-stained fingers had found the crack in all of it in a single night, alone, in a cell, armed with nothing but grief and arithmetic.

Ivo took his hand. The living warmth of it hurt, the way sunlight hurts things that have lived in cellars. He anchored himself to that warmth and began.

"The binding has three movements," he said. "Say them back to me."

"Invitation. Weaving. Naming."

"Good. Invitation — the Hollow must be asked, not forced. That's the part no warden ever understood. Every rite in the journal treats it as an enemy to be bound by strength, and strength just makes it push harder. But it responds to invitation. It always has. Why do you think it wears the voices of people you loved? It learned longing before it learned anything else. If the bearer opens — truly opens, willingly, knowing the cost — the Hollow can be drawn in gently, like drawing a splinter."

"And weaving?"

"Weaving is the craft. The sigils go on the bearer's skin, but they aren't anchors — they're *threads*. Each one ties a piece of the Hollow to a piece of the name. Its hunger tied to his courage. Its patience tied to his stubbornness—" Ivo paused. "Its loneliness tied to his heart. The Hollow is not evil, Marguerite. It is starving, and it has been starving since before stars had names. A ward that feeds it nothing will fail. A ward that feeds it *company*—"

"Will hold," Marguerite finished. His voice had gone rough. "It holds because it's not a prison. It's a home."

"Yes." Ivo looked away, at the blank wall, because looking at Marguerite just then was more than his remaining substance could bear. "And naming is the price. The bearer's name becomes the arch. Every memory of him in every mind but one is drawn into the binding as mortar. He becomes a stranger to the world — beloved by no one, mourned by no one, remembered by one. The one who holds the thread."

"Who holds the thread, Ivo?"

The question hung in the dark between them, and Ivo understood, with the terrible clarity available only to things that were ending, that Marguerite already knew the answer. Had known it since he'd started speaking. Was asking anyway, because he was kind, and kindness sometimes demanded that a person be given the chance to say the unbearable thing aloud rather than have it assumed.

"The anchor must be willing," Ivo said. "And the anchor must be able to hold the weight. And the anchor must—" His voice failed, frayed like his hand. He gathered it. "The anchor must be bound to the bearer already. By blood-rite, by ring, by oath. There is exactly one such anchor in the world, Marguerite, and he is currently running out of himself at a rate that will leave nothing to weave with by midsummer even if he survives tonight."

"No."

"It has to be—"

"*No.*" Marguerite was on his feet, and the small suppressed warmth of his craft flared suddenly through the Cabal's dampening ropes, green sigils stuttering back to life along the walls. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to spend yourself *again*. First the lessons, then the warden seat, and now — what, you dissolve into my name and I walk around for the rest of my life carrying the Hollow and the ghost of you and remembering *everything* while no one remembers *me*? Do you understand what you're offering? You're offering to be the one person who remembers me, and then to *stop existing*, and I'm supposed to just—"

"Yes," Ivo said simply.

The word landed between them like a dropped blade.

"I made a promise to your grandmother," Ivo said, quieter now. "The night she hid you. She was dying, and she was afraid, and she made me swear that the chain would end with you — that no Ishikawa would ever again be born into a furnace. I have kept that promise badly, heir. I kept you hidden, yes, but I let the wards fall, I let the Cabal find you, I let Voss find you, I let my own nature betray you. This—" He lifted his ruined hand, translucent to the elbow now, silver bones like a fish's spine. "This is the only way I have left to keep it. Not as a jailer. Not as a spy. As a choice. Do you understand? Everything I have ever been, I was *made* to be. This is the first thing I will ever have *chosen*."

Marguerite stood over him, shaking, and Ivo watched him fight it — watched the fury and the grief and the terrible tenderness war across his face like weather over the sea. And Ivo, who had worn a dead man's shape for longer than empires lasted, understood in that moment that he had never once, in all his long existence, been seen as clearly as he was being seen right now, by this boy, in this cell, in the wreckage of every plan either of them had ever trusted.

"If we do this," Marguerite said finally, hoarsely, "you tell me the truth about everything. All of it. What you are, what the Hollow is, what my grandmother said, all of it, before the rite. I won't build a home for something I don't understand."

"All of it," Ivo agreed.

"And you let me decide the last movement of the rite myself. Whatever it is. Whatever it costs."

"All of it," Ivo said again, and meant it, and knew that meaning it was the bravest and most foolish thing he had ever done.

Marguerite sat back down. Took his hand again. Outside, far above, the sky over Ashfall was burning, and the bells were tolling wrong again — thirteen times, fourteen, fifteen, counting toward something.

"Now," Marguerite said. "Teach me the weaving. We have until dawn."

* * *

*MARGUERITE*

They worked through the black hours, and it was the strangest night of his life — stranger than the night the stars died, stranger than the door in the dark. Two fugitives in a cell, building salvation out of chalk dust and stolen knowledge, their voices low, their hands moving in the air between them like two people braiding something invisible.

Ivo taught. Marguerite learned. And this time, for the first time, every lesson cost the Hollow nothing — because these lessons weren't leaking anywhere. They were being spent entirely, extravagantly, on him.

By the time the first grey hint of dawn crept under the door, the design was complete. Three movements. Nine threads. One anchor. One bearer. One witness, holding the whole weight of a name.

Marguerite flexed his fingers, feeling the dampening ropes loosen as the Cabal's guards changed shift somewhere above. His arms ached from tracing sigils in the air for six hours. His throat was raw. His chest felt like a cathedral with all its doors thrown open.

Above them, boots. Many boots, descending.

"They're coming," Ivo said, rising, his form so thin now that the torchlight from the corridor showed through him. "Odile will begin within the hour. The gate is open. The Hollow is pouring toward it."

Marguerite stood. In the torchlight spilling through the opening door, he could see the sigils he'd traced on his own forearms during the night — faint, provisional, waiting — and beneath his ribs, deep beneath, the vast slow attention of the thing that had eaten the stars, turning toward him like a sleeper hearing a familiar voice.

"Then we'd better not keep it waiting," he said, and walked toward the door, and the boots, and the burning of the world.

Behind him, so softly he almost missed it, Ivo whispered words in a language older than wards — and Marguerite realized, with a jolt, that they were not spells at all.

They were a promise.
