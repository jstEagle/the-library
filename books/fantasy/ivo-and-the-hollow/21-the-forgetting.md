## 21. The Forgetting

The first thing to go was the sound of his voice.

Marguerite noticed it on the fourth morning after the gatehouse, standing in the ruins of Voss's borrowed quarters with a cup of tea going cold in his hands. He had been trying to remember how Ivo said his name — not *Marguerite*, which anyone could say, but the particular way Ivo shaped it, with that half-smile folded into the vowels, as though the name itself were something Ivo had been given permission to hold. He knew there had been such a way. He could feel the absence of it the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was: a small, specific hollow, shaped exactly like what it lacked.

He set down the cup and went looking for paper.

The sigils along his arms ached when he wrote. They always ached now — a low, warm throb like sunburn, like the memory of fire rather than fire itself — and they ached worst when he reached for magic that no longer answered him. But writing was not magic. Writing was only ink and pressure and the stubborn human insistence that what happened to us should be allowed to remain.

*His voice was low*, he began. *Not deep, exactly. Low the way water under ice is low. He spoke quietly because he never needed to be loud; sound arrived where he wanted it.*

That wasn't right either. Or it was right, but it wasn't *him*. It was a description of a voice, not the voice. He crossed it out. Started again. Crossed that out too.

Outside the window, Ashfall went about its mended morning. The bells rang twelve times, correctly, obediently, and no one in the street below so much as glanced up. Voss's proclamation — *the wards are restored; the crisis has passed; resume your lives* — hung on every corner in neat Crown lettering, and the city, hungry for ordinary things, had swallowed it whole. Only three people alive knew what the wards were now, and one of them was forgetting.

He wrote until his hand cramped. He filled six pages before dawn, then six more, and when the light came gray through the shutters he kept writing by candle stub, chasing the details down as they fled.

*He stepped out of a wall the first time I saw him. Floodwater to his knees, witch-finders behind me, and he simply walked out of the brick as if walls had always been doors for him and everyone else had just forgotten how to knock.*

*Ivo laughed once — truly laughed, not his usual dry huff — at Odile's face when Voss turned her coat. I have been trying all morning to hear it again. I can remember that it happened. I cannot remember the sound.*

*He held my grandmother's ring like it was an egg made of grief.*

*He taught me to listen for the Hollow by pressing my palm flat to frozen ground in the dead of winter, and when I asked him how long he'd been doing this, alone, he said "long enough to forget my own name," and I thought it was a joke.*

It had not been a joke. That was the thing Marguerite kept returning to, the wound beneath the wound: Ivo had meant it literally. A fragment of the Hollow, cut loose and bound to iron, four hundred years of watching the world through other people's bloodlines — of course he had lost himself. Of course he had clung to whatever he could keep. And in the end, given the choice between dissolution and service, he had chosen service, chosen to become the keystone of a ward woven into a living man, chosen to spend his last coherent hour making Marguerite promise—

*Promise not to remember me gently,* Marguerite wrote, and his pen dragged, and the sigils flared hot enough that he hissed and shook his arm. *Remember me truly or not at all. Don't make me into a saint. Don't sand off my edges. I lied to you and you hated me for it and then you didn't, and both of those are true, and I need them both to stay true.*

The ink blurred. He told himself it was the candle guttering.

* * *

By the ninth day, he had learned the shape of the erosion.

It did not come all at once, the way the stars had gone out. It came like tide. Each morning he would wake with his memories intact — or apparently intact — and each morning some detail would have gone soft overnight, the way footprints soften in dew. Not deleted. *Thinned.* He could still see the flooded undercity, the lantern-light on black water, the moment Ivo stepped out of the wall. But the fear he'd felt was becoming a fact about fear rather than fear itself. He remembered that he had been afraid. He could no longer feel it.

He tested himself ruthlessly, the way he'd once tested charms for flaws. *What color were Ivo's eyes?* Brown. No — they changed, didn't they, depending on how much of himself Ivo was spending? Darker when he was strong, going pale and glassy near the end, like frost forming across a window. Yes. That was true. Hold onto that.

*What was the promise he made to my grandmother?*

Gone. Completely gone. There had been a promise — the journal said so, in his master's cramped hand, a single line Marguerite had copied out and copied out again: *Ivo swore an oath to Elira Ishikawa regarding the boy. Nature of oath unknown.* He had known its nature once. He was almost certain he had known. Now there was only the shape where knowledge had been, and the growing, animal panic of a man watching his own mind betray him from inside.

So he built the archive.

Voss gave him a room in the witch-finders' garrison — not a cell, though it had once been one; she had been explicit about that, standing in the doorway with her arms crossed and her pragmatism worn openly like armor. "You saved the city," she said. "The city will never know. That arrangement requires you to stay findable." She paused, and something almost human moved behind her eyes. "And alive."

"Findable isn't the same as watched?"

"It is exactly the same as watched," Voss said. "But I'll bring you tea myself, and I won't read your papers unless you're dead. Those are my terms. Take them or take a cell."

He took the tea.

He turned the cell into a scriptorium. Paper covered the walls in layers — he wrote on everything, backs of proclamations, the margins of confiscated rebel pamphlets, butcher's paper Voss requisitioned without asking whose budget it came from. He wrote chronologically at first, and then, as the early days began to slip, he wrote frantically backward, racing the tide, shoring up the oldest memories first because they would be the hardest to lose and the easiest to lose and he could not bear —

He could not bear for the wall to go first.

Because the wall was the beginning. A stranger stepping out of brick in a drowned tunnel, translucent and insufferable, saying *heir* like it was an accusation. Everything else had grown from that. If he lost the wall, he lost the root, and the tree of everything they'd been — teacher and student, liar and betrayed, jailer and freed, the two of them building a third answer out of scraps in a locked room while the world outside tore itself apart — would fall over.

He wrote the wall scene eleven times. He recited it walking the garrison corridors. He whispered it into his cupped hands at night like a prayer to a god who had already left.

On the twelfth day, he woke and could not remember what Ivo smelled like.

He sat on the edge of the cot for a long time, hands open in his lap, doing the arithmetic. Rain on stone. Old iron. Something green underneath, like sap, like crushed leaves — no. Like nothing. The words were there but they pointed at emptiness. He remembered *that* Ivo had a smell the way he remembered that Ashfall had a king: an official fact, carrying no weight.

That was the day he understood what the village had felt like.

* * *

He went back to Hollowmere on the fourteenth day.

He hadn't planned to. His feet decided — carried him out of the garrison before dawn, onto the north road, walking through country still scarred from the gatehouse catastrophe, past farms where people worked their fields with the careful blankness of those who had heard stories and chose not to believe them. Two days' walk. The sigils sang in his arms the whole way, a low harmonic he had stopped resenting and started listening to, because it was the closest thing he had left to conversation.

The village sat in its valley looking exactly as it had when he and Ivo passed through: whitewashed houses, well-tended gardens, washing strung between windows, children in the lanes. Everyone smiling. Everyone alive. Everyone unable to call their own children's faces to mind.

He found the woman he remembered — the baker's widow, who had once stood in her doorway holding a wooden bird and saying, pleasantly, *I know I loved someone very much. Isn't that strange?*

She was older-looking now, or perhaps he was. She offered him bread without being asked, the way villages do, and he sat on her step and ate it and finally said, "Do you remember me?"

"No," she said cheerfully. "Should I?"

"Probably not. Did you ever remember? The person you lost?"

Her smile flickered — not faded, *flickered*, like a lamp in wind — and for one moment something moved behind her eyes that was not peace. Then it settled again, smooth as pond ice. "I remember that I don't," she said. "It's a funny feeling. Like a coat hook with no coat. You reach for it every day, and every day your hand closes on air, and eventually — well." She shrugged, pleasant as Sunday. "Eventually you stop reaching."

"And does it hurt? When you stop reaching?"

"Oh," she said, considering, "no. That's the mercy of it. Forgetting doesn't hurt. It's the *reaching* that hurts."

He thanked her for the bread. He walked up out of the valley without looking back, and halfway up the ridge he stopped and pressed his palms flat against the cold earth, the way Ivo had taught him, listening for the Hollow.

It was there. Of course it was there — it lived under his ribs now, curled around his name like a cat around a hearthstone, vast and drowsing and warm. Through the ward-sense threaded through his body he could feel the village below, feel the thin places where the Hollow had fed, feel the terrible gentleness of it. It had never wanted to hurt anyone. Hurting was wasteful. It wanted longing. It wanted the reaching. It grew fat on the ache of missing things, and so it removed the things and left the missing, and the missing, unfed by anything real, slowly starved its host into smiling placidity.

*And what am I doing,* Marguerite thought, standing on the ridge with his arms full of sigils and his pockets full of pages, *but feeding it?*

Every night he reached for Ivo. Every night his hand closed on thinner air than the night before. He was grieving in slow motion, and the grief had nowhere to go except inward, circling the ward, wearing grooves in it. Resentment pooled in the grooves. He caught himself, on the bad nights, hating — hating the binding, hating the choice, hating Ivo for choosing it, hating himself for agreeing to hold the keystone of the world in a body that couldn't even keep a voice. And the ward felt every ounce of that hatred. He could feel it too: hairline tremors in the seal, frost-cracks in the foundation. A ward carried in resentment would crack. He had written that line himself, weeks ago, copying it from his master's journal. He'd thought it was theory.

The Hollow stirred under his ribs, patient, attentive, tasting his sorrow like a connoisseur.

He understood, then, standing in the wind on the ridge above the village that forgot itself, what the last page of his master's journal had really meant. Not *guard yourself against the Hollow.* Not *run, hide, survive.* The old man, who had spent his life learning what fed the beast and what starved it, had written one final instruction in a hand gone shaky with age:

*What you carry must be carried in love or it will eat you. Find a way to make the grief a gift.*

* * *

He burned the pages on the nineteenth day.

He did it in the garrison courtyard, at dusk, in an iron brazier Voss used for burning correspondence. He had spent three days preparing — reading everything one final time, every wall, every lesson, every lie and forgiveness, every entry in the long catalog of a ghost who had spent four centuries forgetting his name and chose, at the end, to be remembered truly or not at all.

*Truly or not at all.*

That was the hinge. That was the whole of it, and Marguerite had been turning away from it for nineteen days because turning toward it meant accepting the unbearable arithmetic: that the pages were not preserving Ivo, they were replacing him. Already the memories lived more in the ink than in him. He read about the laugh instead of hearing it. He consulted the wall scene like a text instead of standing in it. And each consultation carved the grooves deeper — *you are losing him, you are losing him, look how much is already gone* — and each groove fed the thing asleep beneath his ribs, and the frost-cracks spread.

A ward of resentment. A ward of reaching. A coat hook with no coat, gripped so hard the hand bled.

Whereas the village woman — gods forgive him, the village woman — had shown him the other road. Not forgetting as violation, forgetting imposed from outside by a hungry dark. Forgetting as release. As the final act of the promise: *remember me truly or not at all.* The pages were a way of remembering gently. Curated. Preserved. Pickled in ink. And gentle remembering was exactly the thing Ivo had begged him not to do, with his last hour of selfhood, with his dissolving hands pressed to Marguerite's face.

"Don't make me safe," he'd said. "Safe is another word for false. Carry the true weight or put it down."

So Marguerite put it down.

He fed the pages to the brazier one at a time, and he did not look away from any of them. He read each one as it burned — the wall, the lessons in dead magic, the ring held like an egg of grief, the cell where they built the third answer, the gatehouse where Ivo poured himself into the working like light into glass. He let each one hurt. He let the sigils flare and crackle and sing, and when the Hollow stirred beneath his ribs, rising toward all that rich dark grief like a fish toward baited water, he did the only thing his master's last instruction allowed.

He blessed it.

"I'm not giving you this," he said aloud, to the thing under his heart, in the empty courtyard, as the ash rose. "This isn't yours. This grief is mine, and it's *for* him, and I'm keeping it — not the facts, not the voice, not the color of his eyes. Just the fact that I loved him. That stays. It stays because I choose it, not because you're owed it, and every time you taste it you're going to taste that choice, and it will taste like iron."

The Hollow subsided, sulking, vast. The ward steadied. He felt it settle — not healed, but *honest*, the cracks admitting what they were, load-bearing grief instead of hidden rot.

The last page to burn was the eleventh version of the wall. He held it over the coals and read it through one final time, mouthing the words, committing the rhythm of them to whatever part of him would outlast the content: *a stranger stepped out of a wall and called me heir, and I hated him, and then I didn't, and both were true.*

The paper caught. Light climbed it. For half a heartbeat the flames bent strangely, and the smoke — he would swear this later, to no one, forever — the smoke rose in a shape that might have been a man standing straight, unhurried, translucent against the dusk, before the wind took it apart.

"You're welcome," Marguerite said to the courtyard, hoarsely. "And goodbye."

He stayed until the coals went gray.

* * *

Winter deepened. He kept the stall.

Voss had arranged it with the same brisk mercy she arranged everything: a corner spot near the tanners' bridge, a license stamped with the Crown's new grudging tolerance for *small domestic charms, non-combatant*, and a cover story so clean it had already become true. The hero of the Restoration, they said — some unassuming charm-seller who'd helped the wardens rekindle the great works. People brought him flowers sometimes. He never knew what to do with them, and kept them anyway, in chipped cups, until they dried.

He sold luck-knots to dockworkers. He mended hearth-charms for grandmothers. He wove protection into keychains and birth-blessings into door-lintels, and the sigils along his arms warmed pleasantly at the work, the way a well-fed dog warms at the hearth, and none of his customers ever looked closely enough to wonder why the charm-seller's sleeves were embroidered in patterns that shifted when you weren't watching.

He forgot Ivo the way winter forgets autumn: thoroughly, gradually, and completely.

Some nights he woke weeping and did not know why, and lay in the dark letting the tears come, honoring them as tribute to a debt whose terms he could no longer read. Some days a customer would say a certain word — *heir*, or *listen*, or once, unbearably, *wall* — and something would lurch in him, a great bell swinging silent in a broken tower, and he would stand there with his hands full of string and no idea what had just struck him.

But he did not reach. That was the discipline, the daily practice, the gift he made of the grief. When the ache rose, he let it rise and pass through him like weather, and he said, inwardly, the only liturgy he had left: *this is mine, and it's for someone, and whoever he was, I loved him truly, and that is enough to carry.*

The ward slept warm beneath his ribs. The cracks held. The stars, when they came out over Ashfall, stayed.

And then, on an evening at the edge of winter — the lamps being lit, the tanners' bridge loud with homegoing footsteps, the smell of char and river-smoke on the air — Marguerite looked up from a half-finished luck-knot and saw a stranger hesitating at the edge of his stall.

Translucent, almost, in the lamplight — or that was only the fog. Pale, watchful, with the particular stillness of someone who has been walking a long time without knowing toward what. He stared at Marguerite's hands, at the knot taking shape between his fingers, with an expression like a man trying to remember a word in a language he used to speak fluently.

Something in Marguerite's chest swung hard against its tower, silent, ringing.

He knew nothing. He knew everything. He set down the knot, and smiled, and heard himself say, in a voice gone soft with a grief that had finally, fully, become a gift:

"You look cold. Would you like some tea?"
