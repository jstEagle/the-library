## 22. Stars, Again

The first star came back on a Tuesday, and the whole city pretended not to notice.

Marguerite noticed. Marguerite had been watching the sky the way a man watches a wound for signs of healing — every night, from the flat roof of his tenement on Candlemaker's Row, a chipped cup of barley tea going cold in his hands, counting the dark as if darkness were something that could be paid down in installments. For most of the year there had been nothing to count. The sky over Ashfall had been a lid of soot-colored nothing, and beneath it the city had learned to live like people living in a house where all the lamps have gone out: carefully, by touch, pretending this was always how it was.

Then, one evening in late autumn, low over the harbor where the smoke thinned, a single point of light. Small. Stubborn. The color of a needle's eye.

He hadn't wept. That surprised him later. He had simply sat very still on the rooftop, both hands pressed flat against the cold lead flashing, feeling it echo up through his arms into his chest — feeling the thing beneath his ribs turn over in its sleep, warm as a banked fire, and settle again. *There,* something in him had said, without words. *That one is ours.*

By spring there were forty stars. By midsummer, so many that children were climbing onto warehouse roofs just to look at them, and the old women in the fish market had started arguing about which constellations used to be which, and nobody could agree, and the arguing itself was a kind of festival.

A year now since the bells tolled thirteen times. A year since the gatehouse. A year since—

Since. He kept the word the way he kept a stone in his pocket: smooth from handling, its edges long worn off, its weight the only fact about it left. There was a shape in his memory where something had been cut away, and around the shape everything else grew strange and tender, like grass growing over a grave. He did not know what he had lost. He knew only that he had agreed to lose it — that somewhere inside the forgetting was a choice he had made, made freely, made for reasons that the forgetting had also taken. Some nights this undid him more than grief would have. Grief at least has a face. His loss had none. It was a door in a wall of his own mind, bricked over from the inside, and he had been the one who laid the bricks.

But he was learning to live in the house anyway.

* * *

The stall opened at dawn, as it always did, on the corner of Rope Walk where the fish-gutters' carts rattled past and the gulls screamed their eternal grievances. It was a modest stall — a plank table on trestles, an awning of patched sailcloth, a hand-painted sign that read ISHIKAVA'S CHARMS & SMALL FORTUNES in letters Marguerite had deliberately made ugly, because ugly letters looked honest. Magic remained outlawed. It remained, in practice, tolerated the way a leaky roof is tolerated: everyone knew it was there, everyone lived under it, and only a fool called attention to it in front of the Crown.

So his charms were small, and they were charms, and they worked — mostly.

Luck-knots braided from three colors of cord, for dockworkers who wanted to come home with all their fingers. Salt-and-iron sachets for doorways, to keep out bad dreams and worse neighbors. Little wax seals stamped with sigils he no longer remembered the meaning of, copied from his own forearms in the mirror — the warden-marks that had bloomed along his skin the night the Hollow folded itself into him and went to sleep, intricate as frost ferns, permanent as scars. He sold them as *protective stamps, traditional design.* They were protective. He suspected they had once been something considerably more than traditional.

The witch-finders still patrolled. Captain Voss saw to it that they patrolled slowly, and in daylight, and never down Rope Walk before noon — a courtesy she had never acknowledged aloud and Marguerite had never thanked her for, because between them thanks would have been an admission that either of them remembered what had actually happened at the gatehouse, and the official story — *the wards restored through Crown diligence at great expense* — suited them both fine. Once, though, passing his stall, she had paused, looked at the row of wax seals, and said, "Your stamp-work has improved," and walked on. It was the closest either of them would ever come to saying thank you.

He liked the mornings best. The light coming gold-gray up Rope Walk, smelling of tar and frying onions and the sea underneath everything. Mrs. Adeyemi from the chandlery buying a luck-knot for her son's first voyage and refusing, as she did every time, to accept the one he tried to give free. The dockworker Tam with his missing finger and his cheerful fatalism — *"If the knot works I'll buy two next time; if it doesn't, I'll buy one anyway, out of respect"* — who had become, without either of them deciding it, something like a friend. People who had stood in the burning streets a year ago holding his little charms like talismans against the end of the world, and who now haggled over the price of them, and whose haggling was the sound of the ordinary world having survived.

He charged fair prices. He gave good weight. And when no one was looking — when the street was empty and the gulls were quiet and the light lay just so across the water — he let himself feel the other thing. The warmth under his ribs. The vast slow sleeper coiled through the center of him, dreaming its hunger-dreams, held now not by walls or wards or bloodlines but by a name. His name. Spoken by no one else in all the world, remembered by no one else in all the world, and therefore, by the oldest logic of the craft, unbreakable.

Some days it felt like carrying a lit lantern in a storm. Some days it felt like being the storm's shelter. Most days it felt like neither — like a heartbeat slightly too large for his chest, patient and warm and *his,* and he had stopped asking whether that was a curse or a companionship. Both answers were probably true. He'd stopped needing the question answered.

What he could not do, anymore, was magic that went outward. He had discovered this gently, over months of small failures: knots that wouldn't hold intent, salts that stayed only salt, a candle-flame he once coaxed toward a divination and got nothing back but his own reflection, doubled and doubled again until the reflection was just a boy selling trinkets on a dock. Whatever he was now, he was a vessel, not a spout. The power flowed in and stayed. The Hollow slept in him the way heat sleeps in a stone, and nothing of it could be spent, and nothing more could be taken.

He told customers the charm-trade had gotten harder lately. They nodded sympathetically. Everyone's trade had gotten harder.

* * *

Autumn came round again, a full turning of the wheel, and on the anniversary week of the thirteen bells the city held — of all things — a festival.

Nobody had planned it. That was the beauty of it. It assembled itself out of the collective mood the way weather does: first someone hung lanterns along Rope Walk "just because," then the chandlers' guild declared a half-holiday, then the taverns put out benches into the streets, and by the second evening Ashfall had talked itself into celebrating the night the stars died, because the only alternative was remembering it honestly, and the city had decided, by some silent unanimous vote, to be grateful instead. Bonfires on the shingle below the seawall. Music, mostly fiddles and a drum made from a pickling barrel. Children running everywhere with sparklers that were technically illegal and universally ignored.

Marguerite kept the stall open late, because festivals were good for business and better for watching. He sold salt-iron sachets to young couples who wanted to hang them over beds for reasons the sachet label tactfully did not mention. He sold luck-knots to sailors who already had six. He drank cider someone thrust at him, and ate a honey-cake, and laughed twice at things that deserved laughter, and if there was a hollow place in him that all this conviviality failed to fill — a shape, a negative space, a chair-shaped absence at the edge of every fireside — well. The whole city had that absence. You could see it in how hard everyone was trying. Ashfall was a woman smiling at a funeral feast: radiant, exhausted, determined, and grieving something she couldn't name.

When the bonfires burned low, the crowd drifted toward the seawall for the culminating spectacle — the harbor lights extinguished, all together, so the returned stars could be properly seen. Marguerite heard the crier announce it and felt the thing under his ribs stir, warm and interested, like a dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep.

*Yes,* he thought at it, absently, the way you talk to a sleeping child. *You can look too.*

He began packing the stall. Cord into basket. Seals into box. And that was when he noticed the stranger.

* * *

The stranger was standing at the edge of the lamplight, and had been, Marguerite realized, for some time.

He was tall, or seemed so — hard to tell, because the man did not stand so much as hover at the border of solidity, the way reflections hover on water when the wind is wrong. He wore plain clothes in no particular style, gray and darker gray, and his outline kept doing something Marguerite's eyes refused to commit to: softening at the edges, sharpening, softening again, as if he were a drawing being endlessly redrawn by a hesitant hand. Where the lantern-light touched him it didn't quite land. It passed a fraction of the way through, the way light passes through fog, through breath on glass, through —

Through a wall, Marguerite thought, and did not know why the phrase arrived with such a jolt, or why his hands had gone still on the basket, or why the sleeper beneath his ribs had risen fully awake and was pressing outward, urgent, straining toward the stranger like a flame bending toward a draft.

*Easy,* Marguerite told it. *Easy.*

"Can I help you?" he said. His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect.

The stranger looked at him. His eyes were the problem. Everything else about him flickered and blurred, but the eyes were fixed, and dark, and full of something so raw and bewildered and *familiar* that Marguerite's chest ached on the spot, an ache with no story attached, grief arriving before its own cause like thunder before lightning.

"I don't—" the stranger said. He had a voice like a hearth in winter; even confused, it was a voice built for reassurance. "I don't know why I'm here."

"People usually know that much," Marguerite said gently.

"No. I mean—" The stranger's hand rose, translucent at the wrist, and pressed against his own sternum as if checking whether his heart was where he'd left it. "I've been walking for hours. Days. I don't sleep. I don't eat, or — I do, sometimes, but it doesn't *do* anything. I keep ending up near water. Near this water. And tonight everyone's putting out their lights, and the sky—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, ashamed. "Something's pulling me. Something's been pulling me all year. Like a thread. And tonight it pulled hardest, and I followed it, and it ends here." His gaze dropped to the plank table, to the scattered charms, to Marguerite's hands — and caught, snagged, on the warden-sigils showing at his open cuff. The stranger went very still, in the way of a man seeing a word from a language he's forgotten he speaks.

"Those marks," he said. "I know those marks."

"Do you?" Marguerite asked. His pulse had picked up. Under his ribs, the Hollow lay pressed flat against the inside of his skin, listening, and its listening felt almost like *recognition,* and recognition of what, of whom — the question rose in him and broke apart before it could form, because there was nothing behind it, there was only the smooth brick wall, only the shape of the absence, only the stone in his pocket grown suddenly heavy and hot.

"They're warden work," the stranger said slowly, tasting each word like a word dredged up from deep water. "I don't know how I know that. I don't know anything. I woke up — I don't even know where — and I'm not sure I *did* wake up, because look at me, I can see the lantern through my own hand—" A laugh escaped him, thin and frightened. "I've been telling myself I'm dead. But dead men don't get *pulled,* do they? Dead men don't spend a whole year walking toward a city because something in it hurts like a name they've lost."

The festival noise swelled faintly from the seawall — cheering, the harbor lights dying district by district, the crowd gasping as the full field of stars came out over black water. Neither of them turned to look.

"You're cold," Marguerite said. It wasn't true — the stranger radiated no temperature at all — but it was what you said, it was what his hands were already doing, setting the kettle on the little spirit-stove he kept for himself, taking down the second cup he kept wrapped in cloth and never used, the cup he had bought eight months ago from a potter on Bridge Street for no reason he could articulate, paying far too much for it, carrying it home cradled like a bird. "Sit down. Tea helps. I don't know if it helps your kind, but it won't hurt."

"My kind." The stranger drifted — there was no other verb — to the stool across the table and perched on it uncertainly, as if he'd forgotten how sitting worked and was reconstructing it from first principles. "You're not afraid of me."

"Should I be?"

"Most people cross the street." The stranger watched the kettle with an expression of helpless longing, as though tea were a country he'd been exiled from. "They don't know why. People just... don't quite see me. Shopkeepers forget I'm standing there. Dogs won't look at me. Except you. You looked straight at me from across the square." His voice dropped. "Why did you look at me? Why does looking at you feel like — like the thread pulling tight? Like something in me finally set down a weight it's carried for a year?"

The kettle sang. Marguerite poured: two cups, steam rising, the small domestic ritual steady under his hands while everything in him that had no words roared like the sea in a shell. He slid one cup across. The stranger wrapped his half-solid fingers around it, and where they touched the clay, the clay *warmed,* actually warmed, and the stranger stared at that small miracle as if it were the first real thing that had happened to him all year.

"I don't know your name," Marguerite said.

"I don't either." The stranger laughed again, softer, wonder creeping in under the fear now. "Isn't that ridiculous? I keep reaching for it and there's just — nothing. Just me. Whoever that is." He looked up. The bonfire light came through him, orange and dim, and his eyes were steady and dark and achingly familiar, and Marguerite found, rising in himself from somewhere deeper than memory, deeper than the bricked-over door, a certainty so calm it frightened him:

*Ivo,* said something in him that was not a memory and was not a guess. *Ivo, Ivo, Ivo.*

But he didn't say it. He didn't know why he didn't say it, except that some instinct older than knowing told him the name was not his to give back — that it had been surrendered, freely, for reasons written in a hand he could no longer read, and that handing it over carelessly might undo something precious, might crack something that needed to mend on its own schedule, in its own season.

Instead he said: "It'll come back. Names do. Or if it doesn't—" He shrugged, and heard his own voice go rough. "You can borrow one of mine till yours turns up. I've got a spare."

The stranger blinked at him. And then he smiled, and the smile was the worst and best thing yet, because Marguerite's whole body recognized it — recognized it the way the Hollow had recognized the stranger, the way the unused cup had been bought, the way a house recognizes a footstep — and still his mind offered him nothing, only the ache, only the shape, only love with its address torn off.

"Tea first," the stranger said. "Then maybe you'll tell me what this place is. What you are. Why the sky came back this year, and why I think — I think *you* had something to do with it. I don't know how I think that. But when I look at you, I feel like I've been keeping a promise I can't remember making, and tonight, finally, it's nearly kept."

Above the seawall, the crowd was naming the constellations wrong and laughing about it. The stars burned on, indifferent and generous, and under Marguerite's ribs the sleeper curled close to the inner wall and purred like a cat at a hearth, and the steam rose between the two cups, and the stranger — the thread's-end, the borrowed name, the man-shaped absence that fit his absence exactly — lifted the tea and drank, and it did, against all reason, warm him.

"Take your time," Marguerite said. "We've got all night. We've got, I think, rather a lot longer than that."

And the stranger smiled again, and outside the lamplight the tide came in over the shingle, unhurried, faithful, returning and returning and returning.
