## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley does not forgive magic, which is precisely why I love it. Here, a charm fizzles like a wet match, a scrying bowl shows you nothing but your own tired face, and a gifted person is about as remarkable as a fence post. I have been a fence post for eleven years, and I recommend it.

My name — my real one, the one I keep in a locked drawer like a confession — is Oscar Nakamura, and before the valley I was a cartographer of places that do not exist. Not imaginary places, mind you. Imaginary places are easy; children invent them by the handful before breakfast. I mean places that exist only in memory. A kitchen burned down forty years ago. A city drowned before anyone thought to chart it. The room where you were happiest, which no longer stands anywhere but in your skull, wearing grooves into it like a river wears a canyon.

I map those. I always have. I close my eyes, and the dream gives me its streets, and I wake with ink-stained fingers and a drawing of somewhere I have never been, accurate to the width of a cobblestone. It is a gift in the way a fever is a gift: it marks you, and it burns.

So I came to the valley, where the land drinks magic like sand drinks rain, and I became a maker of honest maps. Coastlines. Elevation. The tedious truth. I sold charts to shepherds and smugglers, and nobody once asked why my hands trembled when I slept too near a stranger's grief. I was safe. I was nothing. It was the best thing that ever happened to me.

I should have known the crown would ruin it. Crowns ruin everything; it is their whole trade.

* * *

They came on a Tuesday, which felt insultingly ordinary. Three soldiers and a clerk, riding down the valley road with the particular posture of men who have been told they are heroes and have decided to believe it. I watched from my window, grinding ink, and told myself what I always told myself: they are here for taxes, or conscription, or to requisition my goat, who is honestly more trouble than she is worth.

Then I heard the word.

*Gifted.*

The clerk said it the way you'd say *rat-infested*. "By order of the Regency Council, all persons exhibiting unlicensed talent are to be registered, bound, or burned, per the Edict of Ash. Informants will be compensated."

I stood very still at my window and thought about the stack of maps in my back room. Not the honest ones. The other stack — the dreams, the impossible places, eleven years of a gift I'd starved but never managed to kill. If they searched my house, if they found even one of those charts, I would be registered, bound, or burned, and I had developed strong opinions about which of those I preferred.

I did the sensible thing. I packed nothing, burned nothing, and told myself the soldiers would pass by, because they always had, because the valley forgot things and surely the valley would forget me too.

That was the night Ivo collapsed at my door.

* * *

He was old — old the way a battle standard is old, frayed and faded but still standing because laying it down would mean admitting the war was over. He wore the grey cloak of an oathkeeper, or what was left of one, scorched at the hem. There was a seal on his chest, a wax disc the size of my palm, and it was glowing. Not brightly. Not dramatically. Just a slow pulse, like a heart deciding whether to bother.

He looked up at me with one eye, the other swollen shut, and said, "You're the mapmaker."

"I'm a mapmaker," I said, because I am a coward with a pedant's tongue.

"You're *the* mapmaker." He tried to rise, failed, and settled for gripping my doorframe hard enough to splinter it. "Nakamura. The one who charts the remembered places. I have walked two hundred miles to find a man who does not exist, and I would very much like to not die on his threshold, because that would be embarrassing for us both."

I should have shut the door. Any sensible fugitive would have shut the door. But he said *Nakamura* — a name I had not heard aloud in eleven years — and hearing it was like hearing a bell from a house you'd burned down. Something in me stood up and answered.

I dragged him inside. I am still not sure I decided to. My hands decided, and I have learned to let them, since they are usually wiser than I am.

* * *

He fevered through the night. The seal on his chest pulsed faster, and I noticed — with the particular horror of a person whose gift has been in remission — that I could *feel* it. Not the wax. What was under the wax. It pressed against the inside of my skull like a thumb against a bruise, and in my half-sleep I dreamed, for the first time in eleven years, of a palace.

Not the palace. I'd never seen it. But my dreams don't care about such details. I dreamed of corridors that rearranged themselves when you blinked, of a throne room with a king on it who was somehow two people, of a heart of stone beneath it all that had been *forgotten on purpose*, sealed out of every memory that ever touched it. I woke at dawn with my fingers black with ink and a half-finished chart on the floor beside me, and I sat there shaking, because I knew exactly what I was looking at, and I knew that no one draws that place by accident.

The valley had kept my gift asleep. Ivo had carried it through the door like a fever, and now it was awake, and it was hungry.

He woke near noon, lucid, and looked at the chart before I could hide it.

"Good," he rasped. "You've seen it already. That saves an hour of my being dramatic."

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Ivo. Last oathkeeper of the Inner Court, which is a grand way of saying I'm the only one left who didn't run, die, or forget." He touched the seal with something like tenderness. "This is the Oath of Aldric. The true one. The one the Hollow Thing couldn't swallow."

"The what?"

"You haven't heard?" He almost smiled. It was not a comforting smile. "The king on the throne isn't entirely the king. Hasn't been for thirty years. There's a thing wearing him — wearing the crown, wearing the face, wearing the *name* — and it eats oaths, mapmaker. Every vow sworn to the crown, it drinks. That's how it holds him. And the binding lives in the one place in that palace that no memory can survive." He tapped my chart with a shaking finger. "The forgotten heart. The place you just drew."

"Then whoever goes there forgets why they came," I said. "It's in the drawing. You can feel it. The corridors don't lead anywhere; they lead *away*."

"Yes. Which is why it needs a mapmaker who can chart a place from the inside of a memory — and an oathbearer the Thing cannot digest, because I'm the last man alive bound to the true oath." He closed his eye. "It also needs them both to be fools. Fortunately, I've been a fool for thirty years. You'll have to supply your own foolishness."

Outside, a bell rang. Not the valley's bell — that one is cracked and mournful. This was a soldier's bell, a hunting bell, and beneath it I heard horses, and the clerk's voice, thin and carrying: *"The oathkeeper is wounded and hiding in this valley. House to house. Burn what resists."*

I looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at me. The seal pulsed between us like a second heart, patient and awful.

"Eleven years," I said, to no one in particular. "Eleven years I was a fence post."

"You were never a fence post," Ivo said, and for a dying man his voice was infuriatingly gentle. "You were a door, Nakamura. Doors only look like part of the wall."

I took down the honest maps, and beneath them the dishonest ones, and I packed every chart of places that do not exist, because apparently they were about to. I put water in a skin, my ink in a case, my goat in the neighbor's reluctant care. My hands did not shake, which frightened me more than the soldiers did.

I had spent eleven years learning to be invisible, and in one night a bleeding old man had put a crack in it, and through that crack I could see the rest of my life: the road east, the hunters behind us, the palace of unmemory waiting with its patient corridors.

"East, then," I said, and hated how steady it came out. "But if I die in a corridor that doesn't exist, I want it noted on my map that this was your fault."

"Noted," said Ivo, and smiled his terrible smile, and we went out into the valley that forgets — two men it should have swallowed, carrying between us the one thing it never would.
