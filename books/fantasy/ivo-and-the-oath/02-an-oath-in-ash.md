## 2. An Oath in Ash

The valley let us go the way a miser lets go of a coin — reluctantly, and with an accounting.

I felt it at the tree line, that first morning out. A tug behind my ribs, like a hook set in wool. Behind us the mist had already closed over the road we'd walked, and I knew — the way you know a debt is called in — that the valley was already forgetting our footprints, forgetting the door of Oscar's cottage, forgetting perhaps that anyone had ever lived there at all. That was its mercy and its nature. Things that entered it were unwritten. Things that left were unwritten slower.

Oscar stopped at the edge of the pines and looked back for a long moment. I watched them do it: the small tightening around the eyes, the cartographer's habit of fixing a place in the mind before leaving it forever. They'd told me, on the first night, what their gift was. *I chart realms that exist only in memory.* I hadn't believed them until they showed me a map of a city that burned down two hundred years before either of us was born, drawn with such precision I could smell the tallow shops.

"You'll want to memorize it," I said now. "It won't remember you."

"It never did," Oscar said. "That was rather the point." And they turned east without another look, which is either courage or eleven years of practice at not looking back, and I have learned those are often the same thing.

* * *

We made poor time and worse conversation for three days.

Oscar Nakamura was not a talker. I understood this. Eleven years alone in a magic-dead valley will sand the words off a person like water off stone. But there is silence and there is silence, and theirs had edges to it — the silence of someone counting the cost of every mile and finding it too high.

I gave them space. I was good at giving people space; I'd had forty years of practice outliving everyone who'd ever wanted to stand close to me. There had been six of us once, oathbearers all, sworn in the crypt beneath the old cathedral with candle smoke and king's blood. Maren. The brothers Corvel. Little Tam, who'd joined at nineteen because he thought oaths were romantic. Serah, who could hold a grudge the way other people hold a child.

I was the last one carrying anything. The others carried graves.

On the fourth night, camped in the ruin of a shepherd's hut with rain coming down like the sky held a grudge of its own, Oscar finally asked.

"The seal," they said. They had it out, turning it in the firelight — the oath itself, wrapped in ash-grey silk, humming faintly the way a struck bell hums after the sound has supposedly ended. "You've been guarding it like a man guards his own heart. What's actually in it?"

"An oath," I said.

"I know it's an oath. I can feel it from here. It's like sitting next to a struck bell." Their eyes came up, sharp and grey as a winter coast. "What is it an oath *to*, Ivo?"

And there it was. Forty years of silence, sitting across a small fire from a person who drew maps of things that didn't exist, waiting for me to decide whether they existed enough to tell.

I should say, in fairness, that I had been deciding since the valley. Every night I lay awake listening to Oscar breathe and ran the arithmetic. Tell them nothing, use them as a compass, and if the hunters catch us they kill a stranger who never needed to die. Tell them everything, and they become exactly what the crown is hunting — an accomplice, a traitor, a name on a list. Either way I spent them. That's what I'd done my whole life, spent people, and every one of them had paid willingly and every one of them was dead, and here was this stubborn, brilliant, frightened mapmaker who had given up their invisibility for my sake without being told why.

The least I owed them was the truth. The most I could give them was also the truth. For once those were the same thing.

"King Aldric is alive," I said. "That's the first thing. Everyone thinks he's dead or mad or retired to some southern estate. He's none of those. He sits his throne every day. He holds court. He signs decrees." I turned the seal over in my hands. "But Aldric died thirty-one years ago, more or less. What wears him now is something older."

Oscar didn't laugh. I'd braced for laughter, or disbelief, and got neither — just that stillness, the stillness of someone watching a coastline appear through fog.

"The scholars call it the Hollow Thing. The old priests had better names, names they mostly got killed for using. It doesn't take bodies the way a demon would. It takes *offices*. It wore the crown, so it wears the king. And it keeps him — the real him — folded up inside itself, alive, because a dead king stops being useful but a living one can be bound." I looked into the fire. "Thirty-one years ago, six of us swore an oath binding that thing to its own hunger. Not breaking it — you can't break it, not from outside. Containing it. Sealing the wound so it couldn't spread past the palace walls. Five of us poured our lives into that seal. I'm the only one left alive to carry it, which means I'm the only thread holding the knot."

"And it's fraying," Oscar said quietly.

"It's fraying. And when it goes, the Thing won't be content with one kingdom. Oaths are how it eats. Every vow sworn on the crown's authority, every marriage contract, every soldier's enlistment — it feeds through all of them, and right now it's starving, and starving things get ambitious."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere up the slope a stone came loose and clattered down, and both of us flinched, and neither of us mentioned it.

"Why me?" Oscar said at last. "If you need an oathbearer, fine, you're it. Why drag a cartographer out of the one place your hunters can't follow?"

"Because of where the binding lives." I met their eyes. "Not in the throne room. Not in the crypt. In the palace's forgotten heart — the part of the building that erases anyone who enters it. The architects built it that way on purpose, or the Thing grew it that way; no one knows anymore. The corridors rearrange. The memories go first, then the names, then — well. No one comes back to report. There are no maps of it, Oscar. There have never been maps of it. Because mapping requires memory, and memory is exactly what it eats."

"And yours—" They stopped. I watched them arrive at it, watched the color leave their face by degrees. "Your comrades went in."

"Maren went in first. She was the strongest of us. We waited nine days." My voice did something I didn't authorize; I let it. "Tam went in after her. Then the Corvels together, because they refused to go separately. Then Serah." I looked at my hands, which had done nothing heroic in forty years except hold on. "I was meant to go fifth. The council decided the last bearer shouldn't risk himself until the others mapped a way through. So I sat outside and grew old while the people I loved walked into a place that ate them, and the seal held anyway, barely, and I told myself that was the same as a victory."

"That's not—"

"Don't." I said it gently. "Don't absolve me yet. You don't know me well enough. Wait until you do, then decide."

Oscar was quiet a long moment. Then they reached into their satchel and drew out a roll of paper — blank paper, good stock, the kind that takes ink like skin takes a scar — and laid it on the stones between us.

"Then here's what you should know about me," they said. "In the valley, I dreamed places that never existed and mapped them true. But dreams are just memories that belong to no one. If your palace-heart eats memory..." They put one finger on the blank page. "Then maybe what it leaves behind is exactly the kind of country I've spent eleven years learning to draw."

I stared at them. Forty years an oathbearer, and I swear to you I felt the seal against my chest warm — faintly, like a coal remembering fire — as though the oath itself had heard and approved.

"You understand what I'm asking," I said. "To reach the binding, someone has to walk in and stay themselves long enough to chart it. Stay themselves in a place designed to unmake that. And the hunters are two days behind us at most, and they know your face now, and—"

"They knew my face the moment I opened the door for you." Oscar smiled, and it was the terrible smile, the one I'd apparently taught them over four days of bad roads. "Get some sleep, Ivo. Tomorrow I start drawing a palace that doesn't want to be found."

* * *

They found us anyway, near noon, on the flats below the capital's far hills.

I smelled them before I saw them — witch-hunters travel with censer-smoke and sanctified iron, and the wind brought both. Three riders where the road bent, and behind them, on the ridge, more, fanning out with the unhurried confidence of people who have never once lost their quarry.

Oscar looked at the riders, then at me, and pulled out the blank roll of paper.

"Hold this," they said, "and whatever happens — don't let me forget why we're doing this."

Down in the flats, the lead rider raised a horn to his lips.

The note that came out of it was not a hunting call. It was an oath — sworn aloud, in the old tongue, on the crown's authority — and I felt the seal against my chest shudder like a bell about to crack.

The Hollow Thing knew we were coming. And it was hungry.
