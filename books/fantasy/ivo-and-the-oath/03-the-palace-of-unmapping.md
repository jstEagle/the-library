## 3. The Palace of Unmapping

The capital of Veylmark smelled of coal smoke and fear, which I suppose is what most capitals smell like if you're honest about it.

Ivo and I came in through the tannery district at dusk, two travelers among hundreds, hooded and unremarkable. Or rather, Ivo was unremarkable — a gaunt old man with a cough that had gotten worse since the valley, each rattle of his chest like a door someone kept forgetting to close. I was the problem. My gift had been quiet for six years in the magic-dead valley, sleeping like a dog by a cold hearth, and now that we'd crossed back into the living world it had woken up *hungry*. Every street I walked, my mind tried to chart it. Not the streets as they were — the streets as they remembered being. The lane where a wedding procession had passed forty years ago still glowed faintly gold to my inner eye. The alley behind the chandler's held the shape of a fire no one alive recalled.

"You're doing the thing," Ivo muttered, steering me by the elbow past a checkpoint where soldiers were checking hands for the calluses of spellwork. "Your eyes have gone map-colored."

"Map-colored."

"That grey-gold look. Like you're seeing through the world to its paperwork."

"I'm always seeing through the world to its paperwork," I said. "That's the entire affliction."

But he was right to worry, because the palace was worse. The palace was a wound.

We saw it first from the widow's steps above the river, and even Ivo — who had walked its halls for thirty years before his exile — went still beside me. Aldric's seat should have risen white and orderly over the water, seven towers in a deliberate crown. Instead the towers leaned like teeth in a rotting jaw, and the spaces between them were wrong. Not ruined. *Unwritten.* When I looked directly at the gaps between towers, my gift slid off them like rain off oiled leather, and what it left behind was a sensation I can only describe as having read a page and found it blank when you swear there was print there a moment ago.

"The forgotten heart," Ivo said quietly. "It's grown. In my day it was one corridor. A place servants wouldn't sweep because brooms came back without their bristles. Now—" He gestured at the whole crooked skyline. "Now it's most of him."

"Him being the king or the palace?"

"Yes," Ivo said, and coughed.

* * *

Getting inside was insultingly easy, which is how I knew it was a trap. The gates stood open. The guards at the inner doors looked through us the way men look through weather. And the moment we crossed the threshold, the corridors began to move.

I don't mean portcullises and pivoting walls. I mean the palace itself rearranged its memory of us. We would walk a hall, turn back, and find the hall behind us subtly different — a window where there had been a door, a carpet the color of a room I'd slept in as a child. The architecture wasn't shifting. It was *editing*, and it was editing us along with it, because every step deeper cost something small and irreplaceable.

I lost the smell of my mother's kitchen somewhere on the second stair. I know this because I reached for it — deliberately, the way you touch a sore tooth — and found only the knowledge that she had had a kitchen, and that I had loved her, and that the love remained while everything it had been made of drained away like color from wet cloth. I stopped on the stairs and pressed my hand flat against the banister until the wood bit my palm.

"Oscar." Ivo's voice, rough with an urgency he usually disguised as grumpiness. "Don't fight it here. Fighting makes it take more."

"How would you know?"

"Because I've watched three better oathkeepers than me try." He took my wrist, gently, the way you handle something already broken. "You walk through it like a guest. You keep your memories loose in your hand, not clenched. It takes the clenched ones first."

"And the ones I let go?"

"It takes those too," he admitted. "Just slower. Just kinder." He almost smiled. "The heart isn't cruel, cartographer. It's hungry. There's a difference, though I grant you it's a difference that doesn't help."

So we walked like guests. I unclenched my hands and let the palace skim its tithe: the name of my first teacher, gone; the taste of plums, gone; the particular way light fell through the valley pines in late autumn — that one hurt worst, and I fed it to the dark hallway deliberately, a coin paid so a larger coin might be spared. Ivo recited his oath under his breath as we went, the sealed words he'd carried across half a kingdom, and I noticed that wherever he spoke them, the corridors steadied. Walls remembered themselves. Doors stayed doors.

"It knows the oath," I whispered. "It's listening."

"It's *hungry*," Ivo corrected, but his hand had gone tight on his staff, and I understood then what he would not say aloud: that the binding in his chest was not just the key to the Hollow Thing. It was the bait on the hook, and we were swimming straight down its throat.

* * *

We found the heart at the center of everywhere, which is to say we found it after walking for what felt like three days through halls that may have been minutes, and arrived in a chamber I recognized instantly because I had dreamed it a hundred times in the valley. Round. Windowless. Lit by nothing, and yet lit. And at its center, on a throne built for a king, sat the Hollow Thing wearing King Aldric's crown.

I want to be fair to it. Descriptions of monsters are usually slander. But there is no kind way to describe a shape that is shaped like absence — a tallness where a man should be, wearing the regalia of Veylmark over a body like smoke remembering it once had shoulders. Where its face should have been there was a slow turning, like pages being flipped by a wind that never stops. And beneath us, beneath everything, I felt the thing I had come to feel: the binding. A thread of oath running out of Ivo's chest, across the floor, into the hollow king, thick as a ship's cable and old as grief.

"There you are," the Thing said, in a voice like a library exhaling. It turned its page-face toward Ivo first, and I saw my companion age ten years in a breath. Then toward me, and the flipping slowed, curious. "And the mapmaker. I wondered which of you the oath would drag home first."

"We've come to unmake you," I said. Wry narration is difficult when your voice shakes; I did my best.

"No," it said, almost gently. "You've come to *chart* me. That's different. Unmaking requires hatred, and you—" the pages fluttered, tasting— "you only want to fix things. Drawers are like that. You see a blank space and your hands itch." It rose from the throne, and the crown did not move with it, which was somehow the worst detail yet. "Here is what your oathkeeper hasn't told you, little drawer. I do not eat memories. Memories are gristle. I eat *oaths*. Promises. The shape of a self given away and held to. Your friend has carried thirty years of vow in his chest, and every mile he walked toward me, I grew fatter on it."

I looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at the floor, which for him was a full confession.

"He didn't tell me because he knew I'd come anyway," I said.

"Of course he did. That's what an oath does. It makes liars honest and honest people reckless." The Hollow Thing drifted closer, and I felt my gift rise up to meet it, greedy, mapping, *hungry* in exactly its way. "And here is the arithmetic you must do, cartographer. To unbind the king, you must counter-map the oath — draw its whole shape, every clause, every cost, true down to the last letter. And a map drawn in ink fades. A map drawn in memory fades faster here than anywhere in the world. So you will draw it in the only medium I cannot erase faster than you lay it down."

"Ink?" I said, though I already knew.

"In yourself," it said, delighted, terrible. "Every line of the binding, written into your own remembering. And I will stand here and eat each line as fast as you write it, because that is what I am, and you will keep writing anyway, because that is what *you* are. By the end, you will not remember why you came. You may not remember that there is an end. And the oath will be free regardless, because a name spent is a name spent."

Silence. Somewhere far above, real wind moved through real towers.

I thought of the valley. Of the pines whose light I had already fed away. Of Ivo, watching me with the expression of a man memorizing a face against a coming loss.

"Well," I said, and unslung my satchel, and took out the pen I had carved from valley oak, the one thing in Veylmark that magic could not follow and therefore could not take. "At least the commission pays in advance."

I set the nib against the air, and the air took the ink, and I began to draw.

The first line of the binding went in easy — the date, the names, the old king's dying wish. The second cost me the sound of rain. By the fifth, I could not recall Ivo's face, only that I trusted it. By the tenth, I was drawing shapes I no longer understood for reasons I had already eaten, guided by nothing but the pen's momentum and a stubbornness so deep it lived below memory, in the marrow, in whatever a person is when you take the person away.

Behind me, Ivo began to speak his oath aloud, steady as a heartbeat, feeding me the words faster than the dark could swallow them.

And the Hollow Thing leaned in, pages turning, and waited for the last line — the one I would never remember writing, the one that was my name.
