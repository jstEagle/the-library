## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The night market of Vellagard woke the way a drowned man wakes: slowly, painfully, and with no memory of how it had come to be breathing again.

Ines Oyelaran knew all its hours. He had learned them the way other children learned prayers—by repetition, by candlelight, by the patient instruction of someone who loved him enough to make him memorize what might one day save his life. The market's first hour belonged to the fishwives, who came down from the terraces with lanterns swinging and voices like gulls fighting over a carcass. The second hour belonged to the lamplighters, who walked the canal bridges with their long brass poles and left behind them a string of small yellow suns reflected in black water. The third hour belonged to everyone else—the fearful, the hopeful, the desperate, the merely curious—all of them drawn down to the Sable Quay by the oldest hunger in any city, which is the hunger to believe that tonight might be different from last night.

He set out his stall an hour before that third hour began, because a Saltmaker was always early. *Salt keeps time better than clocks,* Master Ede had said, tapping the little sand-glass she wore on a cord at her throat. *Clocks lie about the hour. Salt never lies about anything.* She had been dead four years now, and Ines still heard her voice most clearly when his hands were busy, which was perhaps why he kept them so relentlessly busy.

The stall itself was nothing—a plank table on two trestles, a striped awning gone grey with canal damp, a folding stool with one leg shimmed with cedar shingles. But the goods upon it were arranged with a precision that would have made a jeweler weep. Luck-knots in three sizes, braided from red thread and river-reed, each one sealed with a grain of salt pressed into wax. Fever-salts in twist-paper packets, coarse grey crystals ground fine as frost. Sleep-salts, bitter as regret. A jar of pickled whelks he sold honestly as food, because even a charm-seller had to eat.

And beneath his left sleeve, wrapped twice around his wrist so that no edge of it could catch the light, a strip of white linen embroidered with nine interlocking circles.

The sigil of the Saltmakers' Guild.

Wearing it openly would cost him his hands. Wearing it at all was, technically, a hanging offense—but Ines had reasoned long ago that the law could not hang him for what it could not see, and so the sigil stayed hidden, a secret worn against his pulse like a second heartbeat. He touched it sometimes without meaning to, the way a man touches a scar. *Nine circles for nine masters,* Ede had told him when she first bound it there, her fingers steady though her eyes were wet. *And you are the tenth thing it protects.*

"Ines! Ines, boy, are the fever-salts in?"

Madame Corvane descended on the stall like weather. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a bollard, and she ran the chandlery two stalls down with a tyranny so complete that even the rats paid her rent. Her granddaughter had the sweating sickness—not the plague, thank every silent god, but bad enough—and the girl had been three nights in fever, talking to people who weren't in the room.

"Ground fresh this morning," Ines said, wrapping six packets while he spoke. "Steep it in barley water, not wine. Wine makes it worse."

"You said that last time."

"Because it was true last time." He slid the packets across. "Barley water, cool cloth, and if she starts speaking clearly again, send for me."

Corvane counted out coppers with the grim ceremony of a woman paying a ransom. "You're a good boy, Ines Oyelaran. Too good to be selling knick-knacks on a quay." Her eyes flicked over the luck-knots, the sleep-salts, the little wax-sealed promises, and something moved behind them—pity, or suspicion, or both. "Your mother's trade, wasn't it? Before."

Before. The word Vellagard used for everything it preferred not to remember. Before the gods went silent. Before the dragons were hunted until only one remained, somewhere far below the world, guttering. Before the Edict of Nothingness had unmade the kingdom's name and laws and left a country standing that legally did not exist, full of people who legally had never been born, selling charms that legally did nothing at all.

"My mother's trade," Ines agreed pleasantly, and let the lie sit where it fell. His mother had been a netmender. It was his master who had taught him everything worth knowing, and his master whose name he could not say aloud in this city without a crowd gathering and then dispersing very quickly, some of them carrying messages to the Inquisition.

Corvane went off with her fever-salts, and the third hour arrived, and the market filled.

It filled the way it always did: with fear wearing the mask of shopping. Ines watched them come and catalogued them the way Ede had taught him—not by what they bought, but by what they were afraid of. The dockhand who bought a luck-knot for his brother going upriver was afraid of drowning; you could tell because he held the knot to his ear, as if listening for water. The young clerk who bought sleep-salts was afraid of his own dreams; he wouldn't meet Ines's eyes, and his hands smelled of ink and candle-tallow, the smell of a man who worked late for men he hated. An old soldier bought nothing but stood across the way for a quarter hour, looking at the salt jars, before walking off shaking his head.

They all wanted the same thing. Not magic—Vellagard had spent twelve years learning to pretend magic had never existed, and the pretending had gone deep, down into the marrow of the place. What they wanted was the feeling of having done *something*. A knot tied. A packet bought. A small, private act of defiance against a world that had outlawed hope along with everything else.

The charms did nothing. That was the law, and also, mostly, the truth. Red thread was red thread. River-reed was river-reed. The salt Ines ground and sold was honest sea-salt, boiled down in his rented room above the ropewalk, good for preserving herring and drawing the sting from wounds and doing absolutely nothing else.

Mostly.

There was the matter of the knotting. There was the matter of the words he murmured under his breath as his fingers worked, old guild-words, cadenced like tide-charts, that Ede had drilled into him until they lived in his hands rather than his head. He told himself these were only habit. Only craft. Only the shape of a thing whose substance had been confiscated. A prayer without a god behind it was just breathing with style—that was what he told himself, every night, tying knots for strangers, and every night the telling got a little harder to believe.

*If the salt fails,* Ede had written, in the last note she ever left him, tucked into the guild ledger like a knife between pages, *everything sealed comes home. Guard the boundaries, boy. And guard yourself. You are the last hand that remembers how.*

She had died before explaining which boundaries. Or perhaps she hadn't died before explaining—perhaps she had explained, many times, and Ines had simply never understood that she meant it literally.

*

The mother came near midnight, when the market had thinned to embers and the lamplighters were making their second round.

He saw her before she saw him—or rather, he saw the quality of her walking, which was the walk of a person who has stopped caring whether she falls down. She was young, mid-twenties perhaps, with the raw-knuckled hands of a washerwoman and a baby wrapped against her chest in a blanket that had been mended more times than Ines could count. The baby was quiet. That was the first wrong thing. Babies at the night market were never quiet—they wailed at the noise, the smoke, the crush of strangers. This one lay against its mother's collarbone with the terrible stillness of something conserving itself.

She stopped at the stall. Looked at the luck-knots. Looked at Ines.

"They say you're the one who sells things that work," she said.

"I sell knots and salt," Ines said. "For luck and against fever. They work as well as anything works."

"Don't do that." Her voice didn't rise. That was somehow worse. "Don't give me the shopkeeper talk. I've been to four stalls tonight and gotten the shopkeeper talk. The woman at the chandlery—" Corvane, he thought; word traveled fast among the desperate— "said if anyone could help my son, it's you. So either she's a fool or you're a liar, and I don't think she's a fool."

The baby stirred. Made a sound like a door opening very slowly in an empty house.

Ines looked at the child and felt the old ache open under his ribs—the ache that had lived there since the day the guild hall was sealed and nine chairs went empty and a girl of fourteen had been told, in effect, that everything she knew how to do was now contraband. He had spent twelve years practicing a gift in secret, alone, at night, with the shutters closed, working small workings on small things: souring milk back to sweetness, easing a spavined cat's hip, keeping the mold off Madame Corvane's stored grain through two wet winters. Small mercies, done small, so that no one would ever have cause to look at him twice.

*The gift isn't yours,* Ede had said. *Nothing is yours. We hold things. That's all a guild is—a chain of hands holding what can't be put down.*

"What's his name?" Ines asked.

"Toma." The word cracked in the middle. "The physicians won't come. I can't pay what they ask, and anyway they say—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "They say the fever has turned. They say to prepare myself."

The fever had turned. Ines knew what that meant in the language of Vellagard's poor: the child was dying, and everyone had agreed to describe it politely.

He should have sold her a luck-knot. Two coppers, a kind word, a blessing shaped like commerce. That was the safe thing, the sane thing, the thing the last apprentice of a murdered guild ought to do. Every instinct Ede had beaten into him—*small, hidden, survivable*—screamed it.

But the baby breathed again, that slow-door sound, and Ines looked at the mother's hands, which were trembling, and thought: *I am the last hand that remembers how.*

"Sit," he said.

*

What he did next took perhaps four minutes and would, if the Inquisition ever learned of it, take considerably longer to answer for.

He drew out his best salt—not the market salt but the true stock, guild-salt, made by rites he performed once a month in his locked room with the shutters barred, from seawater carried at dawn from the estuary mouth where the old boundary stone stood. He had told himself for years that this salt was only salt, only tradition, only the taste of continuity in a world that had outlawed continuity. He laid a pinch of it on the plank table in a line, and the line seemed, in the lamplight, faintly luminous, though he had drunk no wine.

Then he took a luck-knot—the largest, triple-braided—and unpicked it with a bodkin, and re-tied it, and as his fingers worked he spoke the cadence under his breath, the tide-chart words, the ones that lived in his hands. He spoke them properly for the first time in years. Not as habit. As craft.

The candle flame leaned toward the knot. All the candles on the whole quay leaned toward the knot, a hundred small flames bending like grass in wind that did not exist, and Ines felt it go through him—a current, a tide, a vast attention turning its face toward a plank table on the Sable Quay—and he finished the binding with hands that shook, and sealed it with wax and salt, and held it out.

"For the cradle," he said. "Not on him. Near him. And here—" fever-salts, six packets, pushed across with the knot— "barley water. And send for me tonight. Tonight, whatever hour. Promise me."

"I can't pay for all this."

"You'll pay me a copper. One copper, so it's a sale and not a charity, because charity rots and trade keeps." He was aware, distantly, that he was quoting Ede verbatim, and that his voice had gone strange. "One copper, washerwoman, and your son's name is Toma, and you'll send for me."

She paid the copper. She held the knot the way people hold relics, or infants, or the edges of cliffs. Then she went away into the dark with her too-quiet child, and Ines sat down on his shimmed stool and discovered that the market had gone silent around him.

Not empty—silent. The fishwives had stopped their quarreling. The dice game by the chandlery had paused mid-throw. Even the canal itself seemed to have stopped moving, black water holding still as poured glass, and above it all the lamplight burned straight up, unwavering, in a city where the harbor wind never once stopped blowing.

Then, all together, everything resumed. Dice scattered. Voices clashed. The wind returned like a held breath released, and Ines sat alone at his stall with his heart slamming and the taste of salt rising unbidden at the back of his throat.

*That didn't happen,* he told himself. *Wind drops. Candles gutter. Markets pause.*

*Everything does not lean toward a knot of red thread.*

He packed his goods with hands that would not quite obey him. He closed the stall. He walked home through streets that felt newly thin, as though the skin of the world had worn translucent in places, and behind the walls and under the cobbles and beyond the boundary stones something vast had shifted in its sleep, and was, even now, slowly opening one eye.

At his door, a boy waited—one of Corvane's errand-runners, breathless, lantern bobbing.

"She says come quick," the boy panted. "The washerwoman's boy. The fever broke. All at once, not an hour after she got home. Broke clean, like wringing out a cloth." The boy grinned, delighted with the miracle of it. "Whole terrace is talking. They're saying—"

Ines did not hear the rest. He was already running, not toward the terrace but away from it, toward the ruined guild hall on the hill where nine empty chairs sat waiting in the dark, because there was one person left alive he could report this to, and she had been dead for four years.

The salt had answered. Which meant the salt had *heard*.

Which meant something on the other side of it was listening again.
