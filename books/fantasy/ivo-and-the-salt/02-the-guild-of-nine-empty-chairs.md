## 2. The Guild of Nine Empty Chairs

The hall had not been swept in twelve years, and the dust lay on it like a second kind of snow.

Ines came to it the way he always did—through the weaver's yard behind Cordwainer's Row, over the wall with its broken bottles gone soft and green with moss, down the steps that the city had forgotten to condemn because the city had forgotten the building existed. The Saltmakers' Guildhall stood in a wedge of land where three streets refused to meet, wedged between a tannery that stank of lime and a warehouse full of Vellagard's cheapest grief: cheap wine, cheap rope, cheap coffins. Nobody looked at it. That was the point. The guild had paid good salt, once, for the privilege of being overlooked.

He let himself in through the postern door, which opened at his touch the way it always had—not because of any charm, but because his master had oiled the hinges every week for forty years and Ines could not bring himself to stop.

Inside, the light came down through the shattered lantern-dome in long grey columns, and the dust turned in them like something swimming.

Nine chairs stood on the dais at the far end of the hall.

They were old work, older than the Edict, older than the ban—carved from salt-bleached oak by hands that had understood what wood does when it has held brine all its life. Each chair bore its master's mark burned into the backrest: the coiled knot of Master Sef, who had sealed the river-gates; the open hand of Mistress Alouette, who had walked the boundary stones every solstice until her feet bled; the nine-pointed star of Master Corvane, whose name was struck from every record and survived only here, in wood, where no law thought to look.

Ines took off his cap. He always did, though there was no one to see.

"Good evening," he said, because his master had taught him that you report to the chairs as if they were occupied. "It's Thursday. I sold eleven luck-knots, four fever-salts, one mothering-stone, and—" His voice caught on it, the way a sleeve catches on a nail. "And one working charm."

The hall said nothing. The hall was very good at saying nothing. But the dust moved differently in the columns of light after he said it, turning against the drift of the air, and Ines felt the particular cold that lived in this room settle closer around his shoulders like a mantle being laid on him.

* * *

He climbed the dais because you did not shout your reports from the floor like a fishwife. He passed the first chair, and the second, trailing his fingers along the armrests out of habit—the wood was smooth there, worn to silk by decades of dead men's living hands—and stopped before the ninth.

The ninth chair had been his master's.

Master Ede Oyelaran—no relation, though the city had assumed otherwise and neither of them had bothered correcting it—had sat in that chair for thirty-one years, and had died in it too, or near enough. The Inquisitor's men had come on a grey morning in the month of Ashfall, and they had taken the guild's ledgers, and the guild's seals, and the guild's master, and what they brought back three days later was not fit to sit in a chair ever again. Ines had been seventeen. He had buried what was given to him in the salt-flats outside the walls, wrapped in canvas, weighted with a stone, because the law forbade burial in consecrated ground and the salt-flats were not consecrated to anything anymore. Nothing was.

He sat down on the step below the ninth chair, the way an apprentice sits, not on the seat itself—he had never once sat in it, not even now, when there was no one left to forbid him—and he told the chair everything.

"The charm worked." He kept his voice low. Sound carried strangely in the ruined dome; he had heard whispers from the doorway arrive at the dais whole. "You'll want to know how I know it worked, so I'll tell you. It wasn't supposed to. You know the ones I sell—the market charms, the comfort-charms. Knots tied right and salts mixed right, but no *working* in them. A prayer-shaped thing to hold while you wait for nothing. That's all the law allows, and less than the law allows, really, since the law allows nothing at all."

The dust swam. Somewhere in the tannery wall, a rat moved through the wattle, a small dry scratching like a quill.

"But this woman came to the stall tonight. Amara. She has a son with the lung-rot, the kind the physickers shrug at, and she'd spent everything on shrugging. And she asked me—" Ines pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "She asked me the question you're never supposed to ask a charm-seller. She asked me if it would *work*. And I lied to her. I said, it works as well as anything can. And then I tied the knot, and my hands—I don't know what my hands did, Master. They did something I learned at this bench when I was twelve years old and swore I'd forgotten, and the salt went into the knot singing, actually singing, I could hear it, and I gave it to her and told her to hang it over the boy's bed and never speak of it."

He lowered his hands. The ninth chair regarded him with its carved blankness, patient as oak.

"And it worked." The words came out flat, stripped of wonder, because the wonder had curdled somewhere between the market and the wall. "She found me at closing. She was laughing and crying at once, the way they do. The boy breathed all night, she said. First night in a year. And she wants another one. For her sister's child. And she told her neighbor."

Ines got up and walked to the edge of the dais and looked down the length of the hall—at the long tables where apprentices had once graded salt by lamplight, at the racks where crystal pans had dried, at the great cold hearth where the guild fire had burned without ceasing for two hundred years until the morning the soldiers raked it out and scattered the ashes in the river, because a fire that never dies is a claim, and claims were what the Edict had come to erase.

"Here's what frightens me," he said to the empty hall. "I didn't decide to do it. My hands decided. Which means the craft is still *in* me, still live, like a coal under ash. Fine. Good, even. But a coal doesn't burn on its own. Something fed it. Something came up through the salt tonight and answered her, and I don't know what, and I don't know why now, and I don't know why *me*."

The cold mantle settled tighter.

"I checked the boundary stone on Tanner's Walk on my way here," he went on, quieter. "The one in the wall-footing, the hidden face. You remember—you showed me when I was ten, made me swear the oath with salt on my tongue. The seal is supposed to read clean. White salt, unbroken grain, the whole line of it humming if you press your ear to it." He swallowed. "It reads grey, Master. Grey and pitted, like iron left out in sea weather. Like something's been drinking at it."

He had not meant to say that part yet. He had meant to save it, to keep the fear in a closed jar the way you keep a spark. But the hall pulled things out of him; it always had. This was where he had learned that a guildhall is not a building but an ear.

* * *

There was one more duty, and he almost skipped it tonight, because his legs were tired and the dread sat heavy in him like wet wool. But skipping duties was how guilds died—his master had said so, once, mildly, while dying by inches of a cough the damp winters gave him—and so Ines crossed to the master's writing stand beneath the narrow window, and knelt, and reached into the hollow beneath the drawer where the mortar had crumbled away.

The ghost-note was there.

That was what he called it, anyway. It was not a letter, exactly. On the last night—in the hours between the soldiers' first visit and their final one, in the time his master should have spent running and had instead spent writing—Ede Oyelaran had written a page and folded it into salt, actual salt, packed hard around the paper until the words were embossed into the crystals themselves, and spoken three words over it that Ines had never been able to learn and had never dared invent. The paper inside had long since yellowed and gone brittle. But the salt held the shape of the writing, ridge and valley, the way snow holds a footprint, and when Ines laid his fingertips along it in the dark, the words rose in him—not read, *remembered*, arriving whole in the voice he knew better than his own.

He had done it a hundred times in twelve years. It never got easier. It was not supposed to.

He pressed his fingers to the salt and closed his eyes.

*"Ines."*

Just that, first. Always just that. His own name in his master's dry, unhurried voice, and grief went through him like a blade going through water—all resistance, no sound.

*"If you are reading this, then I am dead, and you are the last, and I am sorry for both. Listen now, because I will only say this the way I can say it, and salt is honest but it is not patient.*

*"You know what the guild did. Not the selling—any fool can sell. The sealing. When the gods went silent, they did not go away. Silence is not absence; silence is a held breath. And a country full of frightened people breathing prayers into that silence is a country knocking on a door from the inside. The salt is the door. Every boundary stone, every hearth-blessing, every grain worked true by guild hands for two hundred years—it is all one seal, laid over the cracks where the old ones press. We did not banish them. We shut the door on them politely, and we have been holding it shut ever since, and the Crown, may it rot gently, called our work superstition and our hall a den of fraud, and outlawed the only thing standing between Vellagard and everything it has prayed for.*

*"So hear the warning, boy, and carry it in your bones: if the salt fails—if it corrodes, if it is eaten, if it is *unmade*—then everything sealed comes home. Not gradually. Not politely. Everything we shut out comes home at once, hungry, and owed, and remembering. The gods we bound. The things older than gods that the gods themselves helped us bind. The debts. The wild answers. All of it, through every crack at once, and no guild left to hold the door.*

*"Guard the stones. Guard the craft. Teach no one carelessly and refuse no one desperately. And if the salt begins to fail—*

*"—if the salt begins to fail, then someone is helping it fail. Salt does not rot on its own. Salt is the most patient substance God's world ever made. If the seal weakens, it is being weakened, and you must find the hands, Ines. Find the hands before you find the crack, because the crack is only the mouth. The hands are the mind.*

*"One more thing, and then the soldiers are at the door and I must go be brave in the way that costs nothing to describe and everything to do. You were not my apprentice because you had talent, though you had it. You were my apprentice because you asked, at nine years old, why we put salt on the doorstep, and when I told you it was custom, you said, 'Custom is a memory of a reason,' and I knew then the guild would die with everyone but you.*

*"A duty is not a trade. A trade you can set down. I am sorry, boy. I am sorrier than salt can hold.*

*"Sweep the hall."*

Ines knelt there a long time with his fingers on the salt, until the embossed ridges went smooth under his touch, as they always did, the message spending itself like a candle. Then he took out his knife and cut his thumb, and pressed a drop of blood into the hollow, and packed fresh salt over it from the pouch at his belt—guild-worked salt, the last of the last batch, hoarded for twelve years and nearly gone—and whispered the closing word his master had used, the one that tasted of the sea.

"Swept," he said aloud, to the dark hall. "I'll sweep it tomorrow."

* * *

But he did not leave at once. He climbed the dais again, because there was one more thing, and it would not keep.

He stood before the nine chairs in the column of moonlight falling through the broken dome, and he said, in the formal cadence the old masters had used for reports, the cadence he had practiced alone in this room since he was seventeen:

"Masters of the Saltmakers' Guild. Ines, apprentice of the ninth chair, reports. One working charm confirmed within the walls of Vellagard. Boundary stone at Tanner's Walk compromised. Seal integrity: failing." His voice cracked on the next part, and he let it. "Guild strength: one. Request instruction."

The hall was silent. Of course it was silent. Nine dead masters could not instruct him; his master's ghost-note could say only what it had already said; the craft in his hands answered questions he had not asked and stayed mute before the ones he had.

And yet—

As he stood there, the cold in the room shifted. Not the draft from the broken dome; something else, something with intention in it, moving along the floor the way tide moves along a sandflat, invisible and total. The dust in the moonlight stopped swimming and hung perfectly still, every mote fixed, as if the air itself had been salted.

And Ines felt, distinctly, in the scarred palm of his knife-cut hand, a pull. Faint. Directional. The way a compass needle feels before you look at it.

Toward the wall. Toward the north wall, where the old survey map of the city's boundary stones still hung behind its sheet of rotten sacking—every stone the guild had ever seeded, marked in faded ink, sixty-one of them ringing Vellagard like a rosary.

His heart began to knock.

"That's not instruction," he said softly. "That's a summons."

The stillness held. The dust hung. And beneath his feet, so faint he might have imagined it—might still, later, in the terrible clarity of hindsight, wish he had imagined it—Ines felt the floor give a single slow pulse, like the heartbeat of something very large sleeping under the city, turning over toward wakefulness.

He took the map down from behind the sacking. Sixty-one stones. He counted them with his fingertip, and his finger stopped of its own accord on the fourth stone of the eastern arc, the one set into the foundation of the drowned chapel on Candlemere Lane—a chapel the river had taken thirty years ago, which meant its stone had been underwater, unreachable, uncheckable, for thirty years.

Grey, he thought. Pitted. Drinking.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the Inquisition's third bell, the one that meant edicts posted, orders changed, the city's fear reorganized overnight. Ines folded the map into his coat, pulled his cap down over the guild sigil he no longer bothered to hide from the chairs, and went out through the postern door into streets that smelled, for the first time in his life, faintly and impossibly, of the open sea.
