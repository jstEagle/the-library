## 3. What the Prayers Bought

The bells of Vellagard had not rung for twelve years, and on the morning the fisherman's street drowned, Odile Ferrant woke to their sound and thought at first she was dreaming.

They came raggedly, out of practice, one bell lagging behind the others like an old man walking uphill. She lay in her narrow bed in the Inquisition barracks and listened, and beneath the bronze voice of the bells she heard something worse: screaming. Not the sharp screams of a cutpurse caught or a wife beaten—those were the ordinary music of any city—but a long, collective wail rising from the direction of Saltwharf, the sound a crowd makes when it has seen something it cannot put back.

She dressed quickly. Her uniform was grey wool, stiff with salt-stiffened sweat from yesterday's patrol, and the badge on her breast—a closed eye stitched in silver thread—had faded almost to suggestion. Twelve years since the Edict. Twelve years since the kingdom of Vellagard was declared never to have existed, its laws struck from the archive-stone, its gods declared fictions, its name replaced with the bland administrative word *Province*. Odile had been nineteen when the edict was read aloud in every square. She had stood in the rain with her mother's hand heavy on her shoulder, and she had believed, because believing was easier than the alternative, that erasing a thing was the same as ending it.

Now the bells were ringing, and no one had ordered them to.

* * *

Saltwharf smelled of brine and lamp-oil and, underneath it all, something sweeter that made her stomach turn: the smell of drowned things left too long in warm water.

The street had been called Pilchard Row once. Now it was called nothing, because half of it was gone—not collapsed, not burned, but *gone*, as though the sea had reached up through the cobblestones and taken a bite. Water filled the wound to the depth of a man's chest, black-green and perfectly still, so still it reflected the grey sky like a mirror laid over the missing houses. And floating in that mirror, face-down, were three of Odile's neighbors.

She knew two of them. The cooper's widow, Marta Ilves, who had sold her pickled herring at the night market for thirty years and cheated no one but herself. And a boy—she didn't know his name, ten years old perhaps, wearing a man's coat too big for him, his arms spread wide as though he'd been swimming toward something.

"Back," someone said, and hands pushed at her shoulders. "Sergeant, you can't—"

"I'm Inquisition." She showed the badge out of habit, and hated how the crowd flinched from it. Flinching was new. For twelve years the closed eye had meant order, dull and dependable, the eye that saw everything and did nothing dramatic. This morning people looked at her the way sailors look at a change in the wind.

The fisherman was sitting on a bollard at the edge of the water, wrapped in three blankets, still dripping. His name was Anselm Roke; she'd pulled him out of a tavern brawl once, years ago, and he'd bought her a drink she hadn't accepted. He was staring at the flooded street with an expression Odile had no word for—not grief exactly, though grief was in it somewhere, drowned under something larger.

"Sergeant Ferrant," he said, without looking up. "You'll want to write this down."

"Anselm. What happened here?"

He laughed then, a short terrible bark. "What happened. I'll tell you what happened. I prayed."

The word landed strangely. Prayer had been illegal for twelve years—not punished with the rope, mostly, because you cannot hang a whole city, but fined, taxed, mocked out of respectability. The temples were warehouses now. The priests had scattered or signed oaths. And yet everyone prayed, quietly, the way people hum old songs with the words half-forgotten. Odile herself had prayed, once, at her mother's grave, and felt foolish and human and slightly criminal.

"You prayed," she said carefully. "For what?"

"For a good catch." His voice went flat, reciting. "I knelt on my boat last night, first time in years, I don't know why, I just—the weather was wrong, the season was wrong, and I was tired, Sergeant, tired the way you get when you're fifty and your back is going and there's no one—" He stopped. Started again. "I said, *let the sea give me everything I need.* That's all. Everything I need."

"And the sea—"

"The sea gave me everything I needed." He turned and looked at her then, and his eyes were the worst part, wide open and dry. "It gave me my neighbors' houses. It gave me timber and copper pipe and a child's coat. It gave me enough fish to feed the whole quarter—they're still washing up, look at them, silver on the cobbles like coins. It gave me everything I *need*, Sergeant. It just never asked what I wanted."

Behind him, men with hooks were working the water's edge, dragging out the dead. Nobody spoke. Somewhere in the flooded dark, a church bell that had sunk with the street was still ringing, muffled, patient, as if it intended to go on ringing until someone answered.

Odile wrote it down. *Fisherman's prayer answered. Street lost. Three dead, likely more.* Her pen hesitated over the next line, the line the forms required, the line she had written a hundred times in twelve years: *Cause: unlawful invocation.*

Her pen would not move. Because the cause was not unlawful invocation. The cause was a tired man kneeling on a wet deck asking the sea for help, and the sea, after twelve years of silence, saying *yes*—saying yes the way a wall says yes when it falls on you.

* * *

By evening, the second miracle had come in, and this one was worse, because it involved a child.

The chapel of Saint Verene—it had been a chapel of some older god before that, and would be again, Odile thought grimly, if this kept up—stood in the Tanner's Quarter, small and beloved, deconsecrated on paper but never in the hearts of the women who swept its steps. A girl of six, daughter of one of those sweeping women, had been dying of fever for three days. And on the third night, the story went, the girl had crawled out of her bed, knelt on the chapel floor among the dust and the stored barrels, and prayed for rain.

Not for health. Not for her mother. Six years old, burning with fever, and she had asked for rain—because rain, she told the Inquisitors afterward in a small cracked voice, was what her father used to say the fields needed, back before the fields were taken, and she had gotten confused, or maybe she hadn't been confused at all, maybe a six-year-old simply prays for what the world needs rather than what she does.

The rain came through the roof.

It came through the roof of a sealed building on a cloudless night, and it did not stop. By dawn the chapel was a well. By noon it was a lake, black water standing hip-deep in the streets around it, pouring in through every door and window as though the building had become a mouth. They got the girl out—alive, fever broken, dripping and serene—and they got eleven others out, and they did not get out the old sexton, who had slept in the vestry and who had, Odile learned later, spent the Purge Years informing on his congregation to save his own skin. The water had found him first. It had held him, witnesses said, gently, the way you hold something you intend to keep.

Odile stood in the flooded nave up to her thighs in chill water, holding a lantern above the surface, and looked at the drowned saints. Their statues stood along the walls with water to their chins, faces calm, and in the lantern light the ripples made them seem to breathe.

"They're coming back," said the woman beside her—one of the sweepers, soaked to the waist, refusing to leave. "That's what this is. You can feel it, can't you? Like a house when someone puts a key in the lock. All day I've felt it. The whole city feels it."

"The city should feel the Inquisition," Odile said, and heard how hollow it came out of her, words spoken by rote into water.

The woman looked at her with ancient patience. "And what will the Inquisition do, Sergeant? Drown us?"

* * *

The edicts went up at dusk, pasted to every wall in the Province by clerks on ladders, white paper on wet stone:

*BY ORDER OF THE HOLY INQUISITION OF THE PROVINCE: Let it be known that the recent events at Saltwharf and the Tanner's Quarter are acts of unlawful invocation, being prayers answered outside the sanction of law. All persons having prayed, having witnessed prayer, or having knowledge of prayer answered are required to report to the nearest Inquisition office within three days. Silence is complicity. Complicity is sedition. The Province thanks its obedient children.*

*Province*, Odile noted. Even the edicts couldn't quite say *kingdom* anymore, even to forbid it. You could not forbid what you refused to name.

She stood in the square watching people read. They read in silence, which was itself terrifying—in twelve years she had never seen a Vellagard crowd silent. They read, and they looked at one another, and Odile understood with a cold sliding feeling in her gut that the edict was useless, worse than useless, because you cannot order people to stop praying. Prayer was the one crime committed entirely inside the skull. Every person in that square had already prayed that day—for safety, for rain, for their children, for the dead of Saltwharf—and every one of them now knew, with the certainty of the newly converted, that someone was listening.

A boy near her, maybe eight, tugged his mother's sleeve and pointed at the edict. "Mama, what's unlawful invocation?"

"Hush."

"But if I pray for bread, will the bread come?"

"*Hush.*"

But he wouldn't be hushed, because he was eight and the world had just become interesting. "Did the little girl pray the flood? Will God give me things? Mama, will God hear me?"

His mother dragged him away, and around them the silence broke into a hundred murmurs, and Odile stood alone in the middle of it with her faded badge and her doubt, which had grown all day from a seed to a tree.

Here was the doubt, stated plainly, though she would never state it aloud: the Inquisition existed to keep the gods out. That had been its founding purpose, dressed up in other language—order, purity, the protection of the faithful from superstition—but that was the bone of it. And the gods were coming back anyway, through cracks nobody had authorized, answering prayers with the indiscriminate generosity of a flood. Punishing the praying would be like punishing the tide. And yet the alternative—the only alternative the Inquisition possessed—was Halden's.

She had seen Inquisitor Halden that afternoon, in the corridor outside the archive, standing before the great seal of the Province with his hands clasped behind his back, reading the edict draft with an expression Odile had never seen him wear before. She had known Halden nine years. He was a patient man, a cold one, meticulous as an accountant, and his face ordinarily showed nothing but mild professional interest in the sins of others. But today, reading about drowned streets and answered prayers, he had looked—there was no other word—

*Hungry.*

"Remarkable, isn't it, Sergeant," he had said, without turning. "Twelve years we told them the gods were dead. And it turns out we were wrong in the most useful possible way."

"Useful, Inquisitor?"

"Everything that returns," Halden said, "returns to someone. The question of our age, Sergeant, is not whether the gods come home. It is who holds the door when they do." And he had smiled at the seal on the wall, faintly, the way a man smiles at a contract nearly concluded.

She had walked away from that conversation with her skin crawling and no defensible reason why. Nothing he said was treasonous. Nothing he said was even unusual. But she had served under men who burned villages in the Purge Years, and she knew the smell of a mind arranging a catastrophe it intends to profit from.

Now, in the square, in the murmuring dark, she took out her report book and began the day's summary. *Two miracles. Three dead at Saltwharf, one at Saint Verene. Edicts posted. Public compliance: none. Public fear:* —and here her pen stopped again, hovering, because the honest answer was not *of the gods*.

Public fear: of us.

She looked at the word. She thought about the boy in the man's coat, floating in the mirror-still water of Pilchard Row. She thought about the sweeper woman asking what the Inquisition would do—drown us?—and about Halden's hungry smile, and about her mother's hand on her shoulder twelve years ago in the rain, heavier than a hand should be, as if her mother had known even then what was being taken and could not say so.

Odile crossed out *fear* and wrote, in smaller letters, *grief*, and then she closed the book, because the form had no column for grief either.

Above the square, the first stars were out, and between two rooftops she could see the old boundary stone at the street's end, the one carved with the salt-mark of a guild that officially had never existed. Someone had left an offering against it—a knot of braided string, a cup of oil, fresh, tonight, hours old.

Someone in this city was still making charms. Still doing the old work. Still keeping, or failing to keep, whatever seals were failing.

Odile Ferrant looked at the offering for a long moment. Then, moving like a woman committing a crime—which, by the light of the new edicts, she was—she memorized the sigil pressed into the wax beside the oil, and walked away without reporting it.
