## 4. Salt on the Tongue

Ines had not slept. The charm that worked sat on his table like a confession, and every time he looked at it, his stomach turned over with a slow, cold dread.

He had made it the way he made all of them: fever-salt ground fine as flour, folded into wax while it was still warm enough to take an impression, sealed with a knot of red thread and a word spoken under the breath—a word that meant nothing, that was supposed to mean nothing, that was only habit, only the shape of a prayer his master had taught him to mouth so the salt would remember it was salt and not merely powder. The guild called it *the courtesy*. A politeness paid to nothing, because there was nothing left to pay it to.

Except something had answered.

He turned the charm in the grey light coming through his shutters. The wax held the impression of his thumb, and inside the impression—faintly, the way a footprint holds water—the salt had *bloomed*. Crystallized into a pattern too regular to be accident: a six-pointed star, delicate as frost on glass, perfect as a mason's mark.

His master's voice came back to him across twelve years, dry as chalk dust: *Salt is honest, boy. It doesn't lie, and it doesn't imagine. If you see a pattern in it, a hand made the pattern.*

Whose hand?

There was one way to find out, and it was the last thing he wanted to do. The boundary stones.

* * *

Vellagard had been built against its own edges, the way old cities are—layer on layer, wall behind wall, until nobody remembered which ring of stone had been the first defense and which was only the memory of one. But the boundary stones were older than any of it. Twelve of them, set in a circle wider than the city itself, sunk into earth and road and riverbank at points no map agreed upon. The Saltmakers' Guild had dressed them once a year, every year, for four hundred years: a rite of white ash and coarse grain, worked by nine masters walking sunwise, each carrying a bowl they were forbidden to set down.

Ines had been eleven the last time he'd walked with them. He remembered the weight of the bowl, the burn of brine in a hangnail, his master's hand steady on his shoulder. He remembered thinking it was boring. He remembered thinking it would happen again next year.

It hadn't. Nothing had happened again. That was the whole point of the Edict, wasn't it? Nothing happened, forever, by law.

The nearest stone stood in a tanner's yard off Corviser Lane, half-buried under hides stretched to dry, and Ines reached it in the hour before dawn, when even the tanners' dogs were asleep. He went over the wall rather than through the gate—habit older than fear—and crouched beside the stone with his heart going hard against his ribs.

It was smaller than memory. A knee-high pillar of grey granite, lichened, scarred where cartwheels had kissed it over the centuries. Ordinary. Except for the cap of salt.

Every boundary stone wore a cap of salt, pressed and hardened like a crust of ancient bread, renewed yearly, meant to outlast everything. Ines put his palm against it.

Cold. Colder than the night justified. And soft—not soft the way stone is worn smooth, but soft the way fruit is soft past its time. His fingers sank into the crust as if into rotted wood, and when he drew them back, they came away grey.

Not white. Grey. Salt gone the color of ash, crumbling, threaded through with hairline channels like the burrows of some insect too small to see. He rubbed it between finger and thumb and tasted it, because that was what guild-trained tongues did, automatically, before thought could intervene.

It tasted of iron and rot and, underneath, faintly, of *honey*.

Ines sat back on his heels and felt the night get colder around him.

Salt did not rot. Salt was the one thing in creation that kept—that was its whole nature, its whole office, the reason four hundred years of masters had carried their bowls sunwise without setting them down. Salt kept. It kept meat and it kept wounds and it kept *doors*, and if the salt on a boundary stone had corroded through, then either the world's oldest law had been repealed—

—or someone had repealed it.

He moved along the circle as the sky went from black to bruise. The second stone, in a drainage ditch beyond the Shambles: corroded, the crust holed clean through in one place, a gap the width of two fingers, and through the gap the air moved *wrong*, a slow exhalation that smelled of honey and hot copper and something floral that had no business existing anywhere north of a dream. The third stone, behind a shrine to a god whose name had been legally struck from record: not just corroded but *licked*, the salt melted into runnels like candlewax, and at its base, pressed into the mud, a print.

Not a boot. Not a hoof. Something between—a long splayed thing, three-toed, the size of a dinner plate, already filling with groundwater that steamed faintly, though the morning was cold.

Ines stood very still and listened to his own blood.

Twelve stones. He could not check twelve stones; the light was coming up, and a man crouching over boundary stones at dawn was a man asking questions of himself in public. But he didn't need twelve. Three was a verdict. Someone was walking the circle, unsealing the country stone by stone, and they had been at it for months—years, maybe—for corrosion like this took seasons, took patience, took *method*. This was not a crack. This was a door being opened from the outside, carefully, by someone who knew exactly what the salt was for.

Which meant someone alive still knew what the salt was for. And Ines Oyelaran, last apprentice of the Saltmakers' Guild, hiding his sigil beneath his sleeve for twelve years, had thought he was the only one.

A bell rang somewhere across the city—the early watch, changing shift. Ines pulled his sleeve down over the salt-stains on his fingers and went home the long way, and all the way home the taste stayed on his tongue, sweet and wrong, like a prayer answered by the wrong mouth.

* * *

He lasted two days before the not-knowing became worse than the danger.

On the second evening he went out again, and this time he went armed—not with steel, which he'd never learned, but with salt: a pouch of coarse grain at his belt, a twist of paper holding the last of his master's true preparation, the heavy stuff, the kind mixed with tears and iron filings that the guild had used for *closing* things. He told himself he was only going to look. He told himself a great many things, and believed none of them.

The fourth stone stood alone on the riverbank below the drowned chapel, where the water had claimed the nave years ago and left the tower standing like a man waist-deep refusing to admit defeat. Ines came down through the reeds in full dark, and stopped.

Someone was already there.

A figure knelt at the stone, hooded, working at the salt cap with tools that caught the moonlight—small chisels, a brush, a clay pot that gave off, even at forty paces, that smell of honey and hot copper. Working *carefully*. Not smashing, not defacing. Excavating. The way a scholar opens a tomb.

Ines should have run. Every sensible nerve said run. Instead he did the thing his master had trained into his hands without ever meaning to: he read the work. And what he read froze him where he stood, because the figure was not destroying the seal.

They were *redirecting* it. Chiseling channels in the old crust, laying new lines of fresh salt—grey salt, tainted salt—in patterns Ines almost recognized. Guild geometry. Degenerate, corrupted, secondhand—but guild geometry nonetheless. Whoever this was had learned the craft from someone, somewhere, badly.

And they were not unsealing the country at random. They were building *doors*. Specific doors, aimed at—what? Ines traced the angles with his eyes and felt his stomach drop. The lines converged inward. Not outward, toward freedom for whatever waited beyond the seals. Inward, toward the city. Toward Vellagard. Toward a single point somewhere in the tangle of streets and cellars where all these careful channels met.

Someone was not letting the gods back in.

Someone was *summoning* them—to a destination of their choosing.

The reeds shifted behind him.

Ines turned, and the night turned with him, and there—ten paces off, lantern unshuttered, throwing his shadow long and guilty across the mud—stood a woman in Inquisition grey. Short-cropped hair, a sergeant's knots on her shoulder, a sword at her hip and a ledger under her arm, and eyes that went from Ines to the kneeling figure at the stone and back with the rapid arithmetic of someone counting exits.

"Both of you," she said, "are under edict arrest. Move and I—" She stopped. Because Ines had already moved, but not toward her; he'd dropped to a crouch, and his hand had found the boundary stone, and his other hand had torn open the paper twist, and the heavy salt—his master's true preparation, tears and iron—was pouring between his fingers onto the corrupted crust.

"What are you *doing*—" the sergeant began.

Closing it, he wanted to say. What does it look like. What I was born for. What none of you ever let anyone be born for again.

What actually happened was stranger. The moment the true salt touched the corrupted crust, the stone *screamed*—not in sound, in pressure, a pulse that Ines felt in his teeth and his breastbone and the fluid of his eyes, and the honey-smell flared to a reek, and down in the river the water bulged upward in a slow dome as though something enormous underneath had turned over in its sleep. The hooded figure fled—up the bank, fast, dropping tools, gone into the dark like water into sand.

"Ines—" the sergeant started to say, and then stopped, frowning, as if she'd heard the name from somewhere she couldn't place. "Hands. Show me your hands."

He showed her his hands. They were full of salt, and the salt was glowing, faintly, the blue-white of starlight on snow, and there was no explanation in the world that covered that.

Her sword cleared its scabbard. "On the ground. Now."

Ines ran.

Not toward the lane—she'd have cut him off, and besides, his feet had already chosen, carrying him along a route he'd never walked but that some older, deeper map seemed to know: down the bank, along the chapel's drowned foundation, to a seam in the stonework below the waterline where a slab leaned away from darkness. Smugglers' work. Brannoc Vale's people had used these tunnels since before the Edict, everyone knew that much and no one said it aloud. Ines had bought luck-knots from Brannoc's crew for years; he had never asked where the tunnels went.

He asked now, with his whole body, diving through the seam into cold black air, hearing behind him the splash of boots and the sergeant's voice cracking out across the water—*"Stop, or I swear it—"*, and then, farther, fainter, the sound of her stopping at the seam's edge. Not following. Not yet. Writing something down instead—he could hear the scratch of her pencil, absurd, terrifying, meticulous.

Ines felt his way down the tunnel by touch, salt still clenched in his fist, glowing faintly through his fingers like a caught star. The dark smelled of river-mud and lamp-oil and, very far ahead, of smoke and tar—lived-in dark, trafficked dark. The smugglers' dark.

Behind him, above him, through the stone, the city lay under its lawful silence, and Ines understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the silence was over. Not ending—over. He had glowed in front of an officer of the Inquisition. There was a description of him now, in a ledger, in pencil, in a neat hand: name, face, hands full of light. Whatever invisibility he had bought with twelve years of small charms and hidden sigils, he had spent it tonight, all of it, in the space of ten heartbeats.

And the hooded figure was still out there. Still working. Still building doors that pointed *inward*, toward the city, toward a center Ines couldn't yet name—and now the Inquisition would be hunting both of them, and only one of them knew what the seals were for.

Only one of them had ever known.

He needed help. Not the kind you buy with a luck-knot. The kind that comes with a life-debt attached, and a crew, and a boat, and no love whatsoever for the men who wrote the Edict.

Ines put his head down and followed the tunnel toward the smell of tar, the glow leaking between his fingers lighting the dark ahead of him step by step, whether he willed it or not.

Brannoc Vale owed his master a life. Tonight, Ines had come to collect.
