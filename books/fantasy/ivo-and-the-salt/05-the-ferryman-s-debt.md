## 5. The Ferryman's Debt

Brannoc Vale had three rules for the *Salt-Widow*, and he kept them the way other men kept religion: absolutely, and without much joy.

One — no cargo that breathed. Two — no passengers who prayed. Three — no one aboard who owed him money, because debt was a rope with two ends, and he'd been hanged by both before.

He was breaking all three tonight, and he hadn't even agreed to anything yet.

The tide was coming in over the Mireflats, black water sliding up through the reeds like something patient looking for ankles to bite. From the deck of his flat-bottomed barge, moored against the rotted pilings of the old ferry landing, Brannoc could see Vellagard's lights across the estuary — the good ones on the Spireward terraces, gold and steady as teeth, and below them the night market's scatter of paper lanterns, trembling every time the wind came off the flats. Somewhere between those two kinds of light, he knew, trouble was being born. Trouble was always being born there. It had been born there twelve years ago, on the day they read the Edict aloud from the customs steps and declared that the country he'd been born in had never existed at all.

Brannoc had been a ferryman then. Now he was a smuggler, which was the same trade with better margins and worse company.

"Captain." Wren's voice came down through the hatch, thin and dry as paper. "We've got a visitor walking the flats. Alone. No lantern."

Brannoc spat over the rail. "Then he's either a fool or a ghost, and I don't buy from either."

"He's carrying salt," Wren said. "I can smell it from here."

That changed things. Brannoc went still, one hand on the rail, listening to the water slap the hull. Nobody carried salt openly anymore. Salt was guild-goods, and the guild was nine empty chairs and a sealed hall the Inquisition used for storing confiscated prayer-books. Salt on a man's person after dark was worth a fine, a flogging, or depending on the mood of the officer, a slow walk off the end of a pier.

"Get the lamp lit," he said finally. "Low. And wake Sera. If it's a trap, I want a priestess on deck — even a defrocked one frightens people. They keep expecting her to smite somebody."

* * *

Sera Undine came up the ladder with her collar-less robe belted with rope and her hands already folded in the old attitude of blessing, which she wore now mostly out of irony. She had been a priestess of the Quiet Tide once, back when priesthoods were legal and gods were merely silent instead of outlawed. The defrocking had involved paperwork, she liked to say. The burning had involved more paperwork.

"You look terrible," she told Brannoc, by way of greeting.

"I feel worse. There's a man on my flats."

"There's always a man on your flats. You have very public flats."

"This one smells of salt and doesn't carry a light." Brannoc watched the dark shape come on across the mud, careful-footed, unhurried. "And he's walking the safe path. The one only locals know."

That silenced her. The safe path across the Mireflats wound between sink-holes that would swallow a grown man to the hips and hold him until the tide did its work. It wasn't marked. It couldn't be marked — the Inquisition flattened anything that looked like local knowledge. It was passed mouth to mouth, song to song, and it was the closest thing the drowned country still had to a border crossing.

The man reached the landing without so much as wetting his boots.

Up close he was younger than Brannoc expected — twenty, maybe twenty-two, narrow-shouldered, with the kind of face that markets made: watchful, pleasant at the edges, tired underneath. He wore a fishmonger's oilskin over a finer shirt, and his sleeves were rolled down and buttoned at the wrist, which in this weather meant nothing good. People buttoned their sleeves when they were hiding something on their forearms.

"Evening," the young man said. His voice was market-soft, pitched not to carry. "Is this the *Salt-Widow*?"

"It's a wreck with opinions," Brannoc said. "Who's asking?"

"Ines Oyelaran." A pause, weighted, deliberate. "Apprentice to Master Corvane Aldous, of the Saltmakers' Guild. Who pulled you out of the Gray Reach nine years ago, Captain Vale, when you were drowning in a net of your own contraband, and who never told you what it cost him to do it."

The name went through Brannoc like a fishhook finding old scar tissue. Nine years. He remembered the cold, the green-black water, the way the net had wrapped his legs like the sea deciding to keep him. And he remembered the old man on the pier above — guild robes hidden under a laborer's coat, hauling him up hand over hand with arms too thin for the work, and afterward pressing a paper-wrapped packet into his shaking hands and saying only: *You owe the craft, not me. The craft collects when it needs to.*

Brannoc had spent nine years hoping the craft would forget.

"Corvane's dead," he said. His voice came out flatter than he intended.

"I know," said Ines Oyelaran. "I buried him. Or what the Inquisitor left of him to bury."

* * *

They took tea in the cabin, because Brannoc refused to conduct business on open deck where any spyglass on the far shore might read their lips. The cabin was low and smelled of tar, tallow, and the particular staleness of men who lived on water. Wren sat in the corner with a slate balanced on his knees, ostensibly doing accounts, actually drawing Ines's face from memory with unsettling accuracy. Sera sat where she could see the door. Pell — the boy, fourteen maybe, mute since the fever year, all elbows and enormous grey eyes — perched on the cargo netting above them like a gargoyle nobody had invited but nobody had the heart to evict.

Ines unbuttoned his left sleeve.

Brannoc had guessed a brand, a fugitive's mark, an Inquisitorial tattoo. What he saw instead made Sera inhale sharply through her nose — and Sera did not inhale sharply through her nose, ever; she considered it undignified.

A sigil was inked into the skin of the forearm, old-style, done with needle and salt-ash: nine interlocking rings around a single grain, the mark of the Saltmakers. But it wasn't the mark itself that made the air in the cabin change. It was that the mark was *moving* — faintly, slowly, the way heat-shimmer moves over a summer road. The rings turned. The grain at the center pulsed like a small heart.

"That shouldn't do that," Sera said quietly. "Guild sigils are registry marks. Ink and oath. They don't... breathe."

"They didn't," Ines said. "Three weeks ago, they didn't." He rolled the sleeve back down, and Brannoc realized he'd been holding his breath. "Master Corvane is dead. The other eight masters are dead or scattered. Which makes me, by the guild's own charter, the last holder of its obligations. Including," and here he looked directly at Brannoc, with the flat patience of someone reciting terms he'd memorized in grief, "its debts receivable. You owe the craft a life, Captain. I've come to collect."

The lantern hissed. Above them, Pell shifted on the netting, and the boy's eyes had gone strange — wide, unfocused, fixed on some middle distance where, Brannoc had learned, the boy heard things none of them could. Pell couldn't speak, but he could point, and he was pointing now, slowly, at Ines's covered forearm, with an expression on his face that Brannoc had only seen twice before: once during a storm that turned back from their bow for no reason, and once the night the chapel bells rang with nobody pulling the ropes.

"What does he hear?" Sera asked softly.

Pell answered by tapping his own chest, then pointing at Ines again, then making with both hands the old sign — the one from before the Edict, the one mothers used, the one that had survived twelve years of erasure because you can't legislate a gesture out of a people's bones. The sign for *salt*. The sign for *protection*.

"He says you're marked," Sera translated, though translation was hardly needed. "He says the salt knows you."

Ines went very still. "Your boy hears the cracks," he said. Not a question.

"We don't use words like 'cracks' at my table," Brannoc said automatically, and then heard how foolish it sounded, sitting as they were under a moving sigil, hosting a guild apprentice who'd walked the unmarked flats at night. He rubbed his jaw. "Fine. Cracks. Say your piece, apprentice. Why does the craft need a smuggler?"

So Ines told them. He told it plainly, without theater, which Brannoc found himself respecting — the boy had clearly learned from a master that the truth delivered quietly lands harder than the truth shouted. He told them about the charm sold to a frightened mother that should have been inert and had worked. About the prayers being answered all across Vellagard, wildly, wrongly, a fisherman's wish drowning his own street, a child's rain-prayer flooding a chapel to its rafters. About the boundary stones he'd tested with guild-salt and found corroded — eaten through, not by weather, but by design. Someone was unsealing the country. Deliberately. Carefully. Letting things through one crack at a time.

"And the Inquisition?" Wren asked from his corner, not looking up from his slate. He had a forger's habit of never looking directly at important things.

"Hunting whoever they decide is guilty. Which so far means anyone poor, anyone foreign, and anyone who ever whispered a prayer." Ines's jaw tightened. "There's an Inquisitor named Halden. He killed my master. He'll be widening the net."

Brannoc stared into his tea. He thought about rule two — no passengers who prayed — and about how rules were just ballast, and ballast existed to be jettisoned when the ship was sinking. He thought about the Edict of Nothingness, the day the criers read it out and a woman beside him in the crowd had asked, genuinely bewildered, *then where am I standing?* He thought about nine years of owing a debt to a dead man whose apprentice was sitting in his cabin smelling of salt and courage and bad decisions.

"Here's what I can offer," Brannoc said at last. "Passage. One run, anywhere inside the estuary and the river roads. Food, such as it is. A bunk you'll share with the cargo." He held up a finger before relief could soften the boy's face. "Not alliance. Not yet. I don't know you, apprentice, and I've known the craft to collect debts in ways I didn't care for. You get one run. We'll see what you're made of."

"Captain," Sera began.

"One run," Brannoc repeated, and hated how much it sounded like a promise.

* * *

It was Pell who ended the argument before it could properly begin.

Later — near midnight, the tide turned, the barge rocking gently at its moorings — the boy climbed down from the netting and stood in front of Ines with the gravity of a much older creature wearing a child's body. Then he reached out and took Ines's sleeve, and rolled it back with careful fingers, and laid his palm flat against the moving sigil.

Nothing happened. That was the thing. Nothing happened, and it was the loudest nothing Brannoc had ever witnessed — the lantern flame standing perfectly straight, the water outside going glass-still, Wren's chalk stopping mid-line with a small squeak. Even the wind off the flats seemed to be holding its breath, waiting to see what the salt would say.

Pell's eyes rolled back. His lips moved, shaping soundless syllables — and Sera, white-faced, caught the boy as he swayed and signed rapidly with her free hand: *He's fine. He's listening. Don't touch him.*

When Pell opened his eyes again, he looked at Ines with an expression Brannoc had no name for. Pity, maybe. Or recognition — the way one soldier recognizes another from a war only they survived.

The boy stepped back and made signs, quick and urgent, and Sera translated with visible reluctance, her voice dropping lower with each clause.

"He says the doors are opening from both sides. He says—" she faltered, started again. "He says the salt remembers being an oath, and oaths are waking up. He says something is coming up the river road behind you, apprentice. Something that followed your smell of salt across the city tonight. He says you brought it to our landing."

Wren set down his slate. "How long do we have?"

Pell held up his hand, fingers spread, and folded them down one at a time. Five. Four. Three—

Brannoc was already moving. "Wren, loose the mooring lines. Sera, douse the lamp. Apprentice—" he grabbed Ines by the shoulder and found the boy's eyes steady, terrified, and utterly unwilling to run, which was either courage or stupidity and on the water the two handled identically. "—you wanted to see what the craft collected. Get below, and pray to whatever's left that you bought us more than a head start."

Out on the black flats, far off toward the river road, a light appeared. Then another. Lanterns, moving in formation, unhurried, certain — the walk of men with warrants and no doubts.

Brannoc put his shoulder to the sweep-pole and pushed the *Salt-Widow* out into the current, into the dark, carrying a debt, a stranger, and a boy who heard gods — and understood, with the calm of a man watching his last rule burn, that the one run had just become a voyage.
