## 6. Edict of Nothingness

Twelve years before the charm that worked, and one year before the silence broke, Ines Oyelaran was eleven years old and knew exactly three things about the world: that salt kept things out, that his master's hands smelled of brine and old paper, and that the King of Vellagard was a story adults told each other to make the cold seem organized.

He learned a fourth thing on the morning the criers came.

They came up Saltrow at dawn, when the fog off the harbor was thick enough to chew, and their bells had a wrongness in them that made even the gulls go quiet. Not the market bell. Not the chapel bell. A third sound, flat and official, the sound of something being read *at* you rather than *to* you. Ines was sweeping the guildhall steps—he swept them every morning, though no one but Master Corvess ever walked on them—and he stopped with the broom half-raised because the sound went through his chest like a nail through a board.

There were six criers. They wore the Crown's gray and silver, and they did not look at the guildhall as they passed. That was the first strange thing. Everyone looked at the guildhall. It sat at the top of Saltrow like a jaw full of stone teeth, its nine chimneys smoking nine different colors depending on what the masters were boiling, and travelers crossing the harbor bridge always pointed at it and said, *there, that's where the salt comes from, the salt that keeps us safe.* But the criers looked straight ahead as if the building were not there at all, and Ines understood later that this was not rudeness. It was rehearsal. They had been instructed, very precisely, not to see it.

Master Corvess appeared behind him. She did this sometimes—old people moved quietly in a house built by quiet hands—and Ines felt her palm settle on his head like a warm stone.

"Keep sweeping," she said. "But sweep slowly."

"Why?"

"Because I want you to hear every word." Her thumb moved once across his scalp, an absent blessing. "And then I want you to forget none of it."

* * *

The proclamation was read from the harbor steps, where the tide could serve as witness, and Ines stood at the edge of the crowd with salt dust still on his sleeves, holding his broom like a spear he didn't know how to use. The reader was a narrow man with a voice like a ledger being balanced, and he wore the Crown's gray and the Crown's silver and nothing else—not the livery of any house Ines knew, not the colors of any guild or temple or street. He belonged to no one. That was the trick of it, Ines realized much later. The man reading had been chosen precisely because he came from nowhere, so that nowhere itself could do the speaking.

"Hear now," the man said, "the final determination of the Crown Regnant concerning the territory formerly styled the Kingdom of Vellagard."

*Formerly styled.* Four words, and already the ground was tilting.

"By decree of the Crown, the said Kingdom is determined never to have lawfully existed. Its charters are void ab initio. Its borders are declared administrative fictions. Its laws are hereby unmade, having never been law. Its debts, its titles, its oaths, and its gods are declared null, being the instruments of a sovereignty that was never sovereign."

The crowd did not understand yet. Ines could feel it around him, a thousand bodies breathing in the fog, waiting for the sentence that would make sense of the nonsense. Surely it was a clerical matter. Surely some tax dispute, some border renegotiation with the southern principalities. People shifted their baskets. A fishwife near Ines muttered, "Void of *what*, then?" and her neighbor hushed her without knowing why.

"The persons resident within the former territory shall be deemed persons without kingdom, whose names shall be struck from the rolls of nations and entered upon the rolls of administration. Their prayers shall be addressed to no recipient, there being no recipients. Their dead shall rest in unregistered ground. Their history shall be held in trust by the Crown until such time as the Crown determines it never occurred."

A child began to cry somewhere behind Ines—not from understanding, but because the grown-ups had gone so quiet that the silence frightened her. Ines did not cry. He was doing arithmetic, the way Master Corvess had taught him, breaking the enormous into pieces small enough to hold. If the laws were never law, then the guild's charter was never a charter. If the charter was never a charter, then the saltmakers were never licensed. If the saltmakers were never licensed—

Then the salt that kept the old gods out of the world had been poured by no one, under the authority of nothing, for a country that did not exist.

His hands went cold around the broom handle.

"And let it be known," the crier concluded, and here his voice did something terrible—it warmed, it softened, it took on the gentle cadence of a kindness being done, "that no punishment is intended, no penalty assessed, and no malice borne toward the persons formerly of the Kingdom of Vellagard. They have lost nothing. There was never anything to lose."

* * *

They walked back up Saltrow through a city that was busy pretending it hadn't heard. Shopkeepers opened their shutters. Bakers fired their ovens. A woman beat a rug from an upper window with the steady violence of someone refusing a thought. This was the genius of the Edict, Ines would come to understand: it demanded nothing of you. It asked no renunciation, required no ceremony, burned no books in the square. You simply woke up the next morning inside a country that had never existed, and if you were wise, you went on living in it anyway, quietly, the way a ghost goes on haunting a house it is too tired to leave.

Only the guildhall resisted. As Ines and Master Corvess climbed the hill, the building seemed to lean toward them, its nine chimneys still smoking their nine colors—white for warding-salt, blue for boundary-work, green for the fever-salts, and the others Ines could not yet name—all of them going about the business of keeping the world sealed as if paperwork were weather, as if a decree could boil the sea dry.

Inside, the Hall of Chairs was already filling. Ines had never seen all the masters together in one room. There were nine chairs, ancient and high-backed, carved from salt-bleached timber, and eight of them were occupied by old men and older women who looked, this morning, suddenly mortal. The ninth chair—the Chair of the First Pour, at the head of the room—stood empty, as it always had, reserved for the founder who had died four hundred years ago and left instructions that no one should ever be promoted into absence. Ines slipped along the wall to his corner, the apprentice's corner, a single stool beneath the window where the light came in gray and honest.

Master Corvess did not sit. She stood in the center of the floor, and Ines watched her take off her rings—one, two, three, each set with a different salt crystal—and lay them on the arm of her chair with the care of a surgeon setting down knives.

"You've all heard it," she said. "Some of you twice. I'll not read it back to you; my throat won't survive the words a second time." She looked around at them. "I'll say only what matters. The Edict does not order us to stop. That's the trap. Read it again tonight and you'll see—there is no clause commanding the cessation of our work. Because a command can be defied. And defiance," her voice cracked like drying mud, "defiance requires a subject. You cannot disobey a law that never was, in a country that never did, on behalf of a crown that never reigned. To defy the Edict would be to admit the kingdom existed. And the moment we admit it existed, we inherit every debt it owed, every war it fought, every oath it broke—including," and here she looked at the empty chair, "the one that bought us four hundred years of quiet nights."

An old master near the window—Halewyn, whose beard was white as his own product—spoke for the first time in Ines's memory. "Then we pour in secret. We've done it before. The Silence Years—"

"The Silence Years ended because we signed the Accords, and the Accords were law." Corvess shook her head. "Nothing we sign now binds anyone. Nothing we build stands on anything. Don't you see? They haven't outlawed the salt. They've outlawed the *ground under it*. You cannot be a heretic against a church that was never consecrated. You can only be—" she stopped, and Ines watched her choose the word the way she chose crystals, testing each one for weight, "—you can only be *unlicensed*. Forever. Unlicensed, unwitnessed, unprotected. Every jar we seal from this day forward seals us inside it with the work."

The hall was silent except for the chimneys, which drew wind and made the low moan Ines had slept beside all his life, the sound he had thought, until this morning, was simply the sound of safety.

Halewyn rose. He crossed to the head of the room, to the empty Chair of the First Pour, and laid his hand on its armrest the way you touch a dying friend. "Then the question is not whether we continue," he said. "We continue. Salt doesn't ask permission of kings. The question is who signs."

"Signs what?" someone asked. "There's nothing to sign."

"There is always something to sign," Halewyn said. "That's how they'll know we understood."

* * *

It took them nine days to draft the surrender, because the guild did nothing without ritual, and the ritual of surrender turned out to be longer than the ritual of war. Ines spent those nine days doing everything an eleven-year-old apprentice does—grinding, stirring, memorizing the grain-tables, fetching water from the deep cistern that tasted of minerals and centuries—but he did it all while listening, the way Master Corvess had taught him to listen, with his whole spine.

On the ninth day, they brought the document down to the harbor.

Ines had expected soldiers. There were almost none—two officers in gray and silver, standing at ease on the tide-steps, bored as customs men. That was the horror of it, Ines thought later. The end of a kingdom required less ceremony than the closing of a fish stall. The officers had come not to enforce anything but to *witness*, because a surrender unwitnessed is just a letter, and letters burn.

The document lay on a trestle table between two candles. Ines read it over and over while the masters argued in low voices about the final clause. It was written in the Crown's plain hand, no flourishes, and it was short—shorter than a recipe. *The Guild of Saltmakers of the territory formerly styled the Kingdom of Vellagard, being a body without lawful existence, surrenders its instruments, its seals, and its works into administrative custody. The Guild affirms that its charter was never granted, its authority never conferred, and its gods never answered.*

*Its gods never answered.*

Ines stared at that line until the letters swam. He was eleven years old, and he had been raised on the truth the whole coast knew: the gods had answered, once, terribly, wonderfully, until the salt was poured and the doors were closed and the world got four hundred years of sleep. The line wasn't a lie exactly. It was worse than a lie. It was a lie that had been *notarized*.

Master Corvess approached the table last. She had put her rings back on—all three—and she carried the guild seal in a cloth at her breast like a bird with a broken wing. When she reached the trestle, she paused, and her eyes found Ines across the crowded little square, found him the way they always found him, instantly, as if he glowed.

She beckoned.

He crossed the square with his heart hammering, and she drew him up beside her, her hand heavy on his shoulder, and said, quietly, so that only he could hear: "Watch closely, love. Someone has to remember how it was done properly."

Then she uncapped the ink, took up the pen, and signed.

Her name went onto the page in her own hand—the hand that had taught his, that had guided his fingers through his first sigil, that had stirred the warding-vats for forty years—and the moment the ink touched the paper, Ines felt it. Not saw. *Felt*. Something in the air over the harbor thinned, the way broth thins when you add water to stretch a meal. The gulls went up all at once. The fog pulled back from the shore like a hand withdrawn. And far away—or maybe very close, maybe right beneath the cobblestones—something that had been sleeping the long sleep behind the salt turned over in its dreams, and did not wake, but *noticed*.

One by one, the other masters signed. Halewyn. Old Mother Quill, whose hands shook so badly she had to steady the pen with both fists. Eight signatures for eight chairs, and then Corvess pressed the guild seal into the wax, and the sigil that had warded the coast for four centuries sank into the blob like a face sinking into water, and the officer in gray stamped the Crown's mark beside it—a mark Ines did not recognize, because the Crown it belonged to had been invented eleven days earlier.

"That will be all," the officer said, and rolled up the paper, and walked away with a kingdom under his arm.

* * *

That night, the guildhall kept its fires lit, which it never did. Nine chimneys smoking into the dark, and below, in the Hall of Chairs, the eight remaining masters sat awake, not speaking, watching the empty Chair of the First Pour as though it might advise them. Ines sat on his stool in the apprentice's corner, too frightened to sleep, too young to leave.

Near midnight, Master Corvess rose and came to him. She knelt—she had never knelt to him, never knelt to anyone—and took both his hands in hers. Her palms were rough as whetstones and warm as bread.

"Do you understand what happened today?" she asked.

"They made us never have been."

"No." Her grip tightened. "They made a *paper* say so. Papers are salt too, Ines—they seal things in, they keep things out, and they corrode. All seals corrode." She lifted his hands, turned them over, studied the faint salt-burn scars on his fingers that marked him as guild-raised. "Someday, when I am dust and this hall is dust, someone will stand at a boundary stone and find the sealing failed. And they will need to know that it was not the salt that failed. It was the *signing*. Remember that. Whatever comes through those cracks—and something will come, child, the gods do not sleep forever, they only wait for bad bookkeeping—whatever comes, you tell it this: we did not abandon you. We were made to say we had never promised you anything. And a promise that never was cannot be broken. Only... postponed."

She pressed something into his palm then, small and hard and cool: a crystal of guild-salt, the old kind, poured before the Edict, when the pouring still counted. "Keep it on your tongue when you're afraid. It won't save you. But it will remind your mouth what truth tastes like, so you'll know it when you hear it again."

Ines closed his fingers around it. Above them the nine chimneys smoked, and somewhere beyond the harbor, beyond the border stones, beyond the paper walls of a country that legally had never been, something vast and patient settled back down into the dark, and waited, and kept accounts.

Twelve years later, in a night market full of people buying luck against impossible odds, a charm would work that should not have worked, and an apprentice with a hidden sigil would taste salt on his tongue and finally understand what his master had spent her last decade trying to teach him:

Seals do not fail all at once.

They fail the way kingdoms do—by decree, by corrosion, by the slow patience of things that were told, in writing, that they had never been locked out at all.
