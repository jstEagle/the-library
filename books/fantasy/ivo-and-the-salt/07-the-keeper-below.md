## 7. The Keeper Below

The ash-caves had a heartbeat.

Tamsin had learned it over eleven years of listening: a slow, deep pulse that came up through the soles of her boots and settled in her sternum like a second heart she had been lent and could not return. It was not the dragon's heart, exactly. Serevan's own beat was slower than tide, slower than season, a sound more felt than heard, and on the bad nights—the nights when the light in the great hollow dimmed to the color of an ember cupped in dying hands—Tamsin would press her palm flat against the warm black stone and count his breaths the way other people counted coins they were afraid of losing.

She was counting them now.

*One.* The cavern exhaled. Ash lifted from the floor in a soft grey sigh and drifted against the current of her lamp, settling on her shoulders, her lashes, the crockery she never bothered to wash because everything in the caves wore ash eventually, even hope.

*Two.* The walls answered with their faint gold shimmer—that veinwork of old fire threaded through the rock like writing in a language only one living creature still read. When Serevan breathed out, the veins brightened. When he drew breath in, they dimmed, as if the mountain itself were borrowing light from him and forgetting to give it back.

*Three.*

Three breaths a minute. In the first year it had been nine. She kept the numbers scratched into the wall beside her sleeping pallet, a ledger no one had asked her to keep, in a hand that had grown shakier each winter. *Year one: nine. Year four: seven. Year eight: five.* Last month she had carved *four*, and the chisel had slipped, and the mark looked less like a number than like a wound.

They were coming today. That was the other thing she knew, the way she knew weather in her knees. Brannoc Vale had sent word through the smuggler channels three nights past—word wrapped in the usual gruff courtesies, *the boy hears something below, and the salt-witch wants to see the fire*. Tamsin had read the note twice by lamplight and then sat for a long time doing nothing at all, which was its own kind of prayer.

*The salt-witch.*

She had heard the name drifting up through the tunnels for months now, attached to rumors like burrs: a young man in Vellagard selling charms that worked; charms that *worked*, in a country where working charms had been illegal since before Tamsin was born, in a country that legally was not a country at all. She had told herself the stories were smoke. Smoke was plentiful down here. But Brannoc did not spend favors on smoke, and Brannoc was bringing him.

And if even half of what the mute boy heard was true—if the cracks in the world were widening, if the seals were thinning—then this stranger might be the first person in eleven years who could look at Serevan's fire and understand what he was seeing.

Tamsin set another log on the small cookfire she permitted herself—a fire so small it embarrassed her, a candle's ambition—and went to check the dragon's water.

* * *

The way down to the great hollow was a stair cut by no human hand. The Vellagard masons called such places *godwork* and forbade them; the smugglers called them *shortcuts* and charged extra for them; Tamsin called it *home*, which said more about her than she liked to examine. Two hundred steps spiraling through stone veined with that patient gold light, the air growing warmer and stranger with each turn, until the smell changed—from cold mineral to something older, animal, vast: hot copper and rain-on-stone and beneath it all a sweetness like the inside of a struck bell.

Serevan lay coiled in the hollow at the bottom like a hill that had decided to dream.

He was smaller than the dragons of the murals—everything was smaller than the murals—but he was still too large for her eyes to hold whole. She had stopped trying, years ago. Instead she had learned him in pieces, the way you learn a coastline: the wedge of his skull resting on the ledge she used as a shelf; the great folded wings, leathery and dull now, their membranes gone translucent at the edges like old parchment held to a window; the long tail curled around the base of the hollow's central pillar, where the deepest vein of fire ran. His scales, once the color of storm-light over the sea, had gone the grey of banked charcoal. Along his spine, where the fire-veins met his body, a faint glow pulsed with each breath—in, dim; out, bright—as though he were a lantern someone had forgotten to refill.

"Morning, old one," she said, because she always said it, because the alternative was admitting there might come a morning when saying it felt foolish.

One amber eye opened. It was the size of a cartwheel and it regarded her with the weary patience of a grandfather listening to a child describe a dream. He did not speak. He had not spoken aloud in six years—not since the last time men with Halden's seal on their papers had come poking through the upper tunnels, and Tamsin had begged him to stay silent, stay dark, *be stone*, and he had obeyed, and something in the obeying had closed a door in him that had not fully reopened.

But he spoke to her anyway, in the older way. Warmth against her mind like sun through a window. A pressure of attention, vast and gentle. And sometimes—rarely, on the good nights—an image, given whole: a country green to the horizon, rivers unbroken, a sky full of wings.

*You are afraid,* the warmth said, or she translated it as such. Hard to say, after eleven years, where his meaning ended and hers began.

"I'm always afraid," Tamsin said. "It's practically my trade."

She checked the water basin first—a natural depression in the floor that she kept filled from a seep in the eastern wall, because a burning creature dehydrates even when it barely moves, and dehydration was a slow thief she could actually fight. Then the meat, such as it was: goat, mostly, bought through Brannoc's people at prices that made her wince, lowered on a chain-and-pulley rig she'd built herself from salvaged mine equipment. Serevan ate little now. That was the cruelest arithmetic of the whole slow dying: the less fire he made, the less he needed to eat, and the less he ate, the less fire he could make. A circle drawn smaller every year, closing like a hand.

She was hauling the chain back up when she heard them.

Footsteps on the godwork stair—several sets, uneven, careful. Brannoc's tread she knew, heavy and deliberate, a man who walked like he expected the floor to betray him. With him came lighter feet, quick ones, and one set that made her frown: a child's step, but placed with strange precision, heel-to-toe, silent at the edges. And one more, hesitant, stopping twice on the descent—someone who had never seen the deep places and was afraid of them, and was coming anyway.

Tamsin wiped her hands on her apron, thought briefly and uselessly about her appearance—ash in her hair, ash everywhere, eleven years of ash—and went to meet them at the mouth of the hollow.

* * *

Brannoc Vale filled the stairway's exit the way weather fills a valley. Behind him: a woman with a defrocked priestess's cropped hair and a sword she carried like an apology; a wiry young man whose fingers were stained with three colors of ink; and the boy, Pell, small and solemn, who stopped dead at the threshold and tilted his head as though listening to a choir.

And behind them, last, a young man in a patched coat with the wary posture of someone who had spent his life being hunted in small ways and had recently begun to be hunted in large ones.

Ines Oyelaran. The salt-witch. He looked, Tamsin thought, far too young to carry the name the city had hung on him, and far too tired to argue about it.

"Tamsin," Brannoc rumbled. "Brought your visitors. Mind the third step, it's loose."

"You've been promising to fix that step for nine years."

"And I've been meaning to keep the promise for nine years. We're even." He surveyed the hollow behind her—the dim gold veins, the vast grey coil of the dragon—and for a moment the smuggler's iron composure flickered, showing the boy underneath who had grown up on mural-dragons and outlawed songs. "Gods below," he muttered. "Every time."

"He's asleep half the time. You're admiring furniture."

"Furniture doesn't breathe like that."

The priestess—Maren, Brannoc had named her in his note—was murmuring something under her breath, an old liturgical cadence worn smooth by disuse. The forger, Wren, had gone very still, eyes tracking the veinwork in the walls with the expression of a man reading a document he'd only ever seen forged copies of. And Pell walked straight past all of them, across the hollow floor, and stood looking up at Serevan with his face open and unafraid, and the dragon opened one eye and looked back, and between them passed some communication that needed neither words nor warmth.

Then Ines stepped forward, and everything changed.

Tamsin saw it happen and would never afterward be able to describe it properly, and she resented that. The young man took perhaps four steps into the hollow, and the veinwork in the walls *answered* him. Not flaring—nothing so dramatic—but brightening, the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar footstep. A ripple of gold ran outward from where he stood, threading away into the dark along paths Tamsin had mapped and memorized for a decade, and every path led, sooner or later, to the seals.

Serevan's head came off the ledge.

Eleven years, and she had seen him move perhaps a dozen times, always slowly, always with the terrible economy of a creature rationing itself. This was not that. This was the old speed, the mural speed, the speed of wings over a green country—his skull swinging around with a whisper of displaced air, both eyes open now, molten amber fixed on the young man with an intensity that made the air feel thinner.

Ines froze. Wisely. "I—" he started.

The dragon breathed in, long and deep, drawing air across whatever ancient instrument dragons used instead of noses, and Tamsin watched understanding arrive in Ines Oyelaran's face one piece at a time, like a shipwreck washing ashore.

"He can smell it," Ines said. His voice had gone strange. "The salt. My master used to say our blood carried it—generations of Saltmakers, handling the worksalt, rendering it, sealing with it. She said it got into us. Into our—" He touched his own wrist, where Tamsin knew the guild sigil lived, hidden under his sleeve. "She was joking. I thought she was joking."

"She was not joking," Tamsin said. Her own voice surprised her—steady, when her hands were not. "Come here. Slowly. Let him look at you."

Ines came. Slowly. The dragon's head tracked him the whole way, and when he stood within arm's reach of that wedge-shaped skull—close enough that Tamsin's chest tightened with eleven years of proprietary terror—Serevan exhaled, and the breath washed over the young man like a wind off a forge, and the gold veins in the walls blazed.

Not dimming. Not guttering. *Blazing.*

Pell made a sound—a small, cracked, urgent sound, the first noise Tamsin had ever heard the boy make—and grabbed Ines's sleeve and pointed, urgently, at the dragon's spine, at the pulsing line of light there, and then at Ines's covered wrist, and then back again, over and over, his face alight with a frustration that needed words and couldn't have them.

"What's he saying?" Wren asked softly.

Maren answered, her liturgical murmur gone reverent. "He's saying they match. The fire and the blood. He's saying the dragon knows him."

Serevan made a sound then. Not speech—something older, deeper, a resonance that came up through the floor and through Tamsin's borrowed second heartbeat and through the soles of everyone's boots, a sound like a bell the size of a cathedral being struck once, gently, in greeting. Ash sifted from the ceiling. The water in the basin rang into concentric circles.

Ines Oyelaran put his hand flat against the dragon's jaw, and wept without seeming to notice, and the dragon leaned into his palm with the whole impossible weight of his head, trusting, and Tamsin had to turn away and busy herself with the water chain because eleven years of solitary vigil does not prepare a person for the sight of the thing they guard choosing someone else.

* * *

Later—after the crew had been fed barley stew and Brannoc had pretended not to notice how thin the stores were, after Maren had quietly blessed the cookfire in a rite two generations outlawed, after Pell had fallen asleep against the dragon's foreleg as though it were a hearthstone, which perhaps it was—Tamsin took Ines aside, to the wall with her ledger of numbers.

She showed him the scratches. *Nine. Seven. Five. Four.*

"Tell me what you see," she said. "When you look at the veins. Truly. No guild training, no theory. What do you see?"

Ines studied the wall for a long moment. Lamplight moved on his face. "A map," he said finally. "No—a circulatory system. These are arteries. They run out of the caves, under the boundary stones, under the chapels, under—" his finger traced a line southward, toward Vellagard, toward everything, "—under the Door of Ash. The saltworks my guild maintained weren't buildings. They were *nodes*. We thought we were making charms and sealing stones. We were maintaining a body." He looked up at her. "Whose body? His?"

"The fire is his," Tamsin said. "The seals drink it. That was the covenant, in the old days—before your Edict, before the Silence. The dragons burned, and the gods stayed outside, and the country stayed *real*, because realness is a thing that has to be tended, did you know that? Most people don't. Realness is a fire on a hearth. Someone has to feed it." She touched the newest scratch, the one shaped like a wound. "Four breaths a minute. Eleven years ago, nine. Whatever feeds the veins, whatever keeps the seals fat and strong—it comes from him. And he is going out."

"How long?"

She had done the arithmetic a hundred times, in the dark, in the bad winters. She made herself say it plainly, because this young man had the look of someone who had inherited a duty and not yet understood its weight, and kindness would only delay the understanding.

"At the current rate? Two years. Perhaps three. And the seals won't fail all at once—they'll fail the way a dam fails, quietly and then catastrophically. The thin places go first. The cracks widen. Things start answering prayers." She watched him flinch, and knew the stories had reached him. "You've seen it already. In the city."

"Yes."

"Then you know I'm not telling you a ghost story." Tamsin folded her arms, ash sifting from her sleeves, and made herself say the rest, the part she had never said aloud to anyone, the part that had kept her awake through eleven years of counting breaths. "There's more. Something's wrong with the rate itself. The decline isn't smooth—it *steps*. Drops sharply, then holds. As if something is taking from him in amounts. Drawn off. Measured." She met his eyes. "Dragons don't gutter like lamps, Saltmaker. Lamps gutter because they're empty. He's not empty. Someone is drinking his light."

Behind them, in the great hollow, Pell woke suddenly and sat bolt upright, staring into the dark of the eastern tunnel with his whole small body rigid, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear yet.

And far above, faint as a memory, the mountain's heartbeat stumbled.
