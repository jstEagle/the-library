## 8. Inquisitor Halden's Ledger

The Bastion of the Question had no windows on its upper floors, and Odile Ferrant had long ago stopped wondering whether this was to keep the city out or the work in.

She climbed the spiral stair with her report under her arm and rain still dripping from her braid, and at every landing the candles burned a little whiter, a little steadier, as though the building itself were holding its breath for what she had to say. Three answered prayers in one night. A street drowned by a fisherman's wish. A chapel flooded by a child's plea for rain. Forty-one dead, or forty-three—the count changed hourly, because the water kept giving up bodies it had swallowed whole.

Inquisitor Halden would want to know everything. That was the trouble with Halden: he wanted to know everything, and he was never once satisfied merely to know.

His study door stood open, which meant he had been expecting her. It always stood open when he expected someone; closed doors were for people he did not intend to see again.

"Serjeant Ferrant." He did not look up from his desk. "You're wet."

"The rain is wet, sir. I only borrow it."

That earned her half a smile, the way it always did—Halden enjoyed wit the way a collector enjoys a coin: briefly, and then into the drawer. His study smelled of lamp oil, vellum, and something faintly mineral that Odile had never been able to name, like the air over a stone yard after frost. Shelves climbed the walls to the ceiling, crammed with ledgers bound in grey calfskin, each spine lettered in a small precise hand. She had catalogued them once, early in her service, and found the dates went back further than Halden's appointment. Further than the Edict of Nothingness itself. When she'd asked about them, he had said only: *History is an inventory, Serjeant. Someone must keep it.*

"Sit." He turned a page. "Tell me about the fisherman."

She told him. The man on Rope-Walk whose wife had died in the winter fevers, who had gone down to the harbor at low tide and prayed aloud—not to any god, because there were no gods left to pray to, everyone knew that, it was law—but to the dark water generally, the way grieving men do, asking anyone at all to give her back. And the tide had come in four hours early and nine feet high, and taken the whole street, and among the drowned things it set down gently in the market square, wrapped in kelp like a gift, was a woman's body that was not his wife's but wore her face.

Halden listened without moving. Lamp light pooled in the hollows of his cheeks. He was not an old man, but he had the stillness of one, the economy of motion that comes from decades of never wasting a gesture on anything that wasn't necessary.

"And the chapel?"

"A girl of six. Her mother was dying. She asked for rain—for tears, really, children don't distinguish—and the roof came off the sky above Saint Verre's. The nave filled to the windowsills. Eleven parishioners drowned standing up." Odile heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for reports, and hated it a little. "The mother survived. She's in the sanctuary ward. She keeps saying the water was warm."

"Warm," Halden repeated, softly, tasting the word.

"Yes, sir."

"And the third?"

Odile hesitated. This was the part she had rehearsed on the stairs, and rehearsing had done nothing, because the thing itself refused to be made orderly. "A beggar on Candlemas Steps. He didn't ask for anything. He was praying *against* something—he was frightened, he'd seen the flood, and he prayed to be somewhere else. Anywhere else." She looked at her hands. "He is now. Or rather, he isn't. There was a sound like a page turning, and where he'd been sitting there was a scorch mark shaped like a kneeling man, and the stones were cold enough to burn skin. We found his bowl two streets away. Full of coins. New-minted coins with the old kingdom's crown on them, sir. Coins that haven't been struck in twelve years."

For the first time, Halden looked up.

It was a small thing, that lifting of the eyes, but Odile felt it the way one feels a change in wind direction—everything in the room subtly reoriented around it. His gaze was pale grey, almost colorless, and it had the peculiar quality of making the person looked at feel *inventoried*.

"You brought samples?"

"In a lead box. As you've instructed for all anomalous materials."

"Good girl." He rose, took the box from her without ceremony, weighed it in his palm. Then, instead of setting it with the other evidence, he carried it to the far wall—to the shelf of grey ledgers—and slid it behind them, out of sight.

"Sir," Odile said carefully, "the assay office will need—"

"The assay office will need nothing." He sat back down and folded his hands. "Because you are going to write a different report than the one you just gave me."

The candles flickered. Somewhere below, the Bastion's bells marked the hour, and Odile realized with a lurch that she could not remember which hour it was. Time moved strangely in this building. It always had.

"Different how, sir?"

"Honestly. That's the irony." Halden smiled, and it was the worst thing she had seen all day, worse than the drowned chapel, because it was patient. "You'll write that the prayers were *unanswered*. That the flooding was storm-surge, that the chapel roof failed from rot, that the beggar was a vagrant found dead of exposure. You'll write that the rumors of answered prayer are panic, contagion of belief, nothing more. And you'll sign it."

"Why?"

"Because fear is a solvent, Serjeant, and I am trying to build something, and one cannot build in a dissolving medium." He opened a drawer—she noticed, with the peripheral clarity of shock, that his hand did not hesitate, that he had clearly done this before—and drew out a ledger. Grey calfskin. Recent. Its spine bore a date from three months ago.

"Do you know what I was doing during the Purge Years?" he asked. He did not wait for an answer; he never waited; questions from Halden were rhetorical architecture, load-bearing. "I was a junior clerk in the Office of Dissolution. My task was the gods themselves. Not hunting their priests—that was the sword-work, ugly business, mostly done by men who enjoyed it. No. My task was *paper*. Every hymnal, every votive record, every contract between congregation and divinity, catalogued and burned. Twelve years of burning." He touched the ledger's cover almost tenderly. "Do you know what I learned? Gods are not fire. They are not even power, particularly. They are *creditors*. They answer prayers the way a moneylender answers a petition—with terms attached. And a country that burns its contracts does not free itself of its creditors. It merely loses the ledger that tells it what it owes."

Odile's mouth had gone dry. "Sir—"

"Three months ago," Halden said, "I began keeping accounts again."

* * *

He showed her, because showing her was the point. She understood that even through the roaring in her ears: Halden did not confess. He *enrolled*.

The ledger's pages were ruled in columns, and the columns were beautiful, and that beauty was the most frightening thing in the room. Dates. Locations. Prayers, transcribed verbatim in his small precise hand—prayers he had collected, copied, and then, she understood slowly, with a nausea that began in her stomach and climbed, *delivered*. Fed into the cracks. Carried to the boundary stones at night, read aloud against the corroded salt, offered up like letters posted through a broken wall.

*Petition of Marla Voss, laundress, for healing of her son's lungs. Granted. Terms recorded: see folio 12.*

*Petition of the Brotherhood of Harbor Pilots for fair winds. Granted. Terms: see folio 12.*

Folio 12 was a single page near the front, and it held, in the same meticulous script, a list of conditions so brief it might have been a shopping memorandum. Odile read it twice before her eyes would accept it.

*All grants issued under the authority of the Crown-in-Exile, administered solely through the Office of the Inquisition. Recipients shall acknowledge said authority. No grant shall issue except through channels approved by the undersigned.*

Below it, Halden's signature. Below that, a space left blank, ruled and waiting, labeled in tiny letters: *Counter-signature of the contracting party.*

There was no counter-signature yet.

"They haven't agreed," Odile said. Her voice sounded very far away to her own ears. "Whatever's answering—they haven't signed your terms."

"No," Halden agreed pleasantly. "But they are answering anyway, which tells me they are eager, and eagerness is leverage. Everything that has come through so far is small—a tide, a rain, a coin. Threshold-things. Things that slip through cracks. But cracks widen, Serjeant. They widen faster when worked. And when something large comes through—something with a name and a memory and a hunger for worship—it will find waiting for it a fully drafted covenant, a functioning administration, and a state prepared to recognize it." He spread his hands, and for a moment his face held something that was almost, terribly, sincere. "Twelve years ago we told the people their gods were dead and their country had never existed, and they believed us because the alternative was madness. Now the prayers are being answered, and the people are terrified, because terror is what unanswered hope becomes. But imagine—" he leaned forward, lamplight catching the grey of his eyes until they seemed lit from behind— "imagine the prayers are answered *lawfully*. Imagine a god returns, and the first thing it finds is a government already holding the pen. Legitimacy, Serjeant. The new regime was built on a legal fiction, and fictions crack. But a regime that *brokered the return of the divine*? That regime is eternal."

The candle flames had gone very still. Odile realized she was gripping the arms of her chair hard enough to ache.

"And the people who die?" she asked. "Rope-Walk. Saint Verre's. Are those also administration?"

"Collateral of negotiation." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse—cruelty would have been a handle she could grip. This was arithmetic. "Every covenant in history was written in someone's blood. At least mine is being written deliberately, in advance, rather than in rivers after."

"You're feeding them," she said. "You're feeding prayers to whatever's out there. You're widening the cracks yourself."

"I am *managing* an inevitability." For the first time, a hairline fracture showed in his calm—irritation, quickly smoothed. "Would you prefer it happen chaotically? Would you prefer the fisherman's grief and the child's tears be the only channels? Unregulated, unrecorded, unbound? Because that is the alternative, Serjeant, and I have read enough burnt hymnals to know what unbound gods do with a hungry country."

And here was the trap, laid out so elegantly she could admire its joinery: because he was right. That was the poison in it. She had stood in the flooded chapel with water to her knees and the dead floating like leaves, and some cold clerk-part of her had thought exactly what Halden wanted her to think: *someone should be controlling this.* He had simply volunteered himself for the role, the way ambitious men volunteer for everything, and dressed the volunteering in reason.

"Your report," Halden said, sliding fresh paper across the desk. "Storm-surge. Rot. Exposure. Due on my desk by morning bell."

* * *

She wrote it.

That was the thing she would carry afterward, the thing that woke her at odd hours in the weeks that followed: she sat in the anteroom with the Bastion's white candles burning their windowless light, and she wrote the lie in her best clerical hand, and it took less than an hour, and her pen never shook. She had thought it would shake. She had counted on it shaking, the way one counts on a wall to stop a fall. It didn't. Twelve years in the Inquisition teaches the hand obedience deeper than the heart's objections, and the words *storm-surge* flowed out smooth as water finding level.

She signed it. She blotted it. She carried it in and laid it on the corner of Halden's desk, and he thanked her without looking up, and she walked back down the spiral stair past the steady white candles, and no lightning struck her, and no god rose out of the floor to note her perjury. The silence of the gods, she thought bitterly, was the one doctrine that had never needed enforcement.

But in the stairwell's third landing, where the shadow pooled deepest, she stopped.

She thought about the beggar's bowl full of impossible coins. About the warm water. About a god—or something wearing the office of one—that had answered a frightened man's prayer to be elsewhere by taking him *elsewhere*, carelessly, the way a child moves a toy. Small things through small cracks. Halden thought eagerness was leverage. Halden thought he was holding the pen.

But Halden had never stood at a boundary stone. Halden worked by lamplight, on paper, in a building with no windows. He believed the world was an inventory because he had only ever inventoried paper.

Odile had walked the perimeter. She had seen the boundary stones up close, on her patrols—had noticed, weeks ago, filed away and unexamined, the corrosion on the sealing salt, the way it flaked like old pastry. She had assumed weather. Neglect. The slow decay of a country that officially did not exist and therefore could not be maintained.

Now she wondered. Halden's ledger went back three months. The stones she'd inspected had been corroded longer than that—she was nearly sure of it, nearly, though certainty would require going back and looking properly, which would require access, which would require—

Which would require lying to Halden a second time, in the other direction.

She stood in the dark of the stairwell and examined herself the way she'd examine a suspect: motive, means, opportunity. Motive: a drowned street, a flooded chapel, eleven parishioners dead standing up, and a superior who called it arithmetic. Means: her warrant, her patrol routes, her knowledge of which stones were worst corroded and where the smuggler tunnels ran beneath them—every officer knew the tunnels existed; few bothered memorizing the mouths. Opportunity: Halden slept four hours a night and spent the other twenty with his ledgers.

Against all of it, one fact, plain as a coin in a beggar's bowl: if she did nothing, the next grant would be larger. The cracks widened when worked, Halden had said, and he intended to keep working them, and eventually something would come through that no edict could file and no report could rename. And it would find, waiting for it, a covenant drafted by a man who believed gods were creditors—and it would either laugh, or sign, and she genuinely could not say which outcome frightened her more.

There was also, she admitted in the honest dark, a smaller and more shameful motive, and she let herself look at it directly because looking away was how officers like Halden were made: she had written the false report, and the false report was in the archive now with her signature on it, and there were only two ways a signature like that got redeemed. One was confession. The other was making the truth outrun the lie.

She chose the stairs going down instead of up. Past her own floor. Past the records room. Down to the patrol registry, where she took out the duty roster and, with the same obedient hand that had written *storm-surge*, assigned herself the western perimeter circuit beginning tomorrow at dusk—the circuit that passed the three worst-corroded boundary stones, and the sealed grate in the Saltmarket wall that every constable knew was bricked up and none had ever checked.

Halden wanted a monopoly on the cracks. Very well. She would find out what he was feeding—and whether it was only him.

On her way out, past the great seal of the Crown-in-Exile in the entry hall—crown, scales, and the motto *BY LAW ALONE*—she paused. The marble beneath the seal had cracked sometime that winter; a thin fault line ran straight through the scales, and maintenance had never repaired it, because officially the building was perfect and therefore the crack did not exist.

Odile put her thumb in the crack. It was cold, and faintly damp, and it smelled—faintly, impossibly—of the sea.

Tomorrow at dusk, then. Tomorrow she would walk the perimeter, and look properly at the stones, and perhaps find nothing but weathered salt and her own guilty imagination.

Or perhaps she would find the person the Inquisition's new edicts were hunting—the salt-witch, the charm-seller, whoever was out there seeing what she was only beginning to suspect: that the seals were failing, that someone had been unsealing them, and that the question of *who* had just become the most dangerous question in a country that legally had no questions left to ask.

She pulled her collar up against the rain and stepped out into the wet dark of Vellagard, and behind her, high in the windowless Bastion, a lamp burned late over a ledger with one signature too few.
