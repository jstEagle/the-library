## 9. A Market Burned

The night market smelled of tallow and wet wool, of fried dough gone cold in the wind off the harbor, of the particular sourness of fear that had lived so long in Vellagard it had become a seasoning. Ines knew all its scents the way he knew his own hands. He had sold here for six years, six years of luck-knots and fever-salts, of charms that were mostly theater and comfort, and he had learned to read the market's moods the way sailors read weather.

Tonight the weather was wrong.

It began with the lamps. The market's lanterns hung on wires strung between awning-poles, dozens of them, each one lit by a lamplighter who made her rounds at dusk with a long brass pole. Tonight they burned unevenly—some guttering low, some flaring too bright, as though something beneath the cobbles were breathing against their flames.

Ines stood behind his stall, arranging his wares with hands that wanted to shake and did not quite dare. Fever-salts in their twist-paper packets. Luck-knots of red cord, three knots each, no more—the guild pattern, though no one living but him remembered why three. Dried rue for doorways. A small tray of river-stones he had blessed with nothing more than patience and salt water, which was, if he was honest, almost nothing at all.

Almost nothing. That word again. *Almost.* It had been following him for weeks now, since the mother with the burning-eyed child, since the charm that should have been theater and had instead done *something*, since the boundary stones he had found eaten through like moth-eaten cloth.

He touched the sigil beneath his sleeve without meaning to. The salt-brand on his inner wrist, the guild mark pressed into his skin by Master Corvane's own thumb on the day of his binding, hidden under linen and leather cuff for twelve years. He had worn it like a wound. He had worn it like an inheritance.

"Ines." Old Marette, who sold candles, leaned across the gap between their stalls. Her voice was pitched low, meant only for him. "They're coming from the Tanner's Gate side. Greycoats. A lot of them."

"How many is a lot?"

"More than we can run from."

* * *

They came through the market the way floodwater comes through a cellar door: not fast, not dramatic, just inevitable, filling every gap. Ines counted them out of habit and hated himself for it—sixty, perhaps seventy, grey coats and black boots, moving in pairs, and behind them men with carts. Not prisoner wagons. Carts. As though they had come to collect goods.

At their center walked a tall man in a coat the color of wet ash, and Ines felt the recognition land in his stomach before his mind caught up: Inquisitor Halden. He had never seen the man before in his life, and yet he knew him instantly, the way you know a shape you have seen in nightmares. Odile Ferrant had described him once, in the smuggler tunnels, in the low voice she used for things that shamed her: *He walks like the law is a coat that fits him.*

Halden walked like that. He walked like a man wearing a country.

A crier went up ahead of them, unrolling a scroll with a snap of vellum:

"By edict of the Inquisition: this market is closed for purification. All persons will submit to search. Possession of unsanctioned charms, relics, or written prayers is a capital offense. The seller known as the salt-witch is sought for questioning. Informants will be compensated."

*The salt-witch.* Ines almost laughed. Six years of selling luck-knots to fishermen's wives and fever-salts to grandmothers, and somewhere in the machinery of the Inquisition he had become a witch. He supposed it was true enough now. He supposed it had always been true enough.

The searches began at the far end of the lane, and they were not gentle. He watched a greycoat tear Marette's candle stock apart looking for written prayers inside the wax. He watched a boy of maybe ten get struck across the mouth for crying. And he watched them find things—because there were always things. A saint's medal sewn into a hem. A scrap of the old liturgy kept in a locket for twenty years because a dead woman had asked. Small heresies, the size of grief, and for each one the carts took another person.

Ines's hands moved without his permission, sweeping his own wares into their case, closing the lid. Run. The tunnels were four streets away; Brannoc's people would take him in; he could be gone before Halden reached this end of the lane. Every instinct the last twelve years had trained into him said *run, hide, survive, be nothing.*

Then he saw Pell.

The mute boy was crouched beneath the eave of the pie-seller's stall, where he sometimes sat on market nights, listening—to what, Ines had never been able to ask. Pell heard gods. Brannoc had told him that much: the boy had been born hearing them, back when hearing them was only madness and not also heresy, and his voice had left him around the same time the gods did, as though he had given it up in sympathy. Tonight Pell was not crouching still. Tonight his whole body was rigid, his eyes fixed on the greycoats, and his hands were moving—fast, urgent, shaping words in the air with the desperate fluency of someone shouting.

Ines couldn't read hands. But he didn't need to. He understood *run* in any language.

Two greycoats had seen the movement too. They turned toward the eave, and one of them grabbed Pell by the collar and hauled him up into the lantern light, and Pell fought—not like a child, but like something older than a child, twisting with a strength that made the soldier grunt—and the second greycoat drew his baton back—

And Ines stepped out from behind his stall.

Later, he would try to reconstruct the decision and fail. There was no moment where he weighed escape against exposure, no ledger of costs. There was only Pell's face, and the baton falling, and the fact that some part of him had been waiting twelve years for exactly this choice and had known, all along, how he would make it.

"Let him go," Ines said, and his voice came out strange—calm, level, carrying further than a voice should carry, as though the air itself were leaning in to listen. "Your quarrel isn't with him. It's with me. I'm the salt-witch you're hunting."

Every head in the near half of the market turned. The baton stopped mid-fall.

Halden looked up.

* * *

What happened next, Ines would remember all his life in fragments, the way you remember a fire: in light, in heat, in pieces too bright to look at directly.

He remembered walking forward, unhurried, while the crowd parted around him like water around a keel. He remembered pulling back his cuff, slowly, deliberately, and holding up his wrist to the lantern-light so that all of them could see the salt-brand gleaming white against his skin—the mark of the Saltmakers' Guild, nine interlocking rings around a single grain, the seal-sign, the sign that had not been shown openly in Vellagard in twelve years.

"I am Ines Oyelaran," he said, "last apprentice of the Saltmakers' Guild, and everything sealed in this country was sealed by my order."

That was not strictly true. It was a lie of inheritance, and he told it anyway, loudly, because the truth—that he was one frightened man with a dead master's duty—would not have stopped what needed stopping. But the salt heard him. That was the terrible thing he learned that night: the salt heard him, and believed him, or believed the office if not the man.

He felt it wake. Under the cobbles, in the mortar of the walls, in the packed earth between the stalls—a vast, patient attention turning toward him like a sleeper rolling over. The guild's salt was everywhere in Vellagard; it had been worked into the city's bones for centuries, and it had been listening all along, and now it had a voice it recognized.

The greycoat holding Pell let go. Not chose to—*had to*. His fingers simply opened, the way a fist opens when the arm goes numb, and Pell dropped free and scrambled away toward the pie-seller's stall, and the greycoat stared at his own hand as though it had betrayed him.

"Stand fast," Halden said. His voice was quiet, and the market went quieter to hear it. "Arrest the witch."

Six of them came at Ines, and he raised his hand, and he did the thing he had sworn never to do in public, the thing that ended the life he had built, and he did it without hesitation because hesitation would have killed Pell or Marette or the candle-boy.

He spoke a working.

Not a charm. Not theater. A working, in the old cadence, the guild cadence, the words Master Corvane had drilled into him in a cellar full of salt-barrels: *What is seasoned stays; what is claimed holds; what is witnessed cannot be taken.* He drove the words down into the salt in the stones and the salt answered—oh, how it answered. A ring of pale frost bloomed outward from his feet across the cobbles, crackling, spreading, and where it touched the boots of the advancing soldiers they stopped as though their feet had grown roots. Not frozen. Held. Seasoned. Claimed. Their faces went white with a terror that Ines recognized, because he felt it too: the terror of standing inside something enormous and old and awake.

"You see?" he said to the market, to Halden, to everyone, and his voice cracked on the second word and he let it crack. "You see what your Edict buried? You see what you've been starving? This is what answers when prayers go up and nothing lawful is left to catch them. I am not the monster in this story. I am the lock. And you—" he turned to Halden, and found his hatred and his fear had braided themselves into something steady, "—you have been picking the lock for years and calling it piety."

Halden's face did not change. That was the worst of it. Somewhere behind those pale eyes, Ines understood, gears were turning, accounts being balanced, and the arrest of a salt-witch was being recalculated into something else entirely: *a resource.*

"Take him," Halden said softly. "Dead or alive. Preferably alive."

But the salt held the six, and it held the next six, and the ring of frost crept wider, and the crowd—Vellagard's fearful, careful, bowed crowd—began, very quietly, to press inward instead of away. Marette was suddenly at Ines's shoulder. Then the pie-seller. Then others, faces he had sold luck to for six years, standing between him and the greycoats with the stubborn, doomed courage of people who had finally been given something worth being afraid for.

Pell appeared at his elbow, breathless, and gripped his sleeve, and pointed—at the sky, at the guttering lanterns, at the smoke beginning to rise from the far end of the lane where a cart had overturned into a brazier. The market was catching fire. Whether by accident or design, Ines never learned. The flames ran up an awning, leapt to another, and the night filled with orange light and screaming, and in that chaos the greycoats' discipline broke entirely.

Ines made his choice in the space of one breath.

He could hold the working and hold the line—or he could spend it saving the market. He could not do both. The salt was answering him, but it was answering through him, through his blood and his brand, and it was taking something out of him with every heartbeat, a cold drawn up through his wrist like marrow being sipped.

He turned to the fire.

"*Stay,*" he said to the flames, in the guild cadence, and the word went out of him like blood leaving a vein. "*Be seasoned. Be still.*"

The fire stopped.

Not died—stopped, mid-leap, mid-roar, hanging in the air above the burning awnings like a held breath, and the whole market saw it, hundreds of witnesses, the entire city's night population watching flames stand obedient in the air over their heads. Then Ines exhaled, and released the working, and the fire sank down into itself and went out, all of it, every flame in the lane, leaving the market in sudden darkness and the smell of scorched canvas and the sound of people realizing what they had seen.

In the darkness, hands found him. Brannoc's hands—he knew the grip, rope-hard and sure—hauling him backward through the crowd, Wren's voice hissing *this way, this way,* Pell clinging to his other sleeve. The crew had come for him. Of course they had. They had watched from the shadows the way smugglers watch everything, and when the moment broke they had moved.

"The tunnels," Brannoc growled. "Now. Before their eyes adjust."

"But the others—" Ines twisted to look back. The greycoats were regrouping; Halden's voice was cutting through the dark, ordering arrests, ordering the market sealed. Marette. The pie-seller. The ones who had stood for him.

"They stood for you knowing the price," Brannoc said, grim as ballast. "Grieve later. Move now."

Ines moved. But as the smugglers pulled him down into the dark of the tunnel-mouth behind the chandlery, he looked back one last time, and by the light of the greycoats' torches he saw them loading the carts—Marette among them, wrists bound, chin high, and beside her the pie-seller, and a dozen others who had chosen to stand in a witch's frost-ring rather than kneel in a lawful street.

They had seen his face. They had heard his name. By morning, every wall in Vellagard would carry it.

He had spent twelve years learning to be invisible, and he had unlearned it in ten minutes, publicly, irreversibly, in front of the entire city—and the price of the lesson was already being paid by everyone who had ever bought a luck-knot from his stall.

The tunnel swallowed him. Behind, above, the market burned on in patches the salt could no longer reach, and Inquisitor Halden stood in the torchlight among the ashes, and Ines knew—with the certainty of a man who has finally shown his enemy his hand—that whatever came next would not be a hunt.

It would be a war.

And somewhere in the dark ahead, Pell's hand found his in the blackness and squeezed it once, hard, deliberate—a message in the only language the boy had left—and Ines understood it without needing translation:

*We heard you. We are coming with you.*
