## 10. Outlawed Together

### Brannoc

The drowned chapel had been a chapel once, before the river Vell changed its mind and took the lower quarter with it. Now only the roofline broke the water at high tide—a black rib of slate and rot—and at low tide the mud gave up its secrets: pews gone soft as bread, an altar furred with salt-crystal, saints whose faces the flood had worn smooth into suggestion.

Brannoc Vale had moored the *Kestrel's Mercy* in her shadow for six years without anyone in the Inquisition thinking to look there. Why would they? Nothing lived in a place that God had already let drown.

He brought Ines Oyelaran in through the belfry, hauling him up the rope one-handed while the boy—no, not the boy anymore; the man was twenty-three and wore his brand like a wound that hadn't decided whether to close—swung over the black water and dropped onto the choir loft with less grace than a sack of eels.

"Charming," Brannoc said. "You bleed on my floor, you scrub it."

"It's mud."

"Then it's holy mud."

Ines laughed, and it cracked halfway through, and became something else. Brannoc had heard that sound before—from men he'd pulled out of the water too late, from women who'd watched their houses burn with everything still inside them. It was the laugh of someone who had run until running stopped being possible and found, at the end of the road, a door that opened anyway.

Below, in what had been the nave, Wren had the fire going in a copper washtub lined with river clay. Smuggled fire—the Inquisition's edicts against open flame after curfew were three years old now, and Brannoc's crew had made an art of small illegalities. The light climbed the drowned walls and caught the salt crystals, so that the whole ruined room glittered faintly, like the inside of some enormous geode, like the sky of a country that no longer existed.

Tamsin came down from the loft where she kept watch, ash-grey and quiet, and Pell appeared from wherever Pell went when nobody watched—the boy moved through the chapel like water finding its level. The priestess, Sefa, last of a defrocked order, sat by the fire turning her prayer beads over and over, though her order's god had been struck from the rolls twelve years ago and the beads were, technically, contraband.

Brannoc counted them. Seven, counting himself. A guild apprentice with a brand on his arm, a dragon-keeper, a mute child who heard gods, a priestess of nothing, a forger, and a smuggler who owed a dead woman a life-debt he could never repay to her, only to her student.

"Sit," he said to Ines. "Eat first. Talk after. That's the rule."

"There are rules?"

"I make them up as needed." He shoved a bowl at the boy—broth, eel, river-weed, better than it had any right to be because Sefa cooked like she prayed, with total attention. "Rule one: nobody runs tonight. Rule two: whatever got branded into your arm stays outside this fire until you're ready to bring it in. Rule three—" he looked around at all of them, and his voice went rougher than he intended, "—rule three is we don't lie to each other here. Everywhere else in this country—this non-country—lying's how you stay alive. Not here."

The fire popped. Salt glittered. And Ines Oyelaran, hunted since sundown, wanted since dawn, sat down among strangers and did not run.

Brannoc thought: *Mara, you old witch. Is this what you meant?*

### Wren

Wren had forged documents for eleven years, and she could tell you that the hardest thing to counterfeit was grief, because grief had no fixed format. It didn't come with seals or watermarks. You couldn't age it with tea-stains or distress it with a blade. It just showed up, uninvited, wearing other people's faces.

So when the silence stretched after the meal and Brannoc said, "Well then. Rule three," Wren reached for her pipe, found she'd left it aboard, and began instead—because it was easier for her to speak of paper than of pain—to talk about maps.

"The Edict didn't just outlaw magic," she said. The firelight made her freckles into constellations. "That's what people remember, because that's what hurt most. But read the text. Article Four. 'All boundaries, jurisdictions, names, and records of the former Kingdom of Ashfall are declared void ab initio'—void from the beginning—as if it had never been. They didn't banish us. They *unsaid* us."

She had spent years unsaying herself, professionally. She knew how it worked.

"My father was a surveyor," she went on, quieter. "He drew the boundary stones. He knew every mile of Ashfall the way you know your own handwriting. When the Edict passed, his maps became evidence. Owning one was sedition. So we burned forty years of his work in our own hearth, page by page, and he stood there telling me the names as each one went—*here's Marrow Ford, here's the pass at Cold Hallow, here's where your mother and I—*—and I memorized every name, because I was nine and it was the only inheritance they couldn't confiscate."

She pulled something from inside her coat. A scrap of oilcloth, soft as cloth from handling, dense with tiny inked lines. Everyone leaned in.

"Reconstruction," she said. "Twelve years of it. Every song that mentions a place. Every gravestone. Old women's memories. The kingdom doesn't exist, legally. But it exists *here*, in ink, and ink can be argued with."

Ines touched the edge of the map with one finger, careful, the way you touch a sleeping animal. His eyes were wet.

"Where's the Guildhall?" he asked.

Wren turned the cloth. Her finger found a district, a street, a building drawn in lines fine as hair.

"There. Still standing, last I heard. Nobody dares tear it down—it's not on any legal register, see? Can't demolish what officially isn't there." She smiled, thin and fierce. "The Edict cut both ways, Ines. They erased us—but they also erased their own claim to erase us. There are cracks in the paperwork. You've seen them. Literally."

Around the fire, the salt crystals caught the light again, and for a moment the drowned chapel looked less like a grave and more like an archive, like a vault where a country had been stored against the rain.

Wren put the map away. Her hands weren't quite steady. Grief, she thought, was the one document she'd never learned to forge—because it was the one thing she'd never had to fake.

### Sefa

Sefa had been a priestess for nine years and a defrocked one for ten, and she had learned that the gods' silence was not the same as the gods' absence. Silence was a thing done *to* you. Absence was a thing you were sentenced to.

She spoke last of the women, because she always did, because she had spent a career learning when to speak.

"I lost a vocabulary," she said.

They waited. Pell, sitting cross-legged closest to the fire, tilted his head at her the way he did when he was listening with more than ears.

"When the Edict struck the gods from the rolls, it also struck every word we used to reach them. My order's liturgy—four hundred years of it—became contraband overnight. Not banned speech, worse: *meaningless* speech. The courts ruled that prayers addressed to nonexistent entities constituted fraud if performed publicly, madness if performed privately." Her beads clicked, one by one. "I was arrested for saying grace over a dying man. His family thanked me. The court fined them for facilitating it."

She had told this story before, to magistrates, to inquisitors, to Brannoc on the night he'd fished her off a prison barge. It had never gotten easier. It had only gotten truer.

"But here is what I learned in the cells under the Bastion," she said, "where they sent me to be reasoned out of my delusions. The gods went silent. Fine. But silence has texture. And the silence after the Edict was different from any silence before it. Before, when we prayed, it was like calling into a great hall—you never knew if anyone answered, but you knew you were *heard*, or might be. After the Edict, it was like calling into a hall that had been bricked shut." She lifted her eyes to Ines. "Someone bricked it, child. Prayers don't seal themselves."

The fire hissed. Somewhere above, water dripped from the belfry with the patience of a clock.

"And now the bricks are coming loose," Sefa said. "And people are praying again, wildly, blindly, because no one taught them how—and the things that answer are not always the things they're calling. I have heard Pell hear them. I have felt them in my teeth, like weather coming." Her hand closed around the beads. "Ten years I thought I had lost my faith. I had not. I had lost my *job*. Faith keeps. Like salt."

Ines looked down at his bandaged forearm, where the brand sat under the linen like a second heartbeat.

"Your guild," Sefa said gently. "Your masters sealed the bricks, didn't they. All those centuries, the Saltmakers weren't keeping magic *in*. They were keeping the door *shut*."

"Yes," Ines said. It came out very small.

"Then you understand why you frighten me," Sefa said, "and why I would follow you anyway."

### Tamsin

Tamsin did not tell stories well. She had spent fifteen years underground with a dying dragon, and conversation had become, for her, a muscle gone slack. But the fire was warm and the company was strange and kind, and Brannoc had said rule three, and rules mattered to her—rules were what she had left of her order.

"I lost a post," she said. "Keepers don't have homes. We have posts. Mine was the Ash-Caves, and Serevan was my charge, and when the Edict dissolved the kingdom, it dissolved the office that paid for my post. Provisioning stopped. Replacement stopped. I wrote to the Crown for eleven years. The letters were returned unread—there was no department to read them, you see. The department had been part of a country that wasn't there."

Pell leaned against her side, the way he did, absorbing warmth and giving it back.

"People think keeping a dragon means feeding it," Tamsin said. "It doesn't. It means *listening*. Serevan is older than the kingdom, older than the Edict, older than most of the gods currently trying to squeeze through your cracks, Ines. He remembers when the seals were young. He remembers the taste of every oath ever sworn on salt." She looked at Ines across the fire, and her grey eyes held the cave-dark of the ash-caves in them, permanent now. "When he smelled you today—your blood, your guild-salt—he wept. Dragons don't weep easily. Their tears are worth more than the crown's entire treasury, and he spent two of them on a branded apprentice he'd known for four minutes."

Ines's breath caught. "Why?"

"Because you smell like Mara," Tamsin said simply. "And she was the last person who came down to us and *listened*."

The name landed in the room like a stone dropped in still water. Brannoc turned his face toward the dark. Wren went still.

"She visited the caves," Tamsin continued. "Three times, in the year before the Edict. She asked Serevan about the Door of Ash. She asked what would happen if the seals failed. She asked—" Tamsin's voice frayed, mended itself. "She asked who would keep the fire when she was gone. Serevan told her: whoever inherits the salt inherits the debt. And she said, *then I'll leave mine a heavy inheritance.*"

Ines had both hands pressed flat against his knees. Under the linen, Brannoc knew, the brand was burning—healing brands did that, in the presence of strong feeling. Or maybe it wasn't the brand burning at all. Maybe it was the inheritance.

"Heavy," Ines repeated. "Yes. It is."

Tamsin reached across the fire and took his wrist, above the bandage, grip light as ash.

"You're not carrying it alone anymore," she said. "That's what this fire is for."

### Pell

Pell could not tell his story in words, because words had left him at seven years old the same week his village left the maps. What he had instead was listening, and chalk, and a face that said everything slowly and completely, like weather crossing a valley.

He took the slate that hung around his neck, spat on it, wiped it clean with his sleeve, and drew.

First: a house. Small, crooked, smoke curling from the chimney. Then a tree beside it, and beneath the tree, two larger figures and a smaller one holding hands.

"My family?" Sefa guessed softly.

Pell shook his head. He tapped the drawing, then pointed east, toward the wall, toward the drowned world beyond it. Then he wrote, in a child's careful capitals:

**NOT MY HOUSE. EVERYONE'S HOUSE.**

Wren leaned forward. "A village?"

Pell nodded. Then he crossed it out—one hard stroke of chalk through roof and tree and figures—and below the ruin he wrote:

**GREY MARSH. WAS ON MAPS. NOT NOW. NEVER WAS.**

The chapel was very quiet. The river muttered overhead.

"They erased your whole village," Wren whispered. "Not just from the registers. From the *maps*. From the songs?"

Pell nodded again. His eyes had gone distant, listening to something none of them could hear—or perhaps all of them could, faintly, under the drip of the belfry.

He wrote more, slower now, the chalk squeaking:

**THE GOD OF GREY MARSH HAS NO NAME ANYWHERE. WHEN THE COUNTRY STOPPED, IT STOPPED TOO. IT IS STILL THERE. IN THE MARSH. WAITING TO BE NAMED.**

He underlined the last word twice, pressing hard enough to snap the chalk, and then he sat back and looked at Ines with those wide, patient eyes, and everyone understood at once that the child was not speaking for himself. He was speaking as a mouthpiece, as a bell rope, as a keyhole.

Ines stared at the slate. "It wants a name," he said. "From someone. From the guild."

Pell nodded.

"Gods," Sefa breathed, half laughing, half weeping, "gods waiting in line like beggars at a gate."

"Not beggars," Ines said. His voice had changed—steadier, older, the voice of a man reading terms aloud. "Claimants. They want to come home *lawfully*. Someone has to draw up the papers."

### Brannoc

Brannoc let the silence sit a long time. He was a man who trusted silence the way other men trusted rope—tested it, weighted it, believed it would hold.

Then it was his turn, and he found, to his irritation, that eleven years of deflection deserted him all at once.

"You know my debt," he said gruffly. "Your master pulled my crew out of the Bastion cells, forged our release papers, hid us in a salt-barge for nine days, and never asked a coin for it. Said a life-debt was interest-bearing and she'd collect someday. Never did." He fed a stick to the fire, watched it catch. "But that's not what the Edict took from me. The Edict took my *flag*."

Wren frowned. "Your flag?"

"The Ashfall merchant ensign. Blue field, white salt-tower, red border. My grandfather carried it. My father carried it. When the kingdom was unsaid, ships registered under its flag became—what did the lawyers say, Wren?"

"Pirates," Wren said. "Legally, vessels flying a flag of a nonexistent polity may be seized or sunk by any nation without declaration."

"Pirates." Brannoc smiled without humor. "Overnight. Three hundred years of lawful trade, and my family's ships went from honored to sinkable between one tide and the next. My father ran the ensign down in Vellagard harbor with the whole waterfront watching. Folded it up neat as a shroud. He was dead within the year—not the Inquisition, just... stopped. Men like him don't survive being erased. They're built out of belonging, and when you take the belonging away, there's nothing structural left."

He looked around the fire—at the forger with her impossible map, the priestess with her contraband beads, the keeper with ash in her veins, the mute prophet, the branded boy.

"So here's my confession, since it's confession night. I spent twelve years telling myself I was a smuggler because I liked the money. Lies. I've been flying a ghost flag this whole time. Every run I make, every law I bend, I'm doing it under colors that officially don't exist." He jabbed a finger at each of them in turn. "And so are all of you. Every one of you is sailing under a flag the world says was never raised. And tonight, sitting in this drowned church, looking at you lot—" his voice went gravel-and-smoke, "—I'm thinking maybe the flag was never the cloth. Maybe it was the crew."

Nobody spoke. The fire burned. The salt on the walls glittered like a sky full of patient stars.

It was Ines who finally moved. He unwound the linen from his forearm, slow, deliberate—the brand exposed to the light for the first time since they'd dragged him in: the salt-tower sigil, seared over the older guild-mark, ugly and healing and true.

"You've all given me your losses," he said. "Here's mine. I lost the right to say *I belong*. Twelve years ago I watched my master sign away the guild, and I thought—I'm the last apprentice of nothing. A guild with no country, a craft with no law, a mark that means contraband." He turned his arm so the firelight filled the brand's lines. "Tonight I watched a forger rebuild a kingdom from songs. A priestess keep faith alive in a cell. A keeper tend a god's oldest friend. A child carry a nameless god on a slate around his neck. And a pirate fly a flag that never existed—which means it can never be taken down."

His voice shook, and he let it.

"We were all erased together. Which means—we exist together. That's the covenant nobody wrote yet. That's the flag."

Brannoc rose. From a locked chest beneath the altar—salt-furred, flood-proofed, older than his own smuggling days—he drew a bundle of oilcloth and unwrapped it with the care of a man handling relics.

Blue cloth. White tower. Red border.

The Ashfall ensign, folded twelve years, smelling of salt and tar and a dead man's hands.

"My father's," he said. "I swore I'd never fly it again unless—" He stopped. Started again. "Unless there was a country to fly it for. Or one worth making."

He spread it across the drowned chapel floor between them all, and the firelight made the white salt-tower seem almost to glow, and seven people looked down at a nation the size of a blanket, and understood that nations begin exactly that small.

Above them, through the broken belfry, the night sky was doing something no one had seen it do in twelve years. The stars were smearing. Shifting. As if the sky itself were a document being rewritten—or as if something vast on the far side of the sealed doors had turned its attention, all at once, toward the one city where a branded boy was learning to say *we* instead of *I*.

Pell grabbed his slate and wrote fast, chalk breaking, eyes wide with what poured through him:

**THEY HEARD YOU.**

**ALL OF THEM. THEY ARE COMING TO THE DOOR—AND SOMETHING ELSE IS ALREADY AT YOUR GUILDHALL, AND IT IS WEARING HER VOICE.**

Ines went white.

"Mara," Brannoc said. "Something's wearing Mara's voice."

The fire guttered. Outside, far across the drowned quarter, bells that had not rung in twelve years began, one by one, to ring.
