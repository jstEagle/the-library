## 12. Maps of a Country That Isn't There

Wren had been erasing things for eleven years, and she had grown very good at it. She could take a name off a deed so cleanly the parchment forgot it had ever carried one. She could unmake a man from a shipping manifest with two strokes of her pen and a breath held just so. Forgery, properly understood, was not the art of making false things; it was the art of making true ones disappear.

Which was why it galled her—galled her like a splinter worked under a fingernail—that when the Crown had erased an entire kingdom, they had done it badly.

She sat in the drowned chapel's undercroft with every lamp in Brannoc's hoard lit around her, and she spread across the wet-flagged floor the wreckage of their crime: forty-one documents, salvaged over years of quiet thieving, each one supposed to have wiped Vellamark from the world.

The chapel dripped. Somewhere above, Pell was teaching Ines a round song about herring boats, his hands moving through the verses because his voice could not, and the sound came down through the floorboards like rain on a roof—comforting, half-heard, patient.

"Look at this one," Wren said, for perhaps the tenth time that evening, to no one who wanted to hear it. "Edict of Nothingness. Royal seal, chancery hand, countersigned by three ministers. It declares that the Kingdom of Vellamark never existed, that its laws are void, its charters null, its names struck from all registers." She tapped the vellum. "And then look what they did."

Brannoc looked, because Brannoc always did what Wren asked eventually; it was one of the load-bearing habits of their friendship. He crouched at the edge of the lamplight, arms crossed over his chest, a man built like a dock crane and about as talkative.

"They left the rivers," he said.

"They left the *rivers*." Wren laughed, and it came out bitter as brine. "You cannot legislate water, Brannoc. The Velle still runs where the Velle has always run. The roads still go where the roads go. They burned the maps—the great survey, the coronation atlas, every cartographer's plate—and then they discovered that a country lives in more places than paper."

"So draw it back."

"That," said Wren, "is exactly what I intend to do."

* * *

It took her nine days.

She began with memory, which is the least trustworthy ink and the only kind that cannot be confiscated. She sat with Brannoc and made him talk—not the way he talked, in cargo weights and tide-tables, but the way he hated talking: about being a boy. About his grandmother's house on a street whose name no register admitted anymore. *Kettleman's Row,* he said, after she'd asked four times, and then he said it again louder, as though daring someone to arrest him for it. *Kettleman's Row, by the tannery cut, third bridge past the salt-gate.* And Wren wrote it down, and wrote it again, and drew the curve of the street from his stumbling description until he stopped describing and simply pointed, his finger moving through the air of the undercroft, tracing ground he hadn't stood on in twenty years.

Then the songs. This was Pell's gift to the map, though the boy didn't know he was giving it. Mute children, Wren had learned, carry whole countries in their throats' silence. The round songs, the counting songs, the dirges sung at funerals the law said were illegal—all of them named places. *Over the Velle and under the Wyrmstone, down to the meadows my love went alone.* Under the Wyrmstone. There was no Wyrmstone on any surviving chart of the region. But there was a standing stone outside the drowned village of Corrow that fishermen called something else now, some Inquisition-approved name, and when Wren took a boat out and looked at it—at the worn spiral carved into its flank, at the way the field paths bent around it like iron filings around a lodestone—she knew. Songs don't lie about geography. People need landmarks to hang a tune on.

Then the gravestones. That was the worst work and the best work. She and Tamsin spent six days walking churchyards in the gray rain, and Tamsin—who had spent thirty years underground with a dragon and had opinions about stone that Wren trusted absolutely—would kneel and brush moss from a slab and read out what the weather had spared. *Here lies Aldous Merrow, of Harrowgate-upon-Velle.* Harrowgate-upon-Velle. A town the Edict said had never existed, attesting to itself in granite, generation after generation, too stubborn to die even when the people died. Some stones had been defaced—the names chiseled off, leaving dates and epitaphs hovering ownerless—but the chisels had missed more than they'd caught. You cannot chisel a churchyard. There are too many stones, and the dead do not cooperate with regimes.

On the seventh day she laid everything together on the undercroft floor: memory and song and stone, scraps of pre-Edict wax seals she'd lifted from archives over a decade, the river courses copied from a smuggler's tide-book that predated the ban by fifty years. She had bought a fresh skin of calfskin vellum in Vellagard's last honest week, before Halden burned the market, and it had waited in oilcloth ever since, and now she stretched it flat and weighted its corners with the guild's old brass salt-weights that Ines had lent her without being asked why.

Ines came down the second night and watched for a long while before saying anything. He stood with his sleeve pushed back—she'd noticed he'd stopped hiding the sigil since the market burned, wore it open now like a wound he'd decided to stop apologizing for—and studied the growing shape of lines.

"You're drawing it wrong," he said finally.

Wren's brush froze mid-stroke. Eleven years of forgery, and an apprentice charm-seller was telling her—

"The river," Ines said, crouching. "The Velle bends here—" he touched a point upstream of the city, "—there's a weir. My master used to send me to collect grey-salt from the reed beds below it. Two days' walk. Everyone says the river runs straight there now because the new maps show it straight, but I've *walked* it, Wren. It bends. The country remembers even if the surveyors don't."

So she redrew the river, and after that she stopped trusting any line she hadn't verified against a living witness or a dead stone, and the map got slower and truer, day by day.

By the ninth night it was nearly whole. Vellamark lay on the calfskin in brown ink and faded madder-red: the rivers running their real courses, the towns wearing their real names, Kettleman's Row and Harrowgate-upon-Velle and the Wyrmstone and ninety other resurrected places, each one testified to by somebody's grandmother or somebody's grave. And in the center-north, where the old coronation field should be—a broad green shelf of land between two low ridges, marked on her draft only by a question mark, because no song named it, no grave claimed it, and none of them had ever walked it—

There, Wren believed, was what they were looking for.

"It makes sense," Tamsin said, when Wren explained her reasoning. The keeper sat wrapped in ash-grey wool, smelling always faintly of smoke and sulfur, her eyes holding the particular patience of someone who has watched a fire gutter for decades. "Coronation fields aren't chosen for convenience. They're chosen for *threshold*. Old kingdoms crowned their kings at doors between worlds—it's in the oldest rite fragments Serevan still remembers. The king stands between the country and everything beyond it and swears to keep the door shut behind him, or open, depending on the age. If Vellamark kept a Door of Ash anywhere, it kept it there."

"But we can't know," Wren said. "Not until we walk it. And if we walk it wrong—if the Door isn't there—we'll be on open flats with Halden's riders at our backs and nothing to show for it but a beautiful lie I drew myself."

"You've never drawn a beautiful lie in your life," Brannoc said from the doorway. "You've drawn forgeries. There's a difference, and the difference is you always know what's true underneath." He came forward and set his palm flat on the map, over the coronation field, over the question mark. "This is the truest thing you've ever made, Wren. Everything on it was erased, and everything on it came back anyway. Rivers, graves, songs, a boy's memory of a weir. A country that won't stay dead." His voice dropped lower. "I say we bet our lives on it."

Nobody answered him directly. Nobody needed to. Ines was looking at the question mark the way a man looks at a debt he intends to pay.

* * *

They heard Odile before they saw her—which was strange, because Odile Ferrant moved like a woman who had been trained out of sound entirely.

It was Pell who caught it first. He had been sitting on the chapel steps in the blue hour before dawn, and suddenly he went rigid, both hands rising, fingers splayed, the way he did when the gods pressed close against the cracks. Then he turned and signed rapidly toward the interior: *someone coming. alone. afraid.*

Brannoc put out the lamps. Wren slid into the shadow behind the font with a knife she hoped not to use. The chapel settled into its dripping silence, and they waited, and the dripping counted out a hundred drops, two hundred, before the west door eased open on a wedge of gray light and a silhouette.

"I know you're in here," Odile Ferrant said quietly. Her voice was roughened, as though she'd been walking all night and arguing with herself longer than that. "The boy will have told you. Sergeant Odile Ferrant, formerly of the Inquisition, presently—" a short, unsteady laugh, "—presently nothing. No rank. No warrant. No country to serve, which I understand is fashionable these days." She stepped inside and let the door swing shut behind her. "I'm unarmed. Mostly. And I'm either your prisoner or your ally by morning, and I've come to beg you to choose the second, because the first will waste time we don't have."

They brought her down to the undercroft with her hands visible and her weapons surrendered—two knives and a pistol of Inquisition make, which Brannoc handled like it might bite. She stood at the edge of the lamplight, mud to the knee, a healing cut above her brow, and she looked at the map.

Wren watched her face as she saw it. Watched the sergeant's professional blankness crack and reform, crack and reform, like ice on a thawing river. Because Odile Ferrant had grown up inside the erasure same as all of them, but she had served it too, had enforced the blankness, had torn down signs and corrected records and arrested people for singing the wrong songs—and here it all was, spread on a floor in lamplight, undeniable, *named*.

"Harrowgate-upon-Velle," she said at last, barely audible. "My mother was born there. I reported a family for using that name when I was nineteen. They were fined. I told myself it was the law." Her jaw tightened. "It's real. The town is real. It's been real the whole time and I—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. Opened them. "Halden knows where you're going."

The word fell into the room like a stone into a well.

"He doesn't think he does," Odile went on, faster now, a woman emptying herself of a secret before her courage failed. "He thinks the Door is a legend the salt-witch invented. But he's been planning for a Door regardless, because a man like Halden plans for every possibility he intends to own. I copied his campaign book three nights ago, before I ran. Listen." She crouched by the map, and her finger—steady now, soldier-steady—began to move. "Two hundred picked riders mustered at Cindermarsh, disguised as a customs patrol. Light guns on pack-harness, the new pattern, breach-loaders. He's requisitioned the entire ferry stock on the upper Velle. And here—" her finger stopped on a point south of the coronation field, and tapped twice, "—he's already sent an advance party. Sappers. Twelve men, ordered to dig at the old barrow-field, because Halden believes the Door lies beneath the burial mounds. He's wrong. He's digging in the wrong place. But his main force takes the ferry road, which means they reach the flats a full day sooner than any route you can take on foot."

Silence, except the drip of the chapel.

"And you?" Brannoc said. "Why bring us this? Why turn?"

Odile looked at the map for a long moment. At Harrowgate-upon-Velle. At the river bending where a boy remembered it bending.

"Because I read his campaign book all the way through," she said, "and on the last page, in his own hand, there's a list. Gods. Names of gods, old ones, ranked by usefulness—by how controllable they'd be, how much worship they'd demand, which pantheon would legitimize the regime fastest. He hasn't written the country back into existence. He wants to *own* whatever comes through the Door, and call it restoration, and rule under it." Her voice went flat and cold. "I joined the Inquisition at seventeen because I believed someone had to hold the line between people and the things that answer prayers wrongly. I stayed through the Purge Years because I told myself the line still mattered. But Halden isn't holding a line. He's building a leash. And a leash needs a hand at the collar, and he intends it to be his." She straightened. "I won't help build that. I'd rather be nothing on the right side than everything on his."

Tamsin spoke from the shadows, her ash-dry voice carrying no doubt at all: "She's telling the truth. I've lived long enough with a creature who smells lies. So does the fire in me. She's true."

Brannoc looked around the circle of lamplight—at Wren, at Ines, at the keeper—and something in his heavy face settled, like ballast shifted and found.

"Then she walks with us," he said. "Get her boots. We move tonight, ahead of the ferry road, cross-country through the fens. It'll cost us two days of misery instead of giving Halden his day back." He paused, and his eyes went to the map, to the question mark at the heart of the resurrected country. "One day sooner or later, we find out if Wren's map tells the truth."

Wren looked down at her own work—at every resurrected name, every stubborn river, every grave that had refused to forget—and felt something she had not felt in eleven years of making true things disappear.

She had made a true thing *appear*. And tomorrow they would walk on it, and see if it held.

"Pell," she said quietly, and the mute boy looked up. "Sing us out. With your hands. The old marching song. I want to leave this chapel the way the country's leaving the dark—"

But before Pell's fingers could shape the first verse, the chapel bell above them shivered once, though no wind stirred and no hand touched it—a thin bronze note rolling down through the stones, and Pell's hands flew up again, urgent, spelling fast: *they know. riders on the fen road. now.*
