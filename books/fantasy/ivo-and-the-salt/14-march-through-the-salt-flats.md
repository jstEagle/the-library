## 14. March Through the Salt Flats

Brannoc Vale had crossed the salt flats twice in his life, and both times he'd sworn he'd rather drown.

The third time, he was doing it at a walk, with a dragon's keeper, a fugitive priestess, a forger with blistered feet, an Inquisitor who'd turned her coat inside out, and a mute boy riding on his shoulders because the ground would have eaten him alive.

The flats began where the road ended, which was to say they began where Vellagard's law ended too—though Halden's riders had never much cared for law when it inconvenienced them. Behind them the last grey scrubland gave way all at once, like a tide going out, and then there was nothing but white. Salt to every horizon, baked into plates and ridges and long frozen waves, so bright under the sun that it wasn't a color anymore but an argument against your eyes. Brannoc had seen men go snow-blind on northern passes; the flats did it faster, and without the mercy of cold to make you lie down.

"Cloth," he said, tearing a strip from his shirt hem before anyone asked. "Everyone. Tie it low over your eyes. You'll look like a band of idiots."

"We already do," Wren said, but she took the cloth. Her hands were shaking—not fear, just the flat's particular hunger getting into the blood early. He tied hers himself when she fumbled the knot, and she let him, which told him how bad it already was.

Tamsin walked at the front, and did not blindfold herself. The keeper's eyes were strange anyway, pale as skimmed milk, and she read the salt the way Brannoc read water: by feel, by years, by some sense that had no honest name. She carried a clay jar against her chest, wrapped in three layers of wool, and she never once set it down. Inside the jar was fire. Serevan's fire, what little of it remained—the guttering heart of the last dragon, banked down to a coal no bigger than a fist. Tamsin had scooped it from the ash-caves with her own two hands when the caves became a tomb-in-waiting, and now she carried it the way other people carried infants or grudges: close, constant, and willing to die for it.

"Why carry it?" Odile had asked on the first night, when they still talked freely. "If he's dying anyway—"

"Because dying is not the same as being put out," Tamsin said, and that had ended the conversation.

Now, four days onto the white, nobody talked much at all. Talking cost water. Everything cost water. They had calculated their skins for six days' march; the Door of Ash lay somewhere past the old coronation field, which Wren swore was nine days out by her reconstructed maps, and Brannoc had spent one long evening watching her argue with herself over whether the field was nine days or eleven, because the songs disagreed and the gravestones couldn't be consulted from here. Nine, she'd finally decided, and written it in ink she'd mixed from wine dregs, and everyone had chosen to believe her because the alternative was arithmetic none of them could survive.

Pell rode his shoulders most of each day. The boy weighed nothing—a sparrow's weight, a bundle of sticks—and his silence had changed texture since the caves. Before Serevan, Pell's silence had been empty space; now it was full, charged, like the air before lightning. Sometimes his fingers tightened on Brannoc's collar and his whole body went rigid, listening to something far off across the flats, and once he'd turned his face toward the south and made a sound—not a word, just a small cracked note of grief—and Brannoc had known without asking that somewhere behind them, something holy had come apart.

That had been the day the riders appeared.

* * *

They came up out of the southern haze on the fifth morning, a smudge first, then a line, then the unmistakable glint of lances catching a sun that had nothing else to reflect off. Eight points of light. Maybe twelve. Distance on the flats lied worse than a customs officer; they could be two miles out or five.

Brannoc stood in the blinding noon and counted the lights until his eyes watered, and the count didn't improve. "Halden's," he said. "Has to be. Nobody else rides the white."

Odile shaded her eyes beside him. Even muffled in smuggler's wool, even with the brand of desertion she now wore as surely as any mark on her skin, she stood like an officer, spine straight, weight settled, reading the terrain the way she must once have read battlefields. "Light cavalry. They'll run us down by tomorrow night if we hold this pace."

"So we don't hold this pace." Brannoc spat, dryly, an old habit that cost moisture he couldn't spare. "We cut north. There's a ridge of rock out there—old causeway, pre-Edict, my father ran salt along it. If it's real."

"If it's real," Wren echoed bitterly. She sat on a salt ridge with her feet in her hands, and there was fury in her, the good kind, the kind that kept a person walking. "Everything I gave you is if-it's-real. The map is memory. The memory is songs. The songs are drunk."

"The songs are all we have," Tamsin said quietly. "That's why Halden burned the archives. Not to hide the Door—to hide the way of walking to it. A country can't be erased while its paths are still remembered."

"Well," Brannoc said, "then let's go be difficult to erase."

They went north-northeast, off Wren's line, trading certain progress for uncertain cover, and the flats punished them for it within the hour. The surface changed underfoot—plates of salt that rang hollow like crockery, then stretches of soft brine-mud that sucked at boots and stole a stride's worth of strength from every step. The heat came up from below as well as above, the white throwing the sun back at them from underneath, so that they walked inside a furnace with no walls. Sweat vanished before it formed. Lips split. By midday Ines Oyelaran—who had insisted, in his quiet stubborn guildmaster way, on carrying double rations so the others could carry less—was staggering, and refused help, and staggered on.

Brannoc watched him and thought about life-debts. He'd owed Ines's master a man's life since before the Edict, and he'd paid it forward instead of back for twelve years, telling himself the ledger stayed open because open ledgers kept doors ajar. Now here was the debt's heir, stumbling across hell's own tablecloth to un-erase the world, and Brannoc found the old accounting had quietly become something else. Not debt. Crew. Worse. Family, if he was honest, and he was trying, these days, to be honest.

Pell tugged his collar and pointed—not at the riders, at the ground ahead—and made his small urgent sound.

Brannoc saw it a heartbeat later: a seam in the white, running crooked toward the horizon, where the salt had buckled and beneath it showed a darkness that was not shadow. Old stone. The causeway. His father's road, real after thirty years of nobody walking it, buried under a hand's depth of crust.

"Dig," he said, and they dug with hands and a broken oar-blade and Wren's boot heel, and by late afternoon they had uncovered fifty yards of ancient paving, wide enough for four abreast, hard underfoot, and mercifully lower than the surrounding flats so that it hid them from the south like a trench.

They moved along it as the light went long and copper. For a few hours it was almost kind. The stone held the day's heat and gave it back through their boot-soles like a hearth. Pell slept against Brannoc's neck, finally, truly slept, and Brannoc matched his stride to the boy's breathing and thought about nothing, which was the only luxury left.

Then the boy woke screaming without any sound at all.

* * *

It happened fast after that. Pell's whole body arched, his mouth wide, his hands clawing at air, and Tamsin was there in three strides with the fire-jar pressed to his chest, and whatever passed between the coal and the child Brannoc never saw—but Pell sagged, shuddering, and pointed south, and pointed again, insistent, desperate, and Brannoc understood: the riders weren't following tracks. They were following something else. Something Pell could hear. Whatever Halden had fed to the cracks, whatever thing he'd bought or bound or bribed, it was out on the white behind them, hunting them by prayer.

"They're not tracking us," Odile said grimly. "They're being tracked. There's a difference, and the difference is that we can't lose them by losing our trail."

Night came down violet-black and shockingly cold, the flats giving back the day's heat all at once, and they walked by starlight because stopping meant freezing and the riders presumably had torches. Brannoc counted water by mouthfuls. Two swallows each at midnight. One at dawn. The arithmetic was ugly and everyone could do it, and nobody said it aloud, which was its own kind of courage.

Near dawn Pell collapsed.

No warning this time—or maybe warning Brannoc had missed, the boy lighter and lighter on his shoulders, quieter even than quiet, burning hot. He simply slid sideways off Brannoc's back and onto the ancient stones and did not get up. His eyes were open. His lips moved around words that wouldn't come, and Tamsin knelt and put her ear to his mouth as though silence could be listened to, and her face went grey.

"He's been carrying it," she said. "All this way. Every god that's loose out there, every crack—they've been pouring through him because he listens, because someone has to listen, and he's a child so they chose him, the way water chooses the lowest ground." She looked up, and her milk-pale eyes were terrible. "He's been holding the door of himself shut for five days. He's done."

"We carry him," Brannoc said. It came out flat and final, the tone he used for prices.

"On what?" Wren whispered. "Water? Strength? We're forty miles out if the map is kind. Carrying him kills us all by tomorrow."

"Then we're all killed tomorrow," Brannoc said, "and today we carry him," and he bent and gathered the boy up, and found Ines already taking the other side, and found that Odile had silently redistributed half the water-skins onto her own back, and found that a thing he'd have called impossible a year ago had somehow become simply what they did. That was family, he supposed. Nobody voted. You just looked around and it had happened while you weren't guarding yourself.

They carried Pell through the dawn. The sun rose and the white ignited and the world became pain again, and behind them, faint and patient, the lance-light followed.

* * *

It was near noon, with Pell limp as wet rope between them and the riders close enough now to resolve into individual shapes—helmets, pennons, the black-and-white of Halden's household troop—that Tamsin stopped walking.

She set the jar down on the causeway stones. First time in six days. She unwrapped the wool layer by layer, unhurried, a woman performing a rite she'd rehearsed her whole life without wanting to perform it, and lifted the lid, and the coal inside breathed.

Even banked, even dying, it was beautiful. A knot of orange-gold light no bigger than a plum, pulsing slow, like a heart deciding whether to keep at the work. Heat rolled off it and touched their faces, and Brannoc felt every one of them lean toward it the way frostbitten men lean toward a stove.

"No," Ines said. He knew, then. Everyone knew. "Tamsin. No."

"He has perhaps a season left in the jar," Tamsin said. "Perhaps a month. Out here, carried, fading—he has today." She looked down the causeway at the riders, at the dust-plume, at the small black figures beginning to spread into a crescent, cutting them off from the north. "Or he has this. One pass. One true burn, the way he was before we gutted him keeping the seals lit. It will cost him—" her voice caught, steadied, "—most of what's left. But he was a king of the sky, Ines. He is not going to die in a jar."

"You said dying isn't the same as being put out," Ines said desperately.

"And this is neither. This is spending. There's a difference, and it's his to choose, not ours." She cupped her hands around the coal and bowed her head, and her lips moved, and whether she prayed or merely spoke to an old friend, Brannoc couldn't tell and didn't ask.

The coal flared.

Not gradually—it leapt, doubled, trebled, gold flooding up through the cracks of the clay until the whole jar glowed like a lantern, and the heat went from stove-warm to unbearable in three heartbeats, and then Tamsin hurled it upward with both arms, all her strength, a keeper's throw learned across a lifetime of ash-caves, and the fire left the jar and unfolded.

Wings. Brannoc had seen Serevan once, in the caves, a vast guttering shape in the dark, and had thought he understood. He had not understood. Made of flame against the noon-white sky, the dragon was almost invisible—heat-shimmer given outline, a distortion shaped like vengeance—and yet every eye on that salt plain, theirs and Halden's riders alike, locked onto him at once, because the body knows fire the way it knows falling.

Serevan came down the wind out of the south with the sun behind him, and the sound reached them a full breath after the sight: a roar like a mountain learning to speak, ragged at the edges, costing him everything, and magnificent past all decency.

He hit the riders' crescent like a thrown hammer.

Brannoc made himself watch, because they deserved witnesses. The dragon did not land and did not linger—one pass, low, wings beating a storm of superheated air that turned the flats to a blast-furnace. Lances drooped and ran like candlewax. Horses screamed and broke. Men tore off burning cloaks and fled in every direction across the white, and where Serevan's fire touched the salt itself it fused it to glass in long glowing scars, writing his passing into the ground in a script that would outlive them all. Twelve riders became scattered embers and running shadows, and the crescent dissolved, and the way north stood open.

One pass. He'd promised himself one pass, or Tamsin had promised it for him, and he kept to it—banked hard at the end of his run, climbing, climbing, slower with every stroke—

And fell.

Not far. Not to the ground. He came down half a mile away in a controlled collapse that was only barely controlled, wings splayed across the glassed salt, steam rising off him in sheets, and the company ran to him through the shimmering heat with Pell bouncing forgotten on Brannoc's shoulder because the boy had woken, was awake, was straining toward the fallen fire and making his cracked silent sound over and over.

Serevan lay with his great head stretched along the ground, and where there should have been eyes there were two coals banked low again, dimmer now. Dimmer than the jar. Much dimmer. His breathing moved the air in slow tides, and each tide smelled of cinders and, faintly, unbearably, of the ash-caves, of home.

Tamsin knelt at his jaw and pressed her forehead to the hot glass-smooth scale there, and did not weep, though her shoulders shook with the effort of not weeping.

"I'm sorry," Ines said hoarsely. "I'm sorry—we asked—"

*You did not ask,* said a voice like a landslide very far underground, and Brannoc's knees nearly went, because the dragon spoke into their minds the way heat speaks into a closed room, everywhere at once. *I asked. I have guarded a seal for two hundred years and watched it fail by inches, and I have wanted one hour of being what I was. You gave me the hour. The rest is bookkeeping.*

His gaze-coals turned, slowly, and fixed on Pell. The boy reached out and laid both small hands flat against the dragon's muzzle, and the terrible tension in him eased, eased, went out of him like a fever breaking.

*The listener is emptied,* Serevan said. *The gods poured through him and found nothing left to pour into. He will sleep, and wake lighter, and hear them still—but gently now, at a distance, the way one hears rain. Tell him he carried more than any of us.*

"And you?" Tamsin whispered. "What's left?"

The great sides rose and fell. Once. Twice. When the answer came it was quieter, and it was the worst thing Brannoc had heard in a life of bad things.

*Enough to reach your Door. Not enough to matter beyond it. Keeper—I am a candle now, and candles are for spending. Carry me the rest of the way. Let me see what the apprentice builds before the wax runs out.*

So they made harness from rope and cloak-straps and the oar-blade, and they did the unthinkable thing, the thing out of ballads and lies: they loaded the last dragon onto a sledge of salvaged shield-plates and dragged him north across the salt, all of them in the traces, Ines and Brannoc at the front, Odile and Wren behind, Tamsin walking at the great head with her hand on his jaw, Pell asleep in the shade of a wing like a child under a strange new sun.

Every mile was paid for. Brannoc felt each one in his shoulders, his palms, the raw places where the ropes bit, and he paid gladly and counted the cost honestly, because that was the whole of smuggling and, he suspected, the whole of love: you moved the cargo, and you never pretended it weighed nothing.

On the second evening after the pass, the white began to change. Faint dark lines appeared in the salt, running north—old field-bounds, Wren said, voice shaking, hedgerows from before the Edict, remembered into visibility by a country remembering itself. And far ahead, at the edge of dusk, the horizon showed a smudge of grey that was not cloud.

Stone. Walls. The coronation field, and beneath it, waiting, the Door of Ash.

Behind them, on the southern rim of the world, new dust plumes rose—more than before, many more, moving fast.

Halden had brought an army.
