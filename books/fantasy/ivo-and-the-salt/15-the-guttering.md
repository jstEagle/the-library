## 15. The Guttering

Tamsin felt the dragon fall before she saw it.

She was standing at the mouth of the ash-caves when the change came—a wrongness in the air pressure, the way the cave's slow breathing stuttered. For eleven years she had lived inside Serevan's fire the way a wren lives inside a warm wall, attuned to its smallest fluctuations: the bright crackle of his waking hours, the long banked coals of his sleep, the feverish flutter that meant pain or memory. She knew every note of him. And now the whole song dropped away at once, like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

"No," she said, to no one, to everyone, and ran.

The caves were a half-mile of switchbacking dark, walls furred with ash-salt that glittered when her lantern passed. She had run this path in drills of terror a hundred times—when the tremors came, when the guttering worsened through the winter, when Pell first arrived with gods whispering in his silent skull—but never like this, never with the fire itself failing in real time, each heartbeat of it audible as a slowing drum.

She burst into the great vault where Serevan lay coiled beneath the ceiling's broken oculus, the one wound in the mountain's roof through which, on clear nights, a single star could be seen—the last star the old kingdom had named, before the names were struck from the ledgers.

He was down. Not sleeping-down, not resting-down. *Down.* His wings were flung wide across the ash floor like the sails of a wrecked ship, one membrane torn along the trailing edge in a long gray ribbon. His scales, which had once held the color of forge-light under water, had gone dull as old coins. And his fire—

His fire was almost out.

Tamsin had watched it shrink for years. She had told herself stories about it: dragons burn slow, dragons burn patient, a thousand years is nothing to a creature who remembers the world being made. She had measured his flame each dawn with a guild-glass Brannoc had stolen for her from a Saltmaker ruin, marking the level in her ledger-book the way an apothecary marks a dying patient's pulse. The line went down. It always went down. But it went down the way a river falls toward the sea—gradually, inevitably, *naturally*.

What she saw now was not gradual. The light beneath his breast-scales, where the fire-heart lived, was flickering like a lamp out of oil, surging and starving, surging and starving, and around the great veins that carried the flame outward she could see—she pressed her palm against the hot-then-cold hide and felt it plainly—the rhythm of something being *drawn*.

Not spent. Drawn. Pulled out of him in long, steady draughts, the way a leech feeds.

"Serevan." She climbed onto the ledge beside his jaw, the place he allowed her, the only creature alive he allowed anywhere near. His eye opened—a slit of molten gold gone muddy as pond-water—and found her. In it she saw herself reflected, small and furious and already weeping, though she would not have admitted to weeping if you'd asked.

*Little keeper,* he said. He did not speak aloud; he had not the strength. His voice arrived instead the way heat arrives, directly against the skin, inside the bones. *You are shouting.*

"I'm not saying anything."

*Inside you are. You shout very loudly inside.* The ghost of humor, and then the ghost of it died too, and his eye closed again. *I am sorry. I flew anyway. They needed the shield.*

"They needed you *alive*, you great stupid beautiful ruin—" Her voice cracked. She put both hands flat against his jaw and felt the fire stutter under her palms, surge-starve, surge-starve. "Hold still. Let me look at you properly."

She worked the way she had been taught by a keeper three times dead now: hands first, then eyes, then instruments, then grief. She walked the length of him in the failing light, reading the map of his body the way Wren read maps of countries that weren't there. And the more she read, the colder she became, until the cold burned hotter than any fury she had ever known.

Because the siphoning was not new.

It was everywhere, once she knew to look for it. Faint scars along the major fire-channels, pale lines in the living gold like old surgical seams. A pattern of small punctures along the spine, healed over centuries ago—or what passed for centuries in a dragon's slow accounting—and then, lower down, newer ones. Much newer. One, near the root of the tail, so fresh its edges still wept a thin luminous fluid that smelled of ozone and incense and something else, something Tamsin recognized with a lurch: the cold mineral smell of consecrated ground. Of church-floor. Of *Inquisition*.

They had been bleeding him for a very long time. All those years she had sat by his side charting his decline and calling it age, calling it the world's weight, calling it fate—someone had been standing in these caves, or sending others here, opening him vein by vein and drinking his fire down into vessels she could not imagine, and she had held his wing and sung to him and called it natural.

*Tamsin.* His voice came faintly, alarmed. *Your heart is going too fast. Little keeper. Breathe.*

"You knew." It came out flat. "You knew, didn't you. You've always known."

A long silence. The fire surged, starved.

*I knew there were thieves,* he said at last. *I did not know their faces. Dragons do not see well in the dark of our own bodies. And I thought—* the heat of his voice shivered, which was the closest a dragon came to trembling— *I thought if I told you, you would do something brave and brief and fatal. You are built for brave and brief and fatal. It is your whole species' tragedy.*

"And instead I wrote it all down in a ledger," Tamsin whispered. "I made graphs."

*You kept me company,* said Serevan. *That is not nothing. That is perhaps everything.*

She knelt then, in the ash, and let herself shake—for a minute, no longer; she was a keeper, and keepers ration their grief the way sailors ration water. When she rose, her face was dry and her voice was level and somewhere far below the level, in the deep aquifers where Tamsin kept the things she could not yet afford to feel, a fury was gathering that would take years to spend.

Footsteps on the switchback path. Lantern-light. Voices—Brannoc's low rumble, Wren's quick nervous patter, Ines's quieter tone, and Odile's boots, military even in exhaustion. The company had made it off the flats. All of them? She counted shapes in the lantern-glow and her stomach dropped: they carried Pell between them, the boy limp as a dropped puppet, his lips gray.

"Bring him to the warm pool," Tamsin called down. "Now. And someone tell me exactly what happened on those flats, because I have news of my own, and mine is worse."

* * *

The warm pool was a shallow basin at the cave's edge where Serevan's residual heat bled up through the stone, keeping water at blood-temperature year-round. Tamsin had nursed smugglers and fugitives in it before; she lowered Pell into it with Wren holding the boy's head, and within minutes color crept back into his face like dawn coming up over a drowned country. The gods in him, whatever they were, quieted. He slept.

Then they gathered around the fire-pit at the vault's edge—the fire-pit Tamsin kept burning with ordinary wood, ordinary flame, because some hungers should stay honest—and she told them.

She started with the ledger. Eleven years of measurements, the falling line, the story she had told herself. Then she described the scars, the punctures, the fresh wound smelling of consecrated ground. She laid her guild-glass on the stone between them, next to the open book, evidence laid out like a magistrate's table.

"He isn't dying," she said. "He's being *harvested*. Slowly. Methodically. Someone has been tapping the fire-heart for—I don't know how long. Decades, maybe. Centuries, if they had help I can't guess at. The old scars are old. But the newest one is weeks old at most, and it smells like Inquisition marble."

Silence. Ines stared at the glass, at the falling line, and Tamsin watched something move behind his eyes—the particular horror of a craftsman recognizing another craftsman's work. "The seals," he said quietly. "The boundary stones. The salt corrodes unevenly—not everywhere at once, but in *patterns*. I couldn't work out why. If someone was drawing off the fire that feeds the seals, they wouldn't drain evenly. They'd drain selectively. Wherever they wanted a door open. Wherever they wanted a specific god to come through."

"And wherever they wanted doors to stay shut," Odile said. Her voice was careful, the voice of a woman walking on ice over deep water. "Halden has a list. I've seen fragments of it. Gods he considers *compatible with order*. He's been talking for months about a returned pantheon that legitimizes the Crown—gods of harvest-tithe, of borders, of obedience. Useful gods. Manageable gods." She looked up, and her face had gone the color of the ash on the walls. "He wasn't waiting for the gods to come back. He was *choosing* them. And he was choosing them with a straw stuck in a dying dragon."

"The Purge Years," Brannoc rumbled. The big smuggler had not moved since Tamsin began speaking; he sat with his scarred hands open on his knees, very still, the way he went still before violence. "My grandmother used to say the fires never went out right after the Edict. Said the mountains glowed for a generation, then dimmed all at once, like something swallowed. We called it the world healing over." His laugh had no humor in it at all. "Nothing heals over. Something was *drinking*."

Wren had pulled out charcoal and a scrap of vellum without seeming to notice doing it—the forger's hands always moving, always drafting. "If they've been siphoning for decades, they need infrastructure. Vessels. Transport. A route from here to wherever Halden keeps his store." Her eyes came up, sharp. "I've forged shipping manifests for half the contraband moving through Vellagard. Consecrated vessels, reliquary-grade, sealed against scrying—they'd show up somewhere. Give me two days and access to a port archive and I'll find the route."

"You'll find more than the route," Odile said. "You'll find the schedule. Because if Halden's agents have been coming here, they'll come again. The fire's nearly gone. Whatever he needs it for—his coronation-gods, his threshold moment—he'll want the last of it soon. Which means—" she stopped.

"Which means," Tamsin finished, "they're coming back. To finish him."

Every head turned toward the dragon.

Serevan had listened throughout, she realized—of course he had; the fire carried sound to him the way water carries sound to a swimmer. Now his eye opened again, and the mud in the gold seemed to clear a little, hardening into something older and harder. Gold again. Almost.

*Let them come,* he said, and the words pressed against them all like a change in weather. *I have been dying politely for thirty years because dying politely inconvenienced no one. I find I am finished with politeness.*

"You can barely lift your head," Tamsin said, and hated how her voice broke on it.

*No.* The vast head shifted, scales grinding against ash, and he turned his gaze on her alone. *But I remember how to burn. There is a difference between the fuel and the will, little keeper. They have taken the fuel. They have never once touched the will. And there is fire left in me—not much, but enough—enough for one true act. Choose it wisely. Choose it soon. Because I can feel the shape of my own ending now, and it is close, and I would rather it be a door than a drain.*

Tamsin stood in the failing light of the last dragon in the world, in the country that legally did not exist, surrounded by a smuggler, a forger, a priestess, a salt-witch, an Inquisitor turned fugitive, and a sleeping boy full of gods—and understood, with terrible clarity, what the choice was.

The fire that remained could carry them to the Door of Ash. Could shield them across the flats a second time, could hold back Halden's riders, could maybe—maybe—kindle the covenant Ines meant to write. Or it could be saved, hoarded, guarded here in the dark while the company crept toward the Door on foot and Halden's agents came, as they surely would, to take even this last measure of it.

There was no third option. There was never a third option. That was the lesson of her whole life: the Edict took the third options away, and everything since had been people learning to live inside the remaining two.

"We rest tonight," she heard herself say, in the voice of a keeper giving orders, the voice she had not used in eleven years and found, to her surprise, still fit. "All of us. Even you, Serevan—especially you. At dawn we decide. And when Halden's people come for the last of the fire—" she looked around the circle, at Brannoc's stillness, at Wren's sharpened face, at Ines with his salt-marked sleeve rolled up and showing, at Odile with her warrant folded against her heart— "they're going to find out what it costs to steal from a dragon who knows he's dying. And from the people who love him."

Far above, through the broken oculus, the last named star came out.

And in the dark below, so faint that only Tamsin felt it against her skin, Serevan's fire surged once—starved, wounded, murdered slowly across thirty years—and refused, with everything left in it, to go out.
