## 18. The Last Dragon-Fire

Tamsin had spent eleven years learning how a fire dies.

She had learned it the way other people learned grief—slowly, in the dark, with her hands inside it. She had learned that dragon-fire did not gutter like a candle but like a heart: unevenly, stubbornly, with long silences between beats. She had learned to read the color of Serevan's flame the way a physician reads the tongue of a fevered child. Gold meant strength. Amber meant hunger. The pale, almost-white blue he burned now—the color of frost at dawn, of salt crystals under moonlight—that was the color of a fire deciding whether it wished to continue.

Eleven years in the ash-caves below the old coronation field, feeding a dying god-beast charcoal and prayer and her own stubbornness. Eleven years, and now they had carried him up through the tunnels on sledges of greased oak, forty strong-backed smugglers and freed prisoners hauling rope, until Serevan lay coiled at the threshold of the Door of Ash like a river of smoke given temporary permission to be solid.

He was dying. Tamsin knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat. And yet the Door wanted him.

* * *

The Door of Ash was not a door.

That was the first thing Ines had said when they reached it, and Tamsin had loved him a little for it, because it was true and because only a Saltmaker would say it aloud. It was an absence in the cliff face—a seam where the rock forgot itself, tall as a cathedral spire and narrow as a grave, its edges fused into glass by some ancient heat. No handle. No hinge. Only the faintest warmth radiating from it, like the cheek of someone sleeping.

And around its base, in a wide ring, the salt.

Tamsin had never seen sealing-salt in such quantity. It lay in drifts, in dunes, glittering white-pink against the black stone, and when the wind moved through the gorge it sang—a thin, high harmonic, like wet fingers on a bowl's rim. Ines knelt at the edge of it for a long time before he touched it, and when he finally pressed his palm flat against the crystals, his whole body shuddered.

"It remembers," he said. "Every oath ever sworn on guild salt. They're all still here."

"Then let's give it something new to remember," Brannoc said, in the gruff way he said things when he was frightened and didn't want anyone to know.

But first there was the matter of the flame.

Ines had explained it the night before, standing over his maps and his salt-diagrams with ink to his elbows, talking faster as his terror grew. The covenant needed fuel—not metaphorical fuel, not good intentions, but living fire, freely given, poured into the Door's threshold so the terms written in salt could burn themselves into permanence. Ordinary fire would not do; ordinary fire ended. It had to be a fire that could sustain itself across the threshold, that could keep burning on both sides of the world at once.

There was exactly one such fire left in the country.

"There has to be another way," Tamsin had said, and hated how small her voice sounded.

Ines hadn't answered. That was answer enough.

* * *

Now morning came gray and slow through the gorge, and the company gathered at a respectful distance, and Tamsin walked alone to where Serevan lay.

Up close, the last dragon was mostly architecture. His ribs rose like the beams of a burned church. His scales, once the deep red-black of cooling lava, had gone ash-gray at the edges, each one edged in white like paper about to catch. His eye was open—it had been open for three days—and it tracked her with the slow, patient attention of something that had watched centuries go by and expected to watch more, and was now doing arithmetic about how many remained.

"You've brought me to a door," Serevan said. His voice was not sound so much as weather; it moved through her chest and rearranged things. "I remember doors. There were nine of us who kept them, once."

"I know." Tamsin sat down on the cold stone beside his great head, the way she had sat every evening for eleven years, close enough that his breath stirred her hair. His breath smelled of hot stone and, faintly, of salt. "I'm sorry. I know I've said it ten thousand times, and I'll say it ten thousand more. I'm sorry we hunted you. I'm sorry we were afraid of you. I'm sorry it took the end of everything to make us wise enough to come back."

The dragon's nostril flared, a soft bellows-sound. "You were a child when they came for me. You have been apologizing your whole life for a crime you did not commit."

"Somebody has to. Nobody else will."

Something moved in the vast gray face that she had learned, over years, to recognize as a smile. "Keeper. You know why you are here."

"Yes."

"And you know what is being asked."

"Yes." Her hands were shaking. She folded them in her lap so he wouldn't see, which was absurd, because he saw everything. "I've spent eleven years keeping you alive. Do you understand what you're asking me to do? To stand here and help you die?"

"I am asking you," Serevan said, "to help me stop dying."

* * *

He told her then—or perhaps he reminded her; perhaps he had been telling her for years and she had only just become able to hear it.

"A fire that is hoarded dies," he said. "A fire that is given does not. This is the oldest law, older than your gods, older than your salt. Your Ines understands it in his bones; it is why the salt works at all. Every seal ever laid was a gift—someone gave their labor, their blood, their certainty, into the salt, and the salt held it. Your master gave his life into it. You have been giving me yours, day after day, year after year, and do you know what that made? Not a dying dragon. A debt. The largest debt owed by this world to any single soul, and debts, keeper, are the one currency even silent gods must honor."

Tamsin's throat was too tight for words.

"If I pour my fire into that Door," Serevan went on, "and the Door takes it, and the covenant burns—I will gutter out within the hour. That is what your fear tells you, and your fear is not wrong. But the Door opens onto the other side. Onto *them*. And they will see what was given. They will feel the shape of the gift, because a gift has a shape, and no god alive or dead or sleeping can mistake it." The great eye turned fully upon her, and in its depths the pale-blue flame reflected, tiny and fierce. "They will owe me, Tamsin. And gods pay their debts in the only coin they have."

"Which is?"

"Impossibility."

* * *

The company assembled as the sun climbed. Ines stood at the ring of salt with his sleeves rolled back, the guild sigil bare on his forearm for the first time in his life, drawing terms in the air with a rod of white crystal while Wren checked the lines against her reconstructed maps and Pell stood at his elbow with his hands raised, translating the hum of the Door into urgent gestures only Ines seemed able to follow. Odile kept watch on the gorge road, hand on her warrant-staff. Brannoc organized the haulers, the witnesses, the freed prisoners from Halden's cages who had chosen, astonishingly, to stay and see this through.

Tamsin stood at Serevan's shoulder and gave the signal.

It took everything. She had thought she understood cost—eleven years of exile, of cold, of watching something magnificent diminish. She had not understood anything. When Serevan rose, unfolding himself from the black stone joint by joint, the sound was like a mountain changing its mind, and half the company fell to their knees, and Tamsin did not, because someone had to remain standing beside him, and she had decided eleven years ago that it would always be her.

He dragged himself to the ring of salt. He set his head against the ground before the Door of Ash—an act of bowing so immense that the salt-song changed key, dropping into a low resonant chord that Tamsin felt in her teeth, in her sternum, in the scar tissue of her old wounds.

"Saltmaker," Serevan said, and his voice cracked down the middle like drought-split earth. "Open the way."

Ines's hands were steady. Tamsin would think about that later, how steady they were, how a twenty-year-old apprentice stood before a dragon and a door and a gathering of frightened humanity and did not shake. He cut his palm with the guild knife, pressed it to the salt, and spoke the oldest words of the guild—the ones Tamsin had heard him practicing in whispers for weeks, thinking no one listened:

*"By salt remembered and blood witnessed, the seal becomes a door, and the door becomes a table, and all who come to it come as guests."*

The salt began to glow.

* * *

What happened next, Tamsin would spend the rest of her life failing to describe.

Serevan opened his jaws, and the fire came—but not the pale guttering blue of the ash-caves. As it left him, as it crossed the air toward the glowing threshold, it remembered itself. It went gold, then white-gold, then a color Tamsin had no name for, the color of the first morning of the world, and it poured into the Door like water finding the sea, and the Door drank, and the singing of the salt rose and rose until it was less a sound than a state of being.

And Serevan emptied.

Tamsin saw it happen. Saw the last light leave him, saw the great ribs fall and not rise, saw the eye that had watched centuries begin, at last, to dim. She was running before she knew she'd moved, skidding on her knees through the salt drifts to press her hands against the vast cooling scale above his heart, and she was screaming words she couldn't hear herself say, and the company behind her was a blur of kneeling shapes, and the Door blazed like a second sunrise—

—and then the Door *answered.*

Light came back through. It came the way the fire had gone, in a flood, in a tide, gold and white-gold and that unnameable color, pouring out of the threshold and down over the salt and into the hollowed shell of the last dragon, and where it touched him, he *kindled.* From the inside. From somewhere deeper than lungs or blood. Tamsin felt it under her palms first—a pulse, then a rhythm, then a roar, a furnace-heart catching hold after eleven years of dying embers—and Serevan's eye flared open, blazing, blazing, *blazing*, and the sound he made was not pain.

It was laughter. Deep, rolling, impossible laughter, shaking salt from the cliffsides, and every god-touched thing in the gorge answered it—the chapel-bell far off in Vellagard ringing unprompted, the boundary stones humming, the salt itself leaping in little white fountains along the ring as though the ground had begun to applaud.

"They *paid*," Serevan thundered. "Keeper! Keeper, look—they paid the debt!"

Tamsin was laughing too, and crying, and her hands were scorched and she did not care, because under her palms the fire beat strong and even and *gold*, and it was not the borrowed flicker she had nursed for a decade but something self-sustaining, something fed from the other side of the Door, a circuit closed between dragon and divine, each burning for the other, neither permitted to go out.

Death had been refused. Refused not by force, not by trickery, but by reciprocity—the oldest law, honored at last.

* * *

Afterward, when the light had settled into a steady warm glow along the Door's edges and the company had found its feet and its voice, Tamsin sat again beside Serevan's head. He was larger, somehow. Or perhaps he had always been this large, and eleven years of diminishment had made her forget.

"You knew," she said. "You knew they'd send it back."

"I hoped," Serevan said. "Hoping is the mortal part of me. The immortal part merely trusted the arithmetic." His breath washed over her, warm as a hearth, smelling now of clean heat and, beneath it, something green—growing things, spring things, things that had no business existing in a gorge of ash. "You fed a dying fire for eleven years, Tamsin of the ash-caves. The gods counted every coal."

"Then they owe me too."

"They do." The vast eye gleamed. "Ask carefully."

Behind them, Ines Oyelaran stepped up to the blazing threshold with his bloodied hand and his rolled-back sleeves and the terms of a new world trembling in his grip, and Pell tugged Tamsin's sleeve and pointed urgently at the Door, where—faint as figures seen through smoked glass—shapes were beginning to gather on the other side.

They were waiting to hear what the apprentice had written.

Tamsin got to her feet, wiped her eyes on her scorched sleeve, and went to stand witness.
