## 21. Nine Chairs Filled

The hall had been dead longer than it had been alive.

Ines stood in the doorway of the Saltmakers' Guild the morning of the seating and let that truth sit on his tongue like a grain of coarse salt—slow to dissolve, honest in its sting. Twelve years of dust had been scrubbed from the flagstones, but the stones remembered. He could feel it under his boots, the faint grit of old brine worked so deep into the floor that no broom would ever lift it. The walls still bore the pale tide-lines where saltwater had been splashed across them in a hundred years of blessings. Someone had repaired the roof—the smugglers' carpenters, Wren's forged writs turned honest at last—but they had left one hole above the masters' dais, a ragged eye of sky, because Tamsin had asked them to. *Let him see out,* she'd said, meaning Serevan, who could no longer climb the mountain but who circled Vellagard now on slow, wide wings whenever the weather allowed, and who liked, she said, to look down at the place where the salt was kept.

The nine chairs stood on the dais in their crescent, and for the first time since Ines had been a boy carrying buckets, they were not empty.

He had dreamed of this hall for twelve years, and in every dream the chairs were empty. That was the shape his grief had taken: nine vacant seats arranged like an accusation, each one holding nothing but the ghost of a body he had known or known of—Master Corvane with her ink-stained fingers, Master Ilyen who hummed while he worked the drying racks, Master Ossa who had signed the surrender with a hand that shook so badly the Edict's ink had blurred, as if even the law could not quite bear its own signature. And at the crescent's head, the tenth chair, the master's chair, which had been his master's—Master Ede Oyelaran, who had taught him that salt remembers, and who had died on the steps of this very hall with Halden's warrant still warm in her pocket.

Ines had expected, walking in this morning, to weep. He had brought a handkerchief for it, folded in his sleeve where the guild sigil used to hide.

Instead he found himself laughing.

Because the chairs were full, and full was not what he had imagined. Full was Odile Ferrant sitting in Master Corvane's seat with her back straight as a pikestaff and her expression suggesting she suspected the chair of committing a crime. Full was Tamsin in Master Ilyen's, ash still in her hair from the caves, talking quietly to the chair itself as if introducing herself. Full was Wren already bored, already sketching the ceremony on the back of a forged—and therefore beautifully executed—program, and Pell beside him sitting very straight, hands flat on his knees, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear and smiling about it.

Full was Brannoc Vale, who had sworn up and down for three weeks that he would not stand for it, standing in the ninth chair with the sheepish dignity of a man who has lost an argument with a family.

"You're staring," Odile said, without turning her head.

"I'm memorizing," Ines said. "It's different."

"It had better be." She glanced at him then, and something in her face softened by a degree—a degree being, for Odile, a landslide. "I sat vigil last night. Did you know that? Old custom. Wren dug it out of some archive."

"The Vigil of the Empty Chair," Ines said. "You sit in a dead master's seat and ask their permission to fill it."

"And did yours answer?"

"Mine didn't need asking." He looked down the crescent to the head chair—not the tenth, but the one beside it, newly carved from ash-wood salvaged from the Door itself, its back plain, its arms worn smooth before it was ever finished because the wood had already been handled by ten thousand pilgrims crossing the salt flats. "My master told me years ago what to do with empty chairs. I just didn't want to hear it."

*Fill them,* she had said, the night before the signing, her voice coming through the salt like water through cloth. *A guild is not its dead, boy. A guild is whoever swears the oath next.*

* * *

They seated the masters at noon, when the light through the roof-hole fell in a single bright column across the dais, and the whole city seemed to have come to watch.

Not the whole city. But enough. The night market had closed for the day—voluntarily, which Ines suspected had never happened in its history—and its people filled the guildhall's benches: the luck-knot sellers and fever-salt grinders, the charm-menders and prayer-brokers, folk who had traded in the gray margins of outlawed magic for a generation and who now watched, with the wary hope of stray cats offered a bowl, as the trade came in from the cold. Former customers. Former neighbors. A few former prisoners, released from Halden's cells after the arrest, blinking in daylight, holding the writs of license Wren had drafted for them like talismans—which, Ines supposed, they were.

Even a handful of gray-cloaked figures sat near the back: Inquisitors seeking penance, as the covenant's terms permitted. Odile had insisted on that clause. *If the law can't take back its own,* she'd said, *then the law never really changed.* One of them, an old woman with a burn scar along her jaw, caught Ines looking and inclined her head—an inch, no more. It was, he understood, the largest gesture she could currently afford. He returned it.

Brannoc opened the rite, because Brannoc had lost the argument about the chair but won the argument about who spoke first. His voice, built for shouting over storms and harbor bells, dropped to something almost gentle.

"Twelve years ago," he said, "the Crown declared this kingdom dissolved. Said it never existed. Said our names were nothing, our laws were nothing, our gods were nothing." He paused, and Ines saw his jaw work. "I spent those twelve years proving them wrong in the only way I knew how. Smuggling. Carrying things across a border that wasn't supposed to exist, into a country that wasn't supposed to exist, to people who weren't supposed to exist." He looked at the nine chairs. "Turns out there was another way. Turns out a woman with a bucket of salt had been doing it all along. So. Let's get on with it, before I say something sentimental."

Laughter, warm and rough, moved through the benches like wind through wheat.

One by one, they rose and took their seats.

Odile first, because seniority of stubbornness counted for something. She knelt at the foot of the dais and spoke the old words—Wren had reconstructed them from a child's skipping rhyme, of all things, a rhyme about *salt in the shoe and the door held true*—and when she reached the line where the candidate named what they brought to the guild, she did not hesitate.

"I bring the law," she said. "Not the law that erased you. The older one. The one underneath. I bring warrants and witness and the willingness to arrest anyone—including myself—who breaks covenant." She stood, climbed the dais, and sat in Corvane's chair, and the chair—Ines saw it clearly, saw the whole crowd see it—sighed. A small exhalation of white dust from its joints, like breath, like relief. The salt remembered every oath ever sworn upon it. It had been waiting twelve years to remember this one.

Tamsin came next, barefoot, because keepers of dragons apparently did everything barefoot. She brought fire—or rather, she brought *the promise of fire*, cupping her hands around a coal from Serevan's new flame, the flame the gods had kindled from their side of the Door, the flame that burned now without fuel and without end. When she sat, the coal floated up from her palms and settled into a niche above the chair-back, and there it stayed, burning, the guild's first hearth-light in twelve years.

Wren brought forgery, honestly declared—"I bring the making of true things by false means, and I'll be renegotiating that phrasing later"—and the crowd roared. Pell brought silence, and the gods' ear that lived inside it; when he sat, he tilted his head as though listening to the ninth chair, and then he leaned over and whispered something to Brannoc, who went red to the ears and sat down very fast.

"What did he say?" Ines whispered.

"He says Master Ossa wants to know why it took me so long," Brannoc muttered. "And that my mother's watching, and I should mind my posture."

The remaining four chairs went to names the city was still learning: Marisol the chapel-flooder's daughter, who had prayed the rain-prayer that drowned a nave and who had come out of that flood with rain answering her like a well-trained dog; old Ferro from the fish-market, whose wish had gone monstrous and who had spent a year doing penance hauling flood-victims' furniture before Odile ruled his debt paid; a quiet woman named Ashka who had been one of Halden's siphon-technicians and who knew the machinery of stolen godlight better than anyone alive; and, in the last chair, to a gasp from the benches, the scarred old Inquisitor herself, Sister Yeva, who had opened the prison doors during the purge and stood trial for it and been acquitted by a jury of the very people she'd freed.

Nine chairs. Nine masters. Each seat sighing its small breath of salt-dust as it accepted its keeper, until the crescent on the dais was a crescent of the living, and the column of noon light lay across all of them like a blessing neither god nor man had to authorize.

Then it was Ines's turn, and the hall went quiet in a way that had weight, like deep water.

* * *

He walked to the foot of the dais and knelt on the stone, and the stone was cold through his trousers, and the cold smelled like his childhood—drying racks and brine vats and his master's kitchen, where she'd taught him to taste salt the way other people tasted wine. *Bitter means frightened,* she'd said. *Sweet means lying. Clean means telling the truth.* He tasted his own fear now, kneeling there, and found it clean.

"Ines Oyelaran," said Odile, speaking the officiant's part because the rite required a master to call the candidate and no one had argued, "last apprentice of the Saltmakers' Guild. What do you bring?"

He had prepared an answer. Wren had helped him draft it; Odile had edited it twice; Tamsin had made him rehearse it to Serevan, who had listened with one ancient eye and pronounced it, via Tamsin, *adequate for a mammal.*

He abandoned all of it.

"I bring the seal," he said. "Broken, and remade on better terms. I bring the salt, and the memory in it—every oath, every name, every prayer that was ever spoken over these stones, including the ones the Edict tried to erase." His voice cracked on the next part and he let it. "And I bring the tenth chair. Because a guild of nine masters needs somewhere for the apprentice to sit."

Silence. Then Odile, dry as a salt flat: "We discussed this. You're to take a master's chair. You ended the ban. You wrote the covenant. The vote was unanimous."

"The vote was unanimous," Ines agreed, "and I'm declining it." He stood, and nodded to the side of the hall, where the carpenters had waited all morning with a tarp over something. "Bring it up, please."

They carried it up the dais steps together: a chair of pale ash-wood, smaller than the others, plainer, its back just high enough to lean against, its legs slightly uneven in the way of things made quickly by many hands. During the weeks after the signing, apprentices had come to the guildhall every day—not guild members yet, just hopefuls, children and grandmothers and one reformed pickpocket—and someone had suggested building a chair, and everyone had carved a little of it, and no two parts matched, and somehow it held together. On its back, burned into the wood in letters that wobbled like a child's handwriting, were the words:

**APPRENTICE, RETIRED**

"You can't put me in a master's chair," Ines said, "because I'm not finished learning. Twelve years ago this guild was murdered and I survived it by hiding. I hid my sigil, I hid my craft, I hid behind charms that 'shouldn't work.' I'm done hiding, but I'm not done being a student. There are nine masters up here now. Nine people to learn from." He set his hand on the tenth chair's back, on the wobbling burned letters. "So give me this instead. Let me be the guild's apprentice forever. Let me sit where the learning happens."

Nobody spoke. Above, through the roof-hole, a shadow crossed the sun—wide wings, a slow circle. Serevan, checking on his salt. Tamsin laughed softly.

Then Master Yeva, the scarred Inquisitor, rose from the ninth chair and said formally, in the voice of a woman who had once condemned people with exactly this cadence and had never forgiven herself for it: "The guild accepts the candidate's terms. Seat him."

Ines sat.

The chair did not sigh like the others. It did something else—it *warmed*, gently, all through its mismatched joints, as if every apprentice who had ever carved a piece of it had left a little heat in the wood, and all that heat had been waiting for him. He felt it climb his spine and settle behind his sternum, and he thought, absurdly, of his master's kitchen, of the kettle always on, of the seat nearest the stove that had always been his.

The nine masters leaned toward him, and the light from the roof-hole widened, and below, the benches began—not cheering, not yet, but a low sound like the sea deciding, like a wave gathering its courage.

* * *

Afterward there was wine on the steps and salt bread—Tamsin's recipe, ash-baked—and Pell taught Ines a game of knucklebones that was definitely cheating, because the bones kept landing the way Pell wanted, and when Ines accused him of it Pell just smiled his wide silent smile and shrugged, *the gods owe me favors.*

Evening came down over Vellagard the color of brass, and one by one the others drifted off—to the market, to the chapel, to the caves, to whatever lives they were now allowed to have openly. Ines stayed on the dais, alone with the chairs, which was the thing he had dreaded for twelve years and which now felt like sitting with family after a feast: comfortable, unhurried, no need for words.

He laid his palm on his master's chair. The wood was cool. But under the coolness, faint as a heartbeat, the salt remembered.

*Well,* said the memory of Ede Oyelaran, in the tone she'd used for *you've burned the batch again, boy.* *An apprentice, retired. Only you would find the one rank lower than master and make it an honor.*

"You taught me that empty chairs are a question," Ines said aloud. "I finally answered."

*Mm. Badly, slowly, with far too much drama. But answered.* A pause, and then, gentler, the way she'd spoken the night before she died: *The hall looks right with people in it. Don't let it go empty again. And boy—*

The voice thinned, the way voices do at the edge of sleep.

*Boy. Check the north archive. Something's been moved.*

Ines went still. "Moved? What's been—"

But the presence was fading, the way salt dissolves, not gone but dispersed, everywhere and nowhere, and the dais was just a dais, and the evening just an evening, and somewhere below a cart rattled past the guildhall carrying, by the smell of it, ordinary honest sea-salt for ordinary honest tables.

Ines sat a moment longer, heart ticking oddly. Then he took the lamp from its hook—the first lamp lit from Tamsin's undying coal, a flame that had never once guttered—and went down the back stairs to the north archive, where the guild had kept its oldest records, its oaths, its maps of every boundary stone in the country.

The door was open.

He had locked it himself that morning. He was certain of it. He was certain of everything, right up until he raised the lamp and saw, on the long reading table in the middle of the room, a single line of coarse grey salt, poured fresh, laid out in characters he recognized with a lurch that started in his stomach and went all the way up:

Guild script. Old guild script. The hand of a master.

Three words, precise as a signature:

***WE ARE NOT DONE.***
