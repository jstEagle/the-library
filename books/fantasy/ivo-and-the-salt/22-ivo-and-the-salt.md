## 22. Ivo and the Salt

The night market of Vellagard had learned to breathe again.

Ines noticed it first in the smells—no longer the sour tang of fear-sweat and tallow smoke that had clung to the stalls in the years after the Purge, but cardamom and roasting chestnuts, river-mud and cut lavender, the green bite of fresh salt being carried up from the flats in open pails. Salt was legal now. Salt was *licensed*. There were forms for it, stamped with the sigil of a guild that had nine masters and one retired apprentice, and Ines still found it faintly absurd every time he signed his name at the bottom of one.

His stall stood where it had always stood, or near enough—the third turning off the Ropewalk, under the leaning eave of a chandler's shop that had changed hands four times since the market burned. He had rebuilt it himself, plank by plank, from timber salvaged from the old night market's ashes. Wren had offered to forge him something grander. Brannoc had offered to *steal* him something grander. Ines had wanted neither. This stall was where he had sold luck-knots to the frightened and fever-salts to the desperate, hiding his guild sigil beneath his sleeve like a wound he was ashamed to show. It seemed right that the teaching should happen here, in the same narrow space, with the same crooked awning dripping rain when it rained.

And it was raining tonight, softly, the way Vellagard rained in autumn—a fine silver sift that beaded on the canvas and made the lantern light go soft and haloed around the edges.

Three apprentices sat before him on upturned crates. He had chosen them the way the new guild chose everyone: not for bloodline, not for talent alone, but for the shape of their wanting. Maretta, seventeen, quick-fingered, who could tie a luck-knot blindfolded but wept when she spoke of her brother lost on the flats during the March. Oskar, twelve, son of a former Inquisitor, who had arrived at the hall with his father's warrant-papers in hand and asked, in a voice like a cracked bell, whether penance could be *practiced* rather than merely felt. And little Sefa, nine, who heard the gods the way Pell once had—not words exactly, but weather, a pressure behind the ears, a knowing—and who had been brought to Ines by Tamsin herself with the words: "She'll need someone who remembers what it was like to be afraid of your own gift."

Ines rolled his sleeves as he worked. He always rolled them now. The mark sat on the inside of his left forearm where it had always sat—the interlocked rings and grain-lines of the Saltmakers' Guild, inked into skin the year he turned fourteen, back when showing it would have cost him his hands. Now apprentices touched it like pilgrims touching a relic, and he let them, because he remembered what it was to believe the thing you were was contraband.

"Again," he said to Maretta. "From the crossing."

She took up the cord—grey wool, spun with a thread of true salt-silk that caught the lantern light like frost—and began the knot. Her fingers moved well. Too fast, though. That was the trouble with Maretta; she worked like someone fleeing a room.

"Slower," Ines said. "A charm is a conversation, not an argument. You're asking the salt to hold a promise. Would you shout a promise?"

"No, master."

"Then don't rush it." He watched the knot take shape under her hands: the crossing, the binding, the three-fold turn that meant *witnessed*. "Every knot we tie is a small covenant. Consent—" he tapped the cord, "—that's the wool agreeing to hold. Witness—" he tapped the salt-silk thread, "—that's the salt remembering. And answerability—" he tapped her knotted fingers, gently, "—that's you. If the charm fails, you answer for it. If it works, you answer for that too. Magic under law we help write. Say it."

"Magic under law we help write," the three of them recited. Oskar said it loudest, with the fervor of a boy unlearning an old catechism.

Sefa didn't say it. She was looking past the stall, out into the rain, her head tilted at the particular angle that Ines had learned meant *listening*. He waited. He had learned patience from a dragon's keeper and stubbornness from a smuggler and grace, imperfect grace, from a woman who had once worn an Inquisitor's badge and now wore a master's chair.

"There's a god at the fish-stalls," Sefa said at last. "A small one. It likes the smell."

"Then it has good taste," Ines said gravely, "and it's welcome, so long as it doesn't bargain with anyone under the age of consent. That's clause four."

Maretta laughed. Even Oskar smiled. But Sefa kept looking at him with those wide dark eyes, and Ines felt the old ache move through him—not pain anymore, not exactly. More like the way an old break aches before rain. A memory in the bone.

* * *

He closed the stall late, as he always did, walking the apprentices out through the market's winding lanes because he liked to see them home and because, even now, even after everything, he liked to walk Vellagard at night. The city had changed less than people claimed and more than they admitted. The Edict stones were gone—burned clean off the archive-rock in a single white flare the day of the Signing; he still dreamed sometimes of the sound it made, like a held breath released across half a country. But the streets were the same streets. The chapel where a child's prayer had drowned the nave was rebuilt now, its flood-line marked in blue tile so no one would forget. The street where a fisherman's wish had brought the sea uphill bore a small brass plaque with eleven names on it, and flowers at its base in every season, and Ines never passed it without stopping.

He stopped tonight too. Rain dripped off the eaves. Somewhere down the lane a fiddler played one of the recovered songs—Wren's doing, that; half the erased kingdom's music lived again because one forger couldn't bear to let a melody die—and the notes came thin and sweet through the wet air.

"You're brooding," said a voice behind him. "You only stop at this plaque when you're brooding."

Odile Ferrant fell into step beside him, collar up against the rain, grey threading her hair now at the temples. She carried no warrant these days except the one she'd written herself, the famous one, the first legal document of the restored kingdom—an arrest order executed against her own superior, invoking laws that officially hadn't existed until the moment she used them. They taught it in the law schools now. She pretended to find this embarrassing. Ines knew better.

"I'm not brooding," he said. "I'm accounting."

"Difference?"

"A brood goes in circles. An account goes somewhere." He nodded at the names on the plaque. "Eleven. We wrote the covenant so it could never be eleven again. Some nights I want to believe it. Some nights I count anyway."

"That's not brooding," Odile said. "That's being good at your job." She glanced sideways at him. "The hall's quiet without you. Pell keeps asking when you're coming back to teach the listening classes. He says you explain the silence better than anyone. He says you're the only person who ever treated the quiet like it was also a kind of answer."

"Pell flatters me."

"Pell does not know how to flatter. That's rather the point of Pell." She huffed a laugh, then sobered. "Tamsin sends word from the ash-caves, by the way. Serevan flew again last week. A full circuit of the flats. Tamsin says his fire is—" she searched for the word, "—*conversational* now. Whatever that means."

"It means the gods are feeding him and he's feeding them back," Ines said. "It means the covenant is holding. Reciprocity, Tamsin calls it. I call it the oldest magic there is: nobody carries the flame alone."

They walked on together through the rain, two people who had once been hunter and prey, and the city moved around them—lamplighters, late vendors, a pair of wardens in guild-grey nodding to Odile with the easy respect owed to a master. Once, Ines thought, she had hunted people like him through these same lanes. Once, he had hidden from her behind charms that shouldn't have worked and did. Now she carried salt in her pocket the way other officials carried seals, and he trusted her with his life more easily than he trusted most of his own memories.

At the corner where their ways divided, she paused. "The child," she said. "The one who asked you the question today. Pell told me. Word travels."

"What about her?"

"Nothing. Only—" Odile looked out at the rain, at the lamplight swimming gold in the gutters, at the whole breathing, lawful, watchful city. "Twelve years ago I helped enforce a lie that none of this existed. Some nights I count too, Ines. Eleven names. And then I look at what grew back over the grave, and I think: perhaps that's what justice is. Not the verdict. The regrowth." She touched his shoulder, brief and hard, and went off into the rain toward the hall of nine chairs and one empty one, carved and waiting, marked *Apprentice, Retired*, which she insisted he would sit in someday and which he insisted he would not, and which they both knew was a argument they would happily lose together someday, years from now, when the time was right.

* * *

The question came the next evening, near closing, the way the important ones always did.

The market was thick with the smell of frying onions and wet wool. Maretta was minding the charm-rack, Oskar was grinding fever-salt with the careful rhythm Ines had drilled into him—*a conversation, not an argument*—and Sefa was dozing in the big chair by the brazier, wrapped in a blanket, dreaming whatever gods-touched children dreamed. Business was gentle. Luck-knots for a bride's hair. A jar of hearth-salt for a grandmother who swore the old recipe was better, and who was right, and whom Ines refused to tell so on principle.

The child appeared between one customer and the next. Small, maybe seven, hood up against the drizzle, standing at the edge of the stall's light like something that had wandered out of a story and wasn't sure it was allowed to stay. Ines saw her the way he had once learned to see frightened people across a market stall—by the set of the shoulders, the way the hands hid inside the sleeves. Old knowledge. The knowledge of the years when he himself had been contraband.

"Come in out of the wet," he said. "There's room by the brazier."

She crept close enough to warm her hands and stood there a long while, watching Maretta tie knots and Oskar grind salt, saying nothing. Ines let her be silent. Silence was also a kind of answer; he had learned that from a mute boy who heard gods, and he had never forgotten the lesson.

Finally she spoke, without looking up, in a voice pitched low as if the question itself might be illegal.

"My gran says the gods are real again. My da says they're dangerous. The boy at school says if you pray wrong they take you." Her chin came up then, and her eyes were wet and fierce and terribly young. "Is it true? Do they really answer now?"

The market noise went on around them—onions, fiddle, rain. Maretta's hands had stilled on her cord. Oskar had stopped grinding. Even Sefa had opened her eyes in the big chair, listening.

Ines crouched down to the child's height, sleeves rolled, the guild-mark plain on his forearm where she could see it, where anyone could see it, where he would spend the rest of his life making sure it stayed visible. He thought of a mother in this same market, years ago, buying a charm that shouldn't have worked. He thought of nine empty chairs and a ghost-note in his master's hand. He thought of a door made of ash, and terms written in salt, and a dragon's last fire given freely and returned tenfold, and a signature in blood that had made a country exist again—not by decree, never again by decree, but by consent.

"Yes," he said. "They really answer now."

The child's breath caught.

"And we answer back." He took her small cold hand and pressed into it a luck-knot—Maretta's finest work, tied slow, tied witnessed, tied answerable. "That's the whole secret, little one. It was never about kneeling and hoping. It's a conversation. The gods speak, and we speak, and the salt remembers every word, and nobody—god, king, or inquisitor—gets to talk over anyone else ever again. That's the law now. We wrote it together." He smiled. "You'll learn it in school. And if you ever want to learn it properly—with your hands—you come find this stall. There's always room by the fire."

The child looked at the knot in her palm, then at his marked arm, then at his face, and something in her shoulders came undone—the fear going out of them like water out of a loosened knot. She nodded once, solemn as a signing, and ran off into the market with her fist closed tight around the wool and salt-silk, and Ines watched her go until the crowd took her, and found that his eyes were hot, and let them be.

"Well," said Maretta, clearing her throat. "If she comes back, can I teach her the crossing?"

"You'll teach her the crossing," Ines said, "and you'll teach her slower than you tie it yourself, and one day she'll teach someone else, and that's how a guild lives. Not in stone halls. In hands."

Oskar resumed grinding. Sefa burrowed back into her blanket. The rain thickened, gentled, thinned. Ines banked the brazier and rolled down one sleeve—only one, the left, and only halfway, because habits of pride should be practiced in moderation—and began putting up the shutters.

It was Sefa who stopped him, sitting bolt upright in the chair with her head tilted, listening to weather no one else could feel.

"Ines," she said. Children in the reborn guild called him by name; another of Tamsin's arguments, long since won. "There's a god at the edge of the market. A big one this time. It's been standing in the rain for an hour, watching our stall." Her eyes were enormous. "It says—it says it has a question. About the terms. About clause nine." She swallowed. "It says no one has ever invoked clause nine before, and it wants to know if we meant it."

Ines stood very still, one shutter half-latched, the lantern light trembling on the wet cobbles. Clause nine. *The door stays open.* The clause he had fought for hardest, argued bloodiest for, the one Halden's ghost had nearly broken and the gods themselves had finally sealed with their own consent—the promise that the covenant would never close, that any god, any soul, any stranger arriving from beyond the ash could come to the salt and be heard.

He looked at his apprentices. At Maretta with her cord already in her hands. At Oskar setting down the mortar, squaring his shoulders. At Sefa, waiting, translating heaven.

Outside, at the edge of the lantern light, the rain parted around something vast and patient, and Ines Oyelaran—last apprentice of the old guild, first teacher of the new—rolled his sleeve back down, and stepped forward to answer.
