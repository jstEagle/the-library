## 1. The Valley That Isn't

There are three things every child in Vell learns before they learn to read: how to tell foxglove from comfrey by the shape of its shadow at noon, how to be polite to geese, and how to want nothing.

I was slow at the third.

The morning I'm going to tell you about began the way all my mornings began, with Mistress Odile rapping her knuckles on the loft ladder and saying, in a voice that pretended it wasn't an order, "Astrid. The yarrow won't gather itself, and neither will you."

She was right about the yarrow. She was usually wrong about me, though she'd have argued the point until the cows came home, and in Vell the cows always came home, because there was nowhere else for them to go.

I climbed down into the kitchen, where the fire was already lit and the kettle already muttering, and where Odile stood at the table grinding something that smelled like a thunderstorm deciding whether to happen. She was small and sharp as a thornbush, with iron-gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils and a fishhook, and she had raised me since I was found — or left, or delivered; nobody ever said which — on her doorstep one wet spring night wrapped in a blanket embroidered with a name I wasn't allowed to ask about.

Joubert. But I didn't know that yet, not then. Then I only knew I was Astrid, apprentice hedge-witch of the valley of Vell, population too few to bother counting and too stubborn to leave.

"Yarrow," she said again, "and feverfew, and if you pass the Hendersons' field, tell Marta her cow is lying to her."

"Marta's cow is lying to her?"

"Cows know things," Odile said darkly, "and that one knows she's due to calve a week early and hasn't told anyone. Tell Marta to sleep in the barn tonight." She handed me the basket, the good basket, the one with the handle mended three times with copper wire because Odile said copper remembered kindness. "And take your cloak. It's going to spit."

It did spit, the way it always did, a thin gray drizzle that wasn't really rain so much as the sky clearing its throat. I pulled the hood up and went out into the lane, past the well, past the smithy where Bram was already hammering and pretending not to watch for me, past the shrine at the crossroads where somebody had left a saucer of milk for whatever came by — nothing ever came by, but the milk was always gone by morning, which the village agreed to find comforting rather than suspicious.

Vell is a valley the way a cupped hand is a valley. The ridges rise steep on all sides, forested dark with pines that never quite let you see over them, and the river Vell runs down the middle like a silver seam holding everything together. There's one road in and one road out, and both of them go through the ford, and both of them, everyone agrees, lead to more of the same: other valleys, other villages, other people who farm and quarrel and marry and mend. Nobody from Vell goes over the ridge. It isn't forbidden. It's simply not done, the way it isn't done to eat soup with a fork or argue with Odile about dosage.

I gathered yarrow along the riverbank, and feverfew from the stony ground above the mill, and I stopped at the Hendersons' as instructed, where Marta listened to my message about her cow with the particular expression of a woman who has lived in Vell her whole life and has learned that when the witch sends word, you thank her and you don't ask follow-up questions.

"She said the cow's *lying*?" Marta said.

"In so many words."

"Well." Marta looked at the cow, who looked back with an expression of magnificent innocence. "I'll sleep in the barn."

That was the thing about Odile's charms and warnings and weather-sense: they worked. Mostly. Sort of. Her feverfew tea would bring a fever down nine times out of ten, and her protection knots kept wolves off the flocks most winters, and her love charms were famously unreliable but nobody held that against her because, as Bram's mother put it, "some things shouldn't be helped along." But everything Odile made had a faintness to it, a washed-out quality, like a song heard through a wall. Her charms never quite held. Her salves healed a day slower than they should. Her luck-knots frayed at exactly the wrong moment. And when I asked her once — I was eleven, and eleven-year-olds ask — why her magic was like bread gone slightly stale, she'd looked at me for a long moment and said:

"Because we're far from the source, child. Magic is like water. It follows the lay of the land. And here—" she'd gestured vaguely at the ridges, at the whole cupped hand of the world, "—here the water doesn't reach."

I believed her, because she was Odile and because it explained the staleness. What I didn't understand until much later was that she hadn't answered my question at all. She'd just told me the weather report from inside the prison.

* * *

Afternoons were for mending charms, and that afternoon the queue outside the cottage was four deep: Widow Petch with a churn that had soured three batches running, young Tamlin Hale with a hangnail he was convinced was a curse, old Sorrel with her annual request for a charm against her son-in-law, and a farmer named Cobb whose problem was genuine — his best ewe wouldn't stop walking in circles, tight perfect circles, all day, wearing a groove in the hillside like she was trying to draw something.

Odile handled the first three with brisk efficiency and mild insults delivered so warmly that nobody minded. ("A curse? Tamlin, the only curse on you is your own hands. Wash them. Next.") But the ewe interested her. She went out to look at it, and I went with her carrying the basket, and we stood in Cobb's muddy field watching a sheep walk a circle with the grim determination of someone doing penance.

"Hm," said Odile.

"Hm?" I said, because apprenticing is mostly learning to say hm in the right tone.

"That's not sickness. That's *seeking*." She crouched, squinting at the ewe with one eye closed. "Animals do this when the ground under them is wrong. When something's been laid down here, or taken away." She pressed her palm flat to the earth and held it there, and I watched her face do the thing it did when she was listening to something I couldn't hear — a small tightening around the mouth, like tasting something almost-but-not-quite spoiled.

"Nothing," she said finally, standing, brushing mud off her skirts. "There's nothing under the ground. Which is itself strange, because the ground is *expecting* something. Like a table set for a guest who never comes." She frowned at the ridge line, north, where the pines stood darkest. "Like a lot of things in this valley."

She gave Cobb a knot to tie to the ewe's neck — "it won't fix anything, but it'll keep her comfortable while she waits" — and we walked home in the graying light, and I asked, casually, the way you ask dangerous questions when you're sixteen and think you're subtle:

"What would the ground be expecting?"

And Odile said, "Supper," and cooked it, and that was the end of that conversation, the way it was the end of most of our conversations about the edges of things.

* * *

Here is my confession, the one I've never said aloud to anyone in Vell, the one I'm setting down now because paper doesn't gossip and because if I've learned anything since, it's that secrets kept too long start keeping you:

I memorize maps.

Not maps of Vell — there aren't any, officially, though Odile has a hand-drawn one behind a loose stone in the hearth that marks every herb patch and badger sett within a day's walk. No. I memorize maps of places I will never see. I make them up, or rather I assemble them, out of fragments: the peddler who comes twice a year over the southern saddle (the only outsider who ever visits, and even he claims he's from "just over the way," though he never says which way) leaves newspapers sometimes, and catalogues, and once — my treasure, my crown jewel — a school atlas with the cover torn off, missing maybe forty pages, containing the coast of somewhere called Norland and the mountain ranges of somewhere called the Spine and, on the last surviving page, half a city drawn in fine engraved lines, all bridges and spires, labeled only *—holm*, the rest of the name lost to the tear.

I used to lie on my bed in the loft with that page and trace the bridges with one finger and build the missing half of the name out of syllables. Ash-holm. Ell-holm. Wren-holm. Each one a different city, each one realer than the last, each one full of streets I walked in my head while rain came down on the actual roof of the actual cottage in the actual valley where I would spend my actual life gathering yarrow for a woman who thought magic was stale water.

I knew it was foolish. I knew wanting was the one crop that didn't grow well in Vell soil. The valley taught contentment the way it taught everything else — gently, thoroughly, without ever once raising its voice. People here weren't sad. That was the strange, terrible thing. They were *fine*. They were warm and fed and busy and kind, and their lives had the shape of a well-told story with a satisfying end, and if any of them ever felt the walls of the cupped hand pressing close, they never mentioned it, possibly because none of them could see the walls at all.

But some nights I'd climb onto the roof — Odile pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of permission — and look at the ridges black against the stars, and feel something in my chest pull toward them like a tide. Not unhappiness. Longing's cousin, the one who shows up uninvited and stays too long. And I'd recite my maps to myself like prayers: the coast of Norland, the passes of the Spine, the bridges of —holm, and I'd fall asleep with my finger still curled as if around a pen, drawing roads on the slate of the sky.

Once, when I was fourteen, I asked the peddler — his name was Fitch, and he had a laugh like a cart on gravel — "What's past the northern ridge?"

Fitch went quiet in a way that didn't suit him. He looked at me, then at the ridge, then at his own boots, and said, "Past the northern ridge? Nothing you'd want, missy. Bad country. Gets in your head." And then he laughed his gravel laugh and sold me a ribbon and never answered another question about directions for as long as I knew him, which I thought at the time was just peddlers being peddlers.

Everyone in Vell talked about beyond-the-ridge the same way. Bad country. Gets in your head. Not worth the walk. And everyone smiled when they said it, a small fixed smile, and changed the subject to weather or calves or who was courting whom, and I was sixteen years old before I noticed that the smile never reached anyone's eyes, and that I wore it too.

* * *

That evening, after supper, Odile set me to re-inking the labels on her apothecary drawers, which was punishment disguised as chore or chore disguised as punishment, depending on what you'd done lately, and I'd done nothing, which meant it was just Tuesday. The ink smelled of oak galls and iron. Outside, the drizzle had settled in for the night, patient as taxes.

"Astrid," Odile said suddenly, from her chair by the fire, without looking up from her mending.

"Mm?"

"What did you do today? Besides the herbs."

I kept my eyes on the drawer labeled WORMWOOD — EXTERNAL ONLY. "Gathered. Delivered Marta's message. Watched a sheep walk in circles."

"Mm," said Odile, in my own tone, and I felt caught at something I couldn't name. She threaded her needle. "You were up on the roof again last night."

"I was checking the chimney."

"At moonset."

"The chimney keeps odd hours."

The needle paused. For a moment the only sound was the fire and the rain, and I felt the whole weight of the valley in the silence — the warm, low, cupped-hand silence of it, the silence that meant *stay, stay, why would you ever want anything else, stay* — and I braced for the lecture, the *wanting gets you nowhere, child, a small life well-lived is worth ten grand ones botched.*

What Odile actually said was: "The moon will be full thirteen times from midwinter. You might count them, if you're going to sit up counting anyway."

I looked up. She was still mending, face bent to the sock she was darning, expression unreadable, and when I tried to catch her eye she said, "Label the horehound next, it's fading," and that was all, and I spent the rest of the evening with my heart beating strangely, telling myself I'd imagined the weight in her voice.

Thirteen moons. I counted them after that. Of course I counted them. I counted them the way I counted everything — secretly, hungrily, mapping a pattern I didn't yet know was a countdown.

* * *

Two days later, near dusk, with the basket empty and the light going bronze along the river, I took the long way home along the ford — not because it was faster, but because the ford was the closest thing Vell had to a border, the place where the road came down from the southern saddle and crossed the water, and standing there I could pretend I was standing at a gate instead of a bend in a stream.

The water ran shallow and quick over the stones. On the far bank the road climbed away toward the saddle, vanishing into pine shadow, and I stood on my side doing what I always did: reciting. *Coast of Norland. Passes of the Spine. Bridges of —holm.* My private litany. My map of everywhere-but-here.

And that was when I heard the horse.

It came down the road at a broken gallop, lathered and staggering, and the man on it was worse than the horse — slumped forward, one hand knotted in the mane, the other pressed against his side where something dark spread between his fingers. He saw me across the water and his horse plunged into the ford, stumbling, and he slid off it into the shallows before I could move, and I was knee-deep and catching him before I'd decided to, his weight sudden and terrible, blood soaking cold through my sleeve.

He was gray-faced, fortyish, dressed like no one from Vell dressed — travel-worn leathers, a courier's satchel strap across his chest — and his lips were moving. I leaned close.

"The thirteenth turn," he whispered. His eyes were doing something I'd never seen eyes do: looking at me and past me at once, as if I were a door. "It's coming. She said — she said you'd know. She said the blood remembers even when the girl doesn't."

"Who said? Sir — you're hurt, I need to get you to—"

His hand found mine and pressed something into it, hard, urgent: a leather case, long as my forearm, cracked with age and travel, sealed with a drop of red wax stamped with a mark I didn't recognize — a circle broken by a single line, like a door ajar.

"Joubert," the dying man said.

The name went through me like cold water through cloth. I had never heard it. I had never been allowed to hear it. And yet somewhere under my ribs, something turned over — the ground expecting a guest, the table long set — and recognized it.

"Keep it," he breathed. Blood at the corner of his mouth. His grip failing. "Don't let her burn it. Don't let *her*—"

And the valley of Vell, which had never in my whole life let anything in, let him die at my feet in the shallows of the ford, with the last light on the water and the wax seal warm against my palm, and somewhere up on the ridge, I would swear to my dying day, a bird started singing that had no business singing at dusk.
