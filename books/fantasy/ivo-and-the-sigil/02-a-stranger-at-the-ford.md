## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

There were three rules in Vell that everyone agreed on without ever having been told them: don't climb past the ridge, don't count the moon-turns aloud, and don't ask Mistress Odile about the years before she came to the valley.

Astrid Joubert had broken the third rule exactly once, at age eleven, and had been rewarded with a look so cold that her eyebrows had frosted for a week. She'd learned to keep her questions where Odile kept her poisons: labeled, stoppered, and out of reach of children.

The first two rules were easier. The ridge was a long gray spine of stone stitched with pine, and beyond it nothing—no smoke, no roads, no promise. You could stand on it on a clear day and feel the world end politely, like a host who has decided you've stayed quite long enough. And counting moons was simply something nobody did. The valley had seasons the way a cat has moods: you noticed them when they arrived and didn't trouble yourself tallying.

So Astrid gathered herbs. That was the shape of her life, most days, and mostly she didn't mind.

*Mostly.*

On the morning the stranger came up the river road, she was ankle-deep in dew and argument, crouched over a patch of feverfew that had decided, for reasons known only to itself, to grow in the exact shape of an insult.

"You're doing this on purpose," she told it.

The feverfew declined to comment. It never did. That was the thing about charms and herbs in Vell—everything worked *almost*. Odile's mending charms held a hem for a month instead of a year. Her weather-sense predicted rain accurately about half the time, which was better than guessing but worse than just looking at the sky, as Tammas the cooper pointed out every single autumn. Magic in Vell was like trying to whistle through a wall. Something always got through—a note, a ghost of a note—but the tune died somewhere around the ridge.

Odile said it was the altitude. Astrid had privately suspected, since roughly age nine, that this was nonsense. Altitude did not explain why Odile's scrying bowl showed fog no matter what she asked it, or why the letters Odile wrote to cousins who never wrote back were always carried out by the same taciturn tinker who never seemed to bring replies.

She cut the feverfew anyway—insult-shaped or not, it still brought fevers down—and bundled it into her basket with the yarrow and the last of the elderflowers. The morning was doing its best impression of perfection. Mist off the river Vell in long white ribbons; larks going show-offy overhead; the ford ahead glinting like a coin someone had dropped in the grass.

That was when she saw the horse.

It stood in the middle of the ford, swaying, its flanks dark with sweat and something darker than sweat. No rider. Its reins hung snapped, trailing in the water like the legs of a drowned insect.

Astrid stopped. In Vell, a riderless horse meant one of three things: a thrown rider, a dead rider, or a rider who very much wished to be unfindable. All three were interesting. None of them were good.

"Hello?" she called, feeling immediately foolish. Horses rarely answered.

The horse turned its head toward her with an expression of profound equine grievance and stumbled out of the water onto the near bank. And there, sprawled in the shallows where the horse had been standing over him—shielding him, she realized later, the way you shield a candle from wind—was a man.

He was dressed all wrong for Vell. His coat was gray wool, fine-woven, cut close in a style she'd only seen in the woodcuts of Odile's one book about Elsewhere, and his boots had buckles, three of them per boot, which struck her as either fashionable or paranoid. There was blood on him. A great deal of blood, spread from his left side down across the stones beneath him in a fan that the current was patiently trying to erase.

"Oh," Astrid said, uselessly, and then dropped her basket and ran.

She reached him and knelt in the shallows, which soaked her to the knee and which she would think about later, standing in Odile's kitchen, wringing out her skirts, and decide had been entirely worth it. He was maybe forty, hollow-cheeked, with a traveler's tan and a courier's hard hands—she knew calluses like that from the tinker's palms, the kind you earned carrying other people's business across distances. His eyes were open. They found her face and focused on it with the terrible concentration of a man spending his last coins.

"Water," he rasped. "No—" His hand shot up, gripping her wrist with shocking strength. "No. Listen."

"I can get help," Astrid said. "Mistress Odile knows physick, there's a poultice—"

"*Listen.*" The word cost him. She watched it cost him, watched his whole chest hitch around it. "You live here. In the valley." Not a question. He looked at her face as if checking it against something. "How old are you?"

"Nineteen. Nearly."

Something crossed his expression—relief and grief sharing a bench, elbowing each other. "Nearly nineteen," he repeated. "Good. Good, there's time. There's—" He coughed then, wet and horrible, and pressed his lips together until the cough passed. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone urgent and low, as though the mist itself might be eavesdropping. "How many turns since the last full moon? Count them for me. How many?"

Astrid blinked. Nobody counted moons in Vell. But she had, secretly, for years—her one small rebellion, scratched in charcoal on the underside of her bed board, a tidy column of little crescents. She kept the count the way some people kept a diary: not because anything ever happened, but because the counting felt like proof she existed.

"Four," she said. "Four turns since full."

He exhaled. "Eight more. Eight, and then the thirteenth—" He broke off, jaw working. "You don't know what I'm saying."

"Not even slightly," Astrid admitted.

"No. No, you wouldn't. They made sure of that." His grip on her wrist tightened. His fingers were freezing, even though the morning was mild. "Girl. What's your name?"

"Astrid."

"Just Astrid?"

She hesitated. It was such a small hesitation, a hair's width wide, but he caught it the way a fisherman catches a nibble.

"And the rest?" he said. "The rest of your name. Say it."

"I—" She almost said *Joubert*, and then, absurdly, almost didn't, because saying it felt suddenly like opening a door she'd been told was painted on the wall. "Joubert," she said. "Astrid Joubert."

The sound that came out of the man was not a word. It was closer to a laugh and a sob being forced through the same keyhole. His head tipped back against the stones, and for one awful moment she thought she'd killed him by naming herself, which would have been the strangest sentence anyone in Vell had ever had to explain to Odile.

"The Joubert line," he whispered at the sky. "I found her. Maren, you impossible woman, I actually found her." Then his eyes rolled back down to Astrid, and they were fierce and wet and burning. "Your grandmother. Your grandmother sent me."

"I don't have a grandmother," Astrid said. It came out automatically, flat, the way you recite a rule. *Don't climb past the ridge. Don't count the moons aloud.* Nobody in Vell had grandparents. Or rather—everyone had them once, obviously, people did not condense out of mist—but the word belonged to the category of things nobody discussed, along with the ridge and the moons and Odile's youth. Families in Vell were small and forward-facing, like people riding in a cart who are only allowed to look at the road.

"You do," the man said. "You have one, and she is the reason you're alive, and the reason this valley is asleep, and—" Another cough took him. Blood came with it this time, bright on his teeth, and Astrid's stomach dropped through the riverbed.

"Please," she said, and heard how young her voice sounded. "Let me get Odile. She can—"

"There's no time for poultices." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "And your mistress—" Something flickered over his face at Odile's name. Recognition? Fear? It was gone too fast to read, smoothed away by a courier's discipline. "Never mind her. Listen to me now. Can you listen?"

Astrid nodded.

"With your *head*, girl, not just your ears. Because what I'm about to tell you is going to sound like a madman's raving, and you'll want to run home and tell yourself it was a dream. Don't. Promise me you won't."

"That's a lot to promise a bleeding stranger at a ford," Astrid said, because terror had always come out of her sideways, as cheek. Her mother had called it a flaw. Odile called it *insolence* and charged her extra chores for it.

But the man smiled. It cracked his pale face open like sun through cloud. "Oh, Maren," he murmured, to someone who wasn't there. "She's got your tongue entirely."

Then he fumbled at his coat with his right hand—his left had gone slack—and worked free a leather case, long and narrow, the kind tinkers used for carrying documents too precious to fold. It was scarred and travel-darkened, tied shut with a cord, and stamped into the leather was a mark: a circle, and inside the circle thirteen lines radiating from the center like the spokes of a wheel—or like rays drawn around a moon.

Astrid knew that mark.

Not from anywhere. That was the maddening thing. She knew it the way you know a face in a dream—deeply, uselessly, with certainty and no source. Her skin prickled from wrists to shoulders.

"Take it," the man said, pressing the case into her hands. His fingers shook. "Guard it. It cannot be given to anyone else—it has to be *taken freely*, that's part of the working, so I'm asking, and you have to answer. Will you take it?"

"This is madness," Astrid said faintly.

"It is absolutely madness," he agreed warmly. "The best kind. The kind that's true. Will you take it, Astrid Joubert?"

The name again. It rang through her like a bell through a house she'd thought she knew room by room, revealing a corridor behind the pantry. She looked down at the case in her hands. It was warm. Not sun-warm—there was hardly any sun yet—but warm like a sleeping animal, like something with a heartbeat under the leather.

"Yes," she heard herself say. "Yes, I take it."

"Then it's done, and I can rest." His whole body loosened, and she caught him before his skull could knock the stones. "Eight turns," he breathed. His words were slowing, spaces opening between them like gaps between fence posts. "When the thirteenth turn comes... the seal closes forever. Doors lock. All of them. Every valley like yours, every—" A shudder. "Vell was built to be a prison, child. Built by twelve. And the thirteenth..."

"The thirteenth what?" Astrid demanded. "Twelve what? Who built what?"

But he was looking past her now, upriver, at the mist, at something she couldn't see. "Tell your grandmother's ghost I kept my rounds," he said. "Every letter delivered. Even the last one." His hand rose—she caught it, cradled it, his knuckles icy—and his eyes dragged themselves back to her face with the very last of him.

"Joubert," he whispered.

And said nothing else. Ever again.

* * *

Astrid sat in the shallows holding a dead man's hand for a long time.

The river went on being a river. The larks went on showing off. The horse drank placidly at the bank, apparently reconciled to the day. Somewhere behind her her basket lay overturned, feverfew scattered in the grass, and none of it mattered, none of it had ever mattered, because a stranger had ridden out of the nowhere beyond the ridge—the ridge that was the edge of the entire universe, as far as any life she'd ever been permitted—and had died at her feet saying her family name like a password.

*A prison.*

She looked down at the map case in her lap. Up close she could see the stamp better: not thirteen spokes after all. Thirteen marks arranged in a ring, twelve of them identical—small crescent-shapes, moons—and the thirteenth different, set apart at the top of the circle. She tilted it toward the strengthening light. The thirteenth mark was a door. Tiny, stylized, unmistakable once you saw it: a little archway with a keyhole.

Her heart was doing something complicated and arrhythmic.

*Don't ask questions,* Vell said, in the collective voice of every villager who had ever changed the subject. *Be useful. Be small. The hedges need trimming and Mistress Odile needs her yarrow and contentment is a thing you practice, not a thing you find.*

The map case sat warm against her palms and said, in the silent eloquent way of objects that have traveled very far to ruin your life:

*Open me.*

"Not yet," Astrid said aloud, and was mildly horrified to discover she was negotiating with luggage now. "If I open it here, anyone could see, and if it's what you say it is—if any of that was real—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "Eight turns," she said instead, testing it. "The thirteenth turn."

The number settled into her chest and lay there, heavy as a swallowed stone. Four since full. Eight remaining. She thought of the charcoal crescents scratched under her bed board, four years of pointless tallies, and felt her ears go hot. Had she been counting all along because some buried corner of her had been waiting for the numbers to mean something?

She laid the man's hand down in the shallows, gently, and stood. Her knees protested. Her whole body protested. She discovered she was shaking, which was embarrassing, and crying a little, which was worse, and she let herself do both while wringing out her skirts, because there was nobody watching except a horse, and horses were famously discreet.

Then practicality arrived, wearing Odile's face in her mind's eye, arms crossed: *What do we do with a body, Astrid? We fetch help. We fetch Tammas, we fetch the miller's sons, we fetch your mistress, and we say a traveler fell from his horse, and we say nothing else, and we certainly do not mention—*

The map case pulsed warm against her ribs, where she'd already tucked it inside her coat without deciding to.

*We certainly do not mention,* Astrid finished silently, *that he knew my name.*

Because that was the splinter working deepest, wasn't it? Not the dying. Not the blood. Not even the mad talk of prisons and seals and doors. It was the name. *Joubert.* A name she'd carried for nineteen years like a stone in her shoe, a name Odile went quiet around, a name no one in Vell shared—old Widow Marsh was a Kettleworth, the millers were Brandts, Tammas was a Cooper twice over, but Astrid was the only Joubert, an orphan-shaped hole in the valley's ledger, and whenever she'd asked why, the frost had come out and the eyebrows had frosted and the subject had died of cold.

A dying stranger had taken one look at her and read the name off her face like writing on a page.

Which meant the name was written somewhere. Which meant there was a somewhere. Which meant—

"Right," Astrid said out loud, because saying things out loud made them realer, and realer was terrifying, and terrifying was, she was beginning to suspect, the flavor of her entire future. She scooped up her basket, righted it, rescued the feverfew. She gave the horse a long considering look; the horse gave her one back, then wandered off toward the village at a walk, as if to announce the news itself. Fine. Let it gossip. Horses couldn't be interrogated.

She turned toward home. Toward Odile, and breakfast, and the comfortable lie of another ordinary day.

She got perhaps twenty paces before her feet stopped agreeing with the plan.

Because here was the thing she couldn't unknot: the courier had recognized *Odile's* name. Or reacted to it—flinched, smoothed it away. And Odile had a book about Elsewhere and letters that never got answers and a scrying bowl that showed fog, and she had raised Astrid with love wrapped around rules the way briars wrap a tower, and Astrid had never once—not truly once—asked herself the obvious question, the question sitting there all these years plain as a toad in a teacup:

*Why does a hedge-witch in a valley that isn't on any map need a scrying bowl that doesn't work?*

Unless it wasn't broken.

Unless it was *watching*.

The river murmured behind her. The map case beat its slow warm pulse against her heart, patient as an egg.

Astrid Joubert stood in the middle of the path with her basket of herbs and her dead man's secret, nineteen years old and nearly, and understood that the valley she'd memorized maps to escape had just, in a single morning, become a place she needed to survive.

She walked home to ask her mistress some very careful questions.

Behind her, in the shallows of the ford, the water closed over the last of the blood, and the mist began, very quietly, to lift.
