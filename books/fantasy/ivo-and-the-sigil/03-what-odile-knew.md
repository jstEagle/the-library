## 3. What Odile Knew

Astrid had hidden things from Mistress Odile before. A stolen honeycomb at age nine. A romance novel of frankly scandalous quality at fourteen, which she'd kept sewn into the lining of her winter coat for two years until the coat was given away and she mourned the book more than the coat. A broken charm or two, mended badly and blamed on damp weather.

But she had never hidden anything that weighed this much.

The map case sat under her mattress, which was the first place anyone would look and therefore, in Astrid's considered opinion, the best place to put it. She had spent a sleepless hour debating alternatives: the flour bin (mice), the herb loft (Odile's territory, sacred and surveilled), the well (damp, and also drowning her inheritance seemed premature). Under the mattress it stayed, and all through the next morning's chores she felt it there like a second heartbeat, a small leather drum against the small of her back every time she bent to weed the feverfew.

"You're weeding the same plant twice," Odile observed from the doorway. She stood with her arms folded inside her shawl, watching Astrid with the mild, unblinking attention she gave to everything—simmering tinctures, misbehaving goats, Astrid herself. "Either you love it very much or you're not listening to me."

"I'm listening," Astrid said. "I'm just also weeding."

"Mm." Odile came down the path, skirts whispering over the dew, and crouched beside her to inspect the feverfew with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. Her hands were brown and knuckly and sure. They had bandaged Astrid's knees, stirred every soup Astrid had ever eaten, and once—Astrid thought of it now with an odd cold feeling in her stomach—slapped a map out of young Astrid's hands when she'd found one tucked inside a peddler's almanac. *We don't keep pictures of places we can't go,* Odile had said, and burned it in the kitchen fire while Astrid watched the little ink mountains curl and vanish.

That had been eleven years ago. Astrid had assumed it was a quirk. Mistresses were allowed quirks; they earned them the way old dogs earned their gray muzzles.

Now she watched Odile's hands and thought: *she knew. She always knew.*

"Joubert," Astrid said.

The word dropped between them like a stone into still water. Odile went on inspecting the feverfew for exactly three seconds too long, and then she rose, slowly, the way she rose when her hip was bothering her, except Astrid knew her hip wasn't bothering her because it never bothered her before the first frost.

"Where did you hear that name?" Odile asked. Her voice was perfectly level. That was the frightening part. Odile's voice went sharp when she was annoyed and warm when she was fond and wobbly when she laughed, but it had never once gone *level*, and now it was level as a frozen pond.

"A man at the ford," Astrid said. "Yesterday. He was hurt."

"What man."

"I don't know his name. He was carrying—" She stopped herself, barely, teeth clamped around the word *map* like a cork in a bottle. "He was hurt, and he said my name. He said Joubert like he expected me to know what it meant. And then he died."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the lane, Widow Pram's rooster made its idiot announcement, and neither of them so much as glanced toward it, which showed how serious things were, because that rooster could interrupt a funeral and get away with it.

"Come inside," Odile said.

* * *

The cottage smelled as it always smelled: dried lavender, beeswax, the iron tang of the kettle. Odile poured tea without asking whether Astrid wanted any, which was how tea worked in Vell—you didn't want it, you received it—and set the cup in front of Astrid with a small deliberate click.

"You will not leave the valley," Odile said.

Astrid blinked. "I wasn't going to—"

"You were going to ask. I saw you deciding whether to ask. So I am answering the question you haven't asked yet." Odile sat across from her, hands wrapped around her own cup, and for a moment she looked older than Astrid had ever seen her—not frail, nothing so simple as frail, but *tired* in some deep structural way, like a house whose beams had been bearing weight for longer than houses should. "Whatever that man told you, whatever he gave you—and don't look at me like that, girl, I've raised you since you were six, I know what your face does when it's lying—it stays here. You stay here. The valley keeps us, and we keep the valley, and that is the whole of the arrangement."

"The valley *keeps* us?" Astrid repeated. "Like a jar keeps pickles?"

"If you like."

"That's not comforting, that's brine."

Odile almost smiled. Almost. It flickered at the corner of her mouth and died there, and Astrid realized with a lurch that she had never seen Odile frightened before, and that this—this careful, level, teacup-holding stillness—was what Odile frightened looked like.

"There are things about this valley," Odile began, "that I have not told you because telling you would serve no purpose except to make you restless, and restlessness in Vell is like a splinter in a glove. You can work around it for years. But eventually you'll reach for something without thinking, and it'll go deep, and then there'll be blood."

"So tell me anyway. I'd rather bleed informed."

"Astrid—"

"He said my family name." Astrid heard her own voice rise and didn't care. "You've never let me ask about them. Not my mother, not my father, not where I came from before the flood took the low road and you took me in. Every child in this valley has a grandmother's story and a cousin in the next hamlet and a grave to visit, and I have *you*, and I love you, but loving you has been like living in a house with one window. And yesterday a dying man pressed something into my hands and said *Joubert* like it was a key, and you've gone white as the flour sack, so either you tell me what that name means or I start guessing, and I promise you my guesses will be worse than the truth."

It was the longest speech Astrid had delivered in years, possibly ever. She was slightly out of breath at the end of it, and slightly astonished at herself, and entirely unrepentant.

Odile looked at her for a long moment. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the ordinary morning of Vell went on being ordinary—the rooster, the cart wheels, someone calling a child home—and Astrid thought, with sudden vertigo, *how long has it been going on being ordinary on purpose?*

"Eat your breakfast," Odile said finally, quietly. "We'll speak tonight."

Which was not an answer. But it was a door left ajar, and Astrid had learned to recognize those.

* * *

She did not eat her breakfast. She carried her bread and cheese out to the goat pasture and fed half of it to the least deserving goat, a yellow-eyed villain named Turnip who headbutted her affectionately in exchange, and she spent the afternoon doing her rounds badly and thinking.

The courier's journal was in the map case too. She hadn't dared read more than the first page—the words *twelve founders* had made her hands shake so badly she'd had to put it down—but she turned the case's contents over in her mind like stones in a pocket. The sigil stamped in wax on the flap. The same sigil, she was nearly certain, though she'd only glimpsed it, on the letters she'd once seen in Odile's writing desk, back when she was small enough that rummaging was forgivable. Red wax, stamped with a mark like a crescent moon cradling a star.

Why would Odile have letters with the dead man's seal?

She came home at dusk. The cottage windows glowed amber, smoke curled from the chimney, and everything looked exactly as it had looked every evening of Astrid's life, and for the first time the ordinariness itself seemed suspicious, like a stage set viewed from behind.

Odile wasn't in the kitchen.

Astrid stood in the doorway, dripping rain, and listened. No humming. No rattle of the soup pot. Instead, from the direction of the washhouse—a low stone building apart from the cottage, where the dye vats lived and where Odile went to be alone with tasks she called *unpleasant but necessary*—a flicker of orange light danced in the gaps of the shutters.

Washhouse fires were banked by sundown. Always. Odile had rules the way other people had bones.

Astrid crossed the yard on silent feet, a skill honed over years of stealing honeycomb, and put her eye to the gap in the shutters.

Odile stood at the great dyeing cauldron, which was not full of dye. It was full of burning paper. Letters—dozens of them, bundles of them, tied and sealed and feeding the flames one after another as Odile fed them with the mechanical patience of a woman burying a body in installments. Firelight climbed her face. Her lips were moving. Praying, maybe. Or counting.

And on each letter, before the flames took it, the wax seal flashed red-gold in the firelight: a crescent moon cradling a star.

Astrid's hand went to her chest, to the flat place under her coat where the map case hung on its cord, and she felt the leather there, faintly, impossibly, *warm*.

One letter resisted the fire. Odile pulled it back from the flames, frowning, and held it up—and even through the shutter gap, even across the yard of distance, Astrid saw the seal clearly, whole and unburned, and saw Odile's thumb press against it as if testing whether it might still open.

Then Odile's head came up. Not toward the shutter—toward the ridge, the dark line of it against the stars—and she stood motionless for so long that Astrid's eye watered at the gap. When she moved again, she snuffed the fire with one practiced motion, hid the surviving letter somewhere in her shawl, and stepped out into the night.

Astrid flattened herself against the woodpile, heart hammering, and watched her mistress pause in the middle of the yard, face tilted up toward the thirteenth moon's thin new sliver.

"We see you," Odile said softly, to no one Astrid could see. "Not yet. Not yet."

And she went inside, and bolted the door, and left Astrid standing in the rain with a warming map against her heart and the sudden, sickening understanding that the woman who had raised her had known everything—all along, all of it—and had chosen, every single day, to let Astrid live inside the lie like a pickle in a jar.

The map pulsed once against her ribs, patient as a second heartbeat.

Astrid looked at the ridge, black against the stars, and for the first time in her life she wondered what was on the other side of it—and what, exactly, Odile was so afraid would come looking if anyone ever found out.
