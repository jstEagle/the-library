## 4. Thirteen Moons

Astrid did not sleep.

She had told Odile she was going to bed early — a lie so thin it barely qualified as one — and now she lay on her narrow cot with the map case under her blanket like a feverish child, listening to the house settle around her. The rafters ticked. Somewhere below, Odile's kettle muttered itself to sleep on the banked fire. Outside, the moon hung over Vell in its ordinary way, round and silver and utterly unremarkable, and Astrid found herself staring at it as though it might wink.

*The thirteenth turn,* the dying man had said. *Before the thirteenth turn.*

She didn't know what that meant. She knew only that he had known her name — not Astrid, but *Joubert*, the family name no one in the valley had ever let her ask about — and that when he said it, his eyes had gone soft with relief, the way a tired traveler's go at the sight of a lit window.

The map case was made of oiled leather gone dark with age, stitched with thread that had once been gold. It was warm. Not sun-warm, not body-warm — warm from inside, faintly, patiently, like a stone that remembered a hearth. Every time she touched it, the warmth seemed to lean into her palm.

"You're a strange thing," she whispered to it.

The case said nothing, being a case, but Astrid had talked to unresponsive objects her whole life — charms that wouldn't hold, kettles that wouldn't boil, Mistress Odile's cat, who responded only to flattery — and she felt reasonably confident this was the beginning of a working relationship.

She waited until the moon slid behind the ridge and the house went truly dark. Then she sat up, lit the stub of candle she kept for reading after hours (a crime for which Odile docked her dessert, and worth every forfeited plum), and opened the case.

Inside lay two things.

The first was the map: a sheet of vellum folded into eighths, covered in ink so fine it looked spun rather than drawn. It showed a country she half-recognized — mountains in the shape of a sleeping animal, a river that forked like a wishbone — and roads that went places roads in Vell simply did not go. At intervals along those roads were small marks, twelve of them, each shaped like a tiny door. And at the center of the map, where a capital or a cathedral might sit, was a sigil: a circle of thirteen interlocking lines, twelve of them closed and complete, the thirteenth trailing off into nothing, unfinished, like a sentence someone had stopped writing mid-word.

The second thing was a journal. Small, water-swollen at one corner, its leather cover scarred as if something had tried very hard to eat it and given up.

Astrid turned to the first page.

---

*They tell me I'm the last courier who will make this run, which is either an honor or an obituary, and knowing my luck it's both.*

*My name is Perrin Duval, and I have carried letters between the sealed places for eleven years. You will not have heard of most of them. That is rather the point of sealed places.*

*I am writing this journal because my master told me: "Whatever happens, write it down. Paper remembers what people are forbidden to." He died at Corvel. The paper outlived him, so perhaps he was right.*

*Here is what I know, set down plainly, so that whoever finds this — and someone will find it, because I intend to be extremely difficult to kill — will understand what they are holding.*

*Once there were many valleys like yours. Places where magic pooled too deep, the way water pools in a cellar. The founders of each valley looked at that pooling and were afraid — some of them rightly, some of them merely loudly — and so they built seals. Doors locked from the inside. Twelve founders for each valley, each founder binding one turn of the moon, so that the seal renewed itself all year long, turn by turn, like a lock with twelve tumblers.*

*Twelve moons. Twelve locks. A perfect year.*

*But here is the part they do not put in the songs: every seal needs a flaw. A keyhole. A way back in, for emergencies, for mercy, for regret. The founders were proud women and stubborn men, but they were not fools, and even fools know better than to weld a door shut while standing behind it.*

*So each valley kept a thirteenth key. Not an object. Objects can be stolen. The thirteenth key was hidden in the safest vault any of them could imagine:*

*A bloodline.*

---

Astrid stopped reading.

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were waiting. Downstairs, a floorboard creaked — Odile turning over in her bed, nothing more — and Astrid realized she had been holding her breath long enough to feel dizzy.

*A bloodline.* She looked at the word. Then at her own hands, ordinary hands, herb-cracked and ink-stained, hands that mended charms that never quite worked. Hands that a dying man had gripped in the shallows of the ford while whispering a name she'd never been permitted to ask about.

"No," she said aloud, to the journal, to the room, to nobody. "No, that's — that's a story. That's the kind of story Odile reads me to put me to sleep."

But her voice shook, and the map case against her knee pulsed its small patient warmth, and neither of them believed her.

She read on.

---

*You'll want to know why you, specifically. I wondered the same about myself once, before I learned that couriers are chosen for their legs, not their destinies. But keys are different. If you are reading this, then either you are a scholar with excellent taste in stolen documents, or the key has already begun to notice you.*

*It notices through warmth. Through dreams of doors. Through a restlessness you cannot explain and everyone around you insists you should be ashamed of.*

*(If you are the scholar: put this down. Some letters are addressed.*

*If you are the restless one: keep reading.)*

---

Astrid laughed — a single startled bark of a laugh, immediately smothered in her own sleeve. Whoever Perrin Duval had been, dead at the ford with riverwater in his lungs, he had clearly been the sort of person who wrote instructions to his own future readers and expected them to obey.

She had dreamed of doors. Three nights running, before the courier ever came. Doors standing open in hillsides, doors at the ends of lanes she'd walked a thousand times, doors that swung wide onto light she couldn't describe upon waking. She had mentioned it to Odile, once, casually, over breakfast — "I had such an odd dream" — and Odile had dropped a spoon.

She hadn't thought much of it at the time. Odile dropped spoons regularly; her mistress's hands were seventy years old and had opinions about mornings.

Now she thought about it quite a lot.

---

*The twelfth founder of your valley — your valley, the one the maps call Vell when they bother to call it anything — was named Ysolt Joubert.*

*I never met her. She was dust before my grandmother was a rumor. But I have carried her letters, or the letters written in her hand, and I will tell you what I know of her: she argued loudest of the twelve against the sealing, and lost, and then did the most dangerous thing a loser can do.*

*She agreed anyway.*

*They needed her consent to bind the final tumbler. So she gave it — and then she hid the thirteenth key where the other eleven could never reach it without her permission. She fed it down through her own blood, generation by generation, thinned and diluted and sleeping, until the day it would be needed.*

*That day has a date. The seal is not eternal; nothing is. It holds for ninety-nine years, and then the thirteenth turn comes — one extra turning of the moon, unbound by any founder, when the whole lock hangs open whether anyone wishes it or not. On that night, the key wakes fully. If the heir stands at the door, the valley opens. If the heir does not come — if the line fails, or hides, or is never told — the seal closes again for another ninety-nine years, harder than before, and everything outside goes on forgetting Vell exists.*

*Ninety-nine years ends this winter. Before the thirteenth turn of this moon-cycle, the old men who track such things say. Which means soon. Which means I am carrying this map to the heir, and I am probably going to die doing it, and I find I don't mind as much as I expected.*

*Better a letter delivered late than never.*

---

Astrid set the journal down on her knees.

Her heart was doing something complicated — hammering, yes, but underneath the hammering a stranger sensation, one she had no shelf in her mind to store it on. It took her a moment to recognize it, because she had spent her whole life being taught to fold it small and call it ingratitude.

Hope. Enormous, terrifying, room-filling hope.

*Ysolt Joubert.* Her grandmother's mother's mother, or somewhere along that line — a woman who had argued against the walls and lost and then smuggled the way out through her own veins. A woman who had looked at a prison and quietly, patiently, planted a door in it.

And Astrid was the door.

The thought arrived and rearranged the entire furniture of her life. The charms that never quite worked — because magic couldn't follow past the ridge, yes, but also because something in her *interfered*, the way a magnet interferes with a compass. The way Odile watched her sometimes, that long unreadable look she'd always taken for disappointment. The rule, unspoken but iron, that Jouberts asked no questions about Jouberts.

She wasn't invisible because she was unremarkable.

She was invisible because she was *hidden*.

"I'm going to be sick," Astrid informed the candle, and then, discovering she wasn't, "or possibly run away tonight."

She compromised by reading more.

---

*A few practical notes, since destiny rarely includes instructions:*

*The map shows twelve waypoints, one per founder, marking the road to the door beneath the mountain. Each waypoint exacts a toll. Not coin — the founders had enough of that. A toll of truth. You will know it when it asks.*

*There is a Warden. The founders made it out of their own guilt, which should tell you everything about how pleasant it is. It guards the seal. It will offer you bargains. Every bargain it offers will be reasonable. This is how you will know it for what it is.*

*Do not travel alone if you can help it. Sealed places produce exiles, and exiles know things.*

*And — this is the last thing, and I would say it kindly if I could, but I'm bleeding rather a lot at the moment, so I'll say it honestly instead:*

*The key can break the seal. That is the obvious use, and breaking sounds like freedom, and freedom sounds like everything you've ever wanted.*

*But Ysolt Joubert left a note in the archive at the drowned library — the one off the salt coast, you'll find it, follow the gulls that fly wrong — and in that note she wrote: "Do not break what can be amended." I don't know what she meant. I hope you find out.*

*Good luck. Mind the tolls. Trust the ones who gave something up; they're the only ones who understand what a door is for.*

*— P.D., last courier of the outer rounds*

---

* * *

Astrid did not remember deciding to go downstairs.

One moment she was sitting on her cot with the journal in her lap; the next she was standing in the kitchen doorway in her nightgown, bare feet cold on the flagstones, watching Mistress Odile feed papers into the fireplace one by one.

Her mistress was still in her day dress, which meant she hadn't slept either. The firelight caught her face, and Astrid saw — for the first time in fourteen years of living under her roof — that Odile looked *old*. Not comfortably old, the way a well-worn chair is old, but worn-through old, old like a coat patched past saving.

On the table lay a stack of letters. Even from the doorway, Astrid could see the wax seals: a circle of thirteen interlocking lines, twelve closed, one trailing off unfinished.

The same sigil as the map.

"Odile," Astrid said.

Her mistress did not startle. That was somehow worse. Odile simply went still, the way animals go still, the way Astrid herself had gone still at the ford — and then she placed one more letter on the coals and watched it curl.

"How much did you read?" Odile asked. Her voice was level. Her hands were not.

"The journal," Astrid said. "Some of the map. Enough."

"Then you know you must give it to me."

"Do I?"

"Astrid." Odile turned at last, and her eyes in the firelight were wet and furious and afraid, all three at once, which should not have been possible and yet was. "Child. Sweet girl. I have kept you safe for fourteen years by keeping you *small*. Do you understand? Small enough that nothing out there would ever look twice at you. And in one evening you've become the largest thing in this valley."

"He said my name." Astrid's voice cracked on it. "He died holding my hands and he said *Joubert*, and you've never once let me ask — "

"There is nothing to ask."

"There's a *whole journal* of things to ask!"

For a moment neither of them moved. The fire ate another letter. Outside, far off, an owl called across the fields — or something that sounded like an owl, if owls could count.

Odile crossed the kitchen and took Astrid's face in her two old hands, gently, the way she did when Astrid was sick, and said, very quietly:

"Ninety-nine years ago, twelve people were so frightened of what magic does when it runs free that they walled up an entire world. And they were *right* to be frightened, Astrid. Whatever that journal told you about doors and inheritances and glorious thirteenth turns — the last time that door opened, it did not open outward. It opened *inward*. And a generation of Vell walked through it and did not come back."

The fire popped. Astrid's mouth had gone dry.

"So you burn the letters," she managed. "And hide the maps. And raise the key in your kitchen and teach her to mend charms that don't work."

"I teach her to *survive*," Odile said. "It is the only spell I ever mastered."

Astrid stepped back, out of her mistress's hands. The map case hung warm against her ribs where she'd tucked it inside her nightgown without quite deciding to, and she understood suddenly that some part of her had been packing for years.

"The thirteenth turn is coming," she said. "With or without me. Isn't it?"

Odile didn't answer. She looked at the fire, at the burning letters, at the unfinished sigil glowing briefly on the wax before the flame took it — and her silence was the loudest answer Astrid had ever heard.

"Then I'd rather meet it standing," Astrid said, and went upstairs to pack.

Behind her, in the kitchen, Odile knelt alone by the hearth — and did not reach for the last letter on the stack.
