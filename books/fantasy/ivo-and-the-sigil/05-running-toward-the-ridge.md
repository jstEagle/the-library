## 5. Running Toward the Ridge

Astrid had always imagined she would leave Vell with a speech. Something dignified, delivered at the gate of Odile's cottage while her mistress stood wringing her hands and begging her to stay, and Astrid would explain—calmly, reasonably—that a person could not live her whole life inside a sentence someone else had written.

In practice, she left by climbing out the washhouse window with her boots in one hand and half a loaf of yesterday's bread stuffed down her shirt.

The window was not locked. Nothing in Vell was ever locked, because nothing in Vell ever went anywhere. Astrid thought about that as she dropped into the herb garden, wincing when her heel found the chive bed. Odile would know by morning. Odile would count the missing cloak, the missing bread, the missing apprentice, and she would arrive at the sum without any arithmetic at all. But knowing was not the same as stopping, and Astrid was beginning to suspect that Odile's whole life had been built on that particular confusion.

She put her boots on behind the beehives, because bees were light sleepers and held grudges.

The map case hung against her chest on a strap of braided horsehair, warm even through her shift, warmer than it should have been, the way a sleeping animal is warm. She had not opened it since the ford. She did not need to. She had spent two days doing what she had always done best—memorizing. The ridge road first, then the shepherd's track past the standing stone where the moss grew only on the wrong side, then the place where the river went underground and the world above it went quiet in a way that made her teeth ache. Twelve waypoints, inked in brown-black lines that looked less like drawing than like handwriting. Someone had written this valley. Tonight she intended to read the margins.

The bread was a problem. It pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and every few hundred paces she checked it was still there, which was absurd, but so was everything else tonight. Three days ago she had been a girl who mended charms that didn't work. Now she was a girl who carried the key to a prison in a leather tube, and the difference between those two girls was apparently half a loaf of rye and Odile's second-best cloak.

The cloak was gray wool, good quality, patched at both elbows because Odile believed visible mending kept vanity out of a household. It smelled of rosemary and lamp smoke and, underneath that, of something older—dried yarrow, maybe, or simply years. Astrid had stood in front of the peg by the door for a long moment before taking it, telling herself it was practical, telling herself the second-best cloak mattered precisely because Odile would notice its absence last, and then telling herself the truth, which was that she wanted to wear something of Odile's into the dark, the way you carry a stone from home so the road can't claim you're a stranger.

*You're stealing from her,* said the small voice that lived under her breastbone and commented on everything.

*I'm borrowing from someone who lied to me for twenty years,* said the larger voice, the new one, the one that had arrived with a dying man's blood on her hands.

They walked together after that, the voices, all the way up through the village.

Vell slept the way it always slept: completely, trustingly, as though sleep were a civic duty. The shuttered houses leaned together along the lane like old friends sharing a secret they'd forgotten the content of. A dog raised its head outside the cooper's, considered Astrid, and put it back down. Even the river seemed to move more quietly than usual, apologetic, as if it too suspected it wasn't supposed to be going anywhere.

At the well in the square, Astrid stopped. She hadn't meant to. Her feet made their own decision, the way feet do, and there she stood in the moonless dark with her hand flat against the cold stones, looking up at Odile's cottage on its rise above the roofs—the single lit window, the crooked chimney, the herb garden she could name plant by plant in the dark because she'd weeded it by feel a thousand times.

That window was still burning.

Astrid stood very still. Odile was awake. Odile was sitting up, at whatever hour this was, in her chair by the fire, and either she had not yet discovered the empty bed down the hall, or—and this thought arrived like cold water—she had discovered it hours ago and was simply sitting with the knowledge, the way you sit with a diagnosis.

*Go up,* said the small voice. *Tell her. She raised you. Whatever she knows, she owes you an explanation, and explanations are owed face to face, not across a ridge.*

*She'll take the map,* said the large voice. *Gently. Lovingly. She'll hold your hands and tell you it's for your own good, and she will be sincere, and that is the worst part, that she will mean every word.*

Both voices were right. That was the whole trouble with leaving; every direction was true at once.

"Goodbye," Astrid whispered to the lit window, because a speech required an audience and a whisper required only courage. "I'm sorry about the chives."

Then she turned north, toward the ridge, and did not let herself look back again, because she knew exactly how many steps of looking back she could afford, and the answer was none.

* * *

The valley road gave way to the shepherd's track a mile past the last house, and the track gave way to nothing much at all, and Astrid climbed through the dark with the map's warmth against her chest and Odile's cloak settling around her shoulders like a hand.

It was strange, how familiar the escape was. She had rehearsed leaving her whole life, in the way other children rehearsed weddings or battles. Lying in her narrow bed she had mapped the route so often she could walk it blindfolded—which, tonight, she nearly did. Past the standing stone. Along the dry wall that ran uphill like a sentence trailing off. Through the gap in the hawthorn that scratched your arm no matter which side you approached from, as if the hedge itself were ambivalent about visitors.

The hawthorn caught her sleeve. Astrid stood a moment in the dark, held by a bush, and laughed—a small, cracked sound, quickly swallowed. Of course. Of course even Vell's shrubbery had opinions about her departure.

She freed herself, losing a thread of gray wool to the thorns as payment, and climbed on.

The moon was new—not quite dark, a fingernail paring of silver low in the west, and Astrid found herself watching it the way you watch a stranger who has spoken your name. *Thirteen turns,* the courier had whispered. *The thirteenth turn locks it.* She counted backward without wanting to, the way you probe a bruise. How many moons since he died at the ford? One. One turn gone. Twelve remained, and each of them would pass over this valley whether she succeeded or failed, indifferent as weather, and somewhere in that indifference was a deadline.

She walked faster.

The ridge came up to meet her the way ridges do—slowly for hours and then suddenly, all at once, the ground tipping up beneath her boots until she was climbing hand over hand through gorse and loose shale, breath sawing, the bread hammering at her ribs like a fist demanding entry. Below her, Vell lay pooled in the dark, a handful of lights, a curl of woodsmoke, the river a ribbon of slightly-less-dark. Small. It looked so small from up here. It had never once looked small from inside it. From inside, Vell had been the entire world, complete as an eggshell.

From up here, it looked like something drawn by a careful child who had run out of paper.

Astrid crested the ridge at the exact moment the sky decided to consider dawn.

She stopped.

Below her—not far below, a stone's throw, a long shout—the land fell away into country she had never seen. Not imagined. Never once seen. There were hills over there, folded like sleeping animals, hazed in the first gray light. There were woods that went on longer than woods had any right to, and a road—an actual road, pale as scar tissue—winding down and away and out of sight around a shoulder of the world, going, presumably, somewhere, because roads outside of maps went places, that was their whole nature, and Astrid stood at the top of her own valley with her mouth open like a fish at market and felt something in her chest crack quietly along a line she hadn't known was there.

She had memorized maps of this view for twenty years. Every hill was already named in her head, catalogued from stolen glances at the peddler's atlas, from Odile's discarded letters, from the one smuggled chapbook of coastal towns she'd hidden under a floorboard at fourteen. And none of it—not one fold, not one tree—matched. Reality had ignored her homework entirely. The hills were wrong. Beautifully, overwhelmingly wrong, and the wrongness hit her like cold water off a cart, and she stood there laughing and crying at the same time in a way she was deeply glad nobody witnessed.

"That's not where the forest goes," she informed the landscape. The landscape declined to adjust itself.

And then she took one more step, downhill, off the ridge, out of the valley's shadow—

—and magic touched her skin for the first time.

* * *

Later, she would try to describe it to Tam, and fail, and try again, and fail better. But that first time there was no language for it at all, only sensation, and the sensation was this:

It was weather.

Not rain, not wind—weather the way the word means before it splits into kinds. A pressure and a presence, a change in the air that arrives before a storm and makes the horses nervous. It brushed her face like the first warm day after winter, slid over her arms under Odile's cloak, lifted the hair at the nape of her neck. It tasted faintly green. It had texture—coarse in one direction, smooth in another, like running your hand along the nap of velvet and then against it.

For nineteen years, Astrid Joubert had lived on one side of a pane of glass, watching weather she could not feel. She crossed the ridge and stepped into the open sky of it, and it was too much, immediately, all at once, the way sunlight is too much for eyes that have lived in a cellar.

She sat down. Not gracefully. Her knees simply resigned, and she ended up on the hillside with the bread digging into her ribs and her palms flat against grass that was *buzzing*, faintly, sweetly, like the air over a struck bell, and the magic moved through her and around her and—in some way she could not account for and did not dare examine—*noticed her*. It noticed her the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters. It gathered, slightly, toward her. Toward the thing against her chest. Toward her blood.

"No," Astrid told it, her voice thin. "No, thank you. I'm just passing through."

The magic did not answer, because magic does not, but it also did not entirely withdraw. It settled against her awareness the way a cat settles near a warm hearth—not touching, but accounting for the warmth.

She sat on the hillside for a long time, shaking.

Here is what no one tells you about freedom, Astrid thought, with her arms wrapped around her knees: it is enormous. It is not a door opening; it is the entire wall coming down, and beyond the wall there is not a garden but a country, and the country has weather, and the weather does not care that you have never been outside. In Vell, she had been invisible, yes—but invisible the way a fish is invisible in water, supported on all sides, defined by the shape of her confinement. Out here the water was gone and she could feel every current, and every current was a choice, and every choice was a cliff edge, and she wanted all of it so badly her teeth ached, and she was terrified of it in the same breath, and both things lived in her chest at once like two birds in one cage, battering each other's wings.

"I memorized you," she said to the hills, wiping her nose with the back of her hand. "Did you know that? I memorized maps of you under my blanket like other girls memorize love songs. And you're—" her voice broke, infuriatingly, "—you're nothing like I drew you. You're *more*. You're so much more, and I hate you a little, and I've waited my whole life to meet you."

The hills, being hills, said nothing. But the light came up over them, gold and unhurried, and the magic in the grass hummed a note lower, gentler, almost kind, and Astrid sat in it and let herself shake until the shaking became breathing and the breathing became ordinary.

When she finally opened the map case—her first deliberate act as a person outside of Vell—the leather was warm as a living thing, and the sigil stamped in wax on its flap caught the sunrise and threw it back red.

* * *

She ate breakfast on the move, tearing the loaf and chewing while she walked, because the map's first waypoint was marked below the ridge on the far side, and some instinct older than sense told her not to stand still. The bread tasted of Vell—of Odile's flour, of the well water kneaded into it, of the woodsmoke of the oven she'd helped stack last autumn—and she ate every crumb deliberately, the way you eat a meal before a fast, and brushed the salt from her fingers, and allowed herself exactly one minute of grief.

One minute, standing still, facing back the way she'd come. The ridge was a dark line against the brightening east now, and somewhere behind it was a lit window, and a woman who had burned letters with this same seal on them, and a garden with trampled chives, and a life that had been small and safe and a lie—or not a lie, worse, a kindness performed so long it had forgotten it was a performance.

"I'm coming back," Astrid said to the ridge. "Whatever else happens, whatever this costs—I'm coming back, and when I come back the door comes with me. Both directions." She paused. "And I want an apology. From you specifically," she added, jabbing a finger at the hawthorn, which was, from this distance, merely a hedge.

Then she turned south, because the map said south, and walking south felt like the first honest decision of her life.

The morning unspooled. The road took her down through country that made her stop every fifty paces like a fool—wildflowers in colors Vell had never grown, a hawk riding air that *moved*, visibly, in slow ribbons, and once, unforgettable, a deer that looked at her across a clearing with eyes that held not the mild blankness of valley animals but something quick and assessing, something that *saw* her, and bounded off trailing a wake of faint green shimmer that Astrid would remember for the rest of her life.

By midday she had found the first waypoint: a leaning milestone at the meeting of two tracks, marked on the map with a small circle of ink. When she touched it, the stone warmed under her palm, and somewhere deep in the leather case against her heart, something shifted—an almost inaudible click, like a lock considering.

And like a lock considering, it made her think of locks, and of doors, and of who might be walking through the country behind her, following her trail the way she followed the map's.

The thought arrived whole, uninvited, wearing Odile's voice even though it wasn't Odile's thought: *Something will come for that map. Something always comes for keys.*

Astrid stood at the milestone with her hand still resting on the warm stone, the sun high and friendly and suddenly not comforting at all, and looked back up the road she'd traveled. Empty. Long. Innocent.

Mostly innocent.

Far off, at the crest of the ridge she'd crossed at dawn—too far to be sure, too still to be nothing—a figure stood against the sky, watching.

It did not move. It did not need to. The distance between them closed like a hand around her wrist, and Astrid understood, with the perfect clarity that magic-brushed air seemed to lend to terrible thoughts, that her night of walking alone had just ended.

She ran.
