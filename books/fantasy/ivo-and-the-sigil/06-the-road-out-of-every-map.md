## 6. The Road Out of Every Map

The first thing Astrid learned about the world outside Vell was that it lied.

Not in the way Odile lied—warmly, wearily, with flour on her hands and a story ready for every question. The world outside lied the way a funhouse mirror lies: cheerfully, structurally, as if deception were simply how it was built.

She discovered this on her second morning, at a crossroads that should have taken her east toward the river town of Marrow's Bend, if the map's first waypoint could be trusted. The signpost there had four arms. One pointed to Marrow's Bend. One pointed to a place called Little Hassock, which sounded made up. One pointed to *Vell*, which made her stomach drop through her boots and out the other side of the world. And one arm had been snapped off by weather or vandals and pointed, uselessly, at the sky.

Astrid stood before the signpost with her bread gone stale in her pack and Odile's second-best cloak—grey wool, smelling faintly of rosemary and old arguments—pulled tight around her shoulders.

"Right," she said aloud, because talking to herself was a habit she'd developed somewhere between the ridge and here, and she saw no reason to break it now. "East."

East was where the sun came up. She'd checked. She'd checked three times, actually, because in Vell she'd never needed to navigate by anything more complicated than the path from Odile's cottage to the herb garden, and she wanted to be sure.

She walked east for what felt like an hour. The road was pleasant enough—a dirt track lined with hawthorn, the morning fog burning off in gold ribbons—and she let herself enjoy it, briefly, the way you might enjoy the last warm day before winter. Birds sang. A rabbit watched her pass with the insolence of a creature that has never once been asked for its papers.

Then the road bent, gently, almost politely, and brought her back to the signpost.

The same signpost. The same four arms. The broken one still pointing at the sky, though now she could have sworn it pointed slightly more accusingly.

Astrid stopped. She looked back the way she'd come. She looked at the signpost. She looked at the sky, in case someone up there was laughing.

"No," she said.

The signpost said nothing, which she chose to interpret as agreement.

She tried again. This time she counted her steps—one hundred, two hundred, three hundred—and kept her eyes fixed on a crooked oak ahead of her so she couldn't be fooled by scenery repeating itself. The oak grew closer. The oak grew very close. The oak grew close enough that she could see a woodpecker working its trunk, and then the road curved around a stand of birches, and when the birches let go of her view, she was standing at the signpost again, and the woodpecker was now working the signpost itself, which struck her as either a terrible omen or excellent comic timing.

"All right," Astrid said, and sat down on the verge, and ate some stale bread, and thought.

This was something Odile had taught her without meaning to: when a charm misbehaves, don't fight it. Sit down. Have tea. Ask what the charm *thinks* it's doing. Magic, Odile said, is mostly stubbornness wearing a fancy hat, and the trick is to be more patient than it is.

Except this wasn't magic. This was Vell's opposite—the outside world—and it was behaving exactly like one of Odile's mended charms. Roads that circled. Signs that rearranged. A whole landscape refusing, politely but firmly, to lead anywhere.

*The map,* she thought. *The map is the only true path.*

She dug the leather case out from under her cloak, where it had spent the night pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Inside, the courier's map lay coiled in its careful folds, inked in that strange brown-black that wasn't quite any color she could name. And there, at the edge of Vell, a small mark she'd taken for a smudge: a circle with a line through it, like a coin on edge.

The first waypoint. Which was not, apparently, Marrow's Bend.

Which was, now that she looked properly, *here*. At the signpost. Where she'd been standing for the better part of an hour, going in circles like a dog chasing its own tail and catching it.

"Oh," said Astrid. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

* * *

The waypoint, once she knew to look for it, was obvious. The signpost itself was the marker—no, not the signpost; a stone beneath it, half-sunk in the grass, bearing the same sigil that sealed the map's wax and that the dying courier had traced in his own blood on the ford's bank. A circle with a line through it. A door seen sideways.

And beneath the sigil, in letters so small she'd need daylight and patience both:

*A toll is owed.*

Astrid crouched over the stone and felt her mood darken considerably. Of course. Of course the only true road out of every lying map demanded payment. Everything in her life had lately been demanding payment: the truth about her name, the truth about Odile, the truth about the valley that wasn't supposed to exist. She was beginning to feel like a debt collector working in reverse.

"What kind of toll?" she asked the stone.

The stone, being a stone, declined to specify.

She tried blood first, because the courier had used blood and because dramatic gestures were free. She pricked her thumb on her sewing needle—carried always, a hedge-witch habit—and pressed a bead of red to the sigil.

Nothing. The blood sat on the stone like blood on a stone, which is to say, unimpressively.

She tried a lock of hair. Nothing. She tried reciting the only formal words she knew, a blessing Odile used over beekeeping, which felt thematically adjacent if nothing else. The stone absorbed her blessing the way sand absorbs rain, and gave back nothing but silence and one deeply judgmental beetle.

Astrid sat back on her heels and glared at the waypoint until her eyes watered.

It was while glaring that she noticed the traveler.

He'd appeared on the road behind her—or perhaps he'd been there all along, circling like she had, and their orbits had finally crossed. He was older, grey-bearded, dressed in the faded brights of a peddler whose colors had been washed too many times, and he was watching her with the expression of a man who has seen many people talk to stones and has stopped being surprised by most of them.

"You'll want to give it something you'll miss," he said.

Astrid startled so badly she nearly fell into the hawthorn. "I'm sorry?"

"The waypoints." He ambled closer, hands in his pockets, a man in no hurry anywhere ever again. "They don't want blood. Blood's cheap. Everyone's got blood." He lowered himself onto the verge beside her with a series of small groans, like a house settling. "They want cost. Something you'll feel the shape of after it's gone."

"And how would you know that?"

"Because I've been walking this road for eleven years, missy, and I've paid that stone twice." He smiled, and it was a tired smile with real warmth buried in it, like a coal under ash. "Name's Petch."

"Astrid." She hesitated over the surname. It still felt strange in her mouth, this word that had killed a man and raised a prison. "Astrid Joubert."

Something flickered across Petch's face—not recognition, exactly, but the shadow of it, the way a cloud's shadow crosses a field. He looked at her a moment too long, then let it go. Eleven years on a lying road probably taught a man to let things go.

"First time out?" he asked.

"Is it that obvious?"

"You're talking to a milestone and losing." He nodded at the sigil-stone. "Everyone loses, at first. The roads here aren't broken, mind. They're *loyal*. To the old seals. Anywhere a founder's hand touched, the ground remembers who it belongs to, and it doesn't much care for strangers passing through unless they pay respects." He scratched his beard. "You're headed for Marrow's Bend, I'd guess. Everyone is, at first. There's a ferry there."

"There's a waypoint there," Astrid said. "On my map."

Petch went very still, in the way of a man recalculating everything. "You've got a *founder's map*," he said slowly. "An honest one."

"Is that rare?"

"Missy, I've seen maps sold at three markets that swear the river runs north. I've watched a signpost change its mind while I was reading it. An honest map—" He laughed, short and disbelieving. "There are maybe five honest maps left in the world, and four of them are probably eaten."

Astrid looked down at the map in her lap, at its patient brown-black lines, and felt the weight of it settle differently on her shoulders. Not just a way out. A relic. Possibly the last of its kind.

"So the toll," she said. "Something I'll miss."

"That's the shape of it. Different for everyone, near as I can tell. First time, I paid it a memory of my mother's voice. Took me years to notice it was gone—just a hole where a song used to be." He said it lightly, but his hand had curled closed on his knee. "Second time, I paid it the name of my home village. Can't recall it at all now. Just that it had a good well."

Astrid's chest tightened. "That's horrible."

"It's a toll," Petch said with a shrug. "Horrible's the job description. But here's the thing nobody tells you: the road takes only what you offer freely, and it takes only what you can spare, even if you don't know you can spare it. It's cruel, but it's *honest* cruel. There's worse honesty to be found." He glanced at her sidelong. "You strike me as a girl carrying something heavy. Whatever's in that case—it's why the roads keep spitting you back here, isn't it? They know you're meant to pass. They're just waiting for you to mean it."

"Meaning it costs a memory?"

"Meaning it costs *something*. Memory, habit, hope, a grudge—I heard of a woman who paid with her fear of spiders and regretted it immediately, on account of the spiders." He grinned. "The waypoint will take your offering and show you the true road. Until the next waypoint. Then you'll pay again. That's the whole religion of travel out here, missy: pay as you go."

Astrid turned the idea over. It was extortionate. It was elegant. It was, she suspected, exactly the sort of thing the twelve founders would have built—nothing wasted, nothing free, every step forward purchased with a piece of the walker. Her grandmother had helped make this. Had *she* paid tolls, walking these roads? What had she given up, and had she missed it?

*The thirteenth key hid inside a living bloodline.* The journal's words rose in her memory. Well. If her blood was the key, perhaps her blood understood the logic of doors: nothing opens without something closing.

"I don't suppose you know what it wants from me," she said.

"If I did, I'd be selling it to you, and we'd both be poorer." Petch pushed himself upright, joint by joint. "I'm for Little Hassock, myself. Third time today. Maybe the stone will take my patience and I'll get there by dark." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Good luck, Astrid Joubert. Walk like you mean it."

And he set off down the road that circled, whistling something that might have been a song or might have been a complaint, and within fifty steps the hawthorn swallowed him whole.

* * *

Astrid sat with the waypoint until the sun stood straight overhead.

She inventoried herself, the way Odile had taught her to inventory a cupboard before market day: what do we have, what do we need, what can we bear to lose?

Her memories of Vell—of Odile's kitchen, of the herb rows, of the smell of bread and beeswax—she held those up and put them down again, untouched. No. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. Those were load-bearing.

Her fear? She considered offering her fear, the way the spider-woman had, and imagined walking the rest of this road unafraid, and found the picture wrong somehow. Fear had kept her alive at the ford. Fear had made her run instead of freeze. You didn't sell your smoke detector on the eve of a house fire.

Her loneliness, then. That seemed safe. She had plenty; it was weighing her down; surely the road would be doing her a favor—

But no. Even as she reached for it, she understood. Loneliness was the engine. Loneliness was the reason she was sitting at a stone in the middle of nowhere instead of back in a warm kitchen being useful and invisible. Take away the ache, and what remained? A girl with a map and no reason to follow it.

Everything she could spare was something she needed. That, she realized, was probably the point. That was probably the entire design.

So she offered the smallest true thing she had.

She thought about the memorized maps—the coastlines and capitals she'd copied into the back of her mind over years of stolen evenings, the geography of everywhere she'd never go. She'd told herself they were harmless. Souvenirs of longing. But she knew, in the honest hour of offering, that they had also been a wall. As long as she could recite the world from her chair, she never had to admit she was desperate to stand in it. The maps had been a way of staying. A beautiful, detailed, cowardly way of staying.

She took the map case from her side, opened it, and laid her palm flat on the sigil-stone.

"I give up the girl who stayed home and memorized the world instead of seeing it," she said. "I don't want her anymore. She was very tidy and she never went anywhere."

For a moment, nothing. Then the stone warmed under her palm—not the map's warmth, which lived against her heart, but a different heat, drier, older, like a hearthstone remembering fires. Something lifted out of her. It didn't hurt. It was more like setting down a basket she'd forgotten she was carrying: a sudden lightness, and beneath the lightness, a small grief, the exact shape of all those evenings bent over borrowed atlases.

The sigil flared brown-gold, sank into the stone, and went quiet.

And the road—she felt it before she saw it—*changed*. Not visibly. The dirt track looked the same, the hawthorn looked the same, the broken signpost arm still pointed at the sky. But the air along it had a direction now, a current, the way a river has a current under a flat surface. East. Truly east. Toward Marrow's Bend, toward the ferry, toward whatever waited at the second waypoint.

Astrid stood, brushed off her cloak, and shouldered her pack.

"Well," she said to the signpost, which had stopped lying to her and therefore lost most of its menace. "Don't wait up."

She walked, and the road let her, and the rabbit watched her go with what she chose to believe was respect.

* * *

She made six miles before dusk, which Petch would later inform her was respectable for a first-timer, and camped in the lee of a fallen wall that might once have been a barn. She ate the last of the stale bread softened in stream water, wrapped herself in rosemary-smelling wool, and lay looking up at stars she didn't yet know the names of.

Here was the thing she hadn't expected: the lightness hurt.

She'd thought giving something up would feel like loss. Instead it felt like *room*—an empty shelf where the memorized maps used to sit, and the emptiness echoed. All those hours she'd spent copying coastlines, she'd told herself she was building a future. Really she'd been building an excuse. And now the excuse was gone, and the future was a dark road and a tollbooth stone and a name that got a man killed, and there was no one in the world who knew where she was.

No one at all.

The loneliness arrived then, right on schedule, the way it does when you finally stop moving long enough to feel it: total, cold, thorough. It lay down beside her like a large animal and breathed on her neck. In Vell she had been invisible, but invisible inside a web of small familiar threads—Odile's voice, the neighbors' nods, the rhythm of days. Here the threads were cut, all of them, and she was a single knot of person in an enormous dark.

She cried a little. Quietly, into the wool, the way you cry when there's no one to perform for. It didn't help, exactly. But it was honest, and honesty had gotten her through the waypoint, so she figured it was a currency worth spending.

When the crying was done, she did what she always did with hard nights: she talked to the dead, because the dead were excellent listeners.

"You could have explained a little more," she told the courier. "'Follow the map' would have been useful. 'Pay the stones something you'll miss' would have been *very* useful. Instead you died at a ford muttering about moons, and now I'm out here negotiating with geology." She pulled the map case out and rested it on her stomach, feeling its faint warmth through her shirt. "But I suppose if you'd explained everything, I'd have had time to talk myself out of it. So. Fine. Noted. Terrible communication style. Effective anyway."

The map warmed, very slightly, under her hand. She chose to believe it was agreement rather than indigestion.

And underneath the loneliness—she tested this carefully, the way you test ice—there was something else. Something that had been growing since the ridge, since the first brush of wild magic on her skin, since the stone had accepted her offering and shown her a true road.

Resolve. Not the loud kind. The quiet, load-bearing kind, the kind Odile used in charms: not *I will conquer*, but *I will hold*. She was alone on a lying road in a world that rearranged itself to keep people wandering, and she had paid her first toll and the road had opened, and that meant it could be done. It was being done. By her. Badly, coldly, tearfully—but done.

Tomorrow: Marrow's Bend. The ferry. The second waypoint, and its price.

She was almost asleep when she heard it—far off, back the way she'd come, a sound like a bell rung underwater, deep and wrong. Once. Twice. Then silence, the kind of silence that follows a sound rather than preceding it.

Astrid sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and the map against her chest had gone hot as a fresh loaf.

Down the true road, at the edge of sight, something was coming. And it was not paying tolls.
