## 7. The Ferryman's Debt

The drowned village announced itself three ways.

First there was the smell — river mud and old thatch and something sweeter underneath, like apples rotting in a cellar nobody had opened in years. Then came the sound: water lapping at things water had no business touching. And finally, when Astrid crested the last hill with her boots squelching and her stomach complaining in a language she was beginning to understand fluently, she saw it.

Roofs. Dozens of them, just breaking the surface of a wide grey lake like the backs of sleeping animals. A church spire leaned out of the middle of it all at a drunken angle, its bell still hanging in the tower, green with moss, silent for what looked like a very long time.

"Oh," said Astrid, to no one. "That's not encouraging."

She had been walking for two days on roads that lied to her. She had learned to read the map's silver thread by holding it up to the moonlight, and even so, the waypoints had a way of being somewhere else entirely by the time she arrived. The third waypoint had been a standing stone; the fourth, according to the map, was *the bell of the sunken village, where the ferryman keeps his debt.*

Astrid had some questions about that phrasing. She had been carrying them around for half a day like stones in her shoe.

The shore was soft going, all reeds and old fence posts sticking up out of the shallows like the fingers of people who'd changed their minds about drowning. She picked her way along it until she saw the boat.

It was a flat-bottomed ferry, broad as a kitchen table and twice as ugly, tied to a post that must once have been someone's gatepost. Sitting in it, mending a net with the patience of a man who had nothing else whatsoever to do, was a young man with dark hair cut short and a face that seemed to have been assembled from spare parts and then never finished. He didn't look up when her boots crunched on the shingle. He didn't look up when she coughed. He did look up — briefly, without much hope — when she said, "Excuse me. Hello. Are you the ferryman?"

"No," he said.

Astrid looked at the boat. She looked at the net. She looked at the man sitting in the boat mending the net, which was, she felt, fairly strong evidence.

"You're mending a net," she pointed out, "in a ferry."

"I'm sitting in a boat," he said, "mending a net. That's not the same as being a ferryman."

"Then what are you?"

He considered this with the gravity of a philosopher deciding whether the soul is round. "Unemployed," he said finally, and went back to his net.

Astrid stood there. Somewhere behind her, the road she'd come by was almost certainly rearranging itself into a sheep track or a hedge, because that was what roads did here when they thought she wasn't looking. The map pressed warm against her ribs, inside Odile's second-best cloak, which was too big for her and smelled faintly of lavender and disapproval.

"I need to cross," she tried.

"Do you."

"To the other side of the lake. There's a waypoint. I think." She pulled the map case out before she could talk herself out of it — and immediately regretted it, because the man's hands went still on the net.

Not dramatically. Not the way Odile had gone pale at the name Joubert, all gasp and dropped teacup. He just stopped, the way water stops moving when the wind dies. His eyes went to the leather of the case, and then to the sigil stamped into the wax seal that showed through the flap, and something happened in his face that Astrid couldn't name but recognized anyway, because she'd been wearing it herself since a dying man whispered her family name into the mud at a ford.

"That mark," he said.

"It's mine," said Astrid, which was true in a way she couldn't explain and false in every way that mattered. "Sort of. It's complicated."

"Everything worth carrying is." He set down the net. "Get in the boat."

"You just said you weren't the ferryman."

"I'm not. But you're going to drown otherwise, and I've seen enough drowning." He untied the rope with one hand. "The lake doesn't let people cross who aren't supposed to. You're supposed to. Get in."

* * *

His name was Tam, and he spoke the way some people count money — carefully, reluctantly, and only when he absolutely had to. Astrid learned this over the next hour as the ferry slid out across the drowned village, past rooftops furred with weed, past a chimney standing alone in ten feet of water like a monument to somebody's abandoned porridge.

Tam rowed. Astrid asked questions. Tam answered approximately one in five, and those answers were short enough to fit in a thimble.

"Where are we?"

"Corvel's edge."

"What's Corvel?"

"A valley."

"A valley like—" She stopped. "Like Vell?"

Something shifted in his shoulders. "You know Vell."

"I'm *from* Vell."

Now he did look up, and his eyes were the color of the lake on an overcast day, which is to say they gave away nothing while seeming to promise that if you fell in, you would not be getting out quickly. "There's no such place," he said. "Vell isn't on any map."

"I know," said Astrid, with feeling. "It's rather the whole problem."

They passed the church spire close enough that she could have touched it. Up close the bell was enormous, taller than she was, crusted green, and carved around its lip with a ring of symbols that made the hair on her arms stand up — because she knew them. Or rather, she knew their cousins. They were the same shapes as the ones on the courier's map, the same flowing interlocked script, drawn by the same kind of hand.

"That's the same work," she said. "On my map. Who carved that?"

"The founders." Tam said it the way you'd say *the weather*, flat and final, but his oar hesitated, just for a stroke. "Twelve of them. One for each turn of the moon. They sealed Corvel the way your people sealed Vell. Same sigil-work. Same lies afterward." He nodded at the flooded village below them. "This was the market town. When the seal went up, the founders decided the border needed to be... legible. So they drowned it. Said it would remind people not to leave."

"Did it?"

"It reminded people not to argue." He shipped the oars and let the boat drift. "I left anyway. Eleven years ago. I was nine."

Astrid waited. She had learned, growing up under Odile's roof, that silence was sometimes the only question worth asking.

"My sister didn't make it," Tam said, to the bell, not to her. "Maren. She was twelve. We got through the ridge together and the Warden caught us on the far side. It took her back through. I ran." His jaw worked. "Everyone always asks why I didn't go back for her. Nobody ever asks why I keep mending nets."

"Why do you keep mending nets?"

He blinked, as though the question had arrived in a language he'd studied years ago and mostly forgotten. "Because if the net's ready," he said slowly, "then someday there'll be something worth catching. And if there's nothing worth catching, at least the net's ready." He picked the oars back up, ears slightly red. "Anyway. Your waypoint's the bell. Ring it, or whatever it is you do."

"How do you know that's my waypoint?"

"Because the lake told me." At her expression, he added, with the ghost of what might, in a more expansive man, have been called a smile: "You get to know how places talk, when you live on the outside of a seal. Everything near one gets a bit strange. Roads move. Lakes gossip. Bells remember." He nodded at the great green shape looming over them. "Go on. Touch it. See what happens."

"And if what happens is bad?"

"Then I'll row fast," said Tam. "It's the one thing I'm good at."

* * *

The bell was cold and slick under her palm, and the moment her fingers closed around the rim, the map against her chest went hot as a fresh loaf.

Astrid yelped. The sigil-script on the bell lit up — not fire, not magic exactly, but a kind of remembering, the carvings filling with pale light like veins filling with blood. Below the surface of the lake, dim and vast, something answered: a second ring of light, deeper down, where the founders' original work lay drowned with the village. For one heartbeat the whole lake glowed from beneath, every rooftop and lane and garden outlined in silver, the drowned town visible entire, beautiful and terrible, a memory refusing to stay forgotten.

Then it faded, and the lake was just a lake again, and Astrid was gripping the bell with both hands and breathing like a woman who'd been underwater herself.

"Well," she managed. "That happened."

Tam hadn't moved. He was staring at her — not at the bell, at *her* — with an expression she was starting to dread on other people's faces, because it was the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"The map answered you," he said.

"The map answers everyone. Probably. Presumably. I haven't done a controlled study."

"It didn't answer the courier who carried it." His voice was very quiet now. "I found him, you know. Two days ago, at the ford upstream. Or what was left of him. He'd been running from the Warden for a week, and the map never warmed for him once. He told me so himself, before—" Tam stopped. Started again. "Before he sent me downstream to watch the crossings, in case whoever he was dying to reach came this way."

The bottom dropped out of Astrid's stomach. "You were at the ford."

"I was in the reeds." He met her eyes. "I watched him give you the case. I watched him say your name. Joubert." He pronounced it carefully, like a word in a spell. "And I've spent two days telling myself it was coincidence, because the alternative is that the thirteenth key of the founders has been delivered into the hands of a girl who talks to bells and can't lie convincingly about it."

"I *can* lie convincingly," Astrid said with dignity. "Odile trained me. I once convinced her the cat ate an entire ham."

"Did the cat eat the ham?"

"...No."

"There it is." But something in him had loosened, fractionally, the way a knot loosens when you stop pulling at it wrong. He sat back on his thwart and looked at her for a long moment, and Astrid had the distinct feeling of being weighed — not her body, but her intentions, measured against some scale he kept in the same place he kept his grief.

"If you're the key," he said, "then you can open Vell."

"So the map says. Eventually. After several more of these charming waypoint experiences."

"Vell opens," Tam said, "and every seal made by the same hand feels it. Every door built on the same sigil-work hears the lock turn." His knuckles whitened on the oar handles. "Including Corvel's."

The lake lapped against the hull. Somewhere below them, the drowned market town kept its silver secret.

"You want me to open your home too," Astrid said.

"I want you to *try*." The words came out of him like splinters, one at a time. "I'm not asking you to promise it'll work. I'm asking you to promise you'll stand in front of that door and put your hand on it and mean it. Because I've spent eleven years readying nets, Astrid Joubert, and I am *tired* of readying nets."

Astrid looked at him — this laconic, unfinished-faced boy who had run from a seal at nine years old and had been running ever since, in circles, on a lake, mending nets for a catch that never came. She thought of Odile's kitchen, warm and small and suffocating. She thought of the courier's blood on her hands, cooling faster than she could hold it in.

She was, she realized, starving. Not for bread, though also for bread. For this: someone to make a promise *to*. Someone whose promise would cost her something real.

"All right," she said. "Here's my promise, and I don't say it lightly, because Odile says I say everything lightly. If I open Vell — when I open Vell — I will come to Corvel, and I will stand at your door, and I will put my hand on it and mean it. Whatever's behind it. Even if it's the Warden. Even if it's worse than the Warden." She stuck out her hand, because Odile had taught her that bargains made without a handshake were just wishes with ambition. "That's my debt. Write it on the bell if you like."

Tam looked at her hand for a long moment.

Then he took it. His grip was calloused and careful, like a man used to handling things that tear.

"One condition," he said. "You eat something. You look like a heron that lost an argument."

"I've been rationing."

"You've been *failing* at rationing. There's smoked fish under the tarp and bread that's only slightly haunted. Rowing makes you hungry, and from here to the far shore, you're rowing."

"Why am I rowing? It's your boat!"

"Because I'm guiding you now," Tam said, already reaching for the fish, "and guides supervise. Also my shoulder's been sore since Tuesday, and you owe me a debt, and debts start accruing immediately. That's what makes them debts."

Astrid took the oars, muttering, and began to pull. The ferry swung around toward the far shore, where the map's silver thread waited, patient as moonlight. Behind them the bell hung silent in its leaning tower, and beneath them the drowned town slept on, dreaming in sigils.

Halfway across, Tam spoke again, quietly, without looking up from the fish he was dividing with great fairness and no generosity whatsoever.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the courier told me one more thing, before the end. He said the key was a girl who'd grown up in a cage and never once stopped memorizing maps of the outside." He handed her the bigger half. "I told him he was being romantic. But he was right, wasn't he?"

Astrid rowed, and chewed, and didn't trust herself to answer.

It was only when the far shore was close — close enough to see that the reeds there were bent all in one direction, away from the water, as if something enormous had recently passed through and the grass was still recovering — that Tam went still, and said, in a voice stripped of everything except warning:

"Astrid. Put the oars down."

"Why? What—"

"Something's been following us since the bell rang." His eyes were fixed on the treeline, where the shadows between the trunks had begun, very slowly, to arrange themselves into shapes that had shoulders. "And I don't think it's the Warden."

"I thought the Warden was the thing that follows keys."

"It is." Tam reached, very calmly, for the net. "So the question you should be asking yourself, Astrid Joubert, is what else out here knows you've started opening doors."
