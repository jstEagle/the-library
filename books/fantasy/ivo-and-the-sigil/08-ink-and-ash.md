## 8. Ink and Ash

Tam had a rule about fires: never build one bigger than you could put out with your boots and a bucket of river water. It wasn't caution, exactly. It was arithmetic. Everything that burned bright drew eyes, and everything that drew eyes on the road out of every map was either a Warden or about to become its business.

So the fire he built that night was small and mean, tucked into the lee of a fallen oak, barely bigger than two cupped hands. Enough to boil water. Enough to take the edge off the cold. Not enough to be seen from anywhere that mattered.

The girl sat across from it with her knees pulled up to her chest, watching him the way cats watch unfamiliar furniture—like it might be a trap, but an interesting one.

"You're staring," Tam said.

"You're not talking."

"That's usually how I prefer it."

She made a sound that was almost a laugh and mostly a scoff. Astrid Joubert. He'd turned the name over in his head all afternoon as they walked, testing it against what he knew, and it had fit together in his head like a key fitting a lock—which was, he supposed, exactly the problem.

He poured hot water over the last of his tea leaves. Third steeping. The flavor was more memory than tea at this point, but he offered her the cup anyway, because his mother had raised him right before Corvel had raised him wrong.

Astrid took it with both hands. "You said you recognized the sigil-work. On the map." She said it carefully, the way people say things they've been chewing on for hours. "You said it like it meant something specific."

"It does." Tam fed a twig to the fire. Watched it catch. "It means you're carrying something that wants you dead."

* * *

He told it plainly, because plainness was a kindness. He'd learned that from the ferryman who'd taught him the trade—a man named Osk who spoke in flat sentences and believed that dressing bad news up in soft words was just lying with better manners.

"The founders didn't just seal their valleys," Tam said. "Sealing takes guilt. You have to believe, deep down where you can't argue with yourself, that what you're doing is mercy. Twelve founders, twelve valleys, twelve helpings of guilt. And guilt doesn't evaporate when the seal is set. It has to go somewhere."

"Where?"

"Into the seal itself. Into the work. All that fear and shame and grief, pressed down into the sigil-lines like fat into salt pork." He turned his cup slowly. "And after enough years—enough centuries—it stands up and walks around."

Astrid had gone very still. "The Warden."

"The Warden." The word tasted like ash in his mouth; he supposed that was fitting. "It's not a person. It's not even really a creature. It's what's left over when twelve people decide they know better than everyone else and can't live with it. It hunts anything that threatens the seals. And above all—" he looked at her, and hated what he had to say next, and said it anyway, because Osk would have—"it hunts keys. Founder's blood. Anyone the sigil might answer to."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"How do you know all this?" she asked quietly.

"Because I grew up in a sealed valley too. Corvel. North and east of here, if 'here' meant anything, which it doesn't." He kept his voice level. Flat sentences. "Corvel's founders sealed out magic, same as Vell. Sealed out sickness too, and flood, and the blight that killed the wheat in the old stories. Sealed out everything. Including, eventually, my sister."

Astrid lowered the cup. "What happened to her?"

"She found out about the seal. Same way you did—someone dying told her something they shouldn't have." Tam watched the flames instead of her face. It was easier. "Maren was older than me by two years and braver than me by about twenty. She decided the valley should be opened. She went looking for the way through. And the Warden came for her."

"What does it—what does it *do*?" Her voice had gone thin. "When it comes for someone?"

"It wears faces." He said it simply because there was no simple way to say it. "Faces of people you've failed. People you've disappointed. My mother's face, when Maren found it. Mine. It talks to you in the voices of everyone whose opinion ever mattered, and it offers you a bargain: give up the key, go home, forget. And most people take it. That's the worst part. Most people *want* to take it." He paused. "Maren didn't."

The silence stretched long enough that the owl tried again.

"She walked into the Warden's shadow and she wouldn't let go of what she was carrying, and the shadow—" His voice caught, just once, on a snag he hadn't known was still there. He cleared his throat. "The shadow closed. When it opened again there was no Maren. No body. No grave. Just her shoes, side by side, neat as church, on the path home. Like she'd stepped out of them on purpose. Like she'd finally agreed to stay."

Astrid's hand came across the fire without asking permission and covered his where it rested on his knee. Her fingers were cold from the night air and steady as a mooring line.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Yeah," Tam said. "Me too. Every day. Which is why I'm telling you this instead of taking your map and dropping it down the deepest well I can find, which is the other option, and honestly the smarter one."

* * *

She pulled her hand back and hugged the cup against her chest. "You could still do that."

"I could."

"Why don't you?"

Because his sister's ghost would haunt him worse than any Warden. Because he'd spent eleven years poling a ferry back and forth across a drowned village, going nowhere, being nowhere, and he was tired of nowhere. Because somewhere under all the ash of him there was still a boy who believed doors were meant to open.

"Because," he said, "I've been sitting outside your valley for three years waiting for someone to walk out of it. And now someone has. And she's got founder's blood in her and a map warming against her ribs, and if I let the Warden eat her, then Maren died for nothing, and I'm not prepared to let that be true."

"Warming." Astrid went rigid. "How did you know it's warming?"

Tam looked at her.

She looked down at herself, at the place where the leather case rode beneath her cloak, and he watched the realization move through her face like weather. "It's been doing it since the ford," she whispered. "I thought—I thought it was just my own heat. Body heat. I thought I was imagining it." Her hand pressed flat against her chest. "It's warmer right now. It's warmer when I'm near you. Why is it warmer when I'm near you?"

"Because you're not imagining it, and it's not body heat." Tam leaned forward and turned the map case gently until he could see the wax seal glinting in the firelight—the sigil of thirteen interlocking turns, twelve dark and one pale. "That's bloodwork. Old bloodwork. Your grandmother didn't hide the thirteenth key in a box or a stone or a book, because boxes get opened and stones get moved and books get burned. She hid it in a bloodline. In people. In a line of daughters nobody thought to watch." He tapped the pale turn with one finger. "That mark isn't ink, Astrid. It's you. The map knows its owner the way a dog knows its family, and it's been calling out to you since the day you touched it."

"And the Warden can hear it calling?"

"The Warden can hear it from three valleys away."

Astrid sat with that. He gave her the time. He'd learned from Osk that people needed time the way bread needed proving—you couldn't rush it, and if you poked at it too soon it collapsed.

"So it's already coming," she said at last. "For me. Because of what I am."

"It was always coming for you. It just didn't know your name until the courier died saying it." He hesitated, then added the rest, because she deserved the whole ugly shape of it: "There's one more thing. About what happens to people who carry keys and don't run fast enough."

"I figured there would be."

"They don't all die like Maren. Some of them take the bargain. They hand over the key, they go home, they forget—and the forgetting is the cruelest part. They wake up the next morning happy. Genuinely happy. Small and safe and happy, with a little hole in their memory shaped like everything they might have been." He met her eyes. "I've seen it. A boy from Corvel named Pell. He took the bargain and now he tends bees and smiles at everyone and he doesn't remember he ever had a sister who loved him. I stood in front of him and he looked straight through me like I was weather."

Astrid's jaw tightened. "Odile."

"What?"

"My mistress. Odile. She knew my name—my real name, Joubert—and she forbade me to leave, and she was burning letters with the same sigil as the map." Astrid stared into the fire. "I thought she was trying to protect me. Maybe she was. But maybe she took the bargain once, a long time ago, and forgot why she was protecting me, and just... kept doing it. Kept doing it with a hole where the reason used to be."

Tam considered that. It had the shape of truth. The Warden's bargains left strange sediment behind—habits without reasons, kindnesses without memories, wards built by hands that no longer knew what they were warding against.

"Then when it comes for you," he said, "it'll wear her face first."

* * *

They sat with that until the fire burned down to a red eye and the cold crept in close around them. Astrid fed the flames another twig, and then, unexpectedly, another after that, breaking his careful arithmetic, and Tam found he didn't mind.

"Tell me about her," Astrid said. "Your sister. Not how she died. How she lived."

Nobody had ever asked him that. Eleven years of carrying Maren around inside himself like a stone in a boot, and no one—not Osk, not the strays who sometimes rode his ferry—had ever asked about the living part. He realized, with a small internal jolt, that he'd nearly lost the living part himself. Grief files things down. After enough years, the dead become only their deaths.

"Maren laughed with her whole body," he said. "Not just her face. Her shoulders, her knees. She once laughed so hard at Osk's terrible singing that she fell off the ferry into the Corvel river in midwinter and came up swearing like a dockworker and laughing at the same time, which shouldn't be possible." He heard his own voice change—loosening, warming—and let it. "She was the one who taught me to read the water. She said rivers were just roads that couldn't lie. She wanted—" His throat worked. "She wanted to see the sea. We lived our whole lives in a valley with a river running through it, and she used to say, someday we'll follow it all the way down and see what it becomes. She never got to find out."

"What does it become?" Astrid asked softly.

"I found out. Two years ago I followed a river south until it gave up being a river." He smiled, and it hurt, and it helped. "It becomes enormous. It becomes the sky with salt in it. She would have loved it so much she'd have been unbearable about it."

Astrid laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and then pressed her sleeve to her eyes and pretended she hadn't. "When I open Vell," she said, "if I open Vell—"

"When," Tam said.

"—when I open Vell, I'll take you to the sea. That's a promise. Officially. Out loud, in front of witnesses." She gestured at the owl. "Him."

"He's a poor witness. He sleeps on the job."

"He's the best we've got."

Something in Tam's chest unclenched—a knot he'd been carrying so long he'd mistaken it for his own ribcage. He looked at this girl with her borrowed cloak and her impossible map and her grandmother's blood singing quietly against her ribs, and he thought: *Maren would have liked her. Maren would have liked her enormously and been completely unbearable about it.*

"All right," he said. "Sea. Witnessed. Now sleep. We move before dawn—the map will want us at the drowned village by tomorrow night, and the Warden travels faster than either of us."

Astrid settled back against the oak, pulling the cloak around her. Then, drowsily, half-gone already: "Tam? Why did you really agree to guide me? The real reason."

He watched the fire. Thought about a girl falling into a winter river, laughing. Thought about shoes placed neatly on a path.

"Because you're the first door I've seen open in eleven years," he said. "And I intend to hold it."

But she was already asleep, and the map against her chest glowed faintly through the leather, warm as a sleeping bird, and far away across the dark hills something old and guilty lifted its many borrowed faces and turned toward the light.
