## 9. The Wayhouse of Small Mercies

The wayhouse appeared the way good things had begun appearing to Astrid lately: suddenly, and when she least believed in them.

One moment they were trudging through a drizzle that Tam insisted was "a light mist" and Astrid insisted was a personal insult from the sky, and the next there was a glow between two hills — lamplight, woodsmoke, and a smell so aggressively of onions and roasting meat that Astrid's stomach made a sound like a small animal being stepped on.

"That's Sefa's," Tam said. He said it the way other people said *sanctuary* or *home* or *the one place I would die before leading the Warden to*, which, knowing Tam, might have been all three at once.

"It looks like a barn."

"It is a barn. It used to be a barn. Sefa won it in a card game and never saw any reason to change what it fundamentally was." He paused at the door, rain running off his hood, and gave her a look she was beginning to recognize as his version of a long speech. "Rules. You eat what's put in front of you. You thank her for it even if it's terrible. It will not be terrible. And if anyone asks about your map—" he tapped his own chest where, beneath his coat, Astrid knew the sigil-marked leather case sat warm against her ribs like a second heartbeat— "you lie badly, because you're a bad liar, and Sefa will respect the effort."

"I'm not a *bad* liar."

"You told the ferry inspector last week you were my wife."

"You told him *first*!"

"And I committed to it. That's the difference." He pushed open the door.

Heat rolled out to meet them, thick with steam and rosemary and something frying. The room inside was enormous and chaotic — the barn's original beams still overhead, hung with drying herbs and copper pots and at least one fishing net full of onions — and crammed with tables at angles that suggested no carpenter had ever been consulted, only need. A dozen-odd people were eating, arguing, mending clothes, and in one corner an old man was teaching a girl of perhaps ten to play a fiddle, both of them producing sounds that could not strictly be called music but were being enjoyed immensely anyway.

And behind the longest table, stirring a pot nearly as tall as herself, stood a broad woman with iron-gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be an actual fork, sleeves rolled to the shoulder, forearms like dock rope.

She looked up. She looked at Tam. Her wooden spoon did not stop moving.

"Well," she said. "The river finally spat you back out. You look like something drowned twice on purpose."

"Sefa."

"Don't 'Sefa' me. Three years, Tam. Three years, and not one letter, not one visit, not so much as a rumor of you dead, which is the *least* I'd expect—"

"There were circumstances."

"There are always circumstances with you. Sit down before I remember I'm angry." Her eyes slid to Astrid, sharp and dark and taking inventory — boots, cloak, hollow cheeks, the way Astrid instinctively half-turned to keep her left side, map-side, toward the wall. Whatever Sefa concluded, she kept it behind her teeth. "And who's this? Another stray?"

"This is Astrid."

"Astrid what?"

"Just Astrid," Astrid said, and heard how thin it sounded, and hated it.

Sefa studied her for a moment longer. Then she jerked her chin toward an empty stool. "Just-Astrid sits. Just-Astrid eats. Nobody in my house explains themselves until they've had soup. House rule." She turned back to her pot. "Wren! Two more bowls, and if you've eaten the crusts off the bread basket again I'll have your fingers instead."

A wiry boy of maybe fifteen materialized beside them as if he'd been folded into the air waiting. He grinned at Tam with unmistakable delight and at Astrid with unmistakable curiosity. "You're the wife, then," he said.

"The *what*—"

"He tells everyone," Wren confided. "There was a tax collector at the ford bridge, and Tam panicked, and now there's a ledger somewhere that says he's married. He keeps doing it. Last month we were sisters."

"We are *not* discussing this," Tam said, with feeling, and went to sit down.

Astrid followed, and the soup arrived, and the first spoonful of it made her stop breathing for a moment.

It was nothing. It was barley and onion and some smoked fish and herbs she could name and three she couldn't. Mistress Odile had fed her well enough, in the way Odile did everything — competently, precisely, without waste. But this was different. This was soup made by someone who wanted, specifically and personally, for whoever ate it to feel less cold. Astrid put her face close to the bowl and discovered, to her horror, that her eyes were stinging.

"Steam," she said quickly.

"Mm," said Sefa, passing behind her, and set a heel of bread on the table without comment, and touched Astrid's shoulder once, briefly, on her way by.

* * *

After supper — and Astrid learned that at Sefa's, supper was not a meal but a weather system that moved through the whole room, pulling everyone in — the work began.

Because there was work. Astrid had assumed a wayhouse meant rest, but within an hour of the bowls being cleared she was hauling water, Wren was arguing with the old fiddler about whether a broken chair leg should be pegged or glued, and a huge quiet man called Brannic was carrying an entire pig carcass over one shoulder as casually as a scarf.

"We all pay rent," Wren explained, handing Astrid an armful of kindling. "Sefa doesn't take coin. Well — she takes coin, but only from rich merchants who don't know better, and then she spends it on us. Everyone else pays in labor or stories. Stories are worth more."

"What do you pay?"

Wren's grin flickered into something older. "I fix things. Anything with a hinge or a string or a leak. Found me sleeping in her woodshed winter before last, ate half a pie off the sill, thought she'd skin me." He shrugged. "She hired me instead. Said thieves who liked her cooking clearly had taste and just needed direction."

"And Brannic?"

"Brannic doesn't talk much. Carries things. Used to be a soldier somewhere east; doesn't say where. Pays his rent by making sure nobody ever has a reason to be afraid in this house." Wren glanced across the room at the big man, who was at that moment patiently holding a lantern while the little girl from the fiddle lesson showed him, with great seriousness, a drawing of a cat. "Works out pretty well, I think."

Later, when the fire had burned down to a comfortable roar and most of the strays had drifted up to lofts and pallets, Astrid found herself at the hearth beside Sefa, who was darning a sock the size of a flour sack and radiating the particular contentment of a woman whose kitchen was finally, blessedly empty of people demanding things from it.

"You're staring at my mending," Sefa said.

"I'm staring at the room." Astrid wrapped her hands around a cup of cider someone had pressed on her without asking. "Everyone in it came from somewhere else, didn't they."

"Everyone everywhere came from somewhere else. Most people just pretend otherwise harder." Sefa bit her thread. "But yes. Wren ran from the toll-towns. Brannic from whatever it is Brannic ran from. Old Pietro lost his village to a flood and his wife to grief and decided the second loss was the one he could do something about, so now he teaches every child under this roof the fiddle whether they want it or not. Little Maren there — the cat artist — her mother left her on our step with a note and a turnip. A *turnip*. As if that was currency." She snorted. "We ate the turnip. We kept the girl."

"Why?" The question came out of Astrid before she could dress it up. "Why take all of you? There's no profit in it. There's no sense in it."

Sefa looked at her then, really looked, the way she had at the door — that inventory-taking stare that missed nothing, including, Astrid suspected, the shape of the leather case under her coat.

"When I was younger than you," Sefa said, "someone fed me when I had nothing to trade for it. Didn't ask why I was hungry. Didn't ask what I'd done. Just put the bowl down and said, 'Eat first. Explanations after.'" She tied off her thread with a snap. "I've been paying that debt forward for thirty years and I've barely made a dent in it. That's the trouble with kindness. It compounds."

Tam had gone quiet in the corner, Astrid noticed. Quiet in the specific way of a man hearing a story he already knew the ending to.

* * *

She woke in the night, as she had woken every night since the ford, with her hand already at her chest checking the map.

But this time the room around her was not the ditch or the ferry deck or the cold. This time it was a loft above a warm kitchen, smelling of apples stored in straw, and she could hear — faintly, through the floorboards — Sefa below, banking the fire, humming something tuneless and unhurried. Somewhere a floor creaked under Brannic's rounds. Pietro's fiddle case clicked shut. Small sounds. House sounds. The sound of a place keeping itself alive on purpose.

In Vell, nights had sounded like this too, Astrid realized. Crickets, wind, Odile's kettle ticking as it cooled. She had loved those nights. She had thought she was happy in them.

But she had never once lain awake in Vell listening to the sounds of the house and felt *included* in them. She had always been listening from outside — the apprentice in the attic room, useful and invisible, memorizing maps of places she'd never see. Here, in a stranger's loft, in a stranger's house, she lay listening to strangers breathe and move and hum, and felt — absurdly, dangerously — like part of the sound.

*This is what I was starving for,* she thought, and the thought frightened her more than the Warden did, because you could run from a hunter. You couldn't run from wanting this.

The map shifted against her ribs. Not warming, exactly. Not yet. But aware, the way a dog is aware of its name being spoken in another room.

*Not yet,* she told it. *Let me have tonight.*

* * *

Morning came with rain again, real rain this time, drumming on the barn roof, and with breakfast, and with Sefa standing at the table with her arms crossed wearing the expression of a woman about to ruin everything by being right.

"You're leaving today," she said. Not a question.

Tam set down his cup. "Sefa—"

"Don't. Don't 'Sefa' me twice in one lifetime, it wears grooves." She looked at Astrid. "You slept four hours. You checked under your shirt six times before the bread was even buttered. You flinched when Wren dropped a pan. And my boy Tam here has that look he gets when he's decided to walk into something that killed him once already." She sighed, a great theatrical sigh that fooled no one, least of all herself. "So. You're leaving today."

"Yes," Astrid said.

"Good." Sefa turned back to the stove. "Hate a liar at my table worse than a fool. Eat your eggs."

They ate their eggs. Then Sefa packed them food — real food, wrapped in cloth, enough for days — and pressed a small clay jar into Astrid's hands. "Goose fat and comfrey. For blisters. And a sprig of rowan over the door of wherever you sleep, and if anything with too many shadows asks to come in, you tell it Sefa sent you and watch its face fall." She grunted. "Doesn't work. But it annoys them, and annoying evil is a service."

At the door, the whole strange household gathered to see them off, which seemed to Astrid wildly disproportionate — they'd known her one night — but nobody else seemed to find it odd. Wren shook her hand solemnly and slipped something into her pocket that turned out later to be a bent nail and a note reading *for luck, it worked for me.* Pietro played them out with a tune that had almost stopped being a threat to public safety. Maren gave Astrid the drawing of the cat. Brannic simply nodded at her, once, the way mountains nod.

Then Sefa took Astrid's chin in one strong hand and tilted her face up.

"One night," she said quietly, so only Astrid could hear. "That's all it took for you to stop looking over your shoulder like the world owed you a beating. Imagine what you'd be with a month. With a year. With a *life* of it." Her grip was gentle and her eyes were not gentle at all. "Whatever you're carrying, girl — and I know you're carrying something, I can smell trouble the way I smell burnt onions — whatever it is, you come back through that door after. You hear me? Strays who eat at my table are mine. That's not affection. That's law."

Astrid's throat was too tight for words. She nodded.

"Good." Sefa released her, loud again instantly. "Now get off my doorstep, the pair of you, you're letting the heat out. And Tam—" she pointed the fork from her hair at him like a scepter— "*letters*. Actual letters. I know you can write, I taught you."

The rain had softened to mist as they walked out between the hills. At the crest, Astrid turned back. The wayhouse glowed against the gray, small and stubborn, a lit window in a landscape that had spent weeks teaching her that windows were things other people had.

"Three years," she said. "You stayed away three years from people like that?"

"They deserved better than what was following me." Tam didn't look back. His jaw was set in the particular way it got when he was carrying something heavy and refusing to put it down. "You'll understand eventually. Everything we love, the Warden finds a use for."

He said it lightly. He said it the way people say true things when they're trying to make them weigh less.

Behind them, the wayhouse light held steady in the mist. Against Astrid's chest, beneath Sefa's bread and the goose-fat jar and the bent nail for luck, the map had begun — very softly, very patiently, like a heart learning to beat faster — to warm.

By nightfall, she knew, it would be hot.

By nightfall, she suspected, they would no longer be alone on the road.
