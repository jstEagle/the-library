## 11. Salt and Second Chances

Sefa had learned a long time ago that grief traveled better with bread in it.

That was why she was kneading dough at dawn on the shingle beach at Gullwrack, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, flour ghosting her forearms, while the survivors of the Wayhouse of Small Mercies picked their way down the cliff path behind her like a funeral procession that had gotten lost on the way to the churchyard. The wayhouse was ash and smoke-smell and a scorch mark shaped like a hand across the doorframe. The Warden had seen to that.

Sefa had built that wayhouse with her own two hands. Well — her hands, Tam's back, and a roof borrowed from a man who'd owed her a debt since the winter of the drowned nets. She had fed forty years of hungry people under that roof. She had watched it burn from the ridge with a sack of onions over her shoulder and had not cried, because crying was for people who hadn't already made their peace with losing things.

She punched the dough harder than it deserved.

"You'll kill it," said Pip, who had appeared at her elbow the way he appeared everywhere, silent as a cat and twice as judgmental. He was fourteen, all elbows and opinions, one of the strays she'd collected over the years. His whole family had walked out of a sealed valley called Merrow's Hollow when he was six, and he didn't remember any of it except that his mother had hummed while she cooked, which was why he always hung around Sefa's kitchen pretending not to listen.

"The dough is dead already," Sefa said. "Everything is dead. We're just waiting for the rest of us to notice."

"That's very inspiring. I feel better already."

"Go fetch me the good salt from the boat."

"We don't have a boat."

"We will by noon," Sefa said, and did not explain, because explaining was for after, and before was for kneading.

* * *

The company assembled around her driftwood fire as the sun came up properly: Tam, sitting slightly apart as always, mending a net he had no use for because mending was how he prayed; Odile, the hedge-witch, upright and terrible and gray-faced, holding a cup of tea she hadn't touched; the two sisters, Brin and Maren, who'd come off the mountain road three days before the fire and hadn't stopped talking since, though today even they were quiet; old Hollis, who was mostly deaf and mostly asleep and entirely loyal; and Pip, back with the salt, glaring at everyone on principle.

And Astrid Joubert, sitting at the edge of the circle with the burned map case in her lap, looking like a girl who had watched her whole world learn a new shape overnight and hadn't decided yet whether she liked it.

Sefa knew that look. She had worn it herself, once, on a dock very much like this one, in a country whose name she still couldn't say without her chest tightening.

"Right," she said, and clapped flour off her hands. "Breakfast first. Then business. Nobody makes good decisions hungry, and nobody makes *any* decisions around Odile when she's doing that face."

"I am not doing a face," said Odile.

"You are doing the face you do when you're composing an apology so long it could be read aloud at a coronation. Eat your egg."

They ate. It was good eggs, because Sefa did not know how to make bad ones. Slowly the circle loosened — Maren started a whispered argument with Brin about whether gulls were technically seabirds or sky-rats, Hollis woke up and demanded to be told what he'd missed, and Pip laughed once, sharp and surprised, at something Tam said that wasn't even meant to be funny. That was the trick of feeding people, Sefa thought. You couldn't fix them. But you could get them to a place where laughing was possible again, and then they'd do the rest themselves.

When the plates were empty, she stood, brushed off her apron, and looked at Astrid.

"Come walk with me," she said. "The rest of you — wash up. Yes, all of you. Yes, Hollis, you too, especially you."

* * *

The tide was going out, leaving the sand dark and firm and printed with the tracks of every bird on the coast. Sefa walked until the camp was a smudge behind them and the only sound was water doing its ancient, patient work against the stones.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said, "and you're going to let me finish it, because if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve, and I've been losing my nerve for thirty years and I'm tired of the taste of it."

Astrid said nothing. Good girl. She'd learned listening in a valley where listening was survival.

"I wasn't born here," Sefa said. "Nobody out here was born anywhere interesting — that's rather the point of us. I was born in a sealed place called Vessamy. Little country, big hedges, same as yours. My mother was a keykeeper. Not a founder, nothing grand — the founders made twelve locks and then, being practical women, they made keys and gave them to ordinary people to hold. Bakers. Ferrymen. Cooks." She smiled without much humor in it. "My mother held hers inside a wooden spoon. Passed down through nine generations of Vessamy kitchens. You never stirred anything with it. You never let anyone borrow it. You kept it oiled and you kept it secret and when your hands got too old you put it into your daughter's hands and you said, *this is heavier than it looks.*"

Astrid's hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the map case at her chest. Sefa nodded at it.

"Yes," she said. "You understand already. That's the trouble with you Jouberts. Understanding comes too easy and too late."

"What happened?" Astrid asked quietly.

"My mother died when I was nineteen. Fever, quick and ugly. And there I was with a spoon full of somebody else's courage and a whole life I hadn't asked for, and a young man named Aldous who had a boat and a laugh like a cart going downhill, and I—" Sefa stopped. Looked at the water. Made herself go on. "I chose him. Or I chose the easier thing and called it him, which is what we do. I took the spoon to the seal-wardens of Vessamy and I gave it up. Voluntarily. They thanked me. They gave me a little ceremony and a pension and told me I was wise. And then they sealed my valley a little tighter, because a returned key is a stronger lock than a lost one, and I walked out the gate feeling *light*, Astrid. Light as air. And I have spent forty years learning that what I felt wasn't lightness. It was the absence of weight. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

The waves came in. The waves went out. Somewhere behind them, faintly, Maren was winning the argument about gulls.

"Vessamy is still sealed," Sefa said. "Still locked. Still small. Everyone I grew up with is still in there, living their small lives, safe as houses, and every year the walls are a little thicker and the magic outside can't reach them and they can't reach out, and it's *fine*. It's fine. It's comfortable. It's exactly what I chose." She turned to look at Astrid straight on. "I opened a wayhouse afterward. Did you ever wonder why? A cook, feeding strays of every kind, in the one place between all the sealed countries where the roads tangle together. I told myself it was charity. It wasn't. It was penance. Every stray I fed was a person who'd done the brave thing I didn't. I filled my table with other people's courage because mine had gone stale in my mouth, and I called it hospitality."

"Sefa—"

"No. Let me finish, girl, I told you." She held up a floured hand. "Here is the part that matters. Last night, when the Warden came wearing faces, it wore Aldous's face at me. It does its homework, that creature. And do you know what I felt? Not guilt. I've lived with the guilt so long it's furniture. What I felt was *relief* that it was only a face, because if it had been the real Aldous, standing there asking me why, I would have had no answer that wasn't shameful." She breathed out. "So. Business."

She reached into her apron pocket and pulled out a ring of iron keys, old and salt-eaten, and pressed them into Astrid's palm.

"The boat's called the *Second Chance*, because I bought her with the pension they gave me for cowardice and I have a sense of humor about these things, or I did once. She's moored in the cove past the black rocks. She leaks a little at the stern seam and lies about it. Tam knows her hull better than I do — he'll patch her. Pip is coming with you, and before you argue, remember that I raised him and I know exactly how stubborn he gets when someone tries to leave him behind, and you do not have time for that fight."

Astrid stared at the keys. "Why?"

"Because I won't watch you make my retreat," Sefa said. "I've watched it in the mirror for forty years and once was more than enough. You're going to that door under the mountain, and you're going to do whatever impossible thing the map wants done, and when it's finished — *if* it's finished — you're going to send this boat back to me with news. That's the price. Not payment. News. I want to know how the story ends, and I want to know it from someone who stayed for the last chapter."

Astrid's eyes were bright and wet and furious about being either. "What if I fail?"

"Then send the boat back anyway and I'll say I told you so for eternity, which is its own kind of inheritance." Sefa pulled her into a hug before she could protest — flour and salt and onion-smell, the whole architecture of a kitchen transferred by embrace. "Listen to me. Courage isn't a thing you have. It's a thing that gets handed to you, usually by someone who couldn't use it in time, and your only job is not to drop it. My mother handed me a spoon and I dropped it. Someone's handing you a valley. Don't you dare."

* * *

By noon the *Second Chance* rode at anchor in the cove, patched, provisioned, and arguing with the tide. Tam worked the stern seam in silence, competent and grave. Pip stowed crates with the ferocity of someone proving a point. Odile stood at the waterline looking, for the first time since Sefa had known her, uncertain of her footing — a woman who had spent decades being the wisest person in a small room discovering the room was bigger.

Sefa handed baskets down the side: bread, hard cheese, smoked fish, a jar of honey, and — wrapped in cloth at the bottom of the last basket, where only Astrid would find it — a wooden spoon, plain and old and very well oiled.

It was empty now. Whatever had lived in it had gone home to Vessamy's lock long ago. But some vessels keep the shape of what they carried, and Sefa had found that a spoon that once held courage still stirred a braver pot.

"Cast off before I change my mind," she called, and waved them out past the black rocks, and stood on the shingle long after the sail was a white sliver, kneading nothing, watching the sea take her second chance toward the thirteenth turn.

Behind her, far inland, the horizon over the mountains had begun — very faintly, very wrongly — to glow.
