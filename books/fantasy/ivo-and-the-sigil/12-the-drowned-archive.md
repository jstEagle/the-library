## 12. The Drowned Archive

The sea took the library a long time ago, and never quite finished the job.

We saw it first at dawn, from Sefa's boat: a black shape breaking the swell like the back of some enormous sleeping animal, gulls wheeling around it in slow, indignant circles. As we drew closer, I understood what I was looking at and my breath caught. It was a tower. Half of it, anyway—the upper floors sheared away or swallowed, but the lower stories still standing, drowned to their second-floor windows, windows that stared out at us full of green water and light.

"Caedmon's Rest," Tam said, shipping the oars. His voice had gone quiet in the way it did when he was handling something fragile. "It was a library once. Before Corvel sealed. Before most things sealed."

"It's underwater," I said.

"Most true things are hard to reach." He began uncoiling rope with quick, economical movements. "That's rather the point of them."

Sefa snorted from the stern, where she'd appointed herself captain on the grounds that it was her boat and none of us were going to argue with a woman who could fillet a fish and your self-esteem with the same knife. "The archive's dry," she said. "Air pocket. The old builders made sure of it—founders' work doesn't drown easy. You'll want to go down through the bell tower. There's a stair."

"You've been here before," I said.

Sefa's hands stilled on the tiller. For a moment she looked out at the drowned tower with an expression I couldn't read—grief, maybe, or its close cousin, regret.

"Once," she said. "A lifetime ago. When I still thought keys were things you could hold." She jerked her chin at the water. "Get in before I change my mind about lending you my morning. The tide won't wait for heroes, and neither will I."

* * *

The descent was cold in a way that got into the bones and made itself comfortable.

Tam went first, one hand on the guide-rope, feeling for the stair treads slick with weed. I followed, the map case strapped tight against my chest, where it had been warming steadily all night—a low, patient heat, like a cat that had decided I belonged to it. Below us the water glowed faintly, lit from within by something that wasn't quite light. Magic, I thought, and the word didn't frighten me the way it would have a month ago, when I'd been a hedge-witch's girl mending charms that never held because magic couldn't follow me past the ridge. Now it was just weather. Strange weather. Weather with opinions.

The stair spiraled down through the flooded floors, past shelves that rose out of the green like coral reefs, every book fused into a single stone mass, spines and pages pressed together into something new. I trailed my fingers along one as I passed. It was warm.

"They didn't burn them," I said softly. "They turned them into something else."

"The founders' work," Tam said over his shoulder. "Everything they touched, they changed. Remember that."

Then his foot found the last tread, and we stepped off the stair into air.

The dry floor opened around us like the inside of a lung. It was a great round chamber, ceiling ribbed with stone arches, and every arch held a window—and every window held sea. Light came down through forty feet of green water in wavering columns, moving across the floor like slow searchers looking for something they'd misplaced. Dust hung in those columns, golden and unhurried. In the center of the room stood a single desk, and on the desk lay a single book, open, waiting, as though the librarian had only stepped out for a moment and might return any minute, slightly annoyed, to find us touching things.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me high and startled, echoing off the stone.

"What?" Tam asked, mouth twitching.

"It's been drowning for—I don't know, centuries? And someone left a book out."

"Some habits survive anything," he said. "Come on. We're not here for the desk."

* * *

The records were kept below, behind a door carved with twelve symbols, and the door knew us before we touched it.

I felt it happen—the map against my chest flaring warm, then hot, then almost painfully bright through the leather—and when I pulled it out, the sigil on the old hide was glowing, and so, answering, were three of the twelve carvings on the door. A moon. A river. And a third symbol, a small closed circle with a line drawn through it, that I had seen before: burned into Odile's letters, stamped on the courier's map case, inked into the journal in a hand that shook.

"That's yours," Tam said quietly. "That's Joubert work."

The door swung inward without a sound.

Inside was a round room lined with niches, and in each niche rested objects under glass—or what had once been glass, now clouded to sea-pearl opacity. Twelve niches. Twelve founders. I walked them slowly, reading the little brass plates beneath each, and with every step the warmth in my chest grew until I felt lit from inside, a lantern walking through the dark.

A ring, worn smooth. A tuning fork of white metal. A child's wooden horse, which seemed an odd thing to enshrine until I read the plate: *Maren of the Ninth Turn. She gave up her own childhood so that others might keep theirs.* A pair of spectacles. A lock of hair braided with wire. An empty niche, its plate scratched nearly illegible—only *the Twelfth* remained, and I thought of Odile burning letters in the dark of her kitchen, and my chest went tight.

And then the eleventh niche, and the plate that stopped my heart.

*Mireille Joubert, of the Eleventh Turn.*

My grandmother. My grandmother, whom no one in Vell had ever named aloud, whose name I hadn't even known to ask after, because asking questions in Vell was like digging in a garden that had been salted—you learned early not to bother. She stood there in brass letters, patient as the tide, waiting two hundred years for someone to come down and read her.

"She was real," I whispered.

Tam had gone very still beside me. "Astrid—"

"She was one of them. One of the twelve. She built the prison." My voice cracked on the word *prison*, and something underneath it cracked too, because I realized I didn't know whether I meant Vell or myself. "She helped seal my own home. She helped seal *me*."

"Don't stop there," Tam said. "Read the rest."

Beneath the plate, in a niche built deeper than the others, lay a document. Not under glass. Loose. Deliberately loose, I understood suddenly, deliberately accessible, placed where any hand of Joubert blood might find it. The paper was heavy and soft with age, and the writing on it was fine and fierce, slanting downhill like something written fast, by someone who feared interruption.

I read it aloud, because some words are too large to hold silently.

*"To whoever comes after—and you will have my eyes, or you would not be reading this—"*

I stopped. My hands were shaking.

"Your eyes," Tam said softly. "Green?"

"How did you—"

"Lucky guess," he said, not smiling at all. "Go on."

*"To whoever comes after: I write this on the last night, with the seal half-made and my sisters and brothers already forgetting why we wept while we worked. They will tell you we built Vell to imprison something evil. Some of us believed that. I no longer do. What we sealed was magic itself, wild and hungry, yes—but also singing. We were afraid of what it might devour, so we locked away everything it might have fed. I have watched the valley since. The people there are safe. They are also starving and do not know it.*

*I could not give back what we took. But I could refuse to make the theft permanent. So hear me, heir: the other eleven hid their keys in objects, and objects can be found, stolen, guarded, lost. I hid mine in the only vault the Warden cannot watch and time cannot rust. I hid it in a bloodline. I hid it in a person. I hid it in you—not you, whoever-you-are, but the line, the long red thread, the daughter of daughters, until one of you stood at the door with enough love for the valley to stay and enough grief for it to leave.*

*You are not the thief's descendant. You are the restitution. Do not let the guilty dead tell you who you are. Not even me.*

*Especially not me.*

*—M.J., Eleventh Turn"*

The last column of light from the high windows had crawled all the way across the floor and come to rest at my feet, as if the sea itself wanted to read over my shoulder.

I stood there a long time.

I had come down into this drowned place a runaway. That was the whole story, the simple story, the one I'd told myself crossing the ridge with bread and a borrowed cloak: a girl fleeing a warden in a witch's kitchen, running because staying had become unbearable. Runaways run *from*. It had been a comfort, that grammar. Guiltless, almost. Things chase you; you are not required to explain yourself; you simply keep your legs moving and your heart shut.

But you couldn't stand in front of your grandmother's handwriting and remain a runaway. Runaways don't inherit. Heirs do—and inheritance, I understood now, looking at the fierce slanting script, was not a gift at all. It was a debt with your name already written on it, signed two hundred years before you were born, by a woman who had loved her valley enough to steal its future and loved it still more, later, too late, to leave a way to give it back.

She'd hidden the key in her descendants the way you press a seed into soil: trusting the dark, trusting the waiting, trusting that someday something would be stubborn enough to grow.

The map against my chest was no longer warm. It was *alive* now—pulsing gently, in time, I realized with a start, with my own heartbeat. It had always known what I was. It had been calling me the way a bell calls, quietly, patiently, through every ordinary day I'd spent memorizing maps of places I'd never see. Those weren't daydreams. They were instructions. I had been rehearsing my own inheritance my whole life without knowing it.

"Astrid." Tam's voice was careful, the way you speak near a frightened animal. "What is it?"

I wiped my face with the heel of my hand and discovered I was smiling, which felt strange against the wet. "I'm not stealing anything," I said. "Or escaping anything. I'm collecting a debt. From the dead. To the living." I folded Mireille Joubert's letter with the same care I'd use on a charm that had to hold. "There's a difference. I think it matters."

"It does," Tam said. Something moved behind his eyes—hope, maybe, or its dangerous cousin. "Because if you're an heir and not a fugitive, then what happens to Vell when you open it—"

The map flared.

Not warmly this time. Hard—a white heat through leather that made me gasp and fumble the case open. Inside, the inked coastline of Vell shimmered, and along its border the sigil-line flickered, guttered—

—and one of the thirteen waypoint marks went dark. Snuffed out, neat as a candle pinched between wet fingers.

Twelve left.

Tam stared at it. Then at me. Then at the letter in my hands, as if it might explain.

"The waypoints are sealing themselves," he said slowly. "Closing early. The thirteenth turn isn't waiting for the full moon anymore." He looked up, and for the first time since I'd known him, the laconic ferryman sounded genuinely afraid. "Astrid—something knows you're coming. And it's closing the road ahead of you."
