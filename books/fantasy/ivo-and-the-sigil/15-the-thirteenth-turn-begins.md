## 15. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The moon changed on a Tuesday, which felt insultingly ordinary.

Astrid knew it had happened because the map told her so. She'd spread it across the table in Sefa's galley—the boat rocked at anchor off a fishing village called Brackwater, which appeared on no chart and was, Tam said, "the sort of place that prefers it that way"—and as the last sliver of the old moon slipped below the horizon, the first waypoint inked itself shut.

It didn't burn or bleed or do anything dramatic. It simply closed, like an eye deciding it had seen enough: a small circle of script near the map's western edge going dark, then smooth, then gone entirely, leaving parchment where a path had been.

"Was that—" Sefa began.

"The mill at Harrow's Bend," Astrid said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The first waypoint. Where the courier would have stopped." She pressed her thumb against the blank spot, half expecting it to open under her touch the way the others did when she offered her blood. It stayed stubbornly, permanently paper.

Tam leaned over the table, squinting. He had a way of looking at sigil-work that made Astrid feel like a child peering over a master's shoulder, though he always denied being any kind of master. "Twelve waypoints left," he said. "And one turn of the moon to walk them."

"That's fine," Sefa said briskly, already reaching for her kettle. "That's *fine*. We've done worse with less. I once crossed the Saltmarsh in a rowboat held together with prayer and gravy fat."

"You've never crossed the Saltmarsh."

"I've *nearly* crossed the Saltmarsh, which is nearly the same thing and considerably more instructive." She thumped three cups down. "Sit. Drink. Panic after tea, in that order. It's the only civilized way."

So they drank tea while the landscape behind them quietly stopped existing, and Astrid found that panic, properly scheduled, could wait its turn.

* * *

They made landfall before dawn and walked.

The country east of Brackwater was the kind that maps gave up on—rolling moorland stitched with sheep tracks, stone walls that wandered off mid-sentence, villages that consisted of a chapel, a well, and an opinion. Under any other sky it would have been pleasant walking. But the sky was doing something wrong.

"The light," Odile said, on the second morning. She'd come up beside Astrid without sound, the way she always moved, sixty years of hedge-craft worn into her bones like a riverbed. "Look at the shadows."

Astrid looked. The shadows were fine, actually. Long, silvered, early-morning soft. Then she understood what Odile meant: they were all leaning the same direction. Not away from the sun. Toward the ridge of hills ahead, the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone, the way Astrid herself had once leaned toward every map she wasn't allowed to own.

"Everything's being pulled," she said.

"Or pushed," Odile murmured. "From behind."

By noon they saw what she meant. They crested a hill and looked back the way they'd come, and the road they'd walked that morning was gone. Not washed out, not blocked—*gone*, meadow running unbroken from hillfoot to hillfoot, without so much as a wheel rut to show where wheels had rolled. A shepherd stood in the middle of it, frowning at his flock, who were refusing to cross a patch of empty grass for reasons only sheep understood.

"It erases behind us," Tam said quietly. "Not just roads. Look at his face."

The shepherd wasn't confused, exactly. He was *unbothered*, in the terrible way of people who have forgotten something so thoroughly they don't know there's a hole where it used to be. When Astrid asked him about the road, he smiled kindly and said there hadn't been a road through here in years, miss, and weren't the moor lovely this time of year, and would she like to buy some cheese.

She bought the cheese. It seemed cruel not to.

"We're walking on the inside of a closing hand," Odile said that night, over the fire. She said it gently, the way she delivered all her worst news, wrapped in something almost like tenderness. "The seal is drawing itself tight, Astrid. Every waypoint you pass is one the Warden can't use to catch you—but it's also one less piece of world. By the thirteenth turn, everything between here and Vell will be smooth as an egg. No roads. No villages. Possibly no *us*, if we're still standing on the wrong side of it when it finishes."

"Then we don't stand on the wrong side," Astrid said.

Sefa snorted into her stew. "Listen to her. Three weeks ago she apologized to a door for opening it."

"I did *not* apologize to a door."

"You curtsied at a gate in Corvel. I watched you do it."

"That was *politeness*—"

"It was groveling at architecture," Sefa said fondly, "and it was very sweet, and look at you now, threatening geography." She pointed her spoon at Astrid. "Good. Keep threatening it. That's the spirit that gets boats home."

Astrid laughed despite everything, and then, when the fire had burned low and the others slept, she took out the map and looked at the eleven remaining waypoints, each one a small knot of glowing script, and thought about how the courier must have felt, carrying this thing across a country that was eating itself. *Hurry*, the map seemed to say, in the patient voice of things that never hurry themselves but expect everyone else to.

* * *

The trouble with racing a sealing landscape, Astrid discovered, is that the landscape cheats.

The third waypoint was a standing stone on a hill called, according to the map, the Widow's Tooth—and the map insisted the path to it ran along the river valley, two days' easy walking. Except that by the time they reached the valley floor, the river had decided to be somewhere else. They found it a mile west, lying in a bed it had clearly never occupied, fish blinking in the unfamiliar current with what Astrid swore was embarrassment.

"Rivers don't *move*," she said.

"This one's been sealed-adjacent for a month," Tam said. "Seals leak. Everything gets... suggestible." He crouched at the water's edge and studied it the way he studied sigils, head tilted, lips moving. "It's not flowing downhill anymore. It's flowing toward the ridge. Same as the shadows."

"So we follow the river?"

"We follow the river."

They followed the river for a day and a half through country that grew stranger with every mile. Hedgerows bloomed out of season and then, behind them, un-bloomed; birds flew in perfect silence, as if sound itself were being skimmed off the land like cream. Once, passing a farmstead, Astrid waved to a woman stacking wood, and the woman waved back and then frowned, slowly, like someone trying to remember why her arm had done that—and by the time they'd walked fifty paces, when Astrid glanced back, the woman was staring at the middle distance with the mild face of someone who had never seen anyone at all.

"She'll remember tonight," Odile said softly. "In dreams, maybe. Or she won't. Both are mercies, in their way."

"Do you believe that?" Astrid asked. "Really?"

Odile was quiet for a long moment. "I believe I need to," she said, "and that needing a thing true does not make it so, and that I am too old to tell the difference anymore. Walk, love. We're losing the light."

On the evening of the second day they reached the Widow's Tooth, and Astrid opened the waypoint with a drop of blood and a word her grandmother had written into her blood before either of them existed, and the stone hummed like a struck bell, and for one glorious moment the whole valley *remembered itself*—the river snapping back toward its old bed with a roar, birdsong crashing in like a wave, the hedges blazing with impossible roses—and then the moment passed, moving on ahead of them like weather, and the country behind went quiet again.

But ahead, Astrid noticed, it stayed loud.

"The seal isn't erasing everything," she said, staring down the valley. "Just... behind us. Along our wake."

"Because you're not fleeing the seal," Tam said slowly. "You're *carrying* it. Or part of it. The thirteenth key." He looked at her with an expression she couldn't read—awe and fear sharing a chair. "Wherever you stand, the seal has to stay... unfinished. Waiting to see what you'll write."

Astrid looked at her own hands, ordinary hands, herb-chapped hands, hands that had spent nineteen years mending charms that never quite worked. "No pressure," she said weakly, and Sefa laughed so hard she had to sit down on the standing stone.

* * *

The fifth waypoint nearly killed them.

It stood in a marsh called the Drowned Orchard, where apple trees rose from black water, their branches heavy with fruit that had ripened underwater and gone strange—pale, luminous, faintly wrong. The map showed a causeway. The causeway, when they found it, was dissolving even as they watched, stones sinking into the peat with soft sighs, the seal tidying it away like a maid clearing a table.

"We can't cross that," Sefa said flatly. "Not with the boat gear, not with Odile's hip, not in a month of Sundays."

"Then we swim the gear across and carry Odile," Astrid said.

"I will do no such—"

"You will," Astrid said, and heard, to her surprise, the voice of someone who expected to be obeyed. It sounded a little like Odile. It sounded, more than a little, like the courier at the ford, spending his last breath on her name. "Nobody gets left behind because the ground is being rude. We go together or we find another way together."

There was a silence. Then Tam started laughing—a real laugh, rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in years.

"What?" Astrid demanded.

"Nothing. It's just—you sound like my sister." His face did something complicated and settled. "She used to say exactly that. *Together or another way.* Drove everyone mad." He shrugged off his coat and started rolling his trousers. "Right. Another way. There's a boat under that third tree—I can see the hull. Sefa, you're the sailor. Odile, you're cargo, and don't argue, you'll enjoy it. Astrid—"

"Yes?"

"Keep the map dry if it kills you. It's the only thing in this marsh that knows where anything is. Including us."

The crossing took four hours and cost them a tent pole, most of Sefa's dignity, and one boot of Astrid's, claimed by the peat with the enthusiasm of a collector. But they crossed. And when Astrid waded to the waypoint—a stone half-sunk in the shallows, its script dimming like a guttering lamp—and opened it with blood and inheritance, the whole orchard lit up from beneath the water, every drowned apple glowing gold, and for a moment it was the most beautiful place she had ever seen.

Then the light passed on ahead, the dark closed behind, and they stood on the far bank, nine waypoints from home, in a country that now ended thirty miles back at a wall of smooth, unbroken nothing.

"There's no going back now," Odile said quietly. "Even if you wanted to."

Astrid realized she'd been waiting for those words—for the grief of them, the trap of them. But what she felt instead, examining herself honestly in the cold moonlight, was something closer to relief. All her life, Vell had been small and safe and *behind* her, pulling. Now it was simply gone, folded away, and the only direction left was forward, toward a door in a mountain and a choice nobody could make for her.

"No," she said. "I don't suppose I do want to." And she was surprised to find she meant it.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruined chapel, and Astrid sat up late with the map, counting waypoints like a miser counting coins. Nine. Nine knots of light in a sea of closing dark.

Tam sat down beside her without asking, the way he did. For a while neither spoke. The drowned apples glowed faintly across the marsh, like lanterns hung by someone with unusual taste.

"My sister," Tam said at last, "used to count things when she was scared. Stars. Sheep. Steps. Said numbers were the only magic that always behaved." He nodded at the map. "How many?"

"Nine."

"And how many days of moon?"

Astrid did the arithmetic and didn't like it. "Eight. Maybe seven, if the seal hurries."

"Then tomorrow," Tam said, "we stop being careful."

Across the fire, Sefa snored with tremendous commitment. Odile slept sitting upright, hands folded, looking for all the world like a woman napping in her own kitchen instead of a fugitive in a dissolving marsh. Somewhere far behind them, the Warden was surely walking, wearing borrowed faces, unhurried, because it had never once needed to hurry in its long guilt-made life.

Astrid looked at the ninth waypoint, far to the northeast, at the edge of the map where the mountains began and the ink turned restless. And as she watched, the script around it flickered—not dimming, not yet, but *trembling*, like a candle hearing a draft.

"Tam," she said. "Come look at this."

He leaned over. The flicker steadied, then came again, stronger, rhythmic. Almost like breathing. Almost like—

"A heartbeat," Tam said. "Waypoints don't have heartbeats."

"This one does."

They stared at it together, and far off in the dark, beyond the marsh, beyond the erased roads, something in the mountains began—very faintly, very patiently—to knock.
