## 17. Company of the Unsealed

The Warden had worn her mother's face for an hour now.

Astrid had no memory of her mother—only a name on a page in Odile's careful hand and a locket she'd never been allowed to open—but the face was right anyway. That was the cruelest part. The Warden reached into her and found something she hadn't known was there, a shape of cheekbones and a way of smiling with one corner of the mouth, and it wore that shape while it spoke in a voice like warm bread and cold milk.

"Come home, little one," said her mother's face. "The valley is quiet. The valley is safe. You can be small again. You can be nobody again."

"I don't want to be nobody," Astrid said. Her voice cracked on the second word, which annoyed her enormously.

"Everyone wants to be nobody. It's the kindest thing anyone ever gave you." The Warden tilted its head, and her mother's hair fell the way hair falls in dreams. "Your grandmother knew. She built the seal so you would never have to choose. And look what choosing has cost already—a burned wayhouse, scattered friends, a ferryman who will drown for you if you let him."

"That's not cost," Astrid said. "That's company."

The Warden smiled with one corner of its mouth, and it was such a perfect theft of a smile Astrid had never seen that she almost laughed at the absurdity of it. Almost. The penultimate waypoint hummed around them both—a ring of standing stones on a bald hill, each stone carved with a moon-turn, eleven of them dark and dead, the twelfth glowing like a coal wrapped in ash. Beyond the stones, the sky was doing something wrong. The thirteenth moon was rising out of turn, fat and early, dragging the horizon with it.

Then she heard the cart.

It was not a dramatic sound. That was the thing about Sefa—everything about her arrived like a market day. Wheels squeaking, a mule complaining, someone arguing about onions. Astrid turned, and there, cresting the hill behind the stones, came a cook's cart with a sail rigged to it out of pure stubbornness, and standing in the bed of the cart like a general reviewing troops: Sefa, arms crossed, apron still on, flour to the elbows.

"You," Sefa called down, "are impossible to feed properly when you run off. Sit down. All of you. There's stew."

"All of—" Astrid began, and then the cart disgorged them.

Tam first, dropping off the back with his usual economy of motion, as though he'd been planning the dismount since the last village. Behind him, two of Sefa's strays—the twins Pip and Perrin, who claimed to be brothers but disagreed on everything including their names—and old Marta the mender, whose hands shook until they held a needle, at which point they became the steadiest things in any room. And at the rear, wrapped in a traveling cloak that had once been fine and was now merely determined:

Odile.

Astrid's heart did something complicated and unhelpful.

"You're supposed to be scattered," she said. "You scattered beautifully. I watched."

"We regathered," said Pip.

"It's what we do," said Perrin. "We're strays. Strays regather. It's practically our whole nature."

"The Warden burned your home," Astrid said, and her voice went thin. "Because of me. I brought it to you."

Sefa climbed down from the cart, took Astrid by the shoulders, and looked at her the way she looked at a sauce that was about to break—assessing, unimpressed by panic, already deciding how to fix it.

"The Warden burned my kitchen," Sefa said. "My home is people, and people are standing right here being extremely annoying about onions. Those are different things. Now." She released her and raised her voice to address the hilltop at large, the stones, the Warden, the wrong-rising moon, as if all of it were a slightly unruly dinner service. "We are not here to fight. Put that thought down, Tam, I can see you reaching for the boat-hook."

Tam lowered the boat-hook with dignity. "I wasn't."

"He was," said Marta.

"We are here," Sefa continued, "to witness. Which is a fancy word for standing where we can be seen while someone does a brave thing, so they know it counted. I've read about it. Marta read it to me."

* * *

The Warden had gone still among the stones, wearing Astrid's mother's face, watching the cart unload with an expression that might have been puzzlement if puzzles could frown.

"You are not bound to this place," it said. Its voice had shifted—still warm, but less certain of its warmth, a fire discovering drafts. "None of you. Your seals were elsewhere. Corvel. Greywater. The drowned archive keeps its own records; I have read them. You escaped. You owe this valley nothing."

"That's true," Tam said.

He stepped forward, and Astrid realized she had never actually heard him talk this much in one breath. He planted himself between her and the Warden—not blocking, exactly. More like anchoring. Like a post driven into riverbed mud.

"My sister's name was Liss," he said. "Corvel sealed when she was nine and I was twelve. We grew up inside a place that only had four roads and all of them went in circles, and everyone told us that was safety, and we believed it because believing was easier than drowning, which was the other option." He paused. His jaw worked. "She didn't die in the escape. She died after. The Warden of Corvel found us on the water and offered me the same bargain you're offering her—go home, forget, be small. I nearly took it. She stepped in front of the offer instead. So no, I don't owe Vell anything. I owe the idea of Vell. I owe every door that's ever been locked from the outside a very bad evening."

The Warden regarded him. Then, horribly gently, it changed faces.

Liss's face. A girl of perhaps nineteen, river-tanned, laughing at something just off-camera of the world.

Tam did not flinch. Astrid watched him not flinch, and understood that the not-flinching was costing him something enormous, and understood also that he had decided, in advance, to pay it.

"She's not here," he said quietly. "You're wearing a memory with the blood drained out of it. Put her down or I'll put you down, and either way I'm staying."

* * *

Marta went next.

Astrid hadn't expected it. Marta was the oldest of them, the one who dozed through meals and woke only when something needed mending, and she walked to the front of the group with her shawl clutched around her like armor made of wool.

"I was a seal-breaker's daughter," Marta said, in the voice she used for telling children stories—level, unhurried, leaving room for listening. "In Greywater. My father broke our seal when I was young, and the magic came back to the valley all at once, like a flood, because he broke it instead of opening it. It took the orchards. It took my sight in one eye. It took my brother entirely—he walked into the returned magic and became weather, they said. A wind that smelled of him." She folded her hands. "I have spent sixty years being afraid of doors. And then a girl sat at my table at the Wayhouse of Small Mercies and memorized a map with her whole face, the way I used to memorize my father's sigils, and I thought: oh. Oh, there she is. There's the thing I was before I got afraid."

"Marta—" Astrid started.

"Hush, child, I'm witnessing." Marta turned to the Warden, and her clouded eye and her clear eye fixed on it together. "I lost a brother to a broken seal. I will not lose another child to an unbroken one. Whatever you are—you are made of guilt, Sefa says, and guilt always talks loudest when it's losing."

* * *

Pip and Perrin went together, because of course they did.

"We're from Hollowmere," said Pip.

"Don't interrupt me," said Perrin.

"You weren't saying anything yet!"

"I was inhaling meaningfully."

They bickered their way to the front, shoulder to shoulder, and then stopped bickering, which from them was more solemn than silence from anyone else.

"Hollowmere sealed slow," Pip said. "Not all at once. Things just... stopped arriving. New songs. New seeds. New ideas. Everyone got very good at the same twelve jokes."

"We left because we realized we'd both laughed at joke number seven the same way for ten years," said Perrin, "and it wasn't funny anymore, and neither was anything, and we couldn't remember what funny felt like from the outside."

"So we're not here because of grand tragedy," Pip said. "We're here because of joke number seven."

"And because Astrid laughed at ours," Perrin said. "At the wayhouse. At the terrible one about the mule and the tax collector. She laughed like she'd never heard it before. Because she hadn't. Because she grew up somewhere with no jokes coming in." He shrugged, suddenly awkward, suddenly young. "Somebody should laugh like that forever. Somebody should get to hear new jokes."

Astrid discovered she was crying, which was inconvenient, and that Sefa had pressed a handkerchief into her hand at some point without her noticing, which was Sefa all over.

* * *

Sefa herself did not make a speech.

She stood at the front with her arms crossed and her floury apron on, facing the Warden wearing a dead woman's face, and she simply said: "I had a key."

The hilltop went quiet enough to hear the mule chewing.

"Greywater's twin seal, down the coast. Saltmere. I was young and the key was heavy and the road was long, and there was a man, and there was a kitchen that needed me, and I told myself I'd go next season. And next season. And next season." Her voice stayed perfectly level, which somehow made it worse. "Saltmere is still sealed. I checked, years ago. Still sealed. Everyone in it still eating the same soup, still telling the same twelve jokes, still waiting for a next season that somebody else was supposed to start." She uncrossed her arms. "I chose comfort. It was a choice, and it was mine, and I've had forty years to get comfortable with how uncomfortable it made me. I will not stand here and watch this child make my choice just because my choice wore a kind face and called itself mercy."

She pointed a floured finger at the Warden.

"You want to know what we lost? Everything. All of it. The whole unarrived world. That's what sealed places take—not what you remember, but what never got the chance to happen. And you—" her finger swung to Astrid— "are what happens next. So happen."

* * *

Odile came last.

She walked slowly, leaning on a stick she'd cut from a hedge somewhere along the road, and Astrid saw with a lurch that her mistress had aged years in the weeks since the ford. Or perhaps had simply stopped hiding it.

The Warden changed faces one final time.

It became Odile.

Younger—thirty years younger, strong-handed, sharp-eyed, the Odile from before whatever had bent her toward smallness. It stood among the stones wearing her mentor's youth and said, in Odile's voice: "Tell her, teacher. Tell her what you taught me. Smallness is mercy. Sealing is shelter. A locked door is a lullaby."

And the real Odile stopped three paces away from her own stolen face and looked at it for a long moment with an expression Astrid had never seen on her: grief without shame.

"Yes," Odile said. "I did teach that. I believed it. Listen, child, because I will only say this once, and then I will spend the rest of my life being wrong in a different direction." She drew breath. "I helped build the seal. Not the founding—I was not born yet—but the maintenance. The twelfth founder, Elowen, was my mistress as you were mine, and when she lay dying she asked me to keep the lock oiled and the hinges quiet, and I said yes, because I had seen what unsealed magic did when it came back all at once. Marta's flood. The founders' devouring generation. I had read the archives. I thought I was tucking a wounded world under a blanket."

She looked up at the wrong-rising thirteenth moon.

"But a blanket becomes a lid, and a lid becomes a coffin, and I knew it, child. I knew it the day you were born and I felt your blood answer the ridge, and I burned the letters, and I lied to you, and I called all of it love." Her voice finally cracked, and she let it. "I cannot tell you what to choose. If I tell you, it is only my fear wearing my voice, and you have had enough of faces that aren't true. I can only tell you what I lost. I lost you. Every year I kept you small, I lost a little more of you, and I told myself that was the price of keeping you, and it was, and it was too high, and I paid it anyway, because paying is easier than admitting the bill was wrong."

She turned to the Warden—to her own young face—and said, gently, the way you speak to something that is about to end:

"You are made of our guilt. All of ours. Twelve founders' worth and mine besides. But guilt is not a law. It's a letter. And letters are meant to be answered."

* * *

The Warden stood among the stones wearing seven borrowed faces' worth of silence.

Astrid wiped her eyes with Sefa's handkerchief, blew her nose with a sound that startled the mule, and stepped forward to stand beside Odile, facing the thing that had hunted her across half the map.

"You came," she said to all of them, not looking away from the Warden. "I want it noted that you came. The Warden showed me everyone I'd failed and told me you'd all be better off without me, and here you all are, having walked halfway across a landscape that is actively erasing itself, to prove it wrong in person."

"We brought stew," said Pip.

"It's a witness stew," said Perrin. "Witnesses need feeding."

"It's lamb," said Sefa. "Don't ask where I got lamb. Ask the mule how he feels about it."

The mule looked profoundly conflicted.

Astrid laughed—really laughed, wet and hiccupping, the laugh of someone who has been terrified for days and has just set some of it down—and the sound went out over the hilltop, and the twelfth stone flickered.

The Warden noticed. Astrid saw it notice. It looked down at the stone, then at her, and for the first time its borrowed face did something that belonged to none of the people it had stolen: it wavered. Uncertain. Almost hopeful, in the way that chains are hopeful of a key.

"You understand what you carry," it said, and now the voice was only its own—wind through a keyhole, dust settling in an empty house. "The founders' magic, freed, devoured a generation. I am what remains of their promise: never again. If you open the door wrongly—"

"I know," Astrid said. "I've read the journal. I've seen Marta's eye. I've heard Odile. I know exactly how badly this can go." She lifted her chin. "That's why I'm not going to break it. I'm going to rewrite it."

The Warden went very still.

Behind her, she heard Tam exhale, slow and controlled, the breath of a man watching a river decide whether to rise. She heard Sefa mutter, "There's my girl," around what sounded suspiciously like a full mouth. She felt Odile's hand find hers—old, hedge-roughened, trembling—and hold on.

The thirteenth moon cleared the ridge.

Every stone on the hill lit at once—all twelve dead waypoints blazing back to life, and beneath them, far below the roots of the mountain, something vast and patient and older than the founders turned over in its sleep and began, unmistakably, to listen.

"The last waypoint opens at moonset," the Warden said. Its voice had changed again—quieter now, almost afraid. "One night. One chance. Come, then, heir of Joubert. Bring your witnesses. Let us see what you write."

Astrid squeezed Odile's hand, shouldered her pack, and started down the hill toward the mountain's heart—with a ferryman, a cook, two jokers, a mender, a mule, a pot of witness stew, and every unlocked thing she had ever loved walking behind her.
