## 20. Homecoming, Revised

The morning after the door opened, Astrid woke in her own narrow bed beneath the eaves of Odile's cottage and lay very still, listening.

She had expected silence. Vell's mornings had always been silent in a particular way—the hush of a held breath, the quiet of a valley keeping its head down. But this was different. This was birdsong with *layers* to it, thrushes answering wrens answering something she couldn't name, and underneath it all a low hum she felt more than heard, like the valley itself had begun, cautiously, to hum along.

Magic. In Vell. On a Tuesday.

She got up, dressed, and went downstairs to find Odile standing in the garden with her hands on her hips, staring at the hedge.

"It's blooming," Odile said, without turning around. "Astrid. The hedge is blooming."

Astrid looked. It was. Blackthorn, out of season by a full three months, white blossoms crowding every branch as though the hedge had been holding them in its cheeks for years and had finally been given permission to spit them out. Bees—bees that should have been asleep—drunk and staggering among the flowers like villagers after a wedding.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Astrid asked.

"Nothing is *supposed* to do anything anymore," Odile said. "That's rather the point." She turned, and Astrid saw that her mistress looked ten years younger and completely undone, which on Odile's face was an unusual combination. "I've been mending charms against a wall my whole life. Do you understand? Every ward I ever laid, every knot I tied, I tied it knowing the magic would leak out through the seal before it could set. I built beautiful things out of wet sand. And now—" She gestured at the hedge, helpless. "Now the sand is stone."

Astrid touched one of the charms hanging from the cottage lintel—a small woven thing of rowan and red thread that Odile had remade every season for as long as Astrid could remember, and which had never once done anything except look decorative. As her fingers brushed it, the charm *warmed*, and a faint ring of light pulsed outward from the doorway, soft as a sigh.

Odile made a sound Astrid had never heard her make before. It might have been a laugh. It might have been a sob. It was possibly both at once.

"It holds," she whispered. "It actually—" She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. "Twenty years of work, and it holds on the first try."

"You're welcome," said Astrid, because someone had to keep the household running.

* * *

By midmorning, word had spread—which was remarkable in itself, since news in Vell had always traveled at the speed of suspicion—and the lane outside the cottage filled with neighbors who had no idea what to do with their faces.

Old Marta from the mill stood in the road holding a bucket that had sprung a leak, watching the water knit itself back together in front of her eyes. The cooper's twin boys were chasing a light that moved through the grass like a fish through water, shrieking with delight every time it darted away. And Bram Tuller, who had not been seen outside his house since his wife died six winters past, stood at his gate with his hat in his hands, saying over and over, quietly, to nobody: "Elsbeth always said it would come back. She said they couldn't keep it out forever."

He wasn't wrong, exactly. But he wasn't right either, and Astrid found she didn't have the heart to explain the difference—not yet, not while the whole valley was still deciding whether to be terrified or delighted.

They settled on delighted, mostly. There were exceptions. Widow Crane threw a shoe at a will-o'-wisp that drifted into her kitchen, then apologized to it when it flickered apologetically and left. The reeve spent the entire day posting notices declaring that magic was hereby permitted within the bounds of Vell, provided it did not disturb the peace, block the roads, or occur on Sundays, which was so perfectly Vellish that Tam laughed until he had to sit down on a fence.

Tam. Astrid glanced across the cottage yard to where he sat on the woodpile, as he had sat every day since they'd come home, whittling nothing in particular and watching the valley the way a man watches weather he doesn't trust. He'd crossed the threshold into Vell two days ago looking like a man walking into deep water. He still looked like it. But he hadn't left, and he ate what Sefa sent over from the wayhouse cart, and last night he'd asked—casually, too casually—whether the blackberries here ripened sweet or sour.

Sweet, Astrid had told him. Sweeter now, probably.

* * *

It was Sefa who brought the letter.

She arrived at noon in the wayhouse cart with half the misfit crew crammed in the back—Piet and the twins and old Hollis, all of them gawking at the hedges and the lights and the bees like children at a fair—and climbed down holding a leather satchel with the particular expression of a woman about to deliver either wonderful news or terrible news and determined to handle both the same way.

"Courier work," she said, handing the satchel to Astrid. "Came up the river this morning. A boatman brought it as far as the ford, wouldn't come past the ridge himself. Said the water felt 'different' up there." Her mouth twitched. "Different how, I wonder."

Astrid opened the satchel. Inside was a single letter, sealed with plain wax, addressed in a careful hand:

*To the Doorkeeper of Vell.*

"That's you," Piet said helpfully, from the cart.

"I gathered," Astrid said. Her heart had started doing something complicated. She broke the seal.

The letter was short. It was written in a hand that shook only a little, and it read:

*We have watched your valley these past weeks. We felt the moment the seal changed—we felt it the way you feel a door open in a far room, a draft on the back of the neck. Some of us were afraid. Some of us wept. All of us are asking the same question, which is: how?*

*Our own seal has stood for four hundred years. It was laid by founders who told us it was mercy, and we believed them, because believing was easier than grieving. But your valley says otherwise now. Your valley says there was another way, and that a girl with ink-stained fingers found it.*

*If what you wrote is true—if a seal can be amended instead of broken, if magic can be invited instead of feared—then we would learn how. Send us your method. Send us your mistakes. Send us whoever among you is brave enough to make the journey to Corvel, and we will meet them at our border with bread and open hands.*

*The seal of Corvel is loosening. Not breaking—loosening, the way a knot loosens when someone on the other end stops pulling. We think it is you. We think it has been you since the night you rewrote the words.*

*Come home to us. We did not know we had one until you wrote.*

There was a signature, but Astrid barely saw it, because Tam had come off the woodpile like a man pulled by a hook through his chest, and he was reading over her shoulder, and he had gone absolutely still.

Astrid had seen Tam afraid. She'd seen him furious, exhausted, grief-struck, and quietly, stubbornly hopeful. She had never seen him like this—both hands pressed flat against his thighs, jaw working, eyes fixed on the page as though it might dissolve if he blinked.

"Corvel," he said. His voice came out wrecked. "That's—that's Corvel writing. That's *home* writing."

"Tam—"

"They say it's loosening." He laughed, one broken note of it. "Four hundred years, and it's loosening, and they're asking for *bread*—" He turned away sharply, swiped his sleeve across his face, and pretended very hard to be interested in the hedge.

Nobody pretended to believe him. Sefa put a hand on his shoulder and left it there. Old Hollis, who had lost a brother to a sealed place called Marrow, took off his hat.

"We'll go," Astrid said. It came out before she'd decided to say it, which was how most true things came out of her lately. "As soon as the rounds are settled here. We'll go to Corvel, and we'll bring everything we learned, including the mistakes. *Especially* the mistakes."

Tam nodded. He didn't trust his voice yet. He picked up his knife, went back to the woodpile, and whittled nothing in particular—but he was smiling now, wetly, helplessly, at the hedge, at the bees, at the whole ridiculous blooming world.

* * *

The days that followed settled into a rhythm Astrid could only describe as *revised*.

Vell woke slowly, the way a sleeper wakes—not all at once but in stages, stretching, blinking, testing old limbs. Hedges bloomed out of season and kept blooming. The ford ran clear in a way the oldest villagers swore it hadn't run since their grandmothers' time. Odile's charms held—not just the ones she'd remade, but the ancient faded things tucked into rafters and doorposts all over the valley, waking up one by one like lamps being lit down a long street.

And the villagers greeted magic like an old friend returning from a long voyage. There was awkwardness at first—they'd forgotten its manners, and it had forgotten theirs. The first week, a hearth-spirit moved into the wayhouse chimney and refused to leave until Sefa negotiated terms involving nightly stew. The second week, the reeve's notices grew a third clause about spirits. By the third week, children were leaving saucers of cream on windowsills as casually as they left out boots, and Widow Crane had named her will-o'-wisp and complained about it to anyone who'd listen.

Astrid walked the valley's lanes and felt the difference under her feet. For nineteen years she had lived in a place that flinched. Now the whole valley leaned toward the light.

Odile changed too—or rather, stopped changing herself to fit. She worked openly in her garden now, sleeves rolled, laying wards that *held*, muttering to her herbs, arguing cheerfully with a hedge-wight that had taken up residence in the blackthorn and had opinions about pruning. Some evenings Astrid found her simply sitting on the step with her hands open in her lap, palms up, as though catching rain.

"What are you doing?" Astrid asked her once.

"Feeling it," Odile said simply. "Forty years of holding my breath. I'm learning to breathe."

* * *

On the evening before they were to leave for Corvel, the valley threw them a feast.

It happened in the wayhouse yard, organized with terrifying efficiency by Sefa and attended by what appeared to be the entire population of Vell plus several entities of unclear status. There were long tables under strung lanterns. There was Bram Tuller playing a fiddle badly and joyfully. There was a pie contest judged by the hedge-wight, whose criteria remained opaque but whose verdicts were accepted as law.

Astrid sat between Tam and Odile and let the noise wash over her. Tam had eaten three plates. Odile had drunk two cups of the miller's cider and was telling the story of Astrid's childhood—specifically the incident with the beeswax and the reeve's wig—to a table of strangers who were laughing so hard they couldn't breathe.

"—and she looks me dead in the eye," Odile was saying, "this child, seven years old, covered head to foot in wax, and she says, 'Mistress, I believe the wig started it.'"

"She *did* start it," Astrid protested.

"The wig," Odile agreed solemnly, "started it."

Later, when the fiddle slowed and the lanterns burned low, Tam found Astrid by the gate. He was holding his cup and looking out at the dark line of the ridge, beyond which lay the road, and beyond that, Corvel.

"My sister used to talk about coming home," he said. "Not escaping—coming *home*. Like it was a place that existed somewhere, waiting." He turned the cup in his hands. "I thought she meant somewhere specific. A town, a house. Now I think she just meant—" He gestured vaguely at the yard, the lanterns, the laughing, the pie. "This. People who know your name and feed you anyway."

Astrid bumped her shoulder against his arm. "We'll find yours."

"Yeah." He cleared his throat. "Yeah, I know. That's the terrifying part."

* * *

She went up to the mountain door one last time before they left—not because it needed checking, but because she wanted to see it.

The great stone face of the mountain stood quiet in the moonlight, and there, where no door had been for four hundred years, the seam of light still showed: a finger's width of silver glow, steady as a heartbeat. Unlocked. Unlocked on purpose. She had promised herself that, and she had kept it, and every time she walked past and saw the light still burning she felt the promise renew itself, simple as breathing.

She stood there a while, hand resting on warm stone.

Behind her, footsteps on the path. Odile, wrapped in her good cloak, breath fogging in the cold.

"I thought I'd find you here," Odile said. She came to stand beside Astrid and regarded the glowing seam with the expression of a woman auditing a very large account. "You know, when I helped build this thing, I told myself it was mercy. A quiet place. A safe place." She shook her head slowly. "It took a courier dying in a river and a girl with ink on her fingers to show me the difference between a sanctuary and a cage. One has a door."

"One has a door somebody chose to close," Astrid said. "And can choose to open."

"Mm." Odile was quiet a moment. Then: "The letter from Corvel. You're really going."

"Tomorrow."

"And you'll teach them what you taught me?"

"If they'll listen."

Odile smiled—one of the new smiles, unguarded, that Astrid was still collecting like rare coins. "Then go well, apprentice." She paused. "No. Not apprentice. Go well, *colleague*. Go well, Doorkeeper. And write to your old mistress, or I shall assume you've been eaten by something interesting and come after you myself."

Astrid laughed and hugged her, quick and fierce, smelling rosemary and woodsmoke and forty years of small stubborn mercies.

* * *

They left at dawn: Astrid, Tam, Sefa, and whichever of the wayhouse crew could be pried loose from the feast's memory. The valley turned out to see them off—Bram with his fiddle, the twins with garlands, Widow Crane pressing a jar of honey into Astrid's hands "for the road, and don't argue." The reeve presented a document certifying that the bearers were engaged in official courier business between Vell and parts unknown, which was legally meaningless and emotionally enormous.

At the ford, Astrid stopped and looked back once.

Vell lay green and gold in the early light, hedges white with impossible blossom, chimney smoke rising straight and contented into a sky that seemed, somehow, larger than it used to be. Somewhere in it, a charm was holding. Somewhere in it, a hedge-wight was complaining about pruning. Somewhere in it, a lamp burned in a doorway that no longer needed guarding.

Belonging, she thought. Not granted. *Achieved.* Carried out of a drowned archive and rewritten in blood and ink and stubbornness, and handed back to everyone.

"Coming?" Tam called from the far bank. He was grinning, and his eyes were still red-rimmed, and the morning light caught the tears he hadn't quite managed to hide.

"In a minute," Astrid said.

She took one last breath of valley air—thick with blossom and woodsmoke and the low hum of a place that had remembered its own name—and stepped across the ford, out of the country that wasn't on any map, onto the road that led, at last, to one that was.

Behind her, the door in the mountain glowed its finger-width of silver, patient as a promise.

Ahead, the road bent toward Corvel—and toward whatever waited there, loosening, in the dark.
