## 21. Apprentice No More

Spring came to Vell the way a letter comes after a long silence: all at once, and slightly damp.

Astrid noticed it first in Odile's garden, where the feverfew bloomed three weeks early and the rosemary had grown so enthusiastically it was attempting the fence. Magic had settled into the valley like a cat into a sunbeam—unhurried, proprietary, occasionally knocking things off shelves. The mending charms that had never quite worked now worked too well. Astrid had repaired a neighbor's kettle last Tuesday and it now whistled in harmony with itself, two notes at once, which the neighbor's wife declared either a miracle or an abomination depending on the hour.

"You've overfed it," Odile said that morning, peering at the kettle through her spectacles. "Charms are like bread dough. You can't just keep kneading."

"I didn't knead anything. I said the words you taught me."

"I taught you those words when they were mostly decorative." Odile set down the kettle with a small sigh. "Half of what I taught you was scaffolding, Astrid. Ritual for ritual's sake, because in a sealed valley the ritual was all we had. Now the magic's actually listening, and it turns out it has opinions."

This was, Astrid had learned, as close as Odile came to admitting she'd been wrong about anything. She'd been wrong about a great many things—the seal, the Warden, the wisdom of keeping a foundling ignorant of her own name—but she apologized the way old stone walls lean: slowly, structurally, and only if you knew where to look.

"I have opinions too," Astrid said. "About the letters."

Odile's hands went still over the mortar and pestle. It had been four months since the door in the mountain opened, since the Warden dissolved into ordinary moonlight and the sigil rewrote itself in Astrid's blood, and still certain subjects made Odile go careful and quiet, like someone walking on a frozen pond in April.

"The courier's rounds," Astrid pressed on. "Ivo's rounds. There are eleven more valleys out there, Odile. Eleven seals, most of them still shut, and every one of them has people in it who think they're the whole world. I know because I *was* one." She turned the leather map case over in her hands—Ivo's case, worn soft at the corners now from her carrying it. "And I know because of the letters."

The letters had started arriving in the second month. Not by road—there was no road yet—but the way water finds a crack. They came folded into crows' feathers, tucked inside hollow fence posts, drifting down the millrace wrapped in oilcloth. The other valleys had felt Vell open. And somewhere in each of them, someone had remembered—or dreamed, or inherited—a story about a valley that says yes.

Most of the letters asked the same question: *How?*

Some asked: *Is it safe?*

One, written in a shaking hand on birch bark, simply said: *Please.*

"And what do you intend to do about them?" Odile asked. Her voice was level. Her knuckles were white around the pestle.

"Answer them," Astrid said. "In person. Ivo had unfinished rounds—thirteen waypoints, twelve deliveries he never made. The map still shows them. The roads between valleys are waking up; Tam says Corvel's seal is thin enough now that you can smell rain through it. Someone has to carry the mail."

"That someone does not have to be you."

"No," Astrid agreed pleasantly. "But it's going to be."

* * *

They argued about it for six days, which both of them privately considered restraint.

It was not the argument of apprentice and mistress anymore, and that was the strange part. When Astrid had been fifteen and foolish, their arguments had ended with Odile saying *because I said so* and Astrid seething into the herb loft. Now they argued like two practitioners who respected each other's craft, which meant they argued thoroughly, with citations, and occasionally with diagrams drawn in flour on the kitchen table.

On the sixth day, Odile ran out of objections and sat back, flour to her elbows, looking older and younger at once.

"You'll take the east road first," she said. "Corvel. Because Tam will be with you, and because if the seal there is loosening anyway, your arrival is a courtesy rather than a rescue. Do not open anything without being invited. Do not promise anyone anything you can't deliver. And do not—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—do not come home in a box, because I am too old to dig."

"I know," Astrid said gently. "You'd make me dig my own."

Despite everything, Odile smiled. It was a real smile, crooked and reluctant, the kind she'd spent forty years pretending she couldn't make.

"There is one more thing," Odile said. "Before you go. Sit down."

Astrid sat. Odile disappeared into her bedroom and returned with a small wooden box that Astrid had seen perhaps twice in her life and never been allowed to touch. Inside, on a bed of faded blue cloth, lay a ring of keys—not iron but bone, pale and worn smooth—and a rolled parchment tied with green thread.

"When I took you in," Odile said, "you were seven years old and you bit me. I decided then that I would teach you everything I knew, and I have spent fourteen years discovering how little that was." She unrolled the parchment. It was a certificate of apprenticeship, formal and yellowed, with Odile's signature at the bottom and a blank line where the release should go. "An apprenticeship ends one of two ways. The master releases the apprentice, or the apprentice surpasses the master and everyone pretends it was planned. I would prefer the first. It involves less pretending."

She dipped a pen and signed the release line with a flourish that shook only a little.

"Astrid Joubert," she said formally, "last heir of the sealing bloodline, rewriter of the sigil, overfeeder of charms—you are no longer my apprentice. You are a practitioner in your own right, and the valley of Vell knows it, whether or not you've noticed it knows."

Astrid found, to her enormous surprise, that she was crying. She hadn't cried at the Warden. She hadn't cried burning the wayhouse's ruins or reading her grandmother's name in the drowned archive. But this—a piece of paper, a crooked signature—undid her entirely.

"Thank you," she managed.

"Don't thank me yet." Odile folded her hands on the table, and Astrid saw that she was nervous. Actually nervous. Odile, who had faced the twelfth founder's ghost without flinching, was nervous. "Because there is a second part, and you're within your rights to refuse it."

"Which is?"

Odile took a breath. "Teach me."

The kitchen went very quiet. Outside, the over-enthusiastic rosemary rustled against the fence, and somewhere a charm-whistling kettle sang its two-note song.

"I built half the prison," Odile said. "I helped write wards that kept a whole valley blind for two hundred years, and I did it believing it was mercy. My craft is sealing craft. Closing, binding, hiding—it's the only grammar I speak fluently. And now the world needs the opposite, and I find myself..." She looked down at her hands. "I find myself illiterate, Astrid. You opened a door that twelve founders couldn't open and a Warden couldn't close. You rewrote a sigil older than the valley. Whatever that is—that thing you did—I want to learn it. Before I die, I would like to learn how to say yes."

Astrid stared at her. Fourteen years of being the one holding the mortar, fetching the comfrey, memorizing without understanding. And now Odile—Odile, whose shadow had been the shape of all knowledge in the valley—was asking to hold the mortar for her.

"You could have just asked," Astrid said at last, when she could speak. "You didn't need the ceremony."

"The ceremony matters," Odile said. "Ask any founder. Beginnings and endings deserve witnesses." A pause. "Also I wanted to see you cry. You never cry. It was starting to worry me."

Astrid laughed wetly and wiped her face with her sleeve. "Fine. But I warn you, I'm a terrible teacher. I'll assign readings. I'll make you grind things."

"I've missed grinding things," Odile said wistfully. "Grinding is honest work."

* * *

The first lesson happened that evening, in the garden, under a moon that was only a fingernail paring in the sky.

"Sealing craft starts with a boundary," Astrid said. She'd thought about this all afternoon, pacing the herb loft, trying to reverse-engineer her own inheritance into something teachable. "So opening craft starts there too—with a boundary. But instead of asking *how do I keep things out*, you ask *what am I actually keeping out, and why, and who decided*."

Odile nodded along, student-attentive, which was deeply strange to witness.

"The sigil on Vell wasn't evil," Astrid continued. "That's the trap. It was kind. It was protecting people from something real. Every seal I've read about since—the founders always had a reason. So the first question isn't *should this door be open*. It's *what would change if it were*, and *did anyone ask the people inside*."

"And if the answer is that opening it would be dangerous?" Odile asked. "Genuinely dangerous?"

"Then you don't break the seal," Astrid said. "You amend it. You sit down with the danger like it's a person—which it usually is—and you negotiate. Invitation instead of conquest. That's the whole trick. That's what I wrote into Vell's sigil: nothing enters by force, and no door locks from outside. Everything else follows from that."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Then she picked up a stick and drew a small circle in the garden dirt, around a patch of chamomile.

"Show me," she said. "Amend this. It's a ward I put up thirty years ago to keep slugs off the chamomile. It's cruel, actually—I made the boundary taste of copper so the slugs would turn back. I've been torturing gastropods for three decades and calling it gardening."

Astrid laughed until her ribs hurt. Then she knelt beside the circle, laid two fingers in the dirt, and showed her former mistress—her new student—how to rewrite a wall into a welcome.

The slugs, for the record, remained a problem. But now the chamomile smelled faintly of mint, which slugs disliked and bees loved, and the garden hummed with a negotiation instead of a punishment. Odile studied it for a long time.

"Huh," she said. "Thirty years of craft, amended by a girl who bites."

"You've mentioned the biting before."

"It bears repeating. It was excellent judgment on your part."

* * *

Tam arrived on a gray morning three days later, poling his flat-bottomed boat up the millrace because the rivers of Vell, newly awake to possibility, had rearranged themselves to let him. He stepped off onto the bank with his usual expression—a face like a closed ledger—and a satchel of letters.

"Corvel's leaking," he said, by way of greeting. "Rain through the seal, like I said. And light. People there are seeing light where there shouldn't be light. My mother's village sent this." He handed Astrid a letter wrapped in river-reed. "They remember me. Some of them, anyway. Enough."

Astrid read it. Then she read it again. Then she sat down on the bank, because her legs had opinions of their own.

"They're asking," she said. "They're actually asking. Not begging—asking. They want to know the terms. They want to send representatives to look at Vell first." She looked up at Tam, and her eyes were stinging again, damn it, twice in one week. "Tam, they're doing it *right*. Carefully. Together."

"That's what happens," Tam said, "when someone shows them it can be done without the sky falling." He almost smiled. On Tam, an almost-smile was a festival. "My sister used to say the sealed places would only ever open from the inside. She was right. She just didn't live to see it."

"We'll carry that letter ourselves," Astrid said. "First delivery of the new rounds. Corvel, by way of the east road, with a stop at every waypoint Ivo marked. Sefa's feeding us at the coast, and the wayhouse strays are meeting us at the ford with pack-mules, and—" she stopped, overwhelmed suddenly by the sheer logistics of hope. "There's so much to carry."

"Then we'd best start carrying," Tam said.

* * *

They left on the first of Thaw-Month, at dawn, which Odile insisted was the proper hour for beginnings even though Astrid maintained that dawn was a concept invented by people who didn't value sleep.

The whole valley came to see them off, or nearly. The kettle-whistler and his wife. The miller, whose millstone now turned itself on windless days and who had stopped apologizing for it. Children who had been born into a silent valley and were growing up in a singing one, chasing each other through hedges that bloomed out of season. Sefa had sent a hamper of food that could provision a small army, labeled in her sharp handwriting: *EAT THIS FIRST, THEN THIS, AND IF YOU TOUCH THE PICKLES BEFORE WEEK TWO I WILL KNOW.*

Odile walked Astrid to the ridge itself—to the place where, a year ago, magic had brushed her skin like weather for the first time. The old woman moved slowly now, but she refused an arm, and she stood at the top of the pass with the wind pulling at her shawl, looking out at the waking roads.

"I have a first assignment for you," Odile said. "As your teacher. Well—as your student assigning homework to your teacher, which is a sentence I never expected to say."

"Go on."

"When you get to Corvel, watch how they greet each other. Really watch. Sealing folk forget greetings—we never had visitors, so we never practiced. I want a full report on the grammar of homecoming." She paused. "And I want you to bring me back a slug remedy that doesn't involve cruelty. Consider it your examination."

"I'll expect payment in lessons," Astrid said. "Real ones. You still know nine hundred things I don't."

"Eight hundred and sixty," Odile corrected. "I counted while you were gone last year. Losing forty to grief, apparently." She reached out and straightened Astrid's collar, a gesture she had performed ten thousand times and never once explained. "Your grandmother hid the key in a living bloodline because objects can be stolen, Astrid. But living things can also leave. Come back. That's not an order anymore. It's a request, from one practitioner to another."

Astrid hugged her—briefly, because Odile tolerated hugs the way cats tolerate baths—and then turned and walked down the eastern side of the ridge, toward the road that was no longer refusing to lead anywhere.

At the bend, she looked back. Odile was still standing there, small against the big sky, and she raised one hand—not waving, exactly. More like a blessing. Or a salute. Or possibly she was testing the wind direction for her weather charms, because some things never changed.

Astrid raised her hand back.

Then the road curved, and the valley of Vell slipped behind the hills, and ahead of her the world unfolded—all its locked doors, all its waiting letters, all its people who had never once been asked. In her satchel, Ivo's map case lay warm against her ribs, and the first letter of the new round pressed against her heart:

*To the people of Corvel, from the valley that says yes: We were you, once. Here is how we opened the door. Come and see, whenever you're ready. —A.J., courier.*

Somewhere far to the east, past the drowned archive and the salt coast and the thinning seal, a village was watching light leak through the walls of its world.

Astrid adjusted her satchel strap and started walking.

Behind her, faint but unmistakable, carried on the morning wind all the way down from the ridge, came the sound of Odile practicing—badly, stubbornly, hopefully—the first amendment of her long life: a small hedge-charm, rewritten, saying *welcome* to the bees.
