## 22. The Unlocked Door

One year, and the door had learned to stand ajar.

Astrid noticed it the way you notice a child who has finally stopped slouching—the mountain's great stone mouth no longer strained against its own hinges, no longer ground its teeth in the night. It simply rested there, open the width of one determined hand, letting the wind of the high passes move through it like breath.

She had tried, in the first weeks after the rewriting, to close it properly. Old habit. Odile's habit, if she was honest—Odile, who had spent sixty years keeping things shut on principle. But every time Astrid pulled the door toward latching, the sigil-work along its frame dimmed like a lamp running low on oil, and something in the valley itself seemed to hold its breath. So she had propped it instead with a river stone the size of a loaf, and after a month she'd carved a small blessing into that stone, and after three months Tam had sat beside it whittling until the stone looked less like a wedge and more like a sleeping cat, which everyone agreed improved it considerably.

Now the cat-stone sat in its place, worn smooth by a year of hands touching it for luck, and Astrid stood beside it in the blue hour before dawn, holding an unlit lamp.

The lamp was Sefa's idea, originally. *People walk at night,* she'd said, ladling stew with the ferocity of a woman who had never once in her life served a small portion. *And they've spent their whole lives in places where a light in the distance means someone deciding whether to hunt you. Give them a light that means something else.*

So Astrid lit it each evening and carried it down each morning, and somewhere in the year the ritual had stopped being charity and become simply what her hands did, the way they once had gathered feverfew for Mistress Odile before the world had any edges to it.

* * *

The valley woke below her in its new voice.

Vell had always been beautiful in the way of pressed flowers—lovely, and flat, and silent. Now it made noise. Hedges bloomed out of season and argued about it. The millrace sang three distinct notes depending on the mood of the water, and old Piet the miller had taken to conducting it with his breakfast spoon. Odile's charms held now—not just held but flourished, the protective knots over doorways fattening themselves with little braided tassels like vain old men growing splendid beards. The first time a charm of hers had worked properly, Odile had stood in front of it for a full quarter hour with her arms crossed, and then said, very quietly, "Well. It only took me a lifetime," and gone inside to make tea strong enough to strip paint.

Magic hadn't come back to Vell like a flood. That had been the founders' fear, and the Warden's argument, and the temptation Astrid herself had felt at the penultimate waypoint—that freed magic would devour what it touched. But magic, it turned out, was mostly manners. It came back the way a long-absent relative comes back: cautiously at first, bringing gifts, asking after everyone's health, weeping a little at dinner. It entered by invitation because Astrid had written it so, and the valley, having spent twelve generations being told no, had turned out to be remarkably gracious about saying yes.

Below her, smoke rose from the wayhouse—which was not the burned wayhouse, that one was ash and memory on the far side of the ridge, but a new one, built in Vell itself at the crossroads where the ford met the low road. Sefa ran it, because Sefa would have run a wayhouse in the mouth of a volcano if the volcano promised steady custom. She had strays again: a one-eyed fiddler from a sealed place called Merrow whose songs came out slightly damp; twin brothers who spoke only to each other and to the soup pot; a girl named Wren who had arrived seven months ago unable to say her own name and now shouted it from rooftops for practice.

Astrid watched the smoke and thought, not for the first time, that belonging was not a thing you were granted. It was a thing you kept lit.

* * *

The arrivals came most often at dawn or dusk, when the light made the mountain door look less like a wound in the rock and more like a threshold, which is what it was.

They came drawn by rumor, and the rumor had traveled farther than anyone expected. *There is a valley that says yes.* Four words, passed hand to hand through sealed places like a coal passed between cupped palms. Astrid had heard versions of it from nearly everyone who walked through: a woman from Corvel's outer farms who'd heard it sung as a nonsense rhyme children jumped rope to; a hollow-eyed stonemason who'd heard it carved into a prison wall by someone who never lived to see if it was true.

Tam met them at the ferry crossing when they came by water, and Astrid met them here when they came by road, and between the two of them they had developed a protocol that was mostly Sefa's cooking applied at strategic moments.

This morning there were three of them on the road: an old man walking with a stick, a young woman carrying a child too big to carry, and behind them, hesitating at the treeline like a deer deciding about a clearing, a boy of perhaps fifteen with a bundle tied to his shoulders and the particular stillness of someone who has practiced being invisible for years.

Astrid knew that stillness. She had worn it like a second cloak for twenty years.

She did not call out to him. She had learned that much. Instead she set the lamp on its hook beside the door—lit, though the sun was rising, because rules about when lamps should be lit belonged to other valleys—and she sat down on the cat-stone where he could see her plainly, and she took out her rounds.

Because that was the other inheritance. The courier's satchel had been hers for a year now. Ivo—the dying man at the ford, whose name lived in the map case and in the journal and in the rounds she walked—had died carrying letters between valleys that were never supposed to touch, and Astrid had taken up the work unfinished. Most of it was small: a grandmother's recipe traveling from Vell to a coastal village, a letter of forgiveness that had waited eleven years for a road to exist. Some of it was larger. Last spring she had delivered, to a woman in Corvel who had given up her name to escape, a single line in her mother's handwriting: *You were never lost, only hidden.* The woman had read it standing in the shallows of the ferry crossing, and then she had sat down in the water entirely, and Tam had stood beside her holding his hat, and nobody had said anything at all, which was correct.

This morning's letters were ordinary ones. Astrid sorted them on her knee, and let the boy at the treeline watch her do something as unremarkable as mail.

It took him the better part of an hour to come down the road. When he did, he stopped a careful ten paces away and looked at the door, and the lamp, and her, in that order.

"Is it true," he said. His voice had rust in it, disuse. "That this is the valley that says yes."

"It's true that we say it a lot," Astrid said. "Some days we say it to the hedges, and they're insufferable about it." She held up the letters. "I'm the post. If you're coming in, there's bread in the wayhouse and Sefa will ask you your whole life story whether you want her to or not, but she'll do it while feeding you, so it balances out."

The old man laughed—a surprised sound, like something breaking open. The young woman shifted the child on her hip and took a step forward, then another, as though testing whether the ground would permit it.

The boy didn't move yet. He looked at Astrid with eyes that had done arithmetic on every kindness ever offered him and found the sums wanting.

"What does it cost?" he asked. "Everything costs. What's the toll?"

And there it was—the question the old map had asked at every waypoint, the question the Warden had worn like a face. Astrid set the letters down and stood, and she thought about how to answer honestly, because these people deserved honesty more than comfort.

"The door doesn't take a toll anymore," she said. "I rewrote it. But—" and she held up a finger, because the *but* mattered, "—belonging isn't free either. It costs showing up. It costs letting people know your name eventually, even the parts you're ashamed of. It costs staying when staying is harder than leaving, and it costs leaving when leaving is right. That's the whole price. Nobody collects it at the door. You pay it across a lifetime, in installments, to each other."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a customs official.

"That's worse than a toll," he said finally.

"Much," Astrid agreed. "But you get change back."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

He stepped past the threshold.

* * *

Later—after Sefa had fed all four of them twice and extracted the boy's name (Dov) and the young woman's story and the old man's opinion on everything—Astrid climbed back up to the door with the day's outgoing post and found Odile waiting there, leaning on her stick, watching the light come down the pass.

Her mistress was seventy-nine now and claimed to be delighted about it, mostly as ammunition. Her hair had gone fully white and she refused on principle to call it anything but "weather damage." She had asked Astrid, eight months ago, to teach her, and the lessons happened weekly and were conducted with the exact same severity Odile had once brought to mending charms, except now Astrid gave the criticism and Odile received it with the face of a duchess being told her gown was on backward.

"You left the lamp burning," Odile said, nodding at it. "In daylight."

"I know."

"Your predecessor would have called that wasteful."

"My predecessor died doing his rounds," Astrid said. "I think he'd have called it finishing the job."

Odile made the sound she made instead of conceding a point, which was a short exhale through the nose that had ended more arguments than any spell. Then she was quiet a while, and Astrid let her be quiet, because a year ago she'd have needed to fill the silence and now she understood that some silences were gifts being wrapped slowly.

"I helped build a prison," Odile said at last. "Out of love. I want that written down somewhere, when I'm gone. Not that I was wicked—I wasn't, that's the terrible part—but that love can build locks too, and you have to watch it. You have to ask it, every day, *is this protection or is this fear wearing protection's coat?*" She turned and looked at Astrid, and her eyes were as sharp as they'd been on the worst morning of Astrid's life, the morning of the burning letters. "You ask it better than I ever did."

"Only because you taught me to ask questions at all."

"I taught you to gather herbs." But her mouth softened. "The questions you stole fair and square."

They stood together a moment, the two of them, apprentice and mistress and neither and both, and then Odile tapped her stick against the cat-stone twice, the way she did when dismissing a class or blessing a house, and started down toward the valley, calling back over her shoulder: "Supper at sundown. Bring Dov. Anyone that suspicious-looking needs feeding and examining, in that order."

* * *

Evening came up gold. Astrid trimmed the lamp, checked the sigil-work along the doorframe—it glowed faintly now, contented, the amended script holding its gentle terms: *by invitation, not conquest; no door locked from outside*—and she thought about the year behind her and the years ahead, and about Ivo, whom she visited in her mind the way you visit a grave that has become a garden.

Somewhere out there, beyond the pass, other sealed places still held their breath. Corvel's seal was loosening—Tam had word of it monthly now, carried by fishermen who spoke of strange tides and doors that no longer stuck in their frames. Merrow too, maybe. Others she hadn't mapped yet. There would be more work. There would always be more work. Doors wanted tending. Sigils wanted reading. And every valley that opened would send its frightened, hopeful people walking toward a rumor of a place that says yes.

Astrid lifted the lamp down from its hook.

She had meant to carry it to the wayhouse, as she did every night. But at the last moment she turned instead and hung it back up, just inside the threshold, where its light spilled out through the gap—the width of one determined hand—and lay gold along the road into the mountains.

Not a beacon. Just enough. Enough for whoever was walking tonight, in some other sealed country, hearing four words passed palm to palm. Enough for the next Dov, hesitating at a treeline. Enough to say what the whole rewritten sigil said, in its small glowing script, to anyone who came near:

*You were never lost, only hidden. Come find yourself here. The door is unlocked. We left it that way on purpose.*

Astrid stood a moment longer in the doorway of her valley—apprentice no more, courier, heir, keeper of a lamp—and then she went down toward supper and lamplight and the noise of her loud, unsealed, unfinished family.

Behind her, the door stood ajar.

It stayed that way.
