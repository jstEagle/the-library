## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The dead came to Lior Voss through her hands, and they never said anything useful.

She had learned this early, sitting at her mother's workbench with a splinter of sheep bone in her fingers, feeling the faint warmth that meant something unseen had leaned close. Her mother had called it the family gift, though there was no one left in the valley to compare it against. The gift was this: when Lior held bone — carved or raw, boiled clean or still carrying its old shape — she could feel the residue of what had lived in it. Not memories, exactly. Not voices in any proper sense. More like the way a house remembers footsteps: an impression pressed into the material, faint as breath on cold glass.

But sometimes — and this was the part she told no one, because there was no one to tell — the impressions spoke. Half-words. Fragments. A syllable rising through her knuckles like a bubble through dark water, breaking at the surface before it could become anything whole. *—remember—* it might say, or *—the door was—*, or simply a sound like grief with no name attached. The dead murmured through her hands the way wind murmured through a keyhole: present, insistent, and impossible to question.

Tonight they were restless, and Lior did not know why.

She sat at her workbench beneath the one window of her cottage, lamplight pooling yellow on the wood, and turned a piece of river-bone in her fingers. It had come from the millpond — a boy's charm-bone, the small flat plate from behind the ear of a carp, which folk in the valley believed carried the soul's echo of anyone who drowned. Old superstition. But old superstitions were the only kind the valley kept, so when Tam Ellery's son went under the ice three weeks ago and did not come up until two days after, his mother had walked through the snow to Lior's door with the bone wrapped in a scrap of linen, and had not been able to say a single word. She had only set it on the workbench and looked at Lior with the particular emptiness of a woman whose body had forgotten how to weep.

Lior had taken the commission because taking commissions was what she did. She took them all: memorial charms for the drowned, luck-knots for brides, teeth-charms for teething infants, small carved promises for farmers who wanted their fields to remember the sun. In a valley where nothing strange ever happened, Lior Voss was the strangest thing in it, and the valley tolerated her the way one tolerates weather — necessary, occasionally inconvenient, never invited to sit down.

She fitted the bone into the clamp and picked up her finest graver.

The first cut always felt like opening a door.

The blade touched the bone and the murmur rose immediately, closer than usual tonight, as if the dead had gathered at her shoulders like crows on a fence. *—cold—* said something near her left hand. *—under—* said another voice, and she knew that one belonged to Tam's boy, or to whatever echo of him the pond-water had soaked into the bone. Drowned bones always spoke of cold and under. They were the least surprising dead in the valley.

"Quiet," Lior murmured, out of habit more than hope. "I'm working."

They did not quiet. They never quieted. But they settled into their usual rhythm, a low tide of half-syllables washing through her wrists while she worked, and Lior let herself fall into the carving the way she fell into all her carvings — completely, gratefully, alone.

This was the part no one understood about her craft, the part that made her hands worth more than her company: a memorial charm was not decoration. It was architecture. Every line she cut was a wall; every curve, a roof. She was building a small house in the bone, a place where an echo could live without leaking, where a mother could hold her son's last cold moment without it spreading through the rest of her life like rot. Other carvers made pretty things. Lior made containers. She made the shape of grief bearable by giving it walls.

*—the light was—* whispered the bone. *—there was light, even—*

Lior's graver paused.

That was new. Drowned boys spoke of cold and under. They did not speak of light. She bent closer, lamplight catching the fine dust of bone powder on the wood, and listened with her hands the way other people listened with their ears.

*—even under, there was—* the murmur said again, and then, in a tone that made the hair rise along Lior's forearms, *—she is coming—*

"She," Lior said quietly, "who?"

Silence. The dead, when they chose to be silent, were more eloquent than any living creature. She waited, graver poised, but the bone gave her nothing except the ordinary residue of cold and under, and beneath that, faintly, something that felt almost like anticipation.

Lior exhaled and told herself it was fatigue. Winter worked on a person that way. Three weeks of snow, weeks of short grey days and long grey nights, weeks of talking to no one except the occasional client who came to her door and stood too far away and left too quickly. She was twenty-six years old and she had lived in this valley her entire life, and she could count her friends on a hand with no fingers raised. The valley needed her charms. It did not need her. There was a difference, and she had made her peace with it years ago, mostly.

She returned to the carving. Wall, roof, door. She cut the little house into the bone with steady strokes, and around it she cut the border-patterns her mother had taught her — the running knot that meant *held fast*, the interlocking rings that meant *not lost, only kept*. When she finished, near midnight, she held the charm up to the lamp and turned it slowly, checking her lines.

It was good work. It was always good work. And it would hang above the Ellery mantel for a generation, and Tam's mother would touch it some evenings when she thought no one saw, and the boy's echo would stay warm inside its little bone house, and that was the whole of what Lior Voss's life amounted to: making small rooms for large griefs, in a valley that forgot her the moment her door closed.

The dead murmured their approval, or their boredom, or their grief. With the dead, it was hard to tell.

* * *

She woke before dawn to a sound that did not belong.

At first she thought it was the mountain settling — the valley did that sometimes in deep cold, a groan of stone contracting, felt more than heard. But this was different. This came from underneath, from far below the roots of the mountains, a long rolling shudder that passed through the floor of the cottage and up through the legs of her bed and into her chest, where it stopped her heart for one full second before releasing it.

Lior sat up in the dark, listening.

The tremor faded. The cottage creaked back into its familiar shapes. Somewhere outside, a shutter knocked once against its frame and went still.

And then the silence afterward — that was the wrong part. Because the valley did not tremble. Everyone knew it, the way everyone knew the sun rose in the east and the millpond froze in winter. The valley was still ground, safe ground, the reason her great-grandparents had come here in the first place, the reason folk built here and buried here and never questioned why the old stories all agreed: *nothing follows you into the valley.* No spirits. No curses. No magic of any kind. The land itself refused it, flatly and absolutely, the way a locked door refuses a knock. Farmers joked about it. Priests from beyond the passes, when priests came at all, grew pale and quiet and left early. Whatever the world outside did — and the world outside did many things, if the peddlers' tales were true — none of it crossed the ridgeline. The valley was a room with the magic shut out.

Which meant the tremor had not been magic. It could not have been. It had been geology, or deep frost, or her own dreaming mind inventing catastrophe out of loneliness.

Lior got up anyway. She dressed in the dark, lit the lamp, and stood a while at the window, watching the snowfields go from black to grey as the sky considered dawn. The mountains stood in their usual places. The village below her slope lay huddled and unbroken, smoke already rising from a few chimneys. Everything was exactly as it had been yesterday, and the day before, and the thousand days before those.

Except that her hands were humming.

Not the ordinary murmur. Something else — a vibration in the bones of her fingers, faint and rhythmic, like a plucked string somewhere below hearing. She held her hands up to the lamplight and watched them tremble very slightly, and felt, with absolute certainty she could not explain, that they were resonating. Responding. As if something enormous had sounded a note somewhere far away, and everything in her that was capable of ringing had rung in answer.

*She is coming,* the drowned boy's bone had said. *Even under, there was light.*

Lior lowered her hands. She stood in her cold cottage in her cold valley where nothing strange could follow, and admitted, to the silence and to herself, that the strange had not followed her anywhere. It had been here all along. It had been waiting under the stillness the way the boy had waited under the ice — patient, lightless, holding its breath.

Outside, far off toward the northern ridge, a bird cried out — sharp, alarmed, twice — and then the whole valley's birds went silent at once, hundreds of small throats closing together, and the silence that followed was so complete and so deliberate that Lior's skin prickled from her wrists to her jaw.

The dead, through her open palms, began to whisper all at once.

Not fragments now. Not half-words. For the first time in her life, the murmur gathered itself, dozens of voices braiding into one current that ran up her arms and pooled in her chest, and though she still could not parse the words, she understood their shape perfectly, the way one understands a scream without needing translation:

Something was coming up the valley road.

And the valley, which forgot everything, was about to remember why it had been made to forget.
