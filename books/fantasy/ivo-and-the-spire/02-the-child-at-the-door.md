## 2. The Child at the Door

The snow came down in the slow, deliberate way of valley snow — as if the sky itself had learned patience from the mountains and refused to be hurried by anything so vulgar as weather elsewhere. Lior Voss watched it through the small window above her workbench, her knife resting mid-stroke against a rib bone that had once belonged to a nameless pilgrim, and she thought, as she often did, that the snow was the only thing in the valley that ever arrived without asking permission.

The dead had been restless all morning.

Not loud — they were never loud here, where even whispers seemed to lose their nerve at the border stones — but restless, a friction under her skin like wool worn too long. She had felt it while grinding white ash into her carving oil. She had felt it when she set the kettle on, and the kettle's handle had hummed faintly against her palm, though kettles did not hum, and neither did handles, and certainly not in this valley where nothing hummed, nothing glowed, nothing stirred that ought not stir.

She had learned to distrust such feelings the way other people learned to distrust a creaking floorboard: not with fear, exactly, but with attention.

The boy's charm lay half-finished before her. Tam Weller, drowned three days past in the millrace, eleven years old, fond of frogs and of lying to his mother about where he'd been. The dead boy himself had already come to her hands twice — not as a voice, for the newly dead rarely managed words, but as a pressure, a damp coldness in her fingertips, an image pressed into her palms like a seal into wax: green water, the wheel turning, his own boot caught in the sluice-gate's iron teeth. He had wanted her to know it was quick. That was the mercy the dead asked for most often, in her experience. Not justice. Not remembrance, though they took it gratefully enough. Just someone to know how it ended.

Lior carved what they asked. It was, she supposed, the only honest trade in the valley. The farmers brought her their griefs wrapped in linen and paid in eggs or mended fences, and she gave them back a piece of bone with a name worked into it, and the dead went quieter, and everyone pretended this was ordinary. Everyone pretended she was ordinary. A carver of trinkets. A woman who talked to herself over her bench, poor thing, living alone up against the tree line since her mother died.

Her mother, who had taught her which bones to use and which to burn. Her mother, who had gone silent one winter morning mid-sentence and never spoken to her hands again, and whose absence Lior still felt as a specific silence, shaped like a woman, standing just behind her left shoulder.

"Tam," Lior murmured to the rib, working the letters fine as frost-fern. *Tam Weller, who loved the millrace too well.* The knife whispered. Outside, the snow kept its patient falling, and the valley held its breath the way it always did, the way it had for as long as anyone could remember — a hush so complete that some folk said you could hear your own heart apologize for beating.

That was when the knocking started.

Not at the door. At the *bones*.

It began in the basket beside her bench — the basket where she kept her finished charms waiting for delivery, each one wrapped in cloth, each one quiet. They had never made a sound in her life. Charms were finished things; the dead in them settled like silt in still water, and a settled dead stayed settled until the charm was given and buried or burned. But now a low tone rose from the basket, thin as a wet finger on a glass rim, and then another joined it, a third, until every charm in the basket was singing softly out of tune with every other, a discordant little congregation, and the hair on Lior's arms stood up beneath her sleeves.

She put down the knife. Her heart was doing something foolish and fast.

"No," she told them quietly, the way she spoke to the dead when they fretted. "None of that. Settle."

They did not settle. The tone climbed. And underneath it — beneath the singing of her own handiwork, which should have been impossible, which *was* impossible, because magic could not enter this valley, could not cross the border stones, had never crossed them in all the generations the Voss women had lived here with their strange gift and their careful silence — underneath all of it, Lior heard something else.

Footsteps in the snow. Small ones.

She was at the door before she decided to move, which frightened her more than the singing had. Her hand was on the latch. Beyond the window, the light was going blue with evening, and the snow fell thick enough to erase the world past ten paces, and somewhere in that whiteness something small was coming toward her house with the stumbling, stubborn gait of a creature that had been walking far longer than it had strength for.

She opened the door.

The cold hit her face like a slap of wet cloth. And there, three steps down, knee-deep in the drifts that had swallowed the path whole, stood a child.

A girl, Lior saw at once, though the snow had packed itself into the child's hood and hair and lashes until she looked like something half-carved, unfinished, a figure abandoned by whatever sculptor had started her. Seven years old, perhaps eight. Barefoot. *Barefoot*, in this, the feet blue-white and split at the heels, standing in snow without flinching, without shivering — and that was the first wrongness, the wrongness that would sit in Lior's chest for days afterward like a swallowed stone. Children froze. Children died of less than this. But the girl stood in the drift and looked up at Lior with dark, level eyes, and she was not shivering at all.

Behind her, the snow showed no tracks. Not one. As if she had fallen from the sky, or grown up out of the ground, or simply begun existing at the edge of the light from Lior's window.

"You'd best come in," Lior said, because there was nothing else a person could say, and because her hands had already decided — her hands, which had never once acted without her, were reaching out, opening, offering.

The girl looked at the hands. Looked at Lior. And then she spoke.

It was not a word in any language Lior knew, and Lior knew — imperfectly, through her mother's teaching — the old prayer-tongue of the valley's founding families, and the market-speech, and enough of the river-folk's dialect to bargain. This was none of these. It was a sound like water deciding to be speech, like wind given grammar, two syllables that seemed to arrive not through the ears but through the bones of Lior's inner ear, through the small dense architecture of her skull.

And every charm in her house answered.

The basket sang. The finished pieces on their wall-pegs sang. The half-carved rib on the bench rang out Tam Weller's name in a clear bright tone, and the memorial beads strung above the door — her mother's work, forty years silent — trembled on their cord and gave off a sound like distant bells heard through deep water. The whole cottage became an instrument played by a child's voice, and Lior stood in the doorway of her own home feeling like a stranger in it, feeling the dead rise up around her not in their usual murmur but in something close to *attention*, close to joy, the way a room of sleeping people might stir if someone began to sing a song they all remembered from childhood.

The girl stopped speaking. The sounds faded, reluctantly, like a tide going out.

Then she swayed, and Lior caught her — caught bare skin cold as riverbed stone — and pulled her inside, and shut the door on the snow and on whatever waited behind the child in the whiteness, and stood in her own kitchen holding a stranger who weighed almost nothing, whose feet were bleeding thinly onto her clean floor, and whose eyes, closing now with the terrible sudden trust of the utterly exhausted, had looked at Lior the way no one had looked at her in thirty years.

Like she was known. Like she was expected.

* * *

Lior worked the way she always worked when her mind wanted to run somewhere her body couldn't follow: she warmed milk, she melted snow for a basin, she found the good salve and the soft wool wraps. The girl let herself be handled like a doll, pliant and silent, watching everything with that unnerving steadiness. Up close, Lior could see details the snow had hidden. The child's clothes were fine once — a tunic of dyed wool, a collar worked with thread-of-gold in patterns that hurt Lior's eyes slightly to follow, spirals that seemed to turn when she wasn't looking directly at them. Someone had dressed this child with care and money. Someone had also cut the hem off raggedly, recently, hastily, as if shortening it for disguise, or tearing away anything that might identify her.

There was a mark on the inside of the girl's wrist. Lior turned the small hand over gently, expecting a bruise, and found instead a scar — old, deliberate, precise. A circle bisected by a line. It had been made with a blade and made carefully, on a child far too young to have chosen it.

"Ivo."

Lior's head came up. The girl was looking at her, and had said it clearly, in the market-speech, her first word in any tongue Lior understood — and Lior realized with a lurch that the girl hadn't been naming herself. She had been reading the name off the half-carved rib that sat on the bench between them, the rib that read *Tam Weller* in Lior's own lettering.

No. Not reading it. The girl's lips had moved around the shape of it strangely, tasting it, and when she said it again — "Ivo" — she touched her own sternum with two fingers, the way folk did when swearing an oath or giving their true name.

"That's your name? Ivo?"

The girl nodded once. Then she said something else, long and flowing, in that other language — the one that made the walls ring faintly, the one that made the basin water shiver into rings — and stopped, and looked at Lior with an expression of such patient expectation that Lior understood, with a sinking feeling, that the child had asked her a question and fully intended to wait for the answer until the mountains wore down.

"I don't understand you," Lior said helplessly. "Child, I don't—"

Ivo tilted her head. Reached out — quick as a bird, before Lior could react — and laid her small cold fingers flat against the back of Lior's hand.

The dead spoke.

All of them. At once. Not the usual seep and murmur, the half-words Lior spent her life straining to parse, but *clearly*, a chorus rising through her arm like water through a pipe: Tam Weller laughing about frogs; the pilgrim whose rib she was carving reciting road-prayers; her mother — her mother, whose voice Lior had not heard in nine years — saying, calm as Sunday, *"Ask her what she carries. Ask her what broke."*

And beneath the voices, beneath all of them, something older. Something that was not a dead person at all but sounded like the ground itself remembering, a vast slow recitation in that same water-and-wind language, syllables that Ivo's touch translated without translating, meaning pouring into Lior like heat into cold iron:

*A promise was made. A promise was broken. The hinge is rusted. The door is shut. The last voice is coming. Keep the last voice.*

Lior tore her hand free and stumbled back against the bench. The charms sang and sank silent. The fire popped. Ivo withdrew her fingers and folded them in her lap, watching Lior with those dark unblinking eyes, and outside the wind picked up with a low moan along the eaves.

"What are you?" Lior whispered. It came out harsher than she meant. She softened it the only way she knew how — she knelt, so their eyes were level, and said, gentler: "What happened to you? Who is after you?"

Because someone was. She knew it the way she knew weather in her knees. No child walked out of trackless snow in a valley no traveler entered unless they were running from something that made trackless snow seem safe.

Ivo answered in the old tongue, four words, and pointed north — toward the high ridge, toward the pass that didn't exist anymore except to sheep and smugglers — and then she drew one finger across her throat, slowly, and looked at Lior without any expression at all.

Lior's mouth went dry.

And that was when she saw the lights.

Through the window, up on the shoulder of the northern ridge where the trees broke, lanterns were cresting the skyline. One, two — five, six, seven, strung out in a disciplined line, descending through the snowfall with the unhurried pace of hunters who have run something to ground and know it. The light was wrong for valley lanterns. Valley lanterns burned amber and friendly. These burned white, hard-edged, casting shadows that seemed to fall in directions the flames didn't justify, and even at this distance Lior felt them the way you feel a stare across a room.

Seven lights. Coming down toward the valley floor. Toward the only lit window for miles.

Toward her door.

Lior rose slowly, her heart hammering a rhythm she hadn't felt since childhood — since the day her mother had gripped her wrists and said, *if anyone ever comes asking about us, Lior, we are nobody, we have always been nobody, say it until you believe it.* She had believed she believed it. Thirty years of careful, quiet, harmless life. She carved bones for grieving farmers. She paid her tithes. She had never told a lie larger than "the bread's not ready yet."

Ivo was looking at the lights too. She did not panic. She did not cry. She simply stood, barefoot on Lior's clean floor, and took hold of the hem of Lior's apron with one small hand, and held on.

Later — for the rest of her life, in fact — Lior would try to locate the exact moment the decision was made, and she would never find it. There was no moment. There was only the weight of that small hand, and the memory of a chorus of dead saying *keep the last voice*, and the sight of white lantern-light crawling down her ridge like a spill of something caustic, and then she was moving.

She snuffed the lamp. Fed the fire down to a red murmur. Wrapped Ivo in the quilt from her own bed, blankets and all, and carried her — the child weighed nothing, nothing, a bundle of sticks and silence — to the cellar door behind the pantry. Down the steps, into the cold earth-smell dark, past the hanging hams and the turnip bins, to the far corner where her mother's old chest stood. She lifted the false bottom her mother had built and never explained, and laid Ivo in the space beneath, among the dried herbs and the wrapped bundles of bone-dust, and the girl looked up at her and said, in the market-speech, perfectly clear:

"Ivo stays. You lie."

"Yes," Lior said. "You stay. I lie."

Ivo reached up and touched Lior's cheekbone once, lightly, with two fingers — the same gesture, Lior realized with a chill, that she had used to give her own name — and then pulled the quilt over her head and vanished into stillness, breathing so quietly that within moments Lior could not tell whether the child breathed at all.

She closed the chest. Closed the trapdoor. Raked flour over the seam of the pantry boards with the broom, straightened the rug, and put the kettle on, because a woman alone in the evening would have a kettle on.

The knock came four minutes later. Three blows, evenly spaced, hard enough to shake dust from the lintel.

Lior opened the door to snow and white lantern-light and six tall figures in grey, their hoods rimed with frost, their faces shadowed — and one of them, the one in front, taller than the rest, wearing a coat the color of wet slate, looked down at her with pale eyes that did not blink in the wind.

"Goodwoman," he said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst thing about it. "We're seeking a runaway. Small child, dark-haired, foreign. Have you seen her?"

Lior Voss, who had never in her careful life told a lie larger than bread, heard herself say, calmly, in the flat bored tone of a tired woman with better things to do:

"Ain't seen a soul since the Wellers' boy drowned. Come in out of the snow if you've a mind, but I'll thank you to wipe your boots."

The tall man smiled. Behind him, the lanterns burned white, and down in the cellar, beneath the flour and the floorboards and forty years of her mother's secrets, something ancient and small and hunted held its breath — and Lior stepped aside to let the hunters in.
