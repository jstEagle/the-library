## 5. Crossing the Border

They left by the goat track, because the road was watched.

Lior had known it would be. She had spent three days learning the warden's habits from the dead, who saw everything in the valley and understood almost none of it — a useful combination, if you had patience. The dead did not sleep, and so neither, properly, did she. She carved through the nights with Ivo asleep against her back, wrapped in her own cloak, and by the third night she had made what she needed: nine small charms of sheep bone and river clay, each one a lie told to the world's skin.

*Hollow here,* they said. *Nothing worth taking. Nothing at all.*

She strung them along Kess's belt, along her own hem, along the strap that bound Ivo to her spine, and hoped the Spire's hounds read lies as easily as living men did.

Kess came for them an hour past moonset, moving through the frost like a man who had once been paid to do exactly this and hated remembering it. He carried no lantern. His eyes had adjusted to the dark the way exiles' eyes did — out of necessity, out of shame.

"The eastern ridge," he breathed. "There's a fold in it, below the old cairn. The boundary runs thin there. Thinnest crossing in forty miles."

"You've crossed before."

"I was thrown across." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "It doesn't get gentler with practice."

Lior looked back once at her cottage, black against the blacker hillside, its chimney cold, her tools laid out on the bench inside exactly where she'd left them three days ago when the world still made sense. She had not packed. Packing meant believing there would be somewhere to unpack.

"Keep her quiet," Kess said. "Whatever happens on the other side — whatever she does, whatever *happens to her* — keep moving. The warden's riders can't follow across fast, but they don't need to. They only need to see where we went."

"And you know this how?"

"I helped design the pursuit protocols." He almost smiled. It died quickly. "I told you. Disgraced."

The valley held its breath around them. That was the thing about this place no visitor ever understood: it didn't feel empty of magic. It felt like a room after an argument, when everyone has agreed to say nothing. The silence had weight. Lior had lived inside that weight her whole life, had mistaken it for peace, and only now — with a child's breath warm against her neck and a fugitive's guilt walking beside her — could she hear the shape of what the silence was pressing down upon.

The dead murmured at her hands as they climbed. Not words yet. Something older than words. A rhythm, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

*Soon,* they seemed to say. *Soon, soon.*

---

The fold in the ridge was exactly as Kess described: a shallow depression where two hillsides leaned together like conspirators, floored with frost-hardened grass, crowned by a cairn so old its stones had grown lichen faces. No marker. No warning. The border of the valley was invisible, which Lior had always taken for proof that it didn't exist.

She knew better now.

"Here," Kess whispered, and stopped so abruptly that Lior walked into his shoulder.

She felt it then. Standing three feet from nothing, she felt it — the way you feel a cliff edge before you see it. A pressure difference. A change in the temperature of the air against her skin. On one side of that invisible line, the world held its tongue. On the other side, the world was *screaming*, constantly, in a language made of everything that wasn't sound.

"I can't," she said. It came out flat, factual, the way she stated things when she was most afraid. "Kess. My whole life I've lived without magic touching me. My body doesn't know how to—"

"It doesn't have to know how." Kess turned, and in the starlight his face was hollowed and honest. "That's the mercy of it. You're not carrying anything in. You're just... letting the world back in. Like opening a door that's been painted shut."

"And if the door was painted shut for a reason?"

"Then we'll find out together." He glanced at Ivo, asleep or pretending to be, small fists knotted in Lior's hair. "We're already dead if we stay. Serevan found the cottage. He'll burn it tonight whether we're in it or not — he wants us running scared, and he wants the valley to know what sheltering covenant-children costs. Staying is dying slowly. Crossing is dying fast, maybe. But maybe isn't certain."

Behind them, far down the valley, a light appeared. Then another. Lanterns, riding the valley floor toward her home.

Lior did not let herself think. Thinking was a luxury of people with time.

She stepped across.

---

Pain is a poor narrator, but it was the only one available to her for a long while afterward, so she will tell it as pain told it to her.

First: cold. Not the cold of snow — the cold of *absence*, as though every warm thing she had ever been were being subtracted from her one degree at a time. Her knees hit frozen grass. Her hands, her carving hands, her whole livelihood and language, pressed into the earth and she felt the ground *notice* her — felt the land's attention land on her like a physical weight, vast and slow and ancient, turning over in its long sleep to ask: *what is this? what walks here?*

Second: light. Magic poured into her the way water pours into a cracked vessel, and she was all cracks, forty years of them, every year of magicless living a fissure in her skin that now drank and drank and could not stop drinking. She felt the dead — not the valley's polite murmuring dead but *all* the dead, everywhere, layered in the soil beneath her like sediment, like rings in a tree — and every one of them touched her hands as they passed through her awareness, and each touch was a word, and the words were—

"—the third sign, and the river keeps its—"

"—witnessed under crown and root, the hinge shall—"

"—*name her, name her, the voice returns, the voice returns—*"

"Lior." Kess's voice, very far away. "Lior, breathe. Breathe through it. Don't fight the current, just—"

Third: Ivo screamed.

Not cried. Screamed — a sound with no childhood in it at all, a sound like a hinge tearing free of a door, and the land *answered*. Every blade of frosted grass within thirty feet stood up straight. The cairn stones rolled aside of their own accord. Somewhere beneath them something enormous shifted its weight, and Lior, still on her hands and knees in the flood of the world's returning attention, felt the recognition move through the ground like a struck bell:

*The last-born voice. The last-born voice has come home.*

Ivo's eyes were open and they were not a child's eyes; they were the color of deep water over old stone, and her mouth was shaping words in the language older than kingdoms, and the words were not screams anymore. They were sentences. They were *greetings*. The land was answering her, and she was answering it, and between the two of them Lior felt herself become briefly, terribly unnecessary — a stick caught between two meeting rivers.

Then Ivo collapsed against her back, shuddering, and buried her face in Lior's shoulder, and was a child again. Small. Terrified. Clinging.

"It knows me," Ivo said — in trade-tongue, brokenly, the words Kess had been teaching her on the road of practice and fear. "It knows me and I don't—" Her breath hitched. "I don't want to be known by it. I want—"

"I know," Lior said. "I know what you want."

She got her feet under her. It took three tries. The magic was still pouring into her, slower now, finding the level, and every part of her ached as though she'd been remade slightly too large for her own skin. But she stood. And Ivo's arms locked around her neck. And Kess took point again, white-faced, saying nothing about whatever he had seen happen to the cairn, and they moved.

Behind them, on the southern horizon, the sky over the valley turned orange.

---

Lior did not look back. She wants that recorded, wherever such things are recorded: she did not look back, because she knew that if she saw the cottage burning she would stop being able to walk, and Ivo needed her walking.

But she smelled it. Woodsmoke has a particular signature when it's eating a home — the rendered fat of beeswax candles, the bitter ghost of dried herbs hung from rafters, the sweet char of a bedframe that has held your weight for twenty years. And underneath it, fainter, crueler: burning bone. Her offcuts. Her failed carvings. Twenty years of practice pieces, every mistake she'd ever kept to teach herself humility, going up in a single orange column.

Kess looked back. She saw him do it, saw his jaw tighten.

"What?" she asked, because she had to ask, because not knowing was worse.

"Cottage is gone. Riders are pulling back — they won't cross tonight." A pause. "Serevan's making a statement. He wants any other valley family thinking about sheltering strangers to watch that fire and do arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Lior repeated. The word tasted like ash already.

"He's not cruel for pleasure," Kess said quietly. "That's the worst part. He's cruel the way a mason is cruel to a crooked wall. He thinks he's building something."

They walked. The frost thickened. Ivo slept at last, truly slept, her tear-streaked face pressed into the join of Lior's neck, and Lior carried her and carried her grief and carried the new, unbearable hum of the world in her bones, and did not stop walking until the sky went grey at the edges.

They made camp in a hollow beneath a rowan tree, the first trees Lior had seen since the ridge, their berries red as small accusations. Kess built no fire. He sat across from her, knees drawn up, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

"Your cottage," he said finally. "Everything you had."

"My tools are in my hands," Lior said. "My material is under my feet. Everything else was storage." She heard how it sounded — brave, clean, false. She let it stand anyway. Some lies are load-bearing.

Ivo stirred against her. Woke. Looked at the smoke-stain on the horizon — it was visible even now, a smudge like a thumbprint on the dawn — and then looked at Lior, and reached up, and touched Lior's cheek with one small hand.

In the old tongue, softly, she said a word Lior's bones understood before her mind did.

*Home.*

Not the valley. Not the burned cottage. *Her.* Lior. Home was not a place; it was a person; the child had known this for days and had finally found the word for it, and had chosen to spend the word now, here, on the ashes-morning, like a coin that couldn't be taken from her.

Lior did not cry. She was past crying, in the way that a cup left out in rain is past wetness. But she folded the child close, and pressed her mouth to Ivo's hair, and over the small head she looked at Kess, who was watching them with the expression of a man reading a letter addressed to someone better than himself.

"Rivermoot," she said. Her voice came out steady. Practice, again. "How far?"

"Nine days, if the roads hold. Ten if the river-realm's mood is bad." Kess unfolded himself, stood, stamped feeling back into his feet. "And Lior — the court will want proof. Proof the covenant can live again. They've been lied to before. By people wearing Spire colors, carrying Spire promises."

"Then we give them proof."

"Proof means you. Your gift, in front of the whole drowned court, with Spire spies watching the edges of it." He met her eyes. "You understand what that costs."

Lior looked east, where the sun was coming up over country she had never seen, full of dead she had never met, all of them waiting, all of them murmuring, all of them holding fragments of a promise that someone, generations ago, had let break.

"They burned my house to teach me a lesson," she said. "So I'll teach one back. The dead keep their memories even when the living break their promises. Let's go remind a river of that."

She rose. Ivo slid from her arms to the ground and took her hand — took it the way children take hands, entirely, as though the holding were the whole purpose of hands — and the three of them crested the hill out of the hollow, leaving the rowan and the smoke-smudge behind.

Beneath Lior's feet, faint but unmistakable, the land murmured its greeting to the last-born voice, and followed them as they walked.
