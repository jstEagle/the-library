## 6. The Road of Small Gods

Kess had walked the border roads of six realms in his thirty-two years, and he had never seen a countryside so hungry.

It was not the people who hungered — though they were lean enough, the farmsteads thin and wary, their fences mended with rope and prayer. It was the land between the farms. He felt it the way a man who has lived his whole life beside a river feels an absence of water: not as a sight but as a wrongness. The hedgerows grew crooked. The wells tasted of iron and apology. And at dusk, when the light went amber and long across the barley fields, Kess could see them.

The small gods.

They crouched at field-corners like starved dogs, if dogs could be made of suggestion. One was little more than a stooped shape of mist above a boundary stone, its edges fraying into the evening air like breath in winter. Another huddled under an elder tree that had split years ago and healed badly, all its attention fixed on nothing. They had been hearth-spirits once, hedge-gods, keepers of thresholds and calving seasons and the first frost's timing. Now they watched travelers pass with eyes like guttering candles, and asked for nothing, because asking took strength they did not have.

Kess had been raised by the Spire to believe such things were beneath notice. Minor spirits, the instructors called them, the way a merchant speaks of minor debts. The covenant had fed them — that much the Spire admitted, in the bored tone of men reciting inconvenient history — and when the covenant broke, the small gods had been cut off from the flow of realm-magic like irrigation channels from their river. Some had gone feral. Most had simply thinned, season over season, century over century, until what remained was a habit of presence rather than a being.

He had written reports about it. Neat ones, in his careful clerk's hand, filed in the twelfth gallery of the Spire where no one ever read anything that mattered. *Rural spirit populations continue to decline. Recommend continued monitoring.* As if monitoring were mercy. As if watching something starve slowly were a form of stewardship.

Now he walked behind a bone-carver and a mute child, and every starving god they passed looked not at him but at Ivo — and Kess understood, with a clerk's terrible clarity, exactly what the Spire had spent generations pretending not to know.

* * *

They traveled by day and camped cold, because Lior said fire drew eyes and Kess knew she was right, having been one of the eyes himself not so long ago. The road wound down out of the high country through a landscape that had clearly once been loved. There were old shrine-stones everywhere, waist-high, carved with spirals and cup-marks worn smooth by centuries of offerings — and every offering niche was empty. Not robbed. Empty the way a bowl is empty when there is nothing left to put in it. In one village they passed through, an ancient woman pressed a dried apple into Ivo's hands without a word, then stood in her doorway watching them go, and Kess saw her glance back toward the corner of her own yard, where a hedge-god sat so faint he might have been imagining it.

Ivo ate half the apple and buried the rest at the base of the first shrine-stone beyond the village.

Lior stopped walking when she saw the child do it. She stood very still, her scarred carver's hands hanging loose at her sides, watching Ivo pat the earth flat around the offering with the solemnity of a priest. Then the child turned, looked up at Lior, and made a sound — not a word, not in any language Kess knew, but a small rising note, questioning, hopeful.

And the mist-shape at the boundary stone lifted its head.

It was nothing. A flicker. The frayed edges of the thing gathered themselves for one heartbeat, and two candle-flame points brightened where its eyes should be, and then it sank again, dimmer than before, as if even gratitude cost more than it could pay. But Kess had spent eleven years learning to read power the way other men read weather, and what he had just seen was impossible: a spirit with no source, no covenant, no flow of magic feeding it, responding to a child who carried more raw covenant in her smallest gesture than the entire Spire held in its stolen vaults.

Lior knelt and touched the shrine-stone with her palm. Her lips moved. Whatever she said was too quiet to catch, but Kess saw her hand tremble against the stone, and saw the dead woman's habit of gentleness in how carefully she rose afterward, brushing earth from her knee.

"She felt that," Kess said quietly. "The spirit. Whatever Ivo gave it."

"I know." Lior did not look at him. "I felt it too. Through my hands." She flexed her fingers, staring at them as though they belonged to someone else. "Like a bell rung somewhere far away. Like—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and started walking again.

Kess followed, and did not say what he was thinking: *That is what the Spire is afraid of. That is why they hunt her. Not because she is dangerous. Because she is proof.*

* * *

On the fourth day, Ivo began teaching Lior words.

It happened the way water finds a channel — without decision, without ceremony. They were resting at midday in the lee of a fallen wall, sharing hard cheese and harder bread, when Ivo picked up a pebble, held it out to Lior, and said a word. Two syllables, soft, round at the front. She said it again, pointing at the pebble, and Lior repeated it — clumsily, her accent making the second syllable crack like a stick — and Ivo laughed.

Kess had not heard the child laugh before. It transformed her face entirely, this serious, watchful creature who had arrived barefoot in snow and had not once, in all the days since, behaved like a child. For a moment she was simply seven years old or however old she was — ageless children unsettled Kess; he had seen too many in the Spire's galleries, catalogued and measured — laughing because a grown woman had pronounced a word wrong.

From then on it did not stop. Ivo taught the way rivers teach, constantly, patiently, without any expectation of gratitude. A word for stone. A word for the particular quality of light before rain, which had three syllables and sounded like wind deciding. A word for *hungry* that was also, Kess gradually realized, the same word used for the small gods — as if in the old tongue hunger was a condition shared by bellies and spirits alike, a lack that could be filled by anyone willing to fill it.

Lior learned slowly, and hated learning slowly, and practiced anyway. Kess would wake in the grey hour before dawn and hear them murmuring together on the far side of the cold ashes, Lior repeating a phrase until her tongue obeyed, Ivo correcting her with gentle taps of one finger against her wrist — the way, Kess thought, you would correct someone you trusted not to bite.

One night he heard Lior say, in the old tongue, haltingly, "*You are safe*" — and got the last word wrong, so that what she actually said was closer to "*You are home*," and Ivo went absolutely still, staring at her, and did not correct it.

Kess lay awake a long time after that, looking at the stars through the broken roof of the shepherd's hut they'd claimed, and told himself firmly that what he was witnessing was none of his business. He was a guide. A reluctant one. An exile with a debt he hadn't finished counting. He was not — whatever the ache in his chest suggested — watching a family assemble itself out of spare parts in the ruins of a broken world.

* * *

By the ninth day the road had brought them down into river country, greener now, the fields fenced instead of walled, and Lior began teaching Ivo bread-making.

It began, as far as Kess could tell, out of pure necessity. Their provisions had dwindled to flour bought with Lior's last coin from a miller who charged them double for strangers and triple for the child, whose strangeness even the unmagicked could sense the way animals sense weather. So they rented the use of a farmhouse oven — the widow who owned it asked no questions, only stared a long time at Ivo and then crossed herself with the wrong hand, an old gesture, pre-Spire, that Kess hadn't seen since his grandmother died — and Lior set about making bread the way she did everything: precisely, silently, with total attention.

She showed Ivo how to test the warmth of the water against the inside of her wrist. How flour feels different in dry air than damp. How to fold dough — *press, fold, turn* — with the heel of the hand, and how the dough tells you when it has had enough, going smooth and springy under your palms like a living thing calming down. Ivo watched each motion with ferocious concentration and reproduced it perfectly except the kneading, which she did with a tenderness that would have produced, Lior said gravely, "a loaf with the spine of a jellyfish," and then guided the child's hands firmer, folding them over her own.

"You have to be willing to hurt it a little," Lior said, in her own tongue first, then, stumbling, in Ivo's. "*Press. Fold. Turn. It makes it strong.*"

Ivo considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she pressed, folded, turned — properly, fiercely — and looked up at Lior with an expression of such fierce pride that Kess had to turn away and pretend great interest in the widow's woodpile.

While the loaves baked, filling the farmhouse kitchen with a smell so honest it seemed almost subversive, Ivo taught Lior the word for bread. It was not, Kess noted, a simple word. It had layers. Said one way it meant the food; said another it meant the sharing of it; said a third way — and here Ivo tapped Lior's wrist twice, gently, to show the difference — it meant something like *that which is made by hands for people you—*

The child stopped there. Did not finish the definition. Went pink, insofar as her dusky skin allowed, and became intensely occupied with shaping the next loaf.

Lior let it go. But later, walking out under a sky bruising toward rain, she said to Kess, conversationally, as if discussing fence posts: "In her language, there are nine words for bread. Nine. One of them can't be translated. The closest I can find is—" she searched, failed, shook her head. "It's the word you'd use for a charm you carve for someone you love. Something that carries part of you into keeping them safe."

"That sounds less like a word for bread," Kess said, "and more like a word for covenant."

Lior looked at him sharply. Then away. "Yes," she said. "It does."

* * *

It rained on the eleventh day, a fine grey drizzle that turned the road to grease and the hedgerows to black lace, and near dusk they came upon the worst thing Kess had yet seen on the journey: a shrine that had been maintained.

Not abandoned like the others — tended. Someone had recently cleared the weeds from around it, righted a leaning offering-bowl, and left fresh-cut flowers in the niche, white ones gone translucent with rain. And the god itself, a hedge-spirit shaped like a stooped old man woven from shadow and bramble, was still there — still present, still *there* in a way the others weren't, holding on by some thread of local devotion that had outlasted everything.

But it was starving. Even tended, even loved, it starved, because love alone wasn't food; love was the vessel, and the covenant was supposed to be what poured. The spirit crouched at its stone like a beggar at a rich man's gate, receiving flowers the way a dying man receives letters — gratefully, hopelessly, each gift a reminder of what it could no longer taste.

Ivo knelt in the mud in front of it without hesitation, soaked to the skin, and spoke to it.

Kess didn't understand the words. But he understood the structure, because he had spent eleven years studying covenant-forms in the Spire's dry libraries, and what the child said had the shape of a promise: offer, answer, bond. She was speaking to the god the way the old signatories must have spoken, realm to realm, flow to channel — and for a moment, one shining moment, the drizzle itself seemed to pause, and the hedge-god straightened, and color flooded back into it like blood into a numb limb, and Kess felt the hair rise on his arms because for one heartbeat the road smelled of summer.

Then it faded. The god sank back into its grey half-life. Ivo stayed kneeling a moment longer, her small shoulders rigid, and when she stood and turned back to them her face was wet with more than rain, and she looked at Lior and said one word — the word for *hungry*, the word that meant both bellies and gods — and then another word Kess didn't know, low and bitter, spoken twice.

"What did she say?" Lior asked him.

Kess hesitated. "I don't know the second word."

"But you recognized the shape of it." Lior's voice was flat. "Tell me what shape."

"A promise," he said. "Broken. Or—" He stopped, hearing it himself. "Or a promise waiting to be kept. The grammar's the same. Old tongue doesn't distinguish. Whether a covenant is broken or merely unkept is apparently... the same sentence."

Lior stood in the rain a long moment, looking down at the starving god, and Kess watched her hands curl closed at her sides — the hands that heard the dead, the hands that had been born, he now suspected, for a purpose no one had explained to her — and when she finally spoke, it was barely above the drizzle's whisper.

"Then we keep walking," she said. "Until we learn the rest of the sentence."

They walked. The rain fell. Behind them the hedge-god watched them go with its borrowed flicker of color already fading, and ahead the road bent southward, following a river that grew wider and louder with every mile — a river, Kess realized as full dark came down, that they would follow all the way to Rivermoot, where a court waited with a shard and a price, and where, he thought grimly, watching Lior take Ivo's cold hand in her scarred one, whatever they were becoming to each other was about to be tested against everything the world still remembered how to take away.

Far off downstream, through the rain, lights were gathering on the water — lantern-light, moving against the current, too orderly, too deliberate.

Kess had seen that discipline before. Rivermoot had sentries on its borders, and sentries meant questions, and questions meant someone asking why an exile, a bone-witch, and a covenant-child were walking toward the first shard of a covenant the Spire wanted everyone to believe was dead forever.

He lengthened his stride, and hoped the river's welcome would be kinder than its watch.
