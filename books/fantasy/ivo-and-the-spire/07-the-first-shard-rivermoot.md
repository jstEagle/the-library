## 7. The First Shard: Rivermoot

The river announced them three days before they reached it.

Lior heard it first in her hands — a low, wet murmur that rose through the bones she carried in her satchel and made the smallest of them tremble against each other like teeth chattering in cold. She had felt the dead speak before, had spent her whole life listening to their half-words through her fingertips, but this was different. This was not the dead of one valley, one graveyard, one small human grief. This was vast. Layered. A chorus so old and so deep that individual voices had long since dissolved into something like current.

"The river remembers," Kess said, when she told him. He walked ahead of them on the flood-road, his exile's gray cloak patched at both elbows, his staff worn smooth from years of other people's roads. He did not look back when he spoke. "That's what they call it here. The Remembering Water. Every covenant ever sworn near its banks — every oath, every promise between realm and realm — sank into it and stayed."

"And now?" Ivo asked.

Ivo's voice still startled Lior, even after weeks on the road. The child who had arrived mute at her door could speak now — sparingly, carefully, as though each word cost something — and when she did speak, the air itself seemed to lean in to listen. Her language was older than any kingdom; Kess said it was older than the Spire, which Kess clearly considered impossible and resented deeply.

"Now," Kess said, "it drowns people who lie."

* * *

They came down out of the hills at dusk on the third day, and Lior stopped breathing for a moment.

The Rivermoot was not a city. It was a court — in the oldest sense of the word. Where the great river widened into a braided delta, its waters had been persuaded, ages ago, into shapes: channels and pools and slow green lagoons arranged in rings, ring within ring, like the ripples of a stone dropped in water and frozen there. On the outermost ring stood the houses of the small folk — reed-cutters, fisher-families, eel-trappers — their homes built on stilts above the water, lit by lamps of living glowworms. The second ring held the markets, all of them floating: barges lashed together into streets that swayed gently underfoot. And at the center, rising from an island of black stone, stood the Court of the Delta itself — a hall built of layered white timber and river-glass, its roof shaped like a breaking wave, its doors open to anyone who came by water.

"Nobody walks into Rivermoot," Kess said grimly. "You arrive by boat or you don't arrive."

They hired a ferryman at the outer ring — a broad, silent woman with eel-scars on her forearms who took Kess's coin without counting it and pushed off from the stilt-docks without asking where they were going. Everyone knew where travelers with tired faces and a child were going. Word had come upriver faster than they had.

Lior sat in the stern with Ivo pressed warm against her side and watched the rings of the delta close around them. In her satchel, the bone charms had gone quiet — not silent, but hushed, the way a room goes hushed when someone important enters. Even the drowned boy's memorial charm, the first thing she had ever carved, the one Ivo had pressed back into her hands outside Ashmarket, even that lay still against her palm as though it were waiting.

"They know we're coming," she murmured.

"Everyone knows," Kess said. "That's the problem with courts. Nothing is secret; things are only *unwitnessed*. We're about to be witnessed."

Ivo's hand found hers in the dark of the boat. The child's fingers were cold — always cold now, since Ashmarket, since the voice they had silenced forever in that burned city — and Lior closed her own hand around them and did not let go.

* * *

The Court of the Delta received them in a chamber that was half hall and half flood.

The floor was water — clear, knee-deep, warm as blood — and the petitioners waded across it to reach the throne, so that no one could approach the Delta Queen dry, or proud, or armored. Torches burned in brackets along the walls, and where the torchlight touched the water it turned to gold, so that the whole chamber seemed to be standing inside a struck bell of light.

The Delta Queen herself was old the way rivers are old — not wrinkled but *worn*, her face smoothed and reshaped by centuries of flowing past things. Her hair was white and moved as if underwater even in still air. She sat on a throne of woven reeds that had been growing, uncut, for longer than any kingdom Lior knew of, and her eyes were the flat silver of open water under cloud.

She looked at Ivo for a long time.

Then she looked at Lior, and her voice, when it came, was the sound of a river deciding.

"You bring a child of the old tongue to my court," the Queen said. "And a broken knight of the Spire." Kess flinched; the Queen's gaze did not soften. "And a bone-carver from the valley that forgets. You ask for the First Shard — the Anchor of Currents, which my ancestors took into keeping when the covenant was whole, and which my house has guarded through every year of its breaking. You ask me to give up the last proof that my realm ever mattered to the others."

"We don't ask you to give it up," Lior said. "We ask you to give it *back*."

A murmur ran through the courtiers lining the walls — reed-lords and mud-mayors, eel-priests and the pale, silent figures Lior had marked at once and could not stop marking: watchers in traveling cloaks who stood too still, whose eyes never moved to follow the torches, whose attention rested on Ivo like a weight.

Spire spies. They had found them already. Of course they had.

"The covenant is broken," the Queen said. "Broken for nine generations. My grandmother's grandmother watched the other realms forget us. Watched the ash-realm seal its gates. Watched the Spire rise and begin to hoard. Why should I believe that handing over our shard will mend anything? Words are cheap. Rivers are not. Bring me proof."

"What proof?" Lior asked.

The Queen leaned forward on her living throne, and the water around her stirred, though nothing else in the chamber moved.

"Your valley forgets magic," she said. "So they say. A place where the dead are quiet, where charms go dark, where the old tongue cannot be spoken. Yet you carry the dead with you, bone-carver. I have watched your hands since you entered my water. They have been *listening* the whole time." Her silver eyes narrowed. "Let them speak. Here. Before my court. Let the dead of my river testify through you — let them say whether the covenant can live again. If they speak truly, the shard is yours. If they cannot speak at all—" the Queen smiled, and it was not a kind smile, "—then you are simply a woman with clever fingers and a stolen child, and my eel-priests will see to the rest."

The chamber went very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, water dripped, counting seconds.

Lior's mouth had gone dry. She was aware of Kess shifting beside her, of Ivo's cold fingers tightening around hers, of the Spire watchers at the edges of the room leaning forward almost imperceptibly, hungry.

Her gift had always been private. That was its nature — hands in earth, whispers through bone, the dead murmuring their half-words to her alone in the dark of her workshop while the valley forgot around her. To open it here, before strangers and enemies, was to strip herself bare in a room full of knives. If she failed, they would take Ivo. If she succeeded, they would mark her — the woman through whom the dead speak — and the Spire would hunt her until the end of her days.

But Ivo was looking up at her. Not afraid. *Certain.* As if the answer were obvious.

Lior knelt in the warm water. It soaked through her trousers, climbed her shins, and she set both palms flat on the flooded floor and closed her eyes.

*Come,* she thought, toward the river. *If you remember. Come.*

For a moment, nothing. The torches crackled. A courtier coughed.

Then the river answered.

It came up through her hands like a tide — not the thin whispers of valley graves but a *flood*, a pressure, a thousand voices braided into one deep note, and Lior gasped because it was too much, far too much, nine generations of oaths and promises and small kept faiths all trying to pour through one pair of human hands. Her arms shook. Her vision whited at the edges. Somewhere far away she heard Kess shout her name.

And then — a smaller warmth settled over her shoulders, light as a shawl. Ivo had knelt beside her and laid both small hands on the back of her neck, and the flood *organized*. The thousand voices sorted themselves, found their places, lined up like beads on a string, and began, together, to speak.

Through Lior's mouth — not her words, she was only the doorway — the dead of the river recited:

"*Current answers current. Shore answers shore. What the river keeps, the mountain keeps; what the mountain breaks, the river mourns. We swore in water and in stone, and water does not forget what stone has sworn.*"

The Delta Queen rose from her throne.

All around the chamber, the courtiers had gone pale. The eel-priests had prostrated themselves flat in the shallows. And the Spire watchers — Lior saw them even with her eyes still full of river-light — the Spire watchers were moving, quietly, unhurriedly, toward the doors.

"*Who speaks?*" the Queen demanded, and her voice cracked like ice on a thawing river. "*Whose oath is that?*"

Through Lior, the dead answered, and the answering voice was suddenly, unmistakably, a single voice — young, clear, and familiar:

"*Mine,*" said Ivo — speaking through Lior's borrowed throat in the old tongue, and then, wonderingly, in words the whole court could understand — "*and mine,*" said the Queen's own grandmother's grandmother, some anchor-wife of nine generations ago, "*and mine,*" said a hundred drowned fishermen and oath-keepers and reed-queens, all of them speaking at once through one kneeling bone-carver, "*we swore, and we were forgotten, and still we keep. Let the shard go home. Let the hinge be found. Let the covenant live again.*"

The last word had barely left Lior's lips when the water of the chamber floor *rose* — all of it at once, a single sheet of golden liquid lifting like a living thing, hanging in the air around the throne, reflecting ten hundred torches — and then fell back with a sound like applause.

Silence. Total, ringing silence.

Then the Delta Queen descended from her reed throne, waded through her own court, and knelt in the water before Lior — a queen kneeling to a bone-carver — and pressed into Lior's hands a shard of something that was neither glass nor crystal nor stone, but caught the torchlight the way moving water does, cool and heavy and humming faintly against her palms.

"The Anchor of Currents," the Queen said quietly. "Kept nine generations for a promise I never believed would be kept. Take it. And bone-carver—" her silver eyes lifted, and for a moment they were not hard at all, only ancient and tired and hopeful, "—when you stand before the others, tell them the river kept faith. Tell them we were never the ones who broke it."

"I will," Lior said. Her voice was raw. "I swear it."

* * *

They left the court through the eastern doors, into the blue hour between torchlight and dawn, and Kess did not relax until they were three canals away and the reed-houses hid them from the island of black stone.

"That went well," he said, in the tone of a man describing a disaster.

"You think?" Lior said. Her hands would not stop shaking. She wrapped the shard in cloth and tucked it against her heart, where it hummed like a second pulse.

"Every spy in that court saw what you are now," Kess said. "Not what you carry — what you *are*. A vessel. A doorway. Do you understand what the Spire will pay for a doorway?" He shook his head. "We need to be on the water before sunrise, and off it by midday, and I want us a hundred miles inland before Serevan's people finish writing their report."

Ivo tugged Lior's sleeve. When Lior looked down, the child was holding out her cupped palms, and in them lay a single river-smoothed pebble, gray and ordinary, except that it glowed — faintly, warmly, the way the drowned boy's charm used to glow.

"A gift," Ivo said. "From the water. It says—" she frowned, translating, "—it says *thank you for carrying us*. It wants to travel with you."

Lior took the pebble. It was warm as a held hand.

Somewhere behind them, across the sleeping delta, a bell began to toll — once, twice, three times — the alarm-rhythm Kess had taught them to listen for, rung from the Court of the Delta's highest tower.

Kess went very still. "That's not for us," he said slowly. "That's a warning *to* us. Someone rang it to wake the whole moot." His face, in the last of the starlight, had gone the color of ash. "Which means someone wanted us running scared. Which means—"

He stopped. Across the water, at the far edge of the outermost ring, where the reed-houses met the hills they had come down three days ago, lights were kindling. Lanterns. Many lanterns, moving in formation, closing along the shore road like a necklace of fire drawing tight.

Lior's hand found Ivo's shoulder and drew her close.

The warden had not waited for a report after all.
