## 8. Ivo Speaks

Ivo had never counted her words before. In the valley, in the snow, in the long dark of the cottage with its smell of bone-dust and tallow, words had simply been there — a river inside her that no one could drink from. She had stopped trying to give them away years ago, or what felt like years ago; time moved strangely when you were small and running. But now she sat on the cold stone lip of the Rivermoot court and counted them, one by one, because Lior was about to open her mouth and speak them for her, and Ivo wanted to know exactly how many of herself she was about to hand over.

Seven words. The opening line of the covenant. *Ael-varessa il dommureth, il dommureth an.* Seven words, and the old tongue wrapped around them like water around stones.

She did not know how to be afraid properly yet. Fear, for Ivo, was still a shape without edges — hunters' lanterns on a ridge, a warden's smile, the way Kess went quiet and looked at doors. But this morning the fear had edges, because this morning the fear had a face: hers.

*They will hear you,* the dead had told Lior, and through Lior's hands pressed warm against Ivo's own, the dead had told *her* too. That was the strange arithmetic of their gift-sharing: whatever passed through Lior's bones left an echo in Ivo's skin.

The court of the river-realm gathered around them like a held breath. Ivo had never seen so many people who were not entirely people. The river-folk stood in ranks along the flooded terraces — tall figures whose lower halves dissolved into current, whose hair moved against gravity, whose eyes were the flat silver of water over stone. Above them, on tiers of wet rock, sat the elder court: three shapes so old they had gone translucent, more memory than flesh, wearing crowns of woven reeds that had rotted and regrown a thousand times. Their faces were turned toward Lior, but their attention — Ivo could feel it, a pressure like deep water — was on her.

Lior knelt at the center of the moot-pool, where the river ran shallow and clear over white stones. Her hands were bare. She had taken off her rings, her gloves, everything between her skin and the world, and Ivo understood that this was Lior's version of the same counting she was doing: stripping down to exactly what would be given.

*I'm ready,* Lior's hands said, in the language of small pressures and angles that they had built together across a hundred miles of road. It was not the old tongue. It was better, maybe. It was theirs.

Ivo pressed her palms against Lior's shoulder blades and closed her eyes.

Here is what Ivo had never told anyone, not even Lior, not even in the wordless way: she had spoken before. Once. A lifetime ago, in a house with a red door, before the men came with their lanterns and their nets of woven silence. She had been very small. Her mother had been singing, and Ivo had sung back, and the song had been in the old tongue because her mother's milk had been full of it, and for one moment the whole kitchen had *glowed* — the cups, the fire, the bread rising in its bowl — and then the door had come down, and her mother had pushed her into the root cellar, and Ivo had learned the most important lesson of her short loud life:

When you speak, they come.

So she had not spoken since. Not aloud. Not in any tongue. She had poured all seven words of herself into Lior instead, teaching them through touch, through the bones of Lior's hands, through nights of patient shaping — *this sound means the river remembers, this sound means the promise is not broken yet* — until Lior could hold them the way you hold something hot and alive.

And now Lior opened her mouth, and Ivo felt the words leave her like birds leaving a cage she hadn't known was locked.

"Ael-varessa il dommureth," Lior said, and her voice was her own voice, low and rough from the road, "il dommureth an."

But it was not only her voice. Ivo heard herself in it, folded under, the way a river holds the sky. And she heard something else too — the dead, all the dead, every murmuring half-word that had ever haunted Lior's carving hands, rising at once like sediment shaken loose in floodwater, and they were not murmuring anymore. They were singing. They were saying the line with her, a thousand throatless voices, and the sound was not sound at all but a shaking in the water, in the stones, in Ivo's teeth.

*Ael-varessa il dommureth.*

*The promise is not broken. The promise is sleeping.*

The pool around Lior went still. Not calm — still, the way a dog goes still before it decides. Every current in the moot-pool stopped moving. The river-folk froze mid-gesture, water-hair hanging motionless, and the three translucent elders leaned forward on their tier of rock with a slowness like continental drift.

Then the river rose.

It did not rise the way floods rise, angry and indiscriminate. It rose the way a sleeper rises when called by name — slowly, gathering itself, turning toward the voice. The water lifted out of its banks in a single unbroken sheet, curving over the terraces, over the frozen court, over Ivo and Lior and Kess standing rigid at the pool's edge, and it did not fall on them. It *looked* at them. Ivo had not known water could look. It hung above them in a vast trembling ceiling, and in it she saw reflections that were not reflections: faces, hundreds of faces, old ones, drowned ones, faces of people who had made promises to this river a thousand years ago and meant them.

One of the faces spoke. Its voice came through the water the way light comes through water, bent and beautiful and slightly wrong.

"*An dommureth varessa?*" it asked. *Who keeps the promise now?*

And Ivo — mute Ivo, careful Ivo, Ivo who had learned that speaking brings men with lanterns — found that the question was aimed at her like an arrow finding the gap in armor, and that she could not bear to let Lior answer it alone.

She stepped forward. Her hand found Lior's shoulder. And she opened her mouth.

No sound came out. Of course no sound came out; her voice had been taken or buried or traded away somewhere behind a red door, and seven words taught through touch do not make a voice. But it didn't matter. Because the moment she tried, Lior's mouth moved instead, and the words came out in Ivo's cadence, Ivo's rhythm, the small fierce music of a child who had learned language in hiding — and the river understood. The river understood *completely*, and knowing it understood, Ivo felt something crack open in her chest that she suspected would never fully close again.

She pointed — at Lior's hands, scarred and steady, at the bone charms strung at Lior's belt, at the shard of pale covenant-stone they had won from this very court two days ago after Lior had let the dead shout through her in front of strangers. Then she pointed at herself. Then she made the sign they had invented on the Road of Small Gods, the one that meant *together*, two fists joined at the wrist.

*We keep it,* she said, without a single sound. *Both of us. All the way.*

The water-ceiling trembled. The drowned faces looked at each other. And then, one by one, they began to nod — a slow ripple passing through the reflection like wind through wheat — and the great sheet of river-water lowered itself gently, reverently, back into its banks, and the current resumed, and the court of the river-realm bowed.

All of it. Even the elders. Even the eldest, the one whose crown had regrown the most times, who had not bowed to anything in living memory. It inclined its translucent head toward a mute child and a bone-carver from a valley that forgot, and the gesture said as clearly as any of the seven words: *the promise is not broken. We have witnesses now.*

Kess made a sound beside them. Ivo glanced over and saw the exile on his knees in the shallows, soaked to the waist, staring at the water with an expression she'd seen once on a starving man given bread — joy and shame fighting for the same face. He crossed himself with a Spire sigil he must have carried since before his disgrace, then seemed embarrassed by it, then did it again anyway.

"I served them eleven years," he said hoarsely, to no one, to the river. "Eleven years, and nobody ever *bowed*."

Ivo felt Lior laugh before she saw it — a shiver through the shoulder under her hand — and for one whole minute, one entire golden minute, Ivo let herself believe the worst part was over. The river had answered. The first shard was truly theirs, witnessed and sealed. The old tongue worked. *She* worked. Maybe the world was not a place of red doors and lanterns after all. Maybe it was a place where rivers rose when little girls tried to speak.

That was when she saw the crows.

There were three of them, perched on the highest terrace among the reed-crowns of the elders, and they were not river-crows. River-crows were fat and iridescent and sang snatches of drowned songs. These were lean, black-eyed, utterly silent, and their feathers had the faint grey sheen of ash. Ivo knew that sheen. Every child who had ever fled the Spire knew it: ash-feathers meant warden-birds, birds fed on burnt spellbooks until they became what they ate.

Two of them were watching the court. The third was watching Ivo.

As she met its eye, it did not startle. It tilted its head — a precise, mechanical, *recording* tilt — and opened its beak, and though no sound came out, Ivo felt the air between them change the way air changes just before lightning. Somewhere, far away, in a tower of hoarded centuries, something was being written down. *The last-born voice speaks through a vessel. The vessel is a bone-carver from the forgotten valley. Location: Rivermoot. Witness: the exiled Kessan Vael, returned.*

The bird closed its beak. Folded its wings. And flew — not away, but up, up past the terraces, past the mist-line, straight as a drawn arrow toward the horizon and the grey smudge beyond it where the Spire stood against the sky like a needle through the world's cloth.

The second and third crows followed. The court had seen them too — Ivo watched the elders' attention slide after the birds, watched the river-folk murmur in their currents — but none of them moved to stop it. This was not their war. It had not been their war for a thousand years. That was rather the point of the covenant, and the point of breaking it.

Lior's hand found hers and squeezed, hard, and through the squeeze came the question they both already knew the answer to: *how long?*

Ivo looked at the fading specks against the clouds and did the arithmetic of fear. Warden-birds flew fast. Wardens rode faster. Serevan — and she knew it would be Serevan; the warden who smelled magic through stone would not delegate a prize like this — would want to intercept them on open road, away from river-realm protection, away from witnesses.

Three days. Perhaps four.

Her hand answered Lior's squeeze with one of its own: *not long enough.*

Behind them, the eldest of the river-court rose from its tier, water streaming off its reed-crown, and spoke in a voice like a mill-wheel turning. Ivo did not need translation. Some things needed no tongue at all.

"You have shown us the promise can wake," the elder said, and the river-folk translated for Kess in a hushed chorus of currents. "Now run, promise-keepers. Run faster than the thing that hoards."
