## 9. What Power Promises

Kess had not slept in a proper bed in eleven years, and he had stopped counting the nights before that. But this one he counted. This one he lay awake for, staring at the smoke-dark rafters of the ferryman's loft above the Rivermoot road, listening to the small sounds of the house below: the tick of the stove cooling, the river muttering to itself in its sleep, and — fainter than either — the sound of Lior Voss breathing in the next room, and Ivo breathing beside her, in the rhythm of a child who had finally, after weeks of running, decided the world was safe enough to be unconscious in.

He should have been asleep. He needed to be asleep. Tomorrow the road bent south, toward Ashmarket, toward the second shard, toward whatever fresh ruin the covenant intended to make of them. But sleep would not come, and Kess knew why, and the knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

The Spire had found him again.

Not with lanterns. Not with hunters. With a letter, tucked into his boot while he'd bathed in the river that afternoon, folded so precisely that he'd known the hand before he'd read a word. *Brother Kess,* it began, in the courteous copperplate of the Order of the Ascendant Flame, *the Spire remembers its own.*

He had read it three times, kneeling in the shallows with the water pulling at his knees, and then he had read it a fourth time because his eyes kept sliding off the middle paragraph as though the words were oiled.

*The child is not a child. You know this now; you have seen it. The covenant-voice cannot be permitted to speak whole. Deliver her to any warden's waystation and your sentence is dissolved. Your name restored to the rolls. Your cell in the east tower re-made as it was, with the window facing the mountain. The Spire forgives what the Spire understands: that you were always more faithful than you were brave.*

*You were more faithful than you were brave.*

That was the knife of it. Not a threat — the Spire had long since stopped threatening Kess; excommunication was a door that only swung one way. It was a compliment. It reached into him and found the truest thing there and held it up to the light, the way you'd hold up a coin to see if it was counterfeit.

He lay in the dark and turned the coin over and over.

* * *

He had been twenty-three when they brought the girl in.

Her name was Wren — no, that was a lie his memory told him now, softening things, giving the child a small brown bird for a name because he could not bear the name she'd actually had. He had made himself forget it, deliberately, in the first year, the way you press a thumb into a healing cut to keep it from itching. He had been proud of that, once. Of the discipline of it. Now the forgetting felt like the worst thing he had ever done, and he had done considerably worse things than forget.

She had come from the fen-country, south of the Spire, where the old water-pacts still half-held and children were sometimes born *listening* — born with the covenant's ear, the way some were born with the covenant's voice. The wardens called them vessels. The ledgers called them assets. The Order, in its kinder moments, called them instruments of order, and Kess had believed that at twenty-three, believed it the way you believe in the floor beneath your feet: totally, and without ever once looking at it.

He had been a junior archivist then. That was the title that let him sleep. *Archivist.* A man of books, of catalogues, of careful hands. What he had actually been, what the title was built to conceal from the man wearing it, was the last step of the taking. The wardens found the children; the binding-priests prepared them; and the archivists — quiet, gentle, unthreatening men with ink-stained fingers — performed the *receiving*. They sat with the child in the white room and asked them, kindly, to recite what they heard. And when the child recited, the archivists wrote it down, and the writing-down was the binding. The covenant's words, once fixed in Spire ink, belonged to the Spire. The child, once emptied of them, belonged to no one.

Wren — the girl, the fen-child, whatever her true name had been — had sat across the table from him with her feet not reaching the floor, and she had swung them, the way children do, and she had asked him if the room had always been so quiet.

"The quietest," he had told her. Kindly. That was the horror of it; he had been *kind*. "That's why they brought you here. You'll help us keep it quiet."

And she had nodded, because adults were to be believed, and she had recited — a fragment, only a fragment, a water-blessing from some fen-shrine that had starved to death a century before she was born — and Kess had written it down in the Spire's black ink, and he had watched the words leave her. He had watched them go the way you watch breath leave a window in winter: a fog of something alive, fading, the glass left clear and cold. She had stopped swinging her feet. She had looked at her own hands as though they had been returned to her by mistake, empty, and she had said, in the small reasonable voice of a child being polite about a disappointment:

"Is that all it wanted?"

He had told her yes. He had told her she'd done beautifully. He had walked her to the dormitory wing himself, because he was kind, because he was thorough, because the Order praised thoroughness in its archivists, and she had taken his hand on the stairs and her hand had been *cold*, cold as the white room, and that was the last he had let himself feel of it for eleven years.

They had told him, afterward, that she had been sent to a satellite chapter-house in the mountains, where vessels lived out their days in comfort. He had believed that too. He had wanted to believe it so badly that belief had cost him nothing, which should have told him everything.

He learned the truth in his ninth year, from a ledger he was not supposed to read. *Received: one vessel, fen-country, age approx. seven. Disposition: rendered. Residue: transferred to central reserve.* *Rendered.* The word they used for animals. The word they used for candles.

He had gone to the Provost of Archives with it. That was the last of his faith: that the Order would be horrified, that some clerical error had occurred, that the Spire he had given his life to could not have done the thing the ledger said in the word it said. The Provost had listened with grave attention, thanked him for his vigilance, and reported him. Not for the discovery — for the *tone*. For the doubt in it. Doubt, in the Spire, was a structural flaw. You did not punish doubt; you amputated it.

They had not tortured him. The Spire was too elegant for that. They had tried him, quietly, in a room with one window facing the mountain, and they had read out his sentence in the same copperplate voice: *expelled, name struck, memory of his service retained for instructional purposes.* They kept his case in the teaching scrolls. *Here,* they told the young archivists, *is what happens to a man who looks at the floor.*

* * *

Now he lay in the ferryman's loft with the letter folded against his heart like a second, colder heartbeat, and below him the true child slept, the last-born voice, the one whose death would make the Spire's power permanent — he had not understood that from the letter, the letter was too careful for that, but he understood it now, he had assembled it over these weeks of road from a hundred small glances at Ivo: at the way fire leaned toward her when she laughed, at the way the starving roadside spirits had pressed their faces to the edge of sight just to be near her, at the way Lior's carved bones rang like struck glass when the girl spoke in her sleep.

*Rendered,* he thought. *Residue: transferred.*

And the Spire was offering him his window back. His cell. His name. Eleven years of exile, undone, in exchange for one walk to a waystation with a child's hand in his — and he knew exactly how he'd do it, too, that was the filth of it, he knew the method because he had *invented* half of it. You didn't seize a covenant-child. You grew trusted. You became the kind one, the patient one, the one who carried her when the road got steep. You waited for a bad night, a fever, a moment of fear, and then you said *come with me, I know a safe place*, and covenant-children had been raised on the covenant's oldest law — *follow the trusted voice* — and they came. They always came. They came holding your hand.

He could do it in a week. He could do it in three days. Lior trusted him — not fully, she was not a fool, but functionally, the way you trust a bridge you have no other way across. And Ivo — Ivo liked him. Ivo had begun, in the last few days, to bring him things: a smooth stone, a feather, a cup of water carried with the immense solemnity of a child performing a sacrament. She was learning to give. The Spire would hollow that out of her and call it order.

He turned the coin again. *You were more faithful than you were brave.* Yes. God help him, yes. And faithfulness, the letter whispered between its lines, was not a thing you lost. It was a thing you had *deferred*. The Spire did not want his repentance; it wanted his return, and it could wait, because the Spire had always understood the one truth Kess had taken eleven years to learn:

That a man does not betray once. A man betrays along a gradient. Each step small, each step reasonable, each step taken in kindness, until the floor of him is made entirely of steps and there is no ground left anywhere to stand on.

He had stood on that floor once already. He knew its exact texture.

* * *

Near dawn he gave up on sleep and went down to the kitchen, and found he was not the only one awake.

Lior sat at the table with her bones spread before her on a cloth, the fire banked to embers, her hands hovering over the pieces without touching them. She looked up when he came in, and in the ember-light her face had the carved quality he had first mistaken for coldness and had since learned was simply what a lifetime of listening to the dead did to a living face: it stopped you performing.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"Neither did you."

"I slept. The dead don't, and they keep me polite about it." She nudged the cloth aside, making room, and he sat, because she had made room, and there was a terrible comfort in that — in being given a place at a table by a woman who did not yet know what he was holding against his heart.

For a while neither spoke. The river spoke instead, low and constant, and Kess thought: *the realms used to talk to each other like that. Constantly. That's what we broke.*

"I'm going to tell you something," he said, "and then I'm going to ask you to decide something, and you should decide it with every piece of your mind."

Lior's hands went still above the cloth.

"The Spire contacted me today. While I was in the river. A letter." He laid it on the table between them, face up, the copperplate stark in the ember-light. "They want the child. And they're offering to un-exile me for her delivery. My name, my cell, my window facing the mountain. Everything they took."

Lior read the letter the way she did everything — completely, and without hurry, and without a single flicker of the face she must have been making inside. When she finished she looked at him for a long moment.

"You're telling me this because you're going to refuse."

"I'm telling you this because I *want* to refuse, and I don't trust the difference between wanting and doing. Not in me." His voice came out lower than he intended, scraped raw by the sleepless dark. "You should know what I did, Lior. Before you cross another border with me. Before you sleep another night under the same roof. I was the one who took them. Not with chains — with *kindness*. I sat in a white room with a seven-year-old girl who trusted the ink-stained man, and I asked her to recite, and I wrote down everything she was until there was nothing left, and they called it *receiving* and I called it *service*, and I believed in it. I believed in it the way you believe in the floor."

The embers ticked. Somewhere above them Ivo turned over in her sleep, and both of them went silent until she settled.

"What was her name?" Lior asked at last. Quietly. Not cruelly. The way you ask a man to hand over the last thing he's hiding behind.

"I don't know," Kess said, and the confession of *that* — of the deliberate forgetting, of the discipline of it, of the pride — came out of him in a flood then, the fen-country and the swinging feet and the cold hand on the stairs and *is that all it wanted*, and Lior listened to all of it without moving, without softening, without looking away, which was the only mercy he could have borne.

When he was done, she was silent for a long time. Then she said: "The dead I listen to — most of them aren't angry. People think they'd be angry. Mostly they're just *unfinished*. A sentence that never got its last word." She folded her hands. "You're unfinished, Kess. That's not the same as forgiven. But it's not the same as damned, either."

"And if the letter comes again?" he asked. "If they raise the price? They will. They always raise the price. Next time it won't be a window. Next time it'll be her — they'll tell me they can undo a rendering. They'll tell me the fen-girl is alive in the reserve, recoverable, if only I deliver one more." He heard his own voice shake and did not fight it. "I need you to understand that I will *want* it. Whatever they offer. I will want it the way a drowning man wants air, and I will hate myself for wanting it, and I need to know — I need to know if you can travel with a man who carries that in him. Because the temptation isn't going to stay in that letter. It's going to walk beside us. It's going to sit at this table. It's going to learn Ivo's laugh."

Lior looked at him across the dead men's cloth and the banked fire, and she did not offer him absolution, because she was not a woman who dealt in cheap coin.

"Then it sits at the table," she said. "And it watches us not do it. Every day. That's the only cure I know for a thing like that — you don't kill it, you outlive it, one refusal at a time, until one morning it's smaller than you are." She began gathering her bones, one by one, into the cloth. "Now sleep. We cross into the ash-country tomorrow, and Ashmarket eats the unprepared."

He climbed back to the loft as the sky went grey, and he did sleep, finally, a thin and dreaming sleep in which a girl with cold hands took his on a staircase and asked him if that was all it wanted, and he answered her — at last, eleven years late — *no*.

He woke to shouting.

It came from the road below, and then from the yard, and when he reached the window the ferryman was standing in his doorway pointing south, and Kess followed his finger to the horizon, where the clean grey of morning had been stained — a long, low smear of it, rising, widening, the color of a city's worth of something burning, or a city's worth of ash on the wind, and beneath it, faint on the air, came a sound he had hoped never to hear again outside the Spire's walls: bells. Many bells. Ringing not in alarm, but in *greeting*.

Ashmarket knew they were coming.
