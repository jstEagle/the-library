## 10. The Second Shard: Ashmarket

They smelled the city before they saw it — a smell like a hearth that had never been put out properly, like bread and char and old smoke soaked so deep into stone that no rain could ever wash it free.

Kess called it Ashmarket, and said it with his collar pulled up and his eyes down, the way he did everything in cities now. "Built on a god," he said. "A real one, not one of the small roadside spirits. It burned four hundred years ago, and they built the market on its ribs because there was nowhere else flat enough."

"And nobody thought that was disrespectful?" Lior asked.

"Nobody here thinks anything is disrespectful," Kess said. "That's what makes it work."

The road crested a hill of slag and cinder-grass, and Ashmarket opened below them like a wound that had learned to smile. It was evening, and the city was already lit — but not with lamps, or not only. Fires burned in iron braziers at every corner, and the light they threw was the wrong color, a deep sullen orange, as if the flames remembered being something larger and were sulking about it. Above the rooftops rose the ribs themselves: vast arcs of blackened bone, each one taller than the Spire's distant silhouette on the horizon, arched over whole districts like the vaulting of a cathedral whose architect had died mid-prayer. Houses clung to the ribs. Stairs spiraled up them. Laundry hung between two vertebrae the size of hills.

Ivo stopped walking. She stood very still in the road, her bare feet — she still refused shoes most days, though Lior had long since stopped fighting it — planted in the ash-grey dust, and her face turned up toward the bones.

"What is it?" Lior asked.

Ivo's hands moved. She had been teaching Lior her language for weeks now, a few words at a time, patiently, the way one teaches a child — except that Ivo was the child and Lior was the slow one, fumbling signs that Ivo had been signing since before she could walk. *Old*, Ivo signed. Then she hesitated, searching, and settled on a word Lior knew: *hurt*.

"The god?" Kess said. "It's dead. Dead things don't hurt."

But Lior's hands were already answering her. They had gone cold and heavy in the way they did when the dead pressed close, and through her palms, faint as a pulse under ice, came something enormous and slow. Not words. Words were what the small dead gave her — murmurs, half-sentences, the leavings of ordinary lives. This was deeper than language. It was the feeling of a body lying still for four centuries while people built markets on its chest, and it was not angry, exactly. Anger would have been easier. It was *sad*, in the way mountains are sad, in a register no living throat could hold.

"It hurts," Lior said quietly. "She's right. It hurts all the time, and it has gotten so used to hurting that it's stopped expecting anything else." She flexed her fingers, trying to shake off the weight. "We shouldn't stay longer than we have to."

"We have to stay exactly as long as it takes," Kess said, "to get the shard out of the hands of Mother Veyra, who is — I want to be clear about this — not a mother, not a priest in any way the old orders would recognize, and not someone you want to owe a favor to. She runs the name-trade. Half the city's debts, feuds, and marriages pass through her ledgers. And the other half passes through her other ledger, the one nobody sees, where she keeps names the way other people keep coin."

"Stolen names," Lior said. She had heard of such things only in stories her grandmother told to frighten children, back when the valley still had grandmothers who told stories. A name was not just a sound. It was the shape a life made in the world, and if you held someone's true name — their first one, the one given before memory — you held a hook in them that no distance could pull free.

"She didn't steal them all," Kess said. "Some she bought from people too desperate or too stupid to know what they were selling. Some she won at cards. One or two, they say, she took from the dead, which is why the Spire has never moved against her. Even Serevan finds her useful." He said the warden's name carefully, without heat, the way you'd handle a knife by the blade. Since the river-realm, since the spies who had watched Ivo speak the covenant's opening line and the water rise in answer, they had all been careful of that name. "There's an inn near the Rib-Walk called the Kindling. We'll be safe there for a night. As safe as anyone is."

---

The Kindling's common room was warm and loud and full of the particular smell of a city that had never once been clean: tallow, wet wool, frying onions, and underneath it all, always, the char. Lior sat with her back to the wall and her hands wrapped around a clay cup of wine she wasn't drinking, and watched Ivo eat bread with the total concentration she brought to everything, tearing it into pieces and lining them up before eating them in order of size.

"You're staring," Kess said.

"I'm thinking."

"That's what worries me. You get a look when you're thinking. Like a woman deciding how to tell someone bad news." He turned his cup. In the weeks since Rivermoot, since the court of the drowned and the moment Lior had let the dead speak through her before a hall of watching strangers, something had shifted between them. He no longer performed his cynicism quite so loudly. It was still there, worn like a coat he couldn't take off, but underneath it he kept glancing at Ivo when he thought no one saw, and his voice had lost its edge whenever he spoke to her. "Whatever Veyra asks for, it'll be worse than coin. Coin she has. She deals in things people can't buy back."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've seen what happens to people who think they can bargain with the name-priests and walk away whole. There's a man three tables down missing his shadow. Sold it. There's a woman by the fire who sold her grief after her son died, and now she laughs at everything, everything, and her eyes are like two holes in her face." He leaned closer. "Lior. What will you give her?"

Lior looked at Ivo, who had finished her bread and was now watching the room with those dark, unchildlike eyes, cataloguing everyone, missing nothing. The dead murmured faintly through Lior's palms even here, even in this city of the living — thinner than usual, muffled, as if the burned god's vast sadness lay over every smaller voice like snowfall. But they were there. They were always there.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But I know what I won't. I won't give her Ivo. I won't give her my hands. And I won't give her any more of myself than I have to."

Kess almost smiled. "You know, that's the same speech everyone makes. Right up until they don't."

---

Mother Veyra received them the next morning in a chapel that had never been consecrated to anything.

It stood in the hollow of one of the great ribs, a space scooped and polished until the blackened bone gleamed like obsidian, and it was hung — this was the detail Lior could not stop looking at — with thousands of small clay tablets, each one stamped with a name, strung on cords from the rib-vault above so that they chimed softly when the draft moved through. Names. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each tablet a person, or a piece of one, hanging in the smoky light like wind chimes made of lives.

Veyra herself sat behind a table of black wood, a broad, comfortable-looking woman in grey robes with a face like a friendly butcher — pink, open, lined around the eyes with genuine laugh-wrinkles. That was the worst part, Lior thought later. She looked kind. She looked like someone's grandmother, if someone's grandmother had spent forty years learning exactly what everything was worth.

"The bone-carver," Veyra said, and her voice was warm and unhurried. "From the silent valley. Yes, I heard you were coming. I hear most things." Her gaze slid to Ivo, and Lior stepped — just slightly, just enough — between them. Veyra's smile deepened by a millimeter, as if Lior had confirmed something pleasant. "And the exile, and the little mute. What a family you've become. Sit, sit. Tea?"

"No tea," Kess said.

"Suit yourself. More for me." She poured anyway, three cups, and pushed them aside untasted. "You want the second shard. Of course you do; everyone wants something, that's the whole architecture of this city. Now. Here is my problem." She folded her plump hands. "The shard is mine. I paid for it — not in coin, in years, in favors, in one very unpleasant decade of my life I don't intend to describe. If I simply gave it to you, I would be telling every priest, thief, and lord in Ashmarket that my holdings can be taken by asking nicely. So you must pay. And it must be a price worth the story, do you understand? The payment matters less than what the payment says about me."

"What do you want?" Lior asked.

Veyra studied her for a long moment, and then, instead of answering, rose and walked to the far wall of the rib-hollow, where the tablets hung thickest. She lifted one down from its cord — Lior noticed her hands were gloved, fine grey silk — and brought it back to the table.

"This," Veyra said, "is a name I have never been able to sell."

The tablet was old, older than the others, its clay fired dark and dense. Stamped into it was a name in no script Lior recognized — angular, deliberate, each character carved rather than pressed. And even before her fingers brushed it, even before the dead spoke, Lior felt the wrongness of it, the way you feel a cold draft from a door that should be shut.

"A man died owing me," Veyra said. "Not money. A debt of service, sworn and witnessed, and he died rather than pay it. His name came to me by right of contract — the old law, the law older than your valley's covenant, if you can believe such a thing exists. But a name is only currency if its owner fears you. If its owner is dead—" she tapped the tablet with one gloved finger— "and if the owner's ghost *refuses* to acknowledge the debt, the name goes quiet. Worthless. Do you know what a dead man's refusal does to my reputation? Every debtor in this city checks whether old Corvess still hangs silent on my wall, and every one of them draws conclusions."

Through Lior's palms, faint and furious, came a voice. An old man's voice, dry and proud and unbending: *I owed her nothing. I owed her nothing. Let the tablet hang. Let it hang forever.*

"He's been dead eleven years," Veyra said, watching Lior's face with bright, patient interest. "And he has argued with me every single day. Oh, don't look so shocked, carver. I know what you are. The dead whisper to you; it's in every report from the river-realm, and reports travel faster than rivers. You speak with ghosts. You carve their voices into bone." She leaned forward. "So here is my price, and it is a fair one, and it is the only one. Make me a charm that silences him. Permanently. Not a tablet on a wall he can ignore — a working, a true carving, that closes his mouth in whatever country the dead go to. When his voice stops, the name becomes mine in truth, and the shard becomes yours. That is the trade. Take it or leave it."

The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere above, the tablets chimed in the draft, hundreds of small clay voices saying nothing at all.

"You're asking me to murder a ghost," Lior said.

"I'm asking you to collect a debt," Veyra said pleasantly. "The difference between those two things is mostly a matter of which side of the table you're sitting on."

---

They walked back through the morning market in silence, or near it — Ivo's hand in Lior's, Kess a pace ahead, shoulders hunched. Around them the city roared and bargained and lived, vendors crying fish and charcoal and secondhand shoes, children chasing each other between the legs of the crowd, and overhead the great black ribs held it all up like the skeleton of something that had loved the world once and been repaid with smoke.

At a quiet corner, beneath a rib-arch where the noise fell away, Ivo stopped and tugged Lior's sleeve.

Her hands moved, quick and grave. *The old man. He is afraid.*

"You heard him?" Lior signed back, and Ivo nodded. Of course she'd heard. Ivo heard everything the covenant touched; she was its last-born voice, the thing the whole broken world had been waiting for without knowing it. "He's afraid of being silenced. Of ending. Of — going somewhere even the dead don't go."

Ivo considered. Then, slowly: *Is it ending? Or is it sleeping?*

Lior hadn't taught her that distinction. No one had. It was the question Lior herself had been turning over since dawn, since Veyra's warm butcher's smile, since the feel of that furious old voice pressing against her palms — *let it hang, let it hang forever* — and it stopped her where she stood.

Because here was the truth she had been avoiding: she did not know. She knew what her charms did for the dead — gave them a channel, a shape, a way to be heard a little longer in a world that had stopped listening. She had always believed that when a voice finally faded, it faded onward, into whatever comes next, the way a river reaches the sea. But Veyra was not asking for a fading. Veyra was asking for a cutting. A charm that closed a mouth *in whatever country the dead go to*. And Lior did not know, could not know, whether the country of the dead had doors, or whether a silenced voice simply ceased — unwitnessed, unmourned, erased from even the memory of the world.

*I owed her nothing,* the old man had said, eleven years without rest, eleven years of pride holding him upright like a splint. Proud. Stubborn. Afraid, under it, the way all the dead were afraid, the way they pressed against Lior's hands not because she was special but because she was *warm*.

"Lior." Kess had come back and was standing close, his voice low. "Don't do it."

"You heard."

"I always hear. Look — find another way. Steal the shard. I know thieves here, good ones, ones who owe me—"

"You've been running from the Spire for six years because you stole something once," Lior said quietly, "and it cost a child everything. Are you offering to teach me that lesson twice?"

He flinched as if she'd struck him. She hadn't meant it as a blow; it came out as one anyway, and she watched him absorb it, jaw tight, and say nothing, because there was nothing to say.

"There might be another way," she went on, gentler. "I keep looking for one. But Kess — the warden's spies marked us at the river. Every day we spend haggling, Serevan's net draws tighter around Ivo. And the third shard is in a tomb guarded by the ghosts of the people who broke the covenant. If I can't face one stubborn dead man and one crime-lord grandmother, I have no business walking into that tomb." She looked up at the ribs, at the smoke-stained vault of the burned god's chest. "I just need to understand what I'm actually doing before I do it. That's all. Give me tonight."

---

She worked by lamplight in the inn's smallest room, door barred, Ivo asleep on the pallet beside her — or seemingly asleep; Lior had learned that Ivo's sleep was shallow and watchful, a habit of a child who had been hunted across half the world.

Before her on the table lay a piece of bone. Not human — she would not, could not — but ox-bone, dense and pale, cut and sanded smooth. Her knives were laid out in their worn leather roll, each one known to her hands the way old friends are known. And beside the bone, wrapped in cloth, sat the clay tablet Veyra had grudgingly lent her for the night, gloved hands and witnessed transfer and a contract drawn up in triplicate, because even lending a stolen name required paperwork in Ashmarket.

*I owed her nothing.*

The voice came through the tablet thin and tired now, the fury worn down to a pebble smoothed by eleven years of water. Through the lamplight Lior listened to him the way she listened to all the dead: completely, patiently, without judgment, because judgment was useless to the dead and listening was the only gift she had ever really owned.

His name had been Corvess. He had been a tally-keeper, a man of exact accounts, and he had sworn service to a predecessor of Veyra's in exchange for — Lior pried at the memory gently, the way you pry at a knot — in exchange for a daughter's life. A fever year. A promise made at a bedside. And then the priestess died, and the debt passed down like an heirloom, and Corvess had decided, in his precise and stubborn heart, that a debt to a dead woman died with her, and no one — not Veyra, not the law, not death itself — would make him pay otherwise.

Except that he had died instead. Eleven years ago. And discovered, presumably to his horror, that debts outlive the debtor.

"You know," Lior said aloud, softly, to the tablet, "you're right. By any decent accounting, you owe her nothing. Debts to the dead should die with the dead. That's the oldest law there is, older than contracts." She turned the bone in her hands. "But that's not what she's buying, is it. She's not buying justice. She's buying your silence. And your silence isn't hers to buy, because your voice was never hers. Your voice belongs to you, and after you, it belongs to whatever comes next."

The voice in the tablet was quiet. Listening. The dead always listened best when you stopped performing for them.

Lior picked up her finest knife.

Here was the compromise she had arrived at, turning it over all night like a stone with a sharp edge: she could not refuse. Refusal meant delay, delay meant the warden, the warden meant Ivo in a white room at the top of the Spire with her voice peeled out of her like fruit from its skin. Lior had weighed it a hundred ways since dawn and the scale never tipped differently. So she would pay. But she would pay in her own currency, on her own terms, and if Veyra wanted a silencing, Veyra would get a silencing — done properly, done mercifully, done the way Lior did everything: with the dead man's own consent wrung honestly from him, or not at all.

"Here is what I can offer you, Corvess, tally-keeper," she said to the tablet. "Not erasure. Sleep. A charm that closes your mouth and closes your account both — that carries you past this argument, past this wall, past eleven years of standing in a chapel refusing to admit you're tired. I can't promise you what's on the other side of sleep. Nobody can. But I can promise you won't hang on her wall anymore. And I can promise you'll go as yourself — proud, stubborn, paid in full — and not as a trophy." Her hands were steady. Her voice nearly wasn't. "It's not the trade she thinks she's making. But it's the only trade I'll make. Will you take it?"

The silence stretched so long the lamp guttered. On the pallet, Ivo's breathing had gone very quiet and very awake.

Then, through the clay, faint as a hand raised in an assembly of one: *Paid in full. Witnessed.*

"Witnessed," Lior repeated, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because some vows deserved the flaw. "Then witness it, whoever's listening. God of this city, if you can hear me under all that ash — witness."

She began to carve.

It took her until the grey hour before dawn, the finest work of her life, every line of it a closing and a blessing at once — the account balanced, the ledger shut, the mouth closed not in violence but in rest. When it was done, the charm was a small thing, a rib of bone no longer than her finger, incised with a sleeping figure and a settled sum. She held it in her palm, and through her skin she felt the old man's voice gather itself, and grow quiet, and — there was no other word for it — *lie down*. The murmur faded the way a fire fades when it's banked, not the way it dies when it's drowned. Warmth, then embers, then a gentle dark.

In the silence afterward, Lior sat with her head in her hands and felt the shape of what she had done settle onto her shoulders. It was not murder. She would tell herself it was not murder, and some mornings she would believe it. But it was a door she had opened in herself — proof that she could be moved, that her beliefs had a price, that the line she'd sworn stood fixed could be negotiated down to a line slightly to the left. Every person who ever did harm had crossed some line like this one, she thought, at some grey dawn, for reasons that felt like mercy at the time.

A small hand touched her arm. Ivo knelt beside her in the lamplight, hair sleep-tangled, eyes ancient and soft. She looked at the charm, and at Lior's face, and she did not sign anything at all. She only leaned her head against Lior's shoulder, and stayed there, warm and breathing, while outside the window the fires of Ashmarket burned their sullen orange and the great ribs held up the sky.

* * *

Mother Veyra turned the charm over twice in her gloved hands, held it to her ear as though expecting it to speak, and smiled her grandmother's smile.

"Clever," she said. "Not what I asked for. Better than what I asked for, which is rarer than you'd think." From beneath the black table she drew a wooden case, and from the case, on a bed of grey velvet, the second shard: a sliver of something that was not quite glass and not quite stone, catching the brazier-light and holding it wrong, the way ice holds summer. "A bargain concluded. Witnessed and witnessed again. Go safely, bone-carver."

Lior took the shard, and it hummed against her palm like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing, and she was at the chapel door, Ivo's hand in hers, before Veyra spoke again — lightly, idly, the way one mentions weather.

"One more thing, since you've paid so fairly. A courtesy, freely given." The tablets chimed overhead in the draft. "There are men in my chapel most mornings who aren't buying names. Grey cloaks. Very polite. They asked after a mute child from the silent valley, and I told them nothing, because I liked your price." Her smile widened by that same millimeter. "But courtesies end at my door, carver. And they left not an hour ago — toward the tomb-road, where I'm told you're headed next. The Hollow Crown, they call it. Do travel quickly."

Behind them, high in the rib-vault, a thousand clay names stirred in the wind, and for one moment Lior's hands went cold with a whisper that was not Corvess — a whisper like frost forming, like a warden's patience — before the draft died and the chapel was only a chapel again, and the road ahead was already darkening with the shapes of men in grey.
