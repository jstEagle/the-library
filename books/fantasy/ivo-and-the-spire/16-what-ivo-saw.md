## 16. What Ivo Saw

Ivo had learned to count in Lior's language before she learned to speak it.

She counted the way Lior counted: the strokes of a carving knife, the turns of bread dough, the breaths between one of Kess's jokes and the next. Counting was how you loved things, she had decided. You counted them so you would know exactly what you stood to lose.

So she counted what she saw at the Hollow Crown. She counted the ghosts — nine of them, though only seven still had faces. She counted the words they demanded, and the price they named, and the seconds it took for Lior to step forward before anyone else could move.

*Me,* Lior had said. Not bravely. Not like the heroes in the songs Kess half-remembered from his years in the Spire's lower halls. She said *me* the way she said *yes* to a commission, or *no* to a bad trade — plainly, with her hands already moving to do it.

And Ivo, standing in the tomb's cold doorway with the old tongue rising in her throat like water finding its level, had understood everything.

That was the thing no one guessed about her. They thought her silence was emptiness, a cup waiting to be filled. Even Lior sometimes spoke slowly to her, kindly, as if the words might need somewhere soft to land. But Ivo was not empty. She was full — fuller than any of them knew — because the covenant lived in her the way marrow lives in bone, and marrow knows everything the bone endures.

She had understood the ghosts' bargain. She had understood the shape of the hinge they described: not a person who carries magic, but a person magic passes *through*, forever, the way water passes through a lock. The hinge holds the gate. The hinge does not walk through it.

The hinge stays on the borderlands. The hinge does not go home. The hinge does not sleep deeply enough to dream, does not love closely enough to be held, because every hour of her remaining life she will feel the realms pulling through her ribs like tides through a sea-cave — and if she ever lets go, even once, even to rest, the gates slam shut and everything they have done comes undone.

Lior had heard *sacrifice*. Ivo had heard *sentence*.

She had tried to say so. That was the worst part, remembering it now by the banked fire while Kess snored against his pack and Lior slept sitting up, her knife still in her hand out of habit older than comfort. Ivo had opened her mouth and the old tongue came out — great rolling phrases about hinges and thresholds and the weight of doors — and she had watched Lior's face go gentle and determined at once, the way it did when a customer argued about the price of a charm, and Ivo knew that Lior had heard none of it. Or worse: had heard all of it, and stepped forward anyway.

*I know,* that face said. *I hear you. And me.*

Ivo had spent four days since then learning Lior's language properly. Not the counting words, not the bread words, but the grammar underneath — the way promises were built there, the way futures were shaped. It was a language made for carving, and carving was made for permanence, and so when Lior said *I will*, the sentence did not bend afterward. It set, like bone in resin.

Which meant Ivo could not argue her out of it. Which meant there was only one argument left, and it was not made of words at all.

* * *

She slipped away an hour before dawn, when the night is thinnest and the world least willing to notice anything.

She had prepared. That too, no one would have guessed — the mute child, the foundling, preparing like a general. She had watched Lior pack for journeys all their road together, and she packed the same way: bread wrapped in cloth (she had learned the folding; hers was clumsy but sound), the waterskin, the three shards wrapped separately so they would not speak to each other in the dark. Two of them she could carry. The third — the one from the Hollow Crown, surrendered grudgingly once Lior gave her word — sat in Lior's own keeping, and taking it would mean waking her, and waking her would mean losing.

So she left it. One shard. Two in her pack, heavy as sleeping hearts. It would have to be enough, and if it was not enough, then it was not enough, and better that than the other thing.

Better any ending than watching Lior become a door.

Kess stirred as she crossed the campsite. She froze, one foot raised, and for a long moment his breathing changed — quickened, caught, the breathing of a man surfacing from bad dreams into worse ones. His eyes opened a slit. In the gray pre-light she saw him see her: the pack, the direction of her gaze down the slope, away from the tomb, toward the Spire.

He knew. Of course he knew. He had carried a child to the Spire once before, and he had spent every mile since trying to carve that sin out of himself, and he recognized the shape of a child walking toward a tower the way a drowning man recognizes water.

"Ivo," he whispered.

She put one finger to her lips. Then, because he deserved more than silence, she signed to him in the language they had invented together on the road — the small language of shared watches and stolen moments, *bread* and *cold* and *are you well* and *do not tell*.

His jaw worked. His hand crept toward where his knife lay, and stopped, and she understood: he was weighing whether to stop her by force, and finding that he could not, because stopping her meant either waking Lior or letting her go alone, and both were unbearable, and he was a man well-practiced now in choosing the unbearable thing that left the most people whole.

*Watch her,* Ivo signed. *When she wakes. Watch her.*

Something broke a little in his face. He nodded, once, and turned his back to her — deliberately, fully, giving her the gift of not being seen leave.

She went.

* * *

The borderlands at dawn were beautiful in the way of things that do not care about you.

Frost silvered the dead grass, and the light came up low and gold through a country of thin trees and older stones, and everywhere — everywhere — Ivo could feel the seams. This close to the Hollow Crown, the broken covenant was not an abstraction. It was weather. She felt the realms pressing against each other through the tears in the old promises, river-realm bleeding into ash-realm bleeding into the hollow dark places that had never signed anything at all, and all of it straining toward her because she was the last-born voice, the final line of a document everyone else had abandoned, and magic wanted nothing so much as to be spoken.

If she reached the Spire and offered herself — her voice, freely given, instead of harvested — the rite would take her differently. She did not know this from learning. She knew it the way she knew bread needed salt: it was in her, structural, true. A voice given willingly could hold the covenant open without becoming its prisoner. A voice taken by force became a lock. The Spire had never understood the difference, which was why everything the Spire built groaned and cracked and needed children to keep it standing.

But the Spire would not ask nicely. The Spire would take, and taking was the whole of its architecture.

It didn't matter. Fear was a small country and she had emigrated from it some miles back, somewhere around the second frozen stream, and what remained was purpose, clean and narrow as a blade.

She thought about Lior as she walked. Not the big thoughts — not *mother*, though the word had been growing in her chest for weeks now, two languages deep, unsaid. She thought about small things. The way Lior's hands looked when they worked, sure and scarred, turning death into keepsakes. The way she hummed off-key when the dough was rising. The way she had stood in the snow on the first night, barefoot Ivo shivering at her door, and lied — lied badly, beautifully, for the first time in her careful life — rather than give up a stranger's child.

*I chose you before I knew your name,* that lie had said. *You are mine to keep.*

And Ivo had thought, all this road long, that she owed a debt. But debts were the wrong shape for it. What she felt was not owing. It was matching. Lior had given her a self, and the only gift equal to a self was a future, and Lior was about to sign hers away, and so Ivo would sign hers first, quickly, quietly, before the ink of Lior's promise could dry.

Let the hinge be me, she told the frost, the stones, the thin gold light. Let the hinge be the one who was born for it. Let her go home and make bread and carve charms for drowned boys and grow old wrong-footed and beloved. Let me be the door. Doors don't mind staying shut. Doors were made for it.

The frost did not answer. But far ahead, past the hills, the Spire had come into view — a black needle stitching sky to earth, drinking the morning, and around its base the warden's fires burned in rings like a crown of lit teeth.

They were already beginning. She could feel it in her marrow: the rite's first movements, the great machine of the Spire warming its gears, hunting for a voice. For *her* voice. Serevan had marked them at Rivermoot; his spies had followed them since Ashmarket; and now, with the third shard won and the covenant nearly whole, he had stopped pretending to hunt and started building the cage.

Ivo tightened the straps of her pack and walked toward it, small and swift and certain, a child-shaped key going to fit itself into a terrible lock.

Behind her, miles back, she felt — not heard, *felt*, the way you feel someone say your name across a crowded room — Lior wake.

Felt the silence where a sleeper should shift. Felt the moment Kess said her name, gently, and then the longer silence after, the one with a whole heart breaking inside it.

Felt Lior stand up.

*I'm sorry,* Ivo said, in the old tongue, in Lior's tongue, in the wordless language of the road, in every language she had. *I'm sorry. I learned counting from you. I know exactly what I'm losing.*

And far behind her, faint as a heartbeat, she felt Lior begin to run.
