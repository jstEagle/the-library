## 1. The Valley That Refuses Magic

The border of Thornvale is a line I have drawn four hundred and eleven times, and it has never once held still for me.

I know that sounds like a complaint. It isn't. A cartographer who wanted her lines to hold still would be in the wrong trade entirely — she'd want to be a stonemason, or a gravedigger, or one of those dismal people who paint the same bowl of pears every year until the pears outlive them. No: the trouble with Thornvale's border is not that it moves. The trouble is that it moves *deliberately*, with what I can only describe as spite, and it does so specifically when I am trying to measure it.

Today was no exception. I had my good compass — the brass one with the mother-of-pearl face, a gift from my mother back when she still gave gifts — balanced on the flat stone at the valley's western lip, and I watched its needle describe slow, thoughtful circles, like a cat deciding where to sit. North was somewhere in there, presumably. The needle had simply declined to commit.

"Come on," I told it. "You've done this before."

The needle wobbled, considered, and pointed decisively at a birch tree.

"That's not north."

It stayed pointed at the birch tree with the confidence of an instrument that has never once been wrong.

This is the thing about Thornvale that no map outside the valley will ever show you, because no map outside the valley knows: magic cannot cross our border. Not *won't*. *Can't.* Something about the valley itself — the shape of the hills, some accident of stone and water and old, old intention — refuses it. Spells die at the treeline like moths against glass. Enchanted compasses spin. Witches who visit from the low country complain of headaches and leave early, offended. And inside, we live our small, dry, utterly reliable lives, lit by candle and lamplight, warmed by honest firewood, navigated by honest instruments that point at birch trees when they feel like it.

I have spent thirty-one years mapping this refusal. Every stream that bends wrong around a standing stone. Every hollow where sound goes flat. Every place where the world's magic presses up against the valley's edge like a tide against a seawall, and finds no purchase. Four hundred and eleven surveys. I could draw the border blindfolded, and have, as an experiment, though the results were too embarrassing to keep.

And here is the confession I keep locked in the drawer with my spare nibs: in all those years, I have never once seen what lies on the other side of my own line.

Not properly. Oh, I've stood at the edge and looked out — everyone has. There are pastures out there, and a road, and eventually the low towns with their bells and their markets. But looking isn't seeing. Looking is what you do through a window. I have charted the exact point where Thornvale ends and the rest of the world begins, down to the pace and the half-pace, and I have never crossed it. Why would I? Everything worth mapping is here. Everything worth knowing fits inside my maps.

That's what I tell myself, anyway, in the voice I use for telling myself things. It's a very convincing voice. It sounds exactly like contentment.

* * *

The Lamplight Inn sits close enough to the western border that on clear evenings you can watch the light fail along the line where the valley gives way to the world. I arrived at half past six, ink-stained to the elbow, smelling of oak gall and river mud, carrying my rolled survey under one arm like a reluctant child.

He was already there, of course. Ivo Thistlewood is always already there. It's one of his most irritating qualities, along with his habit of saving seats by draping his coat over them as though the coat were doing him a personal favor, and the way he looks up when you come through a door as if he'd been counting the seconds since he last saw you and intends to tell you so.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm punctual. You're early. These are different crimes."

"Yours is worse." He pushed the coat off the chair opposite him — his coat, brown wool, one mended elbow, which I would later watch him drop in a doorway and never see again, though of course I didn't know that yet; forgive me, I'm getting ahead of the story, and stories about borders do tend to run ahead of themselves — and I sat, and he looked at the survey tube leaning against my knee with the expression of a man greeting a rival.

"Did it behave?"

"The border? Never does. The needle pointed at a birch tree for a full quarter hour."

"Which birch?"

"The one by the ford."

"Hm. That birch has always struck me as directional." He signaled the innkeep for another cup, then paused. "Two cups," he corrected. "She'll pretend she doesn't want one and then drink all of mine."

"I would never."

"She absolutely will," he told the innkeep gravely, and the innkeep, who has known me since I was small enough to eat chalk, nodded with the weary assent of long experience.

Here is what I should explain about Ivo, and why a first date between two people who have known each other their whole lives is not the contradiction it appears. We grew up three lanes apart. He pulled my hair at the harvest fair when we were seven; I pushed him into the millpond when we were nine, which everyone agreed was proportionate. He apprenticed to the cooper; I apprenticed to old Master Fenwick the mapmaker, whose archive I inherited along with his bad knees and his conviction that any problem can be solved with sufficient vellum. And somewhere in the last decade — neither of us will say when, and we have both conducted thorough investigations into the matter and destroyed our findings — the friendship quietly became something else, the way a stream becomes a river without ever announcing the change of name.

So tonight was not a beginning, exactly. It was more like finally writing down on paper something both parties had known by heart for years. Which, if you think about it, is just what a map is. Perhaps that's why I liked him. He understood that naming a thing doesn't shrink it.

"So," he said, when the tea had come and I had, as forecast, drunk most of his. "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"The map. You've been muttering at it for three weeks. Marigold says you've been talking to the border stones again and Mrs. Hedley saw you lying in a ditch with your notebook, and the ditch, apparently, was winning."

"It was a drainage channel. Cartographically significant."

"You were in it up to your shoulders."

"Cartographically *significant*," I repeated, and unrolled the survey across the table, weighting the corners with the salt cellar, the pepper, his cup, and his hand, which he surrendered with theatrical sighing.

There it was: the western border of Thornvale, drawn in fresh iron-gall ink, running down the page like a seam. And along it — this was the part I'd worked three weeks on — I had marked every station where the refusal was strongest. Where compasses spun. Where a hedge-witch's charm had guttered out mid-word. Where, forty years ago, old Tam Rookley had tried to carry a luck-charm over the line and swore afterward that it went cold as a dead man's hand in his pocket, and never touched a charm again, though he did take up drinking, which some argued was a lateral move.

"What are these little marks?" Ivo asked, tapping the stations.

"Measurements. Of the edge." I ran my finger along the line. "Everyone thinks the border is where the valley stops. But it's not. The valley stops here—" I tapped a second, fainter line I'd drawn just inside the first, "—and the refusal happens *here*, a few paces further out. There's a gap. A margin. Like the blank space around a map's frame. Nothing lives in it. Magic dies in it. Even the birds go around."

Ivo studied it. He has a way of studying things — head tipped, mouth slightly open, entirely unembarrassed by attention — that makes whatever he's looking at feel briefly important. "And what's in the margin?"

"Nothing. That's rather the point."

"No," he said slowly, "I mean — what's it *for*? A frame isn't nothing. A frame is how you know where the picture ends." He looked up. "Who framed us, Bee?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling for three weeks, lying in ditches, arguing with birch trees. Who frames a valley? Frames exist because someone chose where the picture ends. Someone drew the line. And I had spent my whole professional life measuring a decision someone else had made, without ever asking—

"Well," I said lightly, "whoever it was, they had excellent penmanship," and I began rolling up the map, because there are questions you ask at noon with your boots on and questions you ask at an inn at dusk, and I knew which kind this was.

But Ivo was watching me roll it up, and he knows me — curse him, bless him — he knows exactly what my lightness is made of.

"Bee."

"Mm."

"What's out there?" He nodded toward the window, toward the darkening west, toward the line I'd spent all day failing to hold still. "Past your margin. Have you ever—"

"No," I said. Too quickly. I made myself say it again, slower, like setting a bone. "No. I haven't."

He didn't say *why not*, which is why it was a date and not an interrogation. Instead he turned his cup slowly on its saucer, and said, "When I was twelve, I climbed the ridge above the quarry to see the world beyond the valley. Got as far as the border stones. Sat there all afternoon." He smiled at his tea. "Couldn't make myself cross. Felt like — you know when you're about to dive into cold water, and your body just says no? My body said no. For years I thought I was a coward." He glanced up. "Then I thought maybe the valley just holds on to what it loves."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's said to me over tea and a salt cellar."

"I've been saving it." He reached across and put his hand flat on my rolled-up map, right over the border line, sealing it. "For what it's worth — I'm glad you never crossed. Selfish of me. But the valley holds on to what it loves, and I count myself held."

And here is the truth I will set down honestly, because a mapmaker who lies in her own memoir deserves her borders to wander: sitting there, warm and safe and held, with the man I had loved for years finally saying it in candlelight — sitting in the very center of everything I had ever mapped — I felt the longing rise in me like groundwater, quiet and cold and patient. Because I knew every inch of the picture. And I had never seen the frame. And some traitorous part of me, the part that lay in ditches on purpose, wanted — needed — to stand outside the valley and look back at the line, and learn whether it was a wall or a window.

Instead I covered his hand with mine and said, "You count yourself held. I count myself *kept*," and we laughed, and the evening went soft, and for a few hours I almost believed the voice that sounded like contentment.

* * *

We walked home the long way, along the border road, because the night was mild and neither of us wanted it to end, and because Ivo claimed — falsely — that the moon over the western hills was worth the detour. At the fork by the millrace he stopped.

"Next week," he said. "Same table. I'll bring the pie my mother used to make, or an honest attempt at it. You bring the birch tree's apology."

"It's not sorry."

"Nothing in this valley ever is." He kissed my cheek — brief, certain, the way he does everything — and turned toward his lane, and called back over his shoulder: "Hold on to what you love, Bee!"

"Get home before the fog!" I called back, which is what we say instead of the other thing.

I watched him go. Then I stood a while at the fork, alone, with my survey under my arm and the western dark in front of me, and I thought about margins, and frames, and hands that draw lines, and I felt — I want to say I felt nothing but happiness, and mostly I did, but underneath the happiness there was that cold patient water, rising.

That was the night the ink changed. Though I didn't know it yet. That night I only noticed it the way you notice a door left open in a house you thought was shut: a draft, a wrongness, a sense that somewhere in the archive, a page had turned on its own.

I went home. I slept badly, dreaming of birch trees pointing the wrong way.

And in the morning, in the archive, on the last page of a book dated 1897 that had been blank for a hundred and twenty-seven years —

the ink was wet.
