## 3. The Thorns Come at Midnight

I had been waiting at the inn for twenty minutes before Beatrix arrived, and in those twenty minutes I had rearranged the cutlery four times, straightened my collar twice, and briefly considered fleeing through the kitchen window like a man with a warrant.

This was not a date. I want that on the record. It was two colleagues meeting to discuss — well, actually, we had no professional reason to meet at all. Beatrix Brightwater drew maps and I mended fences, and neither of those trades requires candlelit dinners at the Wandering Ewe. So when she'd said, three days ago, in her brisk way, *Come to the inn on Thursday. I'll buy you a pie and you can tell me why your grandmother's fence posts hum,* I had understood perfectly well what it was. And so, apparently, had she, because she'd blushed when she said it and then pretended she hadn't, which is the most honest thing anyone in Thornvale ever does.

The pie arrived before she did. I ate half of it out of nerves and regretted every bite.

Then the door opened and there she was, shaking rain off her coat, cheeks pink from the walk up from the archive, hair escaping its braid in the specific way that suggested she'd been arguing with a book again. She saw me, saw the table, saw the half-demolished pie, and said:

"You started without me."

"I was stress-eating."

"At me?"

"In anticipation of you."

She laughed — that quick, surprised laugh she always seemed slightly annoyed to be caught producing — and sat down across from me, and for about an hour everything was exactly as good as I'd secretly hoped and would never have admitted under torture.

She told me about the border map she'd finally finished. She talked about it the way other people talk about their children: pride and exhaustion and a kind of fierce tenderness. "Eleven years," she said, spreading her hands as if the map were laid out between the salt cellar and the candle. "Eleven years to chart one line. Do you know what's out there, Ivo? Past the border?"

"Other places," I offered.

"Places." She said the word like it tasted strange. "Actual geography. Rivers that flow somewhere. Roads that go to towns where magic works and nobody's compass spins itself silly. I've mapped every inch of Thornvale, and I have never once seen what's on the far side of my own maps. Isn't that absurd?"

"It's home," I said. "Home doesn't need to be interesting."

"That's what people say right before they die of boredom."

"Or comfort," I said. "Some of us are pro-comfort."

She looked at me over the candle flame, and something in her face went soft and complicated. "You're not, though," she said quietly. "Don't tell me you are. You watch the border line like it owes you money."

I didn't have an answer for that, mostly because she was right. My whole life I'd lived inside a valley that refused magic the way some houses refuse guests — politely, absolutely, forever. Nothing strange ever happened in Thornvale. That was its entire selling point. My grandmother used to say we lived in the one place in the world where nothing could reach us, and she said it with such relief, such *cost*, that even as a boy I understood the sentence had a price tag on the back of it I wasn't allowed to read.

"Grandmother Marren," I said instead, "used to say this valley was bought and paid for. I always thought she meant by hard work."

"People usually do," Beatrix said, and then frowned at herself, as if she hadn't meant to say it. "Sorry. Archive brain. Everything sounds like a ledger if you spend enough time with old documents."

It was nearly midnight when the storm picked up properly. The innkeeper had long gone to bed, leaving the lamp burning low and instructions to bolt the door behind us. Rain hammered the windows. The candle guttered whenever the wind found a gap, and each time it did, Beatrix flinched slightly and put her hand flat on her notebook — a habit, I'd noticed, like touching wood.

"I should walk you back before this gets worse," I said, meaning *I don't want tonight to end*, translated into the local dialect.

"In this?" She glanced at the black windows. "You'd lose me in five minutes. And unlike me, you can't navigate by stubbornness alone."

So we stayed. We ordered another round of cider we didn't need. She sketched the tablecloth absently while she talked; I watched her more than I listened, which she almost certainly noticed and mercifully never mentioned. And at some point — I couldn't tell you when, because that's the thing about seams, they don't announce themselves — the night stopped being ordinary.

* * *

It started with the sound. A creaking, slow and deliberate, from the direction of the common room door. Not the wind. Wind doesn't have rhythm. Wind doesn't have *intention*. This was the sound of something walking, and walking badly, the way a creature walks when it has learned legs recently or borrowed them.

Beatrix heard it too. Our eyes met across the candle.

"Dog?" she whispered.

"Dogs don't knock," I whispered back, because whatever it was had paused at the threshold and was standing there, and I could feel it *waiting*, the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder.

The door opened.

What came in on root-legs was a bramble. That is the only word I have for it, and I've had eleven years of Thornvale silence to sharpen my vocabulary. A walking thicket, maybe eight feet tall, all black stems and hooked thorns, moving with a horrible patient deliberateness, each root-foot finding the floorboards with a click like a knuckle cracking. Where a face should be there was a knot of pale wood, and set into the knot were —

No. I won't soften it. There were eyes. Two of them, small and wet and dark, and they were looking directly at me.

Behind it came a second. Then a third, ducking its crown of thorns under the lintel with a scrape that left splinters in the doorframe.

Beatrix moved first. She's like that — she moves before fear finishes arriving. She got between me and them, notebook raised like a shield, which would have been absurd if her voice hadn't come out steady as a surveyor's line.

"This valley is closed to you," she said. "Nothing crosses the border. Nothing crosses."

The foremost bramble tilted its head. The knot-face split along a grain-line, and from somewhere inside the wood — from inside the *wood* — came a voice.

"*Ivo.*"

My name. Spoken gently. Spoken in a voice I knew better than my own heartbeat, because I'd heard it every day of my childhood until the day I'd stood at a graveside in the rain and never heard it again.

My grandmother's voice.

The bottom dropped out of the world. I remember grabbing the edge of the table. I remember the cider going over and spreading toward the candle and Beatrix shifting the candle aside without looking at it, her eyes never leaving the bramble. I remember thinking, very clearly and very stupidly: *the fence posts hum, and I never asked why.*

"Grandmother?" I said. My voice cracked down the middle. "That's — you're dead. You're ten years dead. What — who —"

"*Ivo, love.*" The voice was warm. It was warm the way a trap is baited. "Come here. Come away from her. You know me. You've known me since before you could walk."

And the worst part, the part I still can't write down without my hands going cold, is that my body wanted to obey. Not my mind — my mind was screaming inventory lists, escape routes, the weight of the poker by the hearth. But my legs took one step forward on their own, drawn on a string I couldn't see, and I felt the pull of it like a hook behind my ribs.

"Ivo, don't." Beatrix caught my sleeve. Her hand was shaking now, though her voice still held. "Whatever it is, it isn't her. Dead things don't cross the border. *Nothing* crosses the border — that's the whole —" She stopped. I watched the thought land in her face like a stone in still water. If nothing crossed the border, and this had crossed, then either the rule had broken, or it had been broken for a very long time, or —

"— or it was invited," she breathed. "Something with a claim. Something already inside the walls."

"*Clever girl,*" said the bramble, in my grandmother's voice, and the tenderness in it curdled into something else. "*But this is family business. This is blood business. Ivo. Grandmother's calling.*"

"Don't listen to it," Beatrix said. "Names aren't commands. Names aren't —"

The second bramble moved. It happened fast, faster than anything that size should move, thorn-limbs unfurling like whips, and Beatrix swung her notebook at it — actually hit it, I'll remember that forever, she *hit* it — and thorns raked across the cover and the pages beneath shrieked, actually *shrieked*, ink hissing off the paper like water on a hot pan.

And the third one opened the air.

I don't have a better way to say it. Between two of the brambles, at the height of a man's chest, the darkness of the room gathered itself into a seam — a vertical line of deeper-than-night, thin as a blade, humming faintly, the way, God help me, the way my grandmother's fence posts hummed. Cold poured out of it. Not winter cold. *Ledger* cold. The cold of a place where everything is written down and nothing is forgiven.

"*Time to settle accounts, love,*" said my grandmother's voice, and the hook behind my ribs pulled.

"No—" I said. I dug my heels into the floorboards. I grabbed the table. I grabbed Beatrix's hand, and her grip was iron, and for one glorious stupid second I thought we might hold.

We didn't hold.

The seam widened. Thorns closed around my arms — not piercing, worse than piercing, *gripping*, courteous, like bailiffs — and the last thing I saw clearly was Beatrix's face, lit by the guttering candle, saying my name, just my name, no prophecy in it, no bargain, just a person calling another person back.

I let go of her hand.

I want to be precise about this, because she has never once blamed me and I have spent every hour since blaming myself: my fingers slipped. Or opened. Or were made to open. The distinction matters to no one but me, and I lost custody of it somewhere on the far side of the seam.

The cold took me the rest of the way. The inn, the candle, the rain, the valley that refuses magic — all of it folded shut around me like a book closing, and the last sound I heard was the door bursting open below and boots on stairs, and a voice shouting my name with no warmth in it at all, only terror, only *her*, and then even that was gone, and there was only the dark, and the roots, and the long road down.

* * *

They tell me — I learned later, much later, in a place where learning is a kind of wound — that it took Beatrix Brightwater less than a minute to climb those stairs. That she burst into the common room armed with a candlestick and a cartographer's fury and found nothing but three sets of root-holes punched through the floorboards, a spilled cider, a scarred notebook, and my coat, still hooked over the back of my chair, still warm.

She stood in the wreckage of our evening holding the only piece of me the dark had left behind.

Outside, the storm was ending. Inside, the candle steadied, and burned, and burned, and nobody came to blow it out.

And far beneath the mountain, in a court of salt and candlelight, a contract older than I was turned over its final page, and began, at long last, to come due.
