## 5. The Country of Almost

The door closed behind me the way a mouth closes around a secret.

One moment I was standing in the border grass with my boots in dew and my heart going like a trapped bird; the next, the seam had sealed itself at my back and there was no Thornvale behind me at all — only a country that looked like someone had started describing home to a stranger and given up halfway through.

I did what any sensible cartographer would do.

I sat down on a rock that might not have been a rock, opened my satchel, and took out paper, ink, and the compass my father left me — the one whose needle now spun in slow, thoughtful circles, like it was trying to remember which way was north and suspected north had resigned.

"Right," I said aloud, because saying things aloud is how I keep from screaming. "Let's establish some ground rules."

There were no ground rules. There wasn't even ground, exactly. The hillside under me was grass-colored and grass-textured but when I pressed my palm flat against it, the blades felt like an *idea* of grass, the memory of a description of grass, secondhand and slightly embarrassed about it. The sky above was the color of dusk in a painting done by someone who'd only ever had dusk described to them.

And everything — everything — was *almost*. Almost-trees stood in almost-rows along a almost-path. A river ran nearby with the sound of water but the color of pewter, moving without seeming to go anywhere. In the distance, hills rose and fell like a heartbeat seen from the wrong side.

It was beautiful. It was also the most frightening thing I had ever looked at, and I once watched my aunt argue with a tax assessor for four hours.

I uncapped my ink bottle and drew the first line.

Here is something they don't teach you in apprenticeship, mostly because no one who knew it well enough to teach survived long enough to be smug about it: a map is not a record. A map is a *promise*. You draw a line and you are telling the world, *here, this is where things are, and they will still be there tomorrow.* The world, being obliging most of the time, agrees.

This world was not obliging. This world was a debtor.

The moment my pen touched paper and I drew the river — its bend, its bank, the willow-shaped almost-things leaning over it — the actual river *shivered*. Straightened. Its pewter color deepened toward honest grey-green, and its sound resolved from suggestion into gurgle. Where I had drawn a ford, a ford appeared: flat stones just breaking the surface, wet and real and *staying* real as long as my line held.

My hand shook so badly I blotted the page.

"Oh," I said quietly. "Oh, you're *hungry*."

Because that's what it was. Not obedience — need. The realm fed on forgotten things; I could feel it now that I was paying attention, a faint pull at the edges of me, gentle as a moth testing a sleeve. Every unobserved thing here unraveled and got drunk back into the general fog. And every mapped thing — every line I committed to ink, every place I named and fixed and *witnessed* — became too solid to eat.

I was not walking through a country. I was building one, one stroke at a time, out of a wilderness that wanted very much to swallow my work whole.

I thought of Ivo, somewhere ahead of me or beneath me or inside that black-crowned shape on the horizon, and I thought: *well. At least I'm useful.*

Then I packed up, checked my lines twice, and walked down to the ford I had invented.

* * *

Three days — or what I chose to call three days, since this sky kept no schedule and the light changed its mind like a committee — taught me the grammar of the place.

Rule one: never look away from anything you want to keep. I learned this when I stopped to re-ink a fading line and glanced up to find the copse of almost-trees beside me had rearranged itself into a single enormous tree, then into a standing stone, then into nothing at all, all while my back was turned. When I redrew them, they came back grudgingly, like actors recalled to a stage they'd already struck.

Rule two: names have weight here. Heavier than they do at home. I named the ford *Wrenwater* after a stream near my grandmother's cottage, and the ford grew prouder — clearer water, firmer stones. I named a stretch of path *Patience* as a joke, and the path immediately became twice as long. I have not forgiven it.

Rule three, and this is the one that matters: the realm eats memories first.

I noticed it on the second day, resting against a boulder I'd anchored with three lines and a hatching pattern. I was eating the last of the bread from the inn — Ivo's bread, bought for our date, wrapped in cloth that still smelled faintly of the kitchen — and I tried to picture my mother's face while I chewed.

I couldn't.

Not entirely. I had the shape of her — dark hair, a laugh that arrived before she did — but the center of her face had gone soft and unfocused, like a portrait left out in rain. I sat very still and searched for her eyes and found a blankness where they should have been, and the blankness had a texture, and the texture was *recently eaten*.

I dropped the bread.

Something in the fog beyond my map-lines rustled, satisfied.

"No," I said. My voice came out small. I made it bigger. "*No.* That one's paid for. That one's mine."

The fog said nothing, because fog is a coward, but I felt it circling anyway, patient, the way debt is patient. And I understood — properly understood, in my hands and teeth — what kind of country this was. Everything forgotten ended up here eventually. Lost roads. Dead languages. Faces of the dead. Whole villages that everyone who remembered them had died. It was a country of almosts because it was made of things that were *almost* gone, held in a half-life of mist until something ate the last of them.

Somewhere in that fog, probably, was a piece of Ivo. His grandmother had sold his soul beneath the mountain, and souls must travel through territory like this to get there. Maybe a fragment of him had fallen off the cart on the way. Maybe he was walking around in here right now, ninety percent himself, missing the taste of snow and not knowing why.

I picked up the bread, brushed it off, and ate it anyway. Waste not. Grieve later.

Then I did the hardest thing I have ever done with a pen, before or since: I drew my mother's face. Small, in the corner of my working map, quick lines, from a memory that was already limping. Hair, jaw, the laugh-line before the laugh. As the ink dried, the blankness in my mind filled back in — not perfectly, the map caught only what I could hold of her — but enough. Her eyes came home.

I labeled the little portrait *MOTHER, DO NOT EAT* and felt only slightly ridiculous.

A cartographer's tricks, I was learning, were survival tricks. Witness a thing and it endures. Name a thing and it stands. Love a thing hard enough to draw it, and even a hungry country has to let it be.

I folded her away next to my heart, which is where I keep my best instruments.

* * *

On the third day, the mountain stopped pretending to be far away.

That's the only way I can describe it. For two days it had been a shape on the horizon, black-crowned, patient, the kind of distant that distance uses when it wants to seem harmless. Then the fog thinned — or was eaten, or ate itself — and the road I'd drawn bent, quite without my permission, toward it, and suddenly the mountain was *near*, close enough that I could see it was wrong in ways I hadn't budgeted for.

It wasn't a mountain with a crown of black stone. The black *was* the mountain, rising out of the hinterland like a ledger standing upright, sheer and ruled and terrible, its peak lost in cloud that didn't move. And its slopes weren't rock. From this distance they looked like pages. Thousands upon thousands of pale surfaces, terraced and stacked, catching the dead light — and I swear on my father's compass that as I watched, one of those surfaces turned over. Like a page. Like something up there was reading.

My legs informed me, politely but firmly, that we would not be walking another step in that direction today.

I agreed. I set camp instead — a camp I had to draw into existence, tent-lines and fire-ring and all, because the hinterland offered no hospitality and would have happily rearranged my sleeping body into an almost-something by morning. The fire I built burned with more conviction than anything else in sight, and I sat close enough to singe my sleeves and called it comfort.

While the flames settled, I laid out my maps and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

By my reckoning, the border-seam lay perhaps forty miles behind me. The mountain stood perhaps thirty ahead. Between us stretched the worst terrain I'd ever charted: country that erased itself faster than I could fix it, growing hungrier the closer I came to its master. My ink was down to half a bottle. My paper, six sheets and scraps. And I had slept maybe four hours in three days, because every time I closed my eyes I could *hear* the fog moving at the edge of my firelight, tasting the borders of my little island of real.

Forty miles out. Thirty miles in. One woman, one pen, and a stolen prophecy book that had started all of this.

I took it out — I'd sewn it into my coat lining, because if the court's agents came looking, they'd find it over my cold body — and read the final page again by firelight. The ink that hadn't existed a week ago had dried by now, which somehow made it worse. Wet ink can be a trick. Dried ink is a *decision*.

*A cartographer shall follow,* it said, in its smug century-old hand. *She shall cross the country of almost, and the almost shall not keep her.*

Well. Someone had confidence in me. It was irritating how much it helped.

I was folding the book away when the firelight reached further than it should have.

Beyond my drawn camp-boundary, maybe fifty paces out, the fog had gathered into a shape. Not a creature — I want to be clear about that, because every instinct screamed creature and my instincts, I've since learned, were flattering it. It was more like a *gap* shaped like a person, a silhouette of absence wearing the fog like a borrowed coat. It stood at the exact edge of my map-lines, where my ink declared *here ends the known*, and it stood there the way a creditor stands at a door.

It had been watching me. Possibly for days.

I did not scream. I am recording this for posterity because no one who knows me will believe it.

Instead I picked up my pen — slowly, the way you'd pick up a weapon you're not sure is loaded — and I said, in the steadiest voice I own:

"If you've come to collect, you'll find I've already paid. I'm mapping this place for free. Do you know what that costs?"

The gap-shape tilted its head. And where a face should have been, the fog parted briefly around something old and patient and amused, and I heard — not with my ears, more with the part of me that reads fine print — a voice like paper turning:

*Almost, cartographer. You have paid almost.*

"I don't deal in almost."

*Everyone deals in almost,* it said, and the fog around my camp sighed, and the almost-trees leaned in like an audience. *You have been paying since you crossed. A face here. A name there. The taste of your mother's kitchen. We are owed a great deal more before the mountain.*

My blood went cold in a very organized way, top to bottom, like a ledger being balanced.

"And if I refuse to pay the rest?"

The gap-shape raised one absent arm, and pointed — past me, past my fire, past thirty miles of hungry country — at the black terraced slopes, where, high up, a single pale surface turned over again, and again, and again, like a finger running down a column of figures.

*Then you may take it up with the Magistrate of Debts,* it said. *He keeps excellent records. Ask him what your grandmother's contract says about collateral.*

I want it noted that I never told anyone in that country my grandmother's name.

But the fog already knew it. And the mountain, turning its thousand pale pages in the dark, had apparently been reading about me all along.

I put more wood on the fire, drew a circle of *WITNESSED* around my camp in letters deep enough to bleed, and sat up until the light changed its mind again, pen in hand, mapping the road to a door I was beginning to suspect had been expecting me since 1897.
