## 6. The Thief Who Agreed

The first thing I learned about the court beneath the mountain was that it had excellent handwriting.

That sounds like a small thing to notice while being dragged through the dark by your collar, but I've always been a details man. Beatrix says it's my worst quality — "Ivo, the house is on fire, stop cataloguing the curtains" — but in this case the detail was doing me a favor, because noticing things kept me from screaming, and screaming seemed like a bad opening move.

So: excellent handwriting. The summons that had come through the brambles — the one written in my dead grandmother's voice, which we'll get to, believe me — had been penned in a careful copperplate with little flourishes on the capital letters. The bailiffs who'd carried me down here moved like furniture with opinions, all wax-seal faces and parchment skin, and their warrant of transport was lettered so beautifully it could have hung in a gallery. Somewhere below us, water dripped in perfect iambic rhythm, as if even the plumbing had been typeset.

And now I sat in a room made entirely of paper.

Not papered. Made. The walls were leaves of vellum thick as tree trunks, layered and pressed until they became stone-adjacent, and candlelight came from somewhere without any candles, warm and amber and utterly uninterested in casting shadows. A long table stretched before me, and on it lay a single document, face up, weighted at the corners with four thorns the size of knitting needles.

A clerk stood across the table. It looked mostly like a person, if you didn't mind that its fingers were quills and its face was a page with writing where features should be. The writing rearranged itself when it spoke.

"Ivo Thorn," it said. My name sounded different down here. Flatter. Like it had already been filed.

"That's me," I said. "Though I notice nobody's asked how I'm doing, and honestly? Not great."

"The court does not inquire after comfort. The court inquires after balance." The clerk turned the document around with one quill-finger and slid it toward me. "You have been named. Read your charge."

I read it. Or I started to, because the first line stopped me cold:

*Let there come a thief into our country, one Ivo, who shall steal back what was sold beneath the mountain.*

"You're joking," I said.

"The court does not joke. Jokes are unenforceable."

"No, see, that's the part that doesn't work. I'm not a thief. I once took a scone from Marta's bakery window when I was nine and I felt so bad about it I paid her back double and apologized for a week. She still brings it up. *Still.*" I tapped the page. "This says thief. Capital T. There's a whole prophecy about it, apparently. Who wrote this?"

"A cartographer will find the way," the clerk recited, in the tone of someone quoting scripture they found tedious. "A thief shall enter the court and take back the soul that was sold. These are the terms of the old writ. You were summoned in accordance with them."

"Summoned." I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "Brambles walked into an inn and dragged me through a hole in the air. That's not a summons, that's a kidnapping with stationery."

"All summonses are kidnappings with stationery," the clerk said, with what I can only describe as bureaucratic serenity. "Some are simply better documented than others."

I wanted to argue. I genuinely did. But there was a question scratching at the back of my skull, and it had been scratching since the brambles said my name in a voice I knew, a voice that used to call me down for supper, a voice that had been silent in the ground for six years.

"What soul?" I asked. "Whose soul was sold?"

The writing on the clerk's face went very still. Then it slid the second document across the table.

It was older than the first. The vellum had gone the color of weak tea, soft at the edges, and it was covered in the same beautiful hand — but this wasn't copperplate. This was looser, warmer, the letters leaning slightly right, the way writing leans when the person holding the pen is smiling.

I knew that handwriting.

I'd seen it on shopping lists. On birthday cards. On the recipe for honey cake that lived in a tin above the stove, in a kitchen I would never stand in again. On the last note she ever left me, propped against the milk jug: *Gone to town, back by dark, don't let the fire die.*

My grandmother's hand. My grandmother's handwriting, on a contract of sale, dated twenty-two years before I was born.

The words swam. I made myself read them anyway, because Beatrix has taught me — without meaning to, just by being who she is — that the worst thing you can do with a terrible truth is look away from it. Look away and it grows teeth. Look straight at it and sometimes, sometimes, you find the seam.

*One soul, entire and unbroken, of the bloodline of Ede Thorn,* the contract read. *Payable upon the coming of age of the child so named. In exchange: the valley of Thornvale shall remain closed to magic, its borders sealed, its people safe, in perpetuity.*

There was a signature. Of course there was a signature. Her name, written out in full, in that warm right-leaning hand.

Ede Thorn.

Grandma Edie.

The room got very quiet. Even the dripping water seemed to hold its breath.

"She sold me," I said. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing very far away. "Before I was born. She sold my soul to buy — what, a fence? A really good fence?"

"The valley's peace," the clerk said. "Its freedom from the wild magics that once troubled it. Your grandmother negotiated admirably. The borders she purchased have held for two decades without maintenance. Most clients require renewal clauses."

"Without maintenance." I heard myself repeating his words back at him, because my own words had gone somewhere else. "You're describing my soul like a roof."

"Everything is a roof, if the contract is drafted well enough."

I put my hands flat on the table because they were shaking and I didn't want the clerk to see. The contract sat there under my palms, warm as skin, and I thought about every birthday she'd baked for me. Every winter she'd knitted me scarves with mistakes in them on purpose because she said perfection was showing off. Every time she'd held my hand walking home in the dark and told me nothing under the stars could touch me while she was there.

Nothing could touch me. Because she'd already given away the part of me that anything might have wanted.

"It was kind of her," the clerk said, and its voice had shifted into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "You should understand that. The court offered other currencies. Land. Years. The valley's memory of itself. She refused them all. She would pay only with the thing she loved most, because only that would satisfy the old law. She wept while she signed. We keep the tears. They're in the annex."

"Don't," I said.

"They are catalogued under—"

"*Don't.*"

The clerk's face-words settled into silence. For a moment the only sound was my own breathing, ragged and stupid in the candlelit paper dark.

Then I did the thing I do instead of falling apart, which is ask questions, because questions are load-bearing.

"Okay," I said. "Fine. Sold soul, coming due, that's why the brambles came for me on my birthday month. But this—" I flicked the prophecy page. "This says I'm supposed to steal it *back*. That's insane. Why would you write a prophecy telling the guy you bought a soul from how to steal it? That's like a bank mailing its customers the vault code."

Something happened to the clerk's face then. The writing rearranged, and rearranged again, and I realized — slowly, the way you realize the floor is tilting — that it was trying not to smile.

Or whatever a page does instead of smiling.

"You have misunderstood the nature of prophecies," it said. "They are not warnings. They are not gifts. Down here, a prophecy is a *clause*. And clauses are drafted by interested parties."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning the court wrote it. A century ago. We wrote it, and we arranged for it to reach your valley's archive, and we waited for a cartographer foolish enough and a bloodline marked enough to make it true." The quill-fingers folded themselves neatly. "You are the thief, Ivo Thorn. You will absolutely attempt the theft. We will permit it. We will *facilitate* it. We will leave doors ajar and guards distracted and corridors conveniently unlit."

"Why?" The word came out cracked. "Why would you—"

"Because the old law requires balance." The clerk leaned forward, and the candlelight bent toward it like it was listening too. "One soul, sold, may be reclaimed by theft — that is the ancient remedy, and we honor it. But a thief who fails?" The face-writing bloomed into something that was definitely a smile now, elegant and terrible. "A failed thief forfeits his own soul to the court he robbed. And should the thief arrive accompanied — bound to another, in love or in debt or in simple stubborn loyalty — then the forfeiture extends."

The room tilted. I gripped the table.

"You're not afraid of the prophecy," I said. "You *wrote* it. It's not a prediction, it's a trap with a hundred-year fuse. Someone reads it, comes down here thinking they're the hero of it, fails — and you collect double."

"Triple, historically, when the thief brings siblings." The clerk sounded wistful. "But yes. Double, in your case, assuming the cartographer follows. They usually follow. Love is remarkably predictable. It's why we don't bother contracting for it — it signs itself."

Beatrix.

The name landed in my chest like a dropped stone. She'd been three steps behind me when the brambles took me. She'd been reaching for my hand. If there was one fact in the universe as reliable as gravity, it was that Beatrix Brightwater does not stay behind. She maps the way out. She'd already be finding one.

Which meant the trap was already half sprung, and the only person who could spring the rest of it was currently sitting at a paper table trying not to cry in front of a filing system.

"So what happens now?" I asked. "You parade me past the exits? Leave a map out where I'll find it?"

"Now," the clerk said, rising, its pages rustling with something like satisfaction, "you are shown to your accommodations, which are comfortable, and informed of the court's geography, which is accurate, and left alone with your thoughts, which are ours to harvest later. The new moon is in some days. Until then, you are our guest."

"And if I refuse? If I just sit in my cell and steal nothing?"

The clerk paused at the door — a door that hadn't been there a moment ago, cut into the vellum wall like a signature.

"Then we wait," it said. "We are very good at waiting. And the valley's peace expires with the debt, thief. No payment, no border. The wild magic comes home to Thornvale, and it has been locked out for twenty years, and it is *hungry*. Your grandmother's bargain protects everyone you have ever known — right up until you decline to play your part."

It opened the door onto a corridor of soft golden light.

"Oh, and one more matter." The clerk produced a third document, smaller, and laid it beside the contract with a tenderness that made my stomach turn. "Your grandmother left an addendum. To be delivered upon your arrival. She insisted."

I stared at it. The same warm hand. The same rightward lean. One line, and I couldn't read it from across the table, and some cowardly part of me decided I didn't want to, not yet, not while the clerk was watching, not while my eyes were already burning.

"Take me to the cell," I said.

The corridor swallowed me, and behind me, on the table, the addendum waited — one line in a dead woman's handwriting, patient as ink, saying something I was terrified it would say.

Because I'd caught it, just barely, in the half-second before I looked away.

The word *sorry* is easy to fake. Anyone can write *sorry*.

But the way she crossed out a mistake and corrected it — the little hooked stroke she always made, the one I'd teased her about my whole life —

that, no forger alive could copy.

And it was there. Right there, in the middle of the addendum, crossing out a single word.

She'd meant to write something else. And she'd changed it.

* * *

The cell was, infuriatingly, comfortable. A bed with actual blankets. A window that showed a view of stars that I was fairly sure were fictional. A carafe of water that refilled itself with a smug little glint.

I sat on the bed and did the arithmetic of ruin.

Grandmother sold my soul to save a valley. The court wrote a prophecy to farm the rescue attempt. The rescue attempt requires a thief, and failure pays double, and success — I turned it over, looking for the catch, because there was always a catch — success returns one soul and voids one contract, which means the valley loses its border, which means the hungry magic comes home, which means even winning costs everyone I grew up with everything.

There was no move that saved everybody. Grandma Edie had known that, twenty-two years before I existed, and she'd chosen, and she'd signed, and she'd wept, and the court kept the tears in an annex like jam.

And somewhere above all of this, past the hinterland and the seam and the border line where compasses spin, Beatrix was coming. Of course she was coming. She'd probably already found a door that shouldn't exist and drawn a map of a place that refuses to exist and was currently arguing with the geography itself, and winning.

They expected her. They'd built the whole century-long machine expecting her. Failure binds two souls instead of one.

Unless.

I lay back and stared at the fictional stars, and the thought arrived slowly, the way the best thoughts do — dressed plainly, no fanfare, just standing there with its hands in its pockets:

Prophecies are clauses. Clauses are drafts. And drafts — Beatrix had told me this once, laughing over her drafting table, ink on her cheek — drafts can be *amended*.

The court had written the prophecy. Fine. Let them keep it.

Nobody had written what the thief would do next.

Out in the corridor, faint through the vellum walls, I heard footsteps — light ones, quick ones, moving with the particular rhythm of someone navigating by memory of a map drawn minutes ago.

Someone was in the stacks.

I sat up, and for the first time since the brambles walked into the inn, I smiled.
