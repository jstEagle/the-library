## 7. What Grandmother Bought

They gave me a chair. That was the first thing that unsettled me — not the thorn-crowned nobles, not the corridors of paper and salt, not the fact that my grandmother's voice had walked in on root-legs to collect me like a parcel. The chair. A plain wooden chair, set in the middle of a vault of debts, offered with a small courteous gesture by a creature whose face was a wax seal.

"Sit," it said. "The reading of your account will take some time."

I sat. When you've grown up in Thornvale, where nothing magical has ever happened to anyone, you develop exactly no instincts for this sort of thing. My whole survival strategy was politeness.

A ledger slid across the floor to me on its own spine, opened itself, and turned to a page near the front. The handwriting on it stopped my breath.

I knew that hand. I'd seen it on jam labels and birthday cards and the flyleaf of every book she owned: *Property of Edith Marlowe, and if you're reading this you'd better have asked.* Loops like bird wings. A hard downstroke on the final letter of every word, as if she were nailing each one shut.

*Sale of Soul, One (1), Male Infant, Ivo Marlowe.*

"That isn't possible," I said. "She died when I was nine."

"The dead honor their contracts," said the seal-faced creature. "It is the living who disappoint us."

* * *

Beatrix would have read the whole page in four seconds and found three ways out of it. I thought about that while the creature read aloud. It's strange what you hold onto. She'd only been mine for one date — one date at the inn at the valley's edge, two cups of cider, her finger tracing the border line on her own map while she talked about how compasses lie at the edge of Thornvale, spinning like they're embarrassed. She'd laughed and said, "If I ever go missing, follow the ink."

I hadn't understood what she meant. I was starting to.

"—payable upon the debtor's coming of age," the creature recited, "with consideration received as follows: the perpetual sealing of the Valley of Thornvale against all workings of magic, to endure—"

"Stop." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Say that again."

"The valley's peace," the creature said, with something almost like patience. "Was purchased. With you."

* * *

Beatrix:

The Country of Almost did not appreciate being looked at directly.

I discovered this within an hour of crossing the seam, when the hill I had just mapped turned its back on me like a cat and became a marsh. I sat down on a stone that may or may not have agreed to be a stone, unrolled my map of the hinterland — which now consisted of roughly forty minutes of frantic sketching — and pinned it flat with my compass case, my ink bottle, and one boot.

The marsh held still.

That was the rule, then. Observed ground kept its shape. Unobserved ground wandered off to be something else. Every cartographer learns early that a map is a lie we agree on; here, apparently, the lying went both ways, and the country lied back unless you caught it in the act.

"Fine," I told the marsh. "We'll do this the honest way. You stay put, I draw you, and neither of us mentions the other's flaws."

Somewhere behind me, faint as a bell rung underwater, the mountain's black crown watched from the horizon. It didn't move when I wasn't looking. That frightened me more than anything else so far. Everything here forgot itself. The mountain remembered.

I thought about Ivo's coat, still folded over my arm because I couldn't decide whether carrying it was hope or self-torture, and I stood up, and I walked.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my grandmother, before tonight: she raised me. Here is what I knew about Thornvale: nothing magic crosses the border. Compasses spin at the edge. Spells die there. We were proud of it, in the way you're proud of a fence without asking who paid for the posts.

I was nine when she died, and I remember the last thing she said to me, because it made no sense at the time and perfect sense now. She took my hand — her grip had gone papery by then — and she said, "You'll forgive me when you're older, or you won't, and both are allowed."

Then she said, "Happy birthday," eleven days early, and cried, and I was nine, so I just patted her arm and asked if we could have cake anyway.

My birthday is in spring. I came of age last week.

The seal-faced creature let me sit with that. It was considerate that way. Everything in this court was considerate the way a contract is considerate — every kindness clause-bound, every courtesy a term of service.

"She bought the valley's safety," I said finally. "With me."

"With your soul," the creature corrected gently. "You remained available for other purposes. Grandmother Edith drove a hard bargain. You should be proud."

"I don't want to be proud. I want to understand." And then, because the question had been sitting in my chest since the brambles spoke in her voice: "Why me? Why her grandson? Out of everyone in the valley?"

The creature tilted its head. Wax seals don't have eyes, but I felt looked at.

"Because the valley required a soul that belonged to it," it said. "And she required a soul that belonged to her. You were both. She carried you across the border the day you were born — did you know that? Born outside, named inside. A soul with a foot in each country. Very valuable. Very rare." A pause. "Very stealable."

* * *

Beatrix:

By the second day I had learned the hinterland's appetite.

It eats forgotten things. I noticed it first with small losses — a button gone from my cuff, the name of my primary school teacher slipping out of my head like water through fingers. Then larger ones. I passed a village that was mostly memory: doors that opened onto kitchens someone had once loved, a well full of old birthdays. The things people stop thinking about come here, and the country wears them until they wear out.

Which meant every step toward the mountain was a negotiation with forgetting. I started writing everything down — not mapping, writing. *Beatrix Brightwater. Mother's name, Marion. Marion's face: I am keeping it. Ivo Marlowe. His laugh comes out sideways, like he's surprised he's allowed.* If the country wanted to eat something, it could stand in line behind my notebook.

On the evening of the second day I reached the edge of a river that ran uphill, and there was a ferryman waiting, though I didn't yet know he was waiting for me. He looked like a road looks just after rain — long, dark, going somewhere. He smiled at me with a great deal of professional warmth.

"You'll want passage," he said.

"Obviously," I said. "What's the fare?"

He told me. I'm saving that part, because I paid it, and I'm not ready to write down what I paid. Some entries you make twice — once in ink, once in scar tissue.

But that's tomorrow's chapter. Tonight I camped on observed ground, drew the mountain until my hand cramped, and slept holding Ivo's coat like a very large, very useless talisman.

* * *

They showed me the rest of the ledger. I think they wanted me to see it. There's a cruelty in this court that dresses itself as transparency — *we hide nothing, we simply own everything, including your understanding of what we own.*

Page after page in her hand. Not just the sale. Amendments. Riders. A clause renewing the valley's sealing every year on my birthday — "consideration deferred, principal intact" — so that every year I blew out candles, somewhere beneath the mountain, a debt quietly rolled over. Twenty-two birthdays. Twenty-two payments of nothing, each one a promise that the real bill was still coming.

"Do you know what she got for it?" I asked. "Exactly?"

"The valley's borders sealed against all magic," the creature recited. "No working shall cross, no curse shall enter, no wonder shall intrude. Peace. Safety. Tedium, if we're being honest, but tedium is underrated. Most of our clients purchase far less and pay far more."

"And if the debt goes unpaid? If I don't—"

"If the debt defaults?" The creature's tone changed, and the temperature with it. "The sealing dissolves. Every working in the world remembers Thornvale at once. Every bargain the valley's founders ever declined comes due. Your neighbors have spent a hundred years believing they were safe. They were safe the way a purse is safe. Someone was simply choosing, for a while, not to reach in."

I thought about the inn at the valley's edge. Mrs. Halloway and her terrible meat pies. The way children there grew up never locking their doors, because there was nothing to lock against. All of it mortgaged. All of it mortgaged on a baby who couldn't even hold his own head up yet.

"She loved me," I said. It didn't come out as a question, but it was one.

"Yes," said the creature, and for the first time it sounded tired — the tiredness of a clerk who has explained arithmetic to the grieving since before grief had a name. "That is nearly always the tragedy. Love makes excellent collateral. It means the payer always intends to honor the debt. Intent is the strongest binding agent we know."

* * *

Beatrix:

The ferryman's price was a memory, and I chose, and I hate how easily I chose.

But that's done, and I won't spend more words on it than it deserves, except this: I keep reaching for my mother's face and finding a shape where it used to be, warm and specific and utterly blank, like a portrait hung facing the wall. I know I loved her. The love stayed. Only the picture went. Apparently that's how the fare works — it takes the image and leaves the invoice.

I mention it here because standing on the ferry, watching the black mountain grow, I realized something about Ivo's grandmother that I might otherwise have missed, and it matters.

Edith Marlowe sold her grandson's soul and spent twenty-two years watching him grow up knowing exactly what she'd done. Every birthday, she renewed the deferral. Every year, she could have let the bill come due and didn't. She lived inside the cost of belonging to him, and she paid it daily, in the only currency she had left: time with a boy she'd already traded away.

I know that kind of arithmetic now. I paid a face for a ferry ride and I'd do it again before the boat touched the far bank. That's the thing about these bargains nobody warns you about — the price isn't the worst part. The worst part is discovering what you were always willing to give.

The mountain took three more hours to reach. It looked like a ledger the size of a god had been folded into the shape of a peak, all vertical lines and closed covers. At its base, a door of salt and candlelight opened before I knocked.

"State your business," said the door.

"Archivist," I said, and showed it my maps. "Debts. I hear you keep excellent records."

The door considered me the way the hinterland considered hills — deciding what I was, whether I'd hold my shape.

"Enter," it said, "and be catalogued."

* * *

They put me in a room with a bed, which somehow frightened me more than chains would have. Chains mean prisoner. A comfortable room means asset.

Before they left me, the seal-faced creature paused at the door. "You'll want to rest," it said. "The court has plans for you. There is a prophecy, you see. A hundred years old. It names a thief."

"I don't know anything about a prophecy."

"No," it agreed pleasantly. "But the prophecy knows everything about you. Sleep well, Ivo Marlowe. In three days it will be the new moon, and thieves work best in the dark."

The door closed. I lay in the dark listening to the mountain breathe — it does breathe, slow and papery, like a chest full of filing cabinets — and I thought about my grandmother crying eleven days before my ninth birthday, saying *you'll forgive me when you're older, or you won't, and both are allowed.*

I was older now. I didn't know which one I was allowed yet.

All I knew was that somewhere above all this paper and salt, past a country that ate memories, a woman with ink-stained fingers was walking toward me with a map that shouldn't work — and that whatever the court thought it had planned for a thief, it had made one serious miscalculation.

It had given the thief a cartographer.

And Beatrix Brightwater doesn't chart places that exist. She charts the ones that don't — and makes them sorry.
