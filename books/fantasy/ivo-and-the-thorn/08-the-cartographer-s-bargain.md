## 8. The Cartographer's Bargain

The river appeared on the third day, which was a problem, because I had already mapped the hinterland twice and neither version had a river in it.

I stood at the edge of a field of grey grass that whispered when it thought I wasn't listening, and looked at the water. It ran sideways — not downhill, not toward any sea I could imagine, but laterally across the country of Almost, as if it were trying to leave and couldn't remember which direction leaving was. Its surface didn't reflect me. It reflected a woman who might have been me if she'd slept properly in a decade and had never once been afraid.

"Right," I said to it. "You're new."

Everything here was new, or rather everything here was *renewed*, endlessly, the way a liar retells a story. The hinterland rearranged itself whenever I stopped watching: hills migrated, valleys folded shut like fans, roads unspooled behind me into nothing. My maps were the only reason I'd crossed three days of it without walking in circles until I starved. Every night I drew what I had seen, pinned it down with ink and notation, and in the morning the world around my campsite had obligingly stayed put — because a mapped thing, I was learning, is a thing that has been *promised* somewhere, and even this place kept its promises. Grudgingly. The way a cat keeps a promise not to scratch you.

The river had no map. Which meant the river could do anything it liked.

I got out my compass anyway. Habit. The needle spun, then stopped, then pointed at the water with the resigned insistence of a dog that wants to go home.

"Fine," I told it. "We're going."

* * *

There was a ferryman. Of course there was a ferryman. I had read enough folklore to know that any body of water in an impossible country comes with a man in a boat, and that the man in the boat always wants something, and that the something is never money, because money is honest and therefore useless here.

He was poling a flat grey barge out of a fog bank that hadn't existed ten minutes earlier, and he was made mostly of suggestions. His coat was the idea of a coat. His face was a face the way a word is a sound — technically present, functionally negotiable. When he drew near I saw that his boat was built from road-signs, nailed together at odd angles, and that the pole he used was a milestone with the numbers worn off.

"Lost roads," he said. His voice sounded like gravel being polite. "I carry travelers along roads that were forgotten before they were finished. Roads that were planned and never paved. Roads people meant to take and died before setting out." He considered me with whatever portion of his face was currently in charge. "You are going to the mountain."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it like one, because I've spent my life correcting other people's assumptions about geography and I wasn't going to stop now. "I'm going to the court beneath the mountain."

"Same thing," he said. "Different address." He extended a hand that was more handshake than hand. "Passage costs."

"Money's no good here."

"No," he agreed, with something like approval. "Nothing here is worth anything except what it costs you to lose it. That's the whole economy. That's the entire civilization." He gestured at the whispering grass, the sideways water, the mountain's black crown sitting on the horizon like a period at the end of a sentence nobody wanted to finish. "Memories. That's the currency. A memory given freely weighs enough to buy passage. A memory taken by force buys nothing but trouble, and I have had quite enough trouble."

I had expected this. Somewhere between the field of teeth I'd crossed on day two and the village of abandoned umbrellas on day three, I had done the arithmetic every traveler does in a country like this: *what will they want from me, and can I live without it?*

I had a stock answer ready. I would give him the smell of the archive — dust and lamp oil and the particular squeak of the third-floor window. I would give him Mrs. Halloway's voice saying *Beatrix Brightwater, if you shelve another book upside down I shall retire out of spite.* I would give him something small and true and survivable to lose.

"You may choose," said the ferryman, "but choose honestly. I'll know a decoy. Decoys float wrong."

Of course they did.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about sacrifice, in all the ballads and all the books: it isn't dramatic. In the stories there's always a thunderclap, a moment of terrible choice, a hero standing at a crossroads while angels and devils argue over her shoulder. In practice, you stand on a muddy bank in a country that doesn't exist, your boots are wet, and the thing you're about to lose presents itself so quietly you barely notice it arriving.

I thought about my mother.

Not deliberately. She simply surfaced, the way she always did when I was frightened — which I was, though I'd been filing that under *focus* for three days. She had been a cartographer too, before the fever took her when I was nine. That was the whole inheritance: her instruments, her steady hands, and a daughter who learned early that if you draw a thing carefully enough, it can't be taken away from you. Her face was the first map I ever memorized. I knew it better than Thornvale's border line. Better than my own hands.

And I realized — with the specific horror of a person catching themselves mid-fall — that I had already chosen. Somewhere between *memories* and *decoys float wrong*, some clerk in the back office of my mind had stamped the paperwork. Not the archive smell. Not Mrs. Halloway. Not the taste of the valley's first snow or the sound of Ivo laughing at his own jokes before he finished them.

Her face.

Because I could survive without seeing it. I had already survived six years without hearing her voice; the face was the last room of the house she lived in, and I had kept it locked and dusted and visited daily, and a locked room, I understood suddenly, is exactly the kind of thing this economy runs on. Everything else I owned I carried openly. Only her face did I hoard.

Hoarded things weigh the most. I think the ferryman knew that before I did.

"Well?" he said.

"I want terms," I said, because stalling is also a kind of courage, or at least a cousin of it. "Passage to the mountain's foot. Safe arrival. And you don't take anything else — not a stray thought, not a half-remembered dream. One memory, cleanly cut, and the rest stays mine."

"One memory, cleanly cut." He turned his suggestion of a head, considering. "You bargain like someone who has read contracts written by worse things than me. Good. The mountain is full of bargainers. You'll fit right in, briefly." He held out his hand again, and this time it was unmistakably a hand, and unmistakably waiting. "Choose, cartographer. The river doesn't run sideways forever. Nothing here does."

I thought: *I'll keep the important parts. The shape of her jaw. The way her eyes crinkled. I'll keep those.*

I thought: *that's not how losing works.*

I thought about Ivo, somewhere under that black crown, wearing a coat I still had folded in my pack because I couldn't make myself leave it behind and couldn't make myself wear it either. I thought about a soul sold before its owner could walk, and a grandmother who must have loved a valley more than a boy, and how every safe thing I had ever drawn a border around had been paid for by someone I'd never met.

My mother would have hated this bargain. My mother would also have made it. Those two facts sat down together inside me and refused to speak.

"Her face," I said. "You're asking for my mother's face."

"I'm not asking for anything," said the ferryman. "You're offering. There's a difference, and the difference is the only dignity you get to keep."

* * *

I won't pretend I deliberated nobly. I counted to three, which is what I do before cold rivers and difficult shelves, and I said, "Done," and I hated — genuinely, specifically, with a precision I usually reserved for bad maps — how easily the word came out. No thunderclap. No angelic chorus objecting. Just a woman on a bank, trading the last picture of her mother for a boat ride, in less time than it takes to steep tea.

The ferryman took my hand.

It didn't hurt. That was the worst part. I had braced for pain, for tearing, for some operatic rending of the self, and instead it felt like a page being turned. Like waking from a nap and reaching for a dream that's still warm and already gone. Something lifted out of me — weightless, frictionless, *tidy* — and I felt the shape of the hole it left behind, the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue, probing a absence that insists on being noticed.

I tried to see her.

I tried, right there on the bank, with the desperation of a drowning woman grabbing at driftwood. Brown hair going grey at the temples — yes, I had that, fact without image. Laugh lines — a phrase, nothing more. Eyes the color of — of —

Nothing. A portrait with the face worn smooth as a thumbprint on wet clay. I knew she had been beautiful. I knew everyone had said so. I knew it the way you know a date in a history book: accurately, uselessly, from the outside.

"Oh," I said. Very quietly. Very stupidly. As if the syllable could call it back.

"Cleanly cut," said the ferryman, sounding almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "As negotiated. You'll find the rest intact. Your grief, for instance — grief doesn't need a face. Grief is very economical that way." He stepped aside and gestured at the barge with his milestone-pole. "Board, cartographer. The mountain won't come to us, and unlike most things here, that's a mercy."

I boarded. I don't remember deciding to. My legs filed the paperwork themselves.

* * *

The river ran sideways and the boat went forward anyway, which tells you most of what you need to know about the hinterland's relationship with logic. The ferryman poled in silence. Fog came up around us like a held breath. Behind us, the field of whispering grass folded itself away, job done, witness dismissed.

I opened my satchel and took out my working maps — three days of the country of Almost, drawn and redrawn, annotated in my own cramped hand. My anchor. My proof that I was still a person who did things. I smoothed the top sheet across my knees and began adding the river, because if I didn't write it down it would slide loose behind us, and I needed — God, I *needed* — things to stay where I put them.

Halfway through drawing the shoreline, my pen stopped.

I was shading the water, and my hand moved without instruction, and when I looked down I saw that I had drawn a small figure standing at the rail of the boat. A woman. Seen from behind. And where her face should have been — where, in profile, the ferryman's passenger would show a cheekbone, a brow, a nose — I had left the paper blank. Not unfinished. Blank. Deliberately, neatly blank, the way a cartographer marks territory that exists but cannot be surveyed.

I stared at it for a long time.

"That's new," I said finally.

"They usually are," said the ferryman, not looking at me. "The holes. They show up in your work afterward. Cartographers are the worst customers we get, if it helps. You lot spend your lives putting the world down on paper, and then you come here and find out what it costs to keep a piece of it in your head." He poled us through a fog bank that parted around the boat like a crowd making way for a funeral. "The ones who come after you will thank you, mind. Every map you draw makes this place a little more real. A little more crossable. You're paving roads you'll never walk."

"I'm walking them," I said. "That's rather the point."

"Mm." For the first time, the suggestion of his face resolved into something almost like an expression. Pity, maybe. Or its professional cousin, respect. "The mountain keeps its accounts differently than I do, cartographer. I take memories and give passage, and we're square, and neither of us owes the other a thing. The court beneath it takes souls and gives *safety*, and calls it fair trade, and the debt compounds for a hundred years until a boy comes of age." He tapped the milestone against the deck, twice, like punctuation. "When you get there — and you'll get there, you bargain too well not to — remember that everything in that place is written down. Contracts, clauses, penalties, escape routes. Especially escape routes. Nothing delights the court more than a clause nobody reads."

"Why tell me that?"

"Because you paid well," he said, "and because I ferry lost roads, and yours is the lostest I've carried in a century." He nodded ahead, and the fog tore open like a curtain.

The mountain filled the sky.

I had seen its crown for three days, black and distant, a smudge on the horizon. Up close it was worse and better both — worse because it was vast, a slab of dark stone rising out of the hinterland like a verdict; better because now I could *see* it, and a seen thing is a mappable thing, and a mappable thing can be beaten. Even from the river I could read it: the switchback scars of old roads, the seam-lines where the rock had been argued into shape, and near the base, half-hidden by scree, a doorway. Not carved. *Drafted*. Straight-edged, precise, with the faint geometry of a compass-drawn arch.

Someone had designed that door. Someone with instruments.

"End of the line," said the ferryman, running the barge against a shingle beach that assembled itself out of fog as we approached, stone by obedient stone. "Walk straight. Don't count the steps. If you meet anyone official, lie beautifully." He waited while I gathered my satchel and Ivo's coat, and as I stepped onto the shore he added, almost as an afterthought:

"The woman whose face you gave me. What was her name?"

And I reached for it and found — to my surprise, to my enormous, complicated relief — that the name was still there. *Margaret*. He had taken the face and left the name, the way you take the painting and leave the hook in the wall.

"Margaret," I said. "Her name was Margaret."

"Then she isn't wholly gone," said the ferryman. "Names are heavier than faces. Harder to carry, too." He pushed off from the shingle, and the fog began folding itself around the barge like a letter being sealed. "Good luck, cartographer. Mind the fine print."

The river took him sideways, and was gone.

I stood alone on a beach that hadn't existed five minutes ago, at the foot of a mountain that ate souls, holding a dead man's coat and a hole where my mother's smile used to be. Somewhere above me, behind drafted stone, Ivo was alive — I had to believe it, I had built three days of travel on believing it — and the only way in was the door with the compass-perfect arch.

I uncapped my pen, opened my map book to a fresh page, and started walking.

Behind me, faintly, the blank-faced woman on the river-page watched me go.
