## 9. Court of Salt and Candlelight

The ferryman had told me two things before he poled away into the dark and left me standing on a road made of nothing in particular: that the court beneath the mountain admitted no one who came as themselves, and that the archivists of debts were so overworked, so under-candled, and so profoundly ignored that no one ever looked at their faces.

"Be nobody," he'd said, "and you'll walk through any door."

I'd spent my whole life being somebody with a name on every map she drew. Being nobody turned out to be easier than I expected. Grief hollows you out into a serviceable vessel.

The gate of the court was not a gate. It was a sentence — I could feel it, the way you feel a draft under a door. Words hung in the air above the threshold, old ones, formal ones, the kind lawyers use when they want to sound like gods: *Let all who enter be entered.* And beneath my feet, where stone should have been, there was paper. Not loose paper — bound paper, ledger-paper, vellum gone soft as turf from centuries of being walked upon. The whole floor was a book's spine, and we were the ink moving along it.

I had dressed for the part out of what the hinterland had left me: a coat of undyed wool that might have been grey or might have been the memory of grey, a satchel of instruments, and an expression I practiced against the black glass of a frozen pool — the expression of a woman who has been correcting other people's arithmetic for forty years and has stopped expecting gratitude.

At the threshold, something like a customs officer materialized. It was mostly a wax seal the size of a dinner plate, floating at chest height, with a face pressed into it — a face mid-scream, or possibly mid-yawn; it was hard to tell with seals.

"Purpose," it said, in a voice like a stamp coming down.

"Archivist," I said. "Third sub-vault. Debts outstanding, uncollected, and disputed." I had rehearsed this. I had also, in a moment of inspiration born of pure spite, forged myself a letter of introduction on the ferryman's boat, using his own ink pot while he wasn't looking. The letter named me *Mistress Quill-of-the-Outer-Margins*, which is not a real title, but then, I was beginning to suspect, very little down here was real in the way I understood realness. It was all *recorded*. Recording, apparently, did the work of being.

The seal examined my letter. The face in the wax frowned, which required the entire seal to frown, which was unsettling.

"Third sub-vault hasn't filed in nine years," it said.

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm here."

And that — I swear this is true — that was the whole interrogation. Somewhere behind that screaming-or-yawning face was a clause about delegation of authority to visiting archivists, and clauses, I would learn, are the closest thing the court has to a conscience. It cannot refuse its own fine print. Remember that. It becomes important later.

The doors opened onto candlelight and salt.

* * *

Here is what I had imagined, walking down through the mountain's roots: a throne room. Thorned nobles on thorned chairs, a Magistrate crowned in iron, torches burning green, the usual theatrical apparatus of evil. I had read enough ballads to furnish the place in my head.

What I got instead was a library that had eaten a palace.

Corridors of shelving stretched in every direction, but the shelves were load-bearing walls, and the walls were shelves, and staircases spiraled up inside the spines of enormous folios chained shut. Everything smelled of salt — coarse sea salt, packed into troughs along the base of every shelf, because salt preserves, and preservation is the one commandment down here. Candles burned everywhere, thousands of them, but none of them melted. Wax candles that never diminish. I stopped and stared at one for a full minute before I understood: they weren't burning anything. They were *accruing*. Light as interest, paid out eternally on some principal deposited ages ago by someone who probably hadn't meant to sign what they signed.

And the nobles.

I saw my first one in the Long Gallery of Assumed Obligations — a tall figure whose body was, if you looked at it straight, a column of text. Not text written *on* him. Text *being* him. His robes were paragraphs, his hands were signatures, and where a face should have been there was a seal, though a finer one than the gatekeeper's, with a profile pressed into it: a stern jaw, a high forehead, the kind of face that appears on medals. He was speaking with another noble — a squat, wide creature whose surface was covered in small print, dense as a insurance policy, and I caught fragments of their conversation as I passed with my head bowed and my satchel clutched like a rosary.

"—the collateral has matured—"

"—perpetuity means perpetuity, cousin, whatever the sentimentalists say—"

"—a soul is only worth what someone will pay to keep it—"

They were clauses. That was the revelation that nearly buckled my knees in the middle of the gallery, and I had to pretend to be examining a shelf of debt-bonds to cover it. Every noble in this court was a personified term of contract. The tall one was some ancient covenant; the squat one was a penalty clause, I'd have bet my compass on it, because penalties always look like that — compressed, vindictive, delighted with themselves. The court beneath the mountain wasn't a kingdom that kept ledgers. It was a ledger that had grown so vast, so self-referential, so in love with its own covenants, that it had achieved something like consciousness and called itself a court.

Every fairy tale I'd ever mapped had gotten it wrong. There was no king down here. There was only the fine print, and the fine print had learned to want things.

I walked. I catalogued. This is what I do instead of panicking; Beatrix Brightwater, cartographer of Thornvale, converts terror into inventory. Gallery of Perpetuities. Hall of Contested Wills, where two nobles made entirely of overlapping claims argued in voices like tearing parchment. A rotunda whose dome was a single enormous document — I didn't read it, I didn't dare — beneath which clerks scurried, actual flesh-and-blood clerks, or near enough, small grey people with ink-stained fingers who were, I realized with a lurch, the only beings down here who looked like they could be from anywhere. From a valley, say. Who looked like they had once been somebody's neighbor.

Nobody looked at the archivists. The ferryman had been right. I passed within arm's reach of a penalty-clause the size of a barn and it did not so much as ripple its small print in my direction. To be ignored by the powerful is a humiliation up above. Down here it is a cloak, and I wore mine gratefully.

But I hadn't come to sightsee. Somewhere in this mountain of paper was a contract with a boy's soul written into it, and somewhere in this mountain of paper was the boy himself, and I had three days — no. Let me be honest with myself, since honesty was the only currency I had left after paying the ferryman. I had no idea how many days I had. I knew only that the prophecy's ink had still been wet, and wet ink means fresh terms, and fresh terms mean time is already running.

So I did what I do best. I started mapping.

Not on paper — I couldn't risk it. In my head. The way I'd mapped Thornvale as a girl, lying in the grass, tracing the valley's borders on the back of my eyelids. Shelf by shelf, gallery by gallery, candle by candle, I built the court inside my skull. And here is the thing about maps, the thing they don't teach you in ballads: a map is not a picture. A map is an argument. It says *this is how the land is arranged*, and land, being lazy, tends to agree. Even land made of paper. Even land that officially does not exist.

By the second hour I knew the court better than most of its nobles, who never walk anywhere — why would they? Clauses don't stroll; they're invoked. By the third hour I had found the pattern in the shelving: contracts were filed by age. The deeper you went, the older the debts, until the oldest galleries were lit by candles that predated candlelight and the salt in the troughs was grey as ash.

That was where Ivo's contract would be. His grandmother had signed it a lifetime ago. Maybe longer.

* * *

The Archive of Ancestral Obligations had no door, only a curtain of hanging chains, each link stamped with a seal, and pushing through them sounded like a churchbell falling downstairs. I winced. Nobody else did. Chains, apparently, were furniture here.

Inside, the light was the color of weak tea. The shelves rose beyond sight, and the contracts on them were not books. They were *skins* — I understood this with a horror I had to swallow like a fishbone. Vellum. Of course. What else do you write a soul on? Each scroll hung from its own hook, rolled and ribboned, and each ribbon was a different color, and I stood in the aisle doing arithmetic with my stomach: centuries of contracts, thousands of hooks, and somewhere among them, one with a thorn-colored ribbon and a name I knew.

There was no catalogue. Or rather — there was, but the catalogue was a noble, a thin hovering thing of index-lines that drifted up and down the aisles murmuring references to itself, and asking it questions was not a thing an ignored archivist did.

So I searched. Hook by hook. Row by row. My feet ached; the paper floor gave slightly, like walking on a held breath. I let my fingers trail along the scroll-ends, reading names blind, the way you'd run your hand along a fence. Names of the dead, mostly. Names of bargains struck in desperation: *one harvest for one winter's mercy; one firstborn for one river uncursed; one memory for one more year.* The whole history of the world above, reduced to receipts. People had been coming to this court forever, trading pieces of themselves for pieces of safety, and the court had simply — accrued.

I found it on the deepest row, where the candles burned without flame at all, just a steady glow like the memory of fire.

The ribbon was thorn-red. The scroll was heavier than it should have been, the way a stone is heavier than a leaf, and when I lifted it from its hook the salt in the nearest trough shivered.

*Ivo of Thornvale. One soul, entire.*

His grandmother's signature sat at the bottom, and even now, even knowing everything I know now, seeing her name made my chest hurt. Marta of Thornvale. She'd taught him to whittle. She'd waved at me across the market square every market day for twenty years, a small fierce woman with flour on her apron. And there was her hand, steady and unhesitating, selling her grandson's soul to buy a valley its silence.

I won't pretend I felt nothing. But I'll tell you what I felt *first*, because it surprised me: I felt a cartographer's irritation. Because the contract was badly drafted. Redundant recitals. A consideration clause that contradicted itself in the second paragraph. Whoever had drawn it up had been rushed, or contemptuous, or both — contemptuous, I decided, because the court had never needed to draft well. Who was going to sue them?

And it was precisely because the drafting was sloppy that I did what every mapmaker does with a document: I checked the margins.

Margins are where the truth lives. On maps, the margins hold the scale bars, the declination notes, the little confessions of the surveyor — *here the fog was too thick to see; here I guessed.* On contracts, margins hold the amendments, the later thoughts, the things the parties whispered after the ink dried. I tilted the scroll toward the flameless candlelight.

The left margin was blank.

The right margin was not.

Small marks ran up the margin's edge, tiny, precise, clustered near the bottom third of the scroll. At first glance — at any glance, really — they looked like worm damage, or foxing, or the idle scratches of some clerk's pen. But my fingers knew before my eyes did. There is a notation that cartographers use, one we're half-ashamed of, the way surgeons are ashamed of their scars: surveyor's shorthand, marks for *here be unmapped*, for *boundary in dispute*, for *this line returns to its beginning*. We write it small and slanted and we write it in places authority doesn't look, because half of cartography has always been smuggling the truth past the people who commission the maps.

Someone had written surveyor's shorthand in the margin of Ivo's soul-contract.

My hands were shaking. I'm recording that fact because it matters: the great Mistress Quill-of-the-Outer-Margins, cool as candlewax through seven galleries of personified clauses, stood in the Archive of Ancestral Obligations with her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out.

I translated. It took four passes, because the notation was old-fashioned — pre-war style, the kind my mother used — and because whoever wrote it had been in a hurry and afraid, and fear makes cramped lines. But mark by mark it opened up like a lock:

*A boundary drawn may be redrawn. What is taken by right of purchase may be retaken by right of theft, provided the theft occur before the term's renewal. See the new moon. The clause sleeps until claimed. — M.*

*M.* Marta. His grandmother had hidden an escape hatch in the margin of her own betrayal. She had sold him and then, with the same pen, in the same sitting, had smuggled him a key — written in the language of maps, because the court's clerks read words, every word, but they did not read *notation*. Notation was beneath their notice. Notation was how surveyors talk to each other across distances and decades.

She couldn't undo what she'd done. So she'd left instructions for whoever came after. For the thief in the prophecy. For me.

*Provided the theft occur before the term's renewal. See the new moon.*

I counted back from the wet ink of the prophecy, from the season, from the moon I'd seen rising over the hinterland's shifting hills — thinning, thinning, a coin worn smooth. Three nights. Perhaps fewer. If Ivo's soul was stolen from this court before the new moon, before the contract renewed itself and locked its terms for another generation, the theft would void the sale. Not break it — void it. As if it had never been. And a contract that never was cannot hold a valley's peace hostage, cannot hold *anything* hostage, because you cannot enforce a document that legally never existed.

Hope hit me like weather. I actually had to lean against the shelf, and the shelf — I swear this — leaned very slightly back, the way a dog leans into a hand it trusts. The realm obeyed cartography. I had known that since the hinterland. But standing there in the tea-colored dark with a dead woman's smuggled key in my hands, I understood it differently: the court was a map of itself, and maps can be *amended*.

Somewhere above me, past the galleries and the rotunda and the hall of contested wills, Ivo was alive — the prophecy said thief, and you don't send a thief to steal from a place unless the thing stolen is still there to steal. He was in this mountain of paper, and he thought he'd been abandoned by everyone, including the grandmother whose name sat at the bottom of his own sale, and he didn't know that she'd spent her last act as a free woman carving him a back door.

I rehung the scroll exactly as I'd found it, adjusted the ribbon to the angle of its neighbors, memorized the hook, the row, the aisle, the distance from the chain-curtain — eleven hundred paces, four turns, one staircase disguised as a book spine. Then I copied the margin note into my memory three times, the way you drive a stake into ground you intend to find again in the dark.

And that was when the lights changed.

All down the archive, the flameless candles guttered — not out, but *toward* something, their glow leaning eastward like grass in wind. The salt in the troughs began to hum, a low tone like a wet finger on a bowl's rim. And from far above, muffled by a thousand shelves of souls, came a sound I recognized from the night the brambles took him: a voice wearing a dead woman's cadence, calling a name.

Calling *Ivo*.

The court had noticed its missing scroll's warmth. Or the moon had thinned another degree. Or the trap I'd been half-suspecting since the ferryman's boat had finally decided to spring. It didn't matter which. What mattered was that I had three days' worth of hope crammed into what felt like considerably less than three days of time, and eleven hundred paces between me and the curtain of chains, and somewhere in this paper mountain a boy was hearing his grandmother's voice again and didn't know it was bait.

I ran. Archivists, apparently, do not run — I heard the small print of some nearby clause rustle with disapproval — and I did not care. I had a location, a deadline, and a lockpick written in my own profession's secret hand.

Now I only had to reach him before the court remembered it owned us both.
