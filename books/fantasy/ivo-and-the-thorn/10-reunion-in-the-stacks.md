## 10. Reunion in the Stacks

*Beatrix*

The debt-vaults of the court beneath the mountain smelled like a library that had been left out in the rain and then lied about it. Old paper, old wax, old regret — and under all of it, something sweeter and worse, like ink that had learned to want things.

I had come in as an archivist of debts, which was a real position here, apparently, with a real badge: a sliver of bone stamped with a seal I'd copied from a sleeping clerk's satchel three corridors back. The disguise worked because the court respected paperwork above everything — above blood, above grief, above, I suspected, its own continued existence. Nobody questioned a woman carrying ledgers. They only asked whether she carried them *correctly*, which meant balanced on the forearm, spine outward, at a pace suggesting urgency without admitting panic.

I had been practicing the walk for two hours. My arms ached. Somewhere in this building my person was being held against his will by people who were, technically, clauses.

The vaults went down in spirals. Each ring of shelves held a category of obligation: Debts of Blood, Debts of Silence, Debts Owed to the Weather (a surprisingly full section), Debts the Dead Left Unfinished. The labels were written in a script that rearranged itself when I wasn't looking directly at it, which should have terrified me but instead made me itch for my instruments. Everything here wanted to be mapped. Nothing here had ever been measured. It was the loneliest thing I'd ever seen, and I grew up in a valley where compasses spin.

I found him in the fourth ring down, in the aisle marked *Contracts, Ancestral, Fraudulent or Suspected*.

He was sitting on the floor with his back against a shelf, knees up, turning something over in his hands — a contract, heavy vellum, thorn-sealed. He looked terrible. He looked wonderful. He looked like every map I'd ever drawn of home had gotten up and started walking around without me.

"Ivo," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle like bad porcelain.

His head came up fast — too fast; he'd clearly been listening for footsteps that weren't friendly. For one heartbeat he aimed at me the flat, cornered look of someone deciding whether to run. Then it broke apart entirely.

"Beatrix." He said it like a man testing whether a bridge would hold. Then he was up, and we collided somewhere in the middle of the aisle, and a shelf of ancestral frauds took the impact of both of us and did not complain, because shelves here have seen worse.

He was shaking. So was I. His coat was gone — my fault, technically, since I still had it folded in my pack like a relic — and underneath he was thinner than he'd been four days ago, as if the mountain had been charging him rent by the pound.

"You came," he said into my hair. "You absolute lunatic. You came."

"You say that like there was a version of me that wouldn't."

"There's a version of you that reads the last page of a book first," he said. "I assumed she'd have more sense."

"She died at the border line." I pulled back far enough to look at him, and put my hands on either side of his face, because I needed to confirm he was solid and not some hinterland trick that rearranged when unobserved. He was solid. He was cold. He was mine. "Tell me you're not hurt."

"Define hurt."

"Ivo."

"They haven't touched me," he said. "That's the horrible part. They've been *polite*. They gave me a room with a bed and a candle and a bell I'm not allowed to ring. They keep telling me the court honors its agreements." His jaw tightened. "They showed me the contract, Beatrix. The sale. And Beatrix — I know the handwriting."

I knew. Of course I knew; I'd read the prophecy book until its wet ink stained my fingertips, and I'd spent the ferryman's price and half my sleep wondering how to tell him. But knowing a thing and watching a man say it are different countries.

"It's your grandmother's," I said quietly.

"Yes." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard since the brambles walked into the inn wearing her voice. "She sold me. She sat me on her knee and taught me to whittle and she *sold me*, and the whole valley got to be safe and magic-dead and smug about it, and nobody told me I was the price." He looked down at the contract in his hands. "Do you know what the worst part is? The terms are *fair*. That's what kills me. She paid exactly what they asked. No clause is broken. The court has been scrupulous."

"The court would be," I said. "It's made of fine print."

And then — because I am who I am, because I had crossed a country of almost and paid a ferryman my mother's face to get here — I did the thing that probably saved us, and certainly ruined the moment.

I took the contract out of his hands.

"Hey—" he started.

"Hush. I'm reading."

"You're reading *now*? I pour my heart out—"

"You'll pour it out again later, you're a novelist's worth of feelings, I've met you." I tilted the vellum toward the nearest candle-sconce, where the light burned a color I refused to think too hard about. "There. Hold the light steady."

He held it. He grumbled while doing it, which was so familiar I could have cried.

The contract was dense, thorned script, every clause coiling into the next like roots fighting over the same grave. Most of it I couldn't parse — the language of obligations has grammar I hadn't learned yet. But margins are margins in any language, and cartographers are trained from childhood to read the edges of things, because that's where mapmakers hide their confessions.

There, in the lower margin, in a hand smaller and older than the rest, was a notation.

Not words. Symbols. A bearing mark. An elevation tick. A dashed line ending in an open circle. And beneath them, tiny, almost apologetic:

*§ void upon theft prior to dark-of-moon — see folio ix*

My breath stopped. I made myself keep breathing, because fainting in the fraudulent contracts section seemed like a poor career move.

"Ivo," I said. "Look at this. No — don't look at the words, look at the *marks*. This is surveyor's notation. Bearing, elevation, boundary dash, open circle — that's an unmapped feature, a place not yet charted. Someone wrote an escape clause into your grandmother's contract and hid it in *mapmaker's shorthand*."

He leaned in. His shoulder pressed warm against mine, and I felt him go very still in the way he goes still when he's actually paying attention, which is different from the way he goes still when he's frightened, though I'll admit the difference is subtle.

"That's not court handwriting," he said slowly. "Everything else on this page is... looped. Thorny. Like it grew there. This is straight. Practical. Human."

"And dated?" I turned the margin toward the light again. There — a surveyor's date mark, the kind we use in Thornvale, the kind my mother used, the kind that hasn't changed in a hundred years because cartographers are conservative about anything except lying about distance. "This notation style went out of fashion before I was born. Whoever wrote this knew the old conventions. Knew *our* conventions." I hesitated. "Knew valley conventions."

"Who hides an escape clause in a soul-contract using dead cartographers' shorthand?"

"Someone who expected a cartographer to come looking." My voice came out strange. "Someone who wrote the prophecy book, maybe. Someone who set the whole board up and left instructions in the only language they trusted the right reader to find." I thought of the wet ink, the fresh page, the century-old book waiting for me specifically, and felt the vertigo of a woman realizing she may have been following a trail of breadcrumbs she baked herself in a former life. Or someone had. I filed that horror for later. We had a schedule.

"Read me the actual clause," Ivo said. "The words under the marks."

I found them. Small, cramped, buried under a coil of thorn-script that tried very hard to look like decoration:

*Should the pledged soul be taken — stolen, seized, or otherwise removed from the Court's keeping — before the next dark of the moon, the consideration fails, the bargain voids ab initio, and all claims arising therefrom lapse entire.*

"'Ab initio,'" I translated, "'from the beginning.' Not just your sale. Every claim built on it. The valley's purchase. All of it. If the soul is stolen before the new moon, the whole edifice comes down like — well. Like a map redrawn."

Silence, in the aisle of fraudulent ancestors.

Then Ivo said, very carefully, "When is the new moon?"

I did the arithmetic. I hated the answer. I did it twice hoping the answer would get embarrassed and change.

"Three days," I said.

"Three days." He sat down on the floor again, abruptly, as if his legs had resigned without notice. I sat with him. Our shoulders touched. Above us the shelves creaked with the weight of other people's promises. "Beatrix. Three days to break into the heart of a court that has existed since before the valley had a name, steal a soul out of whatever they keep it in, and get out. And they *know* I'm here. They're not stupid. They wrote the prophecy that brought us both."

"Yes," I said.

"And you're smiling."

"I'm not smiling."

"You're absolutely smiling. It's terrifying. Stop it."

I couldn't stop it. Because here was the thing, the thing I'd been carrying since the border line like a stone in my boot: for four days I had been running on grief and spite, and grief makes a poor rations and spite runs out fast. But this — a clause, a deadline, a door with a lock that had a keyhole — this was *work*. Work I knew how to do. Give me an impossible terrain and a fixed date and I become, frankly, unbearable.

"Three days is enough," I said, "if we treat it like a survey. First, reconnaissance: I need to find where they keep the soul. Second, a route in and out that doesn't exist on any of their maps — good news, nothing here exists on anyone's maps. Third—"

"Fourth," Ivo said, "we survive, which historically is the step we skip."

"We'll add it in pencil."

He laughed — a real one this time, short and startled out of him, and it did something to my chest that I decided not to examine professionally. Then his face sobered, and he reached out and took my hand, and turned it over, and looked at my palm like he was reading it.

"You paid for passage," he said. Not a question. The ferryman's tolls were famous even among the stolen. "What did it cost?"

I thought about lying. I thought about my mother's face, which lived now in a stranger's pocket, traveling roads I'd never draw. I thought about how I'd chosen without ceremony, how easily, and how that ease frightened me more than the mountain did.

"A memory," I said. "One. It's fine. I have others."

"Beatrix."

"Don't 'Beatrix' me. You were sold. I lost a face. We're not keeping score, we're keeping *moving*." I stood, pulling him up with me, and brushed vellum-dust off my borrowed archivist's badge. "Now. Two problems. One: I can't walk out of here with you, because you're a listed asset and the shelves will notice. Two: we need to find the heart-chamber before the court notices *me*, and I've been in this aisle too long already. Fraudulent contracts attract auditors."

As if summoned by the word — and in this place, I was beginning to suspect, everything was — a sound drifted down the spiral from the rings above. Footsteps. More than one pair. Measured, unhurried, the walk of creatures who had never once in their existence been late for anything.

Ivo went rigid. "Bailiffs?"

"Worse," I whispered, straining to listen. "Paperwork. Lots of it."

The footsteps paused at the head of our aisle. A voice like a stamp coming down on wet ink said, "Archivist. Fourth ring. Your requisition does not cover *consultation*."

I looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at me. Three days. One clause. One exit that didn't exist yet.

"Follow my lead," I breathed, "and do try to look contractual."

* * *

*Ivo*

Here is what I learned about my grandmother in the space of one afternoon, holding her handwriting in my hands: she was better at secrets than she was at apologies, and she had known, right up until the end, exactly what she'd done.

The margin-note proved it. You don't hide an escape clause in a contract unless you want it found. You don't write it in dead surveyors' shorthand unless you know who's coming to read it. She had sold me and then, in the smallest, most cowardly, most loving way available to her, she had left the door unlocked.

I hated her. I missed her. Both at once, in the same breath, like breathing in and out.

But I didn't have time to feel any of it properly, because my cartographer had just told me to look contractual, and a thing made of wax seals was standing at the end of the aisle asking questions.

I did what I do best, which is improvise with insufficient information. I stood up straight, held the contract at arm's length like a man bored by his own inheritance, and adopted the expression I use when Beatrix explains contour lines to me for the eleventh time.

"This marginalia," I said, in my best tedium voice, "is misfiled. Surveyor's notation in an ancestral folio. Frankly it's an outrage. Somebody's been doodling in the assets."

The bailiff — if it was a bailiff; it was mostly seals, a torso of layered stamps with a face like a signet ring pressed into tallow — regarded me for a long moment. I felt it reading me the way you'd read a suspicious invoice.

"The archivist," it said, "was not assigned to the fourth ring."

"I reassigned her," I said. "Verbally. There's a form."

"There is always a form," the bailiff agreed, and something in its stamp-face relaxed by a degree, because bureaucracy recognizes its own liturgy anywhere. "The form will be filed."

"It will," said Beatrix, with the serene confidence of a woman who intended no such thing, "first thing tomorrow."

The bailiff nodded, satisfied, and moved on down the aisle to terrorize some other shelf. I let out a breath I'd been holding since the inn.

"You lied to a creature made of official documents," I whispered.

"I amended it," she whispered back. "There's a difference. Come on — we're leaving this ring before it thinks better of us."

We went up the spiral together, her in front, walking that absurd balanced-ledger walk, me behind her carrying the contract rolled under my arm like I owned it, which in a sense I did, in the sense that it owned me. And as we climbed, past Debts of Silence and Debts Owed to the Weather, I watched her work — really watched her, the way I hadn't let myself at the inn, when I'd been too busy being nervous and charming in equal measure. She read the shelves as we passed. Actually read them. Her eyes flicked along the spines and her fingers twitched at her sides like she was drawing bearings in the air, and once she stopped dead in front of a section labeled *Obligations Pending Adjudication* and stared at it with an expression I recognized from maps of places that officially do not exist.

"What?" I asked.

"This section is bigger than the architecture allows," she murmured. "The spiral's circumference shrinks as you go up — I measured it on the way down, don't look at me like that, measuring is how I pray. But this ring isn't shrinking. Which means the court is *adding* space. Growing room for debts it expects to collect soon." Her jaw set. "It's expecting two collections, Ivo. Ours."

"Then we'd better disappoint it," I said, "thoroughly, and within three days."

She glanced back at me, and in the candlelight-that-wasn't-quite-light her smile was sharp as a divider point. "New moon's in seventy-two hours. The soul's somewhere in this mountain, probably close to the heart. And somewhere in these stacks—" she patted the rolled contract under my arm almost affectionately, "—is the loophole your grandmother left us. I need a quiet room, my instruments, and every scrap of paper this court will let me touch."

"And after that?"

"After that," Beatrix Brightwater said, in the voice she used for coastlines that fought back, "we plan a theft."

Above us, far up the spiral, a bell began to ring — not the polite bedside bell they'd given me, but something older, deeper, a sound like a ledger opening. The candles guttered. Every seal in the building, I would have sworn, turned slightly toward the sound.

Somewhere in the court, something had noticed us.

Seventy-two hours. I picked up my pace.
