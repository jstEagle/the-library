## 11. Three Days to New Moon

The cell they gave us had a bed, a washstand, and a window that looked out onto a corridor of salt pillars. I want you to appreciate the absurdity of that. A window, in a prison, in an underworld, where there is no sun to look at and nothing outside worth seeing except more prison. It was not a window at all, of course. It was a clause. *Occupant may observe the passage of justice.* That's what it said, if you knew how to read the walls down here, and by our second day I was learning.

"They're letting us see the bailiffs walk their rounds," Beatrix said, chin on her fist, watching the waxy figures shuffle past below. "It's theater. They want us to feel watched."

"I do feel watched."

"You're supposed to feel *comforted*. Watched-over. There's a difference, and it's the whole trick." She turned from the window and unrolled one of her maps across the narrow bed — the heart-chamber, or her best guess at it, drawn from three days of pretending to admire architecture while counting guards. "Now stop being a prisoner for ten minutes and be a co-conspirator. The lantern's kept in the Court's heart. Literally its heart. The whole mountain grew around it like a pearl around grit."

"And the cage?"

"Thorns. Living ones. They've been growing since before your grandmother signed anything — probably since the first soul ever got ledgered here. Which means we don't break the cage." Her finger traced the spiral of the chamber as she'd sketched it. "We pick it. A lock is just a door that hasn't met the right key yet."

I sat down beside her. The map smelled of graphite and, faintly, of home — of the valley, which I was beginning to understand had never really been mine. "And my soul? What does it look like, when you find it after a hundred years?"

She was quiet a moment. "I don't know," she admitted. "Light, maybe. Or something older than light. But it'll know you, Ivo. Souls are the one thing down here that can't be forged."

*It'll know you.* I held onto that like a rope over a long drop. Because here is what I hadn't told her yet, what I'd only told the dark inside my own skull: pieces of it were already leaking through. Ever since we'd decoded the margin-note, ever since I'd stood close enough to the vaults to hear something humming behind the thorn-steel, I'd been remembering things that never happened to me. Snow falling on a valley I'd never seen in winter. A woman's voice saying *I'm sorry, little bird* in a tone I somehow knew was my grandmother's, though she'd died when I was six and I remembered her only as lavender and cough drops.

A boy who wasn't afraid of anything, walking a fence line at dusk like the world belonged to him.

That boy had my face. I didn't know what to do with that yet, so I did what I always do with things I can't carry: I set them down and helped Beatrix plan a heist.

* * *

We worked by candlelight, because the court provided candles and considered this generosity. Everything down here was generosity: the meals were generous, the blankets were generous, the window was generous. You could drown in courtesy if you weren't careful. I'd begun to understand that the court's cruelty was all in the fine print, and its kindness was the paper it was printed on.

"The new moon is in three days," Beatrix said. "The margin-note says the contract voids if the soul is stolen before then. After the moon turns, the debt comes due — your grandmother's payment matures, the court harvests you, and the prophecy closes itself out neat as a receipt."

"So we steal it tomorrow night."

"We steal it the night *after*. Tomorrow we need one more thing." She tapped the map, at a blank space near the chamber's approach. "This corridor here. My sources disagree about whether it exists."

"Your sources are two bribed archivists and a ferryman who ate your mother's face."

"My sources are impeccable and I resent the characterization." But she didn't smile, and neither did I, because we both knew what that blank space meant. In Beatrix's cartography, a blank space wasn't empty. It was undecided. And undecided ground, in the underworld, belonged to whoever wanted it most.

There was a knock at the door — three soft raps, polite as everything else down here. We'd learned the code by then. One rap meant a servant. Two meant a summons. Three meant someone who thought they were being kind.

It was the Archivist of Minor Debts, the one noble we'd half-befriended, a stooped creature whose body was mostly ledgers bound in grey calfskin, whose face was a title page worn smooth by handling. He brought tea. He always brought tea. I had never once seen him drink any.

"I couldn't help overhearing," he said, setting down the tray, "that you intend to commit theft against the Court."

Beatrix went very still in the way she did when she was deciding whether to lie. I put my hand over hers under the table. *Let me,* I meant. This was my grandmother's sin. My ransom. My court.

"Then you overheard correctly," I said.

The Archivist sighed, and it sounded like pages turning. "I served this Court for nine hundred years. Do you know what I have learned about theft?" He poured the tea himself, hands like pressed flowers. "Every thief believes the vault will be harder than it is, and the escape easier. It is never either. The vault is easy. The Court *wants* the vault tried. But the escape—" his title-page face creased, "—the escape requires a road, and roads are the one thing the Magistrate counts better than anyone."

"You're telling us not to go."

"I'm telling you that the prophecy was bait." He said it gently, the way you tell someone a parent has died. "A century ago, when the contract was drafted, the Court wrote a prophecy into the world and let it drift upriver into your valley's archive. A cartographer. A thief named Ivo. A soul beneath the mountain. Why would we write our own vulnerability into fate?" He folded his flower-hands. "Because a prophecy is a leash. It drags exactly the two people we need down exactly the road we built, at exactly the hour we choose. You were never predicted, children. You were *scheduled*."

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"But," the Archivist said, rising, gathering his tea tray, "I have also learned, in nine hundred years, that schedules are written by clerks. And clerks can be defied by anyone willing to be unscheduled." At the door he paused. "The vaults begin closing tonight. Slowly, floor by floor, so as not to alarm you. By the new moon, every road to the heart-chamber will be sealed but one — and that one will be watched by everything the Court owns."

He left the tea. He always left the tea.

* * *

After he'd gone, Beatrix stood at the false window a long time, watching the salt pillars and the wax bailiffs shuffling their rounds below.

"He's right," she said finally. "It's a trap. It's been a trap since 1897. Maybe longer — maybe the trap is older than the prophecy, maybe the prophecy is just the newest coat of paint on it." She turned around, and her eyes had that look I'd first seen across a table at the valley's edge inn, the look I'd mistaken for restlessness and understood now was hunger. "So we stop trying to slip the trap."

"That seems wise—"

"We *spring* it early. Tonight, before the vaults finish closing. While they still think they have two days of schedule left." She was already rolling up maps, already stuffing compasses into her coat — my coat, actually, the one I'd dropped at the inn doorway, which she'd carried through the veil and never given back. I decided not to ask for it. "A trap sprung early catches the trapper off guard. That's not cartography, that's just common sense."

"It's also insane."

"Yes," she said warmly. "That too."

I should have argued. A smarter man would have argued — would have weighed the odds, counted the bailiffs, asked what happened to the last souls who'd rushed the heart of a court that eats souls. But somewhere beneath my ribs, in the place where the leaking light pooled, something old and unafraid stirred and said: *the world belongs to you; walk the fence line.*

Besides. Three days to the new moon. Two days of schedule left, and shrinking.

"All right," I said. "Tonight. But we do it my way."

"Which is?"

I picked up the candle and grinned at her, and I felt the grin belong partly to a dead boy who'd never learned fear. "Loudly."

Somewhere far above us, or perhaps far below — directions were negotiable down here — a bell began to toll. Not the new moon yet. Just the vaults, closing, floor by floor, like a hand slowly making a fist.

We had until it finished.
