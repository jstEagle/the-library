## 12. The Ferryman's Price Again

The ferryman's boat was waiting where it had no right to be.

That was the thing about the Country of Almost — geography there was less a fact and more a suggestion, and the ferryman treated suggestions as binding contracts. We had left him three days ago on the shore of a river that wasn't on any map because it *was* every map, the one all other rivers were rough drafts of. Ivo and I had walked away inland, toward the mountain with its crown of black thorn, and I had assumed — foolishly, cartographically — that distance meant something.

Now the boat sat in a dry gully between two hills of whispering grass, hull resting on dirt, oars shipped, lantern lit at noon-that-wasn't-noon. The ferryman stood beside it with his hands folded, patient as an unpaid invoice.

"He does that," Ivo said behind me. "Appears where you're about to need him. It's very convenient and I hate it."

"You hate everything that's convenient. You flinched when the innkeeper brought us bread without being asked."

"I'd been having a bad week." He said it lightly, but his hand found mine, and his fingers were cold in the way they'd been cold since the vaults, since he'd started remembering pieces of himself that had spent twenty years in a cage. Cold like something recently thawed.

I looked at the ferryman and felt my stomach do the slow, professional drop of a woman who already knows the terms of a negotiation before it begins. Because you don't summon a ferryman of lost roads by accident. You don't get a dry-docked boat in your path out of coincidence. Somewhere behind his eyes — if he had eyes; the hood was more of a rumor than a garment — he already knew what we needed.

And what we needed was impossible.

* * *

Here is the problem, stated plainly, the way I state everything when I'm frightened: the heart-chamber of the Court Beneath the Mountain could not be reached, because there was no road to it. Not a hidden road. Not a guarded road. A road that did not exist. The Court kept its heart the way misers keep gold — not locked away, but unmade, unwritten, outside the grammar of *where*. Every corridor in that place was paperwork made flesh, and the heart-chamber had never been filed. You couldn't sneak down a hallway that had never been drawn.

I had explained this to Ivo the night before, drawing in the dirt with a stick until the stick wore down to a nub.

"So we're finished," he'd said.

"We're never finished. We're just under-mapped."

He'd smiled at that — the new smile, the one with shadow in it — and said, "You know that's insane, yes? You know that's the sentence of a madwoman?"

"Cartography is applied madness," I'd told him. "Someone has to believe the world can be known before anyone goes looking."

And then this morning the ferryman had appeared, which meant someone had gone looking for *us*.

Ivo hung back as I approached the boat. He'd been wary of the ferryman since the memory-price — since I'd come back from that first crossing missing my mother's face, missing it so thoroughly that when I tried to picture her I got a warm blur and the smell of bread and nothing else. He watched me pay that price and had never quite forgiven either of us for how easily I'd done it.

"Ferryman," I said.

The hood tilted. When he spoke, his voice came from everywhere the way water comes from everywhere in a flood. "Cartographer."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know why everyone is here. They come to cross. The question is only ever the fare." He gestured, one long sleeve moving like smoke deciding to be an arm, and the air above the boat rippled. In the ripple I saw it: the mountain, cut open, its corridors glowing like veins in a leaf. And at the center, a darkness shaped exactly like a room that had never been built. "You want the heart-chamber. There is no road. There has never been a road. The Court made certain of it before your grandmother's grandmother learned to walk."

"But roads can be made."

"All roads are made. That is their whole nature. Someone walks a path, and the world remembers it." The hood tilted again. "The world here remembers what is written. You carry the writing."

My satchel suddenly weighed as much as a cathedral. Inside it: my instruments, my ink, and the prophecy book with its hungry blank pages — the book the Court wanted badly enough to build a trap the size of a century around it. I had been guarding those blank pages like a prisoner. It hadn't occurred to me, until this exact moment, that they might also be a tool.

"You want me to draw the road," I said.

"I want nothing. I am a ferryman. Wanting is for people with destinations." A pause, dry as old vellum. "But a road must be drawn by a hand that knows places intimately enough to lie about them convincingly. Your hand will do."

"And the fare?"

The ferryman was quiet for a moment. Somewhere in the whispering grass, something small and forgotten scurried past — I caught a glimpse of it, a creature like a hedgehog made of lost umbrellas, and it broke my heart a little, the way everything in this country did. This realm fed on forgotten things. It grew fat on them. And it had noticed, over our days of travel, exactly what I carried that could be forgotten.

"The last time," the ferryman said, "you paid with a face. A dear one. You chose quickly. I have often wondered about that."

"Don't wonder. Bill me."

"This time the fare is larger." He raised one hand, and in the air between us, ink bloomed — my ink, my own particular sepia-black, the shade I'd mixed myself at fourteen and used ever since. It wrote itself into words across the empty air:

*Beatrix Brightwater, cartographer. All works her own.*

"Your name," the ferryman said, "as it appears on every map you have ever made."

* * *

I want to be honest about the next part, because honesty is the only instrument I've never lost.

For a moment — one long, cold moment — I said nothing, and in that silence I understood the shape of the price completely, the way I understand terrain from a high ridge.

Every map I had ever signed bore my name. The border survey of Thornvale, four years of work, *surveyed by Beatrix Brightwater*. The valley charts in the archive, cornered in my careful capitals. The schoolroom maps I'd made at twelve, the coastlines I'd invented and labeled *here be Beatrix*. My name was the signature that made my work mine. It was the thread that tied every line I'd ever drawn back to the hand that drew it.

Take it off all of them, and what remained? Maps by no one. Charts unsigned. A body of work orphaned in every archive of two worlds. And worse — because the ferryman dealt in essences, not ink — I suspected the price went deeper than paper. If my name came off every map, perhaps it would come off every *place*. Perhaps Thornvale itself would forget it had ever held me. Perhaps I would become what this country was made of: a forgotten thing, walking.

"That's not a fare," I said. "That's an erasure."

"It is a road," the ferryman said. "Roads are expensive. That is why most people stay home." He let the floating words hang there, gleaming. "Consider: the Court took a boy's soul and called it peace. It wrote him into a ledger and sold the interest. You mean to steal him back from a place that owns the concept of ownership. What did you imagine that would cost? Kind thoughts?"

Behind me, Ivo made a sound — small, involuntary, the sound of a man doing arithmetic he hates.

"No," he said, stepping up beside me. "No. Find another fare. Take another year of her memories. Take—"

"You've nothing she needs," the ferryman said, not unkindly. "You are the cargo, thief. Cargo doesn't negotiate."

"I'm not cargo."

"You were sold once. It leaves a mark. Ask your grandmother."

Ivo's hand closed into a fist, and under his skin — I saw it clearly in the flat light — shadows moved, faint and slow, like fish under ice. His stolen soul, leaking through whatever thin wall still separated him from the lantern in the Court's heart. He was becoming something the valley had never met. Something the valley might not survive meeting. Which was, I reminded myself, the entire reason we were standing in a dry gully negotiating with a hooded entity over the price of an impossible road.

"Time," the ferryman said. "The Court closes its vaults at the new moon. Two nights remain. After that, even I cannot help you, and I say that with genuine regret, because watching the two of you fail would be tedious, and I have been alive since before tedium was invented."

I looked at Ivo. He looked at me. Twenty years of his life sat caged in a thorn-lantern beneath a mountain, and the look on his face was the same look he'd worn in the inn doorway the night the brambles took him — except reversed, except this time he was the one watching someone else be dragged away.

"There has to be—" he started.

"Ivo." I touched his fist until it opened. "Do you remember what I told you in the vaults? About fine print?"

"You said hope lives in fine print."

"I said hope lives in fine print, and so do loopholes." I turned back to the ferryman. "Terms. Precise ones. I want them spoken aloud, because in this country I suspect speech is binding and I intend to bind you right back."

The hood inclined a fraction. Amused. Good. Entities that could be amused could be managed.

"One," I said. "You take my name from my maps. From the *maps*. Not from me. I keep the name in my mouth, in my memory, in the way I say it when I introduce myself to innkeepers. If some stranger asks who I am, I can answer."

The ferryman was silent a moment. "Accepted. Though understand: names live where they are written. Yours will live nowhere but you. When you die, cartographer, it dies with you. No monument, no archive, no student copying your work will know whose hand held the pen."

"Noted. Two: the road you draw from my drafting — it stays drawn. Permanent. A real road, in the world's memory, that anyone may walk after us. If I'm paying to make a place exist, it exists for everyone."

Something flickered under the hood. Surprise, maybe. Or respect. "Accepted. That clause costs you nothing and pleases me strangely. Three?"

"Three." My voice almost failed here, and I hated it, and I pushed through anyway. "When this is done — when the contract is voided or we're dead trying — you take me wherever I ask. One crossing, anywhere, no additional fare. Because if I erase myself from every map I've ever made, I'll want to go somewhere no map has ever reached, and I'll want passage."

"A cartographer's clause," the ferryman murmured. "Always charting the exit." He extended one sleeve-shadowed hand. "Agreed, on all three terms. Now the question that matters, Brightwater. Do you consent to the fare?"

* * *

Here is what I felt, and I record it precisely because later I will want to lie about it:

I felt light.

Not relief — lightness. As though the name had been a coat I'd worn so long I'd mistaken it for skin, and taking it off was cold and terrifying and, in some shameful corner of me, exhilarating. I have spent my whole life wanting to be somewhere else, wanting to see past the edge of the page, and here at last was a bargain that promised it: a self unplaced. Unmapped. Free to be anywhere, because I belonged nowhere.

Then I thought of Ivo's face in the inn doorway, holding his dropped coat, and the lightness curdled into something heavier and better. Love, maybe. Love is just longing that finally has an address.

"Yes," I said. "I consent."

The ferryman's hand closed around the floating words, and they came apart like smoke in rain.

It didn't hurt. That was the worst part. I braced for pain, for the tearing sensation I'd felt when my mother's face was taken — that hot unraveling behind the eyes. But this was quiet. A coolness starting at my fingertips, spreading up my arms, settling over me like frost forming on a window: beautiful, silent, and a sign that the warmth was leaving.

I felt it happen in my hands first. My hands, which had drawn every line, which knew the weight of a ruling pen the way other women know the weight of a wedding ring — they forgot, in some deep muscular way, that the lines had been *mine*. Then the archives of Thornvale, far away beyond the veil, quietly reorganized themselves: four years of border surveys filed themselves under *unknown hand*. The schoolroom maps of my childhood faded their signatures to ghost-gray. Somewhere, in a drawer in an inn I'd stayed at, a napkin sketch of the hinterland lost the little *B.B.* I'd doodled in its corner.

And Thornvale — I felt this one like a bruise — Thornvale shifted me gently out of its memory. The valley where I was born, where I had measured every stream and quarreled with every compass, decided, in its patient geological way, that it had never contained a Beatrix Brightwater. I was a visitor there now. A guest in my own life.

"Oh," Ivo said softly. He was staring at me as though the light had changed. Maybe it had. "Bea."

"I'm still here," I said. And then, testing it, needing to hear it: "Beatrix Brightwater. Say it back to me."

"Beatrix Brightwater." He said it firmly, like a man planting a flag. "Say it again."

"Beatrix Brightwater." It sounded the same in my mouth. That was the term's cruel mercy: inside my own head, I was intact. Only the world's records of me had been emptied out. I was a word that existed solely as speech now, passed mouth to mouth, surviving only while someone bothered to say me.

"Every day," Ivo said. "I'll say it every day. Breakfast, lunch, and dinner. You'll be sick of hearing it."

"That," I said, and my voice cracked only slightly, "is the most romantic threat anyone has ever made me."

* * *

The ferryman had already begun.

He unshipped the lantern from the prow and set it on the ground between us, and from within his robes he produced — I will swear to this until I stop believing my own eyes — a pen. Long, black, elegant, its nib a sliver of night. He held it out to me.

"Your instruments draw what exists," he said. "Mine draws what consents to exist. But the hand must be yours. The road takes your shape, cartographer. That is the nature of roads. Walkers leave themselves in them."

I took the pen. It was warm, and it hummed against my palm like a living thing pretending to be a tool.

"What do I draw on?" I asked. "There's no paper."

The ferryman gestured at the ground, the gully, the hills, the gray-gold sky of the Country of Almost. "Everything is paper here. That is the country's one virtue."

So I knelt in the dirt of a world that rearranged itself when unobserved, and I began to draw.

I drew the way I had always dreamed of drawing — without reference, without survey, without permission. I started with the gully we stood in, because good cartography starts where your feet are, and I gave it banks and a bed and a name I invented on the spot and did *not* sign. I drew the river of all rivers rising out of it, because every road should begin at water; water forgives bad draftsman. I drew the hills folding backward into foothills, the foothills hardening into the mountain's skirt, and then — this was the part that made my breath come short — I drew *up*. Into the mountain. Through stone rendered in careful hachures, through corridors I remembered from the Court's glowing veins, through the bailiff-quarters and the ledger-halls and the salt-and-candlelight nave where the nobles had personified themselves into clauses. I drew past all of it, because none of it was the destination.

At the center I drew a small circle. And around the circle, thorns. And inside the thorns, a flame.

The heart-chamber. Unmade, unfilable, existing nowhere — until now. Until a hand that knew places intimately enough to lie about them convincingly had insisted otherwise.

The ground shivered. Where my pen had passed, the dirt darkened and firmed into actual earth, actual stone, an actual descending way opening in the gully floor like a wound healing in reverse. Air breathed up out of it, smelling of candlewax and salt and old paper. The road to the heart-chamber, which had never existed, now existed, permanent, public, mine-by-consequence-but-no-one's-by-name.

I sat back on my heels. My hands were shaking. Not from fear.

"Beautiful," Ivo said quietly, meaning the road, meaning me, meaning both, and I let myself believe him for exactly three seconds.

Then the ferryman spoke, and the amusement was gone from his voice.

"The Court has felt the pen," he said. "They will have read the road's authorship — or rather, they will have read its *lack* of authorship, and lack frightens them more than any name. Bailiffs are already moving. Writs are being sealed." The hood turned toward the mountain, where — now that I looked — the black crown of thorns seemed to have grown denser, angrier, like a brow lowering. "You have perhaps a day before they find the door you've made and post guards upon it. Less, if they are clever. They are usually clever."

"Then we shouldn't stand here admiring the view," Ivo said. He pulled me to my feet, and his hand — still cold, still thawing — gripped mine like a promise.

I shouldered my satchel. The prophecy book knocked against my hip, its blank pages rustling with what I could almost swear was impatience. Somewhere in my chest, in the space where my mother's face used to live, in the space where my name used to anchor me to every map I'd ever loved, there was a new kind of quiet. Unplaceable. Unwritten. Free, in the way that drowning men are free of gravity.

"Cartographer," the ferryman said, as we turned to the descending road. "One courtesy, since you bargained well."

I looked back.

"When you void their contract — and you will, or you won't, the future is poorly surveyed — remember this: the Court fears you now not because of what you carry." The hood nodded toward the prophecy book. "They fear you because you are the first hand in a thousand years that has drawn a road where they swore there could be none. Ledgers can confiscate anything *owned*. They have no procedure for a woman who belongs to no map."

"Is that a compliment?" I asked.

"It is intelligence," said the ferryman. "Spend it wisely."

We went down into the mountain, along a road that hadn't existed yesterday, two people the world was rapidly forgetting, carrying a stolen book, a borrowed pen, and between us — spoken aloud each morning, like grace — a single surviving copy of a name.

Behind us, far above, the thorns on the mountain's crown began to move.
