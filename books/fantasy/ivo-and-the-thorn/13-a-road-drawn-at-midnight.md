## 13. A Road Drawn at Midnight

### Beatrix

The ferryman had told me the road would open at midnight, and I had spent the hours since learning what it meant to be unplaceable.

It was not, as I might once have imagined, a feeling of freedom. It was the sensation of being a word on the tip of everyone's tongue — present, needed, and impossible to say. The bailiffs who had begun sweeping the vaults an hour ago looked straight through me twice, their wax-seal faces tilting as though I were a smudge on a window. Ivo's hand found mine in the dark and gripped it hard enough to hurt, which I appreciated. Pain is evidence. Evidence means you exist somewhere.

"Tell me again," he whispered, "what you gave up."

"My name. From every map I ever drew." I kept my voice flat, because if I let any feeling into it, all of them would come at once. "Every border I've ever inked has a little signature in the corner. Beatrix Brightwater, cartographer, in her own hand. They're blank now. All of them."

"And you?"

"I'm not blank." I squeezed back. "I'm just unsigned."

Above us, the Court of Salt and Candlelight went on burning its ten thousand tapers, and the ledger-nobles murmured to one another in clauses, and somewhere below our feet the heart-chamber waited with its thorn-caged lantern — but none of that mattered yet. What mattered was midnight, and whether the ferryman kept his bargains.

He did. That was the terrible thing about him. He always did.

At the stroke of twelve — I knew it by the way every candle in the corridor guttered in unison, like a room full of people inhaling — the wall opposite us began to bleed.

Not blood. Ink. A seam split down the black stone, and from it poured a line of silver-white, thin as thread, and the thread began to move. It ran along the floor, turned where no turn should have been possible, climbed a wall that had been solid masonry a moment before, and drew — there is no other word for it — *drew* a road into existence. The stone softened under the line like wax under a stylus. An archway swelled out of nothing. Beyond the archway lay a road that went uphill through a country I had never charted, lit by a moon that was not ours, and the road hummed faintly, the way a fresh line hums under a pen that believes in it.

"He actually drew it," I breathed. "A road that doesn't exist. He drew it anyway."

"Is that possible?" Ivo asked.

"It's impossible," I said. "That's rather the point. Impossible roads are the only ones the court can't follow."

I was wrong about that last part. But I didn't know it yet, and for about ninety seconds, ignorance was bliss.

We ran.

The road took us at a tilt, uphill through that borrowed moonlight, and the country on either side of it was made of everything the underworld had forgotten to be — hills of unfiled grievances, forests of lapsed contracts gone feral and green, rivers of ink so old it had dried mid-sentence and become landscape. My cartographer's heart broke a little with longing even as my lungs burned. I wanted to stop. I wanted to map all of it. There is no ache quite like running past a country that wants to be drawn and having no time to draw it.

Behind us, the archway screamed.

I heard it before I understood it — a sound like a hundred documents being torn lengthwise — and when I glanced back, the moonlit road behind us was filling with shapes. Bailiffs. Not the polite, seal-faced clerks of the vaults. These were writs given legs, parchment bodies rolled tight as scrolls, faces stamped in dripping red wax, and they moved the way paper moves in wind, which is to say horribly fast and with no respect for physics.

"They're on the road!" Ivo shouted.

"That's not possible!"

"A lot of things tonight aren't!"

He was right, and I hated him slightly for it, and loved him entirely, and there was no time to sort out the ratio. I thought fast, the way I think fastest — in lines and borders. Why could they follow? Because the ferryman had drawn the road, yes, but a road is only a permission. Somewhere, the court held the counter-permission. Somewhere there was a warrant with our description on it, and warrants, in that place, were not metaphors.

"We can't outrun them," I said. "Paper beats distance. We have to make the road disagree with them."

"How do you make a road disagree?"

"You amend it." And I did the maddest thing I have ever done while running for my life, which is saying something, given my life lately. I pulled out my compass, knelt — Ivo nearly tripped over me — and pressed the needle to the road itself.

The road was ink. Fresh ink. And fresh ink can still be changed.

I dragged the needle sideways and drew a fork.

The road shuddered. The new branch peeled away downhill, sharp as a margin note, and I wrote it the only way I know how to write anything — with intent, with certainty, with the absolute conviction that a line drawn boldly enough becomes true. The bailiffs hit the fork at full scroll-speed and split, half taking my branch, half continuing after us, and for a moment we gained ground.

Then Ivo made a sound I'd never heard from him before.

It wasn't a shout. It was smaller than that. A caught breath, the kind you make when you see your own name written somewhere it shouldn't be. He had stopped dead in the middle of an impossible road with monsters at his back, staring at his own hands.

"Ivo—"

"I know this hill," he said. His voice was strange. Distant. "Beatrix. I know this hill. I sledded down this hill. There was — there was a wall at the bottom, and Grandmother said don't go past the wall, and I went past the wall, and the snow came up to my—" He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and terrified in a way that had nothing to do with bailiffs. "This isn't a memory. I never sledded anywhere. I grew up in Thornvale. We don't have hills like this."

Behind him, one of the bailiffs raised a wax arm, and its hand unfolded into a summons.

There was no time. There is never time; time is a luxury taxed heavily in that country. I hauled him forward by the collar and we ran, and the summons cracked against the air where his head had been, and the road bent, and I filed his words away in the part of me that does not panic — the part that maps.

*He's remembering,* I thought, as the impossible road carried us down through the ink-dark country. *The cage is leaking.*

And leaking souls, I suspected, could be followed. Which meant we weren't running toward the heart-chamber anymore.

We were leaving a trail.

### Ivo

Here is what nobody tells you about getting your soul stolen: you don't miss it.

You can't. Missing requires the thing that was taken. For twenty-six years I had been a man-shaped absence walking around Thornvale, and I had seemed fine — kind, even, in the way that people are kind when some essential friction has been sanded off them. Beatrix used to tease me that I was the calmest person she'd ever met. I used to feel quietly proud of that.

Now, running down a road that a dead ferryman had dreamed into being with bailiffs of living paperwork howling at our backs, I understood that I had never been calm. I had been *incomplete*, and incompleteness looks a lot like peace from the inside.

And now the pieces were coming back, and they came back the way floodwater comes back — all at once, and carrying debris.

The hill was the first. I knew its exact slope, the squeak of old runners on crusted snow, the specific terror-joy of a seven-year-old going too fast. I knew the wall at the bottom, dry-stone and mossy, and I knew — this arrived like a knife between the ribs — that I had climbed over it on purpose, because beyond the wall the snow fell differently, softer, and I had wanted to catch it on my tongue.

Grandmother had found me there. She hadn't shouted. She had gone white as the drifts, picked me up, and carried me home without a word, and that night I'd heard her crying in the kitchen, and I hadn't known why, and now I knew why, and knowing was worse than not knowing.

She had already signed me away by then. Or she was about to. Either way, she had looked at me sitting in the snow past the wall — past the border of the safe world she'd bought with me — and she had seen the invoice coming due.

"Keep running," Beatrix gasped beside me. She had a cut on her cheekbone from a low-hanging branch of some forgotten oak, and she hadn't noticed it, and I loved her so much in that moment that the feeling itself felt foreign — too large, too hot, like wearing someone else's coat. Was that mine? Was love mine, or had it leaked in with the rest?

More came.

The taste of the valley's first snow — metallic, clean, tasting faintly of iron the way all Thornvale water did, magic-dead and mineral-honest. My grandmother's hands kneading bread, flour in the creases of her knuckles. Her voice reading aloud on winter nights, not stories — ledgers, farm accounts, anything, everything, her voice steady as a metronome, as though rhythm itself could keep something at bay. And underneath all of it, threaded through every memory like wire through paper lanterns, a single sentence in her handwriting, which I had now seen twice: once on the sale-contract in the vaults, and once, impossibly, inside my own skull, where she had apparently written it long ago and I had only just grown tall enough to read it:

*I am sorry. I would do it worse.*

Not better. Worse. That was the part that undid me. Not a grandmother's lie — *I had no choice, I did the best I could* — but the truth, ugly and unbent: given the same valley and the same fear, she would trade me again, and faster, and sleep fine after.

I ran, and grieved, and ran.

"Talk to me," Beatrix said. She always knew. I don't know how she always knew; I suspect it's the same faculty that lets her look at a blank hillside and see the road waiting inside it. "Your soul's doing something. Your eyes keep changing."

"Changing how?"

"Like weather. Like there's more sky in them than there was yesterday." She ducked a branch. "What are you remembering?"

"Snow. Bread. Her." A bailiff's shriek tore the air behind us — the sound of a writ being executed, literally — and I put on speed. "Beatrix, the memories aren't random. They're following the road. The road goes uphill toward the mountain, and the memories go uphill too, like they're — like they're homing."

She was quiet for three strides. I have learned to fear her quiet strides.

"They're leaking through the cage," she said finally, "and they leak *toward* you. Which means the cage isn't sealed. Which means—" Her breath caught, and not from the running. "Ivo. If your soul can leak out, then the theft doesn't require breaking in. It requires opening a door that's already cracked."

"That sounds like good news."

"It is excellent news, and it's also why they're catching up." She flung a glance backward. "They're not chasing us to stop the theft. They're herding us. Every memory that leaks makes you brighter, and brighter is easier to track, and if they let us get all the way to the heart-chamber with your soul half-awake in your chest—"

"—then the theft completes itself," I finished, "and both our souls stand forfeit, and they harvest us at the new moon with the paperwork already done."

"Exactly. Isn't it awful when you start thinking in clauses?" But she smiled, grimly, and I understood: the horror was real, and so was the plan forming behind her eyes, and with Beatrix those two things had never once been mutually exclusive.

The road crested a ridge, and below us the mountain rose at last in earnest — the black crown I'd glimpsed from the hinterland, now filling half the sky, its slopes stitched with corridors of candlelight like veins of fire in obsidian. The heart-chamber was in there. My soul was in there. Three days to the new moon, and the whole court waking around us like a ledger realizing it's been forged.

The bailiffs crested the ridge behind us, thirty strong, wax faces gleaming, and the lead one unrolled its entire torso to reveal a document the size of a sail. Even at a distance I could read the heading, because the court writes big when it's certain:

*WRIT OF PURSUIT AND RECOVERY. RE: ONE (1) SOUL, PARTIALLY ESCAPED. RE: ONE (1) CARTOGRAPHER, UNPLACED.*

Unplaced. They couldn't write my name — Beatrix had burned hers out of every map, and it seemed the court's ink obeyed the same law. For one absurd moment I felt proud of her. Then the writ's red seals began to peel off its surface and fly at us like hornets.

"Down!" Beatrix tackled me behind a boulder of compacted grievance — it was cold and damp and faintly resentful, which describes most of my adolescence — and the seals burst against the stone, each one blooming into a small flaming order: *HALT. RETURN. SURRENDER.* The words hung in the air, glowing, insisting.

And here is the thing. Here is the thing I will carry home with me if I ever get home, along with the shadows under my skin and the taste of other people's snow in my mouth.

The orders almost worked. On me. My legs wanted to fold. My mouth wanted to say *yes*. Twenty-six years of being owned leaves grooves in you, and the writs ran along those grooves like water finding channels, and I felt myself beginning to halt, beginning to return, beginning to surrender—

Then a memory surfaced. Not an old one. A *new* one — or rather, an old one from the self I'd been before the theft, the boy who climbed walls, and it said, in my own seven-year-old voice, clear as a bell across snow:

*No.*

Just that. Just the first word of defiance, preserved in amber all this time, waiting in the cage for the door to crack.

I stood up. The writs flared, furious, rephrasing themselves — *SURRENDER, SURRENDER, SURRENDER* — and I looked at their little burning letters and said, out loud, in a voice that had gravel in it from somewhere older than my throat:

"No. You have the wrong man. I'm not the surrendered one. I'm the *stolen* one. And stolen property," I said, stepping over the boulder, toward the mountain, toward the heart-chamber, toward the thing beating in its thorn-cage that beat in time with my own traitor heart, "runs home."

Beatrix came up beside me. She was bleeding a little, breathing hard, unnamed on every map in two worlds, and she grinned at me like I'd just invented a new color.

"There he is," she said. "There's the thief."

The road plunged downhill toward the mountain gates, and the bailiffs came screaming after us like a blizzard of red tape, and we ran — not away, not anymore.

We ran *in*.

And far above, behind the black crown of the mountain, the last sliver of the old moon thinned, and thinned, and began to go out.
