## 14. What the Soul Remembers

The lantern hummed against my ribs like a wasp in a jar.

I had carried it for two days now — pressed beneath my coat, strapped there with strips of bailiff's wax-seal ribbon that Beatrix had sworn would hold and then, when it held, looked quietly smug about. The soul inside my chest's neighbor was warm the way a stone is warm after a long day of sun: patient heat, older than me, indifferent to my comfort.

And it was leaking.

That was the only word for it. Leaking. Little seeps of *not-me* memory rising through the cracks the court had left in me a hundred years before I was born — no, that wasn't right either. The cracks were mine. My grandmother had made them when she sold what filled them, and everything that came back through them now arrived like water finding its old channels, as if the shape of me had been waiting all along, empty, for exactly this.

I sat in the dark of a corridor the court had forgotten to finish building — one of those places where the paperwork ran out mid-wall, so that the plaster gave way to raw clauses, sentences half-written in a script that hurt to read sideways — and I let it leak.

* * *

The first fragment came as snow.

Not falling snow. *Tasting* snow. I was small — smaller than any memory I owned — and I had caught a flake on my tongue at the valley's edge, and it had tasted the way cold tastes before you know the word for cold: clean, electric, a little wild. And around me the air had done something impossible. It had *shimmered*. A thread of light, thin as spider silk, had drifted down through the pines and touched my open palm, and I had laughed —

I stopped laughing in the present, because I understood what I was remembering.

Magic. A child standing in Thornvale, feeling magic touch him. Except Thornvale was magic-dead. Except compasses spun and spells died on our border and every map Beatrix ever drew said so, in her own hand, in ink she'd have defended with her life.

Except the valley hadn't always been dead. It had been *made* dead. Bought dead. Paid for — and here the memory turned over like a stone showing its underside — paid for with me.

I sat with that for a while. The corridor dripped somewhere. In this place even the drips sounded contractual, like a quill tapping out terms.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about getting your soul back in installments: you don't get to choose which parts arrive first. I might have preferred the good ones. Instead the snow came, and then —

* * *

Then Grandmother.

Not the grandmother I knew. The one I knew smelled of bread and lamp oil and told terrible puns and once chased a tax collector off the porch with a broom while narrating, loudly, that she was defending hearth and home from "a man who could not take a hint or a hint of a hint." That grandmother I loved without complication, the way you love someone whose debts haven't come due yet.

This one stood in a room I didn't recognize — a room with too many doors, all of them shut — and she was crying.

"I'm sorry," she said, to someone across a table I couldn't see. Her voice was the voice from the brambles, the stolen voice the thorn-court had used to call my name at midnight, but younger here, unbroken, and it shook. "I'm sorry. He'll never know. That's the mercy of it — he'll never know what he was for."

Someone answered. The answer was a sound like a seal pressing into wax, and I felt it in my teeth even now, a century downstream.

"He'll be happy," she insisted. "He'll have the valley. Whole and safe and *ordinary*, and ordinary is — ordinary is a gift, isn't it? Isn't it?" Her voice cracked on the last word, and whatever sat across the table did not reply, because things made of clauses do not console, they only countersign. "Take it, then. Take it. But write it down that I asked. Write down that I wept. Let some clerk in hell note that Marta Ivo's-daughter wept."

Marta. My mother's mother. The woman who taught me to whittle and never once, not once in all my years of her, let her eyes go wet where I could see.

I learned something in that fragment that I will carry longer than the scars this place has given me: she hadn't sold me carelessly. She had sold me *carefully*. She had haggled. She had gotten the best price the market allowed — a whole valley's peace for one boy's soul, payable at majority, the court's claim dormant until I came of age — and she had spent the rest of her life making sure the years she'd bought were worth what they cost.

Every pun. Every pancake morning. Every time she'd stood between me and the world's sharp edges and made me laugh instead of look. She hadn't just been being my grandmother. She'd been *paying interest*. Making the principal bearable. Filling the hollow she'd carved with so much love that neither of us noticed it was hollow.

I don't know if that makes it better. I've turned it over both ways and it comes up bloody either time. But it makes it *true*, and true is the currency I have left.

* * *

Beatrix found me there, eventually, in the unfinished corridor. Of course she did. She finds everything; it's her whole profession. She came around the corner with her compass out and her hair escaping its braid in the way it does when she's been running, and she took one look at my face and sat down beside me on the floor of the underworld without a word about how undignified that was, which is how I knew she was frightened for me.

"Leaking again," I said.

"How much?"

"A snowflake. An apology." I swallowed. "My grandmother's apology."

Beatrix went very still. She had heard the brambles speak in that voice same as I had. She had watched me get dragged through a seam in the air by my dead grandmother's words, and she had followed me into a country of almost-things to get me back, and I think she had privately decided the voice was a cruelty the court invented. It was worse than that. It was a cruelty my family had authored and the court merely *quoted*.

"She loved you," Beatrix said finally. It wasn't a question, but it had the set of her jaw that meant she was prepared to defend the statement against all comers.

"She loved me *and* she sold me," I said. "Both things are true. That's the part that — Trix, that's the part that doesn't fit anywhere. I keep trying to put it in a box and it won't go. It's like one of your maps where two roads both claim to be the same road and each one insists the other is the forgery."

"That's called a disputed border," she said. "We'll survey it later." Then, softer: "What else came through?"

So I told her about the snow. About the shimmer in the air above a child's open palm. And I watched her face do the thing it does when a map contradicts itself — the tiny furrow, the recalculation happening behind her eyes like weather.

"Magic touched you," she said slowly. "In Thornvale. Before the bargain closed."

"Before it *closed*, maybe. Maybe the valley was dying already and she finished it off. Maybe the sale needed a witness and the valley itself..." I shook my head. "I don't know. I'm not the cartographer."

"No," she said, and there was something strange and bright in her voice, "but you're the *territory*." She kissed my temple, which under the circumstances was generous, since I probably smelled like wax and despair. "Rest. I'm going to go look at the contract margins again. There's notation in there I couldn't read yesterday and I want another try with fresh eyes."

She left. I stayed. And the soul leaked again.

* * *

This time it wasn't a scene. It was a *self*.

That's the closest way I can describe it. Not an event, not a moment — a whole way of being, arriving all at once like stepping into a warm bath. A version of Ivo who had never learned to be afraid.

I know how that sounds. Everyone is afraid of something; fear is load-bearing; take it out of a person and the roof comes down. But this boy — this earlier draft of me — genuinely had none. Not recklessness, mind. Recklessness is fear wearing a costume. This was something rarer: a child who had never been taught that the world could take things from him. Who walked to the valley's edge alone at six years old because the horizon looked interesting. Who picked up strange animals and strange objects and once, according to the muscle-memory in the fragment, tried to befriend a thornbush and came away bleeding and *unbothered*, laughing, holding the thorn up to the light like a treasure.

A thorn. Even then. The court has a sense of humor. I'd bet my remaining soul on it.

And underneath the fragment, threaded through it like wire through wax, was the knowledge of what happened to that boy. Because the sale hadn't taken his soul all at once — I understand that now, lying here in the dark with the lantern ticking against my ribs. It had taken it in *payments*. Every fear I ever learned, every flinch, every night I lay awake cataloguing everything that could go wrong — those weren't character. Those were *withdrawals*. The court had reached into me across the miles and taken the fearless boy out in pieces, and what it left behind, what grew back around the absence like scar tissue, was the careful, wry, watchful man who checks exits and keeps his heart in escrow.

I grieved him there on the floor of the unfinished corridor. Properly, ugly, hands-over-face grieved him, the way you grieve the dead — because he *was* dead, mostly, that open-handed boy, and what was coming back through the cracks was a resurrection, and resurrections are never tidy. You don't get the person back. You get the person back *plus* everything that happened to the space where they used to be.

Which raised the question I'd been circling for two days, the one with teeth:

Which self did Beatrix fall for?

* * *

She came back before I'd settled it. Found me still on the floor, because apparently I'd stopped moving entirely, which in the court beneath the mountain is a bad habit — stillness invites assessment.

"You're doing the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided something terrible about yourself and are presenting it to yourself as fact." She sat down again. "You have three faces, Ivo. Wry, worried, and *that* one. I've begun keeping records."

"Beatrix." I made myself say it plainly, because wry was the escape hatch and I was trying very hard not to use it. "When you fell for me — whichever way you fell, whatever that was — which one were you talking to? The one with the soul, or the one the court built out of the hole where it used to be?"

She considered this with the seriousness she gives to contour lines. I love that about her. Other people would have rushed to reassure. Beatrix treats questions like terrain: you don't argue with terrain, you *survey* it.

"Can I answer honestly?" she said.

"That's generally my preference, yes."

"When I met you, you were the careful one. The wry one. The man who checks exits." She counted these off on her fingers, cartographer's habit, ordering the world. "And I want to tell you something about maps, because it's the only metaphor I own. When I draw a valley, I don't draw the valley that's *there*. I can't. A map is always missing something — the smell of it, the weight of it, the way light moves across it at four in the afternoon. So what I actually draw is the valley's *shape*. What it does. Where it bends."

She took my hand. Her fingers were ink-stained and cold and I held on like a drowning man, which, fair enough, I basically was.

"You are not the boy," she said. "And you are not the hole. You're the shape the hole made — the way you bend around what was taken. And that shape is who I fell for. If the soul comes all the way home, the shape changes. I accept that risk. I've read enough maps to know that territory changes and you love it anyway or you never loved it at all."

I laughed, which surprised us both, and which hurt, because laughing with a soul leaking into you turns out to be a full-body event — the laugh came out doubled somehow, mine and the boy's, braided.

"And if I change into someone you don't—" I started.

"Then I'll redraw," she said simply. "It's what I do."

* * *

Later — I don't know how much later; time down here is billed by the hour and we couldn't afford much of it — the leaking brought one more thing.

Not a memory. A *warning*.

It arrived as the boy's knowledge, the fearless child's instinct for reading a room, except aimed at a room made of clauses. I was half-asleep against the wall when it hit me: the sudden animal certainty that we were being *watched*, not by bailiffs but by something higher up the org chart. That the corridors had gone quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when a negotiation is about to be sprung. That somewhere above us, a Magistrate was looking at a ledger line with our names on it and smiling the way paper smiles.

I woke Beatrix. She was awake before I touched her shoulder — she sleeps like a compass needle, always already pointing at trouble.

"They know we have it," I whispered. "They've known since the cage. They're not hunting us anymore, Trix. We've stopped running *from* something."

She looked at me in the dark, and the lantern between us pulsed once, slow, like a heart deciding.

"Then what are we running toward?" she asked.

"That's the problem," I said. "I don't think we are. I think they're about to come to *us*. And I think they're bringing an offer."

Because that was the other thing the boy's instinct knew, the fearless child's fluency in the grammar of predators: courts like this don't crush what they can *trade* for. And we were carrying the two most valuable objects in the underworld — a stolen soul, and the girl who could rewrite the walls.

Somewhere far above, unhurried as ink drying, a quill began to move.
