## 15. The Ledger Rebels

They came for me at the hour the court pretended was morning, which I had learned to identify by the candles burning a shade less sullenly and the bailiffs yawning behind their wax faces.

I had been in my cell — *cell* being a generous word for a room papered entirely in contracts, floor to ceiling, every one of them blank, every one of them watching — for what felt like two days. Time beneath the mountain was a rumor. I measured it instead by the stub of candle they allowed me, and by how many times I had redrawn the route from my door to the debt-vaults from memory: forty-one times, each identical, each a small act of rebellion no one could confiscate.

Then the lock turned without a key.

The Magistrate of Debts did not knock. I suspect knocking is beneath a creature who is, in essence, a very old clause wearing robes of vellum. He filled the doorway the way fine print fills a page — taking up more room than he appeared to, leaving less space to maneuver than you'd think. His face was parchment stretched over something that might once have been patient.

"Cartographer," he said. My name — my real one, the one I'd traded away at the ferry — sounded strange in his mouth, like a coin from a country that no longer existed. "You are summoned to negotiate."

"I'm a prisoner," I said. "Prisoners don't negotiate. Prisoners get interrogated."

"Prisoners," said the Magistrate, "who own something we want are called *counterparties*." He gestured with a hand like a rolled scroll. "Come."

Two bailiffs flanked me in the corridor. They were made, as far as I could tell, of writ and wax seal — their bodies dense legal text, their heads smooth red circles embossed with sigils that hurt slightly to look at directly. They walked with the confidence of things that have never once been contradicted.

We went down. The court beneath the mountain was, at its heart, a ledger-room grown vast — I had understood this since the night I'd crept through it disguised as an archivist of debts, my borrowed robes still smelling of someone else's dust. Every corridor was a column. Every arch was a rule. The nobles I passed in the galleries above — tall figures of inked obligation, their faces clauses personified, their voices the rustle of terms and conditions — leaned out to watch me pass, and I felt their attention the way you feel a quill hovering over your name.

I kept my face calm. This is a skill cartographers learn early: the land does not care that you are afraid, and neither does the scale bar. Fear makes you draw crooked lines. So I walked between my bailiffs with the unhurried gait of a woman being escorted somewhere she had already decided to go, and inside, where nobody could ledger it, I was counting.

*Forty-two,* I thought, tracing the vaults' layout in my head again. *The heart-chamber sits below the third gallery. Two guards at the outer door. The thorn-cage beyond.*

The Magistrate led me not to a courtroom but to what I can only describe as a reading room, if reading rooms were designed by something that hated readers. A long table of black wood. One chair for him. One for me. Between us, on a stand of tarnished silver, sat a book.

My book. The prophecy book, 1897, its cover worn soft by a century of hands in the Thornvale archive. Its pages — all but the last — blank. Blank the way an unwritten check is blank. Blank the way a loaded gun is blank.

"Sit," said the Magistrate.

I sat. I even arranged my skirts, because if you're going to be brave, you may as well be comfortable.

"You have in your possession," he began, in the tone of a man reciting terms he had personally drafted, "an instrument of considerable value. A book of unwritten pages. Do you know what such a book is worth, cartographer?"

"Ink's cheap," I said.

"The book is not for writing," the Magistrate continued, unbothered, "but for *binding*. Every contract this court has ever executed was inscribed upon pages of this kind. Pages that do not exist until they must. Pages that cannot be forged, because they were never anywhere to begin with." A dry sound that I suppose was his laugh. "This court is old, cartographer. Older than the mountain. And our book — our last true book — was spent long ago. We have been writing our recent centuries on lesser stock. Inferior bindings. Contracts that fray." His parchment face inclined toward the prophecy book with something almost like hunger. "Yours is virgin. Yours is *strong*. With it, we could bind ten thousand years of new debts."

"And what does that have to do with me?"

"Everything." He folded his hands. "Here is our offer. The book, freely surrendered. In exchange: the thief walks free. Both of you, past the border, past the veil, home to your valley of dead magic. We will even escort you to the seam ourselves. You have my word — and my word, cartographer, is a binding instrument."

There it was. Clean, simple, generous. Which is exactly how you know a deal is rotten — not because the terms are bad, but because the other side wants it too much.

I let silence sit on the table between us like a third party. Then I said, "Ivo's soul stays with him. All of it. Not the portion you've already... harvested."

A flicker across the parchment face. Good. I'd found an edge.

"The soul returns whole," the Magistrate said. "That is implicit in—"

"Nothing is implicit with you," I said. "You're a court. If it isn't written, it doesn't exist. Isn't that your whole theology?" I leaned back. "So write it. Whole soul. Free passage. No further claims on him, on his line, or on Thornvale. And I want it in front of me before I hand over anything."

The Magistrate regarded me for a long moment. Somewhere in the galleries overhead, a noble rustled.

"You bargain well," he said, "for a woman with nothing."

"I bargain well," I said, "for a woman holding the only blank book in the room."

And while he drew fresh vellum toward himself and began drafting the terms in his careful, terrible hand — while his quill scratched and scratched like something burrowing — I did what I have done my entire life, the thing I was doing the night the thorns took Ivo, the thing I will be doing, God willing, on the day I die.

I mapped.

Because here is what the court had failed to understand about me, the fatal error in their otherwise impeccable paperwork: they thought they had taken everything when they took my instruments and my name. But a map is not ink. A map is not paper. A map is a promise you make to yourself about where things are, and I had been making promises since I was six years old, hunched over my mother's table with a burnt-down candle, charting the creek behind our house.

So while the Magistrate wrote, I looked around the reading room with a prisoner's dull, defeated eyes — and behind those eyes, I surveyed.

The reading room sat on the gallery level, third tier. Through its high window — a slit of black glass — I could see down into the central shaft of the court, and at the bottom of the shaft, veiled in candle-haze, the glow of the heart-chamber. I counted the turns of the stair between here and there: seven. I noted the guard rotations visible from the window — bailiffs changed at intervals marked by the relighting of the great candelabra, and the candelabra had been relit twice since I'd sat down. I watched the procession of nobles crossing the upper galleries and saw that they avoided, universally, the western corridor — the one that smelled, faintly, of salt and older dark. Something down there even they didn't like. Worth knowing.

And I noticed — this was the important one, the one I would later carve into memory so deep that no ferryman could ferry it away — that the heart-chamber's defenses were not walls at all.

They were *entries*.

Every approach to the chamber was lined with standing stones, and every stone bore an inscription, and every inscription was a clause — warnings, conditions, forfeits. The court didn't guard its heart with soldiers. It guarded it with fine print. You couldn't be stopped from approaching; you could only be *bound* by approaching. Read the wrong stone, touch the wrong threshold, speak at the wrong time, and you signed yourself over without ever lifting a pen.

Which meant the way through was not force. It was literacy.

I read three of the stones from the window, squinting, committing their terms to memory. One concerned intent: *no hand shall claim the light whose hand intends to keep it.* Another concerned witness: *the theft must be seen to be theft.* The third made my blood run cold and then, strangely, warm:

*What is stolen in fulfillment of a prophecy belongs to the thief.*

The court had written its own loophole. A century ago, when they'd seeded that prophecy into a quiet valley archive, they had drafted the terms of their trap — and in their arrogance, in their certainty that the thief would fail, they had left the success-clause standing. Because why strike it? Thieves always fail. That was the design.

But designs are just maps of intention. And maps can be redrawn.

"...cartographer." The Magistrate's voice recalled me. "Your attention, please. The terms, as drafted."

He turned the vellum toward me. I read it properly — I always read properly; my grandmother taught me that the devil's favorite hiding place is the space between sentences. The terms were fair. That was the alarming part. Whole soul, free passage, no further claims. The Magistrate's word as binding instrument. Any archivist would have signed.

Which is precisely why I wasn't going to.

Because here is what else I knew, the knowledge sitting cold in my stomach beneath all the surveying: the court needed this deal more than I did, and things that need are things that fear. Why would a court of eternal creditors grow desperate enough to trade a harvested soul — the oldest asset in their ledger, the one Ivo's grandmother's contract had fed them for decades — for one blank book?

Unless the book wasn't the only thing they wanted.

Unless *I* was.

I thought of the ferryman's price. I thought of my name, gone from every map I'd ever drawn. I thought of the way the realm beyond the veil rearranged itself when unobserved — and the way it had begun, lately, to hesitate. To hold still under my gaze. To *obey*.

The court had noticed too.

"The book," the Magistrate prompted. "Do we conclude?"

"One condition more," I said, and my voice did not shake, and I have never been prouder of anything. "I want to see him first. Ivo. Alive, whole, in front of me. I don't sign anything against a promise made in the dark."

Something moved behind the parchment face — irritation, perhaps, or respect, or the particular resentment a creditor feels toward a debtor who reads the terms. "That is irregular."

"So am I. Ask anyone."

He considered. Above us, the galleries rustled with the attention of a hundred personified clauses, all leaning in, all listening, all hungry for the moment the cartographer would break.

She didn't break. She was busy. She was memorizing the fourth stone's inscription through the window — *the thief may carry out only what he can hold alone* — and noting that the candelabra had just been relit a third time, which meant the guard change was due, which meant that if a diversion were to occur, now would be the arithmetic of it—

"Granted," said the Magistrate. "You will see the thief. Then you will decide."

"And if I decide no?"

The parchment face did something terrible and gentle. "Then we take the book, and we take you, and we write a new contract on your skin, cartographer, and the valley gets a new prophecy in a hundred years. The court is patient. It is the one thing we never run out of."

I smiled at him. I actually smiled.

"Well then," I said, rising, smoothing my skirts, every inch the calm counterpartie he'd named me. "Let's go see my thief."

And as the bailiffs fell in beside me, as we descended the seven turns toward the vaults where Ivo waited — alive, whole, *waiting* — I carried with me out of that reading room four inscriptions, three guard-rotations, one western corridor everyone feared, and a single blazing certainty:

The court thought it had offered me a deal.

It had offered me a tour.

* * *

They brought me to him through the debt-vaults, past shelves of souls in jars like preserves, and I kept my eyes forward and my hands loose and my mind drawing, always drawing — vault widths, shelf spacing, the exact number of paces between the wardens' stations — until the corridor opened into a small round room with one lamp, and there he was.

Ivo. Behind a barrier of woven thorn that hummed faintly, like a held note. Sitting on the floor. Looking up at me with an expression I will remember longer than my mother's face, which is saying something, because I paid a ferryman for that one.

"Beatrix," he said. Just that. Like a man finding shore.

"Hello, thief," I said.

His mouth twitched. Even now. Even here. "They told me you were negotiating for my freedom."

"They told me I was selling a book." I crouched, put my palm flat against the thorn-barrier. It prickled but held — testing me, tasting my intent. *No hand shall claim the light whose hand intends to keep it.* Fine print, humming under my fingers. "We're both being told stories. It's rather the family business down here."

"Did you—"

"Not yet. Nothing's signed." I looked at him properly. He was thinner. There were shadows moving under his skin, faint and slow, like weather seen from very high up — the stolen soul leaking back into him, piece by piece, and I decided in that moment that whatever happened next, whatever it cost, those shadows were coming home with us. "Listen carefully, because I don't know how long they'll let this last. The heart-chamber is below the third gallery. Seven turns of the stair. The defenses aren't guards — they're clauses. Standing stones. You can walk right up to the cage if you read them correctly."

"Read them—"

"*No hand shall claim the light whose hand intends to keep it.*" I said it fast, low, pressing each word into him like a pin into a map. "*The theft must be seen to be theft. What is stolen in fulfillment of a prophecy belongs to the thief. The thief may carry out only what he can hold alone.* Four stones. Memorize them. Live by them."

Ivo stared at me. And then — this is the man I crossed a country of almost for — he began to smile. Slowly. Terribly. The smile of someone realizing the trap has a door in it.

"They wrote their own escape clause," he breathed.

"A century ago. Arrogance is a kind of blindness." I glanced at the door; the bailiffs' silhouettes shifted. Time. "One more thing. When they come to collect your answer — and they'll come soon — say yes."

His face went still. "Beatrix—"

"Say yes. Let them think the deal is struck. Let them relax the vaults. A court that believes it has won stops guarding the thing it believes it has already bought." I pressed my hand once more to the thorns, and though it stung, I didn't pull away, and neither did he, palm to palm through the barrier with a cage of fine print between us. "Three days to the new moon, Ivo. Say yes, count your stairs, and wait for me."

"And you?" he asked. "What will you be doing?"

I stood. Smoothed my skirts. Put on, I am quite sure, the calmest face ever worn in the history of the underworld.

"Mapping," I said.

The door opened behind me. The bailiffs entered. And Ivo — bless him, trust him, *love* him — looked the Magistrate's emissary dead in its waxen face and said, in a voice of perfect, ruined surrender:

"Yes. The deal stands. Give her the book."

The lie settled over the vaults like snowfall. Somewhere above us, a hundred personified clauses rustled with satisfaction, certain the ledger had won.

The ledger had no idea what was coming.
