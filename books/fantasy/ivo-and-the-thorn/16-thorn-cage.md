## 16. Thorn-Cage

**IVO**

The heart-chamber smelled of hot wax and old paper, which is the smell of every lie ever told properly.

I stood in the center of it with my hands raised like a man surrendering to a very polite firing squad, and I told the bailiffs the truth. All of it. The whole ugly inheritance of it.

"My grandmother," I said, "sold my soul with a fountain pen she bought at a church rummage sale."

The bailiffs did not laugh. Bailiffs never laugh; it's in their contract, probably, clause four, subsection grief. There were six of them ringing the chamber, creatures of writ and wax, their bodies stiff as legal documents and their faces blank as unsigned ones. Behind them rose the thorn-cage, a sphere of black brambles the size of a carriage, and inside it, caged in living thorn, hung the lantern.

The lantern that was me. Or had been me. Or was the part of me that some nights, when Beatrix slept against my shoulder, I could still feel missing — a cold spot behind my ribs shaped exactly like a boy who had never learned to be afraid.

"Fraud," I said, walking a slow circle so their seal-red eyes tracked me away from her. "That's what I've come to tell you. Your oldest asset is built on fraud. You want to hear how?"

One bailiff shifted. It sounded like a page turning.

Because here was the thing I'd learned in three days of hiding in the debt-vaults, learning the court's grammar: these creatures were not loyal to the Magistrate. They were loyal to the *terms*. A bailiff was a clause given legs. And clauses, I had discovered, could be embarrassed.

"In 1947 — or your equivalent of it, down here where the years are filed differently — Edith Marlowe came to this court and asked to buy peace for Thornvale. Magic-dead, safe, sealed. A valley where nothing from below could cross." I kept circling. "You gave her that. In exchange, you took a soul payable on maturity. Her grandson's. Mine."

I stopped. Let them look at me.

"But here's what she didn't tell you, because I found the letter in her effects after she died, folded inside a recipe for plum jam." My voice cracked and I let it, because a crack is more convincing than polish. "She didn't offer me. She offered *herself* first. And you refused her, because an old woman's soul was worth less than a young one's — less interest, less term. So she went home and she waited eleven years for me to be born, and she loved me, and she fed me, and she taught me to whittle, all while holding a bill of sale with my name on it under her tongue like a stone."

The bailiffs had gone very still. Stillness, in this court, meant attention.

"She defrauded you," I said softly. "Not me. Not the valley. *You.* She sold collateral she knew was already promised elsewhere — because I've read the margins, friends, I've read everything your archivist let me see before she remembered I wasn't supposed to be there. There's a prior claim on my soul. A small one. A village god, dead now, but claims outlive their claimants down here, don't they? Don't they?"

I had spent two days finding that prior claim. Two days in the stacks with Beatrix's compass and my own stubbornness, hunting through ledgers for anything — anything at all — that made my soul legally interesting rather than legally owned. And there it was: a dead god of hedgerows, owed one (1) soul, uncollected since 1893.

Which meant the court's title to me was clouded. Which meant, if I could keep enough eyes on me long enough—

Behind the bailiffs, half-hidden by the cage's shadow, Beatrix Brightwater knelt in the dark with a compass needle in her fingers, picking the lock of a prison made of thorns.

I raised my voice, warm as a salesman.

"So ask yourselves: when the Magistrate finds out your prize asset is tainted goods — when she learns you accepted fraudulent collateral and let it sit in your vaults accruing value for seventy years — whose signatures are on *that* failure?"

Somewhere in the chamber, a bailiff made a sound like a wax seal cracking.

I almost smiled. Almost. Because I could see her now, just past the nearest guard's shoulder — the pale line of her wrist, the small precise movements of her hands, the way she worked the needle into the cage's heart-lock the way she'd once worked a pen into a map's margin, confident that any structure, however ancient, however cruel, was still just lines someone had drawn, and lines could be redrawn.

Trust me, I wanted to say. Trust the mapmaker.

Instead I said, louder: "And another thing about my grandmother—"

* * *

**BEATRIX**

The lock was a knot.

Of course it was. Down here nothing was a mechanism when it could be a word instead, and the heart-lock of the thorn-cage was a single gnarled knot of black wood, tight as a fist, grown around itself. No keyhole. No tumblers. Just a knot that had been tied by something with too much patience and no mercy at all.

But knots have logic. Knots have *grain*.

I pressed the compass needle into the wood and felt for it — the direction the tension ran, the way the fibers leaned, the secret current underneath. Cartography, Ivo teased me, was just touching the world until it confessed its shape. Fine. This was that. I closed my eyes and touched the knot until it confessed.

*Left over right, then under, then the long pull back through the middle,* something in my hands understood. *Tied by someone left-handed. Tied quickly. Tied afraid.*

My grandmother had been left-handed too. I put that thought away in the drawer where I kept things I couldn't afford to feel yet.

Around me the chamber echoed with Ivo's voice, rising and falling, telling his grandmother's sins like a man reading a bedtime story backwards. He was magnificent. He was terrible at lying and magnificent at truth, and he had the six bailiffs leaning toward him like reeds in wind, because he was offering them the one thing a creature made of terms cannot resist: the possibility that the terms were broken.

*Left over right. Under. The long pull.*

The knot groaned. A strand of thorn the thickness of my finger slid loose, hissing where it scraped my knuckle, and blood welled up — my blood, real blood, valley blood, magic-dead blood — and where it fell on the cage's roots, the thorns *flinched*.

Oh, I thought. Oh, that's interesting.

Thornvale's border killed spells. But I was from Thornvale, made of its silence, steeped in its nullity like tea gone cold — and this cage was made of magic, hungry magic, binding magic. To it, I wasn't a thief.

I was poison.

I dragged my bleeding hand across the loosened strand and the thorn withered gray where I touched it, curling like paper in flame. The knot slackened. Somewhere above me, Ivo's voice climbed to a crescendo about prior claims and dead gods, and I pulled the whole lock apart with both hands, tearing the cage open along its seam like a letter nobody wanted read.

The lantern hung in the dark hollow of the cage, swinging gently, though there was no wind.

It was small. That undid me a little. All this — the court, the mountain, the prophecy, the century of arrangement — all of it for a light no bigger than a heartbeat, a soft gold glow the color of lamplight through kitchen curtains, the color of a valley at dusk. Inside the glass I could see shapes moving. Snow, maybe. A garden. A boy running through tall grass with his arms out like wings.

*Ivo's soul. The boy he was. The part that never learned to be afraid.*

"I'm here," I whispered to it, absurdly, the way you talk to a frightened animal. "He sent me. We're taking you home."

The lantern turned toward my voice.

And I understood, in the last calm moment I would own for a long time, that souls are not objects. They are *listeners*. They know their names in whatever mouth they hear them.

I lifted it from its hook.

* * *

**IVO**

"—and furthermore," I said, "the valuation itself was rigged, because the appraiser who assessed my soul in 1958 was later convicted of accepting bribes from the Thornvale tithe-office, a fact I can produce documentation for, assuming any of you can still read—"

The lantern flared.

I felt it before I saw it. Every soul in a body knows the temperature of its own absence, and mine had just been picked up by warm hands. The cold spot behind my ribs didn't fill — it *reached*, like a dog hearing its owner's step on the stair, and the leash of it snapped taut through the whole chamber, through me, through the floor.

Every bailiff turned at once.

Six seal-red faces swung from me to the torn-open cage to the woman standing in the wreckage of it, holding a small gold light in her cupped hands like a bird she'd rescued from a chimney. And Beatrix — brilliant, impossible Beatrix — looked directly at me across forty feet of waking catastrophe and said, with the ghost of her usual wryness:

"You should see the other cage."

Then the court woke up.

I don't know how else to describe it. The walls woke. The floor woke. The corridors beyond the heart-chamber, the vaults, the stacks, the endless filing-cabinets of the deep places — all of it shuddered awake at once, a sound like ten thousand drawers opening simultaneously, like a ledger being screamed. Wax seals burst along the walls and bloomed into new bailiffs, whole ranks of them, pouring into the chamber in rows, and above us all, far up through the mountain's throat, a bell began to toll with a voice like the Magistrate's own.

The lantern in Beatrix's hands flared brighter. It knew me. Of course it did. It was me, and it strained toward me across the air between us, and when I ran to her — when our hands met over the little gold light — the thing happened that I will feel behind my ribs for the rest of my life, however long the rest of my life turns out to be.

The soul recognized its body.

Warmth poured up my arms like sunrise, like the first snow of the year, like my grandmother's kitchen, like every good thing that had ever been quietly stolen from me and returned without explanation. For one full second I was whole. One entire second, complete, the boy in the grass and the man at the inn and the thief in the dark all agreeing at last on being the same person.

Beatrix was laughing. I think I was too.

Then the nearest bailiff's hand closed on my shoulder, and the second ended.

"Took you long enough," I said to her, as the wax-seal ranks closed in from every side and the bell tolled louder and somewhere far above the Magistrate began, very calmly, to speak.

"We're not done," she said, pulling me back toward the seam in the wall where the ferryman's road still glimmered, thin as a pencil line. "Run."

We ran.

Behind us, the heart-chamber sealed itself like a wound deciding to scar, and the voice of the Magistrate of Debts rolled down through the mountain, unhurried and absolute, reciting words I knew were about us before I heard them:

"*Let the record show.*"

The road ahead flickered.

"*Let the record show that the thieves have taken custody of the asset.*"

Beatrix's hand tightened in mine. Ahead, the ferryman's impossible road was unraveling, line by line, like a map burning from its edges inward.

"*And let the record further show*—" the bell tolled, and every door in the world beneath the mountain opened at once, "*—that the court now holds two.*"
