## 17. The Court Closes Its Hand

I have run from things before. Dogs, mostly, and once a wedding. But I had never run down a road that was being drawn underneath my feet by a woman who no longer technically existed on any map in the world, pursued by bailiffs made of wax and writ.

So there's that for the memoirs.

"Left!" Beatrix shouted ahead of me, her voice raw from shouting, which she had been doing for what felt like a year and was probably eleven minutes. "The corridor ends left — it *has* to end left —"

"It doesn't end at all!" I shouted back. "That's the problem with roads that don't exist! They're very committed to continuing!"

The lantern-soul beat against my chest like a second heart, because that is precisely what it was. When I'd pressed it into myself — into the hollow where my soul should have been living all along, paying rent to nobody — it had flared so bright the whole court screamed, and then it settled, warm and furious, under my ribs. And with every stride it leaked. That's the only word for it. Memories came through the cage of my own body like water through a cracked jug: snow on a green hill, a woman's hands kneading bread, a boy laughing at something I couldn't quite see because the boy was me and I was watching him from the outside.

I ran faster. You would too.

Behind us the corridors of the court were convulsing. Beatrix's unmaking-map had torn holes in the architecture — whole galleries dissolving into unsigned paper that drifted like ash — but the court was old, and old things are stubborn, and the bailiffs were already reforming out of the wreckage, sealing themselves back together with fresh stamps of red wax. They did not run so much as *proceed*, which was worse. A thing that proceeds knows it will arrive.

"There!" Beatrix pointed with her compass, which glowed faintly, pointing not north but *out* — toward the seam, toward home, toward the thin place where the world remembered itself. "The ferryman's road loops back — if we can reach the hinterland before the new moon rises—"

"How long?"

She glanced up, at nothing, at everything. The court had no sky, only a ceiling of ledger-pages far above, but somewhere beyond them the moon was sliding toward its own erasure. "Hours," she said. "Maybe fewer."

We ran.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about theft: it isn't the taking that's hard. Taking is easy. Any child can take. What's hard is the part after, when you're holding something that belongs to someone else and the whole shape of the world leans toward correcting your mistake.

The lantern-soul belonged to the court. It had belonged to the court for twenty-six years, since my grandmother signed her name in salt and candlelight and bought a valley's peace with her grandson's future. Every clause, every seal, every noble personified out of fine print — they were all leaning now. I could feel the lean like weather in my bones.

But I could also feel the other thing. The leak. My soul, coming home in pieces, and each piece made me more than I had been an hour ago and less able to pretend I didn't know exactly what we were doing.

We burst through an archway into the Antechamber of Accounts — I knew its name the way you know a word you've never said aloud, because my soul had been kept here and some of the keeping had soaked into me — and Beatrix skidded to a stop so fast I nearly went over her.

The way out was full of Magistrate.

Not one Magistrate. The Magistrate of Debts did not have a body so much as a jurisdiction, and tonight his jurisdiction was everywhere: a tall absence shaped like a man, wearing robes of interlocking clauses, his face a blank page with two entries cut into it where eyes should be. He stood between us and the seam-road with the patience of a mortgage.

"Well," I said, because my mouth has always been braver than the rest of me. "Lovely evening."

"You have stolen," the Magistrate said, and his voice was the sound of a stamp coming down, "an asset of this court."

"I've stolen *back* an asset," I corrected. "There's a difference. It's called provenance. Ask any archivist." I looked at Beatrix. She was pale, breathing hard, her compass clutched like a knife, and even now — even here — her eyes were moving, reading the room, reading the walls, reading the Magistrate himself the way she read coastlines. Looking for the flaw. There was always a flaw. She'd found one in a contract a century old; she could find one in a creature made of contracts.

"The soul returns to the vault," the Magistrate said. "The cartographer surrenders the book. In exchange, the court will consider clemency."

"Clemency," Beatrix repeated flatly.

"A reduction of penalties. Your memories may remain largely intact."

"Largely," she said again, and I heard it then, the wobble under the dryness, the fear wearing her sarcasm like a coat. "You're very generous with things that are already ours."

"They are not yours." The blank face tilted. "Nothing here is yours. That is the entire nature of this place. And you—" the entries where his eyes should be fixed on me, and the lantern under my ribs flared hot enough to hurt— "you are the most ours of all. Twenty-six years of accrued interest. Do you know what your soul has been earning, little thief? While you lived above, magicless and safe? It has been compounding."

I should have been afraid. I was afraid. But the leak chose that moment to give me something else — my grandmother's kitchen, steam on the window, her voice saying *Ivo, love, come look at the snow* — and grief and anger got tangled together so thoroughly I couldn't tell which one was making me step forward.

"Then let's settle the account," I said. "Read me the contract. All of it. Out loud. Every clause."

The Magistrate went still, in the way that paperwork goes still.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because," I said, "you want us to believe there's no way out, and in my experience — and I've had a *lot* of experience in the last three days — people who want you to stop asking questions are people whose answers have margins."

Beside me, Beatrix made a small sound. Not a laugh. The ghost of one. Hope, maybe, or its skeleton.

* * *

He read it.

I don't know why. Perhaps because the court runs on procedure the way a body runs on blood, and refusing a formal request for audit is the one thing a creature of clauses cannot do. Perhaps because he wanted us to hear it — wanted us to understand there was nothing there, no trick, no margin, no mercy — and thought the reading itself would be the execution.

He unrolled the contract in the air between us, and it was longer than the room, and he read it in a voice like falling dust.

Clause by clause. The sale of one soul, entire, belonging to Ivo Thornvale — yes, that was my name, my grandmother's name given backward, the valley's name given forward, I'd never thought about that until my soul started leaking back and suddenly I couldn't stop thinking about it. Payable upon majority. Consideration received: the binding of Thornvale against all enchantment, in perpetuity, sealed beneath the mountain. Signatures in salt. Signatures in candle smoke. Her signature last, steady, deliberate, the handwriting I'd grown up watching write shopping lists and birthday cards.

And then, near the end, the Magistrate paused.

It was a small pause. A professional pause. But I had spent three days learning to read this court, and Beatrix had spent her whole life learning to read documents, and we both caught it.

"What is it?" Beatrix asked softly. "Read it."

"The final clause," the Magistrate said, "is a rider. Added by the court at the time of drafting. Standard provision."

"Read it," she said again, and her voice had gone very quiet, the way it went when she'd found the flaw, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the underworld, through the mountain, through the valley, all the way home.

He read it.

*"In the event that the named thief attempts reclamation of the aforementioned asset, the court shall permit such attempt in its entirety. Should the attempt fail, the debtor's obligation shall be deemed doubled: the souls of both the thief and any accomplice shall stand forfeit to the court, in perpetuity, without appeal."*

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and interest rates.

"No," Beatrix said.

"The prophecy," the Magistrate continued, folding the contract away into the air, "was drafted by this court one hundred years ago. We wrote it. We planted it. We waited for a cartographer curious enough to find it and a thief foolish enough to believe it. The prophecy never promised success, cartographer. Read it again, when they give you time to read anything again. It promised a *thief*. A thief who tries. Failure was always the product. Two souls for the price of one grandmother's desperation." The blank face turned to me. "You were never meant to escape. You were meant to *arrive*."

The lantern-soul under my ribs went cold. Not dim — cold, the way metal goes cold, the way truth goes cold.

All of it. The book with its wet ink. The thorns at midnight. The door in the bleeding map. The ferryman. The margin-note we'd decoded with shaking hands, three days to new moon, we can do this, we can *do this* — every step of it laid down a century before either of us was born, a path drawn not to freedom but to the till.

"We ran," I said stupidly. "We fought. We stole it. We *won*—"

"You performed admirably," the Magistrate agreed. "The court commends thoroughness. It makes forfeiture so much cleaner when the assets have exhausted themselves."

* * *

They took Beatrix first.

I want to tell you she fought. She did — she drove her compass into the nearest bailiff and the thing shrieked like a kettle and tore open along its own signature, and for one bright half-second I thought—

Then there were six of them, then twelve, then the corridor behind us filled with proceeding shapes, and hands of wax and vellum closed around her arms, her shoulders, her throat, careful the way you're careful with an asset.

"Beatrix—"

"Don't," she said. She was calm. That was the worst part, that she made herself calm, courage wearing its work face, because I knew what it cost her and she knew I knew. "Ivo. Listen to me. Listen—" A bailiff turned her half around and she twisted back, found my eyes, held them. "A map held nowhere belongs to no one. Remember that. Whatever they take — whatever they think they've taken — remember *that*—"

The Magistrate raised one clause-shaped hand.

They took the book next. It came out of her coat trailing ribbon-marks, and I saw her flinch — really flinch, the first true break in her composure — because that book was hers, she'd bled for it, she'd paid her mother's face for passage and her name for a road, and watching it pass out of her hands into the Magistrate's blank grip was watching a century of her life get repossessed.

"Careful with that," the Magistrate said, almost gently, to the bailiff carrying it. "Blank pages are the most valuable paper in creation. We have been running low." He turned the book over in his hands, and I swear the pages purred. "With this, we will write ten thousand new contracts. New valleys. New grandmothers. New thieves who find new prophecies. Your story, little thief, will be told again and again, forever, and it will end this way every time."

The lantern-soul was being pulled out of me.

I felt it happen — felt their writs wrap around my ribs and draw the light out through the sternum, slow, procedural, itemized. I grabbed at it with hands that passed through. I said my grandmother's name. I said Beatrix's name. I said my own name, which I was only just learning how to say, and none of it mattered, because ownership is the oldest law there is, and the court owned me down to the letter.

The last thing to go was a memory. One leak, squeezing out sideways as the cage closed: snow on a green hill, a boy laughing, a version of me that had never learned to be afraid — and I understood, finally, what we had actually lost. Not the theft. Not the escape. The boy. He'd been waiting in that lantern for twenty-six years to come home, and I'd carried him three days and lost him in the last hundred yards.

They put him back in the dark.

They put us both in the dark.

* * *

The cell — I refuse to call it a vault, though I know that's what it was, I know I was filed — the cell had no door, because doors imply the possibility of the other side. It had walls of standing debt, and I sat against them, and I did not cry, mostly, and I counted the things I had left.

One: my body, minus a soul, minus most of my hope.

Two: Beatrix's voice in my memory, saying *a map held nowhere belongs to no one.*

Three: nothing. Two things.

Somewhere beyond the walls, the court was celebrating, or whatever courts do instead — stamping, perhaps, filing, accruing. Somewhere beyond the walls, the new moon was rising over the mountain, the deadline we'd failed to beat, the finish line of a race designed for us to lose. Somewhere beyond the walls, Beatrix was sitting in her own dark, stripped of her name and her maps and her mother's face, and I ached for her with the one organ I had left.

And then, in the dark, in the silence, in the smallest possible voice — hers, arriving through the debt-walls like light through a crack, or like a line drawn on paper that hasn't been printed yet — I heard her say:

"Ivo. Are you there?"

"Yes," I said.

"Good," said Beatrix Brightwater, who owned nothing, who was written nowhere, whom no ledger in creation could find. "Because I've been memorizing this prison. And I'm going to redraw it."
